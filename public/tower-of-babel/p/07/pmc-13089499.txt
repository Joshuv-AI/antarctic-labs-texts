Longitudinal sampling of New Zealand cockles (Austrovenus stutchburyi) reveals high viral diversity and taxa-specific responses to environmental factors
Authors: Isa F de Vries, Stephanie J Waller, Rebecca K French, Rebecca M Grimwood, Jemma L Geoghegan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBivalves bioaccumulate microorganisms and, therefore, serve as effective indicators of microbial activity in intertidal ecosystems. Monitoring bivalve-associated viruses can provide insight into circulating viral communities and their relationship with environmental change. The New Zealand cockle (Austrovenus stutchburyi) is culturally, ecologically, and economically important, yet little is known about the viruses it hosts, how these change over time, or whether cockles act as reservoirs for pathogenic viruses affecting bivalves or humans. We used a metatranscriptomic approach to characterise the cockle RNA virome over 12 months and assess whether environmental parameters influenced viral dynamics. Longitudinal sampling revealed a highly diverse RNA virome spanning 16 viral orders within the kingdom Orthornavirae. Phylogenetic analysis of RNA-dependent RNA polymerase sequences identified 358 viral transcripts representing 213 distinct viral species, of which 199 were likely novel, including several putative new virus families and three orders. No viruses known to be pathogenic to humans or bivalves were detected. While total virome composition showed no strong seasonal patterns, environmental parameters influenced virus abundance within specific viral orders, including the highly abundant Picornavirales, Wolframvirales, Bunyavirales, and Sobelivirales. Overall, New Zealand cockles harbour an exceptionally diverse RNA virome, with environmental drivers acting in a taxon-specific rather than community-wide manner.

Introduction

Virome-focused meta-transcriptomic studies are shifting toward longitudinal timelines to capture changing virus groups over time. However, this remains relevant only for well-studied species that pose direct significance to humans either economically or biologically (Wallace et al. 2025). This leaves many species understudied, regardless of their environmental and cultural significance, like bivalves. Over 8 500 marine bivalve species exist within the phylum Mollusca, which is one of the most diverse and oldest groups within the animal kingdom (Potasman et al. 2002). Bivalves are keystone organisms, defined as an organism that, relative to their abundance, has an inordinate impact on their surrounding ecosystem and other marine organisms (Dodds 2002). This impact is due to their herbivorous filter-feeding behaviour, concentrating entities of their surroundings, improving water transparency, nutrient circulation and overall productivity (Anderson et al. 2019, Razafimahefa et al. 2020). Consequently, bivalves can be used for surveillance of pathogens in ecosystems and markers of contaminants (Dayaram et al. 2015).

Molluscs typically inhabit nutrient-rich, shallow waters. As such, they are often exposed to high concentrations of contaminants, including free-floating viruses, as well as bacteria and parasites, often originating from human sewage and agricultural runoff. These contaminants are bioaccumulated, creating a diverse microbiome often comprising human-associated pathogens (Potasman et al. 2002, Razafimahefa et al. 2020, Masanja et al. 2023). Human consumption of undercooked bivalves can, therefore, facilitate the spread of potential pathogens associated with food-borne disease (Potasman et al. 2002, Razafimahefa et al. 2020). Among the 172 instances of foodborne human disease outbreaks recorded in New Zealand from 2009 to 2015, 8% were attributed to norovirus infection from shellfish (Hewitt et al. 2019). Worldwide, shellfish consumption has also caused a substantial number of foodborne disease outbreaks (Potasman et al. 2002), including in Shanghai, China, where 290 000 people contracted Hepatitis A virus due to the consumption of raw clams (Halliday et al. 1991). Other investigations into enteric viruses of shellfish marketed in Vietnam revealed that a significant portion contained norovirus, Hepatitis E virus, Hepatitis A virus, astroviruses, and Aichi virus (Suffredini et al. 2020). A notable proportion of globally reported instances of seafood-related illness are not attributed to any specific cause, yet are commonly presumed to be viral in origin (Potasman et al. 2002).

Viral infectious diseases in molluscs pose a threat not only to humans but also to molluscs’ health (Rosani and Gerdol 2017). Disease outbreaks in molluscs have been reported globally, including in North America (Leibovitz et al. 1978), Europe (Segarra et al. 2010, Lynch et al. 2012, Mortensen et al. 2016), China (Bai et al. 2015), and South Korea (Hwang et al. 2013), and occurrences have dated back as early as 1966 (Zannella et al. 2017). For example, Ostreid herpesvirus 1 (OsHV-1) μvar variant, a virus within the Malacoherpesviridae, has caused mass mortalities since 2008 across Europe in numerous bivalve species including Pacific oysters (Crassostrea gigas) (Abbadi et al. 2018). Changes in environmental water conditions, such as nutrients, pollution, salinity, plankton levels and temperature can influence the bivalve microbiome, which is thought to play a crucial role in host health and immune defence systems (Farley et al. 1972, Lynch et al. 2012, Masanja et al. 2023). Unfavourable environmental conditions can therefore contribute to the susceptibility to OsHV-1 μvar and thus monitoring of environmental factors is crucial to accurately assess future disease risk (Masanja et al. 2023).

The New Zealand cockle (Austrovenus stutchburyi), or tuangi in te reo Māori, is a shellfish endemic to New Zealand. This cockle plays a key role as a bed-forming, habitat-defining species. As ecological engineers, their suspension-feeding enhances sandflat primary productivity and nutrient cycling, while their shell material shapes tidal flat biodiversity, alters sediment composition and increases habitat heterogeneity, benefiting a diverse range of organisms (Lohrer et al. 2016). Culturally, these cockles are a key part of the identity and wellbeing of Māori; they have long sustained indigenous communities and aided the transfer of customary practices and knowledge across many generations (NIWA 2021). New Zealand cockles are the most widespread shellfish throughout New Zealand and are central to the seafood sector on a commercial scale, with inshore shellfish accounting for 22% of the overall New Zealand seafood export earnings (Ministry for Primary Industries 2023). It is, therefore, critical to maintain cockle populations to preserve their economic, cultural and ecological value.

Herein, we use a longitudinal metatranscriptomic approach to investigate the RNA virome circulating through three locations in southeast coastal New Zealand via the ecological indicators, the New Zealand cockle. This temporal study design has allowed us to investigate seasonal differences and explore potential environmental drivers of viral community composition. These data will allow us to establish a baseline understanding of the viruses that are harboured by visually healthy cockles, which, in turn, could help inform diagnostics in the event of a disease outbreak.

Methods

Sample collection and permissions

New Zealand cockles were collected once per month over a one-year period (April 2023–March 2024) from three coastal locations across Otago, New Zealand, that resided outside of the New Zealand Department of Conservation’s Public Conservation Land (Department of Conservation 2018). These locations were Waipuna Bay (−45.785895, 170.675184), Doctor’s Point (−45.735021, 170.596645), and Papanui Inlet (−45.854215, 170.691271) (Fig. 1). Cockles are widespread across these shallow embayments and are regularly gathered by locals for human consumption (Peake et al. 2006). While no ethics approvals or permits were required, this project was supported by the East Otago Taiāpure Management Committee, an indigenous, Māori-led community group from the area with whom we consulted about the project.

(B) Water parameters over time, including dissolved oxygen (%DO), pH, salinity (PSU), and temperature (°C), across the three sampling locations, colour coded as in panel (A).

Collection of cockles

Twelve individual cockles with no overt signs of disease were hand harvested at low tide from each site, totalling 36 cockles collected every month. Cockles were only sampled if positioned 10–15 cm below the surface, ensuring water submergence at the time of gathering. Individuals were placed in buckets containing seawater and sediment from the respective locations and immediately transported to the laboratory for dissection. The cockles were opened, and the foot, gills and palps were dissected and removed under a fume hood with sterile scalpels and forceps. These tissues were then placed in 2 ml sterile tubes containing 1 ml RNAlater and stored in a −80 °C freezer prior to total RNA extraction.

Environmental data collection

Alongside cockle collection, water parameters were also measured monthly at each site. A Yellow Springs Instruments multi-meter (Hanna instruments) was used to record dissolved oxygen (% DO), salinity (practical salinity unit, PSU), pH, and temperature (°C) of the shallow intertidal waters. Weather conditions were also taken into consideration, specifically on the day and three days prior to sampling, with the data taken from Geoweather and NIWA (https://data.niwa.co.nz/products/climate-station-daily/files/67f3098a8e220b82bbb76dde, https://geoweather.otago.ac.nz/default.aspx) to correlate any potentially irregular water parameters to any significant weather events such as high rainfall (Kim et al. 2024).

Total RNA extraction

The ZymoBIOMICS MagBead RNA kit (Zymo Research) was used for total RNA extraction from cockle tissue. For this procedure, frozen cockle tissues were defrosted and small pieces were dissected from each section and placed together in ZR BashingBead Lysis Tubes (0.1 and 0.5 mm) (Zymo Research) filled with 1 ml of DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research). The gill, foot and palp tissues dissected from each individual were subsequently homogenised together prior to RNA extraction to maximise viral detection sensitivity. A mini-Beadbeater 24 Disruptor (Biospec Products Incorporated) was used to homogenise the tissue with three intervals of 90 s and one interval of 30 s with 60 s resting on ice in between to avoid heat-causing denaturing of nucleotides. The manufacturer’s protocol, including the DNase1 mix step, was used to extract total RNA. RNA containing guanidine contamination underwent a cleaning protocol using the RNA Clean and Concentrator Magbead Kit (Zymo Research) to minimise contamination. RNA concentrations and purity were quantified using a Nanodrop. Equal volumes of RNA from the 12 individuals collected from the same location and date were pooled. Pooling RNA from individuals per site and time point is a commonly used strategy in virus discovery studies, as viral RNA is often present at low abundance. This approach increases sensitivity for detecting rare viruses and provides a population-level representation rather than individual-level variation. This resulted in 36 samples of pooled RNA (i.e. 12 months of samples across three locations; see Supplementary Table 1).

RNA sequencing

Pooled RNA samples were subject to total RNA sequencing. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep with Ribo-Zero Plus (Illumina) and 16 cycles of Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). Paired-end 150 bp sequencing of the RNA libraries was executed on the Illumina NovaSeq X platform.

Quality control, virus identification, and virome assembly

Trimmomatic (v0.39) was used to trim Nextera paired-end adapters and low-quality bases below five were trimmed with a sliding window approach (Bolger et al. 2014). Trimmed raw reads were analysed using FastQC (v0.11.9) (Andrews 2010). Raw reads were assembled into contigs de novo using Megahit (v1.2.9) (Li et al. 2015). Viruses were identified using a sequence similarity-based search comparing assembled contigs against the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) non-redundant (nr) protein database (May 2024) using Diamond BLASTx (v) (Buchfink et al. 2015). A sequence similarity cut-off value of 1 × 10−5 was used to filter putative viral contigs. Contigs were categorised into higher kingdoms using BLASTx ‘sskingsdoms’ option. Manual screening removed non-viral BLAST hits, including host contigs with sequence similarity to viral transcripts. Based on the BLASTx results, putative viral contigs were examined further with Geneious Prime (2024.0.5) to find and translate open reading frames (ORFs).

Viral transcript abundance estimation

Viral transcript abundance was evaluated using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4) (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). Differences between library read depth were accounted for by standardisation, expressing the viral abundance in reads per million (RPM). This number divides the read count by the total number of library reads, multiplied by one million. Potential contamination from index-hopping was identified when viral reads constituted less than 0.1% of the read count in another library and showed more than 99% nucleotide identity. No viruses meeting these criteria were identified.

Assessing diversity across time

All statistical analysis plots were created using RStudio v2024.04.2. Water parameter data were analysed using line graphs with the ggplot2 (Wickham 2016) and dplyr packages (Wickham et al. 2023). Alpha diversity within each library was assessed using viral richness (the number of viral orders) and the Shannon diversity index, which measures sample richness and evenness [here, using standardised abundance (in RPM)], of viral orders across time and location. We used the taxonomic level of viral order (as opposed to species, genus, or family) due to the lack of classification below this level for many viral species. This diversity analysis function is part of the Vegan package in R (Oksanen et al. 2020).

All trendlines used in the figures were statistically deemed the most appropriate by utilising the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to determine which Generalised Additive Model best represented the data. Differences between overall richness were visualised with pie charts and stacked bar plots for viral order distribution by month using the ggplot2, dplyr, and RColorBrewer (Neuwirth 2022) packages. Shannon diversity variation over time was visualised using box and whisker plots (Wu et al. 2022). An analysis of variance (ANOVA) model was used for statistical analysis (Montgomery 2019). To demonstrate how viral richness varied within each viral order, box and whisker plots, and locally weighted regression (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing (LOESS)) trendlines were employed using the dplyr and readr packages. These data were then visualised with scatter plots showing the correlation of each water parameter and standardised abundances.

Beta diversity was used to analyse variation in viral community composition at the viral order level across the different locations and time points, determining whether environmental variables influenced the standardised abundances and diversity of viral taxa. This analysis utilised Adonis tests (Anderson 2001) (permutational multivariate ANOVA based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix), which is also part of the Vegan package. Results were visualised through non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) with a Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix (Bray and Curtis 1957), presented as an ordination plot using the readr and tibble packages.

Phylogenetic analysis of viral transcripts

The translated protein sequences from viral polymerases (i.e. RdRp) were aligned using MAFFT (v7.490) (Katoh and Standley 2013) using the L-INS-I algorithm with representative virus protein sequences obtained from NCBI. TrimAL (v1.5.) (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009) removed poorly aligned regions with a gap threshold set to 0.9, and IQ-TREE (v1.6.12) was used to estimate a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree for all RNA viral taxa (Nguyen et al. 2015). The LG amino acid substitution model (Le and Gascuel 2008) was selected with 1 000 ultra-fast bootstrapping replicates (Hoang et al. 2018). The phylogenetic tree was annotated using Figtree (v1.4.4) (Rambaut 2012). Additional maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were estimated for each phylum separately using the same parameters as above.

Viral classification using protein structure homology

Many viral RdRp transcripts that were uncovered were highly divergent and classified no further than realm (Riboviria) or kingdom level using sequence comparisons to entries in reference databases. A protein structure homology-based search was used to classify these highly divergent transcripts into a viral order. The translated ORFs of these virus transcripts were subject to RdRp-Scan (Charon et al. 2022), which uses viral structural comparisons and hidden Markov models (HMM) to identify putative viral polymerase sequences.

Viral nomenclature

Viruses were considered a novel species if they shared less than 90% amino acid sequence similarity within a conserved sequence (RdRp). A viral name was proposed for all novel viral sequences, contingent upon validation by the ICTV (International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses) (see Supplementary Table 2). We note that this threshold represents a pragmatic operational definition consistent with ICTV guidelines for many RNA virus groups, and should be interpreted as reflecting genetic diversity rather than strict biological species boundaries.

Data availability

Sequencing reads can be found on NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject Accession (PRJNA1226259) and virus sequences are available under the GenBank accession numbers PV034044–PV034242. R scripts and RdRp-Scan scripts can be found on Github: (https://github.com/isafemke/Cockle-Virome/blob/main/Isa_de_Vries_cockles_stats.Rmd) and (https://github.com/stephwaller/RdRp-Scan), respectively.

Results

Viral abundance and diversity

RNA was extracted from 432 cockles over 12 months and three locations located on the southeast coast of New Zealand. RNA from cockle tissues, taken from the gills, foot, and palps, was pooled based on these three locations and time points, totalling 36 RNA sequencing libraries. The number of sequencing reads produced from the pools varied between 60 and 334 million paired-end reads per library (Supplementary Table 1). Out of the total number of raw reads across all the libraries, 0.1% were characterised as viral (Fig. 2). Viruses with RdRp sequences identified were able to be subjected to taxonomic classification. Out of 358 viral RdRp sequences identified, 90.5% (n = 324) were below the 90% animo acid sequence similarity threshold and were considered novel viral transcripts (Fig. 2).

The remaining ∼19% belonged to 15 other viral orders, as well as unclassified sequences (right pie). (B) Overall abundances of the viral orders across the three sampling locations: Doctor’s Point (left), Waipuna bay (middle), Papanui inlet (right). (C) Percentage amino acid sequence similarity of the 324 viral RNA RdRp sequences to their closest known virus relative.

Virome analysis of the cockle libraries revealed that the RNA viruses present within the intertidal ecosystem were highly diverse (Fig. 2). Viruses detected in cockles shared sequence similarity to viruses infecting a wide variety of hosts, including vertebrates, invertebrates, bacteria, plants, fungi, protists, as well as free-floating viruses and those with no identified host. Overall, we identified viral transcripts from 16 viral orders spanning all five classified RNA viral phyla of the Orthornavirae kingdom (Fig. 2).

Temporal viral trends

We determined the effect of water parameters measured in this study; pH, temperature (C°), dissolved oxygen (% DO), and salinity (PSU), on viral community composition across the 12 months of sampling. The sampling locations were geographically proximate and thus each site experienced similar environmental parameters (Fig. 1). Weather data that were recorded alongside the aquatic parameters showed no major weather events occurred close to the sampling dates that could have significantly influenced the environmental conditions to which the cockles were exposed.

To explore any associations between virome composition and environmental factors, we used a Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix in 2D space to quantify dissimilarity between samples collected across 12 months. With a stress value of 0.1229, this ordination accurately captured the similarities of the samples. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to test the influence of all four water parameters on viral communities (at virus order level). This analysis indicated a non-statistically significant relationship (P > 0.5) between all measured water parameters, location and overall virus communities (see Fig. 3, e.g.).

Generalised Additive Models were used to assess the relationships between viral abundance within each viral order and environmental factor, which were assessed using the AIC. We found that, on average, the Picornavirales, the most dominant viral order (see Fig. 3), was even more abundant during warmer months (November–February) compared to cooler months, with a significant linear relationship between picornavirus abundance and temperature (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4). Similarly, the abundance of viral transcripts in the Wolframvirales was positively associated with temperature (P < 0.01), while those in the Sobelivirales, were weakly yet significantly associated with dissolved oxygen and salinity (Fig. 4; P < 0.05). The abundance of viral transcripts within the Bunyavirales was also associated with dissolved oxygen (P < 0.05), where a unimodal trend indicated viral abundance increased in the middle ranges of dissolved oxygen (Fig. 4). It is noteworthy that the abundance of viral transcripts from these four viral orders was highly variable between sampling locations, with little consistency and only significantly associated when considering mean abundance values across all locations. The abundance of viral transcripts among the other 12 taxonomic orders was not associated with any of the water parameters measured (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Novel viral transcripts

A total of 358 viral RdRp transcripts were uncovered that fell across 16 viral orders, representing 213 viral species, of which only 14 shared >90% amino acid sequence similarity to known viruses, resulting in 199 potentially novel viral species. While other viral transcripts were present, the RdRp is the only hallmark gene that is conserved across the entire Orthornavirae kingdom (Shi et al. 2016). Therefore, we relied on the RdRp to detect and phylogenetically analyse viruses here, which is the gold standard approach for metatranscriptomic characterisation of riboviruses (Urayama et al. 2023). To this end, phylogenetic analysis of the newly discovered viruses in New Zealand cockles revealed that all novel viral transcripts fell within the five established phyla of the Orthornavirae kingdom (Fig. 5).

The closest genetic matches for all viruses uncovered here are included along with other representative species. Branches are scaled to the number of amino acid substitutions per site. Reference sequences are shown with grey branches, and the newly identified viruses from this study are in red. Phylogenetic trees for the individual orders within each phylum can be found in Supplementary Fig. 2. Kitrinoviricota

The Kitrinoviricota, a phylum of positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses, have a broad host range and are known to infect invertebrates, vertebrates, protists, plants and fungi (Canuti et al. 2022). We identified 35 virus transcripts within this phylum, all of which were novel species, sharing 40–60% amino acid sequence similarity to known viruses. Three of these viruses fell within Martellivirales and six across Tolivirales, all of which clustered with known viral families. A further 14 viruses fell within the Nodamuvirales order. Three of the 14 were most genetically similar to the Raina virus identified in penguins (QIS87964.1). The distance of this branch within Nodumavirales (see Supplementary Fig. 2) may be indicative of a new viral family. Seven novel viruses fell within Yan-like viruses found in crayfish and a further four viruses clustered with Wei-like viruses in freshwater mussels (Fig. 5) (Shi et al. 2016, Wolf et al. 2020).

Lenarviricota

The Lenarviricota consist of positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (Hillary et al. 2022). Wolframvirales, Cryppavirales, and Ourlivirales are mostly plant and fungi host-associated orders, while Leviviricetes viruses infect bacteria (Hulo et al. 2011). Viruses from this phylum are phylogenetically diverse and are found in high abundance in most environments (Sadiq et al. 2022). We found 40 virus species within this phylum, 34 of which we considered to be novel. Of these, one virus was found within Ourlivirales, four belonged to the Cryppavirales, and 35 viruses were found across the Wolframvirales. Out of these 35 viruses, six viruses had amino acid sequence similarity greater than 90% to already known viruses, while the other 29 were highly divergent. One novel virus within Wolframvirales was phylogenetically distinct from other known viruses, with 40% amino acid sequence similarity to Ripashad virus (WZH60957.1) within Amabiliviricetes and appeared phylogenetically divergent from other known virus families (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Duplornaviricota

Three viral orders—the Reovirales, Ghabrivirales, and Mindivirales—belong to the Duplornaviricota phylum. The Duplornaviricota consists of viruses that possess a positive-sense double-stranded RNA genome (Je et al. 2023). Viruses within this phylum have a wide host range, including birds, fish, mammals, arthropods, reptiles, fungi, plants, and algae (ICTV 2023). Eleven novel viruses were uncovered within this phylum. Five novel viruses belonged to the Reovirales, sharing ∼20%–60% amino acid sequence identity with their closest known relatives. Even though the amino acid sequence similarity was proportionally low, these viruses clustered closely to other known viruses. The other six viruses were identified within the Ghabrivirales order, sharing ∼36%–69% amino acid sequence identity to known viruses, with a divergent branch clustering with Behai sesarmid crab virus 7 (YP_009333465.1).

Negarnaviricota

The Negarnaviricota comprises negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (Kuhn et al. 2022, Zayed et al. 2022). Thirteen novel viruses were found within this phylum. Seven viruses were most closely related to viral sequences within the Bunyavirales order. A very distinct cluster of viruses all shared amino acid sequence similarity to Behai bunya-like virus 3 (APG79245.1) found in Paphia species. The distance of this branch indicated a potential new viral order (see Supplementary Fig. 2). Four viruses were found within the Mononegavirales, all of which were considered novel, sharing 64%–69% amino acid sequence similarity to known viruses. Two further viruses clustered in proximity to the Tosoviridae, of which only three virus species have been uncovered to date (Waltzek et al. 2022, Grimwood et al. 2023). These viruses shared only 23%–26% amino acid sequence similarity to the rest of this family.

Pisuviricota

As the most abundant phylum in this study, which are commonly found in marine RNA virus surveys, viruses within the Pisuviricota phyla are characterised by predominantly positive-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses with some double-stranded RNA viruses too (Hulo et al. 2011, Hillary et al. 2022). These viruses infect a wide range of hosts from vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, and fungi (Hulo et al. 2011). Transcripts within the Picornavirales were most abundant with 57 viral RdRps uncovered. Of these viruses, 49 were considered novel species with amino acid sequence similarity to known viral species ranging between 25%–83%. Within the Sobelivirales, 22 novel viruses were identified, and the Durnavirales contained 11 novel viruses. There was one novel virus that fell within the Nidovirales, but with only 25% amino acid sequence similarity to its closest genetic relative, it was highly divergent. Finally, 23 viruses had a closest genetic relative matching viruses that were unclassified beyond the Riboviria. Nevertheless, other related viruses that shared sequence similarity to these transcripts were characterised as picorna-like viruses, and these novel viruses also fell within this domain (Fig. 5).

Virus species of notability

No known bivalve-associated pathogens were detected. While it is challenging to determine whether viruses found here were indeed infecting the bivalve hosts, phylogenetic proximity to known bivalve viruses helped to better understand host associations. For example, two novel viruses within the Picornavirales were most closely related to Scallop picorna-like virus 2, found in scallops in the USA (Scro et al. 2023). Another virus within this order was most genetically similar to Behai picobirna-like virus 10 (Shi et al. 2016), found in jackknife clams. Both viruses, however, are not known to cause disease in bivalves. Similarly, we did not detect any known human pathogens.

Discussion

The cockle RNA virome

The temporal RNA virome composition of cockles across coastal Otago, New Zealand, was assessed using metatranscriptomics. While this approach can detect transcripts from DNA viruses, our analysis was intentionally restricted to RNA viruses to focus on a well-defined and comparable component of the virome and to minimise challenges associated with host assignment, particularly for bacteriophage-dominated DNA virus communities. By sampling cockles from the intertidal zone over 12 months, we uncovered a total of 358 RdRp viral transcripts, representing 213 virus species, 199 of which were considered novel. A subset of the environmental parameters from the intertidal zone, including pH, salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen, was investigated, uncovering several correlations between viruses in highly abundant viral orders such as the Picornaviriales, Sobelivirales, Bunyavirales, and Wolframvirales, with the measured environmental parameters.

Composition and host associations of the cockle virome

While we found a wide diversity of viruses in New Zealand cockles, a small number of viruses dominated the virome in very high abundance, while many others were detected only at low abundances, similar to previous findings in the marine environment (Suttle 2005, Steward et al. 2013, Hevroni et al. 2020). Viruses within the Picornavirales were the most abundant, comprising 81.5% of the total cockle virome. Picornaviruses are in high abundance in marine environments (Steward et al. 2013, Vlok et al. 2019, Kolundžija et al. 2022), possibly due to their structural advantage of a nonenveloped icosahedral capsid (Munke et al. 2020) that could assist in the resilience of the virus outside of a host. Picornaviruses also have a wide host range, including protists, fungi, and algae (Burge et al. 2016), which are generally widespread and a key part of the filter-feeding diet of bivalves.

Distinguishing viruses actively infecting cockles from those present due to filter feeding remains a key challenge. To address this, we applied a conservative filtering approach, excluding sequences closely related to viruses known to infect non-molluscan hosts or commonly associated with environmental microbial communities. However, host assignment cannot be definitively resolved from metatranscriptomic data alone, and some environmentally derived viruses are likely still represented in our dataset. Of the other virus orders, viruses belonging to the Sobelivirales, Wolframvirales, and Bunyavirales were also comparatively abundant, ranging from 2%–5% of the total viral richness. Such viral orders also infect other invertebrate hosts such as Arthropoda and Annelida (Zhang et al. 2022). Similar to picornaviruses, viruses within the Wolframvirales possess a seemingly simple structure, as well as infect a broad host range including fungi, arthropods and algae (DeRisi et al. 2019). Such hosts include Bigyra species, which are marine protist (Chiba et al. 2023) and are regularly filtered by cockles. Viruses within the Sobelivirales are also nonenveloped with an icosahedral capsid. This resilient structure, along with the host range of plants and invertebrates, may explain the high abundance of sobeliviruses found in the cockle virome. Lastly, viruses in the Bunyavirales infect a wide range of hosts with 13 families in this order infecting crustaceans, plants, and insects, where they replicate with little cytopathogenicity (Steward et al. 2013, Leventhal et al. 2021). This wide host range likely explains the high abundance of bunyaviruses in cockles.

Environmental drivers of viral abundance

Environmental water parameters have been shown to impact viral diversity and transmission, as well as host behaviour (Adkins 2012, Chen et al. 2019, Santos et al. 2022). Drastically changing environmental parameters such as temperature, oxygen and salinity can cause displacement and dispersal of host organisms into new areas, altering their behaviour, interactions and distribution, ultimately impacting viral inter-host transmission. For example, temperature is a highly influential environmental factor (Pinon and Vialette 2018), with higher temperatures increasing phytoplankton concentrations, resulting in high food quality, promoting bivalve growth (Dowd 1997). Cockles are resilient to the natural temperature fluctuations that occur in intertidal estuaries (Studer et al. 2013). However, too extreme environmental conditions, for example, temperatures above 33 °C, algal blooms and hypoxic events can have lethal consequences (Salmond and Wing 2023). Therefore, a fine balance of optimal environmental conditions is important in promoting growth and reproduction within cockle populations. Denser populations, however, can increase viral shedding and thus horizontal transmission, potentially increasing viral abundance and diversity (Zannella et al. 2017). We found that temperature positively affected the abundance of viruses within the Picornavirales and Wolframvirales orders. Further, the abundance of sobeliviruses was found to correlate negatively with dissolved oxygen and salinity, while bunyaviruses were found to have a unimodal relationship with dissolved oxygen. Such findings highlight the delicate dynamics that exist between environmental parameters, microbial communities and host populations.

Despite apparent associations with highly abundant viral orders, we found little consistency among water parameters and total virome composition, suggesting the interplay between virus diversity and seasonality is complex and highly taxa specific. Further, it is likely that many more unmeasured variables have roles in the fluctuation of viruses and viral communities. Other key environmental variables, including conductivity, total organic carbon, nitrate, nitrite, phosphate, and chlorophyll—a contents and sunlight exposure have been found to co-influence viral community structures in the marine environment (Hegarty et al. 2022, Du et al. 2023, Piedade et al. 2024). Such parameters are projected to vary due to climate change (Maar et al. 2024), altering infection strategies and overall dynamics of viral communities, ultimately impacting viral emergence and disease outbreaks (Howe-Kerr et al. 2023).

Novel viral diversity and evolutionary insights

The 213 viral species identified here fell across the five classified phyla within the Orthornavirae kingdom. Metatranscriptomic approaches often identify viruses that are highly divergent, particularly in under sampled hosts. Phylogenetic analysis of cockle viruses revealed that all viruses clustered within known viral phyla, with the majority related to viral species found in other aquatic meta ‘omic studies (Shi et al. 2016, Bačnik et al. 2021, Chen et al. 2022, 2023, Sadiq et al. 2024). Nevertheless, several viruses identified here were highly divergent and likely signified novel viral families or orders. Two clusters of viruses within Kitrinoviricota, for example, which included the ‘yan-like’ and ‘'wei-like’ viruses, were highly divergent, indicative that yan- and wei-like viruses represent new viral orders yet to be classified within the Kitrinoviricota. Another divergent cluster falling close to the Nodamuvirales consisted of novel viruses that clustered with Raina virus, an unclassified virus infecting penguin ticks (Wille et al. 2020). Again, this cluster likely represents a new viral family within this order. Similarly, the Ghabrivirales, within the Duplornaviricota phylum also possessed a highly divergent cluster of novel ghabri-like viruses that were phylogenetically close to Beihai sesarmid crab virus 7. This distant branch is indicative of a new family within this viral order. Two highly divergent viral clusters were also identified in the Pisuviricota. One cluster, within the Picornavirales, most closely related to Beihai octopus virus 2, may represent a novel viral order within this phylum. Another cluster near the Sobelivirales and most closely related to Behai sobemo-like virus 18 infecting cockle hosts may indicate that these viruses belong to a new viral family.

Several divergent branches within the Bunyavirales are indicative that this order is likely much more diverse than is currently understood. For example, the highly divergent branch most closely related to Beihai bunya-like virus 3 may be indicative of a novel viral order within the Negarnaviricota. Notably, two viruses in the Tosoviridae family were uncovered that were genetically related to Turtle fraser virus 1 (Waltzek et al. 2022) and Chatham eel tosovirus (Grimwood et al. 2023). The two new tosoviruses in this study were highly divergent at less than 27% amino acid identity to Turtle fraser virus 1, demonstrating how little is known about the diversity of this family and its wider taxonomy.

Implications, limitations, and future directions

Prior to this study, nothing was known about RNA virus diversity in New Zealand cockles nor their intertidal habitats. This study has uncovered highly divergent novel viruses, potentially reflecting New Zealand’s isolated geographical and ecological history. These cockles are endemic to New Zealand and have largely evolved in geographical isolation, creating a unique opportunity for highly divergent viruses to adapt locally (Ross et al. 2012). That is, the novel viruses present within these habitats provide a unique representation of viral diversity shaped by a long history of ecological isolation and minimal external disturbance. The viruses naturally present in cockles are likely a crucial part of cockles’ natural ecosystems (French and Holmes 2020). However, because the varying tissue types of each individual were homogenised together prior to RNA extraction, we were unable to assess tissue-specific viral tropism. Future studies analysing tissues separately would provide valuable insight into viral localisation and host-virus interactions. In addition, parallel analysis of seawater viromes would provide an important baseline for distinguishing environmentally derived viruses from those more closely associated with cockles.

Conclusion

This study is the first to characterise the virome of coastal environments through the lens of cockles in New Zealand. A highly diverse RNA virome was uncovered, of which many species were genetically divergent, revealing more of the unexplored virosphere. Environmental factors seemingly impacted the abundance of several viral orders, but prolonged longitudinal sampling over multiple years is required to clarify any such trends. More broadly, this work provides a foundation for detailed research into monitoring coastal aquatic viromes utilising cockles as ecological indicators. This framework is useful for future monitoring efforts to establish rapid mitigation strategies in case of future viral disease outbreaks.
