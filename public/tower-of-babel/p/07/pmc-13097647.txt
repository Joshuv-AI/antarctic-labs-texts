Mitochondrial transplant activates Ca2+/TFAP2A to promote hDPSCs-mediated dentin-pulp regeneration
Authors: Peimeng Zhan, Xinfang Zhang, Zhuo Xie, Lingling Chen, Shuheng Huang, Qiting Huang, Zhengmei Lin, Runfu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe dentin-pulp complex (DPC) is composed of the odontoblastic layer and associated stromal components. It serves key functions in immunological homeostasis and tissue regeneration of dental tissues. Human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs) have emerged as pivotal cells for DPC regeneration. Current research frontiers primarily focus on developing novel strategies to increase the odontogenic differentiation potential and regenerative efficacy of hDPSCs. This study aims to boost the capacity of hDPSCs to regenerate DPC through mitochondrial transplantation.MethodsMitochondria were isolated from donor hDPSCs and transplanted into recipient hDPSCs (Mito-hDPSCs) in the same passage. Subsequently, cell viability and mitochondrial transplantation efficiency were evaluated via CCK-8, β-galactosidase staining, mitochondrial imaging, and flow cytometry. Furthermore, Mito-hDPSCs’ metabolic capacity was assessed by mitochondrial membrane potential assays and cellular oxidative phosphorylation assays. Moreover, Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) activity, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, RT-qPCR, and Western blotting (WB) were performed to assess Mito-hDPSC’s odontogenic differentiation potential. Moreover, a nude mouse model was used to assess how Mito-hDPSCs induce DPC regeneration in vivo. RNA-Seq analysis was conducted to examine the expression of signaling pathways in Mito-hDPSCs. Furthermore, an increased mitochondrial transplant rate was observed at a recipient-to-donor cell ratio of 1:3. TFAP2A was identified as a key transcription factor in the odontogenic differentiation of Mito-hDPSCs. Knockdown array revealed that mitochondrial transplantation effectively upregulated TFAP2A expression in Mito-hDPSCs. Background

The dentin-pulp complex (DPC) is the core unit of pulp tissue engineering and maintains the balance of tooth defense and regeneration by dynamically regulating the odontoblast layer [1, 2]. Root canal therapy is a traditional method for treating pulp necrosis, which involves necrotic pulp tissue removal and root canal filling with gutta-percha [3]. However, the treated tooth lacks the nutritional support of the DPC, posing risks of secondary infection and vertical root fracture [4]. Furthermore, the filling material for the root canal may cause tooth discoloration, affecting aesthetics [5]. Young permanent teeth require complete normal development and apical closure of pulp tissue [6]. Nevertheless, conventional apexification often fails to form apical hard tissue, compromising long-term treatment outcomes [7]. Therefore, more efficient pulp treatment strategies are required.

Stem cell-based DPC regeneration implants exogenous stem cells into the host root canal system to achieve pulp regeneration [8]. Human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs) have significant self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation potentials, making them the key seed cells for regeneration [5, 9]. Limited by the narrow root canal anatomy and suboptimal microenvironment, DPC regeneration mediated by hDPSCs exhibits limited clinical efficacy [1, 7]. Therefore, optimizing the regenerative capacity of hDPSCs within this microenvironment enhances pulp regeneration efficacy.

Mitochondrial transplantation is a novel method of cellular regulation that differs from treatments involving drugs, growth factors, or biomaterials [10]. It can directly modulate cell function without altering the extracellular microenvironment, thus limiting cytotoxicity or biocompatibility-related effects [11, 12]. Furthermore, this method has shown promising efficacy in myocardial ischemia [13], cerebral infarction [14], and renal dysfunction [15]. Moreover, it is regarded as a new anti-inflammatory and tissue repair technique. It has been observed that autologous mitochondrial transplantation can induce immunomodulation as well as regenerative repair of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [11, 16] and adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) [17]. Mitochondrial transplantation using mitochondria isolated from young periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) improves the metabolic function of aging PDLSCs and promotes the repair of bone defects in the periodontium [18]. Based on the aforementioned research results, it was hypothesized that in vitro artificial mitochondrial transplantation may improve hDPSC’s function and enhance the efficacy of hDPSCs-based DPC regeneration. However, it remains unclear how mitochondrial transplantation regulates hDPSCs, including the associated molecular pathways and key gene expression profiles.

Calcium ion (Ca2+) is an important second messenger in cells, and mitochondria can regulate the intracellular Ca2+ balance through Ca2+ channels, participating in processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation [19, 20]. Mitochondrial transplantation alters the number and function of intracellular mitochondria, which may further affect cell function through the regulatory function of Ca2+. Transcription factor activating protein 2α (TFAP2A) plays an important role in craniomaxillofacial and incisor development. Its expression is regulated by various intracellular signals [21-25]. However, the specific function of TFAP2A in the odontoblast differentiation of hDPSCs is still unclear, and further research is needed on the signaling regulatory mechanisms of TFAP2A.

Given the potential application of mitochondrial transplantation in DPC regeneration, this study aims to investigate the effects of autologous mitochondrial transplantation on the odontogenic differentiation of hDPSCs and its regenerative effects of DPC. Additionally, this study seeks to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms involved. Overall, this research will provide a theoretical basis for the clinical application of mitochondrial transplantation in DPC regeneration therapy.

Methods

Cell culture

hDPSCs were isolated and cultured as previously described [26, 27]. Briefly, primary hDPSCs were harvested from the pulp of intact third molars of different patients (aged 18 to 25 years) with impacted dentition.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation potential was determined in 96-well plates using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Dojindo, Japan). The OD values were continuously measured at 24-hour intervals over a period of 7 days.

Mitochondrial extraction and transplantation

The mitochondrial transplantation was performed as previously reported [11, 16, 17, 28]. According to the instructions of the mitochondrial extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), hDPSCs were resuspended and washed with an appropriate amount of Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS), centrifuged at 850 × g for 2 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Then, 800 µL of separation reagent A was added, followed by vortexing at medium speed for 5 s and incubation on ice for 2 min. Next, 10 µL of separation reagent B was added; the sample was vortexed at maximum speed for 5 s, incubated on ice for 30 s, and this vortexing at maximum speed for 30 s and incubation on ice for 30 s cycle was repeated alternately for a total of 5 min. Subsequently, 800 µL of separation reagent C was added, mixed by inversion, and centrifuged at 700 × g for 2 min. The supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, resulting in visible mitochondrial pellets. Then, 500 µL of separation reagent C was added, and the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min; the supernatant was thoroughly discarded. The isolated mitochondria were resuspended in complete culture medium and placed on ice.

Research [11, 16, 17, 28] has indicated that when mitochondria are co-cultured with recipient cells, they are absorbed by endocytosis to enable artificial mitochondrial transplantation. Based on previously described mitochondrial transplantation methods, this study divided hDPSCs from the same batch and passage number into donor and recipient groups. The recipient hDPSCs were seeded into culture flasks, and transferred to the cell culture incubator, where they were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The donor group hDPSCs extracted mitochondria following the above steps. When the recipient hDPSCs had initially attached, the corresponding extracted mitochondria were added, and after incubation at 37 °C in the cell culture incubator for 24 h, the mitochondrial transplantation was completed. hDPSCs after mitochondrial transplantation were referred to as Mito-hDPSCs (Figure S1). The control group of hDPSCs received medium without mitochondria.

Cultivation of TFAP2A overexpressing and knockdown hDPSCs

hDPSCs were infected with lentiviruses, per the kit’s guide (Miaoling Biology, China). For TFAP2A gene overexpression, TFAP2A overexpression lentivirus (TFAP2A-OE) was employed, whereas for its knockdown, TFAP2A knockdown lentivirus (TFAP2A-KD) was used. The overexpression and knockdown lentivirus vector groups are referred to as TFAP2A-OENC and TFAP2A-KDNC, respectively. For cell selection analysis, 4 µg/mL of Puromycin (Beyotime, China) was used.

Flow cytometry

For quantitative verification, lentiviruses (Miaoling Biology, China) were used to label the mitochondria in recipient and donor hDPSCs (mitoGfp and mitoDsRed), which were then isolated, and their mitochondria were quantified via a flow cytometer (FACScan, USA). BD FACSDiva™ software was used for the quantification of at least 10,000 events per sample. Data were analyzed with FlowJo V10.0 (Treestar, USA).

Cell senescence staining

Briefly, after 48 h of culture, cells were stained per the instructions of the β-galactosidase staining kit (Beyotime, China) and observed using a light microscope to determine hDPSCs senescence rate.

Cell metabolism analysis

The mitochondrial membrane potential of hDPSCs was detected using a JC-1 staining kit (Beyotime, China), per the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. CCCP was set as a negative control. Furthermore, hDPSC’s intracellular ATP content and oxygen consumption rate were assessed according to the kit’s recommended procedures (Elabscience, China).

Ca2+ fluorescence probe assay

The Fluo-8 Ca2+ Fluorescence Probe Assay Kit (Applygen, China) was used to detect Ca2+ fluorescence in hDPSCs.

Odontoblastic differentiation

The hDPSCs were induced with an odontoblastic differentiation medium (OM) and culture medium (CM) and classified into three groups. The hDPSCs group cells were cultured with CM for 14 days, the OM + hDPSCs group cells were stimulated with OM for 14 days, and the OM + Mito-hDPSCs group cells received mitochondrial transplantation and OM stimulation for 14 days. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) was performed using the NBT/BCIP staining kit (Beyotime, China). A 2% Alizarin red staining (ARS) Solution (pH 4.2 Beyotime, China) was used to stain mineralized nodules.

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted from each group with the help of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Toyobo Co, Japan). For gene expression levels analysis, qPCR was performed on the Bio-Rad CFX96™ assay system using SYBR PCR Master Mix (Roche, USA). The primer sequences used for amplification are listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB)

Cellular proteins from each group were extracted with RIPA buffer (Millipore, USA), subjected to SDS-PAGE, transferred onto the PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), and then treated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-human DMP1 (1:1000, Bioss, China), RUNX2 (1:1000, Bioss, China), TFAP2A (1:2000, Protetintech, China), OPN (1:1000, Protetintech, China), OCN (1:1000, Protetintech, China), CALM (1:1000, Protetintech, China), and Vinculin (1:2000, Protetintech, China) antibodies as well as mouse anti-human COX IV (1:1000, Abbkine, China) DSPP (1:1000, Santa Cruz, USA) and β-Actin (1:1000, Protetintech, China) antibodies. The next day, the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The chemiluminescence reagents (Millipore, USA) were employed to visualize the target protein bands, and images were captured using a gel imaging analyzer (Bio-Rad, USA).

In vivo transplantation of the HA/TCP scaffolds and treated dentin matrix (TDM)

hDPSCs were divided into four groups: Control, Mito, hDPSCs, and hDPSCs + Mito. Briefly, hDPSCs (106/10 µL) were resuspended and inoculated into the HA/TCP scaffolds (porosity: 75–80%; pore size: 200–500 μm; Baiameng Biological Inc, China). Male BALB/c nude mice (n = 5, age = 6 weeks, 4 HA/TCP per mouse) were used as recipients for transplantation.

The non-carious tooth’s coronal part was removed, and the root canal was prepared to #40 with K-file and cut into about 8 mm diameter. Then, the cells were treated for 10 min with 3% EDTA, sonicated with 2.5% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, rinsed thrice with PBS containing 1% cyane-streptomycin, and cultured in CM for 3 days. hDPSCs were then divided into the hDPSCs and hDPSCs + Mito groups. Subsequently, hDPSCs (106/10 µL) were resuspended in CM, mixed with Matrigel (Corning, USA; 356324) in a 1:1 ratio, and inoculated into TDMs.

This study follows the The ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. BALB/c nude mice (n = 5, with 2 TDMs per mouse) were intraperitoneally injected with 1% pentobarbital for anesthesia and implanted with a TDM on the back. The animals were housed in a controlled photoperiod barrier environment with sterile food and water. After 8 weeks, marking the experiment’s conclusion, BALB/c nude mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) following the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). The HA/TCPs and TDMs were extracted, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with 10% EDTA, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices for subsequent analyses.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining

For H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining, 5 μm sections of the dental pulp tissues and HA/TCP scaffolds were prepared and stained with the H&E and Masson Tricolor Staining Kits (Servicebio, China).

Immunofluorescence staining

The sections were treated with EDTA 9.0 (Servicebio, China) for antigen retrieval, and were incubated overnight with the following primary mouse anti-human DSPP (1:200, Santa Cruz, USA) and rabbit anti-human DMP1 (1:200, Bioss, China) antibodies at 4 °C. Then, the sections were probed with secondary antibodies, including Alexa Fluo-488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (green) (1:500, EMAR, China), Alexa Fluor-594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (red) (1:500, EMAR, China).

RNA-seq

RNA was extracted from the hDPSCs, OM + hDPSCs, and OM + Mito-hDPSCs groups and sent to Beijing Berry and Health Biotechnology Co., LTD for Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical assessments and graphing were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad 9.0). Continuous data with normal distribution are expressed as “mean ± SD.” An independent sample t-test was carried out to compare two groups, while one-way ANOVA was employed for multi-group comparisons. Post hoc comparisons were performed via Tukey’s method, whereas Dunnett’s T3 method was used for pairwise comparisons for unequal variances. The two-sided α = 0.05 and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated at least 3 times.

Results

The recipient-donor hDPSCs ratio of 1:3 achieves higher mitochondrial transplantation efficiency

The hDPSCs were acquired from different donors to ensure the universality of use in mitochondrial transplantation. First, DsRed protein and GFP were stably expressed on hDPSC’s mitochondrial membrane using lentivirus. Then, co-culture was employed for mitochondrial transplantation to construct post-transplant Mito-hDPSCs (Fig. 1A). The hDPSCs were divided into 6 groups, including a control group without mitochondrial transplantation and five other groups based on recipient-donor cell ratios ranging from 1:1 to 1:5. The CCK8 assay revealed that mitochondrial transplantation did not inhibit Mito-hDPSC’s activity and promoted Mito-hDPSCs proliferation at a recipient-donor cell ratio of 1:3 (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, β-Galactosidase staining revealed that at the 1:3 ratio, Mito-hDPSCs lacked the aging phenotype (Fig. 1C). The mitochondrial uptake and maintenance in hDPSCs at different recipient-donor ratios were further assessed via cell fluorescence and flow cytometry. It was observed that mitochondrial transplantation efficiency was highest at a 1:3 ratio (Fig. 1D), and the transplanted mitochondria were stably present in Mito-hDPSCs over the long term and fused with the host cells’ intrinsic mitochondria (Fig. 1E). Moreover, at this ratio, electron microscopy showed a significant increase in mitochondria within Mito-hDPSCs (Figure S2A), while Western blot analysis revealed that the relative expression of the mitochondrial marker COX IV was significantly elevated (Figure S2B). Therefore, for subsequent analyses, a recipient-donor cell ratio of 1:3 was selected as the optimal condition for mitochondrial transplantation.

Mitochondrial transplantation improves the ability of mito-hDPSCs to regenerate DPC

Mitochondrial transplantation regulates stem cell metabolism and differentiation. JC-1 assays indicated that Mito-hDPSC’s mitochondrial membrane potential significantly increased after mitochondrial transplantation compared to the control group (Figs. 2A, B). Furthermore, ATP production and oxidative phosphorylation capacity were also markedly increased (Fig. 2C). The differentiation of hDPSCs into odontoblasts is crucial for the formation of DPC [29]. Here, it was observed that when the hDPSCs were subjected to post-mitochondrial transplantation differentiation (OM + Mito-hDPSCs group), the alkaline phosphatase and alizarin red staining was more intense than the control hDPSCs and differentiation-induced hDPSCs (OM + hDPSCs) groups, indicating an increase in mineralized nodules (Fig. 2D). Moreover, the OM + Mito-hDPSCs group also had a significantly pronounced expression of biomineralization and odontogenic differentiation-related genes (Fig. 2E). Similarly, the expression of odontoblast-specific proteins, DSPP and DMP1, was also markedly increased in the OM + Mito-hDPSCs group (Fig. 2F). These data revealed that mitochondrial transplantation substantially increases the metabolic level and odontoblast differentiation ability of hDPSCs.

Based on the literature [30, 31], stem cells were stimulated with HA/TCP and implanted subcutaneously into nude mice to simulate ectopic DPC regeneration (Fig. S3). The H&E and Masson staining showed that the Mito-hDPSCs group produced more mineralized and collagen-like tissues than the pure material (Control), mitochondria-only (Mito), and hDPSCs-only (hDPSCs) groups (Fig. S4A, B). Immunofluorescence detection revealed increased DSPP and DMP1 expression around mineralized and collagen-like tissues (Fig. S4C), indicating that the Mito-hDPSCs group formed a rich DPC-like structure in HA/TCP and secreted a dentin-pulp-like structure containing mineralized and collagen-like tissues.

Furthermore, to validate Mito-hDPSC’s regenerative effect, TDMs mixed with cells were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice to simulate an in vivo DPC regeneration microenvironment (Fig. S5). The results of H&E and Masson staining revealed the presence of tissues within TDMs in the Mito-hDPSCs group, which were more abundant and morphologically closer to the DPC within human teeth (Fig. 3A). Immunofluorescence analysis also indicated that the Mito-hDPSCs group formed a double-positive DSPP and DMP1 odontoblast-like cell layer on the side closer to the dentin, with more orderly tissue layering relative to the hDPSCs group (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that Mito-hDPSCs can effectively regenerate functional and structurally distinct DPC-like tissues in vivo.

Mitochondrial transplantation enhances odontoblast differentiation by activating TFAP2A expression in Mito-hDPSCs

The odontoblast cell layer formation is a classic marker of hDPSCs regenerating into DPC; however, the underlying regulatory mechanism of mitochondrial transplant remains undetermined. To elucidate how mitochondrial transplant promotes hDPSCs differentiation into odontoblasts, gene expression patterns of mitochondrial transplant in the hDPSCs, OM + hDPSCs, and OM + Mito-hDPSCs groups were assessed using RNA-Seq (Fig. S6A). Many differential genes were observed between the groups (Fig. S6B). KEGG and GSEA analyses revealed that the OM + Mito-hDPSCs group was more enriched in Ca2+ (Fig. S6C) and odontogenesis (Fig. S6D) related signaling pathways and biological processes than the hDPSCs and OM + hDPSCs groups.

The enrichment analysis of the most significantly upregulated genes in each group revealed 57 significantly increased genes in the OM + Mito-hDPSCs group, which were selected for in-depth analysis (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, 7 tissue mineralization-related genes were identified (Fig. 4B), of which SOST, TFAP2A, and COL15A1 had the highest expression and were chosen for RT-qPCR validation. The results revealed that mitochondrial transplantation significantly activated TFAP2A (Fig. 4C), which is an important transcription factor for craniofacial development and incisor enamel formation [24, 25]. However, the role of TFAP2A in odontoblast differentiation is not well understood. TFAP2A overexpression in hDPSCs and induction of their differentiation into odontoblasts yielded higher formation of mineralized matrix than the control (TFAP2A-OENC) group, with significantly increased expression of mineralization-related proteins, particularly DSPP and DMP1 (Figs. 4D–E). TFAP2A knockdown significantly reduced mineralized matrix formation and odontoblast-related protein levels compared to the control (TFAP2A-KDNC) group. However, mitochondrial transplantation (Mito + TFAP2A-KD group) partially reversed the TFAP2A knockdown-induced reduced odontoblast differentiation (Fig. 4F–G). These data suggest that TFAP2A is a key transcription factor in regulating hDPSCs differentiation into odontoblasts, and mitochondrial transplantation activates TFAP2A in hDPSCs.

Mitochondrial transplantation activates TFAP2A expression by increasing Ca2+ concentration in mito-hDPSCs

Cytosolic Ca2+ has been observed to regulate signal transduction. Furthermore, some transcription factors are specifically sensitive to Ca2+ signaling, such as cytosolic Ca2+ signaling, which modulates the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of NF-κB [32]. Therefore, Ca2+ homeostasis in the cytosol is crucial for normal cell physiology and metabolism. The cytosolic calcium levels are primarily modulated by the two major calcium stores: the mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum [33]. It was inferred that transplanted mitochondria may regulate Mito-hDPSCs via Ca2+; therefore, this study investigated the biological role of Ca2+ in mitochondrial transplantation.

Bioinformatics analysis indicated that cytosolic Ca2+-related biological processes are significantly increased in Mito-hDPSCs (Figure S6C). The analysis of Ca2+ using fluorescent probes revealed that TFAP2A overexpression and knockdown do not affect the Ca2+ concentration in hDPSCs (Fig. 5A, B), indicating a regulatory relationship between Ca2+ and TFAP2A. Furthermore, after mitochondrial transplantation, Ca2+ concentration in Mito-hDPSCs was increased whether cultured in a culture medium (hDPSCs and Mito-hDPSCs groups), calcium-free medium (-Ca2+ hDPSCs and -Ca2+ Mito-hDPSCs groups), or mitochondrial transplantation (Mito-hDPSCs and -Ca2+ Mito-hDPSCs groups) (Fig. 5C, D). The stimulation of hDPSCs with Ca2+ concentration gradients revealed that a 4 mM Ca2+ concentration effectively increased TFAP2A expression in cells (Fig. 5E). After reducing the intracellular Ca2+ concentration using the Ca2+ chelator BAPTA-AM, the expression of Calmodulin (CALM) and TFAP2A in hDPSCs decreased (Fig. 5F). These data suggest that mitochondrial transplantation may modulate TFAP2A via Ca2+, promoting the odontogenic differentiation of hDPSCs.

Discussion

It is difficult to generate DPC via traditional pulp treatment methods, and DPC regeneration treatments based on tissue engineering technology are ideal [5]. However, highly active and differentiable hDPSCs are required for regenerative therapy because the limited number of cells in the root canal and the narrow apical foramen constrain the nutrient supply [34, 35]. The current methods to modulate hDPSC activity for regenerating DPC include genetic engineering technology, growth factors, small molecule drugs, exosomes, or vesicles.

However, all these methods have certain limitations. The biosafety of genetic engineering technology remains elusive; for instance, the viral vectors employed may promote harmful toxic effects and undesirable phenotypes [11]. Gene editing technologies represented by CRISPR-Cas9 and RNA interference have off-target effects and potential genetic toxicity risks during clinical or experimental application [36, 37]. Bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) is a recombinant protein approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for clinical therapeutics, but reports indicate that it may cause ectopic bone formation and even induce oncogenesis [38]. Although vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a well-known growth factor that regulates angiogenesis of stem cells, high doses may also lead to the formation of cavernous hemangiomas [39]. Although exosome or vesicle therapy has demonstrated good safety in preclinical and clinical research, its potential immunogenicity has not been fully studied [40]. Extracellular vesicles have many antigen molecules on their surface, which pose potential immune risks during allogeneic applications [40]. Therefore, the development of biological regulatory methods with low cytotoxicity and high biocompatibility is crucial. Mitochondria have low immunogenicity and strong biological regulatory activity, indicating potential as a safer biological regulatory approach [41, 42]. The quantification methods of donor mitochondria in mitochondrial transplantation are still controversial. One method uses the recipient-donor cells ratio [11, 16], while another quantifies the extracted mitochondria [17]. The mitochondrial transplantation conditions for different cell types warrant further research. This study showed that when the 1:3 ratio of recipient cells to donor cells, the mitochondrial transplantation efficiency of hDPSCs was the highest. The mitochondria stably existed in recipient cells for a long time and had no adverse effect on the recipient cells.

The literature suggests that mitochondrial transplantation can improve brain function [43], alleviate ischemic necrosis of the kidneys [44], and heart [13], etc. Furthermore, mitochondria can circulate in the blood of mice and humans, regulating organ function over a distance [45, 46]. Moreover, mitochondrial transplantation can markedly increase stem cell function, specifically when mitochondria are isolated from the same cell types. Mitochondrial transplantation substantially increases ADSC’s trilineage differentiation capacity to create a super stem cell called mito-ADSCs [17]. Further, mitochondrial transplantation significantly elevates the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs in vitro and improves the efficacy of the BMSCs-mito-EC complex in promoting angiogenesis [11, 16]. Studies have indicated that in the dental pulp, hDPSCs transplant mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum to another hDPSC via tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) structures to remodel mitochondrial function [47]. This indicates the presence of a natural intercellular mitochondrial interaction in teeth, which lays the basis for autologous mitochondrial hDPSC transplants. hDPSCs have higher oxidative phosphorylation and antioxidative damage capabilities than other stem cells, making them excellent mitochondrial donors [48]. Thus, it was hypothesized that using hDPSCs as donors for mitochondrial transplantation could effectively increase the vitality and regenerative capacity of recipient hDPSCs. Therefore, this study developed Mito-hDPSCs, mitochondrial transplantation cells derived from hDPSCs, which indicated significant metabolic capabilities and differentiation potential into odontoblast-like cells. Furthermore, they also form structurally rich and well-organized DPC-like tissue in HA/TCP and TDMs.

Overall, compared to conventional stem cells or cells modified by traditional methods such as genetic engineering, growth factors, and vesicles, Mito-hDPSCs and Mito-MSCs constructed through autologous mitochondrial transplantation exhibit several advantages. In terms of biosafety, because mitochondria lack major histocompatibility complex molecules and other cell surface antigens, they show extremely low immunogenicity in both allogeneic and autologous transplantation, significantly reducing the risk of immune rejection [42, 49]. This indicates that mitochondrial transplantation has great potential as a safe and efficient biological regulatory strategy. Regarding application methods, unlike traditional growth factor activation or genetic modification, mitochondrial transplantation opens a new path for organelle therapy. By co-culture, local injection, or targeted delivery, recipient cells can directly internalize donor mitochondria, functionally replacing damaged mitochondria or directly supplementing cellular energy reserves [11, 16, 17, 50]. This direct intervention in cellular metabolic processes demonstrates higher precision and effectiveness in promoting tissue regeneration. Concerning operational methods, the “MitoPunch” technique using photothermal nanoknives enables high-throughput and rapid mitochondrial transplantation [51]. Microfluidic devices combined with the Sendai virus can achieve quantitative control of mitochondrial transplantation [52]. Cationic surface-modified (cysteine-alanine-glutamine-lysine) sequences can target transplantation to macrophages at the site of spinal cord injury [53]. Additionally, mixing mitochondria with cell suspension followed by centrifugation can also effectively complete mitochondrial transplantation [54]. These diverse technical capabilities not only demonstrate the high feasibility of mitochondrial transplantation operations but also provide extensive technical support for its clinical applications [11].

TFAP2A is an important transcription factor in craniofacial development and is expressed in the ectoderm, cranial neural crest cells, facial mesenchyme, and nasal and oral epithelial cells of mice [23]. Moreover, it is also found in the central and peripheral nervous systems between E9 and E13.5 [25] as well as in the epithelium and mesenchyme of human and mice incisor and molar teeth [23, 24]. However, how TFAP2A modulates DPSC differentiation into odontoblasts is not yet clear. Here, RNA-Seq analysis showed that TFAP2A was specifically and significantly upregulated during Mito-hDPSC differentiation into odontoblast-like cells. Furthermore, TFAP2A overexpression was observed to promote the differentiation of hDPSCs into odontoblast-like cells. Whereas mitochondrial transplantation partially restored the TFAP2A knockdown-induced inhibition of hDPSC odontoblast-like differentiation, indicating that mitochondrial transplantation can stimulate TFAP2A in hDPSCs.

Ca²⁺ is a second messenger that modulates transcription factors by mediating transcription, translation, or epigenetic modifications, thus affecting gene expression and cell function [55]. This regulation depends on calcium-binding proteins (such as CALM) and downstream kinases (such as CaMK and PKC) [55]. The literature suggests that Ca2+ can enter the nucleus through the nuclear membrane transporters or transport across the endoplasmic reticulum and nuclear membrane and regulate the activation of transcription factors [56]. Mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum are important organelles for regulating cellular Ca2+ concentrations in most cells. Mitochondria modulate cellular functions by transporting Ca2+ through the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU), sodium/calcium exchanger (NCLX), and mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MERCS) [57]. This suggests that during mitochondrial transplantation into recipient cells, the increase in mitochondrial quantity and activity may alter Ca2+ concentration, thus regulating the cell. The RNA-Seq results of this study validated that during Mito-hDPSC differentiation, the biological processes regulated by cytoplasmic Ca2+ are significantly increased. Furthermore, mitochondrial transplantation can effectively increase Ca2+ concentration in Mito-hDPSCs independently of their levels in the culture medium. These data suggest that mitochondrial transplantation might regulate the recipient cell’s function by introducing endogenous Ca2+. However, whether the increase in Ca2+ within hDPSCs is due to the release ofCa2+ from the mitochondria or the triggering of Ca2+ release from the endoplasmic reticulum after the mitochondria bind with it, remains unclear and requires further research.

Moreover, it was observed that a moderate increase in Ca2+ concentration can promote TFAP2A expression. This suggests that mitochondrial transplantation increases Ca2+ levels in Mito-hDPSCs; therefore, it increases TFAP2A expression and promotes their differentiation into odontoblast-like cells. In this regard, this study proposes a potential molecular mechanism. In brief, mitochondrial transplantation enhances the mitochondrial function of Mito-hDPSCs and increases their Ca²⁺ release and ATP synthesis capacity. Subsequently, it activates intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling, enhancing downstream calcium-dependent signaling transduction cascades through CALM and other calmodulins. This activation then induces and maintains a high level of expression of TFAP2A at the transcriptional level. Furthermore, TFAP2A activates downstream odontoblast-related genes such as DSPP and DMP1 through transcriptional regulation; this promotes the differentiation of Mito-hDPSCs into odontoblasts and facilitates the formation of DPC. Additionally, given that TFAP2A may be involved in the regulation of mitochondrial fusion and autophagy [58-60], this study speculates that TFAP2A is not only a downstream effector of mitochondrial transplantation but may also act as a positive regulatory factor to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis. This forms a positive feedback loop to meet the high energy consumption and calcium signaling requirements during the differentiation process. However, the precise interactive relationship between mitochondrial transplantation, Ca²⁺ signaling, and TFAP2A remains to be further elucidated.

Overall, this study provides experimental evidence for the clinical application of mitochondrial transplantation for dental pulp regeneration and furnished new avenues for dental DPC regeneration therapy. However, in vivo experiments in large animals, such as Beagle dogs, are required, and future research should comprehensively evaluate the specific regulatory processes of the mitochondria-mediated Ca²⁺/TFAP2A signaling axis. Moreover, how mitochondrial activity can be maintained in vitro and their absorbance mechanism in hDPSCs require further research.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that mitochondrial transplantation is a feasible technique to enhance the differentiation capacity of hDPSCs in dental pulp regeneration therapy. Mitochondrial transplantation can increase Ca2+ levels in hDPSCs, thereby enhancing the expression of TFAP2A, a key odontogenic transcription factor, which subsequently promotes hDPSCs differentiation into odontoblasts (Graphical Abstract). Furthermore, in vivo experiments demonstrated that mitochondrial transplantation can enhance hDPSCs-mediated DPC regeneration. These findings may provide a new strategy to improve the dental pulp regeneration function of hDPSCs for transplantation and tissue engineering applications in clinical settings.
