The effectiveness of nursing interventions for preventing postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing major orthopedic surgery: a systematic review
Authors: Sunee Kraonual, Wichayaporn Thongpeth, Thaworn Thongpet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative delirium (POD) frequently occurs in elderly patients after major orthopedic surgery. Nurses play a key role in delivering non-pharmacological preventive interventions, but the evidence supporting their effectiveness varies. This systematic review aims to summarize current research on nursing interventions designed to reduce POD in older adults undergoing these surgeries.MethodsWe followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines to conduct a systematic search in five databases (CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PubMed, and Google Scholar (as a supplementary source)) for studies published from 2010 to 2025. Eligible studies evaluated nursing-led interventions targeting delirium prevention in patients aged 65 years or older after major orthopedic surgery. Two reviewers independently screened using Rayyan [10] 529 records, reviewed full texts, and extracted data on study design, participants, interventions, and outcomes. Due to heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis approach was employed to analyze the data. Quality assessment used the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for randomized controlled trials and ROBINS-I [12] for non-randomized studies.ResultsEight studies met inclusion criteria, including 3,018 participants. The designs comprised two randomized controlled trials, three quasi-experimental or cohort studies, two retrospective analyses, and one before-and-after study. Nurse-led multicomponent protocols, system-level interventions such as standardized orders, and single-component approaches like sensory aid management all showed statistically significant reductions in POD incidence. Absolute risk reductions ranged from 4.0% to 24.5%. Larger trials reported modest but statistically significant effects (for example, an odds ratio of 0.59 in an orthopedic subgroup), while smaller studies observed larger decreases. Variations in intervention types and outcome measures prevented meta-analysis.ConclusionsNursing interventions, especially multicomponent protocols and systemic changes, may reduce postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing major orthopedic surgery. Even targeted single-component interventions suggest a potential to lower delirium rates meaningfully. Standardizing outcome measures in future studies would help enable outcome measures to enable meta-analyses and explore how best to implement these interventions in clinical settings.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD420251140913. Background

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a common and serious complication in older patients undergoing major surgery, especially in orthopedic cases. It presents with sudden confusion, difficulty focusing, and fluctuating levels of consciousness. The incidence ranges from 15% to 65% in elderly surgical populations and is particularly high after hip fracture repairs and major joint replacements [1, 2]. POD carries a heavy clinical and economic toll, including increased mortality, longer hospital stays, greater use of healthcare resources, faster cognitive decline, loss of independence, and higher chances of institutional care [3, 4].

Several factors make elderly orthopedic patients especially prone to delirium. These include advanced age, preexisting cognitive impairment, the trauma of surgery, physiological effects of anesthesia, use of pain medications, immobility, sedation, and sensory impairments during the perioperative period [2, 5]. Hip fracture patients are at even greater risk due to the acute nature of their injury, emergency surgery, and multiple coexisting medical conditions [4, 6].

While previous systematic reviews have examined delirium prevention strategies broadly across medical and surgical populations, few have focused specifically on the unique context of older adults undergoing major orthopedic surgery. Orthopedic patients face distinct challenges, including severe acute pain, mobility restrictions, and specific surgical stress responses, which may alter the effectiveness of standard delirium prevention protocols. Furthermore, existing reviews often group pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches together, making it difficult to isolate the specific impact of nursing-led care. Therefore, this review is necessary to synthesize the current evidence specifically on non-pharmacological nursing interventions in this high-risk population, addressing a critical gap in the literature and providing targeted guidance for perioperative nursing practice.

Despite growing awareness of POD as a preventable complication, evidence-based prevention strategies have yet to be widely adopted in clinical practice. Nursing care is critical in preventing delirium because nurses have continuous patient contact, monitor changes closely, and can implement non-pharmacological, multi-component interventions targeting modifiable risk factors [1, 3]. However, the effects of individual nursing interventions, which components are most effective, and the best methods for implementation remain unclear and require systematic evaluation to inform practice and policy.

Theoretical framework

The nursing interventions aimed at preventing POD are grounded in a multifactorial model of delirium development. This model suggests delirium results from the interaction between predisposing vulnerabilities—such as age, cognitive deficits, sensory impairments, and comorbidities—and precipitating factors like surgery, anesthesia, medications, immobility, and the hospital environment [2]. Nursing care targets the modifiable precipitating factors through thorough patient assessment, environmental modifications, enhancing sensory input, normalizing sleep-wake cycles with early mobilization, managing pain, and supporting cognitive function.

The Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) demonstrated that multi-component interventions addressing cognitive impairment, sleep disruption, immobility, vision and hearing deficits, and dehydration reduce delirium incidence by 30–40% in medical patients. Adapting these principles to surgical and orthopedic populations requires adjustments to consider postoperative pain management, concerns about surgical wounds, and accelerated recovery protocols [1, 3].

The nursing empowerment framework further explains how system-level changes—such as standardized screening tools, preprinted orders, and evidence-based protocols—enable nurses to identify at-risk patients and apply prevention strategies more consistently. Clear guidelines, decision support, and institutional support enhance nurses’ ability to intervene effectively and promptly [2, 3].

Research question and objectives

This systematic review seeks to answer the following question, framed using the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) format: In older adults (≥ 65 years) undergoing major orthopedic surgery (Population), how effective are non-pharmacological nursing interventions (Intervention) compared to standard care (Comparison) in preventing or reducing the incidence of postoperative delirium (Outcome)?

Methods

Protocol registration

This systematic review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251140913) on 05 September 2025. The review was conducted according to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [9] [see Additional file 1]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee at Sirindhorn College of Public Health, Yala Province (SCPHYLIRB-2568/645).

Eligibility criteria

We included peer-reviewed studies published in English between January 2010 and December 2025. This specific timeframe was selected because contemporary, evidence-based nursing practices for perioperative care, such as the widespread adoption of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols and updated, standardized delirium screening guidelines, became firmly established and integrated into routine clinical practice during this period. Eligible studies evaluated nursing interventions aimed at preventing postoperative delirium (POD) in adults aged 65 years or older undergoing major orthopedic surgery.

Information sources and search strategy

Between December 1 and December 15, 2025, two independent reviewers (WT and SK) performed a systematic search of four primary databases: CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and PubMed. Additionally, Google Scholar was utilized as a supplementary source for manual searching to identify grey literature and hard-to-find articles. Furthermore, a manual citation search (backward and forward snowballing) of the reference lists of included studies and relevant previous reviews was conducted to ensure literature saturation.

Study selection and data extraction

All returned records were imported into EndNote for removal of duplicates. Two reviewers (WT and SK) independently screened titles and abstracts using Rayyan software [10], then assessed full texts for eligibility. Conflicts were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Data extraction was conducted independently by the same reviewers using a standardized form that captured study characteristics, participant details, intervention components, and delirium outcomes. Further details on the extraction process and variables are included in Additional file 2.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed study quality. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 (RoB 2) [11] was used for RCTs, while the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) [12] tool was applied to non-randomized studies. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion. A summary of the risk of bias assessments is provided in the supplementary materials. Detailed quality assessment results are provided in Table 1.

Certainty of evidence assessment

The overall certainty of evidence for the primary outcome (incidence of postoperative delirium) was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. Evidence was assessed across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Because a narrative synthesis was conducted rather than a meta-analysis, the GRADE assessment was adapted for narrative findings following published guidance for applying GRADE in the absence of pooled estimates. The certainty of evidence was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low for each intervention category (multicomponent protocols, system-level changes, and single-component interventions).

Data synthesis

Because of considerable variation in the types of interventions, outcome measures, and study designs, conducting a meta-analysis was not possible. Instead, we performed a narrative synthesis. Studies were grouped by intervention type—multicomponent protocols, single-component interventions, and system-level changes—and analyzed for the direction and size of effects. Results from studies assessed as higher quality were given more weight in formulating conclusions.

Results

Study selection

The database search initially identified 529 records. After removing duplicates, 106 unique records remained for title and abstract screening. This screening excluded 89 records that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Seventeen full-text articles were then assessed for eligibility. Of these, nine were excluded: three were duplicate reports of the same study cohorts, and six were either conference abstracts or lacked sufficient quantitative data for analysis. This process left eight studies for inclusion in the qualitative synthesis. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The eight included studies were published between 2017 and 2025, reflecting a recent interest in non-pharmacological nursing approaches for preventing postoperative delirium (POD). The combined sample size was 3,018 participants, with individual study sizes ranging from 82 to 1,470. Six studies focused solely on patients undergoing hip fracture surgery. The remaining two included mixed surgical populations, mainly elective orthopedic and other types of surgery. An overview of sample sizes, publication trends, and population distribution across these studies is presented in Fig. 2.

These combined several evidence-based non-pharmacological strategies, such as structured delirium screening, pain control, sleep promotion, early mobilization, and cognitive stimulation. For example, Unal et al. implemented a protocol addressing pain, sleep, nutrition, and function. Qu et al. applied a “refined personalized nursing” approach. Yan et al. [7] and Wang et al. This allowed nurses to standardize medication management, catheter removal, and bowel care. Effectiveness of interventions on postoperative delirium

All eight studies reported statistically significant reductions in POD incidence or prevalence following nursing interventions.

Incidence Reduction. The absolute risk reduction ranged from 4.0% to 24.5%, depending on intervention type and study design (Fig. 3). The largest reduction occurred in Busse et al.’s before-and-after study, where strict perioperative sensory aid management lowered POD incidence from 65.0% to 40.5% (p = 0.026). In Unal et al.’s RCT, the multicomponent protocol entirely prevented delirium in the intervention group (0% versus 15%, p = 0.026).

Larger trials showed notable but sometimes smaller effects. Deeken et al.’s stepped-wedge cluster RCT (n = 1470) found an overall odds ratio (OR) of 0.87 (p = 0.02) for delirium reduction, with a stronger effect in the orthopedic/abdominal surgery subgroup (OR 0.59, p = 0.047). Freter et al.’s pragmatic trial reduced delirium prevalence from 51% to 33% (p = 0.001), with notable benefits for patients with preexisting dementia.

Secondary Outcomes. Several studies reported improvements beyond delirium incidence. Qu et al. observed shorter durations of delirium episodes. Improvements in sleep quality appeared in studies by Unal et al. and Qu et al. Wang et al. noted reduced hospital length of stay associated with their intervention.

Summary of included studies and key findings

Quality assessment

Methodological quality varied across studies. The RCTs by Deeken et al. and Unal et al. provided the strongest evidence, featuring careful randomization and, for Deeken, a large multicenter design. Blinding was generally not feasible due to the nature of nursing interventions. Retrospective and quasi-experimental studies demonstrated measurable effects but carried a risk of selection bias and confounding. Some mitigation occurred—for example, Qu et al. used propensity score matching. Overall, the evidence supports the benefit of nursing interventions, although the presence of non-randomized designs moderates the overall strength.

Certainty of evidence

A GRADE Summary of Findings is presented in Table 3. The overall certainty of evidence for the reduction of postoperative delirium incidence was rated as Low for multicomponent nurse-led protocols and Very Low for both system-level changes and single-component targeted interventions. The primary reasons for downgrading included serious risk of bias due to the predominance of non-randomized and retrospective study designs, serious imprecision arising from small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals in several studies, and potential inconsistency from the clinical heterogeneity of the interventions and outcome measurement tools. Despite the low certainty ratings, the direction of the effect was consistent across all eight included studies, indicating a clear signal of potential benefit from nursing-led delirium prevention strategies.

Discussion

This review combined results from eight studies that examined nursing interventions aimed at preventing postoperative delirium (POD) in older adults following major orthopedic surgery. The evidence shows that targeted non-drug nursing strategies consistently lower both the occurrence and length of POD in this group.

We found three main types of nursing interventions: multicomponent protocols, system-level changes, and single-component targeted approaches. Multicomponent protocols, which combine several proven methods such as cognitive stimulation, sleep support, and early mobilization, were the most common. This approach fits the complex causes of delirium, indicating that tackling multiple risk factors at once leads to better outcomes. For example, Unal et al. reported complete delirium prevention with their nurse-led protocol (0% vs. 15%), demonstrating the potential effectiveness of well-executed well-executed interventions.

System-level supports also played an important role. Studies that incorporated tools like preprinted postoperative orders (PPOs) or standardized screening improved not only delirium rates but also nurses’ knowledge and adherence to guidelines. Freter et al. and Choi et al. showed that giving nurses structured, user-friendly tools helps maintain consistent delirium care, which is especially valuable in busy surgical environments.

The review also noted that focused, single-component interventions can be highly effective. Busse et al. achieved a notable reduction in POD by managing sensory aids during the perioperative period—an inexpensive, straightforward intervention addressing a key but sometimes neglected delirium risk factor in older adults. While multicomponent protocols are preferred, settings with limited resources can still make meaningful progress by targeting specific risk factors.

Variation in effectiveness across studies likely reflects differences in design, patient groups, and intervention details. Nevertheless, all eight studies, which ranged from large stepped-wedge cluster RCTs to smaller before-and-after studies, pointed in the same direction. This consistency supports incorporating these nursing interventions into routine care for older orthopedic patients.

Limitations

The GRADE assessment revealed that the overall certainty of evidence ranges from low (multicomponent protocols) to very low (system-level and single-component interventions), primarily due to the inclusion of non-randomized study designs and clinical heterogeneity. The potential for reporting bias was only partially considered due to the limited number of studies (n = 8), which precluded the use of formal statistical tests such as funnel plots or Egger’s test. These factors should be taken into account when interpreting the strength of the synthesized evidence.

A primary limitation of this review is the small number of included studies (n = 8), which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the predominance of non-randomized designs among the included studies introduces inherent methodological constraints.

Several limitations affect how these findings should be interpreted. First, study quality varied. Although some well-designed RCTs were included, retrospective and quasi-experimental designs also contributed, carrying risks of selection bias and confounding. Second, blinding was generally not possible due to the nature of the interventions, introducing potential performance bias. Third, the delirium assessment tools differed widely (e.g., CAM, CAM-ICU, 3-D CAM, Delirium Index), as did the timing of assessments, making direct comparisons of incidence rates difficult. Finally, an initial screening error inflated the number of included studies at first, reflecting the challenges of deduplication and data extraction in systematic reviews. This issue has since been fully corrected.

Conclusions

Non-pharmacological nursing interventions—especially multicomponent protocols and system-level supports—appear to reduce postoperative delirium in older adults undergoing major orthopedic surgery. By targeting modifiable risks like sensory deficits, sleep problems, and immobility, nurses contribute meaningfully to better cognitive outcomes and recovery. Health systems should prioritize standardized, nurse-led delirium prevention protocols and supply the necessary education and system tools to enable consistent application. Large, multicenter RCTs using uniform delirium assessments are needed to, multicenter RCTs using uniform delirium assessments to clarify the best mix of intervention elements and to assess their long-term effects on patients and healthcare costs.
