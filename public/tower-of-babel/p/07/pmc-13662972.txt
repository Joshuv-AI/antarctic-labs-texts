Knowledge of oral lesions, associated risk factors and health care seeking behaviors among HIV enrollees in Osun-State
Authors: Mufutau Bello, Adeniran Elizabeth, Olarewaju Sunday, Oyeneyin Adenike, Agboola Oluwaferanmi, Oluyide Peter, Olajuwon Towoju, Alebiosu Christopher, Adepoju Gbenga, Oyelola Adeoye, Adedire Adejare, Faniyi Akinwale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOral lesions are frequent complications of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, particularly among individuals with advanced immunosuppression. These lesions reduce quality of life and increase vulnerability to secondary infections. This study assessed knowledge, risk factors, and healthcare-seeking behavior related to oral lesions among HIV enrollees in Osun State, Nigeria.MethodologyA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 180 HIV-positive adults with clinically confirmed oral lesions attending selected Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) clinics in Osun State, Nigeria. Participants were recruited from three selected ART clinics using complete enumeration. Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and analysed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics and appropriate bivariate tests were used, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsParticipants were mostly male (51.7%) and Yoruba (74.4%). Only 56.1% had good knowledge of oral lesions. Common risk factors included poor oral hygiene (56.1%), alcohol use (31.7%), and ART non-adherence. Healthcare-seeking within two weeks of symptoms was low (26.7%). Religion, education, and occupation were significantly associated with care-seeking. Poor oral hygiene and low CD4 counts were significant predictors of oral lesions.ConclusionKnowledge and prompt care-seeking for oral lesions remain inadequate among HIV enrollees. Integrating oral health education, routine screening, and dental care into HIV treatment services is essential to improve outcomes in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28621-5.

Introduction

Oral lesions are a global public health concern that have affected humans since the beginning of civilization and continue to draw the attention of healthcare professionals and researchers worldwide [1]. In recent years, the situation has become more worrisome due to increased consumption of processed foods and unhealthy dietary habits [1].

Oral mucosal lesions are abnormalities affecting the oral mucosa and surrounding soft tissues and may range from benign inflammatory conditions to potentially malignant disorders and malignancies [2]. Among people living with HIV (PLHIV), oral mucosal lesions are among the earliest and most common clinical manifestations of immune suppression and disease progression. HIV-associated oral lesions include oral candidiasis, oral hairy leukoplakia, recurrent aphthous ulcers, linear gingival erythema, necrotizing periodontal diseases, Kaposi’s sarcoma, and other ulcerative or inflammatory lesions [3, 4]. The occurrence and severity of these lesions are often associated with declining immune status, particularly reduced CD4 cell counts, and may serve as important clinical indicators of HIV disease progression [3, 4].

A key contributor to the heightened risk of dental caries among People Living with HIV (PLHIV) is reduced salivary flow, often due to HIV infiltration of salivary glands and increased CD8 lymphocytes in saliva [4]. Additionally, the use of antiretroviral therapy (ART) may alter the oral microbiome, further increasing susceptibility to oral lesions [4]. Understanding the knowledge of these risk factors is essential for shaping appropriate health-seeking behaviors among HIV enrollees.

Despite advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART), oral mucosal lesions remain a significant public health concern among PLHIV, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. These lesions can cause pain, discomfort, difficulty eating and speaking, impaired nutrition, reduced quality of life, and poor adherence to HIV treatment. Early recognition and management are therefore important components of comprehensive HIV care. Evidence suggests that oral health conditions tend to be more severe and difficult to manage among PLHIV than among HIV-negative individuals. [4] Consequently, oral health services should be considered an essential component of comprehensive HIV care. Studies conducted among PLHIV in Uganda and Rwanda have reported a substantial burden of oral health conditions, including dental caries and oral mucosal lesions, highlighting the continuing need for integrated oral healthcare within HIV treatment programmes. [5, 6].

Knowledge of HIV-associated oral lesions plays an important role in early symptom recognition and timely healthcare utilization. Previous studies among PLHIV have reported varying levels of awareness and knowledge of oral manifestations, with healthcare workers serving as the primary source of oral health information for many patients. [7] Limited knowledge of oral lesions may contribute to delayed presentation, self-medication, poor utilization of oral healthcare services, and progression of lesions before appropriate treatment is sought. Furthermore, studies have shown that health-seeking behaviour is influenced not only by knowledge but also by factors such as educational attainment, access to healthcare services, health literacy, and socio-cultural beliefs [8, 9].

Despite such alarming statistics, data on oral lesion prevalence, risk factors, and health-seeking behavior among HIV-positive populations in Nigeria remain limited. This study therefore assessed the knowledge of oral lesions, associated risk factors, and healthcare-seeking behavior among HIV enrollees in Osun State, Nigeria.

Methodology

Study design and setting

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among people living with HIV (PLHIV) attending antiretroviral therapy (ART) clinics in selected healthcare facilities in Osun State, Nigeria. The study assessed participants’ knowledge of oral lesions, associated risk factors, and healthcare-seeking behaviour.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population comprised HIV-positive adults aged 18 years and above who had been enrolled in ART care for at least six months. Participants who were critically ill or had cognitive impairments that could affect reliable response were excluded.

HIV-positive status was confirmed using hospital ART clinic records, which documented diagnosis based on national HIV testing algorithms and confirmed enrolment into ART services. Only patients with documented HIV infection and active follow-up in the selected clinics were considered eligible.

Study sites selection

Osun State consists of 30 Local Government Areas (LGAs) distributed across three senatorial districts. For geographic representation, one LGA was selected from each senatorial district using simple random sampling. Within each selected LGA, the ART clinic with the highest patient attendance (based on six-month clinic records) was purposively selected to ensure adequate participant availability.

Sample size and sampling technique

The number of eligible and consenting participants across the selected ART clinics was small and fully accessible during the study period, a total population sampling (census approach) was adopted. Consequently, all eligible HIV-positive adults attending the selected clinics during the study period were included (N = 180). No sampling was performed at the participant level.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire developed from an extensive review of relevant literature on oral health among people living with HIV. The instrument was adapted to suit the local context and study objectives.

The questionnaire was reviewed for face and content validity by a panel comprising two oral health experts with postgraduate training and clinical research experience in oral medicine, and one HIV care clinician with over ten years of ART program experience. Each item was assessed for clarity, relevance, and appropriateness, yielding a Content Validity Index (CVI) of 0.86.

A pilot study was conducted among 20 PLHIV at a non-study ART clinic to assess clarity and reliability. Internal consistency of the knowledge section was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.78, indicating acceptable reliability.

The questionnaire consisted of sections on socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of oral lesions, associated risk factors, and healthcare-seeking behaviour. The knowledge section contained 10 items, with correct responses scored as 1 and incorrect responses scored as 0. Total scores ranged from 0 to 10 and were categorized as poor (0–3), fair (4–6), and good (7–10).

Data collection procedure

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research assistants using the structured questionnaire. Participants were recruited consecutively as they attended clinic appointments until all eligible individuals were enrolled.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were used to summarize variables.

Chi-square tests were used to assess associations between selected independent variables and outcome variables. Specifically, associations were tested between socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, education level, and occupation), clinical characteristics (duration on ART), and behavioural factors (oral hygiene practices, smoking status, and alcohol use) with:

Ethics consideration and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Osun State Health Research Ethics Committee, Osogbo, Osun State (Approval Number: OSHREC/PRS/569T/418). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. For participants who were unable to read or write, the study information was explained in their preferred language, and informed consent was documented using a thumbprint in the presence of an impartial witness. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were maintained throughout the study.oughout the study.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics (Table 1)

A total of 180 HIV-positive individuals with clinically confirmed oral lesions participated in the study. The majority of respondents were within the economically productive age group of 30–59 years (58.9%). Nearly four out of every ten respondents had attained tertiary education (39.4%). The study population was predominantly Yoruba (74.4%), reflecting the ethnic composition of the study area. Male and female participants were almost equally represented (51.7% and 48.3%, respectively). Traders (46.7%) and civil servants (40.0%) constituted the major occupational groups among respondents (Table 1).

Awareness and knowledge of oral lesions (Table 2)

Knowledge of oral lesions among respondents

The association between socio-demographic characteristics and knowledge of oral lesions among HIV enrollees (Table 3)

Knowledge of oral lesions varied significantly across several socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents. Younger respondents demonstrated better knowledge compared with older age groups, with those aged below 30 years having the highest proportion of good knowledge (77.6%), while respondents aged 51–60 years had the lowest proportion of good knowledge (31.6%) (p = 0.001).

Educational attainment was significantly associated with knowledge of oral lesions (p < 0.001). Respondents with tertiary education demonstrated better knowledge, with 67.6% exhibiting good knowledge compared with 48.8% among those with primary education and 35.8% among those with secondary education. All respondents without formal education were classified as having good knowledge; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the small number of respondents in this category.

Ethnicity was also significantly associated with knowledge of oral lesions (p = 0.022). Yoruba respondents demonstrated a higher proportion of good knowledge (62.7%) compared with Hausa (41.7%), Igbo (31.3%), and respondents from other ethnic groups (33.3%).

In contrast, knowledge of oral lesions did not differ significantly by religion (p = 0.346), gender (p = 0.806), or occupational status (p = 0.840). Similar proportions of good knowledge were observed across occupational categories, indicating that occupation was not an important determinant of knowledge of oral lesions among respondents as presented in Table 3.

Association between socio-demographic characteristics and health-seeking behaviour (Table 4)

Health-seeking behaviour varied across selected socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents. Age was significantly associated with health-seeking behaviour (p = 0.008). Respondents aged below 30 years demonstrated the highest proportion of good health-seeking behaviour (19.0%), whereas those aged 30–49 years had the lowest proportion (1.1%).

Religion was also significantly associated with health-seeking behaviour (p = 0.023). Respondents who identified as Christians demonstrated a higher proportion of good health-seeking behaviour (13.5%) compared with those who practiced Islam or other religions (1.4%).

In contrast, health-seeking behaviour did not differ significantly by gender (p = 0.888), educational status (p = 0.360), ethnicity (p = 0.064), or occupational status (p = 0.181). Although Yoruba respondents demonstrated a higher proportion of good health-seeking behaviour than non-Yoruba respondents, this association was not statistically significant. Similarly, respondents engaged in trading and business activities showed relatively better health-seeking behaviour than those in other occupational categories; however, the observed differences were not statistically significant.

Overall, age and religion were the only socio-demographic characteristics significantly associated with health-seeking behaviour among HIV enrollees as presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This study assessed knowledge of oral lesions and health-seeking behaviour among HIV-positive individuals attending ART clinics in Osun State, Nigeria. The findings provide insight into awareness of oral lesions, sources of information, and socio-demographic factors associated with knowledge and health-seeking behaviour among people living with HIV (PLHIV).

The socio-demographic profile of respondents showed a predominance of individuals within the productive age group (30–59 years), with nearly equal gender representation. This pattern is consistent with previous studies among PLHIV in Nigeria, where HIV infection remains concentrated among economically active adults. [8–10] The relatively high proportion of respondents with secondary and tertiary education may reflect increasing access to education and HIV care services within the study setting.

More than half of the respondents were aware of oral lesions, and healthcare workers were identified as the major source of information. This finding supports the important role of routine clinical encounters in improving awareness of HIV-associated oral conditions. Previous studies have similarly reported that healthcare providers are the primary source of oral health information among PLHIV, emphasizing the need to strengthen oral health education within HIV care services. [11] Despite this level of awareness, gaps remained in respondents’ recognition of specific manifestations of oral lesions, suggesting that oral health counselling may not be sufficiently emphasized during routine HIV care.

Knowledge of oral lesions was significantly associated with age and educational attainment. Younger respondents demonstrated better knowledge than older individuals, while respondents with tertiary education were more likely to have good knowledge than those with lower levels of education. This finding is consistent with existing evidence that higher educational attainment is associated with improved health literacy, access to health information, and better understanding of disease-related conditions. [12] Educational interventions targeted at individuals with lower educational attainment may therefore contribute to improved awareness of oral manifestations of HIV.

Health-seeking behaviour was generally poor among respondents. Although age and religion were significantly associated with health-seeking behaviour, gender, ethnicity, educational status, and occupation were not. The generally low proportion of respondents demonstrating good health-seeking behaviour suggests that awareness of oral lesions may not necessarily translate into prompt utilization of oral healthcare services. Similar findings have been reported in studies of oral healthcare utilization, where factors such as perceived severity of symptoms, accessibility of services, cost of care, and competing health priorities influence care-seeking decisions. [13–16].

Importantly, no significant association was observed between knowledge of oral lesions and health-seeking behaviour. This finding suggests that knowledge alone may be insufficient to influence healthcare utilization among PLHIV. Beyond awareness, structural barriers, socioeconomic factors, and individual perceptions may affect whether individuals seek professional care when oral lesions occur. Previous studies have similarly reported that health-seeking behaviour is influenced by multiple interacting factors beyond knowledge alone. [17–21].

Overall, the findings highlight the need to strengthen oral health education within HIV care programmes while simultaneously addressing barriers that may limit access to and utilization of oral healthcare services. Integrating routine oral health screening, patient counselling, and referral pathways into HIV care may improve early detection and management of oral lesions among PLHIV.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge of oral lesions, and health-seeking behaviour. The findings therefore reflect associations rather than cause-and-effect relationships.

Second, some variables, particularly occupation, required regrouping due to sparse data in several categories. While this was necessary to meet the assumptions of inferential statistical tests, it may have reduced the level of detail and masked some subgroup differences.

Third, the study relied on self-reported responses, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly in reporting health-seeking behaviour. However, efforts were made to minimize this through the use of trained interviewers and standardized questionnaires.

Finally, the study was conducted among ART clinic attendees in selected healthcare facilities in Osun State; therefore, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all people living with HIV in the state or other settings outside clinic-based populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that awareness of oral lesions among people living with HIV was moderate, with healthcare workers serving as the primary source of information. However, gaps in knowledge of specific oral manifestations remain evident. Health-seeking behaviour was generally poor across the study population, with variation observed across selected socio-demographic factors such as age, education, and religion.

Importantly, while socio-demographic characteristics influenced both knowledge and health-seeking behaviour to some extent, knowledge of oral lesions did not translate into improved health-seeking behaviour. This suggests that improving awareness alone may not be sufficient to change care-seeking practices.

The findings highlight the need for integrated oral health education within HIV care services, strengthened patient counselling, and improved linkage between HIV care and oral health services to enhance early identification and management of oral lesions among people living with HIV.
