Multi-omics characterization identifies conserved candidate gene and reveals breed-specific regulatory mechanisms underlying growth-related traits in pigs
Authors: Anyang Zhao, Jiakun Qiao, Renzhuo Kuang, Ziyi Chen, Yunru Tan, Mengjin Zhu, Guangliang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAverage daily gain (ADG), age at 115 kg (AGE), backfat thickness (BF), and loin eye area (LEA) are key growth and carcass traits in pigs. However, breed-specific molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying these traits remain largely unknown. Here, we identified multiple candidate genes associated with these 4 traits from Duroc and Yorkshire pigs by three Genome-wide association study(GWAS) methods (MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) and differential genes expression analysis, verified them based on RNA sequencing(RNA-seq), Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq), and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data from Duroc and Yorkshire pigs, and explored the regulatory mechanisms underlying these traits.ResultGWAS across Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire identified 44 high-confidence SNPs. A total of 24, 13, and 19 candidate genes were identified respectively from Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire by GWAS and differential gene expression analysis (in three breeds versus Meishan pigs). The candidate gene ARL8A was found to be highly conserved between Duroc and Yorkshire. GeneMANIA networks of Landrace exhibited tightly connected core modules of candidate genes. Integrated RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq analyses of muscle and fat tissue data consistently suggested that ARL8A exhibits relatively high transcriptional activity and chromatin accessibility, supporting its potential role in muscle and fat development.ConclusionsThe integration of three GWAS methods with differential gene expression analysis revealed that core candidate genes involved were mainly involved in regulating fat deposition, energy metabolism, and cell growth in constructed breed-specific regulatory networks. Among these candidate genes, ARL8A was a highly conserved gene modulating growth-related traits (BF and LEA), as evidenced by the multi-omics data. Our findings provide an insight into genetic architecture and regulation mechanisms underlying pig growth and development, offering important resources for porcine molecular breeding and genetic improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12979-2.Keywords: Multi-omics characterization, Growth-related traits, Gene functional association network, ARL8A, Multiple pig breeds

Background

The domestic pig (Sus scrofa) is one of the most economically important livestock species worldwide, providing a major source of animal meat. Due to their superior growth performance and carcass traits, the Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire breeds are widely used as parental lines in commercial pig breeding, serving as the genetic foundation for three-way crossbred pigs. Growth and carcass traits are critical factors determining production performance and profitability in pig farming. In particular, average daily gain (ADG), age at 115 kg (AGE), backfat thickness (BF), and loin eye area (LEA) are key economic traits that reflect both growth rate and carcass quality, and these traits are strongly influenced by the genetic basis of the parental breeds. Exploring the molecular genetic basis of the traits with moderate-to-high heritability is of great research significance [1, 2]. Some candidate genes associated with growth traits such as IGF2 [3], MC4R [4], CCND2 [5], and PLAG1 have been identified, and genetic loci responsible for ADG, AGE, BF, and LEA have been uncovered using GWAS.

With the rapid development of high-throughput sequencing technologies, transcriptome analysis has emerged as a powerful tool to explore gene expression patterns and regulatory networks underlying economically important traits in pigs. Unlike GWAS, transcriptome sequencing provides dynamic information on how genes are expressed in specific tissues and developmental stages [6]. Some key genes and pathways associated with growth, reproduction, meat quality, and disease resistance have been identified by combining differential expression analysis, co-expression network construction, and functional enrichment analysis [7]. However, single-gene analysis is insufficient to explain the complex trait regulatory mechanisms in pigs. Based on the classical weighted gene co-expression network analysis(WGCNA) [8] and protein-protein interaction (PPI) [9], and more recently to Bayesian models and multi-omics integrative approaches, previous studies have shifted from single-gene to module-, pathway-, and system-level perspectives, providing deeper insights into the genetic basis of complex traits in pigs. Given the relatively limited genome resources in pigs, researchers have attempted to map pig genes to the human reference genome and utilize the more comprehensive human gene network databases to construct pig genome functional modules, thus supplementing resources for pig genomics research [10].

ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq enable researchers to characterize chromatin accessibility, cis-regulatory element activity, transcription factor binding, and key epigenetic modifications, including histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) and histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation (H3K4me3), at single-cell resolution or in individual animals [11, 12]. By integrating these epigenomic maps with transcriptomic differential expression results, co-expression modules, and GWAS-mapped signals, researchers can validate the candidate genes and precisely identify the core cis-regulatory elements and transcription factors that drive phenotypic variation [13]. More importantly, such cross-evidence from multidimensional datasets not only strengthens the functional interpretation of key candidate genes but also allows for reconstruction of multi-layered causal regulatory cascades (from cis-regulatory elements and tissue-specific expression patterns to the final target traits) thereby substantially enhancing the systematic understanding of the molecular basis underlying complex traits [12, 14].

This study systematically identified highly conserved candidate gene across multiple tissues (muscle and fat) and three pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire) by integrating multiple GWAS (MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) methods with multi-omics analyses (RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and H3K27ac ChIP-seq). Our multi-omics analyses identified ARL8A as a key conserved regulator of muscle and fat development. We constructed breed-specific regulatory networks of core candidate genes mainly involved in regulating fat deposition, energy metabolism, and cell growth. Our findings provide an insight into the genetic regulatory mechanisms underlying growth-related traits in pigs, and lay foundation for porcine molecular breeding and genetic improvement.

Methods

Animal sampling and phenotypic determination

The experimental data for the three pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire) were collected from multiple core breeding farms of Guangxi Yang Xiang Co., Ltd. (Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China) during 2020–2024. Four phenotypic traits were recorded, including average daily gain (ADG), age at 115 kg body weight (AGE), backfat thickness (BF), and loin eye area (LEA). All pigs were raised under a unified management system in core breeding farms with standardized feeding, housing, and environmental control. Temperature and humidity were maintained under standard conditions, and animals were managed according to consistent husbandry protocols within each group. Backfat thickness (BF) and loin eye area (LEA) were measured in vivo using B-mode ultrasound. BF was recorded at the 10th–11th rib position approximately 6–7 cm from the dorsal midline, and LEA was obtained from cross-sectional images of the longissimus dorsi muscle using standard image analysis procedures.

Estimation of genetic parameters

The HIBLUP (version 1.5.3) [15] software was employed to estimate the genetic parameters of four traits across three pig breeds. Genotypic data and quality control

For DNA extraction, ear tissue samples were collected from three pig breeds for subsequent genomic analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from experimental pigs using an Omega Genomic DNA Extraction Kit. DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, with OD260/280 ratios between 1.7 and 2.0. Samples with concentrations above 50 ng/µL and total DNA yields above 1 µg were selected for subsequent analyses. SNP genotyping was performed using the Illumina PorcineSNP60 BeadChip with Infinium technology, including DNA amplification, array hybridization, and scanning, to generate genome-wide SNP data. Genotype quality control was conducted using PLINK (version 1.90), removing SNPs located on sex chromosomes, SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05, and individuals with missing SNP data.

Population structure and linkage disequilibrium analysis

To assess the degree of genetic differentiation and diversity among populations, we first calculated the pairwise average FST (Fixation index) values, and then the weighted mean FST across the three breeds using PLINK (version 1.90) by previously reported method [16]. Additionally, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) [17] and retained the top ten principal components for subsequent visualization. Using PopLDdecay (version 3.42) software, the pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNPs within each population (breed) was estimated, and LD decay curves were constructed [18]. Finally, ADMIXTURE (version 1.3.0) was applied to infer the ancestral composition of the three breeds at K = 2 and K = 3 so as to reveal their population (breed) structure differences [19]. Furthermore, genetic diversity parameters, including observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and polymorphism information content (PIC), were estimated for each breed using PLINK 2.0 to assess within-population genetic variation [20].

Genome-wide association analysis using multiple models

The initial dataset consisted of 138,635 (121,714 in Duroc, 127,677 in Landrace, and 120,152 in Yorkshire) pigs. After genotype quality control and the exclusion of individuals with genotype–phenotype mismatches, abnormal phenotypic records, or missing covariate information (including batch, season, sex, and other relevant factors), a total of 22,776 individuals (6,467 Duroc, 6,933 Landrace, and 9,376 Yorkshire) and 188,156 SNPs (121,714 in Duroc, 127,677 in Landrace, and 120,152 in Yorkshire) were retained for subsequent analyses. Target traits were analyzed using three GWAS methods including mixed linear model (MLM), Fixed and random model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU), and Bayesian-information and Linkage-disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway (BLINK). MLM and FarmCPU were implemented using rMVP (version 2.0) in the R package, while BLINK was conducted using GAPIT (version 3.0). To account for potential confounding effects, sex, batch, year, and season were included as fixed effects in the GWAS models, coded as categorical factors.

A genome-wide significance threshold was defined using the Bonferroni correction, calculated as 0.05 divided by the total number of SNPs, and was applied to identify significant SNPs [21, 22]. Different GWAS methods often identify non-overlapping sets of SNPs, and combining multiple approaches can improve the reliability of detected associations. Therefore, candidate SNPs were defined as those that reached significance in at least two of the three models (MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) and were used for subsequent candidate gene analysis [23]. Genes located within a ± 1 Mb window around each significant SNP were defined as GWAS candidate genes (GCGs). All significant SNPs obtained by GWAS were visualized using the CMplot (version 4.2.2) in R package.

Differential genes expressed analysis

Further, we performed the differential genes expression analysis between the three pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire) and the Meishan pig. Meishan pigs have been reported to exhibit great differences in growth rate and fat deposition from above-mentioned three breeds [24, 25]. Meishan pigs as a reference could lay a foundation for dissecting the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying growth-related traits across various breeds. Transcriptome data used in this study were obtained from the public PigGTEx database (https://piggtex.farmgtex.org/). We collected expression matrices of 16 tissue samples across two tissues (muscle and adipose) and four pig breeds (Duroc (n = 4), Landrace (n = 4), Yorkshire (n = 4), and Meishan (n = 4)). This analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 (version 1.48.1) [26]. Meishan pigs were used as the reference group for differential expression analysis. Individual samples were selected from the PigGTEx database based on metadata, including BioSample, BioProject, library layout, sequencing platform, model, breed, and inferred breed. The corresponding SRA datasets used in this study were obtained from PRJNA597497 and PRJNA287471. Batch effects and other potential confounding factors were corrected during data preprocessing. Genes with an corrected p-value (FDR < 0.05) and fold change > 1 or < -1.5 were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes. All DEGs and GCGs were annotated based on the Sscrofa11.1 reference genome.

Construction of gene functional association networks

To further investigate the genetic mechanisms by which genes regulated growth-related traits, overlapping genes between DEGs and GWAS candidate genes (GCGs) were selected and mapped to human orthologs. We constructed breed-specific gene association networks based on the identified orthologous genes using GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) with default parameters. The resulting edge lists were subsequently imported into Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/) for network visualization and refinement. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on the genes within the network to characterize their functional roles and associated signaling pathways. The MCODE plugin was then applied to identify highly interconnected gene modules. Finally, iRegulon was used to infer transcription factor–target gene regulatory relationships within these modules [27].

Identification of conserved candidate gene and multi-omics characterization

Conserved candidate genes were defined as those identified in at least two pig breeds. To identify conserved candidate genes across breeds, we performed an integrative multi-omics analysis using publicly available datasets from the PigGTEx database (https://piggtex.farmgtex.org/). The RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq data from muscle and adipose tissues of Duroc and Yorkshire pigs were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA; accession number PRJNA597497), with two biological replicates per breed. For each tissue, Pearson correlation coefficients were first calculated between replicates, and two replicates with high correlation were subsequently merged for further analyses. Specifically, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on the Galaxy bioinformatics platform (https://usegalaxy.eu/). ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq analyses were performed following the ENCODE standard pipelines available on GitHub (https://github.com/ENCODE-DCC/atac-seq-pipeline and https://github.com/ENCODE-DCC/chip-seq-pipeline2 ). We selected the top nine genes ranked by p-value in both Duroc and Landrace pigs to generate a heatmap, capturing genome-wide expression trends. This was performed to evaluate the correlation among RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq signals within the same samples, providing a basis for subsequent analyses. We performed cis-regulatory element identification to characterize gene regulatory landscapes. We integrated H3K27ac ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq peaks using BEDTools (v2.30.0) merge [28] to identify overlapping regions representing active regulatory elements with both chromatin accessibility and histone enrichment. BEDTools intersect was used to remove promoter-proximal regions overlapping transcription start sites (TSS), thereby defining distal regulatory elements. These distal elements were extended by ± 500 kb and linked to nearby genes based on annotated TSS in the pig genome using BEDTools intersect. Enhancer–gene pairs were prioritized based on enhancer–promoter midpoint distance to infer the most likely regulatory relationships. Finally, signal tracks of conserved candidate gene regions were visualized using IGV.

The results showed that the pairwise FST values between breeds ranged from 0.174 to 0.208, implying a considerable population differentiation (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed distinct clustering of Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire breeds (Fig. 2B). The results of ancestral composition analysis at K = 3 indicated that the three breeds had distinct genomic structures (Fig. 2D). The LD decay analysis revealed that Duroc exhibited the fastest decay, followed by Landrace, while Yorkshire displayed the slowest decay (Fig. 2C). The distribution of Ho indicated that all breeds maintained relatively high levels of genetic diversity (0.3–0.4) (Fig. 2E). Genome-wide association study

Next, we performed three types of GWAS (MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) on 6,467 Duroc (121,714 SNPs), 6,933 Landrace (127,677 SNPs), and 9,376 Yorkshire (120,152 SNPs) pigs that remained after quality control. SNPs that were significant in at least two types of GWAS were defined as, which were then used for subsequent identification of candidate genes. In Duroc, a total of 14 candidate SNPs were identified to be significantly associated with growth-related traits, which were distributed on chromosomes 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, 17, and 18 (Fig. 3, Table 2). Specifically for ADG, 3 candidate SNPs were detected, of which SNP rs333925230 on chromosome 1 reached significance level across all three types of GWAS. For AGE trait, 5 candidate SNPs were identified, of which SNP rs333925230 on chromosome 4 was related to both AGE and ADG. For BF, 5 significant SNPs were detected, of which SNP yz_rs344904552 on chromosome 18 was identified to be consistently significant in all three types of GWAS. For LEA trait, only one candidate SNP was identified (yz_rs80894106 on chromosome 7) and it was consistently significant in all three GWAS models. In the Landrace population, a total of 12 significant SNPs were identified, and they were distributed across chromosomes 1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 14, 15, and 18 (Fig. S1, Table 2). Among these 12 SNPs, 6 SNPs were found to be associated with ADG, 4 with AGE, 3 with both BF, and 3 with LEA. Notably, SNP yz_rs320421714 reached significance level in all three GWAS models for the BF. Moreover, rs333260686 and rs3471841868 were associated with both ADG and AGE, while yz_rs320421714 with ADG, AGE, and BF. The Landrace population exhibited dispersed association signals without distinct hotspot regions, but showed clear pleiotropic effects. Notably, SNP yz_rs320421714 was consistently significant across three GWAS models and multiple traits. In the Yorkshire population, a total of 19 significant SNPs were identified and they were distributed across chromosomes 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10, 11, 13, 14, 16, 17, and 18 (Fig. S2, Table 2). Among them, 5 SNPs were associated with ADG, 7 with AGE, 6 with BF, and 5 with LEA. Notably, SNPs rs321928062 (Chr1: 161045746), yz_rs344034872 (Chr3: 81566105), rs322277665 (Chr10: 55351719), and rs322725768 (Chr16: 24075559) were associated with both ADG and AGE. Particularly, SNP rs321928062 (Chr1: 161045746) reached significance level for both ADG and AGE across all three GWAS models, suggesting strong evidence of pleiotropic effects. Yorkshire pigs showed more widely distributed signals and a more polygenic pattern across growth traits, with multiple loci jointly contributing to ADG and AGE. Subsequently, we defined a 1-Mb window upstream and downstream each of significant SNP as the candidate gene screening region. A total of 198, 89, and 343 GCGs were identified from the defined candidate gene screening region in the Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire populations, respectively. Among the identified GCGs, no overlapping genes were found between Duroc and Landrace. In contrast, Duroc and Yorkshire shared 14 overlapping genes, while Landrace and Yorkshire shared 18 overlapping genes.

Gene differential expressed analysis

Gene expression matrices (31,909 genes per matrix) of 16 tissue samples (including 2 tissues (adipose and muscle) from 4 pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, Yorkshire, and Meishan)with 4 samples per breed), were obtained from public databases for differential gene expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from adipose tissue in comparison of three breeds vs. Meishan were compared with GWAS candidate genes (GCGs) associated with ADG, AGE, and BF traits, while DEGs from muscle tissue were compared with GCGs associated with ADG, AGE, and LEA traits. The results showed that in adipose tissue, a total of 24 DEGs in Duroc vs. Meishan were overlapped with GCGs, including 20 upregulated and 4 downregulated DEGs(Fig. 4A); In total, 13 DEGs in Landrace vs. Meishan were overlapped with GCGs, including 4 up-regulated and 9 down-regulated DEGs(Fig. 4B); and 19 DEGs in Yorkshire vs. Meishan were overlapped with GCGs, including 7 up-regulated and 12 down-regulated DEGs(Fig. 4C). In muscle tissue, a total of 8 overlapping genes of DEGs (Duroc vs. Meishan) and GCGs were identified, 6 overlapping genes of DEGs (Landrace vs. Meishan) and GCGs, and 8 overlapping genes of DEGs (Yorkshire vs. Meishan) and GCGs (Fig. 4D-F). These results highlight the genetic regulatory mechanisms of growth-related traits varied with breeds and tissues.

Identification of candidate genes

Candidate genes were defined as the overlapping genes between DEGs and GCGs. Considering that ADG, LEA, and AGE traits are associated with both adipose and muscle tissues, candidate genes for ADG, AGE, and BF traits were derived from the overlapping between DEGs in adipose tissue and corresponding ADG-, AGE-, and BF- associated GCGs, whereas candidate genes for ADG, AGE, and LEA traits were obtained from the overlapping between DEGs in muscle tissue and the ADG-, AGE-, and LEA-associated GCGs. Among the three pig breeds, Duroc and Yorkshire exhibited three overlapping candidate genes (ARL8A, PGF, and COQ6), However, no overlapping genes were found between Duroc and Landrace, between Landrace and Yorkshire, or among all three breeds. Notably, this screening strategy failed to identify any candidate genes for the LEA trait in Landrace. Detailed information on the identified candidate genes is provided in Table 3.

Construction of gene functional association network

A total of three gene functional association networks (GFANs) were constructed corresponding to three pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire). The gene functional association network for Duroc breed was comprised 46 genes (Fig. 5A), including two subnetworks: one subnetwork contained 4 highly connected genes (PIGT, PIGS, PIGK, and MIF4DH) (Fig. 5B), and the other contained 5 closely connected genes (ADRA2A, ADRA2C, APLP1, COMP, and ZFHX4) (Fig. 5C). The nodes in both subnetworks were primarily connected via gene co-expression and physical interactions. The gene functional association network constructed for the Landrace breed consisted of 35 genes (Fig. 5D), and these genes were predominantly connected through physical interactions and common protein domains. The network constructed for the Landrace breed consisted of two subnetworks: one subnetwork (Fig. 5E) exhibited high density, containing 8 genes (ATF3, BATF3, JUN, DDIT3, JUNB, JUND, CEBPE, and HLF), mainly connected by physical interactions, and the other (Fig. 5F) was relatively small, containing only 3 genes (SNAP91, SLC25A12, and ST6GALNAC5). The network constructed for the Yorkshire breed encompassed 40 genes (Fig. 5G), exhibiting relatively sparse connections, and one of its subnetwork (Fig. 5H) was composed of 4 genes (NT5E, MCPH1, E2F1, and DIRAS2), containing merely 6 edges, and the other of its subnetwork (Fig. 5I) was the largest among all subnetworks, containing 10 genes (ZNF200, PACS1, ZNF367, MUS81, WDR37, RSRP1, PLSCR4, SART1, CPSF6, and DNAJC10), where co-expression was dominant type of interaction.

iRegulon analysis revealed no significant transcription factor–target gene regulatory relationships in the core gene networks of Duroc and Yorkshire pigs, likely due to the small size of the gene sets, weak functional associations, and the fact that GeneMANIA networks primarily capture functional rather than transcriptional relationships. In contrast, the Landrace pig network exhibited significant regulatory relationships, with genes classified into three categories: (1) specific transcription factors, including JUNB and BATF3; (2) genes functioning as both transcription factors and target genes with evidence of autoregulation, including JUN, JUND, and ATF3; and (3) target-only genes, including HLF and CEBPE (Fig. 5J).

GO and KEGG analyses revealed distinct pathway characteristics among the three breeds. In Duroc, enriched pathways were mainly associated with immune and inflammatory responses, including the IL-17 and TNF signaling pathways, stress responses such as the MAPK signaling pathway, and hormone-related metabolic regulation, along with functions related to energy metabolism (Fig. S3A-B). In Landrace, enrichment was primarily linked to muscle development and growth regulation, including the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and focal adhesion, as well as apoptosis and epigenetic processes such as histone acetylation (Fig. S3C-D). In Yorkshire, pathways related to energy metabolism and biosynthesis, including pyruvate metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and carbon metabolism, were predominant, along with ligase activity and lysosome biogenesis (Fig. S3E-F). These differences reflect breed-specific regulation of growth, metabolism, and immune-related traits.

Phenotypic associations and multi-omics features of conserved candidate gene across pig breeds

The heatmap results of RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, and ATAC-seq indicated a high level of consistency among the three datasets, suggesting their reliability for subsequent analyses (Fig. S4). Based on analysis of the PigBiobank database, ARL8A was significantly associated with backfat thickness (BF), consistent with our GWAS and multi-omics results (Fig. 6A, C), suggesting its potential involvement in lipid deposition regulation. Genotype analysis revealed significant breed-specific differences in ARL8A between Duroc and Yorkshire pigs (Fig. 6B). Further characterization identified the promoter region (chr10:24321444–24325233, blue shading in Fig. 6C) and a putative enhancer region (chr10:23811686–24814445, yellow shading in Fig. 6C). Integration of RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and H3K27ac ChIP-seq data showed that ARL8A was highly expressed in both adipose and muscle tissues. Multiple open chromatin regions were detected around the gene, with notable differences in chromatin accessibility across breeds and tissues, accompanied by strong H3K27ac enrichment, particularly in adipose tissue. In contrast, the neighboring gene GPR37L1 showed negligible expression. Collectively, the ARL8A locus exhibits active epigenetic regulatory features, and its expression pattern is supported by multi-omics evidence, suggesting that ARL8A is a candidate gene associated with lipid deposition traits.

Discussion

The pig traits ADG, AGE, BF, and LEA are closely related to growth and development, serving as key economic indicators for assessing growth performance and meat quality, and providing valuable reference for breeding selection and production performance evaluation. It has been reported that heritability estimates of major growth-related traits, such as ADG, AGE, BF, and LEA, in pigs exhibit a certain degree of variation across different studies. Heritability estimates are influenced by multiple factors including estimation methods, population breeds, and feeding environments. Generally, the heritability of growth-related traits has been estimated to range from 0.2 to 0.5, rated as a moderate level [29–31]. In this study, the heritability estimates for the above four growth-related traits were slightly lower than the commonly reported range of 0.2–0.5, which might be attributed to factors such as family structure and measurement age. Although the heritability values (0.176–0.297) estimated in this study are moderate to low, these traits are still under genetic control.

To improve the reliability of our results, we applied three GWAS methods (MLM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) to analyze the Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire populations. Candidate SNPs were selected based on significance in at least two GWAS methods. Based on this strategy, several candidate genes were identified, including MRAP2, VRTN, and FGF23. We systematically compared our findings with previous GWAS studies on growth traits in pigs. The results showed that VRTN and FGF23 have been repeatedly reported in multiple studies, and are associated with body conformation and fat deposition traits, respectively. MRAP2 has been identified in integrative GWAS analyses as a candidate gene associated with ADG [26], although the strength of evidence varies across different populations. ANO1, EYA2, and KCNQ5 have been reported to be significantly associated with BF [32], while FGF23 has also been consistently confirmed to be significantly associated with BF in multiple pig populations [31]. In addition, CCND2 has been identified in several studies as being associated with BF [5]. Meanwhile, we also identified novel candidate genes that have not been reported in previous GWAS studies. For example, ARL8A has been experimentally demonstrated to be involved in intracellular transport and neurotransmission processes [33], suggesting that it may play an important role in growth and development. PHGDH has been identified as the rate-limiting enzyme in the serine biosynthesis pathway and plays a crucial role in regulating cell proliferation and metabolic processes [34], further suggesting that it may contribute to growth and development through the regulation of cellular metabolism and proliferation. It tends to be unreliable to identify candidate genes associated with target traits solely via traditional genomic approaches. Integration of genomic data with transcriptomic data can significantly enhance the reliability of candidate genes identified [35]. In this study, differential expression analysis was performed between each commercial breed and Meishan pigs. The DEGs identified through differential expression analysis were then overlapped with GCGs, and the overlapping genes were considered as high-confidence candidate genes. This integrated strategy not only improves the reliability of candidate genes identified, but also provides an important methodological reference for exploration of the genetic mechanisms underlying key productive traits. Notably, no LEA-associated candidate genes were identified in Landrace pigs through the intersection of DEGs and GCGs. This may be due to differences in genetic background as well as the stringent filtering criteria applied in this study. This does not imply the absence of regulatory genes, but rather that no genes met both selection criteria under the current analytical framework.

Based on regulatory networks and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses in Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire pigs, the three breeds exhibited shared neuroendocrine- and stress-related signaling characteristics that regulate metabolism and tissue remodeling. Breed-specific functional preferences were also observed, consistent with pathway enrichment results. In Duroc, the ADRA2C-centered network was consistent with enrichment of immune- and stress-related pathways, including IL-17, TNF, and MAPK signaling pathways, indicating integrated roles in neural regulation, inflammatory response, and metabolic control. ADRA2C, a sympathetic receptor regulating norepinephrine and cAMP signaling, is involved in lipid mobilization, stress response, and energy metabolism [36], contributing to variation in growth traits [37]. ECM-related genes (MMP3, MMP9, and KRT5) suggest coordinated regulation of extracellular matrix remodeling with neuroinflammatory signaling in adipose and muscle development [38]. Mitochondrial metabolism- and amino acid biosynthesis-related genes further support their roles in growth traits such as ADG and BF [39]. In Landrace, the ATF3-centered network corresponded to PI3K-Akt signaling, focal adhesion, and epigenetic regulation, indicating roles in stress response, metabolic homeostasis, and tissue remodeling. ATF3 and JUN/AP-1 family members form a stress-responsive regulatory axis integrating external stimuli with metabolic signals to regulate energy metabolism and tissue development [40, 41], thereby influencing ADG, AGE, BF, and LEA traits. Genes such as ACADM, NOX5, BDNF, ITGA1, and CENPF suggest fatty acid oxidation, oxidative stress response, and cell proliferation as major contributors to phenotypic variation [42]. In Yorkshire, the NT5E-centered network was consistent with the enrichment of energy metabolism pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, pyruvate metabolism, and carbon metabolism, highlighting its role in metabolic regulation and cellular structural maintenance. NT5E, CAP1, PLSCR4, ARL8A, and E2F1 constitute a potential upstream regulatory module involved in adenosine metabolism and energy allocation [43], cytoskeletal and membrane organization [44, 45], lysosomal trafficking, and cell proliferation/metabolism [46], respectively. Additional genes such as PGF, PPARGC1B, DIRAS2, MCPH1, FBLN1, and MUS81 suggest coordinated regulation of lipid metabolism, cell proliferation, and tissue remodeling underlying growth, muscle formation, and fat deposition. Overall, shared neuroendocrine–metabolic mechanisms exist across breeds, while distinct pathway enrichments reflect breed-specific regulatory architectures underlying ADG, AGE, BF, and LEA traits.

The breed-specific gene interaction networks in this study, together with the sequence polymorphisms of ARL8A among different pig breeds, indicate that the regulatory patterns of ARL8A vary across breeds. In Duroc, ARL8A is primarily connected to BORCS5 and TMEM30A through physical interactions and co-expression, participating in vesicle transport, cell proliferation, energy metabolism, and cytoskeleton remodeling, and indirectly influencing growth-related traits [47, 48]. Moreover, ARL8A indirectly interact with key genes such as CCND1 and PGF, which regulate muscle cell proliferation and energy metabolism, supporting its potential role in growth regulation. In Yorkshire, ARL8A establishes close regulatory links with RHOG, CAP1, NT5E, and other genes through co-expression and physical interactions; these genes are mainly involved in cell cycle control [49], actin filament assembly [50], and energy homeostasis [51], indicating that ARL8A plays an important role in cell proliferation, muscle development, and energy regulation. Furthermore, ARL8A indirectly interacts with core cell cycle genes such as E2F1 and TFDP2, regulating myocyte proliferation, and cooperates with NT5E, PPARGC1B, ACSS2, and other genes to modulate lipid metabolism and energy balance. Through connections with signaling pathway genes (DIRAS2, PLSCR4) and cytoskeleton remodeling genes (CAP1, CAP2, CFL1), ARL8A mediates intracellular growth signaling and muscle cytoskeleton development. PheWAS results indicate that ARL8A is significantly associated with multiple meat quality traits in Duroc and Yorkshire. ATAC-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq analyses show that ARL8A exhibits high chromatin accessibility and enhancer-associated modifications in both adipose and muscle tissues, suggesting that its expression is regulated by tissue-specific active enhancers. Integration of gene interaction networks and multi-omics data indicates that ARL8A participates in biological processes through interactions with growth-related genes and exhibits consistently high expression across breeds and tissues, suggesting that it is a key candidate gene that may regulate pig growth and development through multiple molecular pathways.

In this study, integrating GWAS and differential expression analysis identified a set of candidate genes specifically expressed in muscle and adipose tissues and associated with growth and fat deposition traits, which can serve as a prior gene set for genomic selection in pigs. Traditional genomic selection relying on genome-wide SNP markers shows limited accuracy for complex traits such as backfat thickness and lean meat percentage. Incorporating RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq data can improve the weighting of biologically relevant regions in prediction models and enhance the accuracy of breeding value estimation. Tissue-specific expression patterns further support functionally annotated genomic selection models for precise breeding in Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire pigs.

The use of publicly available RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq datasets provided strong support for our integrative analyses, enabling a more comprehensive multi-omics investigation. Nevertheless, relying partly on these public data may introduce certain limitations and affect the generalizability of our findings. Future studies incorporating additional independently collected datasets or complementary experiments will help further validate and refine these results.

Conclusion

This study screened multiple high-confidence candidate genes by integrating multi-model GWAS data with gene differential expression analysis results, providing new genetic markers for growth-related traits in three pig breeds (Duroc, Landrace, and Yorkshire). Based on these candidate genes, breed-specific gene function association networks were constructed, providing a valuable reference for understanding the interactions and regulatory relationships among genes associated with growth traits in Duroc, Landrace, Yorkshire. Multi-omics analysis revealed a conserved candidate gene, ARL8A, providing a new marker site for cross-breed genetic improvement. Overall, this study reveals the genetic mechanisms underlying growth-related traits of three pig breeds, providing rich genetic resources and laying solid theoretical basis for molecular breeding and genetic improvement targeting important economic traits of pigs.
