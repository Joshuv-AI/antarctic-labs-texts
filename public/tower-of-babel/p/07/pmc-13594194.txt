Ppcd1 corneal dystrophy phenotypes are rescued by Ovol2 disruption and modified by genetic background in a mouse model
Authors: Anna L Shen, Alex C Veith, C Dustin Rubinstein, Kathy J Krentz, Edward Glover, Susan A Moran, Christopher A Bradfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterior polymorphous corneal dystrophy (PPCD) is a rare ocular disorder characterized by both genetic (PPCD1-4) and phenotypic heterogeneity. Genetic mapping and in vitro experiments provide evidence that OVOL2 promoter mutations are associated with human PPCD1, possibly through OVOL2 overexpression. A recent effort to engineer point mutations orthologous to those that induce human disease reported limited phenotypic effects, suggesting that current murine models may be of limited use in developing therapeutic approaches. Here, we provide evidence that, by manipulating the genetic background, a murine model can serve as a robust platform for studying gene-editing therapies for this disease. To support our idea, we employed a mouse model that exhibits PPCD-like phenotypes resulting from a chromosomal rearrangement in a region syntenic to the human PPCD1 locus. Observing that disease presentation is dependent upon a sensitive murine genetic background (DBA/2J), we then demonstrated that genetic disruption of Ovol2 rescues PPCD phenotypes, including corneal endothelial metaplasia, iridocorneal angle occlusion, elevated intraocular pressure, and retinal degeneration. These results support the model that Ovol2 upregulation drives PPCD1 in both humans and the mouse model. Moreover, studies on the genetic background provide opportunities to test novel therapeutic intervention strategies and provide evidence to support investigations into modifiers of human disease and their treatment.Subject terms: Diseases, Genetics, Medical research

Introduction

Human posterior polymorphous corneal dystrophy (PPCD) is an ocular disorder characterized by epithelialization of corneal endothelial cells1. Multiple subtypes of autosomal dominant human PPCD have been associated with mutations in genes of the OVOL2-ZEB1-GHRL2 regulatory network, which is involved in cell-state transitions2,3. Evidence from recent human studies suggests that PPCD1 arises from promoter mutations that result in the upregulation of OVOL2 expression, while PPCD3 arises from loss-of-function (LOF) mutations in ZEB1, and PPCD4 has been linked to promoter and noncoding mutations in GHRL24–7. An association between COL8A2, which has also been linked to Fuchs corneal endothelial dystrophy, and PPCD2 has been reported but not thoroughly characterized8,9. Despite autosomal dominant inheritance, a challenge in studying PPCD is that individuals with underlying mutations display phenotypic variability, suggesting the existence of genetic modifiers or environmental influences on disease presentation. These variable responses range from asymptomatic to clinically relevant corneal changes that can progress to glaucoma10.

We previously described a spontaneous mouse mutant arising from the R1 embryonic stem cell line that exhibited phenotypic characteristics of human PPCD. Evidence supporting our mouse model as representative of human PPCD was the presence of enlarged anterior chambers, multilayered epithelialized corneal endothelial cells exhibiting inappropriate cytokeratin expression, and proliferation of the epithelialized endothelium over the iridocorneal angle and posterior cornea, leading to elevated intraocular pressure and loss of retinal ganglion cells consistent with the development of glaucoma11–13. Corneal neovascularization, infiltration of F4/80-immunoreactive cells, and outer nuclear layer loss were also observed11,13. We determined that the genomic recombination events in these mice mapped to a region of mouse chromosome 2, syntenic to the region of human chromosome 20 that harbors the human PPCD1 locus. Therefore, we designated this chromosomal rearrangement the murine Ppcd1 locus11,12. Molecular analysis of the murine Ppcd1 locus on chromosome 2 indicated a recombination event adjacent to Ovol2, resulting in a 3.9 Mb inversion flanked by 81 Kbp and 542 bp deletions, and producing truncated versions of the genes Kat14 (formerly Csrp2bp) and Dzank1 12. Additional investigation by our laboratory found that haploinsufficiency of Kat14 and Dzank1 failed to recapitulate PPCD1-associated phenotypes12. In agreement with findings demonstrating that PPCD1 mutations lead to OVOL2 overexpression, we found that Ovol2 was overexpressed in the eyes of this mouse PPCD1 model6,12,14.

As monogenic diseases of the eye are ideal candidates for corrective gene therapy, the generation of an animal model of PPCD1 would allow us to develop targeted, possibly curative, therapeutics that overcome existing treatment limitations15,16. Despite strong evidence implicating OVOL2 mutations in human PPCD1, a recent study found that introducing orthologous promoter mutations into the mouse Ovol2 promoter increased Ovol2 gene expression but showed only limited replication of human disease phenotypes17. In this series of experiments, we demonstrate the importance of genetic background in disease presentation and validate the Ppcd1 mouse model as a tool for developing therapeutic approaches to treat this disease. To this end, we provide evidence that disruption of Ovol2 using gene-editing approaches can rescue PPCD1 disease phenotypes in mice.

Results and discussion

High-resolution mapping of the Ppcd1 chromosomal rearrangement

The murine Ppcd1 chromosomal rearrangement was previously identified as a 3.9 Mb inversion on mouse chromosome 2 (chr2) flanked by 81 Kb and 549 bp deletions. This results in the truncation of both Kat14 (previously known as Csrp2bp) and Dzank1, and the upregulation of the adjacent gene, Ovol212 . Using multiple long-read DNA sequencing approaches, we have confirmed and provided a more precise description of this genomic rearrangement (Fig. 1). Using this genomic sequencing data, we confirmed that the Ppcd1 chromosomal rearrangement generates novel alleles of Kat14 and Dzank1. We determined that the proximal end (closer to the centromere) of the chromosomal rearrangement begins at position chr2:144,239,742, and the distal end is at position chr2:148,168,466. The proximal deletion is ~ 79 Kb (Δ chr2:144,239,743 − 144,318,977), and the distal deletion is 534 bp (Δ chr2:148,167,932 − 148,168,465). The intervening sequence (chr2:144,318,978 − 148,167,931) is inverted between these two break points. It is important to note that these positions were defined by mapping reads to the C57BL/6J genome (GRCmm39), which is the most complete mouse reference genome at this time.

Genetic background influences Ppcd1 penetrance

We determined that genetic background plays a pivotal role in the penetrance of PPCD1 phenotypes in mice. The Ppcd1 mouse model originated from the R1 embryonic stem cell line (mixed 129 background) and was originally backcrossed to C57BL/6J (B6). The original description of the model was published after backcrossing to the DBA/2J (D2) background, resulting in a D2 congenic allele designated D2.129 (B6)-Ppcd1/J, hereafter referred to as Ppcd1, and has been maintained as a heterozygote on the D2 background for over 20 years13. Ppcd1 mice on the D2 background have consistently exhibited corneal endothelial cell epithelialization that completely occludes the iridocorneal angle, an enlarged anterior chamber, increased intraocular pressure (IOP), and retinal degeneration11,13. Backcrossing the Ppcd1 mice onto the B6 background attenuated these phenotypes (Fig. 2 ). B6D2F1 animals that harbored the Ppcd1 mutation did not exhibit elevated IOP (Fig. 2E). Intercrossing F1 animals produced a cohort of B6D2F2 Ppcd1 animals that exhibited significant variability in IOP measurements as compared to control B6D2F2 animals (F-test; P < 0.0001), despite similar average IOP values between the groups.

Continued backcrossing to the B6 background further attenuated the PPCD phenotypes (Fig. 2E & F). After six generations of backcrossing, we observed a decrease in the frequency and severity of iridocorneal angle occlusion, correlating with WT IOP levels (Fig. 2E). Moreover, these animals have reduced frequencies of central corneal metaplasia and neovascularization and attenuated retinal degeneration (Fig. 2F and I). Taken together, these observations suggest that the D2 background is sensitizing for expression of high IOP and severe PPCD1 pathology, while B6 is a repressive background.

These results indicate that genetic background strongly influences the penetrance of PPCD phenotypes in mice and may explain the variable penetrance of human PPCD and the limited disease presentation observed in recent attempts to create mouse models of human PPCD1 by introducing orthologous mutations at the Ovol2 promoter in C57BL/6J mice17. Our data suggest that genetic modifiers associated with genetic background differences between D2 and B6 have a significant influence on the PPCD phenotype. This is supported by the IOP data from B6D2F2 Ppcd1 animals, which show marked variability in disease presentation. Our results indicate that additional effort is needed to map modifier genes that mechanistically contribute to the eye pathologies observed in both mice and humans, as they may explain some of the variability reported in human populations18. Critically, our data suggest that attempts to recreate human phenotypes through Ovol2 promoter mutations in mice may be improved by considering genetic backgrounds (e.g., DBA) that influence the penetrance of PPCD-associated pathologies.

Targeting strategies to disrupt candidate genes

Based on other work and our description of the chromosomal rearrangement reported here, we initially identified three candidate genes, Kat14, Dzank1, and Ovol2, that may play a causal role in driving the PPCD phenotypes6,12. We determined that the chromosomal rearrangement in the Ppcd1 mouse removes the last four exons of Kat14 from the endogenous locus. The inversion event fused the Kat14 open reading frame to a sequence 4 Mb downstream, generating at least 5 novel fusion transcripts12. A similar phenomenon was observed at the Dzank1 locus. Here, the last two exons are lost, and fusion to downstream sequences generates at least 7 novel fusion transcripts12. While Ovol2 was not disrupted, the Ovol2 transcription start site is approximately 66 Kbp 5’ of the proximal deletion and inversion breakpoint and 37 Kbp 5’ of the Kat14 start site.

We used CRISPR-Cas9 to disrupt each candidate gene on the chromosome harboring the Ppcd1 mutation. While the Ppcd1 mutation is more penetrant on the D2 background, initial attempts to target D2.Ppcd1 embryos were unsuccessful. Therefore, Ppcd1 male animals (D2) were crossed to B6 females, resulting in B6D2F1 Ppcd1 embryos, which were used for CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. After genotyping, select founders were backcrossed to D2 for at least 6 generations before phenotypic characterization. Kat14 strategy

We used a two-step process to disrupt Kat14 on the Ppcd1 chromosome. Kat14 has two full-length protein-encoding isoforms that differ in their translational start sites: Kat14-201, a 779-amino-acid protein beginning in Exon 2, and Kat14-208, a 568-amino-acid protein with a translation start site in Exon 5. Our approach was designed to prevent the production of both the C-terminal lysine acetyltransferase domain (Exons 9–11) in both isoforms, as well as any unknown function(s) from the peptide generated by isoform Kat14-201 before the edit19. Targeting exon 2 by CRISPR-Cas9 produced an 8-base pair insertion coupled with a single-base pair deletion that would create a premature stop codon and a 29-amino acid peptide (Kat1463_64insATGACGCC, 81delG, L27*; Fig. 3A). We subsequently targeted exon 5 of Kat14, resulting in a single-base-pair insertion that introduced a frameshift, generating a 28-amino-acid peptide from the Kat14-208 translational start site (Kat1463_64insATGACGCC, 81delG, L27*; 645_646insA, E240*; Fig. 3B). The resulting allele was designated as Kat14FSPpcd1. The mouse line obtained from backcrossing to D2 was denoted as D2.B6-Kat14FSPpcd1/+ (hereafter referred to as Kat14FSPpcd1/+).

Dzank1 strategy

Dzank1 also produces two protein-encoding isoforms, Dzank1-201 and Dzank1-204, and disruption of exon 3 is sufficient to affect both. We generated a mouse with an 8-base pair deletion in exon 3 that would produce a 49-amino acid peptide lacking known functional Dzank1 domains (Dzank1131delTTACACCC, P52*; Fig. 3C)20. The resulting mouse allele was denoted as Dzank1FSPpcd1. The mouse line obtained from backcrossing to D2 was denoted as D2.B6-Dzank1FSPpcd1/+ (hereafter referred to as Dzank1FSPpcd1/+).

Ovol2 strategy

The murine Ovol2 gene encodes two protein-coding transcripts, Ovol2-201 and Ovol2-202, that differ by translational start sites in exon 1 or exon 2 and produce proteins of 274 or 241 amino acids, respectively21. Two guide RNAs were designed to introduce double-stranded breaks in exon 2, which would be sufficient to disrupt both isoforms (Fig. 3D). Following the gene-editing event, two founders were born that harbored heterogeneous biallelic mutations in the Ovol2 gene.

Founder 5849 harbored both an Ovol2R48Δ11, 259_260insG, C120* (hereafter referred to as Ovol2FSPpcd1) mutation on the same chromosome as the PPCD1 chromosomal rearrangement and an in-frame Ovol2G51Δ40 mutation on the wild-type chromosome. The Ovol2FSPpcd1 allele carries two mutations: a 30 bp in-frame deletion in Ovol2 exon 2, resulting in a 10 amino acid deletion spanning Arg48 through Cys57, and a single base insertion 86 bases downstream, leading to a frameshift at Glu87 and the generation of an early termination codon at residue 120. The frameshift mutation was present only on the Ppcd1 chromosome. The Ovol2G51Δ40 mutation consists of a 120 bp deletion, resulting in a 40-amino acid in-frame deletion spanning residues Gly51 through Asp90. Founder 5850, which contained the same in-frame Ovol2G51Δ40 mutation on the Ppcd1 mutant chromosome and an Ovol2G51Δ36 mutation, consisting of an in-frame 108-nucleotide deletion in Ovol2, on the wild-type chromosome. Given that Ovol2-null mice have been reported to be embryonic lethal, the presence of biallelic edits suggests that the in-frame (36 and 40-amino acid deletions) retain partial activity22. Therefore, only the Ovol2FSPpcd1 allele is characterized in this report. Crossing the Ovol2FSPpcd1 allele onto the D2 background for 6 generations generated the D2.B6-Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ mouse, with the Ovol2 frameshift mutation linked to the Ppcd1 mutation on one allele and the other as the wild type allele.

Disruption of Kat14 or Dzank1 does not rescue PPCD1 phenotypes

While we previously showed that haploinsufficiency of either Kat14 or Dzank1 failed to produce PPCD1-like phenotypes, it remained possible that the mutant gene products generated by the chromosomal rearrangement contribute to the pathogenic phenotypes through retained functional activity or as a gain-of-function12. Therefore, we disrupted Kat14 and Dzank1 on the Ppcd1 allele and evaluated each allele for PPCD1-like phenotypes. We found that disrupting either Kat14 or Dzank1 did not affect the key eye phenotypes observed in the Ppcd1 mouse (Fig. 4). In this regard, we readily observed enlarged eyes at an early age in both Kat14FSPpcd1/+ and Dzank1FSPpcd1/+ mice, a defined characteristic of our PPCD1 mouse model. We confirmed this histologically, as evidenced by enlarged anterior chambers (Fig. 4A) and substantial occlusion of the iridocorneal angle in both Kat14FSPpcd1/+ and Dzank1FSPpcd1/+ mice, mirroring the findings observed in the Ppcd1 mouse model (Fig. 4B). The corneas showed neovascularization, with evidence of corneal endothelial epithelialization (Fig. 4C). Finally, we found outer nuclear layer degeneration in Kat14FSPpcd1/+ mice consistent with our Ppcd1 mouse model (Fig. 4D). Therefore, we conclude that the truncated alleles or novel isoforms of these genes do not meaningfully contribute to the development of PPCD1.

Disruption of Ovol2 rescues PPCD phenotype

Gross and histological examination of Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ mice revealed that disruption of Ovol2 rescued the PPCD phenotypes (Fig. 5A-D). We previously reported that PPCD1 phenotypes can begin as early as postnatal day 5, with increased IOP evident at 3 months11–13. There was no evidence of enlarged anterior chambers or occlusion of the iridocorneal angles (Fig. 5A, B, & F). Accordingly, Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ mice exhibited WT IOP levels, as late as 8 months of age (Fig. 5E). We did not find any evidence of epithelialized corneal endothelial cells or iridocorneal angle occlusion in the Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ mice examined (Fig. 5G). Similarly, examination of the central cornea reveals normal corneal endothelium with an intact Descemet’s membrane and absence of corneal neovascularization in WT and Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ mice (Figs. 5G & H). Finally, the loss of Ovol2 also rescues the PPCD-associated retinal phenotypes. In this regard, the histology of Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ retinas is unchanged from that of wild-type retinas (Fig. 5D). The patchy loss of retinal ganglion cells and loss of outer nuclear layer (ONL) cells seen in Ppcd1 eyes is not observed in Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ eyes (Fig. 5D & F).

Normal angle morphology and IOP in Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ eyes beyond the typical window for PPCD phenotypes suggest that the pathological features were rescued rather than delayed. Our data demonstrate that disruption of Ovol2 on the Ppcd1 chromosome rescues the gross and histological changes associated with the PPCD1 phenotype and establishes Ovol2 as a driver of PPCD1 in mice. The position of the frameshift is predicted to maintain a significant portion of the N-terminus but disrupt the critical zinc finger DNA-binding domains, suggesting that the functional domains of OVOL2 contribute to the epithelialization of the corneal endothelium23.

As the Ppcd1 chromosomal rearrangement does not alter the Ovol2 gene structure, it is possible that alteration of a regulatory element by the rearrangement increases Ovol2 expression and underlies the development of PPCD1 in our mouse model. One possibility is that the Ovol2 promoter, ~ 35 Kbp from the Kat14 start site, overlaps the Kat14 promoter. We previously found that Kat14 is expressed in the corneal endothelium, and that both Kat14 and Ovol2 expression were upregulated in the eyes of Ppcd1 mice11,12. Given that Kat14 and Ovol2 are arranged in a head-to-head orientation, with 37 Kbp separating their respective start sites, there may be overlapping regulatory elements that drive the simultaneous upregulation of both genes. The disruption of Kat14 by the Ppcd1 chromosomal rearrangement may suggest that Kat14 haploinsufficiency drives genetic compensation to restore normal expression levels19,24. Alternatively, the chromosomal rearrangement may create novel regulatory elements that lead to the overexpression of Ovol2. Given that we have documented Kat14 expression in the eye and that overexpression of Ovol2 in other tissues can lead to neonatal/perinatal lethality, we hypothesize that this change in expression is limited to certain cell populations, such as the corneal endothelium12,25–27. Additionally, as Kat14 is expressed in the retinal pigment epithelium, we cannot yet exclude the possibility that Ovol2 is also expressed in those cells and responsible for the retinal phenotypes12. However, the findings presented here suggest therapeutic potential for targeting OVOL2 in human PPCD1 and warrant additional investigation.

Disruption of OVOL2 in Ppcd1 mice rescues the molecular characteristics of PPCD

The OVOL2 protein is part of the OVOL2-ZEB1-GHRL2 axis that regulates cell-state transitions, including in the cornea2,7,27,28. In the thymus, OVOL2 has been shown to maintain epithelial cell states via inhibition of lysine-specific histone demethylase 1 A (LSD1) and promotion of mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition has been proposed as the basis of PPCD22,28. Therefore, we determined whether disrupting Ovol2 in Ovol2FSPpcd/+ mice is sufficient to restore the OVOL2-ZEB1-GHRL2 axis and rescue the molecular changes observed in our PPCD1 mouse model.

To test the hypothesis that mesenchymal-epithelial transition due to disruption of the OVOL2-ZEB1-GHRL2 axis is occurring in Ppcd1 mice and reversed by Ovol2 disruption, we quantified expression of Zeb1 and Ghrl2 mRNA in neonatal eyes (Fig. 6). Although we were unable to detect changes in Zeb1 expression, we observed a ~ 2.8-fold increase in Ghrl2 expression in Ppcd1 mice, which was reversed by Ovol2 disruption. One possibility is that Zeb1 is also expressed in other cell populations in the eye, limiting our ability to detect changes in the corneal endothelium. However, upregulation of Ghrl2 in Ppcd1 mice and rescue in Ovol2FSPpcd/+ mice support the notion that dysregulation of the OVOL2-ZEB1-GHRL2 plays a role in the development of PPCD in our model.

We also examined expression of epithelialization markers and whether disruption of Ovol2 corrected their expression. The epithelial marker Cdh1 was increased ~ 1.9-fold in Ppcd1 eyes and attenuated with Ovol2 disruption29. Others have shown that OVOL2 activation leads to increased Cdh1 expression, supporting this observation28. Finally, we examined expression of the epithelial marker Krt16, which is normally expressed in corneal epithelial cells30. We detected a ~ 2.9-fold increase in Krt16 expression in Ppcd1 eyes that was rescued in Ovol2FSPpcd/+ animals. These results indicate that at both histological and molecular levels, disruption of Ovol2 in Ppcd1 mice is sufficient to rescue the altered epithelialization of the corneal endothelium that drives PPCD.

Conclusions

In this report, we detail how genetic background can significantly influence the presentation of PPCD1-associated phenotypes in mouse models. This critical finding reconciles our work with recent groundbreaking results from other laboratories that suggested a limited role for mouse models in the study of PPCD pathogenesis and the development of therapeutics. Our work suggests two important avenues for future investigation. First, given that we can rescue the PPCD phenotype by global downregulation of Ovol2, it will be interesting to determine whether downregulating Ovol2 in the corneal endothelium is sufficient to rescue PPCD1 and support somatic cell gene editing therapeutic strategies. Disruption of Ovol2 eliminates aberrant corneal endothelial epithelialization, at the histological and molecular level, rescuing changes in intraocular pressure and retinal degeneration, the most serious consequences of PPCD1. Second, the existence of genetic modifiers may explain the variable penetrance and progression observed in humans. Thus, this model could hold potential for identifying genetic modifiers in humans, which could be used to stratify patient populations by disease risk and optimize treatment and care options.

Materials and methods

Animals

All procedures conformed to the principles embodied in the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research (www.arvo.org), the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health, and in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee, School of Medicine and Public Health, University of Wisconsin-Madison (Protocol Number M005578). The DBA/2J (Strain # 000671) and C57BL/6J (Strain #000664) mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME. The Ppcd1 mouse has been previously described11,12. Regarding nomenclature, we adopted the rules outlined in the Jackson Laboratories guidelines, which state that backcrossing for 5 generations (N5) allows the use of congenic nomenclature. Animals were housed in groups of two to four in plastic cages with corncob bedding, on a 12-hour light: dark cycle. Animals were provided with lab chow (Mouse diet 9 F 5020; PMI Nutrition International) and water ad lib. Euthanasia was carried out with 100% CO2 at a flow rate of 3 L/min in an approximately 5.5 L chamber. CO2 was provided until animals lost consciousness and maintained for at least one minute after respiratory arrest. Death was verified by the absence of respiration and heartbeat before tissue collection or disposal.

Materials

The TaqMan Assays and Gene Expression Master Mix were obtained from Life Technologies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The following TaqMan Assays were used: Cdh1 – Mm01247357_m1, Grhl2 – Mm00518236_m1, Zeb1 – Mm00495564_m1, and Krt16 – Mm01306670_g1. The GoTaq polymerase was obtained from Promega (Madison, WI).

CRISPR targeting

The CRISPR-Cas9-mediated disruption of the target genes was carried out by the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology Center Advanced Genome Editing Laboratory (RRID: SCR_021070) and Animal Models Core (RRID: SCR_024797). F0 pups were screened by PCR and DNA sequencing. All animals were backcrossed to DBA/2J for at least 6 generations before characterization.

Genotyping

Tail DNA was isolated using the Puregene reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen) and stored in distilled water. Genotyping primers were designed using Primer3 software31,32. Genotyping for the PPCD1 allele has been described previously. Genotyping for Ovol2 was carried out using the oligonucleotides 5’-TGGCGCAGGGGACAGGTCAC-3’ and 5’-GTGTGGTTGAGTGTCGGGGTG-3’, using Promega GoTaq enzyme. Genotyping for Kat14 mutants was carried out using the oligonucleotides 5’-GGGCCCTGTCTTCTAAATTGC-3’ and 5’-TGTTGAGGGAAATCCCCACTC-3’ for exon 2, while exon 5 was genotyped by oligonucleotides 5’-GCCCTGTTTATTTCCGGTCA-3’ and 5’-ATTCAACCCCTGAGCCTGTG-3’. Genotyping for Dzank1 exon 3 mutants were carried out using the oligonucleotides 5’-CTTACTTAGAGACAGCAACAGC-3’ and 5’-GGTATACAAGGACTTGTTATTTC-3’. Products were visualized on agarose gels.

Gene expression

Animals were euthanized with CO2. Eyes were enucleated and placed in RNAlater (ThermoFisher Scientific). The optic nerve and excess fat and connective tissue were removed before homogenization in Qiazol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, DEU) and phase separation with chloroform. Total RNA was isolated using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen). Two-step RT-PCR was carried out using the Iscript cDNA synthesis kit (BioRad, Hercules, CA) and TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (ThermoFisher Scientific). Reactions were carried out on an ABI QuantStudio 7 Real-Time PCR instrument (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Intraocular pressure measurement

Intraocular pressure was determined using the TonoLab (TioLat, Helsinki, Finland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and as described previously13. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. All measurements were carried out between 1 and 3 PM. Both eyes of each animal were measured and counted as independent biological replicates. To minimize the number of animals used, control data (D2 and Ppcd1) include historical measurements reported in prior work13.

Histology

For histological characterization, animals were sacrificed by CO2 inhalation and enucleated eyes fixed overnight in 10% (v/v) formalin in phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.2, followed by dehydration in graded ethanol, and processing for routine paraffin-embedded light microscopy. The 5 mm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The ONL thickness was quantified in H&E sections that passed through both the pupil and the optic nerve and was measured at 50 μm intervals beginning at the optic nerve. Data values include two biological replicates per eye, obtained from both sides of the retina. Histological images were collected on an Axioskop using Zen Blue Software (Zeiss, Oberkochen, DEU). Low magnification images were collected using a 1.25x objective, while high magnification images were collected with a 40x objective. The camera adapter is 0.63x.

DNA sequencing

DNA sequencing, including Oxford Nanopore (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) and PacBio (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA), was carried out by the University of Wisconsin Biotechnology DNA Sequencing Core Facility (RRID: SCR_017759). High molecular weight DNA was collected from the blood of an Ovol2FSPpcd1/+ mouse. Reads were aligned to the GRCmm39 reference genome using Minimap2 v2.28-r1209 and merged using Samtools v1.9 or using the SeqMan software (DNASTAR, Inc. Madison, WI)33,34. Reads were visualized on the IGV web app35. For visualization in karyoploteR (v.1.33.0), the genome positions were lifted from GRCmm39 and converted to GRCmm10 using the UCSC genome browser lift genome annotations tools36.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism software version 10.5.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software (Boston, MA, www.graphpad.com). P values for the IOP data were determined using the one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc multiple comparisons. P values for the ONL data were determined by 2-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparisons. Some data groups appear in more than one figure (D2 WT and Ppcd1), but all groups were analyzed together to control for multiple comparisons. An F test was used to compare the variances of selected groups.
