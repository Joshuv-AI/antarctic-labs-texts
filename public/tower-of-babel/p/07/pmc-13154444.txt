Effect of different lung recruitment maneuvers on postoperative respiratory outcomes and lung aeration, assessed by lung ultrasound, in patients undergoing thoracoscopic sympathectomy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Dandan Chen, Yuqian Chen, Yanyu Wu, Xianzhong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtelectasis is a primary cause of impaired oxygenation and reduced respiratory compliance during general anesthesia. Recruitment maneuvers (RMs) can effectively counteract atelectasis; however, the optimal protocol regarding pressure, timing, and assessment method remains undefined. Therefore, this study utilized lung ultrasound (LUS) to evaluate the effectiveness of different RM pressures combined with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) in reducing atelectasis and improving oxygenation in surgical patients.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, assessor-blinded, 2 × 2 factorial trial enrolled adult patients undergoing elective thoracoscopic bilateral sympathectomy. Participants were randomized to one of four groups according to recruitment maneuver peak airway pressure (Ppeak 30 or 40 cmH₂O) and post-recruitment PEEP (0 or 8 cmH₂O): A (30/0), B (40/0), C (30/8), and D (40/8). A standardized recruitment maneuver (15 s) at the assigned Ppeak was performed after completion of the right- and left-sided procedures. After the second maneuver, patients were ventilated in synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) with the assigned PEEP until extubation. Primary outcomes were total LUS score and the incidence of postoperative atelectasis (defined as any region with LUS score ≥ 2), assessed in the PACU 30 min after extubation.ResultsAmong the 116 analyzed patients, the incidence of LUS-assessed postoperative atelectasis decreased across groups: 58.62% in Group A (17/29), 42.86% in Group B (12/28), 35.48% in Group C (11/31), and 21.43% in Group D (6/28). Compared with Group A, Group D had a significantly lower incidence (P = 0.009). Group A also had the highest LUS scores, which were significantly higher than those in each of the other three groups (all P < 0.05).ConclusionsA recruitment pressure of 40 cmH₂O, applied at the end of thoracoscopic bilateral sympathectomy, was superior to 30 cmH₂O in reducing both the LUS score and the rate of atelectasis. Moreover, applying 8 cmH₂O PEEP from recruitment until extubation significantly lowered LUS scores compared to no PEEP.Trial registrationISRCTN31424042; retrospectively registered on 6 February 2026.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03790-3.Keywords: Lung recruitment maneuvers, Positive end-expiratory pressure, Atelectasis, Recruitment pressure, Lung ultrasound score

Background

Atelectasis, characterized by alveolar collapse and reduced lung volume, is a primary cause of impaired oxygenation and decreased respiratory compliance during general anesthesia. Without PEEP [1], approximately 90% of anesthetized patients develop atelectasis, a phenomenon universally confirmed by computed tomography (CT) studies [2]. The clinical spectrum of atelectasis ranges from asymptomatic conditions to severe cases requiring prolonged oxygen support, mechanical ventilation, or even progressing to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [3].

Thoracic surgery may involve substantial alterations in lung mechanics. Even when lung isolation is not applied, pleural opening and general anesthesia can promote atelectasis and impair oxygenation and respiratory compliance. Atelectasis can lead to pulmonary infection, regional hypoxia, lung inflammation, and microvascular injury [4]. Patients with atelectasis exhibit elevations in key pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α [5]; these elevations correlate with the duration of atelectasis and may increase susceptibility to complications after thoracic procedures [5]. RMs have been shown to effectively reinflate collapsed alveoli, prevent atelectasis related to low-tidal-volume ventilation, and improve oxygenation and lung compliance. Substantial evidence [6–10] supports their utility in reversing postoperative atelectasis and reducing respiratory complications across various surgical settings.

CT is the reference standard for diagnosing atelectasis, but its use is limited by radiation exposure and logistical constraints. In contrast, LUS is a portable, radiation-free bedside tool with good diagnostic performance and has gained increasing use in perioperative and critical care settings. In this trial, we used LUS to quantify perioperative lung aeration changes and postoperative atelectasis after thoracoscopic sympathectomy, and to evaluate the effects of different recruitment maneuvers and PEEP strategies.

Methods

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study (Approval No. MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2021]482) was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. Consecutive eligible patients who underwent thoracoscopic bilateral sympathectomy at this institution between January and December 2022 were enrolled, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered at ISRCTN (ISRCTN31424042) and was retrospectively registered on 6 February 2026.

Study population

We included patients who met the following criteria: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I or II, age ≥ 18 years, body mass index (BMI) < 28 kg/m², no history of cardiothoracic surgery or pulmonary disease, and a preoperative chest CT scan without significant abnormalities. Patients were excluded for the following reasons: a preoperative Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score > 2, failure to complete the LUS examination, or conversion to an alternative surgical approach.

Sample size was calculated for this 2 × 2 factorial trial using the total LUS score at 30 min after extubation in the PACU as the primary continuous outcome. With a two-sided α of 0.05, 90% power, and a standard deviation (σ) of 1, we assumed mean LUS scores of 5.25 versus 4.58 for PEEP 0 versus 8 cmH₂O, and 5.50 versus 4.22 for recruitment pressures of 30 versus 40 cmH₂O, allowing for a possible interaction. Therefore, 24 participants per group (96 total) were required. To account for an anticipated 5% dropout/missing LUS assessment rate, we increased the target enrollment to 26 participants per group (104 total).

Randomization

An independent statistician generated a computer-generated random allocation sequence (1:1:1:1) using Randomization.com. The sequence was printed and placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. After enrollment, a nurse not involved in outcome assessment opened the next envelope immediately before induction to assign the intervention. The postoperative LUS assessor and the statistician were blinded to group allocation; the attending anesthesiologist performing the recruitment maneuver could not be blinded.

Anesthesia procedures and interventions

General anesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam (0.05 mg/kg) and maintained using target-controlled infusions (TCI) of propofol (plasma target 3.0 µg/mL) and remifentanil (5.0 ng/mL). After endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation was initiated with a FiO₂ of 60% at a fresh gas flow of 2 L/min. Ventilation was managed using a tidal volume of 6–7 mL/kg of predicted body weight (PBW) and a respiratory rate of 12–16 breaths/min with an I: E ratio of 1:2; the respiratory rate was titrated to maintain PetCO₂ at 35–45 mmHg. PBW was calculated as follows: for males, height (cm) − 105; for females, (height (cm) − 100) × 0.85. Anesthetic depth was guided by hemodynamics and Bispectral Index (BIS) monitoring. To reduce postoperative nausea and vomiting, patients received tropisetron (5 mg) five minutes before the end of surgery. Flurbiprofen axetil (1 mg/kg) was administered as part of multimodal analgesia to reduce postoperative pain and opioid requirement. After extubation, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) and monitored routinely. For moderate pain (VAS ≥ 4), sufentanil 0.1 µg/kg was administered as needed until the VAS score fell below 4.

All procedures were performed under two-lung ventilation via a standard single-lumen endotracheal tube. Lung isolation and one-lung ventilation were not used in our institution for thoracoscopic sympathectomy. Ventilation settings were standardized as described above, and rescue measures for intraoperative hypoxemia included verifying airway patency, increasing FiO₂, and adjusting ventilation parameters as clinically indicated.

Immediately after completion of the right-sided procedure, a standardized RM was delivered via the anesthesia circuit. The adjustable pressure-limiting (APL) valve was set to the assigned target airway pressure (30 or 40 cmH₂O) and the airway pressure was sustained at the target for 15 s under continuous airway pressure monitoring on the anesthesia machine. Mechanical ventilation was then resumed. An identical RM was performed after completion of the left-sided procedure. Thereafter, group-specific PEEP (0 or 8 cmH₂O) was applied and ventilation was switched to SIMV until extubation (Fig. 1). The RM was terminated immediately if hemodynamic instability occurred.

Group-specific protocols were as follows: Group A, RM 30 cmH₂O with PEEP 0 cmH₂O; Group B, RM 40 cmH₂O with PEEP 0 cmH₂O; Group C, RM 30 cmH₂O with PEEP 8 cmH₂O; Group D, RM 40 cmH₂O with PEEP 8 cmH₂O. PEEP (0 or 8 cmH₂O) was applied from completion of the second RM until extubation.

Lung ultrasonography

In the PACU, all LUS examinations were performed by a trained anesthesiologist who was blinded to group allocation. LUS was performed 30 min after extubation in the PACU (T4). A standardized 12-region scanning protocol was employed using a 2–5 MHz convex probe. The chest wall was mapped by dividing each hemithorax into three zones (anterior, lateral, and posterior) using the anterior and posterior axillary lines as borders. Each zone was further subdivided into superior and inferior segments, resulting in 12 regions for assessment (see Supplementary Figure S1). Each region was assigned a score from 0 to 3 based on a predefined aeration grading system [11]: 0 (normal aeration with fewer than three B-lines), 1 (three or more well-spaced B-lines), 2 (coalescent B-lines), and 3 (lung consolidation).

Outcomes

The primary endpoints were the total LUS score and the occurrence of atelectasis. The total LUS score (range, 0–36) was calculated as the sum of all regional scores, with higher scores indicating worse lung aeration. Atelectasis was defined as any region with an LUS score ≥ 2 [12].

No changes were made to the methods or outcomes after trial commencement.

Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic parameters, respiratory mechanics, and arterial blood gas results. These were measured at specified intervals: T0 (pre-induction), T1 (post-intubation), T2 (immediately after the second RM), T3 (5 min after the second RM), and T4 (30 min after extubation in the PACU). Hypoxemia in the PACU was predefined as SpO₂ <92% lasting ≥ 1 min. The incidence of postoperative chest tightness was recorded due to its potential to compromise respiratory effort and thus affect gas exchange, a known consideration following sympathectomy. Postoperative pain intensity was quantified using the VAS upon discharge from the PACU. Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) were assessed based on chest radiographs obtained on postoperative day 1, in accordance with established consensus criteria [13, 14].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) or R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality of continuous variables was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR] as appropriate; categorical data as n (%). For the 2 × 2 factorial design, main effects of recruitment pressure (30 vs. 40 cmH₂O), PEEP (0 vs. 8 cmH₂O), and their interaction were evaluated using two-way ANOVA for continuous outcomes and logistic regression with an interaction term for binary outcomes (atelectasis). When the interaction term was not significant, results were reported as main effects; otherwise, simple effects were explored. Pre-specified pairwise comparisons among the four groups were adjusted using Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No interim analyses or stopping guidelines were planned.

Results

The trial ended after the planned sample size had been reached; there was no early stopping or termination.

Subject characteristics

From an initial assessment of 133 subjects, 12 were excluded: 10 for not fulfilling the inclusion criteria (mainly age < 18 years) and 2 for withdrawing consent. The randomization of the 121 patients resulted in four study groups. We subsequently excluded five patients from analysis: three due to conversion to an alternative surgical approach and two owing to incomplete LUS datasets. Consequently, 116 patients completed the study and constituted the final per-protocol population (Fig. 2).

Postoperative outcomes

The incidence of hypoxemia during the PACU stay did not differ significantly among the four groups (P = 0.534), with rates of 34.48%, 28.57%, 25.81%, and 25.00% in Groups A, B, C, and D, respectively. Similarly, there were no overall differences in postoperative VAS scores or the incidence of chest tightness (P > 0.05). Furthermore, follow-up chest radiographs at 24 h revealed no pulmonary complications in any of the groups (Supplementary Table 2).

Independent factors and predictive model for atelectasis

To identify independent predictors of impaired lung aeration, we performed univariate followed by multivariate linear regression analyses, incorporating variables including age, sex, BMI, VAS score, chest tightness, PEEP application, and recruitment pressure. The multivariate model identified both PEEP application (β = -1.325) and a recruitment pressure of 40 cmH₂O (β = -0.938) as factors independently associated with lower LUS scores. However, no significant interaction was found between PEEP and recruitment pressure in the factorial design analysis. A recruitment pressure of 40 cmH₂O also significantly reduced the odds of atelectasis compared to 30 cmH₂O (OR = 0.365, 95% CI: 0.167–0.802; P = 0.012) (Supplementary Tables 3–7).

Using these two significant predictors, we developed a clinical prediction nomogram. To evaluate the model’s predictive accuracy for atelectasis risk, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.663 (95% CI: 0.561–0.764) (see Supplementary Figures S4 and S5).

Discussion

Given that atelectasis is a common cause of perioperative hypoxemia and PPCs [15, 16], we used LUS for detection. Although CT is the reference standard, it is impractical for routine bedside assessment [17]. Several studies suggest that LUS may detect perioperative atelectasis with higher sensitivity than chest radiography in certain settings [18]. Nevertheless, chest radiography remains the routine postoperative imaging modality in many centers because it is widely available and less operator-dependent; therefore, PPCs on postoperative day 1 were assessed using chest radiographs according to consensus criteria [13, 14]. The elevated LUS scores in posterior regions reflect the known gravity-dependent pattern of atelectasis. This is consistent with findings in supine gynecological surgery (worst scores posterior-inferior) and reports of anterior atelectasis in prone positioning. This consistent pattern confirms atelectasis as a gravity-dependent phenomenon leading to dependent airway collapse. RMs use transient increases in airway pressure to reopen collapsed alveoli, thereby improving respiratory mechanics and oxygenation [19], but the optimal recruitment pressure remains uncertain. Based on prior studies [20–22], we compared a peak pressure of 30 versus 40 cmH₂O. A peak pressure of 40 cmH₂O resulted in lower LUS scores and a lower incidence of atelectasis. This finding is physiologically plausible: general anesthesia promotes atelectasis [23], and Laplace’s law (P = 2γ/r) implies that higher pressure is required to open small, collapsed alveoli. In addition, applying PEEP is important to prevent derecruitment, which can occur even in healthy lungs without it [24]. In our study, 8 cmH₂O PEEP significantly lowered LUS scores compared with zero PEEP, consistent with evidence that moderate PEEP can optimize perioperative outcomes [25]. Mechanistically, PEEP helps maintain alveolar patency above closing pressure, increases functional residual capacity, and improves compliance and gas exchange, thereby supporting oxygenation and reducing atelectasis risk. Recruitment pressure and PEEP act at different phases—recruitment opens alveoli, whereas PEEP helps keep them open—which may explain the absence of a significant interaction in our factorial analysis.

Despite effectively reducing LUS scores, atelectasis, and improving compliance, the combination of 40 cmH₂O recruitment and 8 cmH₂O PEEP only transiently improved the PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio (significant at 5 min only). This likely reflects the maneuver’s short-lived impact against mild atelectasis. Nonetheless, even this brief improvement may be clinically valuable for high-risk patients in mitigating early postoperative hypoxemia.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not obtain preoperative LUS measurements; however, preoperative CT suggested comparable baseline lung status. Second, LUS assessments were performed by a single trained examiner (blinded to group allocation), which improves internal consistency but may introduce observer bias, and inter-observer agreement was not formally evaluated. Third, the fixed PEEP level (8 cmH₂O) may not be optimal for all patients. Fourth, our findings are derived from a low-risk cohort and require validation in higher-risk populations. Fifth, the single-center design and short hospital stay limited follow-up to the immediate postoperative period and precluded evaluation of longer-term outcomes. Finally, the trial was registered retrospectively, which may raise concerns regarding transparency; however, no changes were made to the prespecified methods or outcomes after trial commencement.

Conclusions

In thoracoscopic bilateral sympathectomy, a recruitment pressure of 40 cmH₂O was more effective than 30 cmH₂O in reducing LUS scores and atelectasis incidence, whereas 8 cmH₂O PEEP was superior to zero PEEP. Their combination provided the greatest benefit, optimally improving dynamic compliance and yielding a transient post-expansion boost in oxygenation.
