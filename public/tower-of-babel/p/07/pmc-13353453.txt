Integrative profiling of oral fungal communities across Mycobacterium Tuberculosis burden groups in Xpert-positive patients
Authors: Yilin Wang, Rui Li, Zihan Tang, Zichun Ma, Xiaowei Dong, Wei Shu, Junwei Cui, Mingchong Wei, Zubi Liu, Dan Shen, Liang Li, Yu Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a major global health burden. A growing body of evidence suggests that mucosal microbial communities may reflect or modulate host responses during infection. However, the oral microbiome in TB patients with different bacterial loads remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to investigate alterations in oral fungal communities among Xpert-positive patients, stratified by Mtb burden based on Xpert MTB/RIF testing.MethodsIn this prospective multicenter cohort study (May-August 2024), 278 Xpert-positive TB patients were enrolled across five hospitals in China. Participants were stratified into high, medium, low, and very low Mtb burden groups according to Xpert MTB/RIF cycle threshold values. Paired sputum and tongue swab samples were collected. Oral fungal profiles were characterized via ITS sequencing, followed by taxonomic assignment, diversity analysis, and multivariable association testing (MaAsLin 2) to identify robust biomarkers.ResultsOral fungal community structure varied significantly across Mtb burden strata. Beta-diversity analysis revealed distinct clustering between the very low burden group and higher burden groups (high, medium, low). High-burden patients were enriched with environmental taxa such as Blumeria and Toxicocladosporium, whereas low-burden groups exhibited higher abundances of Candida, Aspergillus, and Penicillium. Notably, MaAsLin 2 analysis confirmed that Penicillium and Podosphaera were independently associated with lower Mtb burden after adjusting for confounders. Neutral model analysis indicated that stochastic processes predominantly drive the assembly of these oral fungal communities. Functional prediction suggested enhanced aerobic respiration and metabolic enzyme activity in high-burden groups.ConclusionsThis study analyzed the oral fungal microbiome stratified by Mtb burden strata in Xpert-positive patients, revealing distinct shifts in fungal composition and functional potential. Fungal dysbiosis and altered microbial metabolic capacity may offer insight into host-microbe interactions in pulmonary TB (PTB). These findings underscore the potential value of fungal microbiome profiling for assessing Mtb burden, beyond its application in TB diagnosis alone.Keywords: Tuberculosis, mtb burden, ITS sequencing, MaAsLin 2, neutral model, functional prediction

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains one of the most pressing global public health challenges. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2025, an estimated 10.7 million new TB cases and 1.23 million TB-related deaths occurred worldwide in 2024, reflecting continued increases in both morbidity and mortality [1]. TB primarily affects the lungs, and its diagnosis traditionally relies on sputum-based methods. Conventional assays, including sputum smear microscopy and mycobacterial culture, remain widely utilized and continue to serve as reference standards. In addition, the WHO has endorsed rapid molecular diagnostic tests such as Xpert MTB/RIF (Xpert) and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (Xpert Ultra) from Cepheid which enable simultaneously detection of Mtb complex (MTBC) and rifampicin resistance [2,3]. Despite these advances, conventional sputum-based diagnostics have notable limitations in certain populations unable to expectorate sputum-including children, the elderly, and patients with paucibacillary TB (PTB). To address this gap, recent studies have demonstrated that oral swab-based molecular assays show comparable sensitivity and specificity to sputum-based Xpert testing, highlighting a promising non-invasive diagnostic alternative [4–6]. Nonetheless, variability in sample quality, microbial interference, and host–pathogen interactions continues to complicate TB detection.

The human oral cavity harbors the second most abundant microbial community, surpassed only by the gut. According to the expanded Human Oral Microbiome Database (eHOMD), more than 700 bacterial species inhabit this environment, with dominant genera including Actinomyces, Fusobacterium, Haemophilus, Neisseria, Prevotella and Veillonella. This complex ecosystem plays a crucial role in maintaining oral and systemic health by contributing to immune modulation, nutrient metabolism, and colonization resistance against pathogens [7]. Accumulating evidence indicates that oral microbiota plays a significant role in respiratory disease pathogenesis. During influenza infection, commensal bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa secrete neuraminidase that enhances viral cellular transmission, while respiratory viral infections concurrently increase susceptibility to colonization by Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis [8,9]. In chronic respiratory disorders, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, microbiota dysbiosis has been implicated in exacerbating airway inflammation [10]. Specifically in COPD, smoking predominantly influences bacterial community composition (Tetropica, Actinomycetes and Prevotella), whereas fungal communities show stronger associations with ambient environmental exposures such as particulate matter (PM2.5), exemplified by increases in Cladosporium species [10,11]. Notably, severe COVID-19 has been associated with reduced pulmonary fungal diversity and increased Candida colonization [12].

TB also involves complex interactions with respiratory microbiota. Significant differences in bacterial composition have been reported between TB lesions and normal lung tissue, although no consistent correlation between microbial profiles and drug resistance patterns has been established [13]. Comparative studies have revealed markedly altered oral bacterial richness, diversity, and composition in TB patients relative to healthy controls, with significant enrichment of Streptococcus and Rhodococcus in the oral cavities of affected individuals [14].

However, despite accumulating evidence that Mtb infection substantially remodels bacterial communities in both oral and pulmonary environments, the dynamics of oral fungal communities during Mtb infection remain poorly characterized. To address this knowledge gap, the present study characterizes oral fungal community structure across a spectrum of Mtb burdens. By integrating mycobiome profiling with clinical parameters, we aim to elucidate the ecological interactions between Mtb infection and the oral mycobiome, thereby providing new insights into host-pathogen–microbiome dynamics in TB.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants enrollment

This study enrolled 278 patients with positive Xpert MTB/RIF testing results between May 2024 and August 2024, representing a spectrum of Mtb infection burdens. Oral swab samples were collected from all participants for Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) sequencing, enabling detailed profiling of the oral mycobiome across different stages of Mtb infection. Patient recruitment was conducted across five designated TB sentinel hospitals in China. To minimize confounding effects, individuals were excluded if they (i) had received anti-TB treatment within the past 12 months, (ii) had used antibiotics in the two weeks prior to enrollment, (iii) were unable or unwilling to provide written informed consent, or (iv) were diagnosed with HIV infection, including HIV-TB co-infection or HIV mono-infection. Notably, only body weight data were collected during participant enrollment, while height measurements were not recorded; therefore, body mass index (BMI/IMC) could not be calculated for the study cohort. All participants were informed of the study objectives and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committees of all participating centers and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400088076).

Sample collection

To minimize confounding from recent oral activities, participants were instructed to abstain from tooth brushing, mouth rinsing, and food or water intake for at least four hours prior to sample collection. All samples were collected on-site at hospital clinics rather than at participants’ homes, following a standardized published sampling protocol described in a previous study [15]. Two specimen types were collected: tongue swabs and sputum. For tongue swab specimen, two sterile swabs were firmly rubbed against the lateral surfaces of the posterior three-quarters of the tongue dorsum for approximately 20 s each. After collection, swabs were immediately transferred into 1.5 mL sterile microcentrifuge tubes prefilled with lysis buffer for subsequent genomic DNA extraction and ITS amplicon sequencing [16]. For sputum collection, participants expectorated deep pulmonary sputum directly into a sterile 3 mL specimen container without prior mouth washing. All samples were temporarily stored at 2–8 °C and processed within 96 h for downstream testing, including sputum culture, Xpert MTB/RIF molecular detection, and fungal ITS amplicon sequencing.

DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Sputum culture and Xpert MTB/RIF testing were conducted following established protocols [16]. Oral genomic DNA was extracted using a standardized commercial kit-based protocol. The Internal Transcribed Spacer 1 (ITS1) region was then amplified by PCR with primers 1737 F (5′-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′) and 2043 R (5′-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′) [17,18]. The amplification products were purified, quantified, and used for library construction, which included end-repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and size selection. Final libraries were quality-checked and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using 250-bp paired-end chemistry according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Bioinformatics processing

Quality control and ASVs denoising

The raw ITS amplicon sequencing data were initially processed with FLASH (v1.2.11) [19]. This step demultiplexed samples based on unique barcode sequences, excised conserved ITS primer regions, and assembled overlapping paired-end reads into full-length consensus amplicons. Post-assembly adapter removal was performed via Cutadapt (v2.10) [20], and rigorous quality filtering was executed using Fastp (v2.10) [21] using standardized read quality cutoffs: reads containing over 20% low-quality bases (Phred score < 20), reads shorter than 200 bp, and sequences with ambiguous N bases exceeding 5% of total length were discarded outright. All retained high-quality clean reads were imported into QIIME 2 (v2024.2) [22] for downstream taxonomic and ecological analyses. The DADA2 plugin [23] was employed for data denoising to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), achieving single-nucleotide resolution to distinguish genuine biological sequences and automatically eliminating bimeric and trimeric chimeric artifacts.

Prior to alpha and beta diversity profiling, read rarefaction was conducted to normalize uneven sequencing depth across samples: the rarefaction depth was set to 10,000 clean reads per sample, determined as the minimum sufficient sequencing depth that captured greater than 98% of ASV richness based on rarefaction curve saturation assessment. For sample exclusion criteria, samples failing two quantitative thresholds were removed from subsequent analyses: (i) samples yielding fewer than 10,000 post-filter clean reads after Fastp and DADA2 processing; (ii) samples with rarefaction curves failing to reach saturation at the preset rarefaction depth, indicating insufficient sequencing coverage to recover the majority of fungal community diversity.

Species annotation and diversity analysis

The ASVs were taxonomically annotated against the UNITE reference database (version 8.2) to characterize the oral fungal community composition [24]. Taxonomic assignments were provided at seven levels: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. For diversity analyses, high-quality sequences were aligned with MAFFT (v7.505) [25], and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree2 (v2.1.11) [26]. Alpha- and beta-diversity were analyzed within the QIIME 2 framework. Alpha-diversity indices, including the Shannon index, Evenness index, and Faith phylogenetic diversity (Faith-PD) index, were calculated to evaluate fungal community diversity and richness. For beta-diversity analysis, the Bray-Curtis distance matrix was generated from the ASV abundance table, and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed using the cmdscale function in R to visualize community structural variation, with 95% confidence ellipses illustrating group clustering trends. To statistically compare the overall fungal community composition across Mtb burden groups (very low, low, medium, high), we performed permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using the adonis2 function in the vegan package (version 2.6-4) with 9,999 permutations. When significant differences were detected (p < 0.05), pairwise PERMANOVA was conducted for post-hoc comparisons, and p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Additionally, we assessed homogeneity of multivariate dispersions (PERMDISP) using the betadisper function followed by ANOVA to confirm that observed differences were not driven by unequal within-group variances.

Analysis of differential fungal and prediction of functional enrichment

To identify fungal biomarkers associated with Mtb burden, we employed a multi-step approach. First, differential abundance of oral fungal taxa across Mtb burden groups was assessed using LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) within the R package microeco (v1.16.0) [27],with a threshold of LDA score greater than 2.5 and p < 0.05 to identify significantly enriched biomarker taxa. To identify fungal taxa most discriminative of Mtb burden levels, a random forest classification model was built using the randomForest package (v4.7-1.2) [28]. To ensure the robustness of these associations against potential confounders, we performed multivariable association analysis using MaAsLin 2 [29] (Microbiome Multivariable Association with Linear Models). The model included Mtb burden groups as the main fixed effect, with sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, cerebrovascular disease, and diabetes status included as covariates to adjust for potential confounding effects.

Community assembly and functional prediction

Beyond taxonomic abundance, the relative influence of stochastic processes on community assembly was evaluated by fitting a neutral community model with the MicEco package (v0.10.0) [30], which estimates the migration rate (m) and the goodness-of-fit (R-squared) between observed and predicted frequency distributions. Finally, putative fungal functional profiles were inferred using FUNGuild (v1.1) and PICRUSt2 (v2.5.0) to compare ecological guilds and metabolic potential across groups [31,32].

Statistical analysis

For statistical comparisons, non-parametric methods were applied to both diversity analyses and taxon abundance assessments due to non-normally distributed data. The Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test was used to identify overall significant differences among multiple groups, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for post-hoc inter-group comparisons. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was employed to adjust p-values for multiple testing and control the false discovery rate. All bioinformatics and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) with the packages ggplot2, vegan, picante, and ggpubr [33–35].

Results

Participants and clinical characteristics

A total of 278 patients who underwent ITS amplicon sequencing were included in the final analysis. Detailed demographic, clinical, and laboratory information was collected and stratified according to sputum Xpert MTB/RIF-determined Mtb burden (Table 1). The cohort had a mean age of 57 years, with 51.80% aged 18–60 years. Male accounted for 75.18% (n = 209) of the overall cohort,with the highest proportion observed in the high Mtb burden group (83.16%). Histories of smoking and alcohol consumption were more frequently reported among patients in the high and medium Mtb burden categories. All patients exhibited generally low body weight, with a mean of approximately 56 kg. Clinically, the most common symptoms were fever, cough, hemoptysis, and night sweating-consistent with classic TB manifestations. Cough was the predominant symptom, present in 246 patients (88.49%). Fever and fatigue were reported in over one-third of the cohort. Among complications, diabetes was the most frequent (n = 72, 25.90%), with the highest prevalence observed in the high Mtb burden group.

Oral fungal community richness and diversity

To assess the association between oral fungal microbiota and Mtb burden, alpha- and beta-diversity metrics were analyzed across four Xpert-defined burden groups. Significant differences in fungal richness and phylogenetic diversity were observed. Specifically, patients with very low Mtb burden group showed significantly greater Shannon diversity, higher community evenness and Faith-PD (p < 0.05) compared to the median Mtb burden group (Figure 1A–C). Furthermore, Faith-PD index was also significantly higher in the very low Mtb burden group relative to the high Mtb burden group, while no significant differences were detected among other inter-group comparisons. Based on unweighted UniFrac distances, PCoA revealed clear separation in fungal community structure among the four groups (Figure 1D). Specifically, the high, medium, and low Mtb burden groups formed distinct clusters that were significantly differentiated from the very low Mtb burden group (Figure 1E). Collectively, these findings indicate that increasing Mtb burden is associated with marked alterations in both fungal community richness (alpha-diversity) and overall composition (beta-diversity).

Oral fungal community composition and differential fungal genera

Following quality control and denoising procedures, a total of 13,253 ASVs were identified. Samples encompassed 15 fungal phyla, and the top 10 genera across all groups included Blumeria, Candida, Parachaetomium, Oligophagozyma, Aspergillus, Penicillium, Pichia, Malassezia, Fusarium, and Podospora (Figure 2A). To identify fungal biomarkers associated with Mtb burden, we initially employed LEfSe and Random Forest analyses. LEfSe indicated that Blumeria was enriched in the high Mtb burden group, Fusarium predominated in the medium group, while Aspergillus and Penicillium were characteristic of the low Mtb burden group (Figure 2B). Random forest analysis further identified Aspergillus, Mortierella, and Penicillium as the most discriminant genera across the four groups based on mean decrease in accuracy (Figure 2C).

Standard differential abundance analysis of the top six fungal genera initially showed that Parachaetomium, Aspergillus, and Penicillium were significantly more abundant in the very low Mtb burden group compared to the high Mtb burden group (p < 0.05). Additionally, Oligophagozyma and Penicillium were enriched in the very low group versus the medium group. However, to distinguish robust associations from potential confounding effects, we performed multivariable analysis using MaAsLin 2. After controlling for these covariates, the associations for Parachaetomium, and Oligophagozyma were no longer significant. In contrast, Aspergillus and Penicillium remained significantly associated with Mtb burden levels (Figure 2D). Concurrently, we visualized the top 24 fungal genera ranked by the absolute value of regression coefficients alongside their distribution across samples in each group (Figure 2E). The analysis revealed that multiple fungal genera exhibited significant linear associations with Mtb burden. Notably, Penicillium and Podosphaera displayed the strongest negative correlation trends (indicated by dark blue coefficient bars), with their abundances dropping to nearly undetectable levels in the high-burden group.

Functional guild classification and neutral model analysis

Fungal functional profiles were predicted using FUNGuild analysis (Figure 3A). The predominant functional guilds across all groups were plant pathogens, animal pathogens, endophytes, plant saprotrophs, and wood saprotrophs. Notably, plant pathogens were significantly enriched in both high and very low Mtb burden groups, whereas animal pathogens, endophytes, plant saprotrophs and wood saprotrophs showed higher relative abundances in the low Mtb burden group. Trophic mode analysis revealed significant differences in fungal ecological strategies (Figure 3B). The proportion of pathotrophs was significantly lower in the low Mtb burden group compared to the very low group. Conversely, Saprotrophs and symbiotrophs were significantly enriched in both medium and low Mtb burden groups relative to the very low group. To assess the relative contribution of stochastic versus deterministic processes in shaping fungal communities across Mtb burden, we fitted a neutral community model to the four groups. The prediction results indicate the migration rates were consistently low across all groups (m < 0.01), while R-squared ranged from 0.431 to 0.704 (Figure 3C).

Predicted pathway involvement and enzyme prediction

PICRUSt2-based analysis predicted putative metabolic signatures associated with Mtb burden (Figure 4A). Specifically, pathways involved in guanosine nucleotide degradation II, glycolysis III (from glucose), and aerobic respiration II (cytochrome c) were predicted to be significantly enriched in the high and medium Mtb burden groups compared to low and very low groups. These groups were also predicted to exhibit elevated relative abundances in (yeast) aerobic respiration I (cytochrome c) and CDP-diacylglycerol biosynthesis I. Conversely, the low and very low Mtb burden groups were predicted to show significantly higher abundances in sucrose degradation III (sucrose invertase), phospholipid remodeling (phosphatidylethanolamine), NAD/NADP-NADH/NADPH cytosolic interconversion (yeast), and monoacylglycerol metabolism (yeast) pathways compared to high and medium groups (Figure 4B). Analysis of predicted enzyme abundances suggested distinct patterns across Mtb burden groups (Figure 4C). Adenosine triphosphatase, DNA-directed RNA polymerase, glucan 1,4-alpha-glucosidase, and histone acetyltransferase were predicted to be most abundant in the high Mtb burden group. L-arabinose isomerase and NADPH:quinone reductase showed maximal predicted abundance in the medium Mtb burden group, while glutathione transferase and NAD(+) ADP-ribosyltransferase were predicted to be significantly enriched in the very low Mtb burden group.

Discussion

In this multicenter cohort study, we performed high-resolution profiling of the oral mycobiome in patients clinically suspected of tuberculosis and demonstrated that oral fungal communities exhibit distinct ecological patterns across gradients of Mtb burden. Stratification using Xpert MTB/RIF semi-quantitative grading revealed significant differences in community diversity, structure, taxonomic composition, and predicted functional capacity. These findings underscore a strong association between Mtb burden and oral fungal ecology, suggesting that active TB infection may reshape the oral niche in a burden-dependent manner.

At the compositional level, we observed marked shifts in dominant fungal taxa correlated with disease severity. Patients with high Mtb burden exhibited a distinct enrichment of Blumeria, Zygosporium, and Toxicocladosporium, whereas those with low burden were characterized by higher abundances of Candida, Aspergillus, and Penicillium (Figure 2). The enrichment of Candida, Aspergillus, and Penicillium in the low-burden group presents an intriguing ecological pattern. While overgrowth of these genera is widely linked to immunosuppression and epithelial barrier disruption in advanced PTB [36,37]. Their high prevalence in the low-burden cohort suggests that these microbes constitute a stable commensal community that becomes perturbed with disease progression. Notably, multivariable analysis (MaAsLin 2) confirmed Penicillium as a robust biomarker for lower burden levels even after adjusting for confounders, underscoring its potential role as an indicator of a less dysbiotic lung environment.

Regarding the enrichment of Blumeria, Zygosporium, and Toxicocladosporium in the high-burden group, we interpret these findings with caution. Although standardized sampling and processing procedures were applied, extraction- or PCR-negative controls were not included; therefore, these environmental taxa should be interpreted cautiously. These taxa are primarily associated with plant or environmental sources and are rarely reported as resident oral microbiota. Their detection may reflect increased susceptibility to environmental inhalation in immunocompromised hosts or the persistence of low-biomass background taxa within the altered pulmonary niche of advanced TB [38]. Nevertheless, their significant differential abundance-validated by both LEfSe and Random Forest analyses-warrants further investigation into whether environmental fungi serve as sentinel markers of severe disease progression.

Neutral community modeling revealed a strong fit between observed and predicted species distributions (R-squared > 0.4, m < 0.01; Figure 3C), consistent with previous ecological studies of PTB patients [39]. This goodness-of-fit suggests that stochastic processes, particularly ecological drift and random birth-death events, may predominantly govern the assembly of oral fungal communities in TB patients. The exceptionally low migration parameter (m < 0.01) indicates limited dispersal of fungal taxa between the oral cavity and external environments, potentially reflecting the semi-isolated nature of the oral niche during active TB infection. The strong neutral model fit further implies that selective pressures exerted by varying Mtb burden groups may have relatively modest effects on fungal community composition compared to random ecological processes – a pattern previously documented in other host-associated microbiomes during chronic inflammatory conditions [40–43].

In silico functional predictions suggested stratified predicted metabolic potentials across Mtb burden groups. Pathways related to aerobic respiration I and II were predicted to be enriched in high Mtb burden patients. This pattern is consistent with a hypothesis of an increased genetic capacity for aerobic respiration in the oral mycobiome under high inflammatory or immune-activation states, although direct evidence of metabolic activity is lacking. These predicted trends could hypothetically be related to the expansion of metabolically flexible taxa such as Candida, which are known to thrive in oxidative and inflammatory environments [44–48]. However, all functional inferences presented here are computational predictions; validation through metatranscriptomic or metabolomic analyses is required before concluding actual functional changes.

Similarly, in the predicted enzyme class analysis, the predicted relative abundance of glucan 1,4-alpha-glucosidase and DNA-directed RNA polymerase decreased with lower Mtb burden groups. According to functional databases, glucan 1,4-alpha-glucosidase is involved in carbohydrate metabolism [49] and could hypothetically support microbial energy acquisition under inflammatory conditions if the predicted abundance reflects true enzymatic capacity. DNA-directed RNA polymerase is annotated as essential for bacterial transcription and proliferation (Figure 4C) [50]. Their predicted decline in low-burden individuals may suggest a reduced predicted genetic capacity for metabolic and transcriptional functions in a less inflamed oral environment, but this should not be interpreted as direct evidence of decreased metabolic activity or transcription rates.

Taken together, these findings indicate that increasing Mtb burden is associated with reduced fungal richness, distinct alterations in taxonomic composition, and substantial remodeling of predicted metabolic functions. The combined taxonomic, ecological, and functional evidence suggests that TB-associated host immune responses may create selective pressures that favor certain opportunistic or stress-tolerant fungi while suppressing others, though stochastic ecological processes remain the primary drivers of overall community structure.

This study has several limitations. First, while our analyses suggest that the composition of oral fungal communities appears to be largely shaped by stochastic (random) processes, the underlying mechanisms driving these ecological dynamics remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation. Second, the lack of a healthy or non-TB group precludes comparison with baseline oral microbial states. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as burden-associated alterations rather than TB-specific fungal dysbiosis. Third, while predictive tools such as FUNGuild and PICRUSt2 provide valuable functional insights, they infer rather than directly measure gene activity, which may limit the depth of biological interpretation. Fourth, potential confounding factors such as diet, oral hygiene, and clinical comorbidities were not fully controlled and could influence microbial composition. Fifth, we did not include extraction or PCR negative controls in the sequencing run, which are necessary to definitively identify reagent or environmental contamination. Therefore, the detection of rare environmental genera such as Blumeria and Toxicocladosporium should be interpreted with extreme caution. It is possible that their presence reflects low-level contamination rather than genuine oral colonization. We have chosen to retain these taxa in the analysis for completeness but refrain from any clinical or mechanistic interpretation. Future studies incorporating negative controls and systematic decontamination pipelines are needed to validate whether these taxa are truly associated with Mtb burden.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that Mtb burden levels are associated with distinct alterations in the oral fungal communities among Xpert-positive patients. The integration of microbial taxonomic and functional profiles with Xpert MTB/RIF semi-quantitative grading reveals novel insights into host-microbe interactions linked to bacterial load. These findings lay the groundwork for future mechanistic and longitudinal studies aimed at understanding causal relationships between host immune status, fungal ecology, and Mtb burden, and highlight the potential of the oral mycobiome as a non-invasive biomarker for disease severity or burden stratification.
