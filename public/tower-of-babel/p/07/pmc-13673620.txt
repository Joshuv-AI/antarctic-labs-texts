The association between adiposity and cardiac function in patients with early breast cancer receiving cardiotoxic therapies
Authors: Gabriel Aleixo, Wei Ting Hwang, Rakesh Sharma, Wonyoung Jung, Congying Xia, Vivek Narayan, Yehoda M Martei, Ramy Sedhom, Walter Witschey, Bonnie Ky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnthracyclines and HER2-directed therapies improve survival in early breast cancer but increase cardiovascular risk, which body mass index poorly characterizes. We evaluated longitudinal associations between pre-treatment CT-derived body composition and echocardiography-derived systolic and diastolic function in a prospective cohort of women with early breast cancer.MethodsIn a prospective cohort of women with stage I–III early breast cancer treated with anthracyclines and/or trastuzumab, skeletal muscle index (SMI), muscle density (SMD), and visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue area and density (VAT, SAT, VATD, SATD) were quantified from pre-treatment CT or PET-CT at L3 using automated segmentation. Longitudinal changes in LVEF and E/e′ were analyzed using multivariable-adjusted GEE models. Time to incident systolic dysfunction (≥ 10% LVEF decline to < 50%) and diastolic dysfunction (E/e′ ≥ 14) were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsAcross 177 participants (median age 50 years, 36.2% HER2-positive, 19.9% triple-negative), 145 (81.9%) received anthracycline-based therapy. Baseline body composition was not associated with longitudinal LVEF change. Higher baseline VAT (β 0.6, 95% CI 0.2–0.9; p = 0.001) and VAT/SAT ratio (β 0.5, 95% CI 0.2–0.7; p < 0.001) were associated with increased E/e′ over time, while higher VATD (β − 0.4, 95% CI − 0.6 to − 0.1; p = 0.002) was associated with decreased E/e′ during follow-up. No associations were observed for SAT or SATD.ConclusionsPre-treatment visceral adiposity is associated with worsening diastolic function during breast cancer therapy. CT-derived body composition may refine cardiotoxicity risk assessment beyond BMI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02350-4.Keywords: Breast cancer, Cardiotoxicity, Body composition, Visceral adiposity, Diastolic dysfunction, Cardio-oncology

Introduction

Highly effective breast cancer therapies, including anthracyclines, HER2-directed, and radiation therapies, are widely used and have resulted in substantial improvements in oncologic survival. However, these treatments are also associated with cardiotoxic effects that increasingly affect a growing population of over 3.8 million survivors of breast cancer [1]. As survivorship increases, cardiovascular (CV) disease has emerged as a major competing cause of morbidity and mortality [2]. In large population-based cohort studies of patients with early-stage breast cancer (n = 98,999), the risk of CV-related death exceeded cancer-related mortality by 10 years after diagnosis among patients older than 65 years, and CV mortality has been shown to surpass breast cancer mortality in this population [3]. Therefore, evaluating factors associated with longitudinal changes in cardiac function following potentially cardiotoxic therapy is important to improve surveillance, minimize treatment disruptions, and reduce long-term CV morbidity in patients with early breast cancer (EBC) [2].

Body composition abnormalities are important determinants of cardiometabolic health and CV risk [4]. However, measures such as body mass index (BMI) cannot distinguish lean mass from adiposity or characterize fat distribution and thus provides a poor assessment of body composition abnormalities [5–8]. In contrast, segmented body composition assessment using CT imaging, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and bioelectrical impedance analysis (Fig. 1) can quantify skeletal muscle and adipose tissue compartments, enabling a more comprehensive and detailed evaluation of body composition and supporting a more individualized approach to understanding CV health [8].

Body composition has been associated with heart failure (HF) and CV disease in the general population. Sarcopenia, or low muscle mass, is present in up to 66% of patients with HF, and in community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults without established CVD, sarcopenia is associated with a 33% higher risk of incident CVD compared with non-sarcopenic controls [9, 10]. In HF, risk factors such as obesity, aging, and inactivity impair energy metabolism and reduce metabolic flexibility, leading to skeletal muscle dysfunction and fat deposits inside the muscles, termed myosteatosis. Myosteatosis has been associated to a 53% higher risk of systolic cardiac dysfunction in older adults [11]. Visceral adiposity is another key driver of cardiometabolic disease and confers up to a tenfold higher odds of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia [12, 13]. Moreover, a higher visceral-to-subcutaneous adipose tissue ratio (VAT/SAT) is a robust marker of adverse fat distribution and metabolic syndrome and is associated with increased risk of CV disease independent of BMI [14]. Mechanistically, the adipokine hypothesis proposes that visceral adipose tissue functions as an active endocrine organ, releasing mediators including leptin, aldosterone, and neprilysin that drive systemic inflammation and adverse cardiac remodeling, supporting a biological basis for adiposity as a contributor to HFpEF independent of body weight [15, 16].

Although a detailed understanding of body composition may be particularly relevant for patients exposed to potentially cardiotoxic cancer therapies, there are limited data evaluating these relationships [17–19]. To date, only two small studies have evaluated associations between body composition and cardiac function during anthracycline treatment, suggesting that sarcopenia confers an approximately threefold higher risk of developing cardiotoxicity and that greater intermuscular fat accumulation over treatment tracks with marked LVEF decline; however, neither study evaluated the full range of body composition measures, diastolic function was not examined, and both were limited by small, single-center designs [20, 21].

To address this knowledge gap, we leveraged a prospective cohort of women with EBC treated with potentially cardiotoxic therapy to comprehensively evaluate the associations between pre-treatment body composition, and longitudinal changes in cardiac function and incident risk of systolic and diastolic dysfunction.

Methods

Study design

Participants were derived from a prospective longitudinal cohort study, the Penn Cardiotoxicity of Cancer Therapy (NCT01173341) conducted at the Abramson Cancer Center at the University of Pennsylvania. Briefly, eligible patients were 18 years of age or older at the time of breast cancer diagnosis, had histologically confirmed stage I-III breast cancer, and received treatment with doxorubicin, trastuzumab, or a combination of these therapies. As part of this cohort study, patients had a baseline transthoracic echocardiogram, and follow-up echocardiograms at standardized time intervals [22]. For inclusion in this subcohort analysis, patients were additionally required to have abdominal CT or PET CT imaging performed between breast cancer diagnosis and initiation of chemotherapy. These were obtained as part of standard of care, and were utilized for body composition assessment in this analysis. The study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania institutional review board and all patients provided written informed consent.

Data collection

Clinical data

Clinical and treatment data were collected prospectively using a standardized protocol, from both the patient’s electronic health records and patient-administered questionnaires. Cancer related variables including systemic therapy, radiation therapy, surgical treatment, tumor stage, and hormone receptor status; and CV risk factors including hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, and BMI were collected at baseline and standardized time intervals.

Body composition

Body composition data were derived from de-identified abdominal imaging and analyzed at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3). Totalsegmentator, a machine learning segmentation model, was used to automatically segment L3 and four tissue types (subcutaneous fat labeled in red, skeletal muscle labeled in blue, intra-muscular adipose tissue labeled in yellow, and visceral fat labeled in green in Fig. 1) [23]. Manual qualitative check was performed for the final tissue masks. Skeletal muscle index (SMI), a measure of muscle mass, was calculated as skeletal muscle cross-sectional area (cm2) divided by height squared (m2); higher values indicate greater muscle mass (Fig. 1, blue shaded region). Skeletal muscle density (SMD), measured in Hounsfield units (HU), reflects variation in muscle attenuation and serves as an estimate of intramuscular fat infiltration (myosteatosis), where higher values indicate better muscle quality with less fat infiltration [24]. Adiposity was characterized using visceral adipose tissue (VAT, shown in green in Fig. 1) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT, shown in red in Fig. 1) areas (cm2) as measures of fat quantity, and visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue density (VATD and SATD, HU) as measures of fat quality, with lower attenuation indicating greater lipid content within adipocytes [25]. The visceral-to-subcutaneous adipose tissue ratio (VAT/SAT) was additionally derived as an index of relative fat distribution.

Quantitative echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiograms were performed in an ICAEL-accredited laboratory using GE Vivid ultrasound systems. Baseline and follow up studies were then analyzed by experienced sonographers blinded to all patient characteristics at the Penn Center for Quantitative Echocardiography where intra observer coefficients of variation have previously been reported as 4.4% for LVEF and 2.3–5.4% for mitral inflow and tissue Doppler velocities [26]. Quantitative echocardiographic outcomes included systolic function using left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) measured by the Simpson’s method and diastolic function, as defined using E over e prime ratio (E/e′), a commonly used noninvasive surrogate of left ventricular filling pressures, which represents the ratio of early mitral inflow velocity (E) measured by pulsed wave Doppler across the mitral valve to early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′) measured by tissue Doppler imaging [27].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to baseline body composition, including SMI, SMD, VAT, VATD SAT, SATD and VAT/SAT ratio. All continuous body composition measures were analyzed as standardized variables and were modeled per 1 standard deviation (SD) increase to allow for comparability across metrics and consistent interpretation of effect estimates.

Linear regression with generalized estimating equations (GEE) using a robust variance estimator to account for within-participant correlation was used to evaluate the association between body composition measures and longitudinal changes from baseline in LVEF and E/e′. In these multivariable models, time since therapy initiation was modeled using cubic splines to capture nonlinear trends [28]. Time and body composition were included as main effects; thus, the estimated coefficients for body composition variables represent time-adjusted associations with longitudinal changes in echocardiographic measures. Predicted changes were derived from multivariable GEE models and plotted over time at ± 1 SD from the mean of baseline body composition variables, with continuous covariates held at their mean values and categorical covariates held at reference levels. Additional potential confounders were selected based on their association with body composition and outcomes, and a priori hypotheses regarding their clinical relevance. These included: age, BMI, cancer therapy regimen [doxorubicin, doxorubicin with trastuzumab, and trastuzumab without doxorubicin], baseline echocardiogram measure, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, radiation therapy (modeled as a time-varying covariate) and history of stroke or heart failure. Wald tests were used to test associations between baseline body composition and echocardiographic change. To evaluate the potential impact of selection bias arising from the requirement for baseline CT imaging, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using inverse probability weighting (IPW). Stabilized weights were derived from a logistic regression model predicting study inclusion based on cancer stage, triple-negative status, chemotherapy regimen, radiation therapy, estrogen receptor status, and surgical type, and were trimmed at the 99th percentile. Weighted GEE models were fit using the same covariates and spline specification as the primary analysis for longitudinal change in LVEF and E/e′. Cox models were not weighted given the limited number of events.

Time-to-event analyses using Cox proportional hazards regression models were conducted to examine the association between baseline body composition measures and the risk of subsequent cardiac dysfunction. Systolic dysfunction was defined as a ≥ 10% absolute decline in LVEF from baseline to an absolute value < 50%. Diastolic dysfunction was defined as an E/e′ ratio ≥ 14, indicative of elevated filling pressures [29, 30]. Baseline body composition parameters (SMI, SMD, VAT, VATD, SAT, SATD and VAT/SAT) were modeled as standardized continuous variables. Multivariable models were fit separately for each body composition measure to avoid overparameterization and adjusted for a parsimonious set of variables given limited event rate. These included age, BMI, and cancer therapy regimen.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18, and a two-sided p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This subcohort study consisted of 177 patients with evaluable baseline CT imaging and longitudinal echocardiographic follow up (Supplemental Fig. 1). Excluded patients were similar to the included patients (Supplemental Table 1).

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 50 years (Q1-Q3 41–60), and 112 (63.3%) patients were White. Tumor characteristics included HER2-positive disease in 64 (36.2%), estrogen receptor–positive disease in 112 (63.3%), and triple-negative breast cancer in 35 (19.9%). Anthracycline-based therapy was administered to 145 patients (81.9%), including 111 (62.7%) anthracycline alone and 34 (19.2%) anthracycline plus trastuzumab. The remaining 32 (18.1%) received trastuzumab without anthracyclines. Radiation therapy was administered in 136 (76.8%), and surgical management included mastectomy in 103 (58.5%) patients. Baseline comorbidities included hypertension in 54 (30.5%), hyperlipidemia in 43 (24.3%), and diabetes in 12 (6.8%). Patients had median EF 57.4% (Q1-Q3 53.6–60.2%) and E/e’ median was 7.2 (Q1-Q3 5.9–8.7).

In multivariable-adjusted GEE models, baseline body composition measures were associated with the average change in E/e′ during follow-up (Table 3). Higher SMI, reflective of muscle mass, was associated with an increase in E/e′ (β 0.4, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.8; p = 0.009). In contrast, higher SMD, was associated with a decrease in E/e′ (β − 0.3, 95% CI − 0.6 to − 0.03; p = 0.03), consistent with better muscle quality tracking with more favorable diastolic function.

Among adiposity measures, higher VAT was associated with increased E/e′ during follow-up (β 0.6, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.9; p = 0.001), while higher VATD was associated with decreased E/e′ (β − 0.4, 95% CI − 0.6 to − 0.1; p = 0.002). A higher VAT/SAT ratio was also associated with a greater E/e′ (β 0.5, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.7; p < 0.001). There were no associations for SAT or SATD, and BMI was not associated with change in E/e.

In sensitivity analyses using IPW to account for the probability of study inclusion, associations between VAT, VATD, VAT/SAT ratio, SMI, and SMD with longitudinal change in E/e′ remained consistent in direction and statistical significance with the primary analysis (Supplemental Table 2). No body composition measure was associated with longitudinal LVEF change in weighted models, consistent with the primary analysis (Supplemental Table 3).

Associations between baseline body composition and incident systolic and diastolic dysfunction

The median pre-treatment LVEF was 57.4% (IQR 53.6–60.2%). There were 34 participants who experienced systolic dysfunction, defined by a ≥ 10% decline in LVEF from baseline to < 50%, that occurred at a median 0.59 years (0.35–1.23). In adjusted Cox models evaluating time to systolic cardiac dysfunction, no body composition measures were significantly associated with the outcome (all p > 0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

In cancer survivors treated with cardiotoxic therapies, CV disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality [31]. In this study, we observed that worse adiposity markers (high VAT, low VATD, myosteatosis [low SMD]) prior to cancer therapy initiation were associated with longitudinal changes in diastolic function (E/e′, E/e′ > 14) in women with EBC treated with potentially cardiotoxic therapies. Our data indicate that body composition is a potentially modifiable CV risk factor and may represent a target for future interventions in this population.

In this study, we carefully assessed the diastolic parameter E/e′ that, when progressively impaired, precedes and is indicative of HFpEF [16, 29]. The consistent associations between adiposity and diastolic function observed in our study extend prior observations in the general population without cancer linking adiposity to impaired diastolic function and provide cancer-specific data. The concept that obesity may be the primary driver of diastolic dysfunction has existed over the past several years in the general population, as it is hypothesized that adipocyte derived hormonal signaling molecules (e.g. leptin, aldosterone, neprilysin) play an important role in HFpEF pathogenesis, although data are lacking in cardio-oncology [15, 32]. Visceral adiposity is associated with systemic inflammation, maladaptive renal effects that increase left ventricular filling, and adverse cardiac remodeling (including hypertrophy, microvascular dysfunction, and myocardial fibrosis) that impair the left ventricle’s ability to tolerate increased filling, that all contribute to HFpEF pathogenesis [16]. While HFpEF is being increasingly recognized as a cause of exercise intolerance in breast cancer survivors and a potential cause of morbidity, our study further adds to the body of literature in cardio-oncology connecting adiposity, HFpEF, cancer therapy exposure and breast cancer [17, 33].

The associations between adiposity with both longitudinal diastolic function as well as incident diastolic dysfunction remained independent of multiple common comorbidities that have been implicated in the pathogenesis of diastolic dysfunction, including hypertension and hyperlipidemia [34]. Notably, VAT and VAT/SAT ratio were not associated with E/e′ cross-sectionally at baseline but were associated with longitudinal changes in E/e′ during follow-up, suggesting that visceral adiposity was not simply reflecting pre-existing diastolic impairment.

Our associations were also independent of BMI. Although BMI is widely used in clinical practice as a surrogate for adiposity, it is very much influenced by lean mass and bone mass and does not provide detailed information about body composition or fat distribution (Fig. 1). More granular measures of adiposity may provide added value to our assessment of CV disease in cancer. For instance, obesity determined by BMI is present in about 60–70% of patients with HFpEF, while central adiposity (waist to height ratio > 0.5) is present in > 95% of those patients [32, 35, 36]. In our study the absence of associations between BMI and cardiac measures, contrasted with the consistent associations observed with VAT, suggests that BMI may not adequately capture the pathophysiologically relevant aspects of adipose tissue. The null BMI finding is therefore unlikely to be a product of how patients were selected into this analysis. Our findings support the hypothesis that detailed characterization of adipose distribution, beyond simple overall body mass, provides additional insights into the relationships with cardiac structure and function.

In women, adipose tissue constitutes a larger proportion of total body weight, as compared to men [37]. Besides the use of cardiotoxic medications, that by itself can lead to cardiac dysfunction and changes in body composition, women with breast cancer can receive aromatase inhibitors, which by impairing estrogen effects, may further alter body composition by increasing fat mass and reducing skeletal muscle mass, while also contributing to increased CV risk [38–40]. These sex-specific physiologic differences, and the treatment related changes in body composition, suggest that longitudinal changes in adiposity warrant further investigation as a potential contributor to CV disease risk in women with breast cancer.

Visceral adiposity may represent a modifiable risk factor, with potential targets to reduce it. Pharmacologic approaches such as incretin mimetics have been shown to reduce VAT and the VAT to SAT ratio [41, 42]. Increasingly, body composition, and specifically the reduction of visceral adiposity, has become a focus of newer therapeutic strategies. In a recent trial of the obesity medication bimagrumab, a type II activin receptor antibody, investigators evaluated not only weight loss but also changes in VAT and skeletal muscle mass. In this study, the combination of bimagrumab with semaglutide reduced VAT by a striking 58.2% at week 72 [43]. Another approach is structured exercise programs, which favorably modify body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness over time, and have demonstrated improvements in oncologic outcomes, including mortality [44–46].

In contrast to prior work showing sarcopenia was associated with worse cardiac outcomes in anthracycline-treated patients, higher muscle mass in our cohort was associated with worse diastolic function both at baseline and over time [21]. Although we cannot exclude the possibility of Type I error, we also note that this result should not be interpreted in isolation, as higher VAT, lower VATD, and a higher VAT/SAT ratio were each associated with greater adjusted average change in E/e′ during follow-up, independent of BMI and traditional CV risk factors. It is possible that the positive SMI association, which was unexpected, may reflect unmeasured confounding. It may be that patients with higher SMI have higher cardiac output and higher E velocity and E/e’ without true diastolic impairment, or patients with higher SMI may also have more advanced disease.

Our study also has several limitations. The study was conducted at a quaternary academic medical center, which may differ from community oncology settings. Further studies should include more patients from community sites to expand the external validity of the results. The post hoc nature of the analysis resulted in a proportion of patients excluded as they did not have baseline CTs. The requirement for available CT imaging may have resulted in preferential inclusion of patients with more advanced or clinically complex disease, as CT scans are not routinely obtained in all patients with EBC. The treatment regimen assignment was not randomized and was driven by tumor characteristics including stage, receptor status, and HER2 expression, which may also correlate with baseline body composition; residual confounding from treatment indication cannot be excluded despite adjustment for chemotherapy regimen in multivariable models. The time-to-event associations between visceral adiposity measures and incident diastolic dysfunction were limited by a small number of events (n = 14), resulting in wide confidence intervals spanning twofold to threefold ranges that preclude definitive inference without external validation. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted with caution pending replication in larger cohorts. We relied on E/e′ ≥ 14 as the sole parameter to define diastolic dysfunction; current guidelines recommend a multi-parameter approach to define diastolic function grade including left atrial volume index and tricuspid regurgitation velocity, and our use of E/e′ alone may have resulted in misclassification of diastolic function status. Similarly, given the small sample size and the natural history of HFpEF, which may take years to develop, we were not able to evaluate clinical HFpEF.

Our study also has multiple strengths, including the detailed phenotypic characterization of both adiposity measures and cardiac function, analysis blinded to all patient characteristics, comprehensive statistical analysis with consideration for multiple potential confounders, including radiation therapy. We also note that the automated assessment of adiposity measures offers a potential practical pathway for routine screening and identification of high-risk individuals in clinical practice.

Conclusions

Higher adiposity was associated with worse diastolic function and increased risk of diastolic dysfunction in patients receiving cardiotoxic therapy. Body composition assessment may improve risk stratification and guide future preventive strategies in early breast cancer.
