Skin immunity and inflammation: cellular interactions and communication
Authors: Divyaa Narayanan, Seen Ling Sim, Snehlata Kumari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe skin is a vital organ that protects organisms from external insults, providing a barrier to the outside environment. The cellular system of the skin consists of highly specialised immune and non‐immune cells, which are equipped to respond promptly, providing effective immunity and restoring tissue homeostasis. Well‐coordinated cellular communication involves cells and soluble factors, which are essential for maintaining the balance and protecting the skin from inflammatory skin diseases, impaired tissue repair and recurring infections. In this review, we will focus on the recent development of understanding how cellular networks orchestrate skin immunity via a coordinated communication system of soluble factors and their signalling pathways, with a focus on psoriasis as a model of inflammatory skin disease.Keywords: adaptive immunity, cellular communication, cytokines, epithelial cells, inflammatory skin diseases, psoriasis

Skin components and skin immunity

The structure of the skin consists of three main layers: the epidermis, dermis and hypodermis. It also contains appendages such as hair follicles, sweat glands and sebaceous glands (Figure 1). 1 , 2 The distribution and function of skin cells are influenced by skin architecture, body site variations, skin pigmentation and ageing, which help facilitate their coordinated response to biological processes (e.g. tissue repair), external insults (e.g. infections, UV damage) or uncontrolled reactions (e.g. inflammatory skin diseases). 3 , 4

Illustration of cellular communications and networks during skin homeostasis and inflammation. During skin homeostasis, epidermal and dermal cells engage in healthy communication to maintain barrier integrity and surveillance. During skin inflammation, keratinocyte‐derived cytokines, chemokines, and alarmins recruit and activate non‐immune and immune cells, which promote Th1/Th17 responses. Th17 responses then further promote recruitment of immune cells and amplify cytokine and chemokine release. This dysregulated cellular communication sustains an inflammatory loop leading to chronic skin conditions, such as psoriasis.

The epidermis is the outermost layer of the skin and is mainly composed of epithelial cells known as keratinocytes. The highly organised epithelial components not only provide host defence through junctional architecture but also mount immune responses through the expression of several innate immune receptors such as Toll‐like receptors (TLRs) and secretion of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs). 5 They can recognise pathogens or danger signals and drive host responses by secreting inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. 6 , 7 The epidermis also contains innate and adaptive immune cells such as Langerhans cells (LCs), CD8+ T cells, melanocytes and γδ T cells, which ensure the readiness of the immune responses against external insults. 1 , 8

The dermis is a fibrous layer of connective tissue and serves as the home to most skin‐resident immune cells during homeostasis, such as myeloid cells (e.g. dermal dendritic cells and macrophages) and T cells (e.g. T helper (Th)1, Th17 and CD8) (Figure 1). 1 The hypodermis primarily consists of adipose tissue and attaches the skin to the underlying muscles and bones. 9 The adipose tissue contains adipocytes that secrete adipokines such as leptin, adiponectin and resistin, which influence both local and systemic immune responses. 10 Within the hypodermis, hypodermal macrophages maintain extracellular matrix integrity by producing insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1). 11 The dermis further accommodates appendages such as blood vessels, sensory receptors, sweat glands, hair follicles, lymphatic vessels and sebaceous glands. 1 , 8 These appendages play crucial roles in how homeostasis is maintained. For example, hair follicles are immune‐rich areas that are regarded as epicentres for the initiation of immune responses. 12 They support the maintenance and survival of tissue‐resident memory (TRMs) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the skin and enable their retention in the upper regions of the hair follicles adjacent to keratinocytes through the production of interleukin (IL)‐17 and IL‐15. 12 Additionally, areas of the skin that are thicker or contain densely populated hair follicles show an abundance of CD8+ TRMs. 12 , 13 , 14 Hair follicle blockage and rupture events (follicular occlusion) have been associated with the aetiology of many skin conditions, including Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS). 15 Additional appendages also play important roles in the regulation of skin homeostasis; however, as these are beyond the scope of this review, further information can be found in other literature. 5 , 16

Environmental factors and biological ageing processes contribute to low‐grade inflammation of the skin (inflammaging), which is coordinated through various cell types, including keratinocytes, fibroblasts, as well as innate and adaptive immune cells. 17 , 18 For example, increased infiltration of LCs was observed in UV‐exposed areas compared with UV‐protected skin sites. 19 Functional deterioration of LCs and TRMs in the ageing skin has also been linked to reduced immune surveillance, leading to an increased risk of infections. 20 , 21 UV exposure also modulates melanocytes, innate cells with the capacity to moderate local immune diversity and provide photoprotection as well as serve as a first line of defence during infection. 22 , 23

The innate immune system in the skin consists of myeloid cells, such as epidermal LCs, dermal dendritic cells (DCs), neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages and innate leukocytes, including mast cells, natural killer (NK) cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), basophils and eosinophils. 24 These cells are crucial as they are involved in phagocytosis, antigen presentation and secretion of pro‐inflammatory factors such as IL‐23, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and IL‐6 to initiate, amplify and prolong the T‐cell immune response. 25 , 26

The adaptive immune system consists of immune effector lymphoid cells, such as T cells. These cells are crucial for pathogen clearance and the secretion of soluble factors, demonstrating a high degree of specificity in their immune responses. This specificity contributes to immunological memory and the prevention of disease recurrence. Both the innate and adaptive immune systems are derived from the haematopoietic lineage. 2 , 25 , 26

The crosstalk between immune and non‐immune cells, such as keratinocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, melanocytes and neurons, facilitates cellular communication during steady state and disease conditions. A well‐coordinated response, driven by effective communication between these cell types, is crucial for maintaining tissue homeostasis and preventing excessive immune activation that can lead to chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis (Figure 1). Psoriasis is one of the most extensively investigated skin diseases, which we will be using in this review as an example to highlight recent advances in elucidating how cellular networks orchestrate skin immunity via coordinated communication through soluble mediators and their signalling pathways. Figure 1 illustrates the cellular networks in mouse and human skin, and their altered communications via cytokines/chemokines during skin inflammation.

Keratinocytes

Keratinocytes are positioned at the interface between the body and the external environment and play a pivotal role in preserving body homeostasis. They are instrumental in relaying signals within the epidermis and to the dermis, particularly to the immune cells. For example, they possess machinery to detect pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) present on microbes in the skin microenvironment and to sense danger‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), secreted by stressed cells. 27 The receptors recognising PAMPs and DAMPs are known as pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and include TLRs, nucleotide‐binding oligomerisation domain‐like receptors (NLRs), retinoic acid‐inducible gene‐I‐like receptors (RLRs) and C‐type lectin receptors (CLRs). 28 , 29 These PRRs are expressed on the cell membrane and intracellular compartments and help keratinocytes to respond and activate the downstream events, such as cytokine and chemokine production, and signalling pathways, such as nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB) and type‐I IFN. 29 However, an activated response needs to be balanced, as excessive production of cytokines and chemokines can contribute to inflammatory diseases, such as psoriasis or autoimmunity. 28 , 30 For example, dysregulation of several nucleic acid sensors that recognise RNA or DNA, such as absent in melanoma (AIM2) and stimulator of interferon genes (STING), has been linked to inflammatory skin diseases, impaired wound healing and infections. 29 , 31

Several dysregulated signalling pathways, such as signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), activator protein‐1 (AP‐1) and NF‐κB, are associated with human inflammatory skin diseases, including psoriasis. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 However, the mechanisms by which they contribute to pathogenesis are poorly understood. Recent studies in mouse models have provided strong evidence that keratinocytes play a key role in intiating skin inflammation. Specifically, mice with aberrant signalling pathways, such as AP‐1, NF‐κB, cell death components (e.g. receptor‐interacting protein kinase‐1 (RIPK1)), or nucleic acid sensors (e.g. Z‐DNA binding protein‐1 (ZBP1)), in keratinocytes trigger skin inflammation. 29 , 36

TNF is a pro‐inflammatory cytokine that is associated with several inflammatory diseases, and TNF blockers are successfully used in the clinic for the treatment of inflammatory skin diseases, including psoriasis. 37 , 38 , 39 However, the exact mechanisms by which TNF induces inflammation were unknown. 40 Studies in mouse models have demonstrated the mechanisms by which TNF pathways contribute to skin diseases, bridging the gap in our understanding of inflammation and the TNF pathway that has existed for decades. Specifically, keratinocyte‐specific modulation of genes involved in NF‐κB or cell death resulted in TNF receptor‐dependent skin inflammation and immune dysregulation. This showed the intrinsic capacity of keratinocytes in triggering inflammation via TNF‐mediated cytokine (e.g. IL‐24) secretion and cell death. 29 , 41 , 42 Both overexpression and inhibition of NF‐κB signalling in the epidermis triggered TNF‐dependent skin inflammation. 41 , 42 , 43 Moreover, the epidermis‐specific deletion of the components of TNF signalling, such as NF‐kappa‐B essential modulator (NEMO), transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β)‐activated kinase 1 (TAK1) or cellular FLICE (FADD‐like IL‐1β‐converting enzyme)‐inhibitory protein (c‐FLIP), in mice increased cell death and skin inflammation. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 Mutations in genes involved in TNF signalling, such as SHANK‐associated RH Domain Interactor (SHARPIN), result in a dermatitis‐like phenotype, which was prevented by the inhibition of TNF or keratinocyte‐intrinsic TNFR1 signalling. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 TNF signalling in keratinocytes in this model triggers RIPK1 kinase‐mediated apoptosis and subsequent inflammation. 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Keratinocyte‐specific deletion of genes involved in cell death, such as Fas‐Associated Via Death Domain (FADD), or RIPK1, results in keratinocyte necroptosis via RIPK3/Mixed Lineage Kinase Domain‐Like Pseudokinase (MLKL) or RIPK3/MLKL/ZBP1, respectively, and triggers both TNF‐ dependent and independent skin inflammation. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 These studies illustrated a central link of TNF signalling to skin inflammation induced via cell death.

In the context of psoriasis, stressed keratinocytes have been shown to trigger the immune response by secreting AMPs, such as LL‐37 and β‐defensins, or DAMPs, including the release of nucleic acids, ATP and high‐mobility group box‐1 (HMGB1). Specifically, LL‐37, β‐defensins and S100 proteins are shown to be elevated in the psoriatic skin. 59 , 60 , 61 These AMPs form complexes with self‐DNA released from the stressed KCs and other damaged cells. The AMP‐DNA complexes then activate plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) to produce and release type I IFN via TLR9. The type I IFN response induces the activation of resident dermal DCs and maturation of monocytes into inflammatory DCs. LL‐37 also binds to RNA, which activates pDCs via TLR7 and DCs via TLR8. Mature DCs migrate to the draining lymph nodes and release IL‐23, IL‐12 and TNF. 62 , 63 , 64 IL‐12 and IL‐23 activate naïve T cells and modulate the maturation and expansion of the Th1 and Th17 subsets, which are responsible for sustaining the psoriatic inflammation in the skin.

IL‐17A is another cytokine that targets keratinocytes and regulates immune responses during psoriasis. 65 IL‐17A induces abnormal keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation and synergises with TNF to induce a variety of pro‐inflammatory factors such as chemokine ligand (CCL)20, IL‐8, TNF, IL‐6 and IL‐1β in psoriatic skin. 66 These factors then form a positive feedback loop, communicating with different cell types. Specifically, CCL20 from keratinocytes helps to recruit IL‐17‐producing cells, such as Th17 cells and γδ T cells, while IL‐1β, together with IL‐23, enhances IL‐17A production from Th17 cells. 66 Th17 cells secrete IL‐17, IL‐21 and IL‐22 that drive keratinocyte proliferation and inflammatory responses, which in turn upregulate IL‐36 cytokines and IL‐19, reinforcing a positive feedback loop that sustains skin inflammation. 67 , 68 In pustular psoriasis, keratinocyte‐derived IL‐36 becomes a central amplifier, especially in the absence of its antagonist, promoting leukocyte recruitment and pustule formation. 66 This creates an amplification loop that helps maintain inflammation in the skin by the production of chemokines and the recruitment and accumulation of leukocytes. 69 , 70 A recent study has shown that IL‐36 release from keratinocytes is triggered by LL‐37/dsRNA complexes via RIG‐I‐like receptor activation and Gasdermin E pore formation, amplifying the inflammatory response, thus deepening our understanding of how keratinocytes may initiate and react to stress responses and contribute to psoriasis pathogenesis. 71

One of the key challenges in the treatment of psoriasis is the recurrence of the disease upon drug withdrawal. 72 This disease relapse has been associated with the presence of TRMs in the skin, which produce IL‐17A. This maintains the ongoing inflammation loop and promotes disease recurrence, consequently. 73 In human psoriasis and psoriasis mouse models, IL‐17 receptors are widely expressed on keratinocytes. Studies have shown that the inhibition of IL‐17 receptor (IL‐17R) signalling in psoriasis mouse models markedly reduces ongoing skin inflammation. 74 This highlights the critical keratinocyte‐intrinsic role of IL‐17 cytokines in driving disease pathology.

Additionally, IL‐17C is a member of the IL‐17 family of cytokines and a potent immune cell recruiter that promotes the expression of antimicrobial peptides and drives the Th1, Th2 and Th17 immune responses in psoriasis. 75 , 76 Clinical evidence has also shown that IL‐17C is differentially regulated in psoriasis. 77 IL‐17C binding to its receptor, IL‐17RA and IL‐17RE, recruits adaptor molecule ACT1 and initiates the activation of the NF‐κB and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, leading to inflammation. 78 The critical role of IL‐17C in keratinocyte dysregulation and skin inflammation was shown by the development of a psoriasis‐like phenotype in transgenic mice that overexpressed IL‐17C in keratinocytes. 79 Furthermore, mouse studies have shown that IL‐17C injections induce epidermal thickening, and the neutralisation of IL‐17C significantly reduces skin pathology in both psoriasis and atopic dermatitis (AD) mouse models. 75 , 80

Interestingly, these cytokines also synergise and amplify the pro‐inflammatory responses. For example, TNF and IL‐17 synergistically amplify pro‐inflammatory signals in keratinocytes, driving the expression of β‐defensin 4, S100A7, LCN2, DEFB4 and IL‐23 through pathways involving NF‐κB, AP‐1, OCT1 and EGF signalling. 81 , 82 , 83 This highlights the cytokine network dynamics in skin immunity, and the knowledge gained by understanding them can be harnessed to identify novel therapeutic targets for skin disorders and homeostasis restoration.

Therefore, it is important to recognise that keratinocytes are not merely passive structural cells, but active players in skin immunity and inflammation. Their ability to produce pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and antimicrobial peptides in stress responses, and respond through various receptors that activate pro‐inflammatory signalling pathways, allows keratinocytes to act as initiators and amplifiers of inflammatory signals, contributing to chronic inflammatory diseases such as psoriasis. Thus, understanding the complex interactions between keratinocytes and other immune and non‐immune cells, offers valuable insights for the development of targeted therapies aimed at restoring skin homeostasis and managing inflammatory skin diseases effectively.

Innate immunity in the skin

Langerhans cells

LCs reside amongst keratinocytes in the stratum spinosum of the epidermis. 84 They act as immune sentinels as they survey the skin for foreign antigens and migrate to the lymph nodes, where they initiate the differentiation of naïve T cells to effector T cells through a combination of processes such as antigen recognition, co‐stimulatory molecule stimulation and cytokine signalling. 85 , 86 , 87

The interaction between LCs and keratinocytes is facilitated via the cell adhesion molecule, E‐cadherin, which allows the anchoring of LCs to surrounding keratinocytes. 88 When LCs migrate, keratinocytes downregulate the expression of E‐cadherin, and LCs lose dendrites, allowing them to migrate to the lymph nodes. 89

Human LCs are characterised based on the co‐expression of Human Leukocyte Antigen – DR isotype (HLA‐DR), Langerin (CD207), and CD1a, while murine LCs are characterised by the co‐expression of major histocompatibility complex II (MHCII), EpCAM, and Langerin (CD207). 89

LC homeostasis and their ability to distinguish between pathogenic and self‐signal are crucial to preventing excessive immune activation that could lead to autoimmunity. LCs are maintained throughout life in the skin under steady state conditions, and two distinct populations of LCs have been shown to populate the skin during homeostasis and inflammation. 90 , 91 Under steady‐state conditions, LCs contribute to skin immune homeostasis by presenting antigens and maintaining or expanding skin‐resident T‐cell populations, such as CD4+ CD25+ Foxp3+ CD127− Tregs. 86 In contrast, stress responses trigger the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF, IL‐1β, IL‐6 and IFNγ from keratinocytes, which regulate the activation, migration and pathogen‐sensing abilities of LCs. 92 Signalling through colony‐stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) is required for the maintenance of LCs in the epidermis, whereas the macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF) helps in the repopulation of LCs in the skin during inflammation. 93 Keratinocytes produce IL‐34, an alternative ligand of CSF1R, which is essential for the migration of epidermal LCs during development. 94 , 95 The survival and regeneration of LCs are controlled by transforming growth factor beta‐1 (TGF‐β1), produced by both keratinocytes and LCs. 96

During inflammation, LCs and DCs secrete IL‐12, which activates Th1 cells and NK cells in both mice and humans. 97 , 98 The activation of these cell populations subsequently drives the maturation of immature LCs and DCs through a positive feedback loop. LCs also promote the proliferation and differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th1 and Th2 subsets, as well as the activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells. 99 , 100 , 101 , 102 LCs express several TLRs, such as TLR1, TLR2, TLR3, TLR4 and TLR9, which recognise PAMPs and DAMPs. 103 Except for TLR3, which signals through TRIF, most TLR activation signals through the myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)‐mediated signalling pathway, resulting in NF‐κB activation. 104 , 105 This interaction amplifies the antigen‐presenting capacity of LCs and upregulates the expression of MHCII and co‐stimulatory molecules, such as CD80, during inflammation. 105

The role of LCs in inflammatory skin diseases has been investigated in both mice and humans for the past 50 years, yet their contributions to disease pathogenesis are not completely understood. 85 , 106 Early‐onset psoriasis patients are found to exhibit impaired LC migration capacity. 107 And, an ex vivo study using human psoriasis and normal skin explants has suggested that the impairment was driven by the enhanced secretion of IL‐17 in psoriasis patients. 108 Additionally, it was found that p38α in LCs is responsible for the secretion of IL‐6 and IL‐23, which act on γδ T and CD4+ T cells to produce IL‐17 in psoriasis. 109 LCs are also activated and highly proliferative in Th2‐driven AD, where the expression of activation markers CD80 and CD86 was found to be upregulated on both LCs and CD1a+ inflammatory DCs. 110 However, the mechanisms by which LCs modulate the Th2 responses via cellular communication, cytokines and chemokines in AD is remain unclear. Further studies on the role of LCs and their interactions with other cell types will help elucidate their contributions to specific inflammatory or repair processes in the skin. Several key studies provide in‐depth knowledge of LC ontogeny, and regulation during homeostasis and inflammation and can be accessed in other literature. 87 , 111

Dermal dendritic cells

Dendritic cells in the skin are crucial sentinels of immune surveillance, strategically positioned in the dermal compartment of the skin. They bridge innate and adaptive immunity by capturing antigens, producing cytokines and chemokines, and presenting antigens to T cells. 112 Skin DCs are broadly categorised into three main subsets: plasmacytoid DCs, conventional DCs and monocyte‐derived DCs (mo‐DCs). 26 , 113 Each subset expresses distinct phenotypic markers and perform unique functions in maintaining skin homeostasis and orchestrating immune responses in the skin. 112

Under steady state conditions, cDC1s and cDC2s are the predominant dendritic cell populations present in the dermal compartment of both humans and mice. In mice, cDC1s are characterised by the expression of CD103, XCR1, CLEC9a (DNGR‐1) and DEC205, and correspond to human CD141+ (BDCA3+) DCs, which similarly express CLEC9a, XCR1 and high levels of CD205. 114 These cells are highly specialized in antigen cross‐presentation to CD8+ T cells and play key roles in antiviral and anti‐tumor immunity. 114 In psoriatic skin lesions, a distinct population of CD11c+ CD141− CD1c− DCs was found to be upregulated by approximately 10‐fold in comparison to healthy skin. 115 The ontogeny of this population remains elusive, with speculation that these cells arise either from DC precursors or from CD1c+ DCs that downregulate CD1c expression in psoriasis. 115 These inflammatory DCs are crucial to disease progression as they produce high levels of IL‐23, sustaining pathogenic Th17 cell responses, and secrete IL‐12 and TNF, promoting Th1 responses and chronic inflammation. 116

In contrast, cDC2s express CD11b in mice and CD1c (BDCA1) in humans, with CD14 often co‐expressed with CD1c in human inflammatory DCs. 117 , 118 , 119 Interestingly, single‐cell RNA sequencing of human skin identified a pro‐inflammatory CD1c+CD14+ DC subset that was enriched in psoriatic lesions. 119 These DCs co‐produce IL‐1β and IL‐23A, suggesting a key role in driving psoriatic inflammation and offering a potential therapeutic target. 119 Additionally, human cDC2s also express SIRPα and Esam, distinguishing them from cDC1s. 114 Functionally, cDC2s are efficient in the induction of Th17 and Th2 responses and are prominent in driving inflammatory processes during skin diseases such as psoriasis. 120 , 121 , 122 However, during inflammation, the DC landscape becomes more complex. Circulating CCR2+ monocytes are recruited from the blood to inflamed tissue via CCL2/CCR2 signalling and differentiate into inflammatory DCs and monocyte‐derived dendritic cells (mo‐DCs). 123 , 124 Following differentiation, mo‐DCs acquire potent antigen‐presenting abilities and produce pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐23, TNF and IL‐1β. 125 , 126 Under inflammatory conditions, mo‐DCs migrate to the draining lymph nodes via a CCR7‐dependent mechanism for naïve T‐cell priming and activation of memory T‐cell responses. This then further amplifies both local and systemic inflammation in a positive feedback loop. 123

pDCs are sparsely distributed in the skin during homeostasis and are typically found in peripheral blood circulation. However, they rapidly infiltrate the skin during inflammation. pDCs are characterised by the expression of CD123 (IL‐3Rα), CD303 (BDCA‐2), CD304 (BDCA‐4), Bst2 (tetherin) and Siglec‐H in mice. 118 , 127 , 128 They are key effectors of type I interferon (IFN‐α/β) production during viral infections and contribute to the initiation of autoimmune responses. 129 , 130 pDCs are described to be the early initiators of psoriasis because of their nature of recognising self‐antigens, alarmins and AMPs such as LL‐37 64 as discussed in the keratinocyte section. However, further studies will elucidate the contribution of pDCs to skin diseases.

Given the complexity of the DC subsets in the skin, recent advances have revealed the importance of using multiple markers to accurately delineate DC subsets in the skin. 131 , 132 Markers such as SIRPα, CLEC9a, XCR1, DEC205 and Esam are crucial for distinguishing DC identities and functional capacities, offering deeper insights into how these subsets contribute differently to immune tolerance or pathology. 132

For readers seeking a more in‐depth understanding of the complex biology of skin DCs, several comprehensive reviews can provide further insights into the development, phenotypes and functions of these cells in both health and disease. 112 , 133

Monocytes and macrophages

Monocytes are highly adaptable mononuclear phagocytes that are key mediators of immune surveillance and homeostasis in the skin. Their ability to sense and respond to environmental triggers allows them to adopt diverse phenotypes according to the cytokine milieu and functional requirements of the surrounding tissue. 134 , 135 Upon extravasation into the peripheral tissues, monocytes can differentiate into macrophages or DCs, tailoring their responses to either support or resolve inflammation. 136 Monocytes are divided into two major subsets: the classical (Ly6C+ CCR2+ CX3CR1− in mice or CD14+ CD16− in humans) and non‐classical monocytes (Ly6C− CCR2− CX3CR1+ in mice or CD14dim CD16+ in humans). During inflammation, classical monocytes rapidly migrate from the bone marrow to the inflamed tissue through CCR2‐mediated signalling. 137 In the skin, classical monocytes can differentiate into M1 macrophages or mo‐DCs and secrete TNF, IL‐1β and type 1 IFNs during inflammation. 138 , 139 Although previous literature suggested that monocytes rapidly differentiate into macrophages/DCs once they enter peripheral tissue, Jakubzick et al. 140 demonstrated that Ly6C+ monocytes help maintain skin homeostasis by transporting antigens to draining lymph nodes and enhancing DC function at steady‐state conditions. This finding underscores a dual role of monocytes as being a rapid responder during inflammation and a critical contributor to skin homeostasis.

Classical monocytes can also sequentially transition into non‐classical monocytes via an intermediate stage that is crucial for inflammation resolution and tissue repair. 141 , 142 , 143 , 144 , 145 At steady state, non‐classical monocytes regularly patrol the vascular endothelium and help remove damaged cells and debris. 141 In addition, non‐classical monocytes were shown to be involved in wound healing, where they produce anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐10 and potentially differentiate into M2 macrophages, which contribute to extracellular matrix remodelling to restore healthy skin architecture. 141

The dynamic plasticity of monocytes highlights their indispensable role in both the initiation and resolution of inflammation. Consequently, the dysregulation of monocyte responses has been implicated in the persistence of chronic inflammatory diseases. Particularly, the failure to switch from an inflammatory to a reparative phenotype disrupts skin homeostasis and initiates a cascade of inflammatory responses downstream. The therapeutic potential of targeting monocyte subsets is therefore emphasised in the literature to modulate skin inflammation and promote healing.

Macrophages are professional antigen‐presenting cells that maintain skin immune homeostasis and are key players in tissue repair and inflammation. 146 , 147 Similar to LCs in the epidermis, macrophages act as immune sentinels, continually surveying the dermal microenvironment for PAMPs and DAMPs to initiate appropriate immune responses. Dermal macrophages are composed of two major populations: long‐lived tissue‐resident macrophages and short‐lived inflammatory macrophages derived from peripheral Ly6C+ CCR2+ CX3CR1− monocytes. 140 , 148

Under steady state conditions, most dermal or tissue‐resident macrophages originate from yolk sacs and/or foetal livers. 149 , 150 During inflammation, dermal resident tissue macrophages are constantly replenished by monocyte‐derived macrophages, with a small subset of long‐lived tissue‐resident macrophages possessing self‐renewal capacity within the skin. 151 These tissue‐resident macrophages typically express pan markers F4/80, CD64, CD11b, MHCII and MerTK, but do not express Ly6C. CD169 is exclusively expressed by tissue‐resident macrophages. Upon recruitment, bone marrow‐derived macrophages can be further classified into classically activated pro‐inflammatory macrophages and alternatively activated anti‐inflammatory macrophages depending on environmental cues. 152 Due to their high degree of plasticity, macrophages can polarise into either a pro‐inflammatory, classically activated M1 macrophage or an anti‐inflammatory, alternatively activated M2 macrophage. 153 In addition to the pan markers, M1 macrophages notably express iNOS, CD80 and CD86, while M2 macrophages typically express CD206, CD163 and Arg. 152 , 154

The polarisation of pro‐inflammatory or M1 macrophages is typically induced by exposure to pathological signals such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1R. 155 , 156 These stimuli activate critical intracellular pathways such as NF‐κB and MAPK signalling that induce the production of TNF, IL‐23, IL‐12, IL‐1β and IL‐6. This amplifies pro‐inflammatory responses and promotes other immune and non‐immune cell activation, such as T cells, neutrophils, DCs, NK cells and epithelial cells. 136 , 152 , 154

Conversely, macrophages exposed to IL‐4, IL‐10, IL‐13 or TGF‐β polarise toward a resolutory anti‐inflammatory or M2 phenotype. These M2 macrophages play a crucial role in wound healing, inflammation resolution and restoration of tissue integrity by promoting angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodelling. 152 , 154

Similar to monocytes, the plasticity of macrophages allows them to dynamically adapt their function based on the signals present in the tissue microenvironment. During skin inflammation, the imbalance of M1 macrophage polarisation can lead to chronic inflammatory skin disease. Additionally, the timely transition of M1 to M2 macrophage phenotype is essential post‐inflammation to initiate tissue repair.

Monocyte and macrophage dysregulation have been implicated in inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis. Psoriasis patients show an increased number of pro‐inflammatory macrophages in the peripheral blood and the skin. Specifically, CD68+ and CD163+ macrophages are elevated in psoriatic skin lesions, where they are major sources of IL‐12 and IL‐23 that influence Th1 and Th17 responses. 148 , 157 , 158 , 159 , 160 , 161

Studies using murine models have provided valuable insight into the role of monocytes and macrophages in psoriasis‐like skin inflammation. For example, in a psoriasis‐like CD18 hypomorphic mouse model, macrophages were found to significantly contribute to the increased levels of TNF secretion, which continually amplifies the immune response in a positive feedback loop. 162 , 163 Similarly, in an IL‐23‐induced psoriasiform murine model, an increase in TNF‐producing macrophages was reported. 164 Interestingly, Ly6C+ MHCII+ and Ly6C+ MHCII− myeloid subsets were found to be the dominant cell types present in inflamed skin, contrary to the expected dominance of Ly6C− MHCII+ activated macrophages. 164

Notably, macrophage depletion using various methods such as CSF1R inhibitors, clodronate liposomes, or anti‐CCL2 and anti‐TNF neutralising antibodies, either prophylactically or therapeutically, ameliorated epidermal swelling. However, the reduction in swelling was more pronounced when the macrophages were depleted before the onset of inflammation. 41 , 162 , 165 Of note, pro‐inflammatory cytokines, TNF, IL‐17A and IL‐1β were significantly reduced after macrophage depletion. 162 These studies have highlighted the critical role of monocytes and macrophages in initiating TNF/IL‐23‐driven inflammatory skin diseases and propagating inflammation.

In another mouse model generated by the specific deletion of the inhibitor of NF‐κB kinase subunit beta (Ikkβ) gene, a kinase that activates NF‐κB, developed TNF‐dependent skin inflammation in which depletion of myeloid cells using liposome‐clodronate improved skin lesion development. 41 , 165 Macrophages were also found to be the predominant cell type in a debilitating inflammatory skin condition, HS. 166 An increased expression of TLR2 on CD68+ infiltrating macrophages and CD209+ DCs in HS patients' skin was observed; however, their contribution to HS pathogenesis remains unknown. 167 Additionally, macrophages were also found to be the primary producers of elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF and IL‐1β in HS lesions. 168 , 169

Overall, the selective studies highlighted in this review revealed the heterogeneity and plasticity of monocyte and macrophage subsets in inflamed skin, potentially driven by the dynamic interaction between the immune cells and the tissue microenvironment. For readers seeking a more in‐depth understanding of the complex biology of macrophages and monocytes in the skin, several comprehensive reviews can provide further insights into the development, phenotypes and functions of these cells in both health and disease. 135 , 170 , 171

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are among the earliest immune cells recruited into the skin following injury, infection or inflammatory stimuli. 172 , 173 They play a pivotal role in host defence as well as in amplifying chronic inflammation, such as in psoriasis and HS.

Pro‐inflammatory factors such as IL‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐36 cytokines, IL‐8, TNF and granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) are released upon tissue damage or pathogenic insult from a range of tissue‐resident cells, including keratinocytes, DCs, LCs and macrophages, which facilitate the recruitment of neutrophils to the skin. 174 , 175 Chemoattractants and their receptors, such as chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2), chemokine ligand 16 (CXCL16) and leukotriene B4 (LTB4), are major recruiters of neutrophils in inflamed skin. 176 , 177 , 178 During chronic inflammation, such as in psoriasis, the IL‐23/IL‐17 axis plays an important role in neutrophil recruitment. IL‐17‐mediated activation of G‐CSF stimulates granulopoiesis, driving the release of mature neutrophils into circulation for extravasation. 174 , 179 Upon infiltration into the skin, neutrophils undergo NETosis, where neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) containing granular enzymes, histones, neutrophil elastase, defensins and cathelicidin are released at the site of inflammation. 179 Coagulation factor XII is also released by neutrophils to sustain NETosis and recruit additional neutrophils, amplifying the immune response with the help of chemoattractants, IL‐8, and LTB4. 180 Once inflammation is resolved, apoptotic neutrophils are phagocytosed by macrophages. 181 This inhibits the production and release of IL‐23 and IL‐17, halting neutrophil maturation and recruitment, and restoring homeostasis through a negative feedback mechanism. 175 , 179

Dysfunction of neutrophils may play an important role in the initiation of psoriasis pathogenesis. The neutrophil degranulation dysregulation has been observed in psoriasis, where neutrophil granule proteins have been increased in circulation. 182 Interestingly, the neutrophil‐derived proteases found in circulating granules (e.g. proteinase‐3) have been shown to cleave keratinocyte‐produced alarmins such as LL‐37. 183 Thus, it becomes pathogenic because of the alteration of its epitopes, which can be recognised as an autoantigen by the autoreactive T cells in the initial phase of psoriasis.

As mentioned previously, IL‐36 is produced by DCs, monocytes and keratinocytes and recruits neutrophils to amplify neutrophilic inflammation in the skin. 184 Proteolytic cleavage of IL‐36 cytokines by neutrophil‐derived proteases, such as Cathepsin G, is required for further cytokine activation. 185 The IL‐36/IL‐1 cytokines are elevated in the skin of psoriatic and HS patients compared with healthy skin. 168 , 186 These cytokines activate inflammatory signalling such as the NF‐κB pathway and promote the secretion of TNF, IL‐1 and IL‐17. This initiates the recruitment of neutrophils through a coordinated cytokine‐chemokine cascade involving CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL5, CXCL8 and G‐CSF. 168 , 187 , 188 , 189 Hence, failure to effectively clear neutrophils can result in chronic unresolved inflammation. Specifically in HS, the defective clearance of apoptotic neutrophils by macrophages leads to secondary necrosis. The persistent neutrophil activation and infiltration cause damage to hair follicles and sweat glands, while contributing to the formation of dermal tunnels, abscesses and scarring. Recent clinical trials using biologics inhibiting IL‐1 and IL‐36 signalling have shown efficacy in reducing symptoms of HS. 190

Adaptive immunity in the skin

T lymphocytes

T lymphocytes or T cells are among the dominant cell types of the adaptive immune system in the skin. Skin‐resident T cells are broadly categorised into helper T cells (CD4+) and cytotoxic T cells (CD8+). Upon antigen presentation by myeloid cells such as DCs, LCs and macrophages, naïve CD4+ T cells differentiate into effector T‐cell subsets, and depending on the local cytokine milieu, drive the Th1, Th2 or Th17 response. 191 Both Th1 and Th2 responses are defined by their unique expression profiles. The cytokines, IFNγ and TNF, and transcription factor, T‐bet, are unique to Th1, whereas Th2 responses are associated with the cytokines, IL‐4 and IL‐13, and the transcription factor, GATA3. 192 , 193 Cytokines, IL‐6, TGF‐β, IL‐21 and IL‐23, and transcription factor, RORγt, are required to initiate the Th17 response, leading to the production of IL‐17, IL‐6 and IL‐22. 194 T‐helper cells also express chemokine receptors, which allow them to respond to chemokine gradients for tissue migration and homing. 191 For example, Th1 cells express CXCR3 (ligand: CXCL9, CXCL10 and CXCL11) and CCR5 (ligand: CCL3, CCL4 and CCL5), Th2 cells express CCR3 (ligand: CCL11, CCL24 and CCL26) and CCR4 (ligand: CCL17 and CCL22), and Th17 cells express CCR6 (ligand: CCL20). 195 As discussed in the previous sections, these cytokines, together with T cell engagement amplify the inflammatory loops in chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis.

Studies have shown that transcription factors of the NF‐κB family, including RelA and c‐Rel, are pivotal for naïve T‐cell activation, TCR signalling and Th1 differentiation by the mediation of cytokine induction. 196 RelA and c‐Rel have also been shown to be involved in regulating the expression of RORγt, a Th17 lineage‐specific transcription factor that induces the differentiation of Th17 cells. 197 , 198 Additionally, IL‐21 expression in CD4+ T cells was shown to be mediated via c‐Rel. 199 This was confirmed by the observations of dysregulated Th17 and T follicular helper cell responses in c‐Rel‐deficient mice, highlighting its role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune skin diseases. 197 , 199

T cells are central to the initiation and perpetuation of skin diseases. For example, IL‐23 and IL‐12 activate skin‐resident T cells to produce IL‐17, IL‐22 and IFNγ during stress. 200 This supports the differentiation of T cells into their respective Th1, Th17 and Th22 subsets. Additionally, IL‐23‐induced γδ T cells also produce IL‐17, contributing to the persistence of inflammation. 201 Recurrence of psoriasis is common because of the persistence of TRMs and the persistent upregulation of inflammatory cytokines post‐treatment. 202 , 203 αβ T cells in psoriatic skin are also major contributors to clinical recurrence because of the increased secretion of IL‐17A even after lesions have resolved. 204

T regulatory cells, T memory cells and γδ T cells are the notable T cell subsets that are discussed below.

Memory T cells

T cells are effective in eliminating pathogens through their ability to migrate to infected or damaged tissues, but they have a short lifespan. 205 However, memory T cells persist long term and respond rapidly upon re‐exposure to previously encountered antigens. 205 Healthy skin is typically populated with different subsets of CD8+ and CD4+ memory cells. These memory cells can be further classified into TRMs, migratory memory cells (Tmm), central memory cells (Tcm) and effector memory cells (Tem). 206 Amongst these, TRMs are of particular relevance to both protective immunity and the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory skin disease. 207 , 208

TRMs are non‐circulating memory T cells that permanently reside in peripheral tissues, where they play a critical role in immune surveillance and rapid response upon reinfection. CD8+ TRMs are predominantly present in the epidermis, while CD4+ TRMs are more abundant in the dermal compartment in both mice and humans. The canonical markers of TRMs are CD69, which inhibits tissue egress via S1P1, and CD103, which binds to E‐cadherin on keratinocytes for epidermal retention. 209 , 210 They can be further characterised by the expression of CD49a, CXCR6, cutaneous lymphocyte‐associated antigen (CLA) and CCR7, depending on tissue localisation and activation state. 208 CD49a marks TRM subsets with enhanced cytotoxic potential and contributes to their retention within tissues. CXCR6 further supports TRM maintenance by promoting their long‐term survival and retention within the skin microenvironment. 211 , 212 , 213 Additionally, CLA serves as a key skin‐homing receptor that guides T cells to the skin and facilitates their adhesion and retention. CLA+ T cells have also been implicated in disease recurrence, particularly in AD, where they persist in the skin even after clinical resolution following treatment. 214 , 215 In contrast, CCR7 is typically downregulated in TRM cells, distinguishing them from central memory T cells and promoting tissue residency by limiting lymphoid tissue egress and recirculation. 216 , 217 , 218 Consequently, the TRM population in the skin exhibits considerable heterogeneity, with phenotypic plasticity that allows adaptation to local cues and dynamic modulation in response to environmental or inflammatory stimuli.

The establishment and maintenance of TRMs in the skin are tightly regulated by cytokines TGF‐β, IL‐15 and IL‐7, which are essential for their long‐term survival. 219 , 220 Precursor TRMs migrate into the epidermis with the aid of chemokine receptors CCR4, CCR10, CXCR3 and CXCR6. 221 Within the epidermis, CD8+ CD69+ CD103+ TRMs represent fully differentiated TRMs that are capable of immediate effector responses during antigen re‐exposure. The expression of CD49a further delineates TRM subsets based on function. CD49a+ TRMs are linked to high IFNγ production and cytotoxicity, while CD49a− subsets are associated with pathogenic functions because of their production of IL‐17. 207 , 222

CD4+ TRMs in the dermis also exhibit functional heterogeneity in mice and humans. 223 CD4+ CD69+ CD103− TRMs have a high proliferative ability but poor effector function. 208 However, in the presence of TGF‐β, CD103 expression is upregulated, giving rise to CD4+ CD69+ CD103+ TRMs, which are the predominant subtypes that populate the skin. Unlike the CD103−subsets, these cells have a limited proliferative ability but enhanced effector function. 222 The phenotypic plasticity of TRMs allows them to adapt to the tissue microenvironment for either immunosurveillance or effector function.

Under steady‐state conditions, TRMs play a protective role against previously exposed pathogens. In cutaneous melanoma, the reactivation of TRMs is associated with anti‐tumor responses. They exert their protective function by expressing checkpoint molecules such as CTLA‐4, LAG‐3 and PD‐1 and immune factors such as TNF and IFNγ, which contribute to anti‐tumor immunity. 224 However, in chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as psoriasis, TRMs are majorly implicated in the recurrence of these diseases as they can rapidly reignite local tissue inflammation upon re‐stimulation, propagating the inflammatory cascade. IL‐17‐ and IL‐22‐producing TRMs were found to be reactivated in relapsed psoriatic lesions, where pro‐inflammatory immune cells are recruited downstream. 220 , 225 Notably, two groups of CD8+ TRMs were found in recurrent psoriatic plaques where CD49a was differentially expressed. CD49a+ CD8+ TRM produced IFNγ and demonstrated greater tissue persistence. 220 , 226 TRMs are crucial regulators of skin immune homeostasis. However, when the balance is disrupted, TRMs have the potential to contribute to the pathogenesis and recurrence of inflammatory skin diseases. A deeper understanding of the balance between protecting and pathogenic TRM responses could pave the way for more effective therapeutic strategies for inflammatory skin diseases like psoriasis, but also for other skin conditions.

T regulatory cells

T regulatory cells (Tregs) are a specialised T cell subset that maintains tissue homeostasis by regulating the function of other immune cells. In healthy skin, CD4+ Foxp3+ Tregs are located adjacent to hair follicles. The abundance and localisation of Tregs in the skin make these cells rapid responders to inflammation and injury, enabling them to maintain homeostasis. They contribute to equilibrium by preserving non‐recognition of self‐antigens, which is critical for immune tolerance to preventing autoimmunity. 227 , 228 , 229

Tregs develop primarily in the thymus but can also be differentiated from peripheral CD4+ T cells in response to specific signalling. 230 They are characterised by the expression of surface markers, CD4 and CD25, and transcription factor, Foxp3, in both humans and mice. 231 These markers are crucial for the differentiation and maintenance of Tregs in the skin. The mechanisms of immunosuppression include IL‐2 deprivation, secretion of anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐10 and TGF‐β, induction of apoptosis via granzyme and perforin, and modulation of antigen‐presenting cells by downregulating co‐stimulatory molecule expression. 232 , 233 , 234 , 235 Expression of inhibitory receptors, LAG‐3, PD‐1, and CTLA‐4 on Tregs further enhances their suppressive capacity and maintains immune homeostasis by downregulating the activity of other immune cells. 234 , 236 , 237 , 238 Additionally, Tregs modulate APCs by downregulating co‐stimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86, thus inhibiting excessive T‐cell activation. 239 , 240 , 241

Treg dysfunction is implicated in the pathogenesis of several inflammatory diseases, such as psoriasis and contact dermatitis. 242 , 243 Elevation of pro‐inflammatory factors TNF, IL‐6, and IL‐23 contributes to Treg dysfunction by downregulating key suppressive molecules, including Foxp3 and IL‐10. 244 Specifically, in psoriasis, IL‐23 further amplifies the loss of Foxp3 expression in Tregs, driving their conversion into pro‐inflammatory IL‐17A‐producing RORγt+ Tregs. 242 Additionally, IL‐17A exacerbates Treg dysfunction in psoriasis by disrupting the TGF‐β‐Foxp3 axis, leading to the downregulation of both TGF‐β and Foxp3. 245 Moreover, evidence have shown that the elevation of IL‐6 and the consequent phosphorylation of STAT3 in Tregs further contribute to the impairment of their suppressive function in psoriasis. 246 , 247 , 248 These Tregs adopt a pro‐inflammatory effector function as opposed to their typical suppressive role. This imbalance between Tregs with effector and suppressive function further exacerbates the inflammation in the skin. 244 This atypical mechanism undermines the intended function of Tregs, contributing to the persistence of inflammation in these inflammatory skin diseases in a positive feedback loop.

Understanding the role of Tregs in inflammatory skin disorders, along with their phenotypic plasticity, is essential for uncovering the mechanisms that govern tissue homeostasis and drive disease progression. This knowledge can inform the development of targeted strategies to restore immune balance and prevent disease recurrence. Gaining deeper insight into the interactions between Tregs and the cytokine profile, APCs and effector T cells can provide a better understanding of disease pathogenesis. Therapeutic implications, including enhancing the suppressive function of Tregs, could potentially restore immune balance without suppressing the whole immune system.

γδ T cells

γδ T cells are a distinct subset of T lymphocytes that differ from conventional alpha‐beta (αβ) T cells in both their TCR structure and functional capacity. 249 Unlike αβ T cells, γδ T cells recognise antigens independently of MHC antigen presentation, allowing rapid responses to a diverse range of antigens, stress‐induced ligands and microbial metabolites. 250 , 251 They are mainly found in epithelial tissues, where they contribute to barrier protection by acting as first responders to infection, tissue damage and the presence of tumor cells. 252 γδ T cells bridge innate and adaptive immunity by producing pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IFNγ and IL‐17, and by exerting cytotoxic functions against infections and dysfunctional cells. 253 They are also key contributors to tissue homeostasis, wound healing and the regulation of other immune cells. 254

In mouse skin, there are several γδ T‐cell subtypes, including dendritic epidermal T cells (DETCs) in the epidermis and Vγ6 and Vγ4 T cells in the dermis. 255 DETCs express Vγ5Vδ1 TCR chains and make up 90% of epidermal lymphocytes in healthy skin. DETCs also play key roles in immune surveillance, epidermal homeostasis and are primarily involved in tissue repair. 256 , 257 Conversely, dermal Vγ6+ and Vγ4+ γδ T cells play a pro‐inflammatory role by producing cytokines such as IL‐17A and IL‐22, thereby contributing to tissue inflammation and immune responses in the skin.259 Vγ6 T cells are developed in the foetal thymus and remain tissue‐resident, while Vγ4 T cells are developed during the late foetal and neonatal stages, with some migration to lymph nodes during steady state and enhanced trafficking during inflammation. 255 Under inflammatory conditions, bone marrow‐derived precursor Vγ4 T cells expand and acquire long‐term memory, which promotes increased secretion of IL‐17A. 201 , 258 The differentiation and function of γδ T cells are regulated by cytokines such as IL‐1β and IL‐23. 201

In humans, γδ T cells are categorised by Vδ and Vγ gene segments, with Vδ1 cells primarily in the dermis and Vδ2 cells in the blood and dermis. 259 , 260 Unlike murine DETCs, human epidermal γδ T cells lack dendritic morphology but similarly promote wound healing and regulate cutaneous carcinomas via IGF‐1 secretion. 261 Dermal γδ T cells, particularly Vγ4 subsets, migrate to the epidermis via the CCR6–CCL20 axis and are essential for efficient wound repair. 255 These cells also traffic between skin and lymph nodes, with CCR2 facilitating their return to inflamed skin. 201 Vγ4 and Vγ6 subsets persist as memory‐like cells, highlighting their pivotal role in inflammation and tissue repair. 262

γδ T cells play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of skin diseases such as psoriasis. A subset of γδ T cells, Vγ9Vδ2, was found to be increased in psoriatic lesions, where they secrete pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐17A, IL‐22 and IFNγ, which drive keratinocyte hyperproliferation and immune cell recruitment. 263 Activated γδ T cells also promote sustained inflammation by amplifying the activation of other immune cells, including Th17 cells, creating a feedback loop of cytokine production and tissue damage. 264

γδ T cells are uniquely positioned at the interface of innate and adaptive immunity and are therefore key orchestrators of cutaneous immune responses. The increase of dermal γδ T cells in epithelial tissue and the production of IL‐17, IL‐22 and IFNγ make them key drivers of skin inflammation. Understanding the specific mechanisms that regulate their activation is crucial for identifying γδ T‐cell‐targeted therapeutic approaches that may be able to either disrupt their effector function or suppress pro‐inflammatory subsets in skin inflammation.

For readers seeking a more in‐depth understanding of the complex biology of T cells in the skin, several comprehensive reviews provide further insights into the development, phenotypes and functions of these cells in both health and disease. 194 , 207

Concluding remarks

Understanding how the cellular network of immune cells orchestrates skin immunity in both healthy and inflammatory states is crucial for advancing our knowledge of immune responses and improving therapeutic strategies for skin disorders. This review provides insights into these cellular communications with a focus on psoriasis as a disease model. In healthy skin, epithelial cells, myeloid cells, T cells, fibroblasts and skin appendages play vital roles in immune surveillance and maintaining tissue homeostasis. These cells detect pathogens, facilitate tissue repair and manage immune tolerance to prevent excessive inflammation or autoimmunity. During inflammation, such as in response to infection, injury, or inflammatory skin diseases, the immune landscape in the skin undergoes significant changes. Epithelial cells contribute to the recruitment, maintenance and accumulation of immune cells via cytokine secretion, cell death and interactions in the skin during inflammation. Myeloid cells increase their recruitment and activation, leading to the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and the recruitment of effector T cells. T cells, particularly Th17 cells and γδ T cells, become more active, promoting inflammation and tissue repair. However, dysregulated immune responses can contribute to chronic inflammation as in psoriasis, where T cells and myeloid cells sustain an inflammatory environment. The identification of key cytokine networks such as TNF, IL‐17 and IL‐23 has already transformed the therapeutic landscape for diseases like psoriasis and atopic dermatitis. However, many skin diseases with high unmet clinical needs remain poorly understood. Understanding these cellular interactions and the balance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory signals is therefore essential, not only for uncovering new therapeutic targets in dermatology but also for guiding the development of immunotherapies aimed at preventing chronic inflammation, managing autoimmune diseases, enhancing anti‐cancer immunity, and restoring tissue homeostasis.
