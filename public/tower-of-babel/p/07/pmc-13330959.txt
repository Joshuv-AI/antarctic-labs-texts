Intestinal neutral ceramidase exacerbates MASH pathogenesis
Authors: Ting Wang, Liang Chen, Chao Lei, Xiaotong Song, Amanguli Tuohongerbieke, Jing Feng, Regina Gasparetto, Xiang Zhang, Craig J McClain, Yi Tan, Zhongbin Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and its more severe manifestation, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), are intimately linked to genetic factors, gut microbiota and barrier alteration. Ceramidases and ceramides are associated with MASH, yet the role of intestinal neutral ceramidase in MASH development remains unclear.MethodsMurine models with intestinal epithelial cell (IEC)-specific depletion of neutral ceramidase (Asah2ΔIEC) or aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhRΔIEC) were subjected to either a Western diet (WD) at 6 weeks old for 10–12 months to induce MASH, or a hydrogenated vegetable oil, sucrose, palmitate and cholesterol (HSPC) diet to accelerate MASH progression. Fecal microbiota transplantation was performed in germ-free mice.ResultsMASH is associated with the induction of neutral ceramidase, which reshapes the intestinal microbiota and metabolite profiles, leading to increased production of 2-hydroxyhippuric acid (2-HHA). We identified 2-HHA as an inhibitor of AhR signalling, a pathway that normally promotes intestinal fucosylation. Elevated 2-HHA suppresses AhR activity, reduces fucosylation and contributes to MASH and associated airway inflammation in mice fed WD or HSPC diet. Notably, IEC-specific deletion of neutral ceramidase decreases 2-HHA levels, restores AhR signalling, enhances fucosylation and protects against MASH. Consistently, intestinal AhR deficiency exacerbates MASH by reducing intestinal fucosylation, whereas supplementation with fucoidan increases fucosylation, improves barrier function and attenuates MASH.ConclusionsThese findings identify intestinal neutral ceramidase as a key driver of MASH through a microbiota–2-HHA–AhR axis that impairs intestinal fucosylation and barrier function, highlighting a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease; Metabolic diseases; Gastrointestinal microbiome; Diet, food, and nutrition; Ceramidases

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) is influenced by gut–liver axis dysfunction, including alterations in the gut microbiota and impaired intestinal barrier integrity. Microbiota-derived metabolites regulate host metabolic and immune pathways. Intestinal epithelial fucosylation and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signalling are key regulators of mucosal homeostasis and host–microbe interactions.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Intestinal neutral ceramidase promotes MASH by reducing epithelial fucosylation. This involves microbiota-derived 2-hydroxyhippuric acid (2-HHA), which inhibits intestinal epithelial cell AhR signalling; accordingly, intestinal AhR deficiency further worsens MASH and is associated with reduced fucosylation.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study identifies the intestinal neutral ceramidase–microbiota–2-HHA–AhR–fucosylation axis as a potential therapeutic target in MASH, supporting the development of microbiome-based and metabolite-based strategies to restore gut barrier function and treat MASH and related metabolic diseases.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) are the most common liver diseases worldwide,1 2 while 20–30% of MASLD cases could progress to MASH.3 The detrimental impact of long-term exposure to a Western diet (WD) is well-documented with increased gut dysbiosis, chronic low-grade gut inflammation and metabolic perturbations.4 5 The gut barrier plays a critical role in metabolic disorders including liver and lung inflammation and fibrogenesis.6 7 In particular, intestinal microbiome composition and microbiota-derived metabolites correlate with MASH severity and lung inflammation.8,11 Due to gut barrier disruption, bacterial translocation contributes to the activation and recruitment of local and peripheral immune cells, driving liver and lung dysfunction.12 Understanding the impact of a WD on gut barrier function is of paramount importance for comprehending the broader implications of dietary intervention on human health.

Ceramide metabolism has been implicated in the pathogenesis of MASH.13,15 Ceramidases are a family of enzymes that break down ceramide into sphingosine and fatty acids. It has been reported that deficiency in alkaline ceramidase 3 and acid ceramidase alleviates inflammation and fibrosis in mouse models of MASH.16 17 Neutral ceramidase, encoded by the Asah2 gene, is predominantly expressed in liver and small intestine.18 In our prior work, we employed global neutral ceramidase knockout mice to demonstrate that neutral ceramidase is a functional regulator of intestinal IgA-producing B cells, thereby altering gut microbiota and accelerating MASH progression.19 However, the role of intestinal neutral ceramidase in MASH progression remains unknown. The barrier function of the intestinal epithelium is partly regulated by the mucosal epithelial glycan system including fucosylation.20 Fucosylated sugar chains are synthesised by fucosyltransferases (Futs).21 Fut1 and Fut2 enable expression of α1-2-fucosylated carbohydrates on intestinal epithelial cells (IECs). Our previous reports showed that intestinal epithelial α1-2-fucosylation plays a crucial role in the host–microbe interplay and is involved in alcohol-associated liver disease22 and colitis.23 Lack of α1-2-fucosylation in Fut2 deficient mice exacerbates chronic ethanol-induced liver injury, steatosis and inflammation.24 However, it remains unclear whether epithelial neutral ceramidase influences MASH through modulation of gut fucosylation. Here, we investigated the role of intestinal neutral ceramidase in MASH using mice with IEC-specific deletion of Asah2 (Asah2ΔIEC). We examined how intestinal neutral ceramidase influences gut microbiota, microbial metabolites and epithelial barrier function, with a focus on the potential role of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signalling and microbiota-derived metabolites such as 2-hydroxyhippuric acid (2-HHA) in regulating intestinal fucosylation and disease progression.

Methods

Detailed methods are provided in the online supplemental material.

Animal models

C57BL/6 mice, Ahrtm3.1Bra/J (Stock No. 006203) and B6.Cg-Tg(Vil1-cre)1000Gum/J mice (Vil-Cre 1000, Stock No. 021504) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). Asah2fl/fl mice generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology (Biocytogen, Beijing, China) and were crossed with Asah2fl/fl Vil-cre to generate control Asah2fl/fl mice and Vil-cre Asah2fl/fl conditional KO mice (designated as Asah2ΔIEC).25 Male and female AhRΔIEC mice, Asah2ΔIEC mice and their wild-type (WT) littermates were placed on a standard chow diet or a WD containing 42% kcal from fat (Teklad, TD.88137; Envigo, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) at 5 weeks of age for 3 months (to induce MASLD) or 12 months (to induce MASH). Food intake data were collected for 24 hours. For other MASH experiments, groups of mice were placed on a hydrogenated vegetable oil (HVO), sucrose, palmitate and cholesterol (HSPC) diet (Teklad, TD. 160785; Envigo) for 4–6 months.

Faecal microbiota transplantation into germ-free mice

Germ-free (GF) mice were provided by the Clean Mouse Facility of the University of Louisville, maintained in sterile isolators and verified monthly for GF status by plating and PCR of faeces. Faeces from 12-month-WD fed Asah2ΔIEC or WT mice were pooled and suspended in 1.5 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) per caecum. The suspensions were immediately administered (0.15 mL/mouse) to 8-week-old GF C57BL/6 male mice that were maintained in sterile cages with autoclaved normal chow (NC) diet food and water for 5 weeks. Some GF mice with faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) were placed on an HSPC diet (Teklad, TD. 160785; Envigo) for 4 months.

Cell culture and treatment

Murine colon adenocarcinoma AhR deficient MC38 cells (MC38AhRKO) was created in our previous report,26 MC38 or MC38AhRKO cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), 5 mg/mL insulin, 5 µg/mL transferrin, 5 ng/mL selenium, 40 ng/mL dexamethasone and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In 6-well plates, MC38 cells or AhR deficient MC38 cells (MC38AhRKO) were incubated with 2-HHA (1 mM) alone, with 50 nM 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) alone or with 2-HHA and TCDD, for 20 hours.

RNA extraction and PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells were performed using a RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and converted into complementary DNAs (cDNAs) using Superscript IV reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time (RT) PCR was carried out in ABI QuantStudio Real-Time PCR system using cDNA as template with SYBR Green Master Mix (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the primers listed in online supplemental table 1. The fold changes in messenger RNA (mRNA) expression were determined by the 2(−ΔΔCt) method after their normalisation to the housekeeping genes Gapdh or Actb as an internal control, which were set at a value of 1. All primers were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, Missouri, USA).

Faecal DNA extraction, gut microbiota quantification by RT-qPCR and microbiota 16S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from faecal samples using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For quantification of gut microbiota by RT-quantitative PCR (qPCR), purified genomic DNA was diluted to 20 ng/µl using MilliQ (Merck Millipore, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) water and was used as template for RT-qPCR analysis according to the method described above. Results for each sample were normalised to the concentration of total 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) by using a universal 16S rRNA primer pair (Sigma-Aldrich). For microbiota sequencing, the V3–V4 region was amplified using primer 319F (ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) and the amplified product was used to prepare sequencing libraries. The sequencing data were analysed by Lianchuan Omics (Hangzhou, China)

Cell staining, stimulation and flow cytometry

Cell suspensions underwent anti-CD16/32 staining to block Fc receptors, followed by a 30-minute incubation on ice with antibodies. The following antibodies were used at dilutions ranging from 1/200-1/600: PerCP-Cy5.5, PE-labelled, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labelled or APC-labelled anti-IL-17A (TC11-18H10.1, 1:200), PE-labelled or APC-labelled anti-IL-10 (JES5–16E3, 1:200), APC-labelled or PE-Cy7-labelled anti-IFN-γ (XMG1.2, 1:200), PE-labelled anti-Foxp3 (FJK-16s, eBioscience, Thermo Fisher Scientific), PE-labelled, FITC-labelled or APC-labelled anti-CD11b (M1/70, 1:300), PE-labelled, FITC-labelled or APC-labelled anti-CD4 (RM4-5, 1:300), PE-anti-CD11c (N418, 1:200), PE-anti-Gr-1 (RB6-8C5, 1:600), PE-labelled or FITC-labelled anti-mouse Ly6G (1A8, 1:200), PercP-anti-CD170 (1RNM44N, 1:300), PE-anti-CD45.1 (A20, 1:400), FITC-labelled, PerCP-Cy5.5 or Pacific Blue-labelled anti-CD45 (30-F11, 1:400), APC-anti-F4/80 (BM8, 1:100), PE-anti-CD206 (MR6F3, 1:200), PE-Ly6C (HK1.4, 1:500), APC-anti-Tim4 (RMT4-54, 1:200), PE-anti-Clec-2 (17D9/CLEC-2, 1:200), anti-I-A/I-E (M5/114.15.2, 1:600) and PE-anti-CD24 (M1/69, 1:300). All antibodies were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific and BioLegend (San Diego, California, USA) unless otherwise noted. Intracellular staining of the transcription factor Foxp3 was performed using the Foxp3 Fix/Perm Buffer Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For detection of intracellular cytokines, cells were first stimulated for 1 hour with 50 ng/mL. Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and 1 µg/mL ionomycin or 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and treated with brefeldin A (5 µg/mL; all obtained from Sigma-Aldrich) for another 4 hours, followed by staining for surface markers. Flow cytometry data were acquired on FACSCanto II and A1 Symphony 5-colour FACScan (Becton and Dickinson Company, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) and analysed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, Oregon, USA). Cell sorting was performed using the FACSAria II.

Isolation of hepatic and lung immune cells

Livers were perfused with saline solution via the portal vein which was followed by enzymatic digestion. Liver immune cells were purified by centrifugation using a Percoll gradient as described previously.19 27 Lung was cut and digested in 3 mL medium (RPMI 1640 containing collagenase I) (LS004197, Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, New Jersey, USA) and DNase I (50 ug/mL; LS006328, Worthington Biochemical) at 150 rpm for 20 min at 37°C. Homogenates were passed through a 70 µm filter, treated with red blood cell lysis buffer, washed and resuspended in PBS with 1% FBS (A5256701, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for surface immunostaining. For flow cytometry analysis, the cells were labelled using standard procedures described above.

Gut permeability assays

Mice were orally gavaged with 200 µl of 4 kDa FITC-dextran (0.5–1 mg per g body weight; FD4-250mg, Sigma-Aldrich) after 20 hours of fasting. Mice were anaesthetised using isoflurane (5% for induction, then set to 2% for blood drawing) after gavage. Blood was drawn by retro-orbital bleed in lithium-heparin capillary tubes or from portal vein. Plasma concentration of FITC-dextran was measured at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 528 nm by fluorescence spectrometry. Serially diluted FITC-dextran was used to establish a standard curve.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Values are shown as mean±SEM except where otherwise indicated. Prism (GraphPad Software, V.9.2.0, San Diego, California, USA) was used to determine statistical significance. Data distribution was assessed for normality before parametric tests were applied. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for more than two groups, two-way ANOVA with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test or two-tailed unpaired t-test were used for analysis. For data that did not meet parametric assumptions, the Wilcoxon test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used as appropriate. The Adonis test was used for beta-diversity analysis, Spearman’s correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis, and permutation testing was used for metabolomic model validation where applicable. Significance levels are indicated as *p<0.05, **p<0.01 and ***p<0.001, with p≥0.05 indicating non-significant differences, ns. n represents the number of independent samples.

Results

Intestinal neutral ceramidase deficiency attenuates diet-induced liver fibrosis in mice

Neutral ceramidase is mainly expressed in the liver and intestine, with higher expression in the intestine than in the liver.19 28 To evaluate whether intestinal neutral ceramidase plays a role in MASH, we generated IEC-specific Asah2 knockout (Asah2ΔIEC) mice25 and fed them NC diet for 3 months or a WD for 3 months or 12 months. Feeding either an NC diet or a WD for 3 months did not result in a significant increase in hepatic steatosis, serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels or food intake in Asah2ΔIEC mice compared with Asah2fl/fl WT controls (online supplemental figure S1A–H). After 3 months or 12 months of WD feeding, WT and Asah2ΔIEC littermates had similar gains in body weight (online supplemental figure S1I–J). However, Asah2ΔIEC mice exhibited a significantly reduced liver-to-body weight ratio after 12 months of WD feeding compared with WT controls (figure 1A). Lower collagen deposition was found in WD-fed Asah2ΔIEC mice as shown by Masson’s trichrome staining (figure 1B) while hepatic lipid accumulation was not significantly changed as evidenced by H&E staining (figure 1C) and triglyceride (TG) analysis (figure 1D). Reductions in α-SMA+ cells (figure 1E) and in serum hydroxyproline levels (figure 1F), a biochemical marker of collagen content and fibrosis, indicate a decrease in hepatic fibrogenesis in WD-fed Asah2∆IEC mice when compared with WD-fed WT controls. Additionally, lower levels of ALT and AST and plasma cholesterol were found in Asah2∆IEC (figure 1G). However, serum TG, very low-density lipoprotein and glucose were similar in both WD-fed WT mice and Asah2∆IEC mice (figure 1G). Asah2∆IEC mice consistently displayed lower levels of hepatic fibrosis markers, including collagen type I alpha 1 chain (Col1a1), fibronectin (Fn1) and alpha-smooth muscle actin (Acta2) mRNAs, compared with WT mice (figure 1H). Moreover, WD-fed Asah2ΔIEC mice have lower hepatic mRNA expression of TGF-β and interleukin (IL)-β (figure 1H). In addition, more regulatory MHCII− CD206+ macrophages (figure 1I) and a reduction in Tim4−Clec2− CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages, but not Tim4+ Kupffer cells and Clec2+ monocytes-derived Kupffer like cells (figure 1J,K), were found in the livers of Asah2ΔIEC mice compared with WT mice.

Intestinal neutral ceramidase deficiency prevents intestinal barrier dysfunction with enhanced gut fucosylation in mice with MASH

An impaired intestinal barrier is a prerequisite for MASH development and airway inflammation through the gut–liver–lung axis.6 7 We investigated whether intestinal Asah2 deficiency may influence the intestinal barrier. Indeed, LPS-binding protein (LBP) levels in the serum, lung and liver (figure 2A), and serum LPS level in the portal vein (figure 2B) were significantly lower in Asah2ΔIEC mice compared with WT mice. Asah2ΔIEC mice showed decreased intestinal permeability, as evidenced by a reduced plasma level of FITC-labelled dextran (figure 2C). Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining clearly showed a higher number of goblet cells with more glycans on the surface of epithelium in the colon (figure 2D,E), but not in the small intestine (online supplemental figure S2A), of Asah2ΔIEC mice compared with WT mice. These findings indicate that intestinal Asah2 may contribute to gut barrier function. We next performed transcriptomic profiling by RNA-seq of colonic tissue in WT and Asah2ΔIEC mice that fed a WD. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed that genes involved in the pathways of tight junction and mucin O glycan biosynthesis were enriched in Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 2F,G). Notably, Asah2ΔIEC mice showed higher expression of genes associated with glycans involved in the fucosylation metabolic process (Fut2, Fut1, B4galt5 and Il-22ra1). In particular, there was an increase in Fut2 in the colon compared with WT controls (figure 2G). RT-PCR consistently revealed that expression of fucosylation-related Fut1 and Fut2 was markedly upregulated in the colon of Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 2H). In addition, colon (figure 2H), but not in the ileum, of Asah2ΔIEC mice (online supplemental figure S2B), showed significantly increased expression of the genes encoding mucins and tight junction proteins (Muc2, Tjp1 and Cldn1) compared with control mice. In contrast, the mRNA expression of inflammatory chemokines and cytokines (eg, Cx3cl1, Ccl20 and Il1b) was decreased in the colons of Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 2H). Using immunohistochemical staining, the intestinal mucosa of WD-treated Asah2ΔIEC mice showed increased lectin staining with UEA-1, which specifically recognises α1,2-linked fucose, when compared with WD-treated WT mice (figure 2I). We further validated higher expression of fucosylated mucin2 in the colon, but not in the small intestine, from Asah2ΔIEC mice with immunofluorescent staining (figure 2J). These results indicate that increased fucosylation and improved gut barrier function might be associated with IEC neutral ceramidase deficiency.

Intestinal neutral ceramidase deficiency induces gut microbiota alterations that attenuates MASH in germ-free mice

Intestinal epithelial glycans, especially α1,2-fucose, mediate the host–microbe cross-communication. Given that gut microbiota plays an important role in shaping the structure and integrity of intestinal barrier,29 we performed 16S RNA gene sequencing on faecal samples of WD-fed Asah2ΔIEC and WT mice. The 16S RNA gene sequencing revealed that Asah2ΔIEC mice harbour a distinct gut microbiota compared with WT mice after WD feeding, when employing principal coordinates analysis (figure 3A and online supplemental figure S3A), and microbial α-diversity analysis was also assessed (figure 3B and online supplemental figure S3B). Differential bacterial taxa were also identified (figure 3C and online supplemental figure S3C–F). Potentially protective bacteria including Ruminococcus_sp, Muribaculum_sp30 and Parabacteroides_distasonis31 were significantly enriched in Asah2ΔIEC mice, and were accompanied by the depletion of obesity-related pathogenic bacteria including the species Desulfovibrio32 and Alistipes sp._DJF_B18533 (figure 3C). Linear discriminant analysis effect size further confirmed that the family Desulfovibrionaceae and genus Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 were in lower-abundance in Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 3D,E). These data are consistent with our previous finding that global neutral ceramidase knockout mice display a reduction in Desulfovibrio and Lachnospiraceae as well as an increase in Ruminococcus.19

To determine whether gut microbiota plays a crucial role in MASH induced by intestinal neutral ceramidase deficiency, we transplanted faecal samples from Asah2ΔIEC mice (GF-Asah2ΔIEC) or WT mice (GF-WT) into GF mice and then fed them an HSPC diet to induce MASH (figure 3F). GF-Asah2ΔIEC mice displayed a lower ratio of liver to body weight (figure 3F), serum LPS and hydroxyproline levels (figure 3G). Lower liver inflammation, but not steatosis, was found in GF-Asah2ΔIEC mice at 4 months following FMT and HSPC diet feeding than GF-WT control mice (figure 3H,I). Of note, GF-Asah2ΔIEC mice had a decreased hepatic collagen deposition as evidenced by Masson’s trichrome staining (figure 3J) and a downregulation of Col1a1, Col1a2 and Col3a1 mRNA expression (figure 3K) in the liver. These findings from FMT-treated GF mice demonstrate that gut microbiota shaped by intestinal neutral ceramidase deficiency plays a protective role against MASH development.

IEC neutral ceramidase deficiency increases intestinal fucosylation via decreasing gut microbiota-derived AhR antagonist 2-HHA

The liver is exposed to metabolites derived from gut microorganisms.34 To evaluate metabolite changes, we performed untargeted metabolomic profiling on stool samples from of Asah2ΔIEC and WT mice. Principal component analysis and partial least-squares discriminant analysis revealed that stool metabolome profile in the stools was significantly different between Asah2ΔIEC and WT mice (figure 4A,B). Among all differentially abundant metabolites, 2-HHA (also known as salicyluric acid) was the top metabolite consistently downregulated in the stools of Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 4C,D and online supplemental figure S4). The 2-HHA is a metabolite derived from the conjugation of glycine with salicylate and is influenced by the body’s detoxification systems and gut microbial activity. It is known that ceramidases can generate bioactive sphingosine from ceramide.28 We did notice that sphingosine with a variety of fatty acids were decreased with statistical significance in the stools of Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 4E), confirming that the function of neutral ceramidase in hydrolysing ceramide into sphingosine. In the de novo guanosine 5′-diphospho-L-fucose (GDP-L-fucose) biosynthetic pathway, GDP-L-fucose is produced from guanosine diphosphate mannose (GDP-D-mannose).23 35 Interestingly, the metabolite mannose was significantly increased in Asah2ΔIEC mice (figure 4F). Integrative analysis further showed that the abundances of species Alistipes sp._DJF_B185, Carnobacterium_sp._St2, Eubacterium and Acetatifactor_sp. Lachnospiraceae36 were positively correlated with 2-HHA level and were negatively correlated with mannose level (figure 4G). These data suggest that the abundance of 2-HHA-producing bacteria, for example, Alistipes indistinctus and Lachnospiraceae, may be linked to the mannose-fucose pathway.

To explore whether bacterial metabolite 2-HHA inhibits the AhR activity and fut2 expression in IECs, MC38 cells, C57BL/6 murine colon adenocarcinoma cell line, were exposed to TCDD in the presence of vehicle or 2-HHA for 20 hours. TCDD is a potent toxicant that exerts most of its biological effects by binding to and activating AhR. Of note, TCDD treatment significantly increased Fut2 expression in mouse MC38 cells, but not in AhR deficient MC38 cells (MC38AhRKO) (figure 5G). Deletion of AhR in MC38 cells also constitutively decreased the Fut1 expression (figure 5G). In contrast, AhR deficiency led to the upregulation of Fut8 expression in MC38 cells (figure 5G). These data indicate that activation of AhR signalling induces Fut2 and Fut1 expression. Notably, 2-HHA treatment alone did not alter basal Fut2 or Fut1 expression (figure 5G). However, cotreatment with 2-HHA significantly attenuated TCDD-induced upregulation of Fut2, as well as canonical AhR target genes, including Cyp1a1, Cyp1a2 and Cyp1b1 (figure 5G,H). Importantly, the effect of 2-HHA and TCDD treatment on the expression of Fut2 and canonical AhR target genes was not evident in MC38AhRKO cells (figure 5G,H). Together, these findings support that 2-HHA inhibits ligand-induced AhR activation and its downstream transcriptional programmes.

Intestinal AhR deficiency accelerates diet-induced liver fibrosis and lung inflammation

Although AhR−/− mice exhibit spontaneous liver fibrosis,38 whether AhR in IECs affects diet-induced liver fibrosis and lung inflammation remains unknown. To investigate whether intestinal AhR deficiency could aggravate diet-induced MASH, IEC-specific AhR knockout (AhRΔIEC) mice were generated by crossing AhRfl/fl mice with villincre mice and were then fed a WD for 10 months. Increased liver inflammation (figure 6A), collagen deposition (figure 6B) and hydroxyproline levels (figure 6C), but not hepatic steatosis (figure 6D), were observed in AhRΔIEC mice compared with AhRfl/fl WT controls. An increase in α-SMA+ cells (figure 6E) was also found in WD-fed AhR∆IEC mice compared with WD-fed WT controls. Consistently, AhRΔIEC mice showed higher expression level of genes associated with liver fibrosis (Col1a1, Fn1 and Acta2) (figure 6F). In addition, the levels of inflammatory tumour necrosis factor-alpha+ macrophages (figure 6G) and monocytes (figure 6H) were significantly elevated in the livers of AhRΔIEC mice compared with WT mice.

Intestinal AhR deficiency reduces gut fucosylation and accelerates intestinal barrier dysfunction

Deletion of AhR in IECs remarkably increased intestinal permeability, as evidenced by higher levels of LPS in the liver and lung (figure 7A) and higher serum LBP levels (figure 7B). RT-PCR analysis showed that Fut2 and Muc2 mRNA levels were reduced significantly in the colon of AhRΔIEC mice (figure 7C). The level of glycan attached goblet cells on the surface of intestinal epithelium was consistently downregulated in the colon of AhRΔIEC mice compared with WT mice (figure 7D). Most importantly, reduced fucosylation and lower levels of fucosylated mucin 2 protein were observed in the intestines of AhRΔIEC mice fed a WD (figure 7E,F). These data suggest that the intestinal homeostasis and fucosylation were compromised after feeding a WD to AhRΔIEC mice.

Fucoidan supplementation reduces MASH in AhRΔIEC mice via improving intestinal fucosylation and barrier function

Fucoidan is a class of sulfated fucose-rich polysaccharides found in brown seaweed and has been suggested to exert antiobesity activities39; including anti-inflammatory and hepatoprotective activities.40 41 To determine whether the reduction of gut fucosylation contributes to the development of MASH in AhR ΔIEC mice, AhRΔIEC mice were fed an HSPC diet along with fucoidan for 4 months. Fucoidan treatment ameliorated steatohepatitis in AhRΔIEC mice, as indicated by reduced ALT and AST levels and collagen deposition but not hepatic lipid accumulation (figure 8A–D). The proportions of M1 pro-inflammatory macrophages were also significantly decreased in treated AhRΔIEC mice (figure 8E). Moreover, the expression of several genes related to liver inflammation and fibrosis, including Col1a1, Fn1, Il33, and Tnfa, but not Acta2, was markedly decreased in fucoidan-treated AhRΔIEC mice (figure 8F). Supplementation of fucoidan also decreased intestinal permeability in AhRΔIEC mice with reduced efflux of FITC-labelled dextran (figure 8G). In addition, glycan attached goblet cells (figure 8H) and fucosylated Muc2 expression (figure 8I) were increased in the colon after fucoidan supplementation. These data suggested that fucoidan treatment is effective against MASH in AhRΔIEC mice via modulating intestinal fucosylation.

Intestinal neutral ceramidase-AhR signal contributes to diet-induced lung inflammation in mice

Obesity and MASLD are increasingly recognised as systemic inflammatory disorders and risk factors for developing certain respiratory diseases.42,46 In MASLD/MASH, gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability can promote the systemic dissemination of microbial metabolites and inflammatory mediators that modulate innate immune responses in distal organs, including the lung.7 47 48 Therefore, we assessed whether intestinal Asah2-AhR deficiency also affects lung function under WD conditions. Asah2ΔIEC mice displayed reduced lung inflammation (online supplemental figure S5A) with significant reduction in the total cell numbers recovered from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (online supplemental figure S5B). Further, Asah2∆IEC mice on a WD had more anti-inflammatory macrophages (online supplemental figure S5C), and a reduction in the percentage of pulmonary CD4+ IL-17A+ Th17 cells (online supplemental figure S5D) compared with WT mice. In contrast, there are no changes in the percentages of CD4+ IFN-γ+ Th1 cells, CD4+ GATA3+ Th2 cells, CD4+ IL-5+ Th2 cells and CD4+ Foxp3+ regulatory T cells in the lungs of MASH Asah2∆IEC mice compared with WT mice (online supplemental figure S5D,E). We also performed transcriptomic profiling of lung tissue by RNA-seq in WT and Asah2ΔIEC mice receiving a WD. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of lung tissue genes revealed that the multiple obesity-related pathways including linoleic acid metabolism, arachidonic acid metabolism and IL-17 signalling were enriched in WT mice (online supplemental figure S5F). We validated the expression of the genes with qPCR that are known targets of IL-17 signalling pathway consisting of Cxcl5, Ccl17 and Il1b and lung fibrosis factors, that included Col3a1, Tgfb and Timp1 mRNA, and confirmed their downregulation in the lungs of Asah2ΔIEC mice (online supplemental figure S5G). In contrast, AhRΔIEC mice exhibited more leucocytes in BALF (online supplemental figure S6A), peribronchial inflammatory cell infiltration (online supplemental figure S6B) and collagen deposition (online supplemental figure S6C) in the lung than did control mice. AhRΔIEC mice have higher expression of genes associated with lung inflammation (Il33, Timp1 and Acta2) (online supplemental figure S6D). Analysis of innate immune cell infiltration revealed an increased accumulation of neutrophils and a reduction of eosinophils in the lungs of AhRΔIEC mice fed a WD compared with WT mice (online supplemental figure S6E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that intestine-specific neutral ceramidase-AhR signal deficiency regulates lung inflammation in mice with MASH.

Discussion

Intestinal epithelial-specific deletion of neutral ceramidase attenuates hepatic inflammation and fibrosis in MASH without affecting steatosis, revealing a dissociation between lipid accumulation and downstream injury. Mechanistically, intestinal neutral ceramidase deficiency alters the gut microbiota composition and inhibits the release of microbial metabolite 2-HHA, which is more likely to reduce gut epithelium fucosylation via inactivation of AhR signalling in IECs. Increased gut fucosylation can enhance barrier integrity and reduce translocation of microbial products such as LPS. This dampening of gut-derived inflammatory signals could limit the recruitment and polarisation of Tim4−Clec2−CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages and thereby blunting stellate cell activation and fibrosis despite persistent steatosis in Asah2ΔIEC mice. Our findings highlight the critical role of the gut–liver axis, where intestinal sphingolipid remodelling and fucosylation shape hepatic macrophage phenotypes and fibrogenic responses independent of hepatocellular lipid load.

We previously demonstrated that neutral ceramidase is a contributor to MASH by regulating intestinal IgA B cells and gut microbiota.19 Here, we further showed that Asah2 ablation in IECs maintains intestinal barrier integrity, concomitant with altered gut microbiota and fucosylated mucin. Fucosylation of mucins has a profound influence on host–microbial symbiosis and gut barrier function.49 In particular, pathogenic Lachnospiraceae, Alistipes sp and Desulfovibrio were significantly reduced. Enrichment of Lachnospiraceae and Alistipes sp. Marseille has been reported in individuals with MASLD and was shown to induce MASLD development in GF mice.50,52 Desulfovibrio is a pro-inflammatory bacterium enriched in individuals with MASH, and it could exacerbate steatohepatitis in mice with MASH.32 Accumulating evidence suggests that gut microbiota regulates MASH through metabolite-host interactions. Through metabolomic profiling, lower levels of 2-HHA and higher levels of mannose were found in stool samples of Asah2ΔIEC mice. Alistipes indistinctus derived hippuric acid has been reported.53 We discovered that 2-HHA was positively correlated with Lachnospiraceae and Alistipes sp, while the mannose and fucose pathway was negatively correlated with these bacteria. We acknowledge that 2-HHA likely reflects a composite metabolic process involving both microbial and host contributions. While our data support an association between microbiota changes and 2-HHA levels, we cannot exclude the possibility that host metabolic pathways also influence its production. In particular, 2-HHA may arise from microbial generation of precursor compounds (eg, 2-hydroxybenzoic acid or related metabolites), followed by host-mediated conjugation with glycine via enzymes such as GLYAT and acyl-CoA synthetases.54,57 Variations in glycine availability or host conjugation capacity in the liver or IECs could therefore contribute to the observed changes in 2-HHA levels. Given these considerations, 2-HHA should be viewed as a microbiota-influenced metabolite that reflects host–microbe cometabolism rather than a purely microbial product. Future studies directly assessing precursor availability and host conjugation pathways will be important to further delineate the relative contributions of microbial versus host metabolism.

Intestinal barrier dysfunction is an important contributing factor in the development of MASH and airway inflammation. As noted, Asah2 expression has been reported to be increased in MASH, whereas it is decreased in the intestine of patients with alcohol misuse.58 In alcohol-associated bowel disease, barrier disruption is primarily driven by the toxic effects of ethanol and acetaldehyde, which impair tight junctions and damage the epithelium, particularly in the proximal small intestine. This is accompanied by immune alterations, including depletion of intestinal CD8+ T cells, leading to acute and severe barrier dysfunction.2 11 20 59 60 In contrast, MASH-associated gut barrier dysfunction is more gradual and is largely driven by metabolic stress, chronic low-grade inflammation and microbiota dysbiosis. In this context, the role of Asah2 on gut barrier dysfunction may differ substantially in these two settings and requires further investigation.

Our group and others showed that the expression patterns of Fut2 and α1,2-fucose on IECs are spatiotemporally regulated in a commensal-bacteria-dependent manner.22 23 We showed that 2-HHA can reduce intestinal AhR activity in vitro and that AhR signalling contributes to intestinal epithelial fucosylation. AhR knockout mice carrying a deletion of exon 1 develop a liver pathology characterised by portal fibrosis and biliary inflammation.61 Using intestinal AhR deficient mice, we demonstrated that AhR deficiency in IECs reduced gut fucosylation and induced intestinal barrier dysfunction, leading to increased LPS translocation and reshaping hepatic macrophage populations without necessarily affecting steatosis. We observed that Asah2 deficiency does not measurably alter small intestinal barrier integrity and AhR signal under WD conditions. This outcome is consistent with the concept that host–microbiota interactions are more prominent in the colon, where microbial density, metabolic activity and epithelial exposure to microbial products are substantially higher. AhR is expressed in both IECs and immune cells, where it exerts distinct but complementary functional interactions.62 63 In IECs, AhR plays a critical role in maintaining barrier integrity and regulating epithelial differentiation, including the induction of fucosylation-related genes such as Fut2. In contrast, in immune cells (eg, macrophages and innate lymphoid cells),64 65 AhR primarily modulates inflammatory responses and cytokine production (such as IL-22). IL-22 ligation with its receptor IL-22RA leads to the subsequent induction of fucosylation and enhances antimicrobial defence.66 In our study, the mechanistic link between 2-HHA, AhR signalling and Fut2 expression was established in IECs, supporting a direct role of epithelial AhR in regulating intestinal fucosylation. However, we do not exclude the possibility that AhR signalling in immune cells may also contribute indirectly by shaping the intestinal inflammatory environment. Future studies using cell type-specific AhR models will be important to further dissect these contributions. Additional experiments such as the administering 2-HHA to GF mice would provide more mechanistic evidence linking this metabolite to AhR activity and IEC fucosylation in vivo.

Fucoidan may serve as a source of L-fucose following partial degradation by gut microbiota, thereby increasing the availability of fucose for host salvage pathways that support fucosylation. In addition, fucoidan has been shown to modulate gut microbiota composition, enriching fucose-using or fucose-producing bacteria, which can in turn influence luminal fucose levels and epithelial fucosylation. We showed that supplementation with fucoidan can promote gut fucosylation and alleviate MASH development in AhRΔIEC mice. These data suggest that the intestinal microbiota and 2-HHA cooperatively orchestrate epithelial α1,2-fucosylation to maintain a homeostatic microenvironment in response to WD exposure. Recent studies highlight that the role of intestinal α1,2-fucosylation in metabolic disease is context-dependent. While fucosylation has been reported to promote obesity and steatohepatitis in WD models,67 other studies in alcohol-related liver disease show that loss of fucosylation exacerbates liver injury and that restoration of fucosylated glycans is protective.24 68 Our findings align with the latter, demonstrating that impaired intestinal fucosylation which is driven by a microbiota–2-HHA–AhR axis contributes to barrier dysfunction and MASH progression, whereas restoration of fucosylation is protective. These differences likely reflect variations in diet, microbiota composition and microbial metabolism, as well as the dual role of fucosylated glycans in host barrier integrity and microbe–host interactions. Taken together, our results suggest that the anti-fibrogenic activity of fucoidan is due, at least in part, to enhancement of gut fucosylation and barrier function, which can suppress the translocation of LPS that leads to hepatic inflammation.

MASH is a systemic disease that affects multiple organs. The lung is particularly susceptible to systemic inflammatory and metabolic signals due to its extensive vascularisation and large immune cell reservoir, especially alveolar macrophages, which are highly responsive to circulating cues. Emerging studies have shown that obesity and metabolic syndrome are associated with altered pulmonary immune responses and increased susceptibility to airway inflammation and lung injury.69 70 In MASLD/MASH, intestinal dysbiosis and altered gut metabolism can promote the systemic dissemination of microbiota-derived metabolites that modulate macrophage and neutrophil activity in the lung. Although our study highlights 2-HHA as a novel candidate mediator, we recognise that additional gut-derived metabolites are likely involved. Accordingly, liver and lung inflammation should be evaluated to assess the broader systemic immune effects of intestinal metabolic dysregulation rather than to imply a single metabolite-driven mechanism.

Collectively, these data highlight that gut–liver immune crosstalk—regulated by gut neutral ceramidase, IEC fucosylation and LPS translocation—critically shapes hepatic macrophage remodelling and fibrogenesis, independent of hepatocellular lipid accumulation. We discovered that metabolite 2-HHA can inactivate intestinal AhR-Fut2 signalling to decrease gut fucosylation and promote diet-induced liver inflammation and fibrosis. Our study has demonstrated the effect of fucoidan on gut barrier function, which may facilitate the development of dietary interventions for the prevention and treatment of chronic metabolic disorders.
