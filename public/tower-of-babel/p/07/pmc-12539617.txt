Impact of an antimicrobial stewardship bundle on the outcome of high-risk neutropenic patients with fever: a pre-post study
Authors: Suzanne M E Kuijpers, Jara R de la Court, Jan M Prins, Rogier P Schade, Jarom Heijmans, Kim C E Sigaloff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid antibiotic initiation is essential for managing potential infections following chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. However, excessive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics should be avoided. Implementing antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) in haematology units is challenging due to high infection-related risks, and data on interventions remain scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the safety of an AMS bundle on a haematology ward.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care centre among patients with high-risk (≥7 days) neutropenia. The AMS bundle consisted of replacing empirical meropenem with ceftazidime, and shortening treatment duration to 2 days in case of defervescence and negative blood cultures. Data on ICU admission, all-cause mortality, antimicrobial use and microorganisms identified were compared with a (retrospective) pre-intervention cohort.ResultsA total of 396 patients were included (206 pre-intervention, 190 post-intervention). Allogeneic stem cell transplantations increased from 21% pre-intervention to 37% post-intervention. There was no statistical difference in the adjusted composite endpoint of ICU admission and all-cause mortality [adjusted HR (aHR) 1.46; 95% CI, 0.76–2.81; P = 0.26]. Pre-intervention, meropenem was prescribed in 99% of patients, which shifted to ceftazidime in 78% post-intervention. Median antibiotic treatment duration decreased from 8.0 to 5.0 days (P < 0.001), and empirical antibiotic consumption decreased from 12 to 8 days of therapy per patient (P < 0.001). Bloodstream infections with Candida spp. decreased from 17 pre-intervention to 5 post-intervention (P = 0.03).ConclusionsThe AMS bundle led to a shift towards narrower-spectrum antibiotics, and reduced treatment duration and overall antibiotic use without a significant impact on the primary safety outcome. The intervention was accompanied by a declining trend in candidaemia incidence.

Introduction

Patients with haematological malignancies frequently experience fever as a complication during episodes of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. Guidelines universally recommend prompt initiation of empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics, as it has been known to improve clinical outcomes.1 Broad-spectrum β-lactams such as meropenem, imipenem, piperacillin/tazobactam or ceftazidime are among the preferred empirical treatments.2 However, prolonged use of these broad-spectrum antibiotics increases the risk of antibiotic-related adverse events such as disruption of the normal gut microbiome, antimicrobial resistance and occurrence of candidaemia.3,4

One way to potentially reduce the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics is to reserve these agents only for patients at high risk of infection with resistant organisms. The use of surveillance cultures can help to restrict the administration of broader-spectrum antibiotics such as carbapenems only to those patients who are colonized with third-generation cephalosporin–resistant (3GCR) Enterobacterales or Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Several observational studies have shown a high predictive value of surveillance cultures for the pathogen identified in subsequent bloodstream infections.5,6 Nevertheless, carbapenems are still widely used, even in the Netherlands, where prevalence of 3GCR is low.7

An additional strategy to limit total antibiotic usage is to shorten the duration of treatment for fever in neutropenic patients.8–11 The European guidelines for empirical antibacterial therapy in neutropenic patients (ECIL4) state that empirical antibiotics can be discontinued after 72 h in patients who have been haemodynamically stable since presentation or have been afebrile since 48 h.12 Since 2022, Dutch guidelines for febrile neutropenia advocate cessation of antibiotic treatment after 48 h if the patient is afebrile, clinically stable, and blood cultures are negative.13 In daily practice, however, the average treatment duration is usually still longer.

Although implementation of antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) interventions have been generally proven safe, data concerning the implementation and safety of AMS interventions in the setting of febrile neutropenia are scarce.14 The objective of the present study was to evaluate the implementation and safety of a combined AMS bundle aimed at reducing both the spectrum and duration of antibiotic treatment in patients with high-risk neutropenia.

Methods

Study setting and design

This pre-post study included a prospective cohort and a retrospective control group. Since June 2018, the two academic hospitals AMC and VUmc in Amsterdam have merged to form Amsterdam University Medical Centres (Amsterdam UMC). The two former haematology departments both performed autologous and allogeneic bone marrow transplantations, but historically employed different empirical regimens for patients with high-risk febrile neutropenia. In one centre, a carbapenem (meropenem) was used. In the other centre a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftazidime) was used. Only data from the centre that used meropenem (pre-intervention) were included in the retrospective control group (Figure 1). In 2019, an AMS bundle was introduced at both sites. This bundle comprised: (i) standard empirical treatment consisting of a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftazidime), with a carbapenem (meropenem) reserved for patients colonized with third-generation cephalosporin–resistant Enterobacterales or P. aeruginosa, and (ii) shortening of the duration of empirical treatment from standard 9 days to 48 h in case of defervescence and negative blood cultures (Figure 2). Data from both sites were collected for the post-intervention cohort (Figure 1). This new local guideline was developed and implemented through a multidisciplinary approach involving a haematologist, an infectious disease specialist, and a medical microbiologist to ensure acceptance among all stakeholders. Additional studies were conducted to demonstrate the safety of the proposed changes.5,15 The active involvement of haematologists in interpreting these findings played a key role in fostering confidence in the guideline’s safety. The protocol was widely disseminated and discussed during clinical rounds. The antibiotic therapy for all patients with febrile neutropenia was discussed and any deviations from the protocol were questioned. A ‘lead-in’ period was used to ensure secure implementation of the protocol before data collection began.

Ethics

This study was approved by the research ethics review committee of the Amsterdam University Medical Centres, Amsterdam, Netherlands. Participants were informed about the study with the option to opt out.

Patients and data collection

The retrospective control cohort has been previously described; inclusion criteria were identical to those applied in the post-intervention cohort.5 For this current study only the first febrile episode registered in this cohort was used. Pre-intervention data were collected from 13 March 2016 until 1 April 2019. For the post-intervention cohort, all admitted patients on the haematology ward were prospectively screened to assess who developed fever after the start of chemotherapy. We included patients receiving haematological treatments known to induce high-risk neutropenia, defined as neutrophil counts <500 cells/mm3 expected to persist for more than 7 days. For the current analysis, we only included data from the first febrile episode in this cohort, regardless of the number of prior treatments received. Patients could be included in the study only once. Patients who received, at the start of the febrile episode, therapeutic antibiotic treatment other than the standard antimicrobial prophylaxis, as described in Figure 2, were excluded.16 The stewardship bundle was adopted in the supportive care protocol on 1 November 2019. After implementation, data collection post-intervention started on 1 September 2020 and was completed on 19 October 2023.

The following variables were collected: demographics, type of haematological malignancy, and type and start date of chemotherapy. For each febrile episode, the start date of fever, the prophylactic antibiotic used at that moment, and the empirical antibiotics started were recorded. It was recorded whether after 3 days antibiotics were stopped, continued or switched. Blood culture results of cultures drawn between 1 day before and 1 day after the start of empirical antibiotic treatment were extracted from the microbiology system (GLIMS). Antimicrobial susceptibility was tested using standard procedures: screening using automated systems supplemented with gradient tests and/or the disc diffusion. The S/I/R interpretation of the susceptibility results was based on the EUCAST 2022 guidelines.17 During the study period, EUCAST issued revised guidelines with regard to the reporting of S (susceptible), I (susceptible, increased exposure) and R (resistant) of clinical isolates. For clarity reasons we retrospectively applied the breakpoint guidelines of 2024 to all isolates that were included in the study. This led to all ceftazidime-susceptible P. aeruginosa being reclassified as I.17 As post-intervention the increased dosage of ceftazidime was used, the adjustment from S to I did not have implications for the study.

After 30 days, information on empirical antibiotic treatment duration, empirical and total antibiotic consumption, neutrophil recovery, ICU transfer, and date of hospital release or date of death was collected. If the primary outcome was reached, additional information was gathered on the underlying cause of ICU admission or death. Results from Clostridioides difficile PCR on faeces, and blood cultures positive for Candida sp., were collected until 6 months after inclusion.

Definitions

High-risk neutropenia was defined as neutrophil counts <500 cells/mm3 expected to persist for more than 7 days. Fever was defined as a body temperature of more than 38.3°C measured once or above 38.0°C measured twice (measurements 1 h apart). ICU admission was defined as admission for more than 12 h. ICU admission or mortality was defined as infection-related when a patient died from direct complications of infection (e.g. sepsis, persistent signs of infection; ongoing fever, persistent positive blood culture). It was defined as possibly infection-related if the event did not fit the definitions of infection-related or non-infection-related. Infection may have been a contributing factor to the patient's death, but it is uncertain whether it was the primary cause. It was defined as non-infection-related when there was a definite other cause, without signs or symptoms of relapse of infection at time of death. This was determined by an adjudication committee of three independent specialists, who were unaware of which cohort the patient belonged to. The duration of antibiotic treatment was determined by counting all consecutive days on which a patient received antibacterial treatment starting from the first day of febrile neutropenia [regardless of (de-)escalation based on culture results]. Antibiotic consumption was calculated using days of therapy (DOTs) for empirical (ceftazidime and meropenem) and total antibiotic use 30 days after fever onset. The DOT refers to the number of days a patient receives an antibiotic regardless of dose, route or frequency.18 DOTs of IV vancomycin were included to analyse whether modification of the empirical antibiotic regimen would lead to overuse of vancomycin to cover Gram-positive bacteria.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was a composite outcome of ICU admission and/or all-cause mortality within 30 days after a febrile episode onset. The secondary outcomes were (all within 30 days): definite or possible infection-related ICU admission and/or all-cause mortality, ICU admission and all-cause mortality separately, duration of antibiotic use and antibiotic consumption, number and type of cultured microorganisms, inappropriate empirical treatment for bloodstream infections with Gram-negative bacteria (GNB), and adverse events (within 6 months) such as C. difficile infections and bloodstream infections with Candida spp. Inappropriate empirical antibiotic treatment was defined as treatment not covering the susceptibility pattern of the cultured GNB. For comparison of inappropriate empirical antibiotic treatment, 3GCR-GNB microorganisms that are intrinsically resistant to both ceftazidime and meropenem (i.e. Stenotrophomonas) were disregarded as neither empirical regimen was intended to cover these pathogens and their inclusion would have biased comparisons. As empirical treatment for febrile neutropenia is primarily targeted at GNB, potential inappropriate treatment of Gram-positive bacteria was not compared pre- and post-intervention.

Statistical methods

Baseline categorical values were presented as numbers with percentages. Continuous variables were presented as median with IQRs. The chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyse categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test to assess differences in non-normally distributed continuous variables. Kaplan–Meier estimates were plotted to compare ICU admission and/or all-cause mortality pre- and post-intervention. Start of survival time for the primary outcome was set at the first day of febrile neutropenia. A Cox regression analysis was performed to estimate the association between the pre- and post-intervention period and the primary outcome. First, a crude analysis was performed. Second, a multivariable analysis was performed for a priori decided potential confounders including age, sex, whether a stem cell transplantation was performed, and duration of neutropenia. In addition, these analyses were repeated to assess infection-related ICU admission and/or all-cause mortality. Covariates that showed non-proportional hazards over time by visual inspection of Schoenfeld residuals were added as time-dependent covariates to the model. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Participants

A total of 396 patients were enrolled: 206 before and 190 after introduction of the AMS bundle. The post-intervention group contained a larger proportion of patients with acute leukaemia/blastic transformation (85% versus 68% pre-intervention) and a smaller proportion of plasma cell disorders (2% versus 12% in the pre-intervention group). In the post-intervention group a larger proportion of patients underwent an allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (37% post-intervention versus 21% pre-intervention) (Table 1).

Outcome data

In total 39 patients reached the composite outcome of ICU admission and/or all-cause mortality within 30 days after a febrile episode onset (16 pre-intervention versus 23 post-intervention). No significant difference was found in ICU admission or all-cause mortality within 30 days after start of fever between the pre- and post-intervention cohort (Figure 3). A multivariable analysis was performed to assess risk factors for ICU admission and all-cause mortality within 30 days after start of fever, including age, sex, whether a stem cell transplantation was performed, and the duration of neutropenia until inclusion. The adjusted HR (aHR) for the combined endpoint was 1.46 (95% CI, 0.76–2.81; P = 0.26) (Table 2). The multivariable analysis was repeated to assess the outcomes of ICU admission (aHR 1.45; 95% CI, 0.70–3.00; P = 0.31) and mortality (HR 1.50; 95% CI, 0.60–3.72; P = 0.38) separately (data not shown). The aHR for the definite or possible infection-attributable combined endpoint was 1.26 (95% CI, 0.60–2.66; P = 0.54).

Antibiotic use

Table 3 shows the results of antibiotic use pre- and post-intervention. A shift in empirical antibiotic treatment was observed from meropenem (99%) to ceftazidime (78%). A decrease was observed in median antibiotic treatment duration in the post-intervention group, from a median of 8.0 days pre-intervention to 5.0 days (P < 0.001). Among patients with positive blood cultures for a GNB, the median treatment duration decreased from 11.5 days pre-intervention to 7.5 days post-intervention (P = 0.01); in patients in whom no GNB was cultured the median treatment duration decreased from 8.0 days pre-intervention to 4.0 days post-intervention (P < 0.001). The combined empirical antibiotic consumption in DOTs of meropenem and ceftazidime within 30 days after inclusion decreased from 2406 (12 DOTs per person) to 1573 (8 DOTs per person) (P < 0.001). Comparing pre- and post-intervention, no increase in vancomycin use as add-on treatment was observed (3 to 4 DOTs per patient, P = 0.41). The DOTs of empirical and targeted antimicrobial consumption combined decreased from 3231 DOTs, 16 per person, to 2677 DOTs, 14 DOTs per person (P = 0.03). Similar results were found when analysing only patients with a negative blood culture at inclusion (see Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Cultured microorganisms

Of the included patients, 59 (29%) patients had at least one positive blood culture in the pre-intervention cohort compared with 69 (36%) post-intervention (P = 0.13); 22 (11% of patients) were positive for at least one GNB pre-intervention, versus 16 post-intervention (8%) (Table 3). In both the pre- and post-intervention cohort two cultures contained a GNB that was not susceptible to empirical treatment: pre-intervention, P. aeruginosa and Pseudomonas putida, neither of which are susceptible to meropenem (but susceptible to ceftazidime); post-intervention, Fusobacterium nucleatum and another anaerobic Gram-negative rod (which could not be specified further), neither susceptible to ceftazidime. No 3GCR Enterobacterales or Pseudomonas species were cultured post-intervention that were not preceded by positive surveillance cultures. More Gram-positive bacteria were cultured post-intervention (76) compared with pre-intervention (48), mainly because of an increased number of CoNS and Enterococcus spp. Table S2 shows the distribution of pathogens in the pre- and post-intervention cohort.

Adverse events

In both the pre- and post-intervention cohort eight patients had a faecal PCR positive for C. difficile within 6 months after start of the empirical antibiotic treatment (P = 1.00). In the same period, 17 patients in the pre-intervention cohort had a blood culture positive for Candida species, compared with 5 in the post-intervention cohort (P = 0.03) (Table 3 and Table S3).

Discussion

In 2019, an AMS bundle comprising empirical antibiotic treatment based on surveillance cultures and shortening of the treatment duration for patients with high-risk febrile neutropenia was implemented on the haematology ward of a tertiary care centre. ICU admission and all-cause mortality rates were not statistically different for the pre- and post-intervention cohort, especially when considering duration of neutropenia before inclusion and restricting the analysis to (possibly) infection-related deaths. A shift was seen from carbapenem to third-generation cephalosporin use, alongside a reduction in both the duration of antibiotic treatment (from 8.0 to 5.0 days) and overall empirical antibiotic use (from 2406 to 1573 DOTs). Furthermore, we observed a possible decline in bloodstream infections with Candida spp. AMS in haematology patients is often described as complicated due to the complexity and severity of the disease with the concomitant vulnerability of patients. However, this study shows that stewardship interventions in a haematology population are possible and effective.

Haematology patients are at risk for MDR microorganisms due to the often prolonged and consecutive courses of antimicrobial treatment.19 Previous antibiotic exposure is an independent risk factor for acquisition of multi-resistant pathogens.20 Where possible, limiting the treatment duration and de-escalation based on culture results are strategies to limit antimicrobial use. In an earlier study by our group we showed that the majority of bloodstream infections with 3GCR-GNB are preceded by positive surveillance cultures with a 3GCR-GNB.5 This corroborates findings from a number of other studies.6,21 These studies conclude that carbapenems can be reserved for patients with 3GCR Enterobacterales or resistant P. aeruginosa in surveillance cultures. Our study suggests that restricting carbapenem use to patients colonized with 3GCR-GNB did not lead to an increase in the number of patients inadequately treated with a third-generation cephalosporin, ceftazidime.

Several studies have shown non-inferiority of short versus extended treatment duration of neutropenic fever of unknown origin in haematology populations.10,22,23 However, the reduction of treatment duration has not been widely implemented in daily practice. Our observational study shows that implementation of a stewardship bundle was successful in shortening the duration of antibiotic treatment, without an increase in ICU admission and overall mortality rates. Importantly, overall empirical antibiotic use (meropenem and ceftazidime), and the total antibiotic consumption (excluding prophylaxis) measured in DOTs in the 30 days following febrile neutropenia also decreased.

Around the time of inclusion we observed an increase in cultured Gram-positive bacteria, predominantly CoNS, which usually reflects contamination. Since blood cultures were drawn between 1 day before and 1 day after inclusion, the intervention itself cannot have caused this difference in cultured microorganisms. However, the overall number of blood cultures drawn increased in the post-intervention period leading to higher overall numbers of cultured Gram-positive bacteria. This increase may partly be explained by a change in practice introduced in the post-intervention period recommending the collection of peripheral blood cultures in addition to cultures from the central venous catheter. Contaminated cultures can lead to unnecessary antibiotic use.24 In our study, however, we did not find an increase in vancomycin use comparing the pre- and post-intervention cohorts.

Our study shows a reduction in bloodstream infections with Candida spp. between the pre- and post-intervention group. This could potentially be explained by the change in empirical antibiotic treatment regimen. Long-term antibiotic exposure promotes susceptibility to invasive fungal infections.25 In several studies, the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics including carbapenems has been associated with the occurrence of candidaemia.26–28 An earlier study in non-neutropenic patients showed that a reduction of broad-spectrum antibiotic use (especially carbapenems) reduced the incidence of candidaemia.26 Another study showed that an educational AMS programme was effective in reducing the incidence of hospital-acquired candidaemia.29

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is that the pre- and post-intervention design shows real-world data after implementation of an AMS bundle and thus underpins the safety of this intervention in clinical practice. The pre- and post-intervention design used in this study also has its limitations. Due to this design the included haematological malignancies and treatments were not equally distributed over the pre- and post-intervention cohorts. Fully correcting for the difference between types of malignancies and increasing rates of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation was not possible due to the sample size. These differences might reflect the increase in disease severity in our centre over the years, but despite this increasing severity, ICU admission and all-cause mortality rates did not increase. Another limitation is the relatively low number of events (ICU admission and mortality), which may have limited the ability to detect small differences in HRs. Moreover, we did not systematically capture compliance with the bundle (e.g. discontinuation at 48 h), which would have provided valuable information on adoption of the protocol. Finally, the study was conducted in a low-resistance setting and antibiotic choices should always be adapted to local epidemiology. We do, however, believe that restricting the use of unnecessary broad-spectrum antibiotics and discontinuation of empirical therapy after 48 h of defervescence and negative blood cultures is also relevant for other settings.

Conclusions

This study shows the results of the successful implementation of a stewardship bundle on a haematology ward in an academic tertiary care centre without a significant increase in ICU admissions or mortality rates. This study indicates that stewardship interventions in a haematology population are possible and beneficial.
