Global status, bias risks, and ecological integration of wild bat and rodent virome research☆
Authors: Xiang Zhou-fu, Wang Xin, Wu Wan-hua, Wang Han, Huang Ting-sheng, Chen Si-jun, Chen Shu-liang, Chen Liang-jun, Hou Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wild bats and rodents are critical zoonotic reservoirs. However, research limitations pose challenges for understanding virome dynamics and predicting emerging risks. Key issues include geographic biases (disproportionate sampling in Asia and North America vs. under-representation in Africa and Middle East) and taxonomic biases (overemphasis on accessible species over ecologically diverse hosts). Additionally, data fragmentation – stemming from inconsistent sequencing protocols and incomplete metadata - hampers comparative analyses. Crucially, most viral risk prediction models exclude ecological parameters (e.g., host behavior, climate), limiting their predictive accuracy.This review evaluates these knowledge gaps and methodological shortcomings, emphasizing the need for a coupled “virus-host-environment” framework. We propose actionable solutions: (i) establishing standardized transnational databases, (ii) strategically expanding surveillance in underrepresented regions, and (iii) integrating ecological variables. These steps will advance robust predictive modeling and enhance global emerging disease monitoring.

Introduction

Wild bats [1], [2] and rodents [3], [4] are well-established natural reservoirs for nume`rous high-risk zoonotic pathogens. These animals harbor diverse viral communities, exhibiting both long-term co-evolutionary relationships with their hosts [5] and demonstrable potential for cross-species transmission [6]. While surveillance of wildlife viruses remains a critical research priority, conventional approaches have predominantly focused on detecting known pathogenic agents [7]. This targeted strategy has created significant gaps in our understanding of the full spectrum of viral diversity (the “virome”) within these reservoirs. The advent of high-throughput sequencing (HTS) technologies - particularly metagenomic and metatranscriptomic sequencing - has revolutionized the systematic characterization of viral diversity. These culture-independent, unbiased methods enable the detection of both recognized pathogenic viruses and previously uncharacterized or novel DNA/RNA viruses. This capability has dramatically accelerated virome discovery and facilitates the sequence-based risk profiling of novel viruses [8], [9]. However, a key limitation persists: HTS-derived sequence data alone cannot determine whether a detected virus represents a stable, co-evolved component of the host virome or a potential “spillover candidate” posing a zoonotic threat [10]. To address this gap, recent studies emphasize the necessity of ecological integration - a multidimensional analytical framework that integrates virological data with host ecological characteristics, environmental factors, and spatial dimensions. For instance, this approach incorporates host systematics, population dynamics, and geographic distribution patterns alongside climatic variables (e.g., temperature, precipitation), land-use types, and topographic features, while also accounting for spatial proximity between species and altitudinal gradients to contextualize viral evolution and zoonotic risk assessment [11], [12].

Bats and rodents, despite both serving as important viral reservoirs, differ fundamentally in their biology, ecology, and evolutionary history—differences that profoundly shape their respective roles in zoonotic pathogen emergence. Bats (Chiroptera), the only mammals capable of sustained flight, exhibit unique immunological adaptations that distinguish them from other mammalian reservoirs. Transcriptomic analyses reveal that bat cells maintain a core pluripotency expression profile resembling that of virally challenged cells, suggesting evolved intrinsic mechanisms to tolerate high viral sequence burdens without triggering detrimental inflammatory responses [13]. Their ability to traverse vast geographic ranges and aggregate in large, dense colonies further facilitates viral maintenance and dissemination. In contrast, rodents (Rodentia), with approximately twice the species richness of bats, have historically been the predominant source of human infectious diseases. Their role as viral reservoirs appears more strongly driven by ecological factors—specifically, wide geographic distributions, large population sizes, and high densities that promote frequent intra- and interspecific contacts. Comparative virome studies reveal that while bats may harbor a greater number of viruses per species, viruses associated with rodents exhibit significantly higher potential for cross-species transmission, likely reflecting their broader habitat utilization and closer proximity to human settlements [12].

Given that existing reviews have predominantly focused on either bat viromes or rodent viromes in isolation, or have examined ecological spillover drivers without systematic integration of HTS-derived viral diversity data, a unified synthesis that bridges these perspectives is needed to guide future surveillance efforts. In this study, we address these gaps through three primary objectives. First, we compile and synthesize recent advances in the application of high-throughput sequencing to bat and rodent virome research. This synthesis encompasses: (i) characterizing the global spectrum of viruses harbored by wild bats and rodents, (ii) elucidating the distribution patterns of risky zoonotic viruses on the WHO priority list, and (iii) documenting established transmission routes. Collectively, this integrated analysis overcomes the limitations inherent in conventional, targeted surveillance approaches.

Second, building on this foundation and while acknowledging the constraints of sequence-based inference, we advocate for two critical methodological needs: (i) the standardization of virome characterization frameworks to enhance research comparability, and (ii) the intensification of virome exploration in understudied geographic and taxonomic groups.

Third, we propose that virome studies on bats and rodents should systematically incorporate environmental metadata at the time of sampling—including precise geographic coordinates, local microclimate, habitat type, and collection date—and integrate these field-derived observations with publicly available environmental datasets (e.g., satellite-derived vegetation indices, land surface temperature, precipitation patterns, land use dynamics, and elevation) to reconstruct the ecological context of viral detection. This environment-centered approach enables the development of geographically scalable models that link viral dynamics to measurable ecological drivers, facilitating ecologically informed spillover risk assessment without requiring intensive in situ monitoring.

Addressing these three objectives is essential to improve the completeness and predictive power of global zoonotic risk monitoring, moving beyond descriptive virome cataloging toward a truly integrative and predictive understanding of zoonotic emergence.

Methods

Collection of NGS based publications

We conducted a search in the PubMed database for literature published prior to November 30, 2024, using the query string: ((bat) OR (rodent)) AND ((virus) OR (virome)) AND ((metagenome) OR (metagenomics) OR (metatranscriptomics) OR (metatranscriptome)). This initial search yielded 484 articles. Three authors independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts against the inclusion criterion of employing mNGS to investigate viral communities in wild bats or rodents. Studies were retained only upon consensus among all three authors, resulting in 147 eligible publications (Supplementary Table A.1). From these studies, we compiled metadata including sampling years, geographic locations, host species, total number of screened specimens (captured or collected) and viral profiles. Additionally, we retrieved all associated sequences deposited in NCBI GenBank, which were annotated for accession numbers, taxonomic classification, host origin, and collection dates.

Phylogenetic analysis of nine viral families' sequences

Viral sequences were categorized by family and aligned using MAFFT (v7.526) [14] with the E-INS-i algorithm (−-maxiterate 1000 –genafpair). Poorly aligned regions were automatically trimmed using trimAl (v1.4.rev22) [15] with the -automated1 heuristic. Only RdRp sequences containing at least one conserved amino acid motif in the RdRp domain (e.g., SDD or GDD) were included, and sequences shorter than 200 amino acids were excluded to ensure alignment reliability and phylogenetic accuracy. Maximum-likelihood trees were reconstructed in IQ-TREE (v2.3.4) [16] based on conserved RdRp domains, with substitution models selected via ModelFinder. Branch support was assessed using ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) with 2000 replicates (−bb 2000). Clades were defined as monophyletic groups supported by an ultrafast bootstrap value of ≥70%. Sequences from different countries falling within the same well-supported clade were classified as transnational genetically similar sequences. Final trees were visualized using MEGA11.

Collection of documented transmission pathways for nine viral families

To evaluate spillover risks, we focused on vertebrate-associated virus families as recorded by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) that are known to circulate in bats and rodents. By cross-referencing these virus families with the World Health Organization (WHO) priority pathogen list [17], we identified nine virus families of concern - eight classified as high-risk (Arenaviridae, Coronaviridae, Filoviridaer, Flaviviridae, Hantaviridae, Orthomyxoviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Poxviridae) and one as medium-risk (Picornaviridae). For each of these nine virus families, we conducted searches on PubMed and authoritative health information websites using the query format “virus family name + transmission”. These targeted web searches focused on official public health agency websites—including the World Health Organization (WHO), the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and local health departments—to capture authoritative outbreak reports, supplemented by reputable medical references (e.g., MSD Manual, WebMD) for comprehensive coverage. We sought to compile documented routes of transmission and vectors from both peer-reviewed literature and authoritative reports. This search strategy yielded 40 peer-reviewed publications and 11 website reports (comprising 7 official public health agency sources and 4 reputable medical references), which were synthesized to characterize the transmission pathways. All data sources mentioned above are summarized in Supplementary Table A.8.

Results

Geographic and species biases in wild bat and rodent virome research

Systematic virome investigations have been conducted through sampling of wild rodents and bats across 50 regions worldwide, accumulating a total of 75,203 pre-sequencing screened specimens in current datasets. China dominates these efforts, contributing 47,001 specimens (62.5% of the global total) across multiple studies [12], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]. Other regionswith substantial sampling efforts (>500 specimens) include Switzerland [23], [24], Brazil [25], [26], [27], Thailand [28], Madagascar [29], Malaysia [26], [30], [31], the United States [32], [33], [34], Cambodia [28], [35], Kenya [36], [37], Germany [38], [39], Myanmar [40], and Bulgaria [39]. This review reveals persistent and concerning geographic disparities in surveillance intensity. Notably, entire regions—including Central America, Oceania, Scandinavia, Central Asia, and Eastern Europe—had no identified studies, representing critical and geographically widespread surveillance voids.

Current research reveals a striking global imbalance between bat and rodent virome surveillance efforts. Bat-related investigations have been conducted in 42 regions, significantly outnumbering only 20 regions with dedicated rodent virome studies. Geographically, bat virome research demonstrates broad coverage, spanning six continents. Europe leads in coverage density with studies in 11 countries, followed closely by Africa (10 countries) and Asia (10 countries). North America (3 countries) and South America (6 countries) also show notable research activity. The studies from two regionsof Oceania focused exclusively on bats. Conversely, rodent surveillance remains markedly insufficient across most regions. Asia stands out as the most active continent for rodent studies, with research in 6 regions(though still representing only about half of its studied nations). Other regions exhibit critical sampling gaps. Europe and Africa each have studies in only 4 countries, as well as South America in only 3 countries. All 3 regions in North America were represented in the available studies, covering Canada [41], the United States, and Mexico [42]. Notably, Southeast Asia (encompassing Laos [28], Thailand, Cambodia [28], [35], and Singapore [43], [44], [45], [46]) represents the most concentrated area of rodent virome research activity. These pronounced disparities underscore an urgent need to strengthen and expand rodent surveillance networks, particularly in the many understudied regions currently lacking adequate coverage.

The observed disparities in viral detection frequency likely reflect substantial sampling biases, as evidenced by the strong correlation between detection rates and specimen numbers (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table A.2 and A.3). Among bats, the highest viral detection frequencies occurred in the genera Rhinolophus, Myotis, Hipposideros, and Miniopterus - directly mirroring their sampling predominance. These bat populations harbored multiple viral families, with Coronaviridae exhibiting particularly high prevalence (especially in Rhinolophus and Myotis), alongside Astroviridae, Caliciviridae, Flaviviridae, Paramyxoviridae, and others. Similarly, rodent studies revealed elevated viral detection frequencies in over-sampled taxa, primarily Rattus, Apodemus, Mus, and Niviventer. The identified viruses spanned diverse families including Arenaviridae, Arteriviridae, Astroviridae, Coronaviridae, Flaviviridae, Paramyxoviridae, Parvoviridae, Picobirnaviridae, and Picornaviridae. Critically, five of these families represent established high-risk zoonotic pathogens: Arenaviridae, Coronaviridae, Flaviviridae, Paramyxoviridae, and Picornaviridae. At the host level, Rattus exhibited the highest viral diversity among rodents. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that robust spillover risk assessment requires both expanded taxonomic coverage and sufficient sampling depth to mitigate current surveillance biases.

The global virome research landscape exhibits profound geographic and taxonomic biases that fundamentally distort our understanding of viral diversity. Current viral surveillance efforts reveal striking geographic imbalances, with substantial sampling gaps persisting across Africa and the Middle East compared to well-characterized regions like Asia and North America. This skewed distribution reflects uneven research capacity rather than ecological priorities. While there are an exceptionally high number of studies from China (>47,000 specimens), which provide valuable insights into viral diversity within its own territory, they do not reflect the virome of high-risk ecosystems elsewhere. Africa is a recognized hotspot for emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) [47], and studies on rodents in the Middle East have revealed significant potential for viral spillover [48]. However, based on our surveillance data collected via mNGS, these regions remain critically understudied.

Similarly, significant taxonomic sampling disparities exist in current monitoring programs, with bat research conducted in 42 regionsversus only 20 for rodents. This imbalance is further exacerbated by disproportionate focus on commonly sampled species - particularly Rhinolophus bats and Rattus rodents, which collectively account for 48% of all specimens. As demonstrated in Fig. 1C and Fig. 1E, the complete concordance between most frequently sampled genera and those with highest virus detection rates indicates that observed “host specificity” likely reflects sampling artifacts rather than true biological patterns. Not all nine WHO-listed priority pathogens are concentrated in intensively sampled taxa - there are differences, and they matter. For example, Arenaviridae and Hantaviridae are primarily rodent-borne [49], [50], while Filoviridae are mainly hosted by bats; moreover, filoviruses often replicate in specific tissues and at low viral loads, reducing their detectability [51]. Most critically, the concentration of 9 WHO-listed priority pathogens in intensively sampled genera highlights a critical uncertainty: whether this distribution represents genuine zoonotic risk or merely detection bias masking potential threats in understudied species.

Data fragmentation in high-risk virus monitoring

Current mNGS analyses of wild bat and rodent viromes show distinct geographical patterns in detecting WHO priority pathogens. Bats exhibit significantly broader geographical distribution of viral detections than rodents, reflecting uneven research emphasis. This highlights critical surveillance gaps in rodent studies that require urgent attention (Supplementary Table A.4 and A.5). Southeast Asia has detected all 9 high-risk viral families (Filoviridae only detected in China [52], [53]), though archipelagic nations remain understudied. In Africa, 7 viral families have been identified in bats and 4 in rodents (lacking Filoviridae), with geographical coverage limited to less than 30% of the continental area. The scarcity of filoviruses from Africa is indeed noteworthy, given the region's recurrent Ebola outbreaks. This gap likely stems from several factors: mNGS exhibits lower sensitivity than qRT-PCR (31.4% vs. 64.5% detection rates during the 2014 Congo outbreak [54]), higher costs and longer turnaround times that hinder its use in emergency field settings [55]. Additionally, sampling and capture limitations may further explain the scarcity of filovirus detections. First, suspected reservoir hosts such as Rousettus and other fruit bats roost in remote, inaccessible caves and forest canopies, making them difficult to capture and sample systematically. Second, filoviruses like Ebola often persist in low-level, transient infections in reservoir hosts; viral RNA may be present only in certain tissues (e.g., spleen, liver) or during narrow time windows, so standard non-lethal sampling (e.g., oral swabs, feces) frequently misses the virus. Third, field conditions in rural Africa limit access to appropriate preservatives, cold chains, and BSL-4 facilities, degrading viral nucleic acid before laboratory testing. Together, these ecological and operational constraints mean that even if filoviruses are circulating in local wildlife populations, current sampling strategies may fail to detect them. Europe also reported Filoviridae [56] alongside 7 high-risk families, while the Americas documented the same viral family number. Notably, Coronaviridae shows the most extensive geographical spread in bats, aligning with species-level detection frequencies (Fig. 2C). These findings emphasize the need for expanded monitoring across wider regions, more diverse host species, and larger sample sizes to establish comprehensive viral profiles. It is crucial to emphasize that the significant sampling biases identified in this review primarily undermine the reliability and generalizability of risk assessment models, rather than directly causing deterministic risk over- or underestimation. Such data biases introduce uncertainty at two critical levels: regarding model completeness, systematic undersampling in viral diversity hotspots (e.g., tropical Africa and Southeast Asia) means existing models fail to incorporate potentially critical high-risk viruses from these regions, rendering them inherently incapable of capturing the full spectrum of global viral threats [57]; regarding model representativeness, overreliance on easily accessible species leads to model parameters overfitting the ecological traits of these “common hosts”, significantly diminishing extrapolation validity when predictions are applied to ecologically distinct “rare hosts” [6]. Consequently, risk rankings derived from current imbalanced data should be treated as preliminary references subject to substantial uncertainty, urgently requiring the filling of critical data gaps to establish more robust predictive frameworks [58].

This review obtained 2376 sequences from 9 high-risk virus families in bats and 902 sequences in rodents. The largest proportion of submissions belonged to the Coronaviridae (Fig. 2C). A total of 61 distinct virus sequence types were documented, with RdRp being the most common submitted. This was followed by complete genome and polyprotein sequences - both of which include the RdRp region. Variations in sequence submission types may stem from the use of different fragments for species identification across studies, impeding rapid virus detection and timely prevention efforts. For DNA viruses, the absence of common rapid-identification regions necessitates species classification based on fragments specified by ICTV criteria. These criteria are family-specific and often rely on phylogenetic analysis of conserved marker genes. For example, in Adenoviridae, genus demarcation is based on the DNA polymerase (pol) amino acid sequence, with species typically defined by >10–15% divergence under maximum likelihood analysis [59]. In Orthoherpesviridae, classification requires concatenated sequences of six core genes (Uracil-DNA glycosylase, Helicase-primase helicase subunit, DNA packaging terminase subunit 1, Major capsid protein, Envelope glycoprotein B, and DNA polymerase catalytic subunit) to establish monophyletic lineages [60]. Similarly, Papillomaviridae genera are distinguished by <60% L1 gene sequence identity compared to other genera, assessed through global or pairwise alignments [61]. This lack of a universal barcode underscores the need for tailored bioinformatic approaches when analyzing DNA viromes via metagenomic sequencing. In contrast, RNA viruses share conserved RdRp regions, which are recommended as the standard identification marker to facilitate rapid virus characterization [8], [62], [63].

This review constructed phylogenetic trees using 1320 RdRp-containing sequences to examine highly similar sequences among previously uploaded data. Six virus families showed cross-region clustering in the same clade (Fig. 2D): Arenaviridae (1 pair), Hantaviridae (1 pair), Flaviviridae (5 pairs), Paramyxoviridae (1 pair), Picornaviridae (11 pairs), and Coronaviridae (2 pairs). Notably, geographically distant viral sequences also clustered together: Flaviviridae included OR248864 [64] (New Zealand) and OQ715366 [12] (China); Picornaviridae featured 3 China-US pairs and 1 Spain-Hungary pair (PP654855 [65] and KP054278 [66]); Coronaviridae contained China-Denmark (MN535734 [67] and OQ175102 [22]) and China-New Zealand (OQ175130 [22] and OR248861 [64]) pairs. The clustering of RdRp sequences from geographically distinct regions in the same phylogenetic clade may reflect shared ancestry; however, it does not constitute evidence of direct or cryptic transmission. In the absence of full-genome data [68], epidemiological context, and ecological metadata [69], the actual transmission pathways and underlying evolutionary mechanisms remain uncertain.

Based on the comprehensive analysis of detection status and sequence submission patterns across the 9 virus families, two key recommendations emerge: (i) strengthen regional research and monitoring of wild rodents and bats; (ii) establish unified standards to accelerate virus identification and information sharing, thereby enabling swifter policy responses. For species identification, RdRp is recommended as the marker for rapid RNA virus characterization, which would better support integration and sharing of RNA virus data [8], [12]. It should be noted, however, that RdRp has limitations, including potentially poor resolution for closely related viruses and its inapplicability to DNA viruses. Thus, complementary approaches, such as conserved domain profiling for DNA viruses, are necessary for a comprehensive virome assessment. Current sequence submission practices lack consistency, with many entries missing relevant metadata. Notably, the two cross-region clustering virus groups (Hantaviridae and Paramyxoviridae) lack corresponding annotation information. This deficiency prevents us from interpreting their sequences and providing references for understanding viral epidemiology. This gap likely stems from limited awareness of metadata standards among submitters, the frequent absence of contextual data collection at the time of sampling, and the fact that key fields (e.g., host, location, date) remain optional in most public repositories.

Therefore, when uploading virus sequences, researchers should specify not only the sequence region type but also basic metadata including host species, region, and collection year [8]. This will facilitate integration of virus sequence data for subsequent studies and enhance traceability efforts. The inability to clarify transmission pathways stems from two factors: insufficient research providing limited reference data, and lack of ecological information preventing elucidation of the specific drivers behind viral similarity.

Ecological data gaps in viral transmission research

Given that viral spillover risk is associated with distinct transmission modes, we compiled documented transmission routes for all nine viral families, as detailed in Supplementary Table A.8 (Fig. 3). Specifically, Arenaviridae viruses primarily transmit horizontally through specific vectors. These include rodents [70], [71], cattle, pythons [72], and fish [71]. Key transmission routes involve vectors: exposure to blood and body fluids [70], [73], [74], inhalation of aerosols [70], [72], [75], and direct contact with infected animals [70], [71], [74]. Additional routes include contact with rodent excreta [71], [72], [74], contaminated items (e.g., rat urine or feces) [74], rodent bites or scratches [76], and ingestion of excreta-contaminated food [73], [74], [75]. Coronaviridae transmission occurs via three main pathways. Respiratory transmission is a primary route [77], [78], [79], [80], [81], [82]. Direct contact with infected animals [79] or contaminated surfaces also facilitates spread [81], [82]. The fecal-oral route represents a third transmission mechanism [81], [82].

Filoviridae viruses are recognized to have bats as their natural hosts [83], and their transmission occurs through multiple routes. Exposure to blood or body fluids is central [84], [86]. Sexual transmission via semen occurs [83], [84], [85], [86], as does mother-to-child transmission [86]. Contact with body fluid-contaminated items [86], consumption of infected animals [83], [86], and direct contact with infected individuals, animals [87], [88], or bat secretions also play roles [83].

Flaviviridae employs diverse arthropod vectors. Mosquito-borne transmission is common [89], [90]. Tick bites serve as another vector route [91]. Blood transmission [92], [93], sexual transmission [93], [94], and vertical transmission [93], [95] are additional pathways for some flaviviruses.

Hantaviridae transmission is predominantly rodent-borne. Aerosol inhalation is a primary route [96], [97]. Rodent bites [97], [98], ingestion of contaminated food [97], [99], and vertical transmission also occur [97].

Orthomyxoviridae spreads through several mechanisms. Aerosol transmission is primary [100], [101]. Direct contact with contaminated surfaces [101], [102], [103] or infected animals facilitates spread [102]. Tick bites represent another transmission route [104].

Paramyxoviridae transmits horizontally via specific vectors, including bats and fruit bats [105], [106]. Transmission routes include consumption of contaminated food [105], [106] and respiratory droplets [106], [107], [108], [109], [110]. Direct contact with infected bats, pigs, humans [111], contaminated surfaces [107], [109], [110], bat secretions [106], or saliva also contributes [112].

Poxviridae exhibits extensive transmission pathways. Aerosol transmission is significant [113], [114]. Contact with infected animals or contaminants plays a role [114], [115]. Some poxviruses also transmit vertically [116] or sexually [114], [117].

Picornaviridae transmission primarily involves ingestion of infected food [118] and fecal-oral exposure [119], [120]. Aerosol transmission [120] and contact with contaminated materials are additional routes [119].

Respiratory transmission constitutes a primary pathway for seven viral families (Arenaviridae [70], [72], [73], Coronaviridae [77], [78], [79], [80], [81], [82], Hantaviridae [96], [97], Orthomyxoviridae [100], [101], Paramyxoviridae [106], [107], [108], [109], [110], Poxviridae [113], [114], Picornaviridae [120]), enabling human infection through inhalation of aerosols or droplets emitted by virus carriers or infected individuals. Ingestion-related transmission occurs via two distinct mechanisms: Arenaviridae, Flaviviridae, Hantaviridae, and Paramyxoviridae are acquired through consumption of infected animal tissues, whereas Picornaviridae employs a fecal-oral route involving ingestion of excreta-contaminated materials.

Direct contact transmission encompasses three scenarios: (i) physical interaction with infected animals (Arenaviridae [70], [71], [74], Coronaviridae [79], Filoviridae [87], [88], Orthomyxoviridae [101], [102], [103], Paramyxoviridae [111], Poxviridae [114], [115]); (ii) exposure to animal excreta, including rodent waste (Arenaviridae [71], [72], [74]), bat guano (Paramyxoviridae [106]), and unspecified excreta (Filoviridae [83]); and (iii) contact with virus-contaminated inanimate surfaces (Arenaviridae [74], Coronaviridae [81], [82], Filoviridae [86], Orthomyxoviridae [101], [102], [103], Paramyxoviridae [107], [109], [110], Picornaviridae [119], Poxviridae [114], [115]).

Vector-borne and blood-borne pathways include: (i) blood-borne transmission (Arenaviridae [70], [73], [74], Filoviridae [84], [86], Flaviviridae [92], [93]) through exposure to infected blood products; (ii) arthropod vector transmission, with Flaviviridae [89], [90] primarily via mosquitoes and both Flaviviridae [91]and Orthomyxoviridae [104] via tick bites; and (iii) rodent bite transmission (Arenaviridae [76], Hantaviridae [97], [98]).

Specialized human-to-human transmission routes comprise vertical transmission (mother-to-child) in Arenaviridae [75], Flaviviridae [93], [95], and Poxviridae [116]- occurring during pregnancy, parturition, or lactation - and sexual transmission in Arenaviridae [70], Filoviridae [83], [84], [85], [86], Flaviviridae [93], [94], Paramyxoviridae [112] (mainly saliva), and Poxviridae [114], [117], facilitating sustained spread independent of zoonotic reservoirs.

While the diversity of viral transmission routes (e.g., respiratory, contact, and vector-borne transmission) has been characterized, traditional research methods remain limited by technical barriers in isolating and culturing pathogens when elucidating these mechanisms. High-throughput sequencing, centered on metagenomics and metatranscriptomics, has revolutionized pathogen detection by overcoming technical barriers in traditional isolation and culture methods [11], [12]. This technology enables direct whole-genome analysis of viral nucleic acids in environmental or clinical samples, markedly improving the efficiency of identifying pathogens in emerging infectious diseases. However, while viral genome sequences reveal genetic and evolutionary characteristics, they fail to address key ecological and dynamic parameters driving cross-species spillover.

Ecological factors are particularly pertinent to zoonotic risk in bat and rodent reservoirs (Fig. 4). Host species diversity and phylogenetic context are major determinants of viral richness—viral richness was significantly influenced by host species diversity, and inter-specific viral sharing is primarily driven by phylogenetic distance between hosts. However, when focusing on genomic similarities within each viral species, differences in both climate and spatial distances emerge as dominant factors, especially along vertical elevational gradients, suggesting that climatic and spatial factors impact virus diversity more pronouncedly at recent evolutionary scales [11], [12]. Climatic variables, including annual mean vapor pressure, significantly influence viral richness, accounting for a considerable portion of the observed variance. Areas with high mammal richness, relatively low temperatures and high precipitation may act as hotspot areas for total viral diversity [11]. Land-use and habitat disturbance also critically determine viral community structure—host taxonomy and land-use types (mountainous versus agricultural) were critical determinants of viral richness in bats and rodents, and viral diversity is linked to climate and land-use factors [11], [12]. Host population density and aggregation shape transmission dynamics: wild rodents occupying diverse habitats and interacting with larger host communities display higher pathogen richness than commensal species restricted to human dwellings, due to broader ecological interactions and greater pathogen exposure [120], [121]. Notably, the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of these ecological factors directly determines dynamic changes in viral spillover risk. To address this, there is an urgent need to integrate multi-dimensional data – including host animal behavior monitoring, environmental viral load tracking, and microclimatic modeling - into a coupled eco - epidemiological framework. Such a model would enable quantitative prediction of viral outbreak risks by bridging genomic insights with ecological dynamics.

Decoding the drivers of viral communities

Host phylogenetic status is a key determinant of viral community structure (Fig. 4). The composition of viral communities closely mirrors the taxonomic relationships of their hosts indicating that host phylogeny is a primary driver shaping the virome [36]. At the order-level taxonomic scale, host order represents the important influential factor affecting viral composition and transmission potential [12]. Chiroptera (bats) and Rodentia hosts exhibit significant differences in viral composition: Coronaviridae, Picornaviridae, and Circoviridae dominate in bat communities, whereas Parvoviridae and Flaviviridae predominate in rodents [36]. Host genetic distance exerts a barrier effect on viral transmission; In species with limited dispersal capacity, genetic distance is positively correlated with virome dissimilarity, while increased host mobility (i.e., the ability of hosts to migrate across different environments) - as observed in Rousettus aegyptiacus and Eidolon helvum - can mitigate the influence of genetic divergence on viral community structure [36]. Moreover, generalist viruses (e.g., Paramyxoviridae) can infect multiple host species, which allows them to partially overcome such genetic constraints through host mobility [64], [122]. Host population dynamics display contradictory effects: high density during the breeding period promotes viral transmission [123]. In contrast, viral transmission in vampire bats shows density-independent patterns, potentially due to enhanced host immune responses or reduced exposure to diverse viral sources in regions where livestock dominate the available prey base [122]. Notably, an increase in the proportion of juvenile individuals significantly enhances viral diversity, suggesting the potential impact of age structure on host susceptibility. However, host sex ratio and group size differ in their links to viral diversity: male-biased populations have higher viral richness, but group size is unrelated, supporting density-independent transmission [122].

Climatic factors indirectly shape viral diversity by regulating host behavior and resource availability (Fig. 4). Seasonal changes significantly influence the compositional turnover of viral communities: when insect prey are abundant in summer, insect-associated viruses (e.g., Picornaviridae) dominate; increased avian prey in autumn is accompanied by a rise in the abundance of bird-associated viruses (e.g., Flaviviridae) [122], [124]. Temperature elevation promotes viral richness, likely because warmer conditions not only increase host population size but also intensify intra- and interspecific host interactions [122], whereas the inhibitory effect of altitude on viral diversity [12], [123] may be related to reduced vector species or the deterioration of transmission environments in high-altitude regions. Existing studies largely focus on the macro-effects of temperature and seasons, but there remains a lack of in-depth analysis of the regulatory mechanisms underlying extreme climate events (e.g., droughts, floods) or microclimatic conditions (e.g., cave temperature and humidity).

Habitat characteristics influence viral spread through dual pathways of geographic isolation and human activities (Fig. 4). Genetic distance analysis reveals that geographic isolation generally constrains viral transmission; however, frequent host migration or recent gene flow can overcome this barrier, leading to long-distance viral spread and the emergence of highly similar virus clusters [36]. The impacts of human activities on viral communities are complex: viral family diversity in natural habitat samples is higher than in human-disturbed areas [125], yet viral abundance in agricultural rodent populations increases significantly [12], potentially linked to elevated host density or altered food chains. Notably, multiple plant viruses have been detected in bat feces from the Pannonian Basin [125], suggesting that pesticide use or crop viruses may indirectly affect host viromes through food chains. The effects of habitat type are host-specific; for example, shrews from mountainous areas exhibit higher viral richness than individuals from agricultural regions [12], while the spleen and lungs consistently harbor the highest viral diversity [126]. These differences show that virus genetic patterns depend on both natural barriers and human activities. When people move animals through trade or habitat disruption, viruses spread and become similar across regions, but in undisturbed areas with limited host movement, viruses remain distinct enabling the discovery of novel viruses and enhancing our understanding of viral origins and evolution. Since wildlife in remote areas typically has limited contact with humans, human-animal interfaces may serve as potential pathways for zoonotic virus transmission to humans. Therefore, surveillance should target both remote populations, where new variants may emerge, and human-animal interfaces, where fast-spreading outbreaks can originate [127], [128].

The biological characteristics of viruses themselves and association with host significantly influence their transmission potential (Fig. 4). Coronaviridae and Circoviridae exhibit strong cross-host transmissibility due to their high-frequency recombination capabilities (e.g., recombination in the receptor-binding domain of the coronavirus S protein) [36]; viruses with multi-organ distribution enhance their spread probability by expanding intra-host ecological niches [12], [126]. Viral transmission dynamics are influenced by viral abundance, with higher densities promoting a greater probability of successful host infection [12]. Genomic recombination and mutation accelerate host adaptation; for example, viruses eliminate deleterious mutations and accumulate beneficial variations through genetic component exchange [36]. Nevertheless, systematic studies on the molecular mechanisms underlying the interactions between viral functional genes (e.g., immune evasion genes) and hosts remain lacking.

The composition and dynamic changes of viral communities are regulated by multiple factors, including host biological characteristics, environmental conditions, and viral genetic features (Fig. 4). In recent years, studies focusing on these ecological factors have gradually revealed the complex interaction network between viruses and host-environment systems, yet there remain significant gaps in the related findings. Current virome research still suffers from a severe lack of ecology-related data - among the 147 screened literatures, fewer than 10% explicitly integrate ecological data analysis, and most focus on traditional variables such as host genetics, climate, and altitude. With the addition of systematic ecological data, researchers can employ models such as Generalized Linear Models (GLM) [11], Redundancy Analysis (RDA) [129] and Variation Partitioning Analysis (VPA) [130] to identify and quantify the key ecological drivers that facilitate viral spillover from animal hosts to humans. This quantitative approach helps transform abstract “ecological correlations” into concrete and measurable risk indicators, thereby enabling more accurate prediction of high-risk scenarios for viral spillover. Therefore, the primary task in subsequent research is to first incorporate more ecological data, integrating these data across studies and regions is not only essential for filling current knowledge gaps, but also enables a shift in viral spillover research from post-hoc explanation to proactive identification and early warning, thereby allowing viral transmission risks to be revealed at a deeper and more predictive level.

Summary

Virome studies of wild bats and rodents have expanded our understanding of mammalian viral diversity, yet substantial biases limit their utility for global zoonotic risk assessment. Geographic sampling effort is uneven, with disproportionate representation from Asia and North America, while Africa, the Middle East and tropical ecosystems remain underrepresented despite their high biodiversity and potential for pathogen emergence. Taxonomic sampling is similarly imbalanced: bats have been surveyed in 42 regionscompared to 20 for rodents, and research frequently concentrates on a narrow subset of genera – such as Rhinolophus and Rattus - potentially inflating estimates of viral richness in these taxa and introducing detection bias.

Further challenges arise from methodological heterogeneity and incomplete data reporting. Inconsistent sequencing approaches, variable bioinformatic pipelines and the use of non-standardized genetic markers hinder cross-study comparisons. Critical metadata – including host species, collection date and precise geographic coordinates - are often missing or inconsistently recorded. Moreover, ecological context is rarely integrated, limiting the ability to assess how environmental or behavioral factors influence viral dynamics. These limitations contribute to data fragmentation, reducing the reproducibility and translational value of current virome datasets.

Future perspectives

Disparities in computational infrastructure and bioinformatics expertise remain a major constraint on the global representativeness of virome research. Many institutions in biodiverse but underrepresented regions lack access to high-performance computing systems or specialized personnel for metagenomic data analysis, limiting their ability to participate in viral surveillance. To bridge this gap, scalable and accessible analytical platforms are essential. In well-resourced settings, deep learning–based approaches such as LucaProt [131] offer high sensitivity for detecting divergent viral sequences, enabling the identification of novel viruses even at low abundance. For laboratories with limited computational resources or bioinformatics training, cloud-based platforms like CZID (by the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative) [132] provide a robust alternative. CZID supports end-to-end analysis of sequencing data through a fully web-based interface, requiring no local computing infrastructure. Its metagenomic pipeline enables taxonomic profiling of microbial communities, including the detection of bacterial, fungal, and viral taxa. In addition, the platform includes dedicated modules for antimicrobial resistance gene identification and targeted alignment to generate consensus genomes of specific viruses of interest. These features allow users to move from raw sequencing data to pathogen detection and genomic characterization with minimal technical overhead. By accommodating diverse levels of technical capacity, such platforms can support more equitable participation in global virome surveillance and enhance the geographic inclusivity of zoonotic risk assessment.

To address the fragmentation of virome data, standardized molecular and metadata practices are urgently needed. For RNA viruses, consistent use of the RNA dependent RNA polymerase gene as a taxonomic marker would enable comparable and reproducible viral classification across studies. This approach provides a common genetic framework that reduces ambiguity in virus identification. Beyond molecular consistency, comprehensive metadata reporting is critical. Researchers should adopt structured standards such as those from the Minimum Information about a Viral Genome initiative and the Genomic Standards Consortium [8], [133], which define essential biological and technical information. This includes host species, sample type, collection date and geographic location, host traits such as age and sex, and environmental context. Experimental methods must be fully documented, including detailed metadata to clarify which animal specimens were subjected to mNGS library construction and sequencing, sample pooling strategies, nucleic acid extraction, sequencing protocols, and bioinformatic processing. All raw sequence data and associated metadata should be deposited in public repositories such as the Sequence Read Archive under persistent accession numbers. Journal and funder mandates for data sharing can help institutionalize these practices. Together, marker gene standardization and rigorous metadata reporting offer a practical solution to enhance data interoperability and support large scale virome analyses.

To enable ecologically informed spillover risk assessment, virome studies on bats and rodents should record basic environmental metadata at the time of sampling, such as precise geographic coordinates, local microclimate, habitat type [134], and collection date. These field-derived observations can then be integrated with publicly available environmental datasets to reconstruct the ecological context of viral detection [135]. Suitable sources include satellite derived indicators of vegetation greenness, land surface temperature, precipitation patterns, land use and land cover dynamics, and elevation, all of which can be spatially linked to sampling locations using geospatial tools [11]. This dual approach leverages accessible field data and open access remote sensing resources without requiring intensive in situ monitoring. For instance, The Marburg virus study in Ugandan Rousettus aegyptiacus exemplifies ecologically informed surveillance: by recording precise sampling dates, locations, and host demographics - and linking them to seasonal birthing cycles - it revealed that viral peaks in 6-month-old juveniles align with 83% of human spillovers, demonstrating how basic field metadata can uncover critical spillover drivers without intensive environmental monitoring [136]. Rulli et al. (2025) emphasize that land use change - particularly habitat fragmentation - alters wildlife community structure, increases host stress and aggregation, and brings humans into closer contact with reservoir species or vectors, thereby amplifying spillover risk [137]. This supports the need to record habitat type and landscape context during sampling, as fragmentation metrics derived from satellite land cover data can serve as proxies for ecological drivers of viral emergence. By anchoring virome data to standardized environmental reference systems - such as satellite-derived land cover, vegetation indices, and fragmentation metrics - researchers can build geographically scalable models that link viral dynamics to measurable ecological drivers, even without detailed host behavioral or physiological data. This integrative, environment-centered approach offers a realistic and reproducible framework for assessing spillover risk across diverse wildlife reservoir systems.

To systematically implement the technical and methodological advancements outlined above, a coordinated policy framework is essential. The accompanying Summary Table of Key Recommendations (Supplementary Table A.9) synthesizes a cohesive strategy by aligning core challenges – from geographic sampling biases to data fragmentation - with specific, actionable recommendations, their responsible stakeholders, and existing policy levers. This integrated framework demonstrates how computational capacity building, data standardization, and ecological monitoring can be operationalized through existing instruments - such as the WHO's Guiding Principles on pathogen data sharing, the EU Zoonoses Monitoring Directive, and national One Health frameworks. By explicitly connecting technical needs with policy action and stakeholder accountability, this table provides a clear roadmap for transforming virome research into a more representative, integrated, and policy-relevant global effort.

Conclusion

To translate virome research into actionable public health surveillance, we advocate for ecological integration as the cornerstone of this transition - moving from fragmented, ad hoc studies toward standardized, ecologically grounded, and globally inclusive frameworks. By adopting common genetic markers, enforcing rich metadata reporting, integrating environmental context through accessible geospatial tools, and leveraging equitable bioinformatics platforms, researchers can generate comparable and interpretable data across regions. These practices will enable the development of early warning systems that detect viral shifts in wildlife populations before spillover occurs. Ultimately, a more systematic and collaborative approach to virome monitoring in bats and rodents can strengthen global preparedness for emerging zoonoses, supporting timely interventions under a One Health paradigm.
