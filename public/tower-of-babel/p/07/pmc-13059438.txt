Glucose oxidase-mineralized scaffolds with multiple bioactivities for accelerated diabetic bone regeneration
Authors: Yancheng Zhu, Xiaohao Liu, Zhuyun Cai, Yunshan Fan, Zhi Zhou, Guoli Deng, Feng Chen, Shaohua Li, Shisheng He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic bone defects pose a significant clinical challenge due to impaired healing under hyperglycemia. However, no effective therapy is available to treat diabetic bone defect in clinic. This study engineers a biomimetic 3D-printed scaffold for diabetic bone regeneration by integrating glucose oxidase-mineralized amorphous calcium phosphate (GOx@ACP) nanoparticles into a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel. Biomineralization encapsulates GOx within ACP, enhancing enzymatic stability and enabling glucose-responsive degradation. The scaffold exerts dual therapeutic actions consuming glucose to alleviate hyperglycemia while generating trace H₂O₂ to promote angiogenesis, alongside releasing osteogenic ions (Ca²⁺/PO₄³⁻) upon ACP degradation. The scaffold’s sustained degradation profile (with 61.02% mass retention after 28 days) ensures matched release kinetics of ions and enzymes, thereby synergistically promoting bone regeneration. In vitro, the scaffold significantly enhances bone marrow stromal cell proliferation, osteogenic differentiation with upregulated OPN/OCN/Runx2/Col-Ⅰ expression, and endothelial tubulogenesis. In diabetic rat cranial defects, the implantation significantly accelerated bone regeneration, with the bone volume (BV/TV) reaching approximately 52% by the 12th week. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, a prevalent chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia [1], is increasing globally due to population aging and rising obesity rates [2]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) projects that over 10% of the global population will have diabetes by 2030 [3], potentially rising to more than 1.31 billion by 2050 [4]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), accounting for approximately 90% of cases, involves insulin resistance and abnormal carbohydrate metabolism [5]. Diabetes significantly impacts multiple organ systems, notably exacerbating adverse effects on the skeletal system [6]. Studies demonstrate that T2DM impairs bone repair through mechanisms including reduced osteogenic differentiation of bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), impaired angiogenesis, and excessive inflammation [7, 8]. Critically, effective treatments for diabetic bone defects remain elusive, with conventional approaches like insulin injection often suboptimal [9]. Therefore, developing targeted strategies to counteract diabetes-induced impairment and enhance bone healing capacity, specifically tailored to the diabetic microenvironment, is essential for improving diabetic bone defect outcomes.

Diabetic bone defect is a common complication of diabetes, pathologically characterized by chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) induced by persistent hyperglycemia. These pathological factors drive macrophage polarization towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, thereby suppressing osteoblast activity while promoting osteoclast function. Furthermore, the frequent co-occurrence of vascular complications in diabetes impairs blood supply to bone tissue, further hindering the repair process [10]. Glucose oxidase (GOx) is a naturally sourced oxidoreductase enzyme, which can specifically catalyze the oxidation of glucose, resulting in the formation of gluconic acid and H2O2 under aerobic conditions [11]. Due to its highly effective capacity for glucose reduction, it is particularly beneficial in addressing diseases associated with abnormal glucose metabolism, such as cancer and diabetes [12]. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is not only a reactive oxygen species (ROS) but also a crucial redox signaling molecule. At low concentrations, it acts as a second messenger that activates various signaling pathways [13]. One study indicated that treating BMSCs with 50 µM H₂O₂ for 12 h significantly enhanced their proliferation, migration, and survival by activating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [14]. Although GOx has favorable biocompatibility, non-toxicity, and biodegradability, there are some drawbacks that may hinder its further application [15]. For instance, intravenous administration of GOx may lead to a temporary reduction in blood glucose levels, as well as eliciting inflammation (intrinsic immunogenicity) and blood toxicity [16]. Furthermore, several studies have indicated that GOx can inflict damage on organs such as the liver and kidney in mouse models [17]. Due to the low in vivo stability (inherent deactivating nature) of the enzyme, GOx exhibits lower bioavailability, ultimately compromising its efficacy in treatment [18, 19]. To address these challenges, GOx can be immobilized within nanomaterials, which not only avoids premature leakage in vivo, but also enables environmentally responsive release at the lesion site, improving stability and bioavailability and reducing biotoxicity.

Nanomaterials have gained significant attention as efficient enzyme immobilization supports due to their large surface area, high loading capacity, and excellent stability [20]. Organic materials typically exhibit bioactivity, whereas inorganic materials possess favorable mechanical properties. Consequently, organic-inorganic composite systems are widely utilized as bone repair materials [21, 22]. Among them, calcium-based biomaterials like calcium phosphate (CaP) and calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), ubiquitous in nature and major inorganic components of bones and teeth [23], offer high biocompatibility and low toxicity compared to polymer, metal, or silica carriers [24]. CaP is stable under neutral conditions but dissolves in acidic environments, allowing degraded products to be safely absorbed or excreted, making it suitable for clinical drug delivery [25]. These unique bioactive and biodegradable properties facilitate its widespread use in biomedical applications, including protein/drug/gene delivery and tissue engineering [15]. However, the inherent physiological stability of highly crystalline CaP limits its transformation into low-crystalline bone apatite, hindering efficient new bone formation [26, 27]. Amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP), an intermediate phase in hydroxyapatite synthesis [28] and a critical bone mineral precursor, overcomes this limitation. ACP accelerates collagen mineralization and promotes bottom-up osteogenesis, enabling efficient bone regeneration [29, 30]. Its hydrated structure also makes it an excellent enzyme carrier, facilitating substance incorporation and maintaining enzyme activity under harsh conditions [31]. Biomineralization, the biologically controlled formation of inorganic minerals [32], allows biomacromolecules (e.g., proteins, enzymes, nucleic acids, peptides) to be encapsulated within CaP matrices via a straightforward process, rather than merely adsorbed onto the surface [33]. This approach preserves biomolecule activity under physiological conditions and enables sustained release [34]. While studies have utilized ACP for enzyme immobilization (e.g., glucose detection [20]) and developed ACP-mineralized complexes for glucose-starvation therapy [35]. Previous studies have reported systems like catalase-assisted biomineralized calcium phosphate (CaP@CAT) co-loaded with glucose oxidase (GOx) for diabetic bone defect repair [2], the encapsulation of GOx within ACP via biomineralization specifically for this application has not been explored. Our approach is distinctly advantageous due to its multifunctional synergy, which actively addresses key pathological aspects of the diabetic bone microenvironment. The alleviation of hyperglycemia, together with the release of mineral ions and controlled generation of hydrogen peroxide, collectively establishes a favorable milieu for enhanced bone regeneration. Therefore, encapsulating GOx in ACP via biomineralization is anticipated to enhance the stability, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy of the complex while minimizing adverse effects on normal tissues.

Enzyme-responsive scaffolds represent a class of smart biomaterials capable of degradation, transformation, or functional release triggered by specific enzymes. They have shown significant potential in the controlled release of drugs and growth factors. These materials not only effectively promote bone regeneration but also exhibit anti-infection and tumor ablation capabilities [36]. Glucose oxidase is often utilized in the treatment of diabetic bone defects by alleviating hyperglycemic conditions, whereas catalase promotes the healing of bone defects through the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide and scavenging of reactive oxygen species [2, 37]. Recent studies on various scaffolding materials have been developed for treating diabetic bone defects through diverse strategies. For instance, Li et al. constructed a bioactive prosthetic interface using a cascade multi-nanoenzyme hydrogel, which induces M2 macrophage polarization and promotes the healing of diabetic bone defects [38]. Wang et al. developed a radially aligned microchannel scaffold that remodels the diabetic pathological microenvironment by scavenging reactive oxygen species, suppressing inflammation, and restoring mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby enhancing angiogenesis and osteogenesis [39]. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP-2/9) are involved in tissue remodeling, wound healing, and immune defense responses [40]. ALP plays a critical role in bone mineralization by catalyzing the hydrolysis of phosphate monoesters to generate inorganic phosphate [41]. Lysyl oxidase (LOX) facilitates collagen synthesis and bone regeneration by catalyzing the oxidation of lysine or hydroxylysine residues in collagen molecules to generate reactive aldehydes, such as aldehyde lysine [42]. Therefore, the development of a bone scaffold strategy possessing multiple biological effects—including the mitigation of hyperglycemia, the release of mineral ions, and the generation of low-dose H₂O₂—holds considerable potential for diabetic bone regeneration.

In this study, we developed a 3D-printed GOx@ACP/GelMA composite scaffold for diabetic bone defect regeneration (Fig. 1). GOx@ACP nanoparticles were synthesized through a facile biomineralization process utilizing GOx and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and subsequently mixed with GelMA for 3D printing. Biomineralization within ACP enhanced GOx stability while preserving its catalytic activity. The resulting scaffolds significantly promoted proliferation, osteogenesis, and angiogenesis in bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Upon implantation into cranial defects of diabetic Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, the scaffold effectively alleviated the hyperglycemia environment via GOx catalysis and released Ca²⁺ upon degradation to stimulate bone regeneration. Collectively, this multifunctional enzyme-loaded composite scaffold demonstrates significant potential for improving the diabetic bone defect microenvironment and offers a promising therapeutic strategy for enhancing diabetic bone repair.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

ATP and GOx were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Calcium chloride (CaCl2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4), dimethyl Formamide (DMF) were purchased from Aladdin Chemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). GelMA hydrogel was purchased from Engineering for Life (GM-60, Jiangsu, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) kit was purchased from Bioland (China). BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development kit, Calcein-AM/PI Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) assay kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology, Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Actin-Tracker green was purchased from KeygenBioTECH (Jiangsu, China). Cryogenic Vials were purchased from NEST Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuxi, China). Cell culture dishes and flasks were purchased from Jet Biofil Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou China) and PakGent Bioscience (Suzhou) Co., Ltd. 0.2% ARS staining solution was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Protein Marker (EM0101-005) was purchased from Bioligo Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Preparation of GOx@ACP and GOx@ACP/GelMA

A total of 250 mg of ATP and 50 mg of GOx were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water. The beaker was covered with fresh-keeping film, and the mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer for 24 h to obtain a yellow suspension. Subsequently, 0.156 g of NaH2PO4 was added, and the pH was adjusted to 7 with NaOH after stirring for an additional hour. Furthermore, 0.11 g of CaCl2 was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water. This solution, along with NaOH, was then added dropwise to the previously prepared suspension using a pipette gun while maintaining the pH between 7 and 7.5. This process resulted in the formation of yellow sediment. The resulting suspension was transferred into a centrifuge tube for separation by centrifugation, followed by lyophilization to yield the final product.

Under sterile conditions, a 10% (w/v) GelMA solution was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions; subsequently, either GOx@ACP or ACP particles were incorporated into the GelMA hydrogel at 30% (w/w) and vigorously mixed to form a homogeneous phase. The resulting mixture was transferred into a 20 mL light-protected printing cartridge, and a straight 0.25 mm inner diameter needle was employed under light-protected conditions. The extrusion pressure was set to 2.8–4.8 bar with a printing speed maintained at 5–10 mm s⁻¹. Cylindrical scaffolds with a pore size of 900 μm were printed using a Z-direction layer height of approximately 0.3 mm, while simultaneous photopolymerization (parameters of photo-crosslinking: 30 s, 25 mW/cm2) was achieved via 405 nm ultraviolet irradiation during deposition. 3D-Printer model: 4th 3D Bioplotter™ (EnvisionTEC GmbH, Germany).

Characterization of GOx@ACP/GelMA

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging and electron dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) element mapping were conducted on a GeminiSEM 300 (Carl Zeiss AG; Oberkochen, Germany). Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) observation was carried out using a JEM-2100 F microscope (Jeol Ltd., Japan). X-ray diffraction (XRD) was carried out on a D2 Phaser (Bruker, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation with 2θ range from 10° to 80° at a rate of 5° min− 1. The fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra patterns were recorded with IRSpirit Spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) with the range of 4000–500 cm− 1. The size distribution of GOx@ACP nanoparticles was measured from SEM image by using Image J software (NIH, USA). According to a previous literature, the release of calcium and phosphate ion during scaffold degradation were measured with calcium colorimetric assay kit and phosphate colorimetric assay kit, respectively (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) [43]. 1 mg of lyophilized GOx@ACP powder was weighed and fully dispersed in 200 µL of dilute hydrochloric acid, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution of calcium phosphate and release of GOx. A standard curve was then constructed using the BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein standard solutions with concentrations of 0, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 mg/mL were prepared sequentially. After adding the working reagent, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min protected from light. The absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader. Based on the standard curve, the GOx content in GOx@ACP was calculated. The experiment was performed in triplicate.

Enzyme activity and stability tests

Enzyme activity: 10 µg mL–1 of GOx@ACP was incubated with glucose solution of varying concentrations (0–1000 µg mL–1) for 4 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the solution was harvested and analyzed using a H2O2 assay kit and a pH meter (SX- 620 pH tester) to measure the concentrations of H2O2 and pH values, respectively.

Thermal stability: GOx@ACP or GOx was incubated at various temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 65 °C for a duration of 10 min. The enzymatic activity of the samples was subsequently assessed through enzyme activity assays. In the thermal deactivation tests, samples of GOx@ACP or GOx that had been incubated at 60 °C were collected every 10 min to evaluate their residual enzymatic activities.

Stability in organic solvent: GOx@ACP or GOx was subjected to 70% DMF aqueous solution at 30℃. The remaining activity was evaluated through an enzyme activity assay following a 30-min exposure.

Storage stability: The GOx@ACP or GOx was dispersed and stored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 25 °C. The residual enzyme activity was assessed at a certain time using an enzyme activity assay.

Biocompatibility evaluation

Cell culture

HUVECs were obtained from Cell Bank of the Committee on Type Culture Collection, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Before tests, all the 3D printed scaffolds were treated with ultraviolet-light radiation sterilization. HUVECs were cultured in high-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS (JYK-FBS-300, Jin Yuan Kang Biotechnology), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were extracted from four weeks old Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats and cultured in low-glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). The isolation process of BMSCs was conducted in accordance with the protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University (Approval Number: SHDSYY-2024-T0125-0102). All cells were at 37 °C cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Preparation of extracts from scaffolds

The ratio of mass of material to extract volume was 0.1 g/mL, in accordance with the ISO 10993-12 2007 extraction standard. Specifically, the scaffold extracts were prepared by immersing the sterilized scaffolds in a culture medium (DMEM) without FBS and incubated at 4 °C for 72 h.

Cell viability evaluation

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the instructions of manufacture. A total of 100 µL of scaffold extracts were co-incubated with BMSCs at a density of 5 × 103 per well in a 96-well plate for 1, 3, 5 and 7 days. Prior to measurement, an additional 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated in a CO2 incubator for another 2 h. Finally, the optical density (OD) values for each well were measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, CA, USA) at wavelength of 450 nm. Hemolysis ratio testing

Live and dead cell staining

The cell cytotoxicity of the scaffolds on BMSCs was assessed following the instructions provided with the Calcein-AM/PI Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit. The BMSCs were co-cultured in prepared scaffold extracts in a 24-well plate with a density of 5 × 104/well for 2 days. The normal medium was set as control group. 1 × working solutions containing Calcein AM, PI, and buffer were prepared according to the manufacturer guidelines. After washing each well three times with PBS solution, we added 250 µL of 1 × working solutions to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Ts2R-FL, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) at the excitation wavelengths of 488 for Calcein-AM and 559 nm for PI.

Cell attachment study

In order to get a clearer view of the attachment of cells, we prepared thin sections of the scaffolds. The sterilized samples were cut and placed at the bottom of laser confocal glass-bottomed dish (SAINING Biotechnology). 1 mL of BMSCs were collected and cultured in the glass-bottomed dish at a density of 5 × 104/mL, followed by culture at 37 °C for 24 h to promote optimal cell attachment. Prior to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, the cells were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, allowing five minutes for each wash. Afterward, the cells were treated with Actin-Tracker green (1:50 dilution in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100) for 45 min in the dark at room temperature. Following this incubation, another round of washing as previously described was conducted before staining the cells with DAPI for 15 min at room temperature. After completing the final washing step, images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 900, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

Bioactivity evaluation

In vitro cell migration assay

The effects of extracts from scaffolds on HUVECs migration were assessed by using a scratch wound healing assay. HUVECs were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well into 24-well culture plates and cultured for up to 24 h until they reached approximately 90% confluence. The wounds were created in each well using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip, after which the medium was replaced with the scaffold extract solution. At both time points, 0 and 24 h, the cells were observed and photographed under a microscope. The area of wound closure relative to the initial area was quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

To investigate the angiogenic potential of GOx@ACP/GelMA in forming tubular networks in vitro, Matrigel-based tube formation assays utilizing HUVECs were conducted. Briefly, 50 µL of Matrigel was deposited onto a pre-cooled 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to facilitate gel formation. Subsequently, a suspension containing 100 µL of HUVECs in different scaffold extracts was plated on the Matrigel-coated 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After culture at 37 °C for 8 h, random images were acquired using a microscope. The resulting images underwent analysis through the Angiogenesis Analyzer Plugin of ImageJ. The number of nodes, number of meshes and total length of tubes were measured to evaluate the tubular formation capacity.

ALP and ARS staining

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin red S (ARS) staining assays were conducted to assess the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs co-incubated with the extracts from various scaffolds. Briefly, BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at an initial density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h for adherence. Following this incubation, the culture medium was replaced with DMEM containing the extracts. The group without extracts served as the control group. After incubation periods of 14 days, the media were removed, and cells were washed with PBS before being fixed in 4.0% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Post-fixation, the cells underwent three additional washes with PBS. According to instructions of manufacture, the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development kit along with a 0.2% ARS staining solution were applied to each respective well for a duration of 30 min. Subsequently, stained cells were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analyses of ALP staining and ARS staining were conducted utilizing Image J software (NIH, USA) to measure the stained areas.

Western blot assay

Western blot analysis was typically employed to assess the expression levels of osteogenic markers. After culture with various scaffold extracts for 7 and 14 days, BMSCs were washed with pre-cooled PBS and harvested in sterilized microcentrifuge tubes. The harvested BMSCs were then treated with RIPA lysis buffer consist of PMSF for 30 min at 4 °C. The volume ratio of RIPA to PMSF was 100:1. Subsequently, the lysates were subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at a temperature of 4 °C. The total protein was subsequently extracted, and its concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. The total protein lysates were extracted in equal amounts and subsequently separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) before being transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. WinnerPage Protein Gel (CW8204S) was purchased from CWBIO Biotch. The PVDF membrane was then subjected to blocking with 7% nonfat powdered milk on a shaker at 50 rpm for 2 h. Following the blocking step, the PVDF membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies, osteopontin (OPN, 1:1000; Abcam, UK), osteocalcin (OCN, 1:1000; Abcam, UK), and β-actin (1:1000; Abcam, UK). Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was washed three times with TBST (TBS containing Tween) and incubated with secondary antibodies at a dilution of 1:2000 on a shaker at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the PVDF membrane was immersed in ECL solution and captured using the Odyssey Scanning System (Li-Cor, USA).

Real-Time quantitative PCR

To investigate the expression of osteogenic-related genes, including osteopontin (OPN), osteocalcin (OCN), runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), and type I collagen (Col-I), quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to assess the osteogenic properties of GOx@ACP/GelMA. Flow cytometry

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h, followed by stimulation with 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h (except for the control group) to induce the M1 phenotype. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with scaffold extracts. After co-incubation for 24 h, the cells were stained with antibodies against CD86 (Elabscience, E-AB-F0994E) and CD206 (Elabscience, E-AB-F1135C). The cells stained with fluorescent antibodies were analyzed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX S, Beckman Coulter, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo 10.8.1 (BD biosciences, USA).

In situ bone defect repair

All the in vivo animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the protocols approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University (Approval Number: SHDSYY-2024-T0125-0102). All the rats were subjected to specific pathogen-free conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 23 °C) and were provided with food and water. Forty SD rats (female, 8 weeks old) with an average weight of 200 g were randomly divided into 4 groups, including blank control, GelMA, ACP/GelMA and GOx@ACP/GelMA group. All the rats were intraperitoneally administered with streptozotocin (60 mg/kg) dissolved in citrate buffer (pH 4.5) to construct diabetic rat model. Among them, those with blood glucose levels measured higher than 16.7 mM one week later were selected for following experiments. The blood glucose levels were measured and recorded by using an electronic glucometer (GA-3, Sinocare, China) at different time points thereafter. SD rats were anesthetized using a 3 wt% solution of pentobarbital sodium, which was prepared with normal saline, through intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 0.1 to 0.2 mL per 100 g of body weight. After sterilization by 75% alcohol and iodophor, a longitudinal skin incision was made on the calvarium with the aid of a scalpel. The subcutaneous tissue and fascia were carefully dissected in a blunt manner to clearly expose the parietal bones. A calvarial bone defect with a diameter of 5 mm was created in the parietal bone using a high-speed trephine, while sustaining drops of saline were administered. Subsequently, sterilized samples were implanted into the region of the bone defect. All the rats were euthanized by hyperanesthesia and cervical dislocation at 6 and 12 weeks postoperatively, the calvaria were harvested and fixed in 4.0% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for further analysis.

Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) analysis

The harvested calvaria were analyzed using micro-CT (skyscan1172, Bruker, Germany) by scanning the cylindrical bone defect region (diameter: 5 mm and depth: 1.5 mm). Typical parameters, including trabecular separation (Tb. Sp), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th) and bone tissue volume to total tissue volume ratio (BV/TV) were subjected to further quantitative analysis via the CTAn software. The micro-CT threshold used for analysing the bone formation in CT is 15 μm.

Histological and immunohistochemistry analysis

All specimens were decalcified, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for pathological examination. Subsequently, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunofluorescence staining (CD31 and α-SMA) were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocols to enable thorough histological analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Every experiment was conducted at least three times with parallel measurements. Statistical differences were analyzed by SPSS (Version 26.0, Chicago, IL, USA) and calculated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post-hoc Tukey’s method for additional multiple comparisons. The levels of significance were labeled with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, n.s.: not significant.

Results and discussion

Characterization of ACP, GOx and GOx@ACP nanoparticles

The ACP nanoparticles were synthesized using ATP as a phosphorus source and a stabilizer for the amorphous phase. The synthesis of GOx@ACP nanoparticles involved a biomineralization process by adding GOx during the synthesis of ACP nanoparticles. From the SEM results presented in Fig. 2a and b, we found that both the ACP nanoparticles and GOx@ACP nanoparticles exhibit good particle structure. However, compared with pure ACP nanoparticles, which are prone to agglomeration, GOx@ACP nanoparticles containing GOx have better dispersion. During the initial formation of amorphous calcium phosphate, ACP spheres are prone to occur aggregation into micrometer clusters [44]. Previous studies have reported that highly stable ACP porous nanospheres with a relatively uniform size and an average pore diameter of about 10 nm have been synthesized with ATP as the phosphorus source and stabilizer [45]. GOx was used as a biotemplate to control the GC NPs nucleation and growth. The morphology of the GC nanoparticles could be modulated by varying the concentration of GOx in the DMEM. In the absence of GOx, aggregated and irregular particles were obtained. As the GOx concentration increased, the morphology was observed to evolve from aggregated irregular forms to monodisperse spherical structures, suggesting that the ordered architecture originates from a GOx-mediated biomineralization process [46, 47]. Therefore, with the assistance of both ATP and GOx, the GOx@ACP composite exhibited improved dispersion and greater size uniformity. This phenomenon was more distinctly observable in SEM images at a lower magnification. The TEM images and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns indicated that the crystal phase of the GOx@ACP nanoparticles was amorphous (Fig. 2c). According to the TEM image illustrated in Figure S1, the GOx@ACP nanoparticles exhibited a spherical morphology, with a mean size of 61.87 ± 12.97 nm. The results of size distribution obtained from the SEM image corroborate those from the TEM image, indicating that the GOx@ACP nanoparticles possessed a particle size of approximately 60 nm (Fig. 2d and Figure S2). The FTIR analysis was further conducted to characterize GOx, ACP, and GOx@ACP. The FTIR spectra of ACP displayed two characteristic peaks of inorganic components (1029 cm− 1, 557 cm− 1, corresponding to O–P vibrations of PO43−), indicating the synthesis of amorphous calcium phosphate. Following the incorporation of GOx, a peak around 1646 cm− 1 (attributed to the C = O vibration in GOx) emerged, confirming the formation of GOx@ACP composites (Fig. 2e). In the XRD pattern, the crystal structure is typically characterized by sharp diffraction peaks, whereas the amorphous phase produces broad scattering peaks. As depicted in Fig. 2f, no distinct diffraction peak was observed in the XRD pattern. Instead, a broad peak appeared around 2θ = 30°, suggesting that the CaP in GOx@ACP was in an amorphous phase.

Biomimetic mineralization is a common phenomenon in nature, and the GOx@ACP is prepared using a biomineralization strategy. Through this process, the GOx is encapsulated within ACP, enhancing the stability and biocompatibility of GOx. Additionally, a pH-responsive release behavior of GOx@ACP is achieved due to the acid-responsive degradation properties of ACP. It has been widely demonstrated that GOx can effectively and specifically catalyze the oxidation reaction between glucose and oxygen to produce H2O2 and gluconic acid. To evaluate the catalytic activity of GOx after encapsulation, the concentration of H2O2 and pH values were measured with various concentrations of glucose for both GOx and GOx@ACP groups (Fig. 2g and h). As the concentration of glucose increased, a greater amount of H2O2 was produced in both the GOx and GOx@ACP groups. The variation in pH exhibited a corresponding tendency, with elevated glucose concentrations resulting in a more pronounced decrease in pH (from 7.4 to 4.0). Next, the generation of H2O2 and variations in pH values from the GOx and GOx@ACP groups were monitored over different time intervals (Fig. 2i and j). The concentration of H2O2 quickly reached equilibrium within only 20 min in the GOx group and 40 min in the GOx@ACP group, suggesting the high catalytic efficacy of GOx even after encapsulation. Additionally, a comparable trend was observed in the variation of pH levels, with treatment by glucose resulting in a significant decrease in pH from 7.4 to 3.9 over time. Finally, the enzyme activity of 3D printed scaffolds was also examined by detecting changes in glucose concentrations over time. Figure S3 illustrates a gradual decline in glucose concentration, decreasing from a diabetic level of 11.2 mM to a normal level of 5 mM over 14 days, in the presence of 3D printed GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds. The GelMA and ACP/GelMA groups that did not contain GOx exhibited a slight decrease in glucose concentration, which may be attributed to the physical adsorption occurring within the scaffolds. Then, BCA protein assay was employed to determine the GOx content in GOx@ACP. Standard curve of BCA protein assay was shown in Figure S4. Calculation results indicated that 1 mg of GOx@ACP contained 0.043 mg of GOx, indicating a GOx loading capacity of 4.3% (w/w).

The biomineralization process can preserve the structural integrity of enzymes by providing a stable ACP matrix that mitigates thermal denaturation. In Fig. 2k, the thermal stability of GOx and GOx encapsulated in ACP (GOx@ACP) was assessed within a temperature range of 25 °C to 65 °C. The residual enzyme activity of GOx decreased to approximately 8%, while GOx@ACP was able to sustain around 60% activity at 65 °C. To further investigate the heat-resistant time of GOx@ACP, GOx and GOx@ACP were incubated in water at 60 °C for 1 h (Figure S5a). Under continuous heating, the activity of GOx showed a significant decrease during the first 10 min and reached approximately 2% after one hour. In contrast, the enzyme activity of GOx@ACP exhibited a more gradual decline, maintaining approximately 52% activity after one hour. Organic solvents can significantly influence the stability of GOx, as polar solvents have the potential to interact with the enzyme’s active site and disrupt the protein structure. The ACP can function as an encapsulating framework to safeguard the active center of enzymes from alterations in protein configuration induced by denaturing organic solvents. After 30 min of exposure to dimethylformamide (DMF), the enzyme activity of GOx decreased by 85%, whereas the enzyme activity of GOx@ACP remained above 55% (Figure S5b). In a storage stability test, the remaining activity of the GOx@ACP group was approximately 80% after 16 days, whereas that of the GOx group was about 36% (Fig. 2l). Additionally, the storage stability of GOx/GelMA and GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds was determined. As shown in Figure S6, the remaining activity of the GOx@ACP/GelMA group was approximately 30% after 12 weeks, whereas that of the GOx/GelMA group was almost 0%. This phenomenon can be attributed to the encapsulation of the ACP structure, which aids in preserving the folding conformation of the enzyme. The results suggest that, in comparison to free GOx, the catalytic activity of GOx is minimally affected by mineralization. The observed changes in catalytic efficiency can be attributed to the obstruction of GOx binding sites. Despite this, the catalytic stability of GOx encapsulated within ACP has been significantly improved.

Characterization of GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds

Next, GOx@ACP nanoparticles were utilized to create a more uniform complex with GelMA. The SEM images (Fig. 3a-c) of the freeze-dried GelMA, ACP/GelMA, and GOx@ACP/GelMA samples reveal porous structures with varying pore sizes, which facilitate subsequent cell attachment, proliferation, migration, and diffusion of nutrients and gases. By incorporating GOx@ACP nanoparticles into GelMA hydrogels for 3D printing, the resulting GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds demonstrated a more compact structure compared to GelMA and ACP/GelMA alone. This occurred because we used the same concentration of GelMA for scaffold printing, and the addition of ACP and GOx@ACP increased the solid content, thereby reducing the porosity of the dried scaffold. In addition, gradually magnified SEM images also show the deposition behavior of GOx@ACP on GelMA. Figure 3d and e depict the images of EDS elemental mapping, which show a uniform distribution of elements C, N, O, P, and Ca within the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds. Similarly, the EDS elemental mapping of ACP/GelMA scaffolds is presented in Figure S7a. The semi-quantitative analysis of the elements in the samples indicated that the content of P and Ca in ACP/GelMA (Figure S7b) was higher than in GOx@ACP/GelMA (Figure S8). This difference may be due to a decrease in the content of P and Ca resulting from the incorporation of GOx. The optical images of the 3D printed GelMA, ACP/GelMA, GOx/GelMA, and GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds are displayed in Fig. 3f, with the scaffold diameter being 25 mm (Figure S9). The scaffold printed with pure GelMA is transparent, and it turns white when ACP is mixed in. When GOx or GOx@ACP is added, the color becomes light yellow. The EDS elemental mapping of the cross-section of GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds and the corresponding semi-quantitative composition of each element are shown in Fig. 3g and S10. An increased presence of P and Ca was detected in the cross-section of GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds, indicating a higher concentration of ACP within the scaffolds.

Figure S11 showed the kinetic release profiles of calcium and phosphate ions from GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds. Both ions exhibited a biphasic pattern, characterized by an initial fast release phase during the first week, followed by a slower phase at the remaining period. We also found that the cumulative release amount of calcium ion from GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds was higher than that of phosphate ion over the whole time period of experiment. Acquiring a degradation profile of synthetic samples in bone defects is crucial for bone tissue engineering. Consequently, in vitro degradation evaluation was conducted by measuring weight loss after immersing scaffolds in PBS buffer (Fig. 3h). After 28 days, due to the excellent biodegradability of pure GelMA, the remaining mass ratio was 13.1% in the GelMA group. When GOx or ACP were introduced, the residual mass ratios increased to 27.54% and 50.62%, respectively. Interestingly, the remaining mass ratio of GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds was 61.02%. Compared to the other groups, this extended scaffold retention facilitates the formation of new bone [48]. Under high glucose conditions in vitro (35 mM), the incorporation of GOx led to an increased final degradation rate in both the GOx@ACP and GOx@ACP/GelMA groups (Figure S12). This phenomenon is likely attributable to the gluconic acid produced by the GOx-catalyzed reaction with glucose, which subsequently accelerated the degradation of ACP. The degradation curve of GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds is crucial for the continuous release of Ca2+ and PO43– ions into bone defects, providing the necessary elements for bone regeneration and matching the rate of new bone formation.

Mechanical properties of GelMA, ACP/GelMA and GOx@ACP/GelMA

The integration of GelMA into the bioprinting process helps maintain the structural integrity of the scaffold and prevents its collapse. The mechanical properties of 3D bioprinted scaffolds are crucial for the process of bone regeneration. Enhanced mechanical properties allow the scaffold to withstand mechanical stress without deforming or sustaining damage. This capability is essential for the ongoing repair of the bone defect area. Figures 4a, b, and c illustrate the stress-strain curves for GelMA, ACP/GelMA, and GOx@ACP/GelMA. All curves exhibit a similar pattern of initial increase followed by a decrease, with the inflection point indicating the sample’s breaking point. The compressive fracture strength of the samples was calculated and is presented in Fig. 4d. Compared to pure GelMA, the compressive fracture strength of ACP/GelMA and GOx@ACP/GelMA significantly improved with the addition of ACP or GOx@ACP. The introduction of organic or inorganic components resulted in a higher strain for ACP/GelMA than for GelMA and GOx@ACP/GelMA, but excessive introduction led to a decrease in strain (Fig. 4e). As evident from the data in Fig. 4f, the calculated compressive Young’s modulus at 50% strain for GOx@ACP/GelMA was significantly higher than that of GelMA and ACP/GelMA. The microstructure of a material dictates its properties, including its mechanical behavior. As observed in Fig. 3b and c, the ACP/GelMA composite exhibits a partially collapsed internal architecture. This is attributed to the inhomogeneous particle size arising from the aggregation of ACP. In contrast, the GOx@ACP/GelMA composite displays a more regular and well-defined structure, which stems from the uniform nanoscale size of the GOx@ACP particles. Consequently, the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold demonstrates superior mechanical properties compared to the other scaffolds.

Fatigue resistance is a crucial aspect to consider when evaluating synthetic bone repair alternatives. Figures 4g-i illustrate the optical images of GelMA, ACP/GelMA, and GOx@ACP/GelMA before and after undergoing the compression recovery test at 50% strain. As depicted in Fig. 4g and h, and 4i, the stress-strain curves for the compression recovery tests across 20 cycles of GelMA, ACP/GelMA, and GOx@ACP/GelMA are shown respectively. Biocompatibility evaluation of 3D-printed scaffolds

In the realm of bone defects, scaffolds implanted for bone regeneration are in direct contact with bone tissue. Therefore, it is crucial that these bone-repairing scaffolds exhibit exceptional biocompatibility to protect healthy tissues from potential harm and to facilitate an effective bone healing process. The scaffold extracts were prepared by immersing the sterilized scaffolds in a culture medium (DMEM) and the concentration of H₂O₂ in the medium was measured approximately 43 µM. To assess the biocompatibility of 3D-printed scaffolds, CCK-8 assays were initially conducted to evaluate cell viability. As depicted in Fig. 5a, the viability of BMSCs co-cultured with scaffold extracts increased over extended incubation periods. Compared to the blank group, a significant increase in cell viability of the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold group was observed after 5 and 7 days of culture, indicating that the incorporation of GOx@ACP effectively promoted the proliferation of BMSCs. In vitro hemolysis assays are also considered a universal and reliable method for evaluating the biocompatibility of materials. A hemolysis ratio of less than 5% is typically indicative of non-toxicity and safety. As shown in the data from Fig. 5b, the hemolysis rate of saline (negative control) and water (positive control) was set at 0% and 100%, respectively. The hemolysis ratios of GelMA, ACP/GelMA, and GOx@ACP/GelMA groups were all found to be less than 1.5%, suggesting good blood compatibility. Additionally, photographs were taken of the solution after 4 h of co-culture with red blood cells and scaffolds. As illustrated in Fig. 5c, a significant color difference was observed among the positive control group, the negative control group, and the experimental groups, from both the top and side views of the solution. Due to osmotic pressure, red blood cells absorbed water, causing them to burst and release hemoglobin, resulting in a red solution. However, the osmotic pressure of both the saline solution and the experimental group was equivalent to that of the intracellular environment, and there were no components present that would cause the rupture of red blood cells. Consequently, these cells remained relatively intact. The settled red blood cells accumulated at the bottom of the plate due to gravity, making the solution transparent. This conclusion was further supported by the light microscope image of red blood cells shown in Figure S11.

In vitro bioactivity of 3D-printed scaffolds

After confirming the excellent biocompatibility of 3D-printed scaffolds, we proceeded to assess their biological activity. The recruitment of endothelial cells is essential for the formation of new blood vessels during the bone regeneration process, which is critical for the restoration of nutrient transport. Cell scratch wound healing assays were employed to examine the effect of scaffold extracts on promoting the migration of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). As illustrated in Fig. 6a, the findings from the cell scratch assay indicated that after 24 h of co-culture with scaffold extracts, cell migration was observed across all groups. Notably, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group demonstrated the most significant enhancement in HUVECs migration, which may be attributed to the release of GOx@ACP nanoparticles. Quantitative statistical analysis results from Fig. 6a demonstrated that the migratory area in the GOx@ACP/GelMA group was significantly larger than that observed in the other groups, thereby substantially enhancing the migration of HUVECs (Fig. 6c). Bone regeneration is contingent upon a well-structured network of blood vessels to facilitate the transport of essential nutrients and oxygen. Consequently, the angiogenic potential of scaffolds was assessed by co-culturing HUVECs with extracts from various scaffolds. From the optical microscope images presented in Fig. 6b, a greater abundance of interconnected networks was observed in the GOx@ACP/GelMA group, while only a limited number of scattered tube-like structures were found in the other groups, particularly within the control group. Further quantitative statistical analysis of the number of nodes, meshes, and total length of tubes was conducted using Image J software per high magnification field following an 8-h incubation on substrate gel (Fig. 6d-f). There were no significant differences between the control group and the GelMA group for any of the parameters, while both the ACP/GelMA and GOx@ACP/GelMA group exhibited significant improvements in these indicators. It was worth noting that the values in each parameter of the GOx@ACP/GelMA group showed the highest level compared to all other groups, indicating better angiogenic activity. The expression levels of angiogenesis-related mRNA (VEGF) were quantitatively analyzed by RT-qPCR (Figure S10). Consistent with the angiogenesis assay results, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group exhibited a significantly higher VEGF expression level compared to other groups.

HIF-1α is an important pro-angiogenic factor that promotes angiogenesis by simulating hypoxic conditions and provoking transcription of its downstream molecule VEGF. In our study, RT-qPCR and WB were used to detect the expression of genes and proteins related to angiogenesis in vitro. The GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold group demonstrated the highest angiogenic gene expression levels among all experimental groups (Figure S14a and b). Specifically, VEGF expression in the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffolds was 2.1-fold and 3.9-fold higher than that in the ACP/GelMA scaffold group on day 1 and day 3, respectively. Regarding HIF-1α expression, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group exhibited a significant increase, with levels 2.6-fold and 5.6-fold greater than those in the ACP/GelMA group on day 1 and day 3, respectively. The consumption of O₂ and generation of low-dose H₂O₂ by the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold enhanced HIF-1α transcriptional activity and upregulated VEGF expression in HUVECs, which facilitated rapid vascularization within the scaffold. To further validate the qRT-PCR results, Western blot analysis was performed on day 3 to assess HIF-1α and VEGF protein expression (Figure S14c). Notably, the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold group showed markedly increased protein levels of both HIF-1α and VEGF compared to the ACP/GelMA group. These findings indicate that the hypoxia-like microenvironment and low-dose H₂O₂ generated by the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold synergistically promoted angiogenic processes.

To further evaluate the osteogenic differentiation potential of the 3D-printed scaffolds, BMSCs incubated with extractions from various samples were further examined by using ALP and ARS assays after 14 days in vitro. ALP serves as a significant early biomarker during the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The optical microscope images were shown in Fig. 6g, all groups exhibited positive results at Day 14. The GOx@ACP/GelMA group exhibited the darkest ALP staining (purple colour), while comparatively light-colored results were shown in other groups. In addition, the percentage of the positively stained area quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ software supported this conclusion (Fig. 6i). ARS could form an orange-red chelation complex with calcium, which serves as a dependable indicator of osteogenic mineralization. After 14 days of incubation with various extractions, significant calcium nodules were observed in the GOx@ACP/GelMA group compared to the other groups (Fig. 6h). The percentage of the positively stained area of ARS indicated that the GOx@ACP/GelMA group had the highest mineralized nodules of all groups (Fig. 6j). During the natural process of osteogenesis, numerous genes and proteins associated with osteogenic activity are activated at the early stage of osteoblastic differentiation. Western blot analysis was used to measure the protein expression levels of osteogenic markers, osteocalcin (OCN), osteopontin (OPN) and runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2). After 14 days of co-incubation with scaffold extracts, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group showed the highest levels of protein expression (Fig. 6k and Figure S16). To further confirm the osteogenic differentiation potential of the various scaffolds, the relative mRNA expression levels of osteogenic-related genes of BMSCs were determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis after co-culturing with scaffold extracts for 7 and 14 days. Figure 6l–o illustrated the expression levels of osteogenic genes, including Runx2, OCN, OPN and collagen I (Col-I). In addition to RUNX2, the expression level of OPN, OCN, and Col-I genes increased from 7 to 14 days, which may be attributed to the essential role of RUNX2 only as a crucial osteogenic marker during the initial stages of osteogenic differentiation. In addition, the related expression level in the GOx@ACP/GelMA group was significantly higher than that in the ACP/GelMA group.

Next, we used flow cytometry to detect the expression of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker) on RAW 264.7 macrophages across different treatment groups. Figure S17a showed that CD86 expression was significantly higher in LPS-treated macrophages but lower in the control, GelMA, and ACP/GelMA groups, with the GOx@ACP/GelMA group showing the most obvious trend. Conversely, the CD206 levels were elevated in the ACP/GelMA group compared to the control, LPS and GelMA groups, with enhanced effects observed in the GOx@ACP/GelMA group. The statistical analysis shown in Figure S17b and c further revealed a notable increase in M2 macrophages (CD206+ cells) and a reduction in M1 macrophages (CD86+ cells) within the GOx@ACP/GelMA group, suggesting that low concentrations of H2O2 generated by glucose oxidation can promote the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype.

To simulate the hyperglycemic in vivo environment of diabetic bone defects, we also conducted additional experiments under in vitro high-glucose conditions to further validate the aforementioned findings. As observed in the optical microscope images shown in Figure S18a, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group still exhibited the most robust tube formation. Unlike under physiological conditions, the results for the control, GelMA, and ACP/GelMA groups showed no significant differences under high-glucose conditions, likely due to the adverse effects of high-glucose environment. Quantitative statistical analysis further supported these conclusions (Fig. 18b-d). Additionally, we assessed cell viability under high-glucose conditions via the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Figure S18e, the high-glucose environment reduced cell viability to some extent; however, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group mitigated this effect partially owing to the glucose-lowering capacity of GOx. Finally, we measured the relative mRNA expression levels of osteogenesis-related genes in BMSCs (Figure S18f-i). Consistent with the findings under physiological conditions, the GOx@ACP/GelMA group demonstrated the highest osteogenic potential even under high-glucose conditions.

Blood glucose levels in rats were continuously monitored throughout the entire experimental period (Figure S12). The results indicated that blood glucose levels remained consistently high during the experiment, with no episodes of hypoglycemia observed, and no significant differences in glucose levels were detected among the groups. The diabetic hyperglycemic milieu impairs bone healing through multifaceted pathways. The sustained hyperglycemia promotes advanced glycation end products (AGEs) accumulation, which activates NF-κB-mediated inflammation (elevated TNF-α/IL-6), disrupts macrophage reparative polarization, and suppresses VEGF-driven angiogenesis [7, 49]. Concurrently, AGEs inhibit osteogenic transcription factors (Runx2/Osterix), destabilizing bone remodeling equilibrium. Glucose oxidase (GOx) ameliorates diabetic dysregulation through its glucose-depleting catalytic activity. By consuming glucose, GOx diminishes substrate availability for advanced glycation end product (AGE) formation while concurrently reducing infection risks [50]. Crucially, this catalytic process generates H₂O₂. This molecule orchestrates multifaceted regenerative responses including HIF-1α/VEGF pathway activation to stimulate angiogenesis, Nrf2-mediated enhancement of antioxidant defenses, and MAPK-signaling-driven upregulation of osteogenic markers such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteocalcin (OCN). Furthermore, H₂O₂ promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 reparative phenotype, characterized by elevated interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) expression, thereby accelerating inflammation resolution [51, 52]. The biomineralization strategy utilizing ATP as a phosphorus source and amorphous phase stabilizer was pivotal to the scaffold’s efficacy. ATP enabled the synthesis of ACP matrices that encapsulated GOx while preserving its catalytic conformation. This mineralization significantly enhanced GOx stability against thermal denaturation (60% activity retention at 65 °C vs. 8% for free GOx) and organic solvents (55% activity vs. 15% for free GOx after DMF exposure). During scaffold degradation, ATP hydrolysis released phosphate ions that synergized with calcium ions from ACP dissolution to potentiate mineral deposition. Furthermore, the liberated adenosine moieties may activate purinergic signaling pathways implicated in osteoblast differentiation and bone formation. The integration of GOx@ACP nanoparticles within GelMA hydrogels via 3D printing enabled spatiotemporal control over H₂O₂ generation and ion release. This design mitigated cytotoxicity risks while ensuring sustained catalytic activity (30% retention at 12 weeks) and continuous calcium/phosphate delivery matching new bone formation rates, as evidenced by the scaffold’s degradation profile (61.02% mass retention at 28 days).

The biological effects of H₂O₂ vary significantly depending on its concentration. Low concentrations exhibit growth factor-like properties, moderate concentrations promote cellular and tissue aging leading to chronic oxidative stress injury, while high concentrations cause acute oxidative stress damage to cells. According to literature reports, concentrations of 20, 25, 50, and 100 µmol/L have all been utilized as preconditioning concentrations for stem cells with hydrogen peroxide [14, 53–55]. One recent study reported that H2O2 could decrease the viability of mature osteoclasts for enhanced osteoporosis therapeutic efficiency [56]. Guo et al. found that treating BMSCs with 50 µM H₂O₂ for 12 h significantly enhanced their proliferation, migration, and survival. This effect was achieved through maximal upregulation of cyclin D1, SDF-1, and its receptors CXCR4/7 expression, activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, along with suppression of p16 and GSK-3β expression [14]. Furthermore, another study has reported that both 25 and 50 µmol/L hydrogen peroxide can enhance stem cell survival in oxidative stress microenvironments, with 50 µmol/L demonstrating a more pronounced effect. This concentration optimally promotes Bcl-2 expression, increases the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, and enhances GSK-3β phosphorylation levels [57].

The microenvironment at bone defect sites constitutes an extremely complex system. The pH of the human microenvironment can alter the chemical structure, physical state, and biological interactions of scaffold materials, directly influencing their responsive behavior [58]. Due to multiple factors such as raw materials, processes, equipment, and environment, batch-to-batch variations in scaffolds are difficult to eliminate entirely. Furthermore, scaffolds often require customization based on the specific bone defect conditions of individual patients, and the same therapeutic approach may yield different outcomes in different individuals. Therefore, the translation of bone repair scaffolds from laboratory research to clinical application remains a considerable challenge [59]. However, with the rapid advancement of 4D printing, organoid integration, and artificial intelligence (AI), incorporating these technologies into the design and manufacturing of scaffolds holds promise for enabling personalized customization and dynamic functional prediction. This integration, through multimodal data fusion, generative design, and real-time feedback optimization, could lead to the development of dynamic, self-adaptive, biomimetic, and precisely personalized bone repair scaffold systems [36].

Bone healing is a complex biological phenomenon and an intricate regenerative process including three successive overlapping phases, namely inflammation, repair, and remodeling [60, 61]. In the inflammatory phase, the blood supply is interrupted due to the disruption of vascular integrity following bone injury. Therefore, reduced blood supply and increased anaerobic metabolism cause hematoma, infections, and inflammatory responses. This subsequently results in lactic acid accumulation and formation of an acidic microenvironment at the bone injury sites [62]. The degradation of ACP in aqueous solution releases calcium ions (Ca²⁺) and phosphate ions (PO₄³⁻). PO₄³⁻, as a weak acid radical, undergoes hydrolysis with water to generate hydroxide ions (OH⁻), which can promptly neutralize gluconic acid and maintain the local pH near the physiological weak alkaline range. The released calcium and phosphate ions directly participate in new bone mineralization, providing “building materials” for bone deposition. The acidic microenvironment generated by GOx accelerates the biodegradation of ACP and the release of ions, ultimately enhancing mineralization and promoting the process of bone repair. In addition, GelMA also partially mitigates the extent of pH reduction. In a recent study, Shen et al. reported on an injectable, rapidly photocurable bone cement composed of methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) and methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA), which is unconstrained by local geometry. This system is integrated with glucose oxidase (GOx)-loaded mesoporous nano-flower stacked particles derived from oyster shell powder (nHAP), referred to as GOx@nHAP-GelMA&HAMA. The in vivo evaluation of bone repair effects was conducted using a db/db mouse tibia amputation model, confirming the efficacy of our approach. However, that study did not address the fate and whereabouts of the hydrogen peroxide and gluconic acid generated by the glucose oxidase-catalyzed reaction, nor did it investigate their specific effects on the bone repair process [63]. Moreover, although the metabolic fate of H₂O₂ has been addressed in some reports, whether gluconic acid influences bone repair remains unclear [64–66]. During the glucose oxidase-catalyzed oxidation of glucose, δ-gluconolactone is initially generated, which then spontaneously and rapidly hydrolyzes to form gluconic acid. This lactone, serving as a direct precursor to gluconic acid, is employed as a material in bone repair [67–70]. In summary, the components ACP and GelMA help alleviate the extent of pH reduction from gluconic acid. Importantly, it should be noted that a body of literature exists where successful bone regeneration was achieved using GOx or its substrate δ-gluconolactone, without the explicit need to correct the acidic microenvironment.

In the present study, we note that the regenerative efficacy was validated only in a rat cranial defect model, which is a non-load-bearing bone site. Future research will employ load-bearing defect models (e.g., in long bones) to comprehensively assess the scaffold’s performance under mechanical stress. Additionally, the evaluation of regenerated bone in animal experiments did not include biomechanical testing (e.g., compression or indentation tests). This constitutes a limitation of this study, as the ultimate functionality of the newly formed bone depends not only on its volume but also on its mechanical properties. Addressing this represents a key objective for our subsequent investigations.

Conclusions

This study pioneers a glucose-responsive enzyme-mineralized platform that addresses the critical clinical challenge of impaired bone regeneration in diabetic conditions. By integrating GOx@ACP nanoparticles within a 3D-printed GelMA hydrogel scaffold, we engineered a biomimetic system capable of dynamically remodeling the diabetic microenvironment. The biomineralization process encapsulates GOx within an ACP matrix, significantly enhancing enzymatic stability and enabling glucose-triggered degradation. The scaffold orchestrates dual regenerative actions, including GOx consumes glucose to mitigate hyperglycemia while generating trace H₂O₂ to activate HIF-1α/VEGF signaling and drive angiogenesis, while ACP degradation releases osteogenic Ca²⁺/PO₄³⁻ ions to directly stimulate bone formation. In vitro, the GOx@ACP/GelMA scaffold significantly enhanced BMSCs proliferation, osteogenic differentiation (upregulated OPN/OCN/Runx2), and endothelial tubulogenesis. In vivo implantation into diabetic rat cranial defects accelerated bone regeneration, achieving ~52% bone volume (BV/TV) by week 12, significantly surpassing control groups. The scaffold’s sustained degradation profile (61.02% mass retention at 28 days) ensures matched release kinetics of therapeutic ions and enzymes, synergizing with new bone formation rates. This work establishes a paradigm-shifting strategy for diabetic bone tissue engineering. The GOx@ACP/GelMA platform not only overcomes the limitations of free enzyme therapy (instability, biodistribution, toxicity) but also leverages the pathological glucose excess as a biochemical cue to self-regenerate bone. By converging biomineralization, enzymatic catalysis, and 3D printing, this technology offers a translatable and multifaceted therapeutic approach for accelerated diabetic bone regeneration.
