Statistical approaches to make cardiac biomarkers compatible for efficient management of cardiovascular diseases in a regional medical care system
Authors: Tokuyoshi Maruyama, Satoshi Sugawa, Satoshi Murakami, Sachi Amano, Sayaka Wakitani, Norihiro Nishimura, Naoto Hiraoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective: Efficient management of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) is an essential function of regional medical care systems. In this regard, the compatibility of cardiac biomarkers such as B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) plays a crucial role.Patient/Materials and Methods: A total of 1,007 participants were included in this study. To make NT-proBNP more compatible with BNP levels, we examined alternative cutoff NT-proBNP levels derived by curve-fitting as well as formulas to convert NT-proBNP to BNP levels derived by multivariable linear regression analyses.Results: In comparison with alternative cutoffs or Formula #1, Formula #2, which included age, platelet count, and creatinine level as parameters, showed the best performance for improving the conformity between BNP and NT-proBNP levels. Using Formula #2, the concordance between BNP and NT-proBNP increased from 94.1% to 98.1% at a BNP cutoff of 40 pg/mL, and from 99.1% to 100.0% at a BNP cutoff of 100 pg/mL.Conclusions: The selection of NT-proBNP cutoffs corresponding to BNP cutoffs and the application of conversion formulas improved the concordance between BNP and NT-proBNP levels to nearly 100%, which would greatly contribute to the efficient management of CVDs in the regional medical care system.Keywords: B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), compatibility of cardiac biomarkers, hs TnI

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for 32% of all deaths1). A screening method that uses a combination of cardiac troponin I (cTnI) and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) measurements has been proposed to detect early-stage CVDs in the general population and thereby prevent deaths attributable to CVDs2). Cardiac troponins (cTn), including troponin I and troponin T, are biomarkers for the detection of myocardial injury3), whereas BNP and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) are representative biomarkers for the detection of heart failure4). While these biomarkers are mainly used in clinical settings, the importance of cTn, BNP, and NT-proBNP as measures of cardiovascular risk in the general population has also been reported5,6,7). In the aforementioned study, cTnI and BNP levels were found to be independently associated with cardiovascular risk in the general population, suggesting that these biomarkers can detect early-stage CVDs from different perspectives2).

For the management of CVDs in regional medical care systems, the compatibility of cardiac biomarkers evaluated at different medical sites (e.g., health check centers, clinics, and hospitals) is essential. For early detection of heart failure, it is also of great importance that BNP and NT-proBNP levels are compatible with each other, especially at BNP levels ranging from 40 to 100 pg/mL. However, many reports have shown discrepancies between BNP and NT-proBNP measurements, possibly due to unmatched cutoffs or the influence of interfering factors8,9,10). In this study, we sought to minimize the discrepancies between BNP and NT-proBNP measurements by adjusting their corresponding cutoffs or by deriving formulas to convert NT-proBNP levels into BNP levels so that the two biomarkers could be seamlessly utilized in the regional medical care system.

Materials and Methods

Study design

The study protocol adhered to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Matsusaka Medical Association (approval no. 2020-1-11-001). Participants who visited the Matsusaka Medical Association for health checkups between September 2021 and March 2022 were included in the study. In this retrospective study, we used the written opt-out method to obtain consent from the study participants, as approved by the Ethics Committee of the Matsusaka Medical Association. In the opt-out method, participants were informed of the study and provided with an opportunity to decline participation if they did not wish to participate. In addition to routine clinical and laboratory tests performed during health checks, plasma and serum samples were collected to measure the BNP, cTnI, and NT-proBNP levels. A total of 1,007 participants with complete data as listed in Table 1, including data for age, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), platelet count (PLT), and cTnI, BNP, NT-proBNP, and creatinine (CRE) levels, were included in this study.

Clinical and laboratory tests

Clinical tests included measurements of body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP, respectively), and the presence of atrial fibrillation (Af) on an electrocardiogram. Information regarding sex, age, and medical history was collected from the participants through interviews.

All participants underwent routine biochemical and hematological analyses during their medical checkups. Blood samples were collected in a sitting position in the morning after an overnight fast. Biochemical tests performed using a LABOSPECT 008 (Hitachi Hi-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) included measurement of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), CRE, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglycerides (TG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels. Hematological tests, including measurement of the white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb) level, hematocrit (Ht), and PLT, were performed using ADVIA 2120 (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany).

cTnI levels were measured using the ARCHITECT STAT High-Sensitivity troponin I assay (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA). According to the package insert of the cTnI assay, the 99th percentile of the cTnI level in an apparently healthy population was 26.2 pg/mL; the limit of detection was 1.9 pg/mL; and the coefficient of variation (CV)% at the 99th percentile was 4.0%. BNP levels were measured using ARCHITECT BNP-JP assay (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA). According to the package insert of the BNP assay, the analytical sensitivity, defined as the mean concentration of the blank + 2 standard deviations (SD), was 5.8 pg/mL. By our previous evaluation, the limit of quantitation of the BNP assay was 0.8 pg/mL, which was defined as the lowest BNP level at which the sum of the CV% and absolute bias was within 30%11). NT-proBNP levels were measured using the Alere NT-proBNP assay for ARCHITECT (Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL, USA). According to the package insert of the NT-proBNP assay, the limit of detection was 4.9 pg/mL, and the limit of quantitation, defined as the lowest concentration at which the CV% did not exceed 20%, was 8.2 pg/mL. The eGFR was calculated using equations reported for the Japanese population12).

Diseases related to cardiovascular risk, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (CKD), were defined as follows: hypertension, SBP >139 mmHg or DBP >89 mmHg; dyslipidemia, HDL-C level <40 mg/dL, LDL-C level >139 mg/dL, TG level >149 mg/dL, or the presence of steatosis; diabetes, FBG level >125 mg/dL, HbA1c value >6.4%, or positive results for urine glucose; and CKD, eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or proteinuria. These measurements were considered for the diagnosis when the patient was concurrently undergoing treatment for the respective disease.

Statistical analyses

The medians and interquartile ranges of the participants’ data are shown in Table 1. To obtain cutoff values for NT-proBNP levels corresponding to BNP cutoff values of 40 or 100 pg/mL, we first obtained equations between BNP and NT-proBNP by fitting four regression functions (linear, quadratic, logarithmic, and exponential) to the two-dimensional distribution of BNP levels on the x-axis and NT-proBNP levels on the y-axis for all study participants. We then substituted the cutoff values for BNP levels (40 pg/mL or 100 pg/mL) into the equations and obtained the corresponding cutoff values for NT-proBNP levels. Current guidelines recommend a BNP cutoff of 35 pg/mL; however, because this study used data from 2021 to 2022, a cutoff value of 40 pg/mL was used.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed using JMP 17.1.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). To avoid the influence of collinearity, we examined correlations among variables of all the parameters listed in Table 1 and omitted those with correlations higher than 0.7 before performing multivariable regression analyses. Specifically, we selected SBP, eGFR, Hb and AST levels, and the HbA1c value for the initial analysis and excluded the DBP, RBC, Ht value, and the ALT, CRE, and FBG levels. In the second analysis, we switched from eGFR to the CRE level. Females and males were designated 0 and 1, respectively. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. We derived formulas for the conversion of NT-proBNP to BNP by identifying the parameters that showed significance in the multivariable linear regression analyses and reconstituted the equations.

To assess the appropriateness of the conversion, we evaluated three indicators: concordance, correlation coefficient (R) of the linear regression, and root mean square error (RMSE). Concordance was calculated as the number of participants distributed in the lower left (LL), lower right (LR), upper left (UL), and upper right (UR) regions separated by horizontal and vertical cutoff lines. The equation for calculating concordance was as follows: concordance = (LL + UR)/(LL + LR + UL + UR) × 100. The equation for the calculation of RMSE was as follows: RMSE = √(Σ(y – ŷ)2/n). Here, y was the dataset of observed (actual) BNP values, ŷ was the dataset of predicted values, and n was the sample size (number of subjects).

In all multivariable linear regression analyses for NT-proBNP→BNP conversion, cTnI was intentionally excluded from the covariate set; significant predictors were identified from clinical and laboratory parameters after collinearity screening.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

The characteristics of the 1,007 participants are shown in Table 1. The median age, BMI, eGFR, and BNP, NT-proBNP, and cTnI levels of the participants were 52 years, 22.9 kg/m2, 80.5 mL/min/1.73 m2, 6.5 pg/mL, 38.8 pg/mL, and 1.5 pg/mL, respectively. In this population, 29.5% of the participants had hypertension, 42.1% showed dyslipidemia, 10.6% had diabetes, and 9.1% showed CKD. In this study, three participants (0.3%) with signs of Af were identified using electrocardiography. Their BNP levels were 62.1 pg/mL, 85.8 pg/mL, and 130.2 pg/mL, and the corresponding NT-proBNP levels were 638.3 pg/mL, 396.3 pg/mL, and 1,107.4 pg/mL, respectively.

Estimation of cutoff NT-proBNP levels corresponding to those of BNP

To estimate the cutoff values of NT-proBNP that corresponded to those of BNP (40 and 100 pg/mL), we obtained equations between BNP and NT-proBNP by fitting four regression functions (linear, quadratic, logarithmic, and exponential) to the two-dimensional distribution of BNP levels on the x-axis and NT-proBNP levels on the y-axis of all study participants and substituted the equations with BNP cutoff values of 40 and 100 pg/mL to obtain the corresponding NT-proBNP cutoff values. As shown in Table 2, quadratic regression showed the best fit with the highest R. Using this equation, we obtained alternative cutoff values of 220 and 673 pg/mL for NT-proBNP levels, which corresponded to BNP cutoff values of 40 and 100 pg/mL, respectively.

Derivation of formulas to convert NT-proBNP values to BNP values

From the multivariable linear regression analyses (Table 3A and 3B), we derived formulas to convert NT-proBNP values to BNP values. We used parameters that showed significance (age, PLT, eGFR value, and NT-proBNP level; Table 3) to derive the following formula (Formula #1) for the conversion of NT-proBNP into BNP values: BNP = 0.114*NT-proBNP + 0.064*age – 0.108*PLT – 0.038*eGFR + 0.770.

We used parameters that showed significance (age, PLT, CRE level, and NT-proBNP level; Table 4) to derive the following formula (Formula #2) for the conversion of NT-proBNP into BNP values: BNP = 1.702 × sex + 0.115 × NT-proBNP + 0.048 × age – 0.111 × PLT – 5.973 × CRE + 9.400. Here, female=0 and male=1.

Discussion

In this study, 3.4% of the participants had BNP levels >40 pg/mL and/or cTnI levels >26.2 pg/mL. In a previous study, the percentage of participants with BNP levels >40 pg/mL and/or cTnI levels >26.2 pg/mL in a general population of 950 participants was 1.8%2). Because the two study populations showed no significant differences in terms of median age, BMI, SBP, eGFR, etc., this difference was considered within the range of fluctuation for different study populations.

As shown in Table 1, 29.5%, 42.1%, 10.6%, and 9.1% of the participants had hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and CKD, respectively. In addition, three participants (0.3%) showed signs of Af on electrocardiography. Two of the three participants with Af showed BNP levels within 40 to 100 pg/mL (62.1 pg/mL and 85.8 pg/mL). Because the study population consisted of non-selected individuals who had visited our facility for health checks, the study population can be considered to include not just healthy individuals but also those with diseases that could lead to advanced forms of CVD.

We identified 27 participants (2.7%) with BNP levels ranging from 40 to 100 pg/mL. Among these, five showed cTnI levels higher than the cutoff value of 26.2 pg/mL. Two of the three participants with Af had BNP levels ranging from 40 to 100 pg/mL. Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure state that individuals whose BNP levels are within 40 to 100 pg/mL are considered “likely to have mild heart failure”8). Therefore, a cutoff value of 40 pg/mL could be used for extensive screening of patients with potential heart failure in the setting of a health check, while a cutoff value of 100 pg/mL could be used for identifying patients with heart failure who need to be attended to by specialized cardiologists.

In this study, we compared the two-dimensional distribution of BNP levels on the x-axis and cTnI levels on the y-axis versus that of NT-proBNP levels on the x-axis and cTnI levels on the y-axis (Figure 1A–1F) and observed that the conventional NT-proBNP cutoff values of 125 and 400 pg/mL, which corresponded to BNP cutoff values of 40 and 100 pg/mL, respectively, could be lower than the corresponding BNP cutoff values in terms of the distributions. By selecting the best-fit regression curve for the correlation between BNP and NT-proBNP levels, we obtained alternative cutoff values of 220 and 673 pg/mL for the NT-proBNP level, which corresponded to cutoff BNP levels of 40 and 100 pg/mL, respectively (Table 2). By applying these alternative cutoffs to the two-dimensional plots (Figure 1C, 1F), the distribution appeared to be closer to the distribution in the BNP and cTnI plots (Figure 1A, 1D).

To derive formulas to convert NT-proBNP to BNP values, we performed multivariable linear regression and confirmed that age, PLT, eGFR, and the NT-proBNP level were significantly associated with the BNP level (Table 3). Using the coefficients of the associated factors, we obtained Formula #1. To determine whether the CRE level reflected the effect of renal function more directly, we performed a multivariable analysis by replacing the eGFR with the CRE level (Table 4) and obtained Formula #2. In the present study, sex was also significantly associated with BNP levels. This could be because sex was included in the equation used to calculate the eGFR from the CRE level12). The inclusion of renal function indicators (eGFR or CRE levels) in these formulas is understandable because NT-proBNP levels are known to be more affected by renal function8, 9). As renal function deteriorates and PLT decreases with age13, 14), this could be why age and the PLT were included as parameters that affect the relationship between BNP and NT-proBNP levels.

cTnI performed an exploratory, visualization-only role in the BNP-cTnI and NT-proBNP-cTnI plots and was therefore not incorporated into the final NT-proBNP-to-BNP conversion models. In a previous study2), the two-dimensional distributions of BNP and cTnI in the general population (N=950) were examined. In that study, covariance structure analysis demonstrated an inverse correlation between BNP and cTnI levels in the “Low BNP” subgroup with BNP level ≤3 pg/mL, and the proportion of individuals with obesity tended to increase in the Low BNP group. These findings suggest that, with respect to cardiovascular risk, the relationship between cTnI, a biomarker of myocardial injury, and natriuretic peptide levels may not necessarily be consistent across different populations. For example, in populations that include a higher proportion of individuals with an elevated BMI, cTnI may have to be considered as an additional parameter.

In line with our multivariable findings (Tables 3, 4), previous studies have suggested that platelet parameters can be related to natriuretic peptide levels. In patients with early diabetic nephropathy and chronic heart failure, NT-proBNP levels were correlated negatively with PLT and plateletcrit in women, indicating a sex-specific hematologic link to natriuretic peptide biology15). In patients with heart failure, natriuretic peptide levels co-varied with complete blood count parameters, including PLT, further supporting a hematologic interface with cardiac strain markers16). Mechanistically, inflammatory activation raises natriuretic peptide levels across diverse settings through cytokine-driven cardiomyocyte expression, providing a milieu in which platelet activation and BNP/NT-proBNP secretion may co-occur17). Notably, platelets possess natriuretic peptide receptors (historically shown for atrial natriuretic peptide [ANP]), and recent experimental evidence indicates that BNP can potentiate platelet activation and thrombosis through natriuretic peptide receptor-A (NPRA) signaling, indicating direct natriuretic peptide–platelet crosstalk18, 19). In addition, shared upstream determinants such as aging, renal function, and obesity influence both natriuretic peptides and platelet indices, potentially generating a composite population-level signal captured by PLT in conversion modeling20, 21).

As shown in Table 6, the alternative cutoffs improved the concordance rates from 94.1% to 97.6% with a cutoff of 220 pg/mL and from 99.1% to 99.8% with a cutoff of 673 pg/mL. Furthermore, eBNP #1 and eBNP #2 further increased the concordance, reaching 100%. Comparing the effects of eBNP #1 and eBNP #2, the latter showed greater improvements in terms of R and RMSE. We speculate that this is because the equation to estimate eGFR included not only the CRE level but also age and sex; therefore, some interaction may have occurred between age and the PLT in Formula #1. Notably, the improvement in concordance was more effective around a BNP cutoff of 40 pg/mL, indicating that this methodology (alternative cutoff and conversion) could be more effective in detecting early-phase CVDs, such as general screening for CVDs with a BNP cutoff of 40 pg/mL.

In 2019, Kasahara et al. reported a conversion formula for BNP and NT-proBNP levels using age, BMI, Hb level, creatinine clearance, sex, and the presence of Af as parameters. The correlation coefficient obtained by their conversion formula was 0.92822), which was higher than those obtained by Formula #1 (0.889) and Formula #2 (0.895) in the present study. The RMSE of their model was 0.251 for log10-transformed BNP and log10-transformed NT-proBNP levels, whereas the RMSE of Formula #1 was 0.9 and that of Formula #2 was 0.8 when transformed with log10. In 2022, Ishihara et al. reported a conversion formula between BNP and NT-proBNP levels that used BMI and eGFR values as parameters23). The correlation coefficients based on their conversion formula ranged from 0.891 to 0.942. The difference in performance between their formulas and ours could partly be due to the different populations; they recruited patients with disorders related to CVDs, whereas we recruited visitors for health checkups. Moreover, the mean BNP levels ranged from 51 pg/mL to 77 pg/mL in the study by Kasahara et al. and 55.5 pg/mL in the study by Ishihara et al., subjects while it was 11.5 pg/mL in our study.

From our perspective, the conformity between BNP and NT-proBNP levels is especially important at BNP levels of 40–100 pg/mL because it could be the basis for determining whether a participant is healthy, has a potential risk for CVDs, or needs to be attended to by cardiologists. Notably, the concordance with the BNP cutoff of 40 pg/mL improved from 94.1% to 97.6% and 98.1% when the alternative cutoff and conversion formulas, respectively, were applied. Therefore, we propose harmonizing BNP and NT-proBNP measurements using simple and practical methods: alternative cutoffs or conversions with easily obtainable parameters such as sex, age, PLT, and eGFR (or CRE).

This study did not include participants with extremely high NT-proBNP levels, such as those with levels exceeding 10,000 pg/mL, which are often encountered in patients with acute decompensated heart failure or markedly impaired renal function. Consequently, the applicability of our proposed cutoff adjustments and NT-proBNP-to-BNP conversion formulas to such extreme biochemical ranges remains unknown. Given that the pathophysiological alterations in these conditions may distort or modify the BNP–NT-proBNP relationship, caution should be exercised when considering the use of these formulas in acute or severe clinical settings. Validation in broader and more diverse patient populations, including those with advanced heart failure or acute presentation, is necessary to establish generalizability.

As mentioned in the Introduction, BNP and NT-proBNP are two representative biomarkers for the management of heart failure4). BNP is less susceptible to reduced renal function and glycosylation than NT-proBNP, while samples obtained for NT-proBNP measurements are more stable than those obtained for BNP measurements8,9,10). In Japan, BNP is more frequently used in clinical settings in hospitals, where patients with reduced renal function are abundant, whereas NT-proBNP is preferred in health check centers, where people with reduced renal function are fewer and samples are stored for a long time owing to limited logistics. Thus, harmonizing the two biomarkers could be instrumental in clarifying the ambiguity caused by unmatched standards between them and in improving the efficiency of heart failure management in the regional medical care system. The improvements in Figure 2B–2D and Table 6 highlight the possibility of making the two biomarkers more compatible with each other in the regional medical care system; however, this needs to be validated further in a wide variety of study groups in the future.

Conclusions

The selection of alternative cutoffs and the application of conversion formulas could make BNP and NT-proBNP more compatible, which is essential for efficient management of CVDs in the regional medical care system.
