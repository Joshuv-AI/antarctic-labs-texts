A protonation/degradation dual control pH-responsive LVF delivery system with rod-shaped core-shell structure for the treatment of bone infections
Authors: Lei Chen, Jieyu Zhang, Xiashu Jiang, Bo Zhang, Yani Sun, Kang Zhao, Binghua Yao, Yufei Tang, Quanchang Tan, Zixiang Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibiotic-loaded poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) bone cement was employed to inhibit bone infection and promote bone healing at the lesion site. However, there are several limitations, including a failure to release the antibiotic in response to the acidic environment of the bone infection, a mismatch between the drug release cycle and the healing cycle of human bone, inadequate mechanical strength, and inferior osteogenic properties. In this research, CaCO3@P(HEMA-DMAEMA)-LVF (C@P-LVF) with a rod-shaped core-shell structure was synthesized and incorporated with PMMA bone cement to create C@P-LBC, a novel pH-responsive LVF delivery system achieved by the dual control mechanism of protonation/degradation of C@P-LVF and its unique rod-shaped structure. Simultaneously, the released calcium ions from C@P-LBC promoted mechanical properties and osteogenic properties. When the addition of C@P-LVF was 50 %, the cumulative release ratios of LVF from C@P-LBC were 34.08 ± 0.68 % and 65.52 ± 1.43 % at pH 7.4 and 5.5, respectively, representing a significant increase compared to PMMA bone cement. C@P-LBC also exhibited excellent pH-responsive antibacterial properties against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Meanwhile, the maximum compressive strength of C@P-LBC was 92.0 ± 1.7 MPa. Furthermore, C@P-LBC enhanced proliferation and stimulated early differentiation of osteoblasts. In vivo experiments confirmed that C@P-LBC promoted new bone formation and bone growth in bone defects. Additionally, C@P-LBC displayed superior in vivo antibacterial properties. Therefore, C@P-LBC emerged as a promising bone repair material to effectively facilitate the healing of bone tissue.Keywords: pH-responsive LVF release, Rod-shaped core-shell structure, Protonation, Antibacterial activities, Osteogenic properties

Introduction

With the increasing severity of population aging, the prevalence of trauma, osteoporosis, and joint diseases has become a significant public health concern [1,2]. Trauma-induced open fractures, osteoporosis-induced compression fractures, and arthroplasty treatments are prone to bone infections, with an incidence rate of approximately 0.4 %–16.1 % [3]. Bacterial infections during bone repair collectively induced a localized acidic microenvironment through both direct (acid production by bacteria) and indirect (triggering hypoxia and immunometabolic responses) pathways. In severe bone infections, the pH was reduced to 5.5 [4], which caused severe corrosion of the bone tissues and further exacerbated bone defects. The typical bone healing cycle for a fracture or arthroplasty is 4–8 weeks [5]. However, in the case of a fracture or arthroplasty accompanied by bone infection, the bone healing cycle is extended to 6–12 weeks, causing the disease to be challenging to treat and resulting in a prolonged healing period in orthopedics [6]. Consequently, controlling and eradicating bone infections represent a pivotal aspect of efficacious treatment in fracture and arthroplasty.

Currently, clinical treatment of inflammatory infection primarily employs specific strategies that combine infection control with the promotion of bone regeneration. Bone repair materials are primarily utilized to treat bone infections resulting from fractures and arthroplasty. Bone repair materials are divided into organic and inorganic, among which inorganic types such as calcium phosphate cement (CPC) [7], magnesium phosphate cement (MPC) [8], and calcium sulfate cement (CSC) [9] possess excellent biocompatibility and resorbability. However, inferior mechanical strength and tendency to collapse limit their application in the fracture of load-bearing areas and arthroplasty [10]. Organic polymethylmethacrylate bone cement (PMMA) has emerged as the most widely used biomaterial in bone repair due to its high mechanical strength, excellent plasticity, outstanding injectability, and rapid curing [11]. As the site of bone infection is typically accompanied by severe and complex inflammation, antibiotics need to be loaded into the bone repair material to inhibit the bone infection [12]. In clinically used antibiotic-loaded bone cements, it was stipulated that the addition amount of antibiotics should not exceed 10 % of the solid phase. In addition, relevant studies indicated that excessive antibiotic addition within this range can degrade the mechanical properties of the bone cement and may lead to an initially excessively high release concentration, potentially triggering toxic reactions [13]. Conversely, an amount within 5 % can enhance drug utilization efficiency [14]. However, PMMA bone cement loaded with antibiotics has the disadvantages of a low cumulative release ratio and short release cycle [15]. Worse still, in the local acidic environment caused by bone infection, the lack of pH-responsive characteristics results in the inability to respond to the actual pH situation for antibiotic release [16], which prevents the pH of the lesion site from recovering to normal, causing further corrosion of the bone tissue [17]. Furthermore, PMMA is biologically inert, and its inability to be absorbed by the body after implantation also has a significant impact on the release of Ca2+ and other biologically active substances, which results in the inferior osseointegration and osteoinductivity. Consequently, in a local environment of low pH caused by infection of bone tissue, there is a pressing necessity to develop a PMMA-based bone repair material with pH-responsive drug release characteristics to inhibit bacteria and facilitate the healing of bone tissue.

Hydrogels and bioceramics with pH-responsive drug release characteristics have been developed for treatment in response to pH changes. The pH-responsive hydrogels [[18], [19], [20]] are mainly used for drug release through protonation response. However, the protonation-responsive antibiotic release is associated with the burst release and a short drug-release cycle, which may lead to surgical failure due to bacterial infections in the later stages of bone infection treatment [21]. Another route of pH-responsive drug release was achieved by the degradation of inorganic substances in acidic environments [22]. It is noteworthy that biodegradable CaCO3 has served as a principal drug carrier due to its sensitive pH responsiveness and favourable biocompatibility. The crystal structure significantly influences the degradation behavior of CaCO3, which in turn affects the drug release properties. The porous structure and high specific surface area of spherical aragonite-type CaCO3 provide excellent biodegradability, while the thermodynamic instability leads to the conversion to calcite-type CaCO3. Calcite-type CaCO3 has the capacity for long-lasting degradation and release of drugs, which provides space for the growth of new bone tissue [23]. However, CaCO3 demonstrated an inadequately controlled antibiotic release in acidic environments, and the drug release cycle did not match the bone healing cycle with concomitant bone infection. Furthermore, the degradation of CaCO3 has a detrimental impact on the supportive function of the material within the body [24]. Although the studies have conferred pH-responsive drug release properties to hydrogels and bioceramics, the hydrophobicity and lack of pH responsiveness of PMMA bone cement restricted its therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of fractures and joint replacements associated with bone infection.

Antibiotic-loaded PMMA bone cement also has the disadvantages of inferior osteoconductivity, osteoinductivity, and osteogenic activity. Existing studies have mainly improved the osteogenic properties of antibiotic-loaded PMMA bone cement by releasing bioactive ions [25] and bioactive substances [26]. The process of bone growth and osseointegration requires a large amount of Ca2+. However, the hydrophobic and non-biodegradable features of PMMA bone cement resulted in a slow or even non-degradation of the calcium-containing inorganic substances, which hindered the release of Ca2+ [27]. Therefore, sustained release of Ca2+ from antibiotic-loaded PMMA bone cement at the site of bone infection has been a crucial approach to improve osseointegration and promote bone growth.

In this research, DMAEMA was selected for protonated pH-responsive antibiotic release, and polymerized with HEMA, which possessed favourable osteoinductive properties [28]. In addition, synergistic pH-responsive drug release was achieved by the degradation of calcite-type CaCO3 in an acidic environment to release internally encapsulated antibiotics. Levofloxacin hydrochloride (LVF) is a quinolone antibacterial agent with significant inhibitory effects against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. LVF can penetrate beyond the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) for susceptible pathogens commonly in bone and joint infections [29]. In addition, LVF exhibited exceptional chemical stability, maintaining the intact molecular structure under acidic conditions (pH 5.5) and effectively resisting hydrolysis, oxidation, or any other reactions that could potentially impair its pharmacological activity. P(HEMA-DMAEMA)-LVF [P(H-D)-LVF] and CaCO3-LVF were synthesized by solution polymerization and coprecipitation method, and CaCO3@P(HEMA-DMAEMA)-LVF (C@P-LVF) with rod-shaped core-shell structure was fabricated by dispersion polymerization method. Subsequently, C@P-LVF was incorporated into PMMA bone cement to construct C@P-LVF bone cement (C@P-LBC), a pH-responsive LVF delivery system with long release cycle achieved by the dual control of protonation/degradation and the special rod structure. Specifically, in the initial stage, rapid LVF release in an acidic environment was achieved relying on the protonation of the shell layer (P(H-D)-LVF). In the later stage, long-term release of LVF in an acidic environment was accomplished through the degradation of the core layer (CaCO3-LVF). The composition, morphology, specific surface area, LVF encapsulation efficiency, and LVF loading efficiency of C@P-LVF were examined. The pH-responsive LVF release profiles and the dual control mechanism of protonation/degradation were highlighted. Furthermore, in vitro antibacterial properties, cytocompatibility, in vivo histocompatibility, and osteogenic properties were also evaluated.

Experimental section

Materials

PMMA bone cement (Mendec Spine) was purchased from Tecres S.P.A. (Verona, Italy). Hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA) and (Dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DMAEMA) were purchased from Tianjin Fuchen Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd.(Tianjin, China). Levofloxacin (LVF) was obtained from Macklin Co., Ltd., (Shanghai, China). Polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) and ammonium persulphate (APS) were purchased from Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd., (Shanghai, China). N, N-Methylene Bis Acrylamide (MBA), calcium chloride (CaCl2) and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) were obtained from Fuchen Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd., (Tianjin, China). Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution was purchased from Macklin Co., Ltd., (Shanghai, China).

Fabrication of CaCO3-LVF@P(HEMA-DMAEMA)-LVF

1.11 g CaCl2 was added to 10 mL LVF solution (0.6 wt%), 0.2 g PVP was added to 10 mL Na2CO3 solution (10.6 wt%), and CaCl2 solution was gradually added to Na2CO3 solution and stirred for 24 h. The product was washed and freeze-dried to obtain CaCO3-LVF.

HEMA and DMAEMA were added to the three-necked flask in a mass ratio of 4:1. The mass ratios of CaCO3-LVF to the polymer precursors (HEMA and DMAEMA) were 1:0.5, 1:1, and 1:1.5, respectively, based on trial experiments. These ratios were selected to obtain rod-shaped core-shell structured C@P-LVF with different aspect ratios and shell thicknesses. The resulting materials were subsequently dispersed in deionized water to acquire CaCO3-LVF suspension. MBA solution (3 wt%), LVF solution (6 wt%), and CaCO3-LVF suspension were added to a three-necked flask and stirred in a nitrogen-filled water bath. Subsequently, APS solution (2 wt%) was added and reacted at 70 °C for 6 h. The products were washed and freeze-dried to obtain CaCO3-LVF@P(HEMA-DMAEMA)-LVF (C@P-LVF), designated as C1@P0.5-LVF, C1@P1-LVF, and C1@P1.5-LVF. Fabrication of C@P-LBC

In this study, C1@P1-LVF exhibited LVF encapsulation efficiency and loading efficiency of 64.0 ± 0.8 % and 3.53 ± 0.08 %, respectively, demonstrating its superior drug-loading performance in the C@P-LVF, as shown in Table 2. When its addition amount reached 50 % of the solid phase, the loading efficiency of LVF in the composite was 1.76 %, indicating a favourable drug safety profile [14]. Therefore, the mass fractions of C@P-LVF in the solid of PMMA bone cement were 10 %, 20 %, 30 %, 40 %, and 50 %, and the solid and liquid were mixed homogeneously according to a ratio of 2 g/mL. The C@P-LVF modified PMMA bone cement was noted as C@P-LBC. The P(H-D)-LVF and CaCO3-LVF modified PMMA bone cement were labeled as P(H-D)-LBC and CaCO3-LBC, respectively.

Characterization of C@P-LBC

The characteristic functional groups were analyzed via Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Nexus 870, ThermoFisher, USA) in the range of 4000-400 cm−1 by the KBr method. The molecular structure of P(H-D)-LVF was tested by a 1H NMR nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (Bruker Avance NEO, Bruker, Germany). The phase composition was observed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Smart Lab 9 KW, Rigaku, Japan). The zeta potential was determined by the Zeta potential analyzer (Model ZS90, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK).

The morphology of CaCO3-LVF and C@P-LVF was observed by SEM (JEM-6700F, Nihon Electronics, Japan), and the distribution of Ca and P elements was visualized by EDS-Mapping. The microstructure of CaCO3-LVF, C@P-LVF, and their diffraction patterns were also observed by TEM (JEM-3010, Nihon Electronics, Japan). Cross-sections of C@P-LVF were prepared by sectioning with a razor blade after freezing in liquid nitrogen. The cross-sections of the rod-shaped core-shell structure of C@P-LVF were observed by TEM (JEM-3010, Nihon Electronics, Japan). In addition, the elemental distribution of Ca and P elements in the cross-section of C@P-LVF was evaluated using an EDS whilst performing TEM. Furthermore, the surface morphology and internal structure of C@P-LBC were characterized by SEM (JEM-6700F, Nihon Electronics, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV after coating with gold particles in a sputter coater.

The dough time, setting time, and maximum temperature of PMMA bone cement, CaCO3-LBC, and C@P-LBC were tested according to ISO 5833-2002 with five replications for each component.

Mechanical properties

The compressive strength and elastic modulus of the bone cement were determined by ISO 5833-2002, and the cylindrical specimens (Ф 6 × 12 mm) were polished with 1000 mesh sandpaper to guarantee complete parallel surfaces. The mechanical test was conducted on the testing machine (BOSE, ELF3200, USA) along the axial direction of the sample at a loading rate of 20 mm/min. The compressive strength was acquired from the stress-strain curve, and the elastic modulus was calculated from the first-order derivative of the stress-strain curve [30]. Five samples were tested to obtain a mean value for each component (n = 5).

In vitro degradation

Samples of CaCO3-LBC and C@P-LBC (Ф 10 × 3 mm) were immersed in 5 mL of PBS at pH 7.4 and 5.5, respectively, and placed in a constant temperature and humidity chamber at 37 °C. After immersion for 1d, 2d, 4d, 7d, 14d, 21d, and 28d, samples were removed and placed in an oven to dry to a constant weight. In addition, the release of Ca2+ from C@P-LBC was measured using a flame type atomic absorption spectrophotometer (SP-3801AA(I)). The pH changes in the PBS in which the bone cement samples were immersed were measured using a precision pH meter (DDS-11A, Qiwei Instrument, Hangzhou), with the pH of the solutions recorded after 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h.

In vitro antibacterial assay

The antibacterial activities were evaluated in environments with pH values of 7.4, 6.7, 6.1, and 5.5 using Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli, ATCC 9637) and Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus, ATCC 12600). The bone cement was immersed in PBS at pH 7.4, 6.7, 6.1 and 5.5, respectively, and the extract was removed after soaking for 7 days, and added to a filter paper with a diameter of 10 mm. The filter paper with the extract solution was impregnated at the center of a sterile agar plate, which was coated with 100 μL of bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1 × 105 (CFU/mL),and then incubating at 37 °C for 24 h in the constant temperature incubator, images of the zone of inhibition (ZOI) were recorded. Thereafter, the extracts of samples were added to the wells culturing E coli & S. aureus, respectively and continue incubated for 24h. The Live & Dead Bacterial Staining Kit (C2030S, Beyotime, China) was used to further assess the antibacterial effect of C@P-LBC according to the instruction of manufacturer, and the live/dead stained images were acquired using a fluorescence inverted microscope (CKX53, Olympus, Japan). Bone cement samples were immersed in PBS buffers with pH values of 7.4, 6.7, 6.1, and 5.5, respectively. After 7 days of immersion, the extracts were collected and applied to filter papers with a diameter of 10 mm. These filter papers, containing the extract, were then co-cultured with bacterial suspension at a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU/mL for 24 h. Cytotoxicity and cell viability

Cytotoxicity and cell viability were evaluated using MMT assay (Sigma, Saint Louis, USA) using the osteoblast obtained from the trypsin-digested calvaria of newborn Sprague-Dawley (SD) rat. The samples were sterilized using 60Co gamma-irradiation (25 kGy). The culture solution was added to the centrifuge tubes loaded with bone cement at a ratio of 0.2 g:1 mL, and the extract was obtained by placing it in an incubator at 37 °C for 48 h. The osteoblast suspensions (n = 5) were inoculated in 96-well plates, and the number of cells added in each well was 2 × 104 (cells/well). The osteoblast suspension was incubated for 4 h, and then the supernatant was aspirated with a pipette gun, followed by adding 100 μL of the extract to each well plate. Osteoblasts were cultured in 96-well plates containing extracts for 1, 3, and 5 days at 37 °C, followed by adding 20 μL of MTT solution and incubating for 4 h at 37 °C in an incubator with 5 % CO2. After removing the supernatant, 0.1 mL of Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well and shaken for 10 min. After the crystals were completely dissolved, the well plates were placed on a Bio-Rad microplate reader (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) to determine each component's optical density (OD) at 490 nm.

Osteoblasts (2 × 104 cells/well) were inoculated onto the surface of different components of bone cement in 24-well plates for 1, 3, and 5 days to determine cell proliferation. After removing the medium on each cultural day, MTT solution was added to the culture solution at 50 μL per well, and the supernatant was aspirated after 4 h of incubation in an incubator. DMSO was added at 500 μL per well and shaken for 10 min. 100 μL of solution per well was pipetted into a 96-well plate (n = 5).

Cell adhesion was observed by seeding osteoblasts (2 × 104 cells/well) onto the samples (Ф 10 × 3 mm) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator for 1, 3, and 5 days. The culture solution was aspirated and washed with PBS, followed immediately by adding 2.5 % glutaraldehyde to the samples and transferring them to a 4 °C environment for cell fixation. Cells were permeabilized with 0.5 % TritonX-100 solution and washed with PBS. The osteoblasts were stained with 500 μL of rhodamine-labeled ghost pen cyclic peptide at a concentration of 100 nM under light-avoidance conditions, and the staining solution was aspirated and washed with phosphate buffer after 30 min. Subsequently, the nuclei were stained with 500 μL of DAPI at a concentration of 100 nM and washed with phosphate buffer after 10 min. The cell morphology was observed under fluorescent microscopy (Axio Imager, ZEISS, Germany), and the cell area and aspect ratio were measured using Image J software.

Osteoblastic differentiation

ALP

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity of osteoblasts was investigated on days 7 and 14 after seeding 1 × 105 cells onto individual cement disks (n = 5) in a 24-well plate. The cell monolayers were washed twice with PBS and were processed with 10 % neutrophilic formalin for 15 min. After washing twice with PBS, leachate from the various bone cement samples were stained according to the manufacturer's protocol and observed with a light microscope (Sigma, USA). ALP activity was quantified using an ALP assay kit (Beyotime, China) after 7/14 days of culture. The total cellular protein was quantified simultaneously using a BCA protein assay kit according to the protocol (Thermo Scientific, USA). After rinsing with distilled water, the cells were stained with 1 mL of ALP staining solution for 20 min. ALP activity was determined after measuring the absorbance at 405 nm on a microplate reader (BX53, Olympus).

Alizarin red staining

The cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were inoculated on the surface of bone cement samples in a 24-well plate. After culturing for 14 and 21 days, the cells were fixed and stained with Alizarin Red (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. Following rinsing, microscopic images of the mineralized nodules were observed and captured.

Western blot

Osteoblasts (1 × 105 cells/well) were added to a 12-well plate containing bone cement samples. After 7 days of culture, 500 μL of RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (G2006, Servicebio Technology, Wuhan) was added to each well for protein extraction and cell lysis. The samples were then centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (G2026, Servicebio Technology, Wuhan). Subsequently, sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was employed to separate the proteins, and the separated proteins were then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After activation in anhydrous ethanol, the PVDF membrane was blocked for 1 h in Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST) containing 5 % nonfat milk, followed by three washes with TBST for blocking. Subsequently, the membrane was transferred to a primary antibody solution under a constant current of 350 mA and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After repeated washing with TBST, the PVDF membrane was incubated in the labeled secondary antibody solution at room temperature for 1 h. The PVDF membrane was subsequently washed three times with TBST. The membrane was carefully removed and placed on absorbent paper to remove excess liquid. Equal parts of ECL substrate (G2014, Servicebio, Wuhan) and ultrasensitive ECL substrate (G2020, Servicebio, Wuhan) were mixed and applied to cover the membrane surface. After incubating for 1 min at room temperature, any residual liquid was gently blotted away using absorbent paper. β-Actin antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was used as an internal reference. Chemiluminescence imaging was performed using the SCG-W3000 imaging system (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) to detect the protein levels of Runx2, OPN, and OCN. The resulting images were quantitatively analyzed using AIWBwell™ analysis software (Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

In vivo study of bone defect repair

The experimental procedures involving laboratory animals were approved by Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committees at the Fourth Military Medical University (No.20230080). SD rats were selected to evaluate in vivo biocompatibility and the osteogenic capacity of C@P-LBC. After anesthesia with sodium pentobarbital (20 mg/kg) injection, the samples (Ф 2 × 15 mm) were implanted in the femoral condyle of rats. After 4 and 8 weeks, rats were executed to acquire the C@P-LBC implanted femoral condyles and surrounding muscle tissue and then immersed in a 10 % formalin solution for 24 h of fixation.

The bone cement implanted at femoral the site was visualized using Micro-CT (YXLON, Hamburg, Germany) to investigate the new bone growth at the material-bone interface and within the material. The CT data were processed using VGstudio MAX 3.0.2 (Volume Graphics, Heidelberg, Germany) to calculate the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (TB. Th), trabecular number (Tb. N) and trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) of the new bone.

Thereafter, the C@P-LBC implanted femoral condyles was decalcified. The decalcified femoral condyles tissue were embedded in paraffin and cut into 5 μm thick sections stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and Masson staining for histological assessment.

In vivo histocompatibility evaluation of C@P-LBC

To ascertain whether the implantation of C@P-LBC had any deleterious effects on SD rats, histological examination was conducted on sections of major organs including lung, liver, kidney, heart, and spleen) and the surrounded muscles obtained from 2.13.

In vivo antibacterial properties of C@P-LBC

Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing 250–300g were selected and randomly divided into three groups: PMMA, CaCO3-LBC, and C@P-LBC (n = 3 per group). The rats were anesthetized by injecting sodium pentobarbital (20 mg/kg), the hair on the left hindlimb of each rat was shaved, and a defect was created in the femoral region using a drill to simulate a bone defect condition. 100 μL of S. aureus suspension (1.0 × 108 cells/mL) was injected into the femoral defect area to establish an infection model. The bone cement samples (Ф 2 × 15 mm) were implanted into the femurs of rats, and the surgical incisions were sutured. Femoral specimens were collected at 2 weeks and 4 weeks after surgery, and ultrasonic treatment was applied to the femoral samples to collect bacteria. After ultrasonic treatment, the bacterial suspension was inoculated onto agar plates, which were then placed in a biochemical incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the bacterial colonies on the agar plates were counted, photographed, and recorded. The mean colony counts for each experimental group were calculated. In addition, the rat femoral specimens were fixed in paraformaldehyde for 2 days and subsequently decalcified using an EDTA solution. The bone cement samples were carefully extracted from the specimens, and the bone tissues were collected. These samples were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices using a pathology slicer (RM2255, Leica Instruments, Shanghai), and finally stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) to evaluate the infection status of the femoral tissues.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation. The significance level of experimental data between independent samples was analyzed by t-test (Graph Pad Software 6, Inc.). A p-value <0.05 was considered as a significant difference.

Results and discussion

Characterization of C@P-LVF

FTIR evaluated the characteristic functional groups of C@P-LVF in Fig. 1(a). The stretching vibrational band of the ester carbonyl (C=O) in HEMA was observed at 1724 cm−1, while the characteristic absorption bands of C-O-C in HEMA were identified at 1071 cm−1 and 1246 cm−1. Vibrational bands at 1091 cm−1 and 2770 cm−1 were attributed to the stretching vibration of N(CH3)2 and C-N in the tertiary amine of DMAEMA, respectively. In contrast, the intensity of characteristic bands C=O and C-O-C in the C@P-LVF was diminished, while the vibrational band -N(CH3)2 of DMAEMA was also weakened. In addition, no characteristic absorption band of C=C was observed in the vicinity of 1680 cm−1, indicating the successful free-radical polymerization of HEMA and DMAEMA in C@P-LVF. The out-of-plane bending vibrational band of CaCO3-LVF appeared at 875 cm−1, which was also identified in the C@P-LVF. In addition, the characteristic absorption band of -OH was detected at 3100-3600 cm−1 in C@P-LVF. In contrast, the characteristic absorption band of C-N-C was blue-shifted to 1253 cm−1, representing the formation of a hydrogen bond between the tertiary amine in DMAEMA and the hydroxyl group in HEMA.

1H NMR was used to further determine the structure of P(HEMA-DMAEMA) in C@P-LVF. Methylene hydrogen [-CH2], methylene hydrogen [H, -O-CH2-CH2-] attached to oxygen and methyl hydrogen [H, -N(CH3)2] on tertiary amine group of PDMAEMA in P(H-D) was observed at 1.97 ppm, 2.25 ppm and 4.05 ppm in Fig. 1(b), respectively. Two methylene hydrogens [H, -O-CH2-CH2-OH] of PHEMA in P(H-D) were observed at 4.02 ppm and 4.06 ppm. In addition, the presence of hydrogen in the terminal hydroxyl group [H, -CH2-OH] and tertiary amine group [H, -N(CH3)2] in the range of 3.90–4.07 ppm provided additional evidence that the free-radical polymerization of DMAEMA with HEMA. The specific process was described as follows: Under heating conditions, ammonium persulfate (APS) decomposed to generate sulfate radicals (SO4−·), which attacked the carbon atoms of the carbon-carbon double bonds in HEMA or DMAEMA, forming monomer radicals (HEMA· and DMAEMA·) and initiating chain propagation. The acrylamide groups in the MBA molecules could react with the radicals on the copolymer chains, linking the linear chains into a three-dimensional network structure and ultimately forming P(HEMA-DMAEMA).

The physical phase of C@P-LVF was examined by XRD in Fig. 1(c). The crystal planes of CaCO3 (104) (110) (202) and (116) were detected at 29.37°, 35.94°, 43.12°, and 48.45° in both C@P-LVF and CaCO3-LVF, which was consistent with the standard card of calcite-type CaCO3 (PDF: 72–1937). Given that the amide reaction affected the surface charge, the zeta potentials were evaluated in Fig. 1(d). The zeta potential of C@P-LVF improved with increasing P(H-D) content, and the values of C1@P1.5-LVF at pH 7.4 and 5.5 were 30.8 ± 7.0 mV and 42.2 ± 5.3 mV, respectively. In addition, according to relevant literature reports [31], the tertiary amine groups of DMAEMA remained electrically neutral in a pH 7.4 environment, with the nitrogen atom not bound to H+. However, a decrease in pH enhanced the protonation degree of DMAEMA. Consequently, C@P-LVF exhibited lower zeta potential values in a neutral environment compared to those in an acidic environment.

The morphology of CaCO3-LVF and C@P-LVF was observed by SEM in Fig. 2(a–h). As illustrated in Fig. 2(a–d), CaCO3-LVF and C1@P0.5-LVF exhibited a rod-shaped structure with a rough surface. Conversely, C1@P1-LVF and C1@P1.5-LVF maintained the rod-shaped structure but possessed a relatively smooth surface in Fig. 2(e–h). These results suggested that P(H-D)-LVF was successfully encapsulated on the surface of CaCO3-LVF at the mass ratios of 1:1 and 1:1.5. In addition, the range of aspect ratios for C1@P1-LVF and C1@P1.5-LVF was 1.89–3.31 and 2.36–7.62, respectively. The specific surface area of P(H-D)-LVF, CaCO3-LVF, C1@P1-LVF, and C1@P1.5-LVF were determined by BET, as shown in Fig. S1. The microstructure of C@P-LVF was further investigated by TEM in Fig. 2(i–t), and the thickness of P(H-D)-LVF in C1@P1-LVF and C1@P1.5-LVF was also analyzed. The electron diffraction pattern in Fig. 2(i–k) proved that CaCO3-LVF was a polycrystalline crystal, and the crystallographic parameters (110) and (202) were consistent with those corresponding to 35.94° and 43.12° of CaCO3 in the XRD pattern. Meanwhile, the surface diffraction pattern of C1@P0.5-LVF proved that CaCO3-LVF was not entirely coated by P(H-D)-LVF in Fig. 2(l–n). In contrast, both C1@P1-LVF and C1@P1.5-LVF in Fig. 2(o–t) contained smooth P(H-D) shell layers with thicknesses of 0.1 μm and 0.2 μm, respectively, and no electron diffraction pattern was identified in Fig. 2(q)(t). The rod-shaped core-shell structure of C@P-LVF was further investigated through cross-sectional TEM images, with the results shown in Fig. 2(u–z). Fig. 2(u) and (v) present the cross-section of the rod-shaped core-shell structure of C1@P1-LVF. The TEM image in Fig. 2(w) indicated that the shell thickness of C1@P1-LVF was approximately 0.12 μm. In Fig. 2(x)(y)(z), the Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum of the cross-section of the C1@P1-LVF revealed a core-shell distribution of Ca and N elements. Specifically, the Ca element (yellow) derived from CaCO3-LVF was distributed within the interior of the structure, while the N element (purple) from P(H-D)-LVF was predominantly located on the exterior. Notably, since the LVF within the core layer (CaCO3-LVF) also contained nitrogen elements, a discernible amount of nitrogen elements can also be observed in the interior of the structure. These results demonstrated the successful fabrication of the rod-shaped C@P-LVF with a core-shell structure.

The formation mechanism of the rod-shaped C@P-LVF is explained as follows: Initially, the synthesis of CaCO3-LVF was accomplished employing the co-precipitation method. In this process, CaCl2 underwent a swift reaction with Na2CO3, leading to the formation of a calcium carbonate precursor. By controlling the concentration of the precipitating agent (Na2CO3) and the reaction time, a gradual transformation of the calcium carbonate precursor into calcite-type CaCO3 was achieved. Concurrently, LVF was effectively encapsulated within the CaCO3 and adsorbed onto the surface of CaCO3. During the co-precipitation procedure, it has been observed that a relatively high concentration of LVF (exceeding 1 mg/mL) exerts a significant inductive effect on the growth orientation of CaCO3, specifically promoting its elongation along the c-axis and consequently leading to the generation of rod-shaped structures [32]. Further employing CaCO3-LVF as a nucleating agent, the dispersant PVP attaches to the surface of CaCO3-LVF via physical adsorption or chemical bonding, thus creating steric hindrance that effectively inhibits particle aggregation. In the presence of an initiator, monomers comprising HEMA and DMAEMA initiate polymerization reactions on the surface of CaCO3-LVF, leading to the formation of polymer chains. The carbonyl groups of PVP form hydrogen bonds with the amino or hydroxyl groups of the polymer chains, thereby enhancing the stability of the shell layer (P(H-D)-LVF).

Dough time and curing parameters of C@P-LBC

The dough time of C@P-LBC in Fig. 3(a) was prolonged with the increase in C@P-LVF contents, ranging from 4.41 ± 0.21 min to 9.75 ± 0.24 min, the dough time of CaCO3-LBC was slightly extended compared to that of C@P-LBC under the same additive content, and the data ranged from 4.75 ± 0.18 min to 11.9 ± 0.14 min, which was longer than that of PMMA bone cement (4.25 ± 0.16 min) [30]. As depicted in Fig. 3(b), when the content of C@P-LVF reached 50 %, the maximum setting time of C@P-LBC was 18.17 ± 0.88 min, which ranged between the setting time of CaCO3-LBC (19.44 ± 0.61 min) and PMMA (7.6 ± 0.86 min) [30]. The incorporation of C@P-LVF influenced the polymerization reaction of the bone cement. Specifically, increasing the content of C@P-LVF extended the setting time of C@P-LBC, while this phenomenon could provide clinicians with a more extended working time during clinical application. In addition, with the increase of C@P-LVF content, the maximum setting temperature of C@P-LBC in Fig. 3(c) gradually decreased from 63.5 ± 0.5 °C to 47.9 ± 1.3 °C, which was lower than the maximum setting temperature of CaCO3-LBC (63.6 ± 2.2°C-51.3 ± 0.6 °C) and PMMA bone cement (92.5 ± 1.3 °C) [30].

Curing parameters of C@P-LBC and CaCO3-LBC: (a) dough time; (b) setting temperature; (c) setting time.

The prolonged dough time and setting time of C@P-LBC was due to the absence of carbon-carbon double bonds in the C@P-LVF, which did not participate in the solid-liquid reaction of bone cement, preventing the initiation process of the initiator and reducing the PMMA content involved in the reaction, thus retarding the rate of the polymerization compared to PMMA bone cement. Meanwhile, the increased content of C@P-LVF in the composite also reduced the heat generated during the polymerization, resulting in the decreased maximum setting temperature of C@P-LBC, effectively avoiding burns to normal tissues around the lesion from excessive reaction heat.

pH-responsive release profiles of LVF

LVF in the C@P-LBC was rapidly released in different pH environments during the initial 7 days and released slowly to reach equilibrium after 126 days in Fig. 5(a) and (d). When the content of C@P-LVF was 50 %, the cumulative release ratio of LVF at pH 5.5 was 65.52 ± 1.43 %, which was 1.92 times higher than that at pH 7.4. After comparison, the cumulative release ratio of LVF from C@P-LBC at pH 7.4 and 5.5 was increased by a maximum of 705.7 % and 1647.2 %, respectively, compared to that from PMMA bone cement in Fig. S2. When the loading of C@P-LVF was 10 %, the cumulative release ratios of LVF from C@P-LBC were measured at 8.50 ± 0.17 % and 5.10 ± 0.16 % at pH 5.5 and 7.4, respectively. Increasing the C@P-LVF content to 20 % resulted in respective release ratios of 12.36 ± 0.69 % at pH 5.5 and 7.25 ± 0.68 % at pH 7.4. The lack of a statistically significant difference in LVF release from C@P-LBC between pH 5.5 and 7.4 was observed at incorporation levels of 10 % and 20 %. The rationale behind this result can be attributed to the following factors: PMMA bone cement, characterized by its inherent hydrophobicity and dense microstructure typically exhibiting a porosity of less than 5 %, acted as a physical barrier that restricted drug diffusion. At lower loadings of C@P-LVF (10 % and 20 %), although C@P-LBC exhibited water-absorbing properties, PMMA bone cement remained the dominant component. Since PMMA is non-pH-responsive, the exposure of C@P-LBC to acidic PBS induced only minimal LVF release, primarily relying on the protonation and degradation of the embedded C@P-LVF. Concurrently, hydrogen bonding interactions between LVF and P(HEMA-DMAEMA) segments of C@P-LVF impeded LVF desorption from the carrier surface. Additionally, the porosity of different components of C@P-LBC was measured upon reaching the drug release equilibrium state. The results in Fig. S5 indicated that the porosity of C@P-LBC increased with higher C@P-LVF content. Specifically, the porosities for composites with 10 % and 20 % C@P-LVF were only 1.18 ± 0.29 % and 4.16 ± 0.19 %, respectively. Such low porosity restricted the ability of LVF to diffuse through the porous structure.

Furthermore, with a 50 % addition of C@P-LVF, the mass fractions of CaCO3-LVF and P(H-D)-LVF were calculated to be 30 % and 20 %, respectively, based on the loading efficiency (LE) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of LVF presented in Table 2. The synergistic release ratio (S) was calculated to be 30.8 % according to equation (3). Since the value is greater than 0, it indicates that protonation and degradation synergistically promoted the release of LVF.

The dual control mechanism of pH-responsive release is illustrated in Fig. 5(j). DMAEMA in P(H-D)-LVF contained tertiary amine groups (-N(CH3)2). In an acidic environment, the nitrogen atom (N) in the tertiary amine group, due to the presence of lone pair electrons, tended to bind with H+, forming a positively charged protonated product-the quaternary ammonium dimethylamino cation (-NH+(CH3)2). Therefore, as the P(H-D) content increased, the number of tertiary amine groups available for protonation with H+ increased, leading to a corresponding increase in the positive charges, as evidenced by the results of the zeta potential in Fig. 1(d). This process enhanced electrostatic repulsion with the positively charged LVF. In addition, the shell layer P(H-D)-LVF experienced volume expansion in acidic environments, forming the pathways that facilitated the diffusion of LVF from the C@P-LVF to the exterior of the bone cement. Concurrently, acidic PBS penetrated the C@P-LBC through osmosis, thereby promoting the degradation of calcite-type CaCO3 encapsulated in the C@P-LVF. The consequent electrostatic repulsion between Ca2+ and LVF promoted the release of LVF. Furthermore, the diffusion rate constant (k) of LVF in P(H-D)-LBC was significantly higher than that of CaCO3-LBC at pH 5.5 in Table 4, which indicated that the initial stage of LVF release was primarily dependent on the protonation of P(H-D)-LVF to provide the driving force for LVF diffusion. The degradation of CaCO3-LVF in the late release period led to the further diffusion of LVF. Moreover, the rod-shaped structure exhibited a shape effect, possessing a larger specific surface area compared to nanospheres and other particle shapes, which was displayed in Fig. S4. This rendered drug molecules on the surface of the rod-shaped structure more susceptible to external environmental factors, such as solution concentration gradient, temperature, and pH. The internal diffusion pathways of drug molecules within the rod-shaped structure were relatively longer and more complex, particularly along the axial direction of the rod-shaped structure, which facilitated sustained drug release [33].

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of composite with different P(H-D)-LVF, CaCO3-LVF, and C@P-LVF contents were investigated before and after immersion, and the results are presented in Fig. 6. As the content of C@P-LVF increased, the compressive strength and elastic modulus of C@P-LBC in Fig. 6(a)(b) were decreased from 92.1 ± 1.7 MPa and 2325.4 ± 87.5 MPa to 86.2 ± 0.7 MPa and 2129.2 ± 69.4 MPa, respectively. Although the compressive strength and elastic modulus of C@P-LBC were lower than those of PMMA bone cement (compressive strength: 94.09 ± 1.65 MPa; elastic modulus: 2346.41 ± 115.62 MPa), the compressive strengths of C@P-LBC before immersion were higher than the implantation standard of 70 MPa as stipulated in ISO5833-2002. The higher amount of C@P-LVF not only enabled the dual pH-responsive release characteristics of LVF but also ensured the superior mechanical strength of C@P-LBC. Since the integration between PMMA bone cement and C@P-LVF was primarily achieved through mechanical interlocking. Furthermore, the HEMA segments within C@P-LVF possessed methacrylate backbone and hydroxyl groups, which facilitated additional interactions via van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding with the molecular chains of PMMA, respectively. In contrast, the increase in CaCO3-LVF content led to a gradual enhancement in the compressive strength and elastic modulus of CaCO3-LBC, with values rising from 95.1 ± 1.6 MPa and 2367.1 ± 64.6 MPa to 100.2 ± 1.6 MPa and 2500.5 ± 82.9 MPa. In addition, the compressive strength and elastic modulus of C@P-LBC were greater than that of P(H-D)-LBC under the same additive amount, which was attributed to the high hardness, superior elastic modulus, and excellent mechanical stability of calcite-type CaCO3 [34] in C@P-LVF, simultaneously the protonation of P(H-D) in C@P-LVF generated the hydrogen bond with C=O in PMMA, thereby conferring enhanced mechanical properties to C@P-LBC.

The mechanical properties of different components after immersion are illustrated in Fig. 6(c)(d). PMMA bone cement still exhibited excellent compressive strength and elastic modulus after immersion, which was 93.13 ± 3.39 MPa and 2338.83 ± 100.16 MPa, respectively. The increase in the content of C@P-LVF resulted in a decrease in the compressive strength and elastic modulus of C@P-LBC from 91.4 ± 1.9 MPa and 2291.8 ± 117.4 MPa to 68.0 ± 1.6 MPa and 1636.1 ± 78.2 MPa, respectively. When the content of C@P-LVF was 50 %, the compressive strength and elastic modulus of C@P-LBC after immersion decreased by 26.1 % and 23.2 % compared with those before immersion. This phenomenon was due to the excellent water absorption properties of C@P-LBC in Fig. 4, where the water molecules acted as a plasticizer for the bone cement [28]. Despite the reduced elastic modulus of C@P-LBC compared to CaCO3-LBC, the values remain within the range of human cortical and cancellous bone [35], effectively preventing the stress-shielding effect and avoiding the risk of adjacent vertebral fractures.

During the drug release process, the pH of the solution was measured at different time points to evaluate environmental stability. The results in Fig. 7(f) demonstrated that with the prolongation of immersion time, the pH values of the PBS solutions containing all bone cement specimens exhibited an initial rapid decline followed by a gradual increase. LVF is typically present in the form of its hydrochloride salt (LVF-HCl) [37]. When C@P-LBC contacted PBS, the Cl− ions from the internally loaded LVF-HCl rapidly diffused into PBS. To maintain the electroneutrality of the solution, the H+ ions from LVF-HCl also diffused concurrently at a fast rate, leading to a decrease in the pH value of the PBS. At pH 5.5, as PBS was continuously absorbed into the bone cement, the CaCO3-LVF embedded in C@P-LVF acted as an alkaline filler that reacted with H+ in the solution to produce CO2 and H2O, thereby neutralizing the acidic environment. Additionally, the tertiary amine groups of DMAEMA in C@P-LBC underwent protonation in the acidic environment, further continuously consuming H+, which led to a gradual increase in the pH value. Notably, after being immersed in PBS at pH 5.5 for 48 h, the pH value of the solution containing C@P-LBC increased to 7.1, providing a stable microenvironment for subsequent bone repair.

Further observations were conducted on the surface morphology and internal structure of C@P-LBC after degradation. Fig. 7(g)–(j) revealed that PMMA bone cement exhibited a dense structure on both its surface and interior after immersion in PBS with pH values of 7.4 and 5.5. In comparison, C@P-LBC displayed a rougher surface morphology following degradation in PBS with pH values of 7.4 and 5.5, as observed in Fig. 7(k) and (m). Furthermore, the number of micropores formed in the interior of C@P-LBC after degradation at pH 7.4 was considerably lower than that observed following degradation at pH 5.5, as shown in Fig. 7(l) and (n). This difference can be attributed to the acidic environment, which induced rapid swelling of the P(H-D)-LVF three-dimensional network within the C@P-LVF. The acidic PBS could more readily diffuse into the interior of the material, thereby accelerating the dissolution and degradation of the core-layer (CaCO3-LVF), resulting in the formation of a greater number of micropores.

Antibacterial activities

The cumulative release concentrations of LVF from CaCO3-LBC at pH 7.4, 6.7, 6.1, and 5.5 over 7 days were 3.51 ± 0.14 μg/mL, 5.63 ± 0.21 μg/mL, 6.37 ± 0.08 μg/mL, and 7.03 ± 0.12 μg/mL, respectively. In addition, the cumulative release concentrations of LVF from C@P-LBC at pH 7.4, 6.7, 6.1, and 5.5 over 7 days were 54.21 ± 0.08 μg/mL, 56.11 ± 0.12 μg/mL, 62.47 ± 0.49 μg/mL, and 67.13 ± 0.10 μg/mL, respectively. These concentrations were higher than the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of LVF against E. coli and S. aureus to ensure effective antibacterial performance [38,39]. The antibacterial activities of C@P-LBC against S. aureus and E. coli in different pH environments were investigated in Fig. 8(a). The diameters of ZOI of C@P-LBC against S. aureus and E. coli in Fig. 8(b, c) at pH 5.5 were 26.16 ± 0.53 mm and 25.82 ± 0.34 mm, which were 1.27 and 1.25 times higher than those at pH 7.4, respectively. These results demonstrated that C@P-LBC provided excellent pH-responsive antibacterial characteristics.

Antibacterial activities of C@P-LBC in different pH environments: (a) antibacterial capacity against S. aureus and E. coli; (b) ZOI against S.aureus; (c) ZOI against E. coli; (d) Inhibition rate against S.aureus; (e) Inhibition rate against E. coli; (f) Fluorescent staining of live and dead bacteria (Scale bar: 100 μm).

The pH-responsive antibacterial properties were further demonstrated by the inhibition rate, as shown in Fig. 8(d and e). The inhibition rates of C@P-LBC against S. aureus and E. coli at pH 5.5 were 49.24 ± 0.94 % and 48.26 ± 0.54 %, respectively, which were higher than those of CaCO3-LBC for S. aureus and E. coli (45.26 ± 1.35 % and 46.97 ± 0.54 %). The staining results of live/dead bacteria of C@P-LBC in Fig. 8(f) demonstrated that the red area was larger than the green area, indicating that the amounts of dead bacteria increased with the decrease of pH.

In vitro cellular properties

The cytotoxicity and cell proliferation were demonstrated in Fig. 9. The OD values of C@P-LBC in Fig. 9(a) at 1d, 3d, and 5d were 0.55 ± 0.07, 0.68 ± 0.04, and 1.02 ± 0.06, respectively, which were significantly higher than those of CaCO3-LBC and PMMA bone cement at the same incubation time (p < 0.05), suggesting that the introduction of C@P-LVF reduced the cytotoxicity. The cell viability of C@P-LBC in Fig. 9(b) after 1d, 3d, and 5d of incubation were 2.62 %, 0.52 % and 5.89 % higher than those of CaCO3-LBC, and the values were also significantly higher than those of PMMA bone cement (p < 0.05). Since the hydrophilicity of C@P-LBC was improved by the tertiary amine and hydroxyl groups of C@P-LVF in Fig. 1(a), enhancing the interaction between the biomaterial and cells [40].

Cytotoxicity and cell viability: (a) Cytotoxicity; (b) Cell viability; (c) Adhesion of osteoblasts; (d) ALP staining; (e) ALP activity. (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001).

The adhesion of osteoblasts was visualized by fluorescence staining after 1, 3, and 5 days of co-culture in Fig. 9(c). The spindle-shaped cells containing filamentous pseudopods were observed on the surface of all groups after 5 days, and the number of cells adhering to the surface of C@P-LBC was higher than other groups. The proteins in osteoblasts are negatively charged [41], and the protonation of amine groups in DMAEMA increased the positive charge on the surface of C@P-LBC, thus facilitating the enhanced attraction of proteins in osteoblasts.

The early differentiation of osteoblasts on C@P-LBC was investigated by ALP staining. Stained images revealed higher levels of blue-black precipitates in C@P-LBC than those in other groups after 7 and 14 days of co-culture in Fig. 9(d). After 14 days of incubation in Fig. 9(e)–C@P-LBC demonstrated significantly higher ALP activity than the other components (p < 0.001), which proved that C@P-LBC promoted the early differentiation of osteoblasts. Since the released Ca2+ from the degradation of C@P-LBC stimulated the early differentiation of osteoblasts and up-regulated ALP expression [42]. Additionally, proteins and biomolecules in the culture medium [43] were attracted by C@P-LBC through electrostatic attraction, thus creating a more conducive environment for osteoblast differentiation.

In the late stage of osteogenic differentiation, Alizarin Red Staining (ARS) was employed to evaluate the formation of calcium nodules on the surface of bone cement, and the results are shown in Fig. 10(a). As the culture time progressed, the red mineralized areas in all groups gradually increased. After 21 days of culture, the calcium nodule content in the C@P-LBC group reached the highest level, indicating its excellent capabilities in promoting mineralization and osteogenic differentiation. Osteogenesis-related genes (such as Runx2, Opn, and Ocn) are involved in regulating the processes of matrix mineralization and bone formation, and can serve as biomarkers for evaluating the differentiation capacity of osteoblasts. The expression levels of these genes were detected by qRT-PCR, with the results shown in Fig. 10(b)–(d). The expression level of Runx2 in the C@P-LBC was 0.53 ± 0.06, which was significantly lower than that in the control group, PMMA group, and CaCO3-LBC group. In contrast, the expression levels of OPN and OCN in the C@P-LBC were significantly higher than those in the other groups, reaching maximum values of 1.22 ± 0.21 and 3.31 ± 0.34, respectively. A lower level of Runx2 contributed to maintaining the balance between osteogenic and osteoclastic activities [44], thereby preserving osteoblast activity and promoting matrix mineralization as well as the formation of mature osteoblasts. In addition, Runx2 can further regulate the expression of downstream genes such as Opn and Ocn. Consequently, the C@P-LBC group exhibited the highest gene expression levels of Opn and Ocn, indicating its excellent in vitro osteogenic capability. To more comprehensively and thoroughly evaluate the osteogenic enhancement capacity of the bone cement, Western blotting (WB) was further employed to analyze the expression levels of osteogenic proteins, with the results shown in Fig. 10(e). C@P-LBC significantly induced the expression of three proteins: Runx2, OPN, and OCN. The specific expression levels of these three proteins are shown in Fig. 10(f)–(h). Notably, the protein expression levels of Runx2, OPN, and OCN in the C@P-LBC group were significantly higher than those in the other three groups, with specific values of 5.06 ± 0.15, 2.77 ± 0.03, and 3.53 ± 0.07, respectively. This enhancement could be attributed to the release of Ca2+ from C@P-LBC, which acted as an “osteogenic signaling molecule” and promoted the expression of osteogenic protein genes by interacting with their binding sites [45], thereby elevating the levels of osteogenic proteins.

In vivo bone defect repair

The osteogenic capacity of C@P-LBC was assessed by immunological and histological tests of bone defect repair after 4 and 8 weeks in Fig. 11(a). Comparative analysis of Micro-CT images in Fig. 11(b and c) revealed that the newly formed bone areas, marked in red, were predominantly distributed on the surfaces of PMMA bone cement, CaCO3-LBC, and C@P-LBC. After 8 weeks of implantation, the C@P-LBC group exhibited a larger red-marked area on its surface compared to the PMMA group and the CaCO3-LBC group, indicating substantial new bone formation between the implant and the surrounding bone tissue, as well as higher interfacial osteogenic activity in this group. However, the growth of newly formed bone within the material remained limited, a phenomenon closely associated with the structural characteristics of the bone cement. C@P-LBC exhibited a certain degree of degradability, with a maximum cumulative weight loss ratio of 13.44 ± 1.09 % in Fig. 7(a). The degradation predominantly occurred on the surface of C@P-LBC, as this region was more readily exposed to the surrounding fluid environment, facilitating the degradation of internal C@P-LVF and the diffusion of calcium ions. SEM results in Fig. 7(k) demonstrated that the degradation induced a porous structure on the surface of C@P-LBC, providing favourable conditions for the adhesion, proliferation of osteoblasts, and subsequent bone ingrowth. However, the interior of the bone cement maintained a dense structure in Fig. 11(b)(c), which restricted the migration and growth of cells and blood vessels toward the interior. Therefore, the osteogenic process predominantly occurred in the surface region. In addition, a considerable amount of material remained 8 weeks post-implantation. This phenomenon not only provided space for new bone growth at the bone cement-bone tissue interface, but also ensured that C@P-LBC, as a filling material, could firmly support the surrounding tissues at the defect site in rats.

Quantitative BV/TV measurements confirmed this alteration in Fig. 11(d). BV/TV in the C@P-LBC group was 13.8 ± 0.2 % at 4 weeks, and the value escalated to 64.60 ± 1.1 % at 8 weeks. The trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), the trabecular number (Tb. N), and the trabecular spacing (Tb. Sp) of three groups at 4 and 8 weeks were further observed in Fig. 11(e, f, g). After 8 weeks of implantation, the values of Tb. Th and Tb. N were significantly higher than those at 4 weeks in all three groups. Tb. Th and Tb. Sp, with values of 0.77 ± 0.01 mm and 0.19 ± 0.02 mm after 4 and 8 weeks, were 49.67 % and 67.80 % lower than that in PMMA bone cement, indicating promoted bone growth in the C@P-LBC group.

Subsequently, the osteogenic capacity of C@P-LBC was assessed by H&E staining and Masson staining in Fig. 12 H&E staining in Fig. 12(a) revealed newly formed bone tissue in the region surrounded the implanted C@P-LBC, whereas only a small amount of new bone formation was observed in the marginal region of PMMA and CaCO3-LBC group. Masson staining in Fig. 12(b) indicated that more collagen fibers and new bone production were found at the interface in the C@P-LBC group than in the PMMA and CaCO3-LBC group. After 8 weeks, the collagen fibers in C@P-LBC were structurally intact and presented an orderly accumulation with continuous new osteogenesis. C@P-LBC exhibited excellent hydrophilicity due to the presence of amine and hydroxyl groups, which enhanced the proliferation and adhesion of osteoblasts and stimulated the formation of collagen fiber and new bone [46]. Meanwhile, Ca2+ released from C@P-LBC and the osteoinductive capacity of HEMA also promoted bone regeneration [47].

Histocompatibility

To ascertain whether the implantation of C@P-LBC had any deleterious effects on SD rats, histological examination was conducted on sections of major organs in Fig. 13. Histological analysis revealed that no significant differences were detected in the histological morphology of the vital organs between the two groups. Furthermore, no apparent cytotoxicity issues were observed in the C@P-LBC. Since the cytotoxicity of C@P-LBC was considerably reduced by the introduction of C@P-LVF. In addition, HEMA-based polymers exhibit superior in vivo biocompatibility by attenuating immune responses and accelerating inflammatory resolution, as evidenced by prior studies demonstrating their capacity to modulate immuno-inflammatory processes through mechanisms including NF-κB pathway inhibition and reduced secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. In summary, in vivo studies demonstrated that C@P-LBC exhibited a satisfactory biosafety profile and provided superior osseointegration and osteogenic properties to PMMA bone cement.

In vivo antibacterial properties

PMMA, CaCO3-LBC, and C@P-LBC were implanted into femoral defect areas with bone infection. The femoral tissues were harvested at 2 weeks and 4 weeks post-implantation to evaluate the in vivo antibacterial performance. A schematic diagram of the in vivo antibacterial experiment conducted in SD rats was presented in Fig. 14(a). After sample extraction, ultrasonic treatment was applied to obtain the residual bacteria, and the inhibition rate was subsequently calculated, as illustrated in Fig. 14(b) and (c). The results revealed that the bacterial colonies in both the CaCO3-LBC and C@P-LBC groups gradually decreased with prolonged implantation time. Notably, the inhibition rates against S.aureus of the C@P-LBC group at 2 weeks and 4 weeks post-implantation were 93.97 ± 1.37 % and 98.29 ± 0.77 %, respectively, which were significantly higher than those in the CaCO3-LBC and PMMA groups. These findings indicated that C@P-LBC exhibited superior long-term antibacterial efficacy and the ability to eradicate residual bacteria postoperatively. The H&E staining results of the rat femurs at 2 weeks and 4 weeks post-implantation are shown in Fig. 14(d). As the implantation time extended, no significant changes in inflammatory cells were observed in the PMMA group. In contrast, the CaCO3-LBC and C@P-LBC groups exhibited a reduction in inflammatory cells (purple regions) after 4 weeks, with the C@P-LBC group showing only minimal infiltration of inflammatory cells while maintaining intact tissue architecture. The superior in vivo antibacterial properties can be attributed to the sustained release of LVF from C@P-LBC, which established a stable and highly effective antibacterial microenvironment within the bone defect region infected with bacteria. This microenvironment contributed to reduced pathogen load and alleviated bacteria-induced inflammatory responses, thereby effectively inhibiting the proliferation of residual post-surgical bacteria. Furthermore, the positively charged C@P-LVF within C@P-LBC disrupted the charge balance and structural integrity of negatively charged bacterial membranes through electrostatic attraction, suppressing biofilm formation and preventing inflammation-induced interference with osteoblast function. In the present study, C@P-LBC achieved the dual effects of controlling infection and promoting osteogenic capacity, which created a favourable osteogenic microenvironment and structural foundation for subsequent secondary bone grafting. Therefore, C@P-LBC was designed to be removed after infection control, aligning with the conventional management protocol for antibiotic-loaded PMMA bone cement.

Conclusion

Antibiotic-loaded PMMA bone cement is employed to treat bone infections at load-bearing sites and facilitate human bone healing at the lesion sites. However, there are shortcomings, including the inability to release the antibiotic in response to the local acidic environment of the bone infection, the mismatch between the drug release cycle and the healing cycle of human bone, weak mechanical strength, and inferior osteogenic activity. In this research, C@P-LVF with a rod-shaped core-shell structure was synthesized, and a pH-responsive LVF delivery system with the dual control of protonation/degradation in an acidic environment was constructed. Meanwhile, Ca2+ released by the degradation behaviour promoted the mechanical properties and osteogenic properties of the composites. The results demonstrated that the release cycle of LVF in C@P-LBC reached 126 days, matching the bone healing cycle of clinical treatment with bone infection. The cumulative release ratios of LVF were 34.08 ± 0.68 % and 65.52 ± 1.43 % at pH 7.4 and 5.5, respectively, which were 705.7 % and 1647.2 % higher than that of PMMA bone cement. The release of LVF obeyed the non-Fickian diffusion mechanism. In addition, the cumulative release ratio of Ca2+ was 91.0 ± 4.9 % at pH 5.5. C@P-LBC exhibited satisfactory pH-responsive antibacterial properties against S. aureus and E. coli. Furthermore, osteoblasts revealed enhanced proliferative adhesion properties and exhibited early osteogenic features on C@P-LBC. The results of in vivo experiments indicated that C@P-LBC promoted new bone formation and bone growth in bone defects of the rat femoral, possessing favourable histocompatibility and osteogenic properties. Additionally, C@P-LBC exhibited superior in vivo antibacterial properties. Consequently, C@P-LBC has emerged as a promising biomaterial for clinical treatment in bone infection. In subsequent research, we will synergistically enhance the osteogenesis capacity of PMMA bone cement through calcium ion release and bioactive signal induction, while also conducting quantitative analyses of immunological indicators like local inflammatory cytokine expression and macrophage infiltration to thoroughly explore the material's immunomodulatory mechanisms.
