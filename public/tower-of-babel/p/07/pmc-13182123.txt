Association between trajectory of estimated plasma volume status and ICU mortality in sepsis patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ende Hu, Jiaxing Ke, Qian Chen, Yang Zhang, Ziming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to elucidate the association between estimated plasma volume status (ePVS) trajectory changes and intensive care unit (ICU) mortality.MethodsData were extracted from the MIMIC-IV (version 3.1) database from 2008 to 2022. Patients aged 18 to 80 years who met the Sepsis-3 criteria were included. Patients who were not in their first hospital admission or first ICU stay, had an ICU stay shorter than 7 days or longer than 100 days, or lacked sufficient hemoglobin or hematocrit data to calculate ePVS at the required time points were excluded. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was used to identify distinct ePVS trajectories. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models were used to compare ICU mortality across ePVS trajectory groups. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze the dose-response relationship between Day 1 ePVS and outcomes.ResultsAmong 5208 patients with sepsis, three distinct ePVS trajectories were identified, namely Trajectory 1 (low-ascend), Trajectory 2 (medium-ascend), Trajectory 3 (high-ascend). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed Trajectory 1 having the highest survival probability among the ePVS trajectories. After multivariable adjustment, Trajectory 3 remained significantly associated with a higher risk of ICU mortality compared with Trajectory 1, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.20 (95%CI 1.01–1.44, p = 0.039). RCS analysis showed a significant linear association between Day 1 ePVS and ICU mortality. Subgroup analyses showed generally consistent results, and no significant interactions were detected.ConclusionDistinct ePVS trajectories were associated with ICU mortality in sepsis patients, with the high-ascend trajectory showing the highest mortality risk.Clinical trialNot applicable.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13200-1.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by infection [1]. Recent global estimates indicate that in 2021, sepsis was associated with approximately 166 million cases and 21.4 million all-cause sepsis-related deaths worldwide, accounting for 31.5% of all global deaths, underscoring its substantial clinical and public health burden [2]. Sepsis arises from a dysregulated host response to infection, with immune dysregulation, microcirculatory disorders, and cardiac injury contributing to hemodynamic compromise [3]. Along with early antimicrobial therapy and source control, correction of hemodynamic derangements is a key component of sepsis management [4]. Among the components of hemodynamic management, volume assessment is particularly important for guiding resuscitation and preventing both hypoperfusion and fluid overload.

Estimated plasma volume status (ePVS), calculated from hematocrit and hemoglobin [5, 6], is a simple and straightforward surrogate marker for total vascular volume. In sepsis, accurate assessment of volume status is essential to avoid organ hypoperfusion caused by insufficient volume as well as complications such as pulmonary edema and acute kidney injury resulting from volume excess [4]. ePVS provides useful information for volume assessment and may therefore serve as a practical tool for evaluating volume status.

ePVS holds clinical significance in the diagnosis, treatment, and prognostic evaluation of sepsis [7–9]. Previous studies have mainly focused on the association between admission or single-time-point ePVS and clinical outcomes in patients with infection, sepsis, or septic shock, whereas evidence regarding the prognostic significance of dynamic ePVS changes remains limited [8, 10]. Because sepsis is a dynamic syndrome, assessment at a single time point may not fully capture changes in volume status over time. The relationship between dynamic changes in ePVS and the prognosis of patients with sepsis remains unclear. This study aims to explore the association between ePVS trajectory and the prognosis of sepsis patients, and to clarify the clinical value of dynamic ePVS trajectory monitoring.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), a large tertiary academic medical center in Boston, Massachusetts, USA. The study period spanned from 2008 to 2022. Data were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1), which contains comprehensive de-identified clinical data from patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).

MIMIC-IV has obtained ethical approval from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (Cambridge, MA, USA, No. 0403000206) and BIDMC (Boston, MA, USA, 2001-P-001699/14), with appropriate informed consent for data use. The author, Ende Hu, has completed the accredited course, had database access, and oversaw data extraction (Record ID: 11552287). All data used in this study were publicly available and de-identified. Therefore, no additional institutional review board approval or informed consent was required [11].

Study population

The study population comprised patients aged 18 to 80 years who were diagnosed with sepsis according to the Sepsis-3 definition [12]. Patients who were not in their first hospital admission or first intensive care unit (ICU) stay were excluded. Patients with an ICU stay shorter than 7 days were excluded because estimated plasma volume status (ePVS) trajectories were modeled using measurements obtained during the first 7 days after ICU admission. Patients with an ICU stay longer than 100 days were also excluded to avoid including patients with prolonged chronic critical illness whose hospital course may be less reflective of the acute-phase physiology captured by the first 7 days of ePVS measurements. Patients without sufficient hemoglobin or hematocrit data to calculate ePVS at any required time point were excluded. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Evaluation of ePVS and group-based trajectory modeling

The Duarte formula incorporating hematocrit and hemoglobin was utilized as follows [9]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ePVS\:(dL/g)\:=\:\frac{100\:-\:hematocrit\:\left(\%\right)}{hemoglobin\:(g/dL)}$$\end{document}

ePVS was calculated using laboratory measurements obtained within three predefined time windows after ICU admission, corresponding to the Day 1, Day 3, and Day 7 assessments, respectively, which were defined as 0 to 24 h, 24 to 72 h, and 72 to 168 h after ICU admission. For patients with multiple measurements within the same time window, only the first measurement was used.

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) is a semiparametric method for longitudinal data analysis that can identify distinct ePVS trajectory groups based on measurements obtained on Days 1, 3, and 7 after ICU admission. The Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), average posterior probability (AvePP), and odds of correct classification (OCC) were used to select the optimal trajectory model. GBTM was performed using the gbmt package in R.

Follow-up and study endpoint

Follow-up began on day 8 following ICU admission. The primary endpoint was ICU mortality, defined as all-cause death during the index ICU stay. Follow-up continued from time zero (day 8 after ICU admission) until ICU death or ICU discharge.

Study covariates

Baseline patient characteristics, including demographic data, vital signs, comorbidities, critical care scores, and therapeutic interventions, were also extracted for analysis. The definitions of these covariates were detailed in the Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

Outliers were identified using boxplots. Specifically, the first quartile (Q1, 25th percentile) and third quartile (Q3, 75th percentile) were calculated, and the interquartile range (IQR) was defined as Q3 minus Q1. The thresholds for outlier detection were set at Q1–1.5 × IQR and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR. Values beyond these thresholds were considered outliers and recoded as missing values (NA). Variables or samples with more than 15% missing values were excluded from subsequent analyses. Missing data for each variable were then imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) method with k = 5. The proportion of missing values for each included variable is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Patients with sepsis were stratified into three groups based on distinct trajectories. For continuous variables, normally distributed data were presented as mean ± SD and non-normally distributed data as median (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as n (%). Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Survival differences across trajectory groups were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used to select covariates for adjustment. These covariates were then included in the multivariable Cox regression model to evaluate the association between ePVS trajectory groups and the outcome. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to explore nonlinear relationships between Day 1 ePVS and ICU mortality risk in sepsis patients. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess heterogeneity in the association between ePVS trajectory and ICU mortality across different subgroups. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The final study cohort consisted of 5208 patients with sepsis selected from the MIMIC-IV database. No significant differences were detected in the overall dataset between pre- and post-KNN imputation (all p > 0.05, Supplementary Table 2).

The optimal ePVS trajectories were identified through evaluation of posterior probabilities and goodness-of-fit metrics for GBTM over a range of group numbers and trajectory shapes (Table 1). A three-group trajectory model was ultimately selected based on clinical relevance and interpretability. Patients were stratified into three trajectory subgroups, including Trajectory 1 (low-ascend), Trajectory 2 (medium-ascend) and Trajectory 3 (high-ascend) (Fig. 2).

Survival analysis

During ICU hospitalization, 922 patients experienced ICU death. The median follow-up duration after time zero (day 8 after ICU admission) was 5.02 (1.94–10.87) days. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed differences in ICU mortality among the three ePVS trajectory groups, with Trajectory 3 showing the lowest survival probability (p < 0.001, Fig. 3). LASSO regression was used to select covariates for multivariable adjustment, with lambda.min used as the selection criterion (Supplementary Fig. 1). In Model 3, adjusted for covariates selected by LASSO regression, the HR was 1.00 (95%CI 0.84–1.19, p = 0.963) for Trajectory 2 and 1.20 (95%CI 1.01–1.44, p = 0.039) for Trajectory 3 (Table 3). With Trajectory 3 as the reference group, the HR was 0.83 (95%CI 0.71–0.96, p = 0.014) for Trajectory 2 and 0.83 (95%CI 0.70–0.99, p = 0.039) for Trajectory 1 (Table 4).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis

RCS analysis was used to visually depict the dose-response relationship between ePVS on Day 1 and the risk of ICU mortality after adjustment for potential confounding factors. As ePVS increased, the risk of ICU mortality showed a continuous upward trend. The p value for nonlinearity was 0.164, whereas the p value for the overall association was less than 0.001, indicating a statistically significant linear association between ePVS and the risk of ICU mortality (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis

In subgroup analyses, the association between ePVS trajectories and ICU mortality remained generally consistent across clinical and demographic subgroups. For Trajectory 3 compared with Trajectory 1, all HRs were greater than 1. No significant interactions were found between ePVS trajectories and any subgroup variable, including age, gender, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, and vasopressin use (all p for interaction > 0.05, Fig. 5).

Discussion

Based on the MIMIC-IV database, this study used GBTM to characterize dynamic changes in ePVS during the first 7 days after ICU admission in patients with sepsis and identified three distinct trajectories, namely low-ascend, medium-ascend, and high-ascend. After adjustment for confounding factors, Trajectory 3 (high-ascend) was associated with a higher risk of ICU mortality than Trajectory 1 (low-ascend). These findings suggest that dynamic patterns of ePVS, particularly a persistently high and increasing trajectory, may help identify patients with sepsis at higher risk of ICU mortality.

The high-ascend trajectory was associated with a higher risk of ICU mortality, which may reflect persistent or progressively worsening hemodynamic disturbances during the early course of sepsis. Hemodynamic management in sepsis includes fluid resuscitation, assessment of fluid responsiveness, evaluation of cardiac function and inotropic support, monitoring of peripheral perfusion, and use of vasopressin [4]. ePVS, calculated from hemoglobin and hematocrit, is not a direct measure of plasma volume, but it may reflect volume status and the degree of hemodilution [13]. Previous studies have shown that ePVS is correlated with the volume of intravenous fluid resuscitation and with poor prognosis in patients with septic shock [7, 8]. In sepsis, systemic inflammation can increase capillary permeability and promote the shift of plasma components into the interstitial space, resulting in a complex state characterized by insufficient effective circulating volume, tissue edema, and microcirculatory dysfunction [14, 15].

At the same time, elevated ePVS often indicates volume overload and has been associated with hemodynamic abnormalities related to venous congestion and impaired cardiac function, including increased right atrial pressure, pulmonary arterial wedge pressure, and left ventricular filling pressure [16–19]. Therefore, a persistently high and increasing ePVS trajectory may reflect sustained volume overload, venous congestion, and impaired cardiac function during early sepsis.

An important implication of this study is that dynamic trajectory analysis of ePVS may better reflect the early clinical course of sepsis in the ICU than a single time point measurement. Sepsis is a highly dynamic syndrome in which volume status, microcirculatory function, and organ perfusion may fluctuate with the intensity of inflammation, changes in organ function, and therapeutic interventions [4]. A single ePVS measurement reflects only one moment in time and may not fully capture these continuous changes during early ICU stay. In contrast, the trajectory model based on measurements obtained on Days 1, 3, and 7 integrates information across multiple time points and identifies distinct patterns with clinical heterogeneity, thereby providing a more accurate representation of disease progression. In the present study, not all patients with elevated ePVS showed the same level of risk, and only the high-ascend trajectory remained significantly associated with a higher risk of ICU mortality after multivariable adjustment. These findings suggest that a persistently high and increasing ePVS pattern may have greater prognostic value than a single measurement and that dynamic monitoring of ePVS may provide supplementary information for risk stratification in patients with sepsis [14, 20, 21].

RCS analysis and subgroup analyses further supported the robustness of these findings and their potential clinical relevance. RCS analysis showed a significant linear association between ePVS and the risk of ICU mortality, with mortality risk increasing progressively as ePVS increased and no clear evidence of nonlinearity. This finding complements the trajectory-based analysis from the perspective of a continuous variable and further suggests that ePVS may reflect a gradual increase in risk. Meanwhile, subgroup analyses showed that the direction of the increased risk associated with the high-ascend trajectory was generally consistent across subgroups defined by age, gender, comorbidities, and vasopressin use, and no significant interactions were detected. These results suggest that the observed association was relatively robust and consistent across different clinical settings.

Dynamic monitoring of ePVS may provide supplementary information for risk stratification and hemodynamic assessment in patients with sepsis. In patients with a high-ascend trajectory, closer evaluation of volume status, cardiac function, and tissue perfusion may help inform individualized management decisions. However, in critically ill patients, elevated ePVS may also be influenced by hemodilution, anemia, bleeding, transfusion, and fluid resuscitation [22–24]. Therefore, the prognostic value of ePVS trajectories should be interpreted within the broader clinical context and not as a direct measure of absolute plasma volume.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective observational analysis based on a single-center database. Therefore, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Second, because the trajectory model was constructed using ePVS data from the first 7 days after ICU admission, only patients with a sufficient observation window and corresponding laboratory data were included. This may have excluded some patients who died early or had a shorter ICU stay, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Finally, because the data were derived from the MIMIC-IV database, some unmeasured or undefined confounding factors may not have been included in the model. In addition, the study endpoint was limited to ICU mortality, and the relationship between dynamic changes in ePVS and long-term prognosis could not be determined. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to further validate these findings and to assess whether risk stratification strategies based on dynamic ePVS monitoring can improve clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Distinct ePVS trajectories were associated with ICU mortality in patients with sepsis. Compared with the low-ascend trajectory, the high-ascend trajectory was linked to a higher risk of ICU mortality, indicating that longitudinal changes in ePVS may provide meaningful prognostic information.
