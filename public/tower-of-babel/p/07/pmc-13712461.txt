Psychological Impact of Fetal Echocardiography Referral on Pregnant Women With a Normal Routine Morphologic Ultrasound
Authors: Camille Dufour, Louis-Olivier Roy, Valérie Taillefer, Maxime Duclos, Evelyne Raiche, Tiscar Cavallé-Garrido, Gitanjali Mansukhani, Richard Brown, Varsha Thakur, Alexandra Ferland-Dorval, Rami Al-Amir-Dach, Melinda Hall, Laurence Vaujois, Frédéric Dallaire, Canadian Congenital and Pediatric Cardiology Research Network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFetal echocardiography (FE) is widely recommended in pregnancies at increased risk of congenital heart defects. Although FE is a safe diagnostic tool, its psychological impact on expectant pregnant women remains poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a prospective observational study in 8 Canadian centers. Women aged ≥18 years with a normal second-trimester ultrasound between 18 and 26 weeks’ gestation referred for an FE were compared with women who were not. Anxiety was assessed using the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory. The primary outcome was the difference between state and trait anxiety (net situational anxiety) after morphologic ultrasound but before FE (T0). Secondary outcomes included the proportion of participants with significant anxiety (State-Trait Anxiety Inventory ≥40) and anxiety trajectories after FE among referred participants.ResultsEighty-four women were included, of whom 38 were referred for FE. At T0, women referred for FE showed slightly higher median net situational anxiety compared with controls. In multivariable analyses, FE referral was associated with a modest increase although estimates were imprecise. A higher proportion of referred women reached a state anxiety score ≥40, though this association was weak after adjustment. Among the 14 women who completed post-FE questionnaires, anxiety trajectories were heterogeneous, with both increases and decreases observed after FE.ConclusionsReferral for FE after a normal second-trimester ultrasound is associated with a small and variable increase in maternal anxiety. These findings suggest that FE referral may not be psychologically neutral and highlight the importance of considering maternal psychological responses as referral criteria broaden.

Fetal echocardiography (FE) plays a crucial role in diagnosing congenital heart disease (CHD) during prenatal care. The guidelines published by the American Heart Association in 2014, with an update in 2023, recommended the referral of pregnant women at elevated risk of CHD for an FE, including in the setting of a normal second-trimester ultrasound.1,2 The threshold for referral for FE has decreased in the past few decades with a consequent increase in the number of referrals for FE.

As in any other screening strategies, the beneficial and detrimental effects of a program on otherwise healthy subjects need to be evaluated. Although FE is well established as a safe diagnostic tool, with no detrimental physical effects for pregnant women or their fetus, its psychological impact on the women has been much less explored. Previous studies have shown that the anticipation of additional testing or abnormal findings during prenatal screening was associated with elevated anxiety levels.3 For instance, women referred for FE reported significantly higher anxiety levels before their FE, compared with women not presenting for FE, regardless of the indication for FE.4, 5, 6, 7 More broadly, anxiety after uncertain or indeterminate prenatal ultrasound findings has also been documented, underscoring that the referral process in itself may contribute to maternal anxiety.8

Few recent studies have specifically examined the psychological impact of FE referral in pregnancies without major cardiac risk factors, particularly within the Canadian health care context where referral pathways and adherence to guidelines vary across provinces. Given the widely recognized adverse effects of maternal anxiety on both expectant women and fetus,9, 10, 11 it is crucial to understand the psychological impact of FE referrals. Such impact must be taken into account when formulating Canadian recommendations for the trajectory of care for pregnant women at an elevated risk of CHD.

We therefore aimed to assess the psychological impact of referral for FE by comparing anxiety levels in women referred for maternal or fetal risk factors with those in women not referred for FE, in the setting of a completely normal morphologic ultrasound. We hypothesized that being referred for specialized cardiac evaluation would be associated with higher maternal anxiety levels, despite a reassuring second-trimester ultrasound showing no cardiac anomaly.

Methods

Study design and population

A Canadian multicenter, prospective observational cross-sectional study was conducted in 8 centers in Québec and Ontario from July 2021 to February 2024. Pregnant women ≥18 years old at 18 to 26 weeks of gestation, inclusively, with a normal second-trimester ultrasound and who were referred for an FE based on maternal or fetal risk factors were compared with a cohort of pregnant women with a normal second-trimester ultrasound not referred for an FE. We excluded pregnancies with confirmed or suspected abnormal karyotype, with maternal or obstetrical complications requiring close medical follow-up, women for whom an FE had already been performed for the current pregnancy, and women unable to read French or English.

Enrollment

Potential participants were identified by screening of the appointment lists or by flagging those with a normal second-trimester ultrasound in participating centers. Potentially eligible subjects were either sent an email or given a short information sheet with access to an online questionnaire on the REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) platform. The questionnaire included an eligibility screening checklist and an electronic informed consent form for those eligible. After consenting, participants were automatically directed to the study questionnaires. This first set of questionnaires (T0) was completed shortly after the second-trimester ultrasound. For participants in the FE group, this T0 first set of questionnaires had to be completed before their FE, and a second set of questionnaires (T1) was completed up to 1 week after their FE.

Data collection

Anxiety was assessed using the Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory Form (STAI), a scale consisting of 40 items: 20 designed to assess state anxiety, and 20 aimed at evaluating trait anxiety.12 State anxiety is a transient emotional response to a specific situation, whereas trait anxiety reflects a more stable personality characteristic that influences how an individual is likely to experience state anxiety in stressful circumstances.13 Scores for both the state and trait measures range from 20 to 80, where higher scores indicate the greater anxiety level.12 The STAI has been well validated in the general population, and its use in a pregnant population is well recognized.3,4,6,7,14 According to the design of the STAI, the state and trait components were completed simultaneously, as this approach has been shown to provide good discriminant validity and reliably for distinguishing transient from trait anxiety.15

In addition, the online questionnaires also collected maternal sociodemographic and clinical information, including age, gestational age at completion of the questionnaire, number of pregnancies, household income, care region, previous child CHD-related mortality, ethnicity, history of termination of pregnancy, and prior mental health care.

Outcomes of interest

Our primary outcome was the difference between trait and state anxiety scores at the time of the initial questionnaire (T0), comparing participants referred or not for FE. This measure, referred to throughout this article as “net situational anxiety,” reflects how anxious participants felt at that moment compared with their usual level of anxiety. Our secondary outcome was the proportion of participants with a state anxiety score above 40 at T0, a threshold recognized as indicative of clinically significant anxiety symptoms.16 Finally, among participants referred for FE who completed the questionnaire after their FE (T1), we examined within-subject changes in anxiety between T0 and T1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data management were performed with R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and proportions. Multivariable linear regression models were used to assess the association between FE referral and net situational anxiety scores (the difference between state and trait anxiety) by referral status, as well as to evaluate within-participant changes in anxiety before and after FE. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the association between FE referral and the odds of having a state anxiety score ≥40 (secondary objective). The multivariable regression models to assess outcomes were built by testing how each potentially confounding variables (age, gestational age at referral, number of pregnancies, household income, care region, child CHD-related mortality, ethnicity, history of termination of pregnancy, and prior mental health care) individually influenced the effect size of the outcome studied. When the inclusion of a variable changed the effect size of the outcome by >10%, the confounding variable was included in the model.17 In accordance with the American Statistical Association recommendations and best practices in reporting statistical test results, we reported statistical tests as 95% confidence intervals (CIs) where appropriate, and we did not use a P-value cutoff as a marker of statistical significance.18

Compared with participants referred to FE, those not referred to FE had a similar median age (31.6 years [27.7-36.0 years] vs 31.0 years [28.4-34.0 years]), proportion of pregnancy termination history due to fetal congenital anomaly (4 of 38 [10.5%] vs 5 of 46 [10.9%]), and level of education. However, those referred to FE were slightly more likely to be at their first pregnancy (19 of 38 [50.0%] vs 19 of 46 [41.3%]) or third or more pregnancy (13 of 38 [34.2%] vs 9 of 46 [19.6%]), and less likely to be at their second pregnancy (6 of 38 [15.8%] vs 18 of 46 [39.1%]). Those referred to FE were more likely to be at weeks 22-23 of their pregnancy (15 of 38 [39.5%]), whereas those not referred were more commonly at a gestational age of 20 weeks or less (19 of 46 [41.3%]). Those referred to FE were less likely to be Caucasians (26 of 38 [68.4%]) vs 40 of 46 [87.0%]) and less likely to have a mental health treatment history (6 of 38 [15.8%] vs 11 of 46 [22.9%]). Those referred to FE were slightly more likely to have a history of child death due to cardiac condition (2 of 38 [5.3%] vs 0 of 46 [0%]). Those referred to FE were less likely to have a household income >CAD$100k (15 of 38 [39.5%] vs 32 of 46 [69.6%]).

Significant anxiety (STAI ≥ 40)

The proportion of participants reaching a state anxiety score ≥40 was twice as high in the FE group (12 of 38 [31.6%] vs 7 of 46 [15.2%]), but this association was much weaker in multivariate logistic regression, with an odds ratio 1.1 (95% CI: 0.5-10.4) after adjustment for gestational age at referral, number of pregnancies, household income, care region, child CHD-related mortality, ethnicity, and prior mental health care.

Change in anxiety before and after fetal echocardiography

Few participants completed the questionnaire after FE (n = 14), and results must be interpreted with caution. Among those completing the questionnaire, anxiety trajectories before and after FE were heterogeneous. While some participants with high scores showed marked reduction of their T0 state score after the FE, others experienced an increase. Overall, univariate regression analyses suggested a slight mean decrease in anxiety after FE, whereas median scores indicated a modest increase (T0 net situational anxiety –2.5 [IQR: –5.8 to 5.2] to –1.5 [–3.0 to 1.5]). Discussion

Our analysis showed a slight increase in anxiety level associated with FE referral when comparing net situational anxiety, although this association was less clear when looking at the state anxiety level or the post-FE change in anxiety level. These findings suggest a modest situational anxiety response rather than a pronounced or uniform psychological effect. Such increases have already been reported in different studies,19,20 such as the one by Rosenberg et al,4 using the same STAI questionnaire. The authors reported state and trait scores separately showing a larger difference in state anxiety for participants referred for FE compared with those not referred (42.1 vs 32.8, respectively), with comparable trait anxiety (34.7 vs 35.4, respectively). The authors highlighted the importance for the patient to understand the reason for referral to FE, because a vague justification for the referral could otherwise increase the anxiety.

The use of the net situational anxiety (state-trait difference) in our analyses was intended to isolate situational anxiety related to FE referral while controlling for individual predisposition to anxiety, as this approach provides a more conservative and clinically interpretable estimate of referral-related anxiety. Individual variation in anxiety responses may further be explained by additional factors not measured, such as prior health care experiences, the empathy of the treating doctor, or further management strategies.1 The participants’ understanding of the reason for referral may also contribute to variability in anxiety levels, as perceived risk may not align with actual risk if not well communicated by the attending clinician.

Importantly, anxiety trajectories before and after FE in participants referred for FE were highly heterogeneous. While some participants experienced a reduction in anxiety after FE, others showed increased anxiety levels, underscoring that FE may not always be uniformly reassuring, even if no abnormality was found. The variability observed in maternal anxiety before and after FE is not unique to our study. A Californian study21 similarly reported both increases and decreases in anxiety after FE, showing that although normal results often bring relief and abnormal results tend to heighten anxiety, this pattern does not apply uniformly to all patients.

We acknowledge that part of the difference in STAI in participants with FE could be explained by the anxiety caused by knowing that a risk factor is present. Moreover, the reassurance typically provided by a normal second-trimester ultrasound may be partially offset by the need for specialized cardiac evaluation. Overall, the roles played by the various factors that shape the participant’s response are difficult to untangle.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size limited the precision of effect estimates and the ability to draw conclusions regarding anxiety trajectories across different characteristics in our multivariate analyses. Although validated instruments were used, our data did not allow identification of the underlying reasons for anxiety, which may relate to family environment, social support, or clinical and hospital context. The small number of women with post-FE data precluded exploration of predictors of heterogeneous anxiety responses, such as individual psychosocial factors, prior experiences, or aspects of the referral process.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings do not challenge the diagnostic value of FE. They rather highlight the importance of integrating psychological considerations into prenatal care pathways. As referral criteria for FE are broad, refining referral practices by incorporating individualized risk factors or risk stratification, alongside clear communication and supportive care, may help ensure that FE is offered to women most likely to benefit, while mitigating unintended psychological burden.
