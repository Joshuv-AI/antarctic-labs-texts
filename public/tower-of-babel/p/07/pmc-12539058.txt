Intention to switch from short-acting to long-acting reversible contraceptives among refugee women in Ethiopia: application of the theory of planned behavior
Authors: Andamlak Gizaw Alamdo, Agnes M. Kotoh, Emefa Judith Modey, Juliana Yartey Enos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe validity of the theory of planned behavior (TPB) in predicting health behavior intention has proven its applicability to various health behaviors, such as condom uptake, HIV prevention, and contraception use in resource-limited settings. However, the effectiveness of the TPB framework in understanding refugee women’s intention to switch from short-acting contraceptives (SAC) to long-acting and reversible contraceptives (LARC) remains unclear. This study examined how the TPB predicts refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC.MethodsThis study is a nested component of a larger retrospective cohort examining contraceptive use dynamics among women in Ethiopian refugee camps. Initially, 1,290 family planning records from selected health facilities in Ethiopian refugee camps were reviewed. These records included 551 women who began using contraceptives between September 2022 and February 2023 (387 started SAC and 164 started LARC). Among SAC users, 238 women who were current users and familiar with LARC methods (implants and/or IUDs) participated in this study. Data collection involved computer-assisted face-to-face interviews conducted using the Open Data Kit (ODK) toolkit. Four constructs, based on the TPB variables, measured using a 7-point Likert scale, were analyzed to explore the relationship between attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control as predictors of the intention to switch from SAC to LARC. The Structural Equation Model (SEM) with the Stata SEM Builder and maximum likelihood estimation was used to evaluate how well the data fit the TPB model and identify the key predictors of the intention to switch from SAC to LARC.ResultsAll specified relations between the three constructs and the intention to switch from SAC to LARC in TPB were exhibited as statistically significant. The TPB model accounted for 66.3% of the variation in the intention to switch from SAC to LARC. A positive attitude toward LARC use (β = 0.16; P = .014), strong subjective norms (β = 0.46; P = .001), and higher perceived behavioral controls (β = 0.17; P = .001) had a positive influence on refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC.ConclusionThe TPB model demonstrated a good fit for examining refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC, with the subjective norm being the strongest predictor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40834-025-00403-1.

Background

Globally, there are now an estimated 91 million individuals who have been under political unrest, war, and other forms of violence that caused people to flee their homes [1]. Out of this number, there have been 20.7 million refugees and nearly 48 million internally displaced persons [2, 3]. Pregnancy-related complications and maternal mortality are highest among women in humanitarian settings due to interruptions or restricted access to maternal health services [4, 5]. Women in humanitarian settings critically need access to contraception for multiple reasons [6, 7]. Contraception can enable them to plan and space their pregnancies so as not to risk delivering a baby with inadequate medical care, while also allowing them time to recover from previous births or trauma they have experienced [8]. In addition, providing contraception can also improve the health of women by reducing pregnancy-related complications from unwanted pregnancies and unsafe abortions and helping to prevent sexually transmitted infections. Finally, access to safe and reliable contraception can empower displaced women to make decisions about their bodies that are often denied by society [5, 9].

Demand for contraception is high in humanitarian settings [10]. A study in six conflict-affected zones of Uganda, Sudan, and the demographic Republic of Congo (DRC) reported that 43–71% of women had a demand to postpone pregnancy or even stop altogether [2]. Another similar study in Lebanon found that 52.1% of women reported unintended pregnancies, and nearly 75% of them wanted to postpone future pregnancies [11]. A study conducted among the refugee population in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) found that although there is a need for contraceptive methods, including LARC, women in humanitarian settings only use them when they are of reasonably good quality and readily available [12]. Despite the high rate of unintended pregnancies and unmet need for contraception in refugee settings, the contraception prevalence rate is very low [13, 14]. Yet, a study conducted in Rwanda’s Nyabiheke camp found that the main reason for low contraceptive uptake is not the availability of FP services, but rather the barriers to the utilization of contraceptives, such as fear of side effects, ignorance about FP, religious and cultural beliefs, and lack of motivation [15]. Compared to LARC, a relatively higher rate of SAC utilization was reported in a survey of Somali refugee women in Kampala, Uganda. According to this study, 29% of women utilized modern contraception, with contraceptive oral pills making up 51% of those, condoms 15%, and injectables 15% [16]. On the other hand, a survey of refugees revealed an increasing interest in LARC, particularly implants [17]. Considering effectiveness, duration of use, reversibility, and the relevance of a prompt and expected return of fertility following method discontinuation, LARCs are potentially well-suited for women in refugee settings [18, 19]. Although both SAC and LARC are provided free of cost, Ethiopian refugee women still prefer SAC [18]. Studies identified factors such as negative attitudes toward the methods, perception of partners’ support of LARC use, educational level, and knowledge of LARC as the reasons not to utilize LARC [13, 20].

Donovan and Jessor’s theory of planned behavior can be applied to the decision to use contraceptives [21]. The theory predicts a person’s determination to act in a certain way at a particular time and hypothesizes that an individual’s behavioral intentions are a function of three determinants: attitude, subjective norms (SN), and perceived behavioral control (PBC) [22, 23]. In contraceptive method choice, a woman’s attitude could be informed by her positive or negative experiences with a particular method [24]. Participants might relate their challenge to the method linked to undesirable side effects [25]. Most women depend on trusted members of the family, friends, male partners, and religious leaders to make contraceptive decisions and intend to use LARC [26]. A woman’s confidence in her ability to obtain the contraceptive option she chooses, knowing where to get the method, and health care providers’ support are crucial in choosing a method [27–29].

The validity of the TPB in predicting health behavior intention has been demonstrated through its application to various health behaviors, such as condom uptake [30, 31], HIV prevention [32], and contraception use in resource-constrained countries [33, 34]. The applicability of the theory is supported by the results of a study conducted in rural Uganda evaluating the TPB’s ability to predict contraceptive use behaviors among postpartum women [34]. According to a US study, the TPB was found useful in determining the intention of COC users to obtain LARC. This study revealed that LARC intention was influenced by attitude and PBC, while SN had no significant effect [19]. Conversely, two African research findings indicated that SN was a better predictor of condom usage intention than attitude [35, 36].

The TPB model is useful for examining health behavior intentions in diverse and low-income countries [32–34]. However, to what extent these findings apply to refugee populations, where the factors that shape contraceptive decision-making are different from non-refugee settings, remains uncertain. Understanding the TPB’s validity in predicting refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC is crucial to improve equity in health outcomes and address the reproductive health needs of women in humanitarian settings. Furthermore, the results can be significant for international organizations like UNHCR, UNFPA in developing strategies to promote family planning in these environments. Thus, this study aimed to explore the extent to which the TPB predicts refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC. The study was based on the following assumptions: The TPB might be useful in understanding the refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC [21, 37]. A woman is more likely to plan to use LARC if she has a positive attitude towards the method, feels that her partner, family, or close friends encourage her to use LARC, and believes she can use LARC [21, 38, 39].

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in selected refugee camps in Ethiopia’s Gambella and Somali regions. Ethiopia offers asylum to refugees coming from 19 different nations. Fifty operational partners are involved in Ethiopia’s response to the refugee crisis, including the Ethiopian government’s Refugees and Returnees Service (RRS), UNHCR (the UN Refugee Agency), and international and national NGOs. Ethiopia is the third-largest Africa’s refugee-hosting nation, with 1,043,602 refugees and asylum seekers officially registered in the country. Of these, men account for 48%, women 52%, and women and children 48%. The majority of the refugees in this study are from South Sudan and Somalia, both affected by prolonged conflict and instability that has resulted in widespread displacement. Many have moved into organised camps in Ethiopia in search of protection [40]. Gambella and Somali regions were selected for the study due to the country’s large number of refugee populations. The study was carried out from March 1 to May 31, 2024.

Study design and population

This study was conducted as a nested study within a larger retrospective cohort on contraceptive use among women in Ethiopian refugee camps. The main study reviewed 1,290 family planning records from women who started contraceptives between September 2022 and February 2024. Of these, 551 women began using methods between September 2022 and February 2023 and were followed for at least one year. Among the SAC users identified in the main cohort, 238 women who continued using their contraceptive method at follow-up and were aware of LARC options were invited to participate. Women with incomplete follow-up records and women with hearing impairments were not included in the study.

Sample size and sampling procedure

First, two regions with the highest refugee populations in Ethiopia, Somali and Gambella, were selected. Then, three refugee camps from the Somali region and two from the Gambella region were chosen through a lottery method. However, we excluded one refugee camp from Gambella due to safety and security concerns when traveling to the study participants’ residential areas. Each refugee camp has one health center that provides free healthcare services, including family planning. A total of 1,290 women who had initiated contraceptive methods (all types) were identified from the registers of four healthcare facilities. These included 551 women who started using contraceptives between September 2022 and February 2023 (387 started SAC and 164 started LARC). Of these, 387 women accepted SAC, and 238 were current users of OCP, injectable, calendar method, emergency contraceptives, and LAM, and were aware that Implants and/or IUDs were approached and interviewed to examine the application of the TPB in predicting refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC. Although the sample size for SEM varies depending on factors like fit index, the strength of path parameters, and missing data, researchers recommend at least 100 to 200 samples per free parameter, specifically, five observations per parameter. Therefore, the number of eligible women (238) was comparable to the sample size required for SEM. The participants were located in collaboration with health workers and community health agents, and interviews were conducted at the women’s homes.

Data collection procedures

First, FP registration books and follow-up registration forms in the health facilities were reviewed. All eligible study participants were identified from the registration books. A total of ten data collectors holding a bachelor’s degree in midwifery or public health and 4 supervisors who completed a master’s degree in public health were recruited and trained by experts before they were deployed for data collection. The study utilized computer-assisted face-to-face interviews using the ODK toolkit for data collection. Considering the country’s current security issues, the data collection was undertaken closely following the local security situation.

Data collection instruments

Data collection tools were adapted from Ajzen’s seven-point, bipolar Likert scales [21] and used in a similar study to measure the TPB variables [19]. Attitudes toward LARC, subjective norm, and perceived behavioral control were each assessed using a 6-item scale. Moreover, the intention to switch from SAC to LARC was measured using an 11-item scale. The tool was formulated in English and translated into the local languages (Somali and Nuer). Then, to ensure consistency and correctness, the language experts back-translated the tool into English. Reproductive health, health promotion, and behavioral science experts reviewed the relevance of each item in the instrument and evaluated its content and face validity. Moreover, the tool was pretested on 5% of the total sample size with married women using contraceptives at Jigjiga health center in the Somali region and Gambella primary hospital to assess language clarity, tool compatibility, and response appropriateness. Finally, the tool was refined based on experts’ suggestions and the pre-test results.

Study variables and measurement

We examined the TPB constructs: attitude toward LARC (measured refugee women’s belief about using LARC and perceived benefits of adopting the behavior), SN (measured perceived social pressure to use or not use LARC), PBC (assessed refugee women’s perceived ease or difficulty of using LARC), and intention to switch from SAC to LARC. Based on TPB variables, four constructs with a 7-point Likert scale were used to explore the link between attitude, SN, and PBC as the predictors of intention to switch from SAC to LARC. All item scores ranged from strongly disagree/extremely harmful to, strongly agree/extremely useful.

Data management and analysis

Data were cleaned through frequency tables, cross-tabulations, and raw data checkups for outliers and missing data. All data were analyzed using Stata16 (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA). For each question, summary statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviations were used to explain the women’s different socio-demographics, birth and reproductive history, and contraception use information. The SEM using Stata SEM Builder with maximum likelihood estimation was utilized to check the data’s fit to the TPB model’s structure and identify the predicting variables of the intention to switch from SAC to LARC. The overall model fitness was examined by using a chi-square ratio (< 3), “comparative fit index” (CFI >0.9), “root mean square error of approximation” (RMSEA < 0.08), and “standardized root mean residual” (SRMR < 0.08). The model fit indices indicated a chi-square ratio (1.74), CFI = 0.910, RMSEA = 0.056, 90% CI: (0.049–0.063), and SRMR = 0.057. The results indicated an acceptable fit for the data [41]. We performed a sensitivity analysis by re-estimating the SEM model after successively eliminating one indicator from each latent construct. The findings demonstrated that structural path coefficients and model fit indices were constant across various models, suggesting that no single factor had an influence on the overall results.

Both unstandardized and standardized beta coefficients were utilized to estimate the path coefficients and assess the relative contribution of exogenous variables to endogenous variables. The squared multiple correlation (R²) was used to indicate the proportion of variance in the endogenous variable (intention to switch from SAC to LARC) explained by its predictors. To evaluate the internal consistency of the constructs, Cronbach’s α reliability coefficients were calculated, with values of 0.7 or higher considered acceptable. Therefore, reported reliability coefficients included 0.78 for attitude, 0.89 for SN, 0.77 for PBC, and 0.91 for intention (Table 1).

Results

Socio-demographic and background characteristics

A total of 238 married women in selected refugee camps in Ethiopia participated in the study. Half of the participants were displaced from South Sudan (122, 51.26%), and 116 (48.74%) identified as Muslim. The respondents lived in refugee camps for a median of 9 years (IQR 7). Additionally, the median age of the respondents was 29 (IQR 9), with ages ranging from 18 to 48 years. Overall, two-thirds of the participants (159, 66.81%) had attended school at some point, with 17% having no formal education; over half, 129 (54.20%), were housewives. More than 40% (102, 42.86%) and over a third (86, 36.13%) of participants fell into the poorer and poorest wealth quintiles, respectively. Furthermore, 43% (101, 42.44%) of the participants’ partners had no formal education or never attended school (Table 2).

Reproductive health and contraception use information

Overall, more than half of the participants (136, 57.14%) had 2 to 4 living children, while 15% (34, 14.29%) had only 0 to 1 living child. Among the respondents who had ever been pregnant, nearly 50% (106, 47.53%) reported that their recent pregnancy was untimely/unplanned. In addition, a significant proportion of participants (207, 86.97%) desired to postpone having an additional child for at least 2 years or decided not to have any more children. Regarding the type of SAC currently utilized, three-fourths (179, 75.21%) were using injectables, while a quarter (55, 23.11%) were oral contraceptive pill (OCP) users. Nearly all participants (233, 97.90%) reported having heard of implants, and around two-thirds (153, 64.29%) reported hearing of IUDs, respectively. Moreover, the participants used short-term contraception with a median duration of 12 months (IQR 5), and only 50% (117, 49.16%) of partners accepted their wives’ use of contraception. Regarding the decision to start contraception, almost three out of four participants (169, 71.01%) made the decision independently, while only 17% (40, 16.81%) made the decision jointly. Nearly all participants (227, 95.38%) intend to use contraception in the future (Table 3).

Structural equation model (SEM)

As indicated in Fig. 1 below, all specified relations between the three constructs and the intention to switch from SAC to LARC in TPB were exhibited as statistically significant. The model accounted for 66.3% of the variation in the intention to switch from SAC to LARC. Specifically, a positive attitude toward LARC use (β = 0.16; P = .014), strong SN (β = 0.46; P = .001), and higher PBC (β = 0.17; P = .001) had a positive influence on refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC. We have used standardized beta coefficients to examine the relative contribution of the exogenous/independent variable on the endogenous variable (intention to switch contraceptives). As indicated by the highest beta coefficient (β = 0.74; P = .001), SN had the strongest direct influence on refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC. Attitude toward LARC and PBC also had direct effects on the intention to switch contraceptive methods (Table 4).

The model fit indices indicated a chi-square ratio = 1.74, CFI = 0.910, RMSEA = 0.056, 90% CI: (0.049–0.063), and SRMR = 0.057, which indicates an adequate to a good fit of the model.

The TPB model was a good fit for examining refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC.

Discussion

The intention to switch from SAC to LARC among refugee women in Ethiopia was modeled using TPB. The study specifically tested the TPB framework regarding refugee women’s intentions to switch from SAC to LARC, a behavior transition not previously explored in a humanitarian context. The study revealed that refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC was low. The TPB model was found to fit the data well. All the TPB variables were significant predictors of the intention to switch, with SN identified as the main predictor. This highlights that TPB could be a useful framework for understanding the intention to switch from SAC to LARC among refugee women. Additionally, the model showed a strong ability to explain factors influencing the intention to change methods. The proportion of variance explained was similar to findings in other settings such as Iran [42] and SSA [43], but higher than reports from some studies in rural Ethiopia [30] and Uganda [34], suggesting that there may be contextual differences in how attitudes, SN, and PBC influence contraceptive intentions.

Previous studies have indicated that the community-level factors and health system-related factors not considered in the TPB framework may largely contribute to the unexplained variance [44, 45]. Moreover, factors such as inconvenient hours [46, 47], long waiting, inadequate privacy, confidentiality, lack of staff, insufficient staff to meet the needs of the community, and language barriers were reported as the common concerns for the contraceptive service provision in humanitarian settings [46, 48].

The result of the study revealed that all the TPB constructs were predictors of refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC. The finding was similar to results reported in previous studies in Uganda [34] and the United States [19, 49]. The finding that SN is the strongest predictor of intention to switch from SAC to LARC is in line with results documented in previous studies in Ethiopia that aimed to assess the applicability of the TPB in predicting condom use [30] and intention to use contraception [33]. However, this finding was inconsistent with the finding reported in South Africa, where only attitude and PBC of condom use intention [32]. Moreover, a study in the US revealed that a positive attitude toward LARC had a significant negative relation with the overall intention to switch a method [19]. Furthermore, previous studies assessing the validity of the TPB constructs to understand health behavior intentions revealed PBC as the strongest predictor of health behavior [42].

The observed difference among the TPB constructs in predicting health behavior in different studies might be related to the difference in study settings [33, 34, 42, 50] and study population [20, 31, 33]. In addition, the fact that the current study was conducted in humanitarian settings among women who had already initiated contraceptive methods might have contributed to the observed disparity. In the current study, SN emerged as the strongest predictor of intention to switch from SAC to LARC among refugee women. It could be associated with strong family and community ties in the refugee population, shared cultural values, and limited autonomy, which play a crucial role in influencing behavior [51].

One key strength of this study is its theory-driven approach, which identifies psychological factors that can inform contextually relevant and culturally appropriate interventions in humanitarian settings. However, we recognize that some limitations could affect the generalizability of our findings. First, all information was self-reported; therefore, it depends on participants’ honesty and accurate recall. However, we ensured privacy and confidentiality during interviews and used trained interviewers with standardized questionnaires to encourage honest responses. Second, the study was conducted among married women in selected refugee camps in Ethiopia. As such, the findings cannot be applied to unmarried refugee women. Furthermore, one refugee camp from the Gambella region of Ethiopia was excluded due to safety and security to travel to the study participants’ residential areas. This could have affected the representativeness of the sample of refugee camps identified for the study. However, in terms of country of origin and culture, the populations in the excluded refugee camp were similar to those in our study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, refugee women’s intention to switch from SAC to LARC was low. All the TPB variables, attitude, SN, and PBC, were significant predictors of intention to switch from SAC to LARC, with SN appearing as the key predictor. The study demonstrates that the TPB could be a relevant framework for understanding contraceptive use behavior in populations in humanitarian settings. Moreover, the results of the current study provide direction for healthcare professionals, program planners, and the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) by highlighting critical elements to consider when designing strategies for enhancing contraceptive utilization in humanitarian settings. Future LARC interventions in humanitarian settings should better emphasize women’s autonomy and the involvement of partners, families, and communities. To fully understand refugee women’s intention to use LARC, a further longitudinal study incorporating community-level and health system-level factors, which are not considered in the TPB framework, is recommended.
