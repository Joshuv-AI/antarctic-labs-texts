Costs in value-based health care dashboards: a qualitative study on stakeholder requirements
Authors: Esmée K J van der Poort, Danny van der Helm, Aiko P J de Vries, Wilbert B van den Hout, M Elske van den Akker-van Marle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn essential element of Value-Based Health Care (VBHC) is the use of population dashboards to evaluate and monitor the improvement of the value of care delivery in integrated practice units (i.e. care pathways). VBHC defines value as patient-relevant outcomes relative to costs, but in practice, costs are often overlooked. Therefore, this study aimed to explore functional, operational, and technical requirements for the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards.MethodsA qualitative study was performed at the organ transplant center of Leiden University Medical Center in the Netherlands. Qualitative data were collected using 15 semi-structured interviews, with stakeholders from four different categories: (1) health care providers, (2) management, (3) finance and IT, and (4) patients. Transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis and deductive coding based on requirements analysis.ResultsThis study identified three functional requirements for the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards. All stakeholder groups aimed to (1) identify costs and resources and (2) evaluate the value of care, whereas management additionally saw a role in (3) monitoring the financial viability of a care pathway. The required cost indicators could be categorized into four categories: cost of care activities, resources used to deliver care, income (e.g., reimbursements), and patient costs (e.g., out-of-pocket). In terms of operational requirements, costs should be reviewed monthly to quarterly in a recurring meeting, collectively by the care team and a financial expert. Technical requirements were a costing model that accurately captures all costs in care delivery, is validated, and provides up-to-date estimates. In addition, the dashboard interface should be embedded in existing IT systems to increase usability.ConclusionsThis study supports the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards through actionable requirements. By understanding key functional, operational, and technical requirements from different stakeholder perspectives, hospital cost data can be effectively integrated to improve the value of care delivery and monitor the financial viability of care pathways. Future research should focus on the role of patient-relevant costs in the data-driven improvement of VBHC delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13054-4.Keywords: Value-based health care, Costs, Dashboards, Requirements

Background

Value-based health care (VBHC) aims to transform health care delivery from a volume-based into a value-based system [1]. Value is defined as patient-relevant outcomes relative to the costs incurred to achieve those outcomes [2]. VBHC implementation is guided by a strategic agenda consisting of the following six steps: (I) organize into integrated practice units (i.e. care pathways), (II) measure outcomes and costs for every patient, (III) move to bundled payments for care cycles, (IV) integrate care delivery across separate facilities, (V) expand excellent services across geography, and (VI) build an enabling information technology (IT) platform [3].

In practice, VBHC programs within hospitals mostly focus on the first steps, (I) organizing into care pathways and (II) measuring outcomes and costs [4]. However, patient-relevant outcome measure (PROM) implementation for patients and clinicians is still poorly understood and does not live up to its potential [5]. Furthermore, costs are not a priority and are somewhat overlooked in VBHC implementation in both the Netherlands [6] and in Europe [7, 8]. A study on the current level of VBHC implementation in 25 value improvement teams in the Netherlands concludes focus on financial aspects could bring VBHC implementation to the next level.

To measure costs for every patient, hospitals need to apply costing models. A costing model estimates the cost of a single health service by allocating resources required to deliver care (such as personnel, equipment, and overhead) to care activities (such as an X-ray, specific type of surgery, or outpatient visit) [9]. There are several methods for costing, such as Time-Driven Activity Based Costing (TDABC) and Activity-Based Costing (ABC) [10]. Kaplan and Porter propose TDABC as a costing method for VBHC, which allocates resources based on their capacity and time of use [11]. ABC is a costing method through which resources are directly allocated to distinguishable activities in the care delivery process. In the Netherlands, ABC is standard practice in the majority of hospitals [12].

There are several challenges to the utilization of costs in VBHC implementation. First, VBHC implementation requires a shift from a budget-driven to a value-based organization. While hospital finance often works with a department structure (vertical silos) [9], patients move across the hospital through care pathways (horizontally), incurring costs in different departments with different budgets and financial responsibilities. Second, hospitals often use costing models and estimated costs to negotiate reimbursement rates with insurers [13], and, thus, this cost information is sensitive and confidential. Third, clinical and financial departments are often not integrated within hospitals and need interconnection for VBHC implementation.

To ensure the shift to value-based organization, hospitals and care pathways require dashboards to monitor the value of care delivery and guide data-driven improvement [14]. Dashboards are tools that visualize key data in a single screen to inform decision-making [15]. Within VBHC, the use of dashboards can help guide data-driven continuous improvement of care delivery and monitoring of performance [16]. However, few examples exist in the literature of dashboards that display cost indicators [17–20], and to our knowledge, no studies identify the necessary elements for such a dashboard.

Interprofessional collaboration in multistakeholder teams is fundamental to VBHC implementation [21], and, therefore, VBHC dashboards need to be tailored to multiple stakeholders, such as health care providers, managers, and quality advisors. Furthermore, patients should have a central role in defining what outcomes and costs matter [2]. Requirements analysis aims to thoroughly understand and document the needs and expectations of stakeholders in software development to successfully develop a particular system or product [22]. Requirements analysis includes functional requirements (what a system should be able to do), operational requirements (under what conditions), and technical requirements (such as data and interface). Actively involving stakeholders and reflecting their different needs is associated with more effective and efficient designs [23–26]. Therefore, this study aims to explore the functional, operational, and technical requirements for the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards.

Methods

Research design

This study was performed at the organ transplant center of Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), a Dutch academic hospital. To study dashboard requirements, this study used an exploratory, qualitative design with semi-structured interviews [27] and thematic analysis [28] based on requirements analysis [22]. The research protocol was reviewed by the patient panel of the transplant center before the initiation of the study. This panel consists of six organ transplant patients [29]. Ethical approval was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Department of Internal Medicine, Leiden University Medical Center, Leiden, the Netherlands (W2022.013).

Setting

The LUMC started the gradual implementation of VBHC in 11 care pathways, including the organ transplant center, in 2018. Enabling information technology platforms were built in the form of population dashboards and supported by a hospital-wide data platform. The population dashboard of the transplant center contains data from an online granular transplant database which combines transplant outcomes, prescriptions, immunology, and digital pathology with hospital data and the dashboard is continuously updated. Simultaneously, a patient-level costing model was implemented in the hospital. This model allowed a detailed registration of all care activities for individual patients, which were coupled with costs estimated through ABC. In 2020, the transplant center was the first care pathway in the hospital that aspired to add cost indicators to its population dashboard. Therefore, this qualitative study was initiated with the transplant center as a case study.

Participants

We purposefully sampled stakeholders of the organ transplant center dashboard (element one of requirements analysis [22]) with different organizational roles. We sampled stakeholders belonging to four groups: health care providers (HCP), management (M), finance and IT (F&IT), and patients (P). Stakeholders were defined as persons developing, implementing, regulating, influencing, and/or using the dashboard within the transplant center. Organ transplant patient representatives were included to provide a patient perspective on costs in VBHC. One researcher (DH) produced an overview of all relevant stakeholders within the organ transplant center and in management or support roles directly related to the transplant center within the hospital. This overview was reviewed and confirmed by a clinician (AV) and health economist (WH). Stakeholders were assigned to one of the four groups before invitation to participate in the study. In total, 52 identified stakeholders related to the organ transplant center and six patient representatives of the organ transplant patient panel were invited for an interview by e-mail. Stakeholders who did not reply were sent a reminder by e-mail or approached by DH to ensure participation of all four stakeholder groups. All participants provided informed consent for participation in the study.

Data collection

A semi-structured interview topic guide was developed by the research team based on the theoretical framework of requirements analysis [22]. EP and DH piloted the topic guide at the care pathway for vestibular schwannoma at the same hospital. The topic guide is provided as an appendix (Table S1). To educate the participants about the cost structures in the hospital, participants received an infographic about the available cost data in the hospital prior to their interview. Participants were asked if they had any questions about the infographic before the start of the interview to ensure sufficient knowledge for participation in the interview. Participants were asked permission to record the interview and told they could stop the interview and/or recording at any time. Interviews were intended to last around 30 min with a maximum of 45 min. The interview started with a discussion of the stakeholder’s organizational role and experience with hospital costs in their daily work. This was followed by specific questions focused on the potential use of cost indicators in a dashboard in the daily work of stakeholders. Interviews were held either in the physical or digital due to Covid-19 restrictions. All interviews were conducted by EP.

Data analysis

All interviews were recorded, either by tape recorder or in Microsoft Teams, and manually transcribed verbatim in Microsoft Word. Interviews conducted online were transcribed using the transcription functionality of Microsoft Teams and manually checked. Interviews were anonymized afterward. Transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis [28] and deductive coding to systematically and efficiently assess the elements of requirements analysis (coding tree is provided in appendix Figure S1). Two coders (EP and EA) independently looked for segments of the transcripts that provided functional (objective and type of analysis), operational (use of the dashboard), and technical (indicators, data, and interface) requirements and coded these segments accordingly. The mentioned barriers and facilitators were also translated into requirements. The two researchers separately coded all interviews in sets of three and consensus was reached after each set. After each set of three, the coders compared the fragments of the text that were coded and what codes were applied. The coders then discussed nonoverlapping codes and their reasoning for the application of a code in relation to the research question until they agreed. The analyses were performed using the ATLAS.ti software (version 22). For this manuscript, the relevant quotes from the interviews were manually translated from Dutch to English by EP, and translations were reviewed by EA and WH.

Fifteen interviews were conducted between April and August 2022. One patient representative turned down the invitation because of insufficient knowledge of costs. Thirty-nine stakeholders did not respond to our request for an interview. The average duration of the interviews was 29 minutes, ranging from 16 to 73 minutes. Stakeholders proposed identifying costs and resources as an objective for the dashboard. Participants in all stakeholder groups mentioned that information on costs is currently not easily accessible within the hospital data infrastructure. Participants stated that they had little access to data on the care activities performed within a care pathway and the related costs and reimbursements by the health insurer.

“Well, I do not think I am administering patients too much care. But I am curious though. What does the hospital earn for the care as I deliver it now? What is the difference between medical specialists and nurse specialists in costs? And, well, what does a transplantation cost? That is what I would like to know.” HCP1

Only nursing staff explicitly expressed an interest in the costs of materials used in care delivery, specifically when there were two or more alternatives available.

“I take individual sterile gloves, a small sheet, and a couple of gauze pads. That is what I use.… But my colleague uses a sort of kit with sterile gloves and more items to manage the equipment. And he says that it is cheaper than just the individual items I use. Honestly, I do not completely believe that.” HCP5

Furthermore, management and financial advisors said the cost information provided by the dashboard could aid them in their daily work, for example in discussions on finances of a care pathway. Some of the health care providers mentioned they would open the dashboard mostly out of curiosity.

“Well what does it cost, you know? A liver transplant patient? I would find that quite interesting.” HCP1

The evaluation of the value of provided care was mentioned as an objective by participants in all stakeholder groups. To start, health care providers talked about the ease with which certain care activities were performed or requested. Often, this was linked to a strict adherence to protocol, in which health care providers for example felt diagnostic testing was too extensive.

Participants described three methods of analysis in the dashboard through which the value of provided care could be improved. First, by increasing efficiency through evaluating what diagnostics are used to inform treatment decision-making and by eliminating any redundant care. This was described by a quality advisor as:

“Thoroughly examine the care pathway and, for example, decide with the responsible clinician: are we going to add something to the care pathway or are we going to remove something from it? And does it have an impact on the quality of care or not?” M3

Clinicians stressed reducing redundant care should be aimed at care activities that contribute the most to the total costs.

“I think that if you have a good understanding of the costs and know what the largest costs are, you can organize your care process in such a way that not too many unnecessary costs are incurred. At the same time, you continue to deliver optimal care.” HCP6

Second, the value of care can be evaluated by monitoring performance over time in continuous improvement cycles.

“The idea is to create a kind of continuous monitoring. If you address the same topic every 3 months, you can also determine that at some point someone might notice a trend or something that stands out. Then you can attach an improvement goal to investigate or address that afterward.” F&IT3

And third, the value of care can be evaluated through benchmarking on both outcomes and costs with other transplant centers in the Netherlands and providing an overview of this benchmark within the dashboard. Benchmarking provides an opportunity to share best practices and compare whether other care pathways, for example, had similar performance at a lower cost.

“For example, if I see that a kidney transplantation at our hospital costs twice as much as in another hospital, I will definitely go to [the transplant center] and ask, ‘Why are you spending so much more money? And then they have to convince me. So, what I really want is to benchmark. I want to understand what they are doing based on the benchmark.” M1

Besides evaluating the value of care, the stakeholder groups of management and finance & IT emphasized the use of the dashboard in monitoring financial viability. They stressed the dashboard could be used for the analysis of cost and reimbursements. For example, by doing portfolio analysis to determine whether care activities performed in the care pathway are feeders (i.e., more reimbursement than costs) or bleeders (i.e., more costs than reimbursement).

“…the financial assurance that there is sufficient income to cover the costs. That should be included, and you should also have a point of reference for when to stop if it falls below a certain threshold.” F&IT2

Operational requirements

In terms of how the dashboard should be used, participants mentioned the dashboard should be reviewed cyclically together with all stakeholders in regular meetings, such as a staff meeting, multidisciplinary care meeting, or yearly department review. The cyclical nature of reviewing the dashboard was important for continuous improvement of care delivery. In addition, participants agreed that the cost indicators should be reviewed each quarter or (bi-)monthly at the most.

“It would be good to schedule a meeting, maybe once every two months or once every three months, where we have agreed-upon moments to sit together, look at it collectively, and make decisions.” HCP2

When asked who should attend these meetings, most participants mentioned the care team. And especially medical specialists working in the care pathway. Participants suggested other health care providers, for example a radiologist or quality advisor, could be invited depending on the topic for discussion. One clinician mentioned it was important to invite medical specialists in training and students in medical school to educate them in value-based care. In addition, participants stressed the importance of a financial expert to explain the inner workings of the data and the financial model of the hospital, as well as someone responsible for the continuous improvement process.

“You really need a university-educated business manager.” F&IT2

“You really need someone in charge who will prepare and present it to say, hey, this catches my attention, and here’s something we can work with, or the past few months look good with no notable issues.” F&IT3

Technical requirements

Indicators

Participants were asked to describe relevant cost indicators for the dashboard (summarized in Table 3). The scope and context of the cost indicators depend on the functional requirements participants mentioned for using the dashboard. Health care providers mostly listed cost drivers of the care pathway, such as diagnostic tests and length of stay. From a managerial perspective, inclusion of income streams in the hospital and the resources required to deliver care were deemed important. Academic hospitals are responsible for training new medical specialists. This provides a secondary source of income for the hospital, but at the same time requires a certain number of procedures to be done as a part of the medical specialty training. Beside the overall costs, participants would like to see these metrics included in the dashboard in the context of the care pathway or hospital department. “You know, the way I see it, in the end, you would want to have the whole picture…when I see a patient, I don’t just think about… I’m only looking at the costs for the work-up now. You know, it’s about the total, like, hey, what are all the costs?” HCP3

Most participants stated that the cost indicators should be displayed at a population level, whereas some participants had an interest in the costs of care relating to individual patients and especially outliers. In addition, participants discussed that costs should be displayed together with the number of performed care activities.

Data

Participants identified qualities and constraints related to the data available in the hospital’s financial administration. Participants questioned the validity of this data and the underlying costing model. For example, nursing staff expected that there are many hidden costs and wondered whether all provided care is well registered and reimbursed:

“If I am unlucky, I see three to four patients in between appointments, and it never gets registered.” HCP5.

In addition, participants doubted whether the costs estimated by the costing model were up-to-date and a true reflection of the resources used in the care process.

“When it comes to the costs per care activity, I always wonder, how reasonable are [the estimates], really?” M1.

In the broader context of the hospital, the vertical organization of departments would sometimes clash with the horizontal organization of the care pathway. Different medical specialties across departments are represented within a care pathway, but their financial responsibility is often to departmental budgets. One financial advisor described reorganization of the hospital is required to obtain valid data to power the dashboard:

“If you have a theme like acute care, for example, or a theme like transplantation in general… When you think from these themes, you already have, well, then you’re increasingly going to get a consolidation of your data, towards a functioning dashboard. And well, right now, we’re actually still too fragmented.” F&IT2

Dashboard interface

According to participants, the interface of the dashboard requires a signaling function, for example by utilizing colors to signal the level of performance. One manager described the added value of a dashboard compared to printouts as:

“You will respond to a traffic light.” M2

Furthermore, participants said the dashboard should be easily accessible and embedded in the systems that they already use for their work. Specifically, IT and financial advisors discussed that a uniform design of the dashboard that can also be utilized for patient populations in other care pathways would help the uptake of the dashboard and that there should not be other tools with competing aims.

Patient perspective

Since patients will not use the cost dashboard, the patient perspective was considered separately from the other stakeholder groups. Both patient representatives mentioned that costs were not a consideration during the first year after transplantation. They trusted that health care providers were adequate at decision-making on what care is independent of the costs.

In the first year, I hardly ever thought about what it all costs. About what is involved. You are in such a rollercoaster that you absolutely do not care

Both patient representatives mentioned that for patients, productivity costs (e.g., salary income) and out-of-pocket expenses may be a concern later on. For example, supplements like vitamin D are not covered by basic health insurance in the Netherlands, causing additional out-of-pocket costs for patients.

Discussion

Drawing on the experience of several stakeholder groups, we identified a set of functional requirements for implementing costs in VBHC dashboards. Health care providers, management, finance staff and IT staff aimed to use the dashboard to (1) identify costs and resources and (2) evaluate the value of care, whereas management additionally saw potential for (3) monitoring the financial viability of a care pathway. In terms of operational requirements, the dashboard should be reviewed monthly to quarterly in a recurring meeting, collectively by the care team and a financial expert. Technical requirements include data from a validated costing model that accurately captures all costs in care delivery, and provides up-to-date estimates. Furthermore, the dashboard interface should be embedded in existing IT systems to increase usability. The identified requirements build on the value agenda [3] and provide actionable steps for the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards.

In dashboard design, translating data into meaningful indicators can be hard due to differing stakeholder demands [30]. This study was grounded in requirements analysis, which emphasizes the need to understand stakeholder requirements for successful software development [22]. We have addressed this by including four stakeholder groups: health care providers, management, finance and IT, and patients. The findings indeed show that the functional requirements depended on stakeholder roles. In contrast to the other groups, management expressed an interest in the financial viability of a care pathway which affected the scope of the dashboard. Overall, the functional features should fit the purpose of the dashboard [31].

The range of identified functional requirements in this study is largely reflected by the objectives of the limited number of published VBHC dashboards that contain costs. Several published dashboards report costs at the care pathway level to control health care spending and utilization [18, 19]. In addition, van Steenbergen et al.. monitor patient-reported outcomes and costs of an improvement initiative [32] and Beck da Silva Egtes et al.. benchmark hospitals on the outcomes and costs of ischemic stroke care [33]. Furthermore, Gabriel et al.. also include reimbursements to estimate the financial margin and value of a joint replacement surgery care pathway [34]. Yet, the accompanying requirements in terms of indicators, dashboard use, data, and dashboard interface have not been reported before.

Two published dashboards from the U.S. benchmark individual surgeons on the costs and outcomes of surgery [17, 35]. However, benchmarking of surgeons was not a functional requirement reported by the participants of our study. Whereas participants would like to compare the costs of certain services to other in-hospital care pathways, no participant expressed the wish to benchmark clinicians. Benchmarking clinicians may be useful to detect where the differences in costs of certain services occur. The availability of granular data will allow for deep-dives into the sources of variation between services.

The required cost indicators for the dashboard should be chosen carefully based on the functional requirements. In this study, we identified four categories: the cost of care activities, resources used to deliver care, income (e.g., reimbursements), and patient costs (e.g., out-of-pocket). These types of costs are generally distinguished within the field of health economics [36], but form no complete list. The costs implemented in a VBHC dashboard should be adapted to the context and specific patient population, as care activities are unique to each type of care pathway.

No previous studies reported on the requirements for cost dashboards in health care possibly due to the sensitive nature of cost data. This data provides important leverage in provider-insurer negotiations, competition with other providers, and management strategies. Yet, our findings on requirements for the data and the dashboard interface mirror studies in the field of quality indicator dashboards. Requirements such as ease-of-use, integration with other IT-systems, and data being up-to-date were also identified by Rabiei and Almasi in a systematic review of requirements for hospital dashboards [37]. In addition, cost dashboards require the same functionalities as quality dashboards for assessing performance. In both this study and a study by Randall et al.. on the requirements for quality dashboards [38], participants wished to compare performance to a standard and evaluate trends over time.

Strengths and limitations

This study has both strengths and limitations. It is the first study to explore the requirements for implementing costs in VBHC dashboards. Furthermore, this study actively involves stakeholders and reflects their different needs, which is associated with more effective and efficient designs [23–26]. However, this study has a small sample size and, therefore, has limited generalizability. In addition, not all stakeholder views may be captured and views may be context-specific. For example, patient-relevant costs will differ between health care systems. In the future, the role of health insurers in relation to VBHC dashboards should also be investigated. Furthermore, the use of deductive coding could result in the omission of emergent themes within the data. Overall, these limitations should be kept in mind when using the results in a different context, and future research is required to assess the generalizability of the identified requirements.

Practical implications

There are several noteworthy practical implications for the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards.

First, an important finding of this study is that patient representatives marked absence or loss of work and out-of-pocket costs as the most patient-relevant costs in the first year after kidney transplantation. However, these patient costs cannot be derived from hospitals’ financial administration, complicating the uptake in a VBHC dashboard. In general, VBHC studies (thus far) focus on the costs of care from a provider perspective [10] whereas value in VBHC is defined from the patient’s perspective. Patient-relevant costs can be quantified with the use of questionnaires frequently used in economic evaluations, e.g. the iPCQ for productivity [39] and the iVICQ for informal care [40], but this may be more difficult for out-of-pocket costs and copayments. To optimize value to the patient, it is important to consider all relevant costs and outcomes of care [41] and future research on this topic is needed.

Second, this study was conducted in a hospital with an IT infrastructure and costing model present. The appropriate IT infrastructure is an important prerequisite for developing and implementing a dashboard and this should be the starting point. This also smoothens the integration of a dashboard into existing systems. Within the LUMC, a hospital-wide data platform couples different data sources, such as electronic patient records, quality registries, and financial administration, and this is the source of many web-based dashboards utilizing programs like Power BI.

A third practical implication is that the hospital under study has implemented a hospital-wide ABC model whereas the VBHC literature largely considers TDABC. TDABC provides granular cost estimates based on the time and resources required to deliver care [11], but it is a resource-intensive method to implement [42, 43]. TDABC may, therefore, be less suitable for hospital-wide implementation on the short term, whereas ABC is already established in the majority of Dutch hospitals. TDABC could provide an additional dimension to VBHC dashboards compared to ABC by identifying inefficiencies and streamlining care pathways based on time estimates. Fourth, to generate actionable data, health care providers, management, and finance and IT all expressed a financial expert was required to help interpret the cost data in the dashboard. The question is whether it is feasible to have a financial expert present every time the dashboard is reviewed. Instead, it may be more appropriate to train users of the dashboard in interpreting cost data. Finally, the proper organizational structure was cited as an important prerequisite for obtaining cost data for a given patient population. This touches upon the essence of VBHC: data-driven improvement is not simply the measurement of outcomes and costs but the shift to a value-based system requires all the elements of the value agenda, including the building of enabling technology platforms [4].

Conclusions

This study supports the implementation of costs in VBHC dashboards through actionable requirements. By understanding key functional, operational, and technical requirements from different stakeholder perspectives, hospital cost data can be effectively integrated to improve the value of care delivery and monitor the financial viability of care pathways. Future research should focus on the role of patient-relevant costs in the data-driven improvement of VBHC delivery.
