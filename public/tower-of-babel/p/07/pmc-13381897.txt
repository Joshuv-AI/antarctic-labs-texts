Selective deoxygenative bis- and monoborylation of carboxylic esters via boryloxy chromium–carbenes
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Yan Zhang, Linhong Long, Hui Chen, Eiichi Nakamura, Xiaoming Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFischer-type metal carbenes are versatile intermediates that mediate a wide array of molecular transformations. However, their classical synthesis typically requires stoichiometric amounts of chromium and hazardous reagents such as diazo compounds or organolithium species, posing significant safety and operational concerns. A recent copper- and palladium-catalyzed Brook rearrangement of aroylsilanes opened a catalytic strategy to alleviate these problems. Herein, we report a complementary strategy that enables catalytic deoxygenative borylation of carboxylic esters under conditions consistent with the intermediacy of boryloxy chromium–carbene species, enabled by a combination of pinacolborane, magnesium metal, and a chromium catalyst. The reaction converts the ester to either bis- or monoboryl alkanes—valuable intermediates for further synthetic diversification—depending on the choice of bipyridine or terpyridine ligand. Multiple lines of evidence, including cyclopropanation, deuterium quenching, and DFT analysis, support a low-energy pathway involving chromium–carbene intermediates. The ligand-controlled bifurcation arises from subtle energy differences between quintet and triplet pathways, elucidated by the DFT calculations.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Homogeneous catalysis, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

The Fischer carbene complex is an archetype of organometallics bearing a carbon-metal double bond. Its discovery in the 1960s marked a paradigm shift in organometallic chemistry and expanded the toolbox of organic synthesis, including cycloaddition, cross-coupling, and single-atom skeletal editing reactions1–5. However, these reactive intermediates are usually derived from high-energy starting materials such as activated diazo compounds and moisture-sensitive alkyllithium reagents (Fig. 1a, b)6–8. Safety concerns and the complexity of these experimental procedures have stimulated the search for simpler and milder alternatives using naturally abundant and thermodynamically stable precursors9–14. The legacy of Fischer continues in today’s efforts to make catalytically active, sustainable, and selective carbene-transfer reagents. In this context, the 1,2-photo Brook rearrangement15–17, which generates a nucleophilic α-siloxy carbene (A), is remarkable for its mildness and cleanliness. First reported by Duff and Brook18, this rearrangement is triggered by photoexcitation, with the 1,2-bond switching providing a driving force through the formation of a stable Si–O bond (452 kJ mol−1) from a less stable Si–C bond, resulting the formation of transient copper and palladium carbene complexes (Fig. 1c)19–24. Inspired by the Brook rearrangement and several experimental findings in our laboratories25, we propose a methodology for converting an ester group into an η2-acyl complex (B, Fig. 1d), which subsequently isomerizes into an alkoxyl metal–carbene complex (D) by an electrophilic organoboron reagent26–29. This reaction is driven by the formation of a highly stable B–O bond (536 kJ mol−1) (C) and is shown here to be feasible by the use of a combination of pinacolborane (HBpin) and a chromium catalyst30–32.

The synthetic utility of 1,1-bis(boryl)alkanes and monoborylalkanes is well-established, especially in cross-coupling and radical reactions for constructing C(sp3)-rich architectures in pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals33–40. The boryloxy chromium–carbene-based deoxygenative coupling reactions of electrophilic aryl ester with Lewis-acidic HBpin represent a new cross-electrophile coupling (XEC), a concept that has recently emerged as a valuable strategy for step-economical synthesis by cost-effective chromium catalysis41–44. Despite the synthetic utility of bis- and monoborylated alkanes as partners by coupling with carbon-centered anions and radicals for the formation of C–C bonds45,46, synthetic approaches to geminal bisboryl compounds are underdeveloped. Recent advances in developing strategies of benzylic C–H bisborylation, borylation of boryloxy motifs, and diboration of alkynes have been demonstrated by Chirik47,48, Liu49–52, and others53–55 enabling the synthesis of 1,1-bisboryl compounds. Additionally, methods for constructing aliphatic bis- and 1,1,2-trisboronates from feedstocks such as formic acid or via ester triborylation have been developed56–58. We became interested in utilizing a low valent Cr (0) species to cleave the C–O bond of an ester to generate an η2-acyl complex (B, Fig. 1d) for deoxygenative XEC reaction with HBpin59. The catalytically active Cr (0) species can be formed in situ by reduction of CrCl2 in the presence of a di- or terpyridine ligand32.

Here, we report the reaction of a carboxylic ester with HBpin, magnesium powder, a catalytic amount of CrCl2, and a bipyridine or terpyridine ligand, which facilitates the stepwise replacement of the ester’s oxygen groups with one or two Bpin moieties (Fig. 1e). Mechanistic experiments are consistent with the formation of a boryloxy chromium–carbene-type intermediate, whose reactivity was confirmed by the inter and intramolecular cyclopropanation of olefins, providing a compelling evidence of carbene-type intermediates3,60–67. Notably, we observed a bifurcation in product formations, where terpyridine ligand (tttpy) predominantly yielded monoboryl products, while bipyridine ligand (ddbpy) favored the formation of geminal 1,1-bis(boryl) compounds. Both reactions performed at 40–80 °C can be carried out on a scale of several grams. Multiple lines of evidence, including cyclopropanation, deuterium quenching, and density functional theory (DFT) analysis, support a low-energy pathway involving chromium carbene intermediates. DFT calculations suggest that the ligand-controlled bifurcation may originate from subtle differences in spin-state energetics and coordination environment.

Results and discussion

Reaction conditions for the selective bis- and mono(borylation) of carboxylic esters

After testing a diverse range of metal precatalysts and ligands for the reaction between aryl carboxylic esters and HBpin, we found a mixture of geminal bisborylated and monoborylated compounds (2a and 3a) formed by the use of CrCl2, metallic magnesium, and HBpin (Table 1, entry 1). Other first-row transition metal salts of FeCl2, CoCl2, NiCl2, and CuCl2 were entirely ineffective (entries 2–5). In order to improve the conversion and chemoselectivity, we studied ligand effects to find that 4,4′-di-tert-butyl-2,2′-bipyridine (L1) accelerates the rection albeit with low selectivity (entry 6). Use of sterically congested 4,4′-di-tert-butyl-6,6′-dimethyl-2,2′-bipyridine (ddbpy) improved the selectivity toward the selective formation of bis(borylated) compound 2a (entry 7). The replacement of tert-butyls by methyl groups at the C4 positions of L1 does not enhance the reactivity of Cr catalysts in the bis- and monoborylation reactions (entry 8). Meanwhile, bipyridine ligands bearing two methyl substituents at the C2 or C3 positions showed no efficiency in promoting the transformations (entries 9 and 10). Interestingly, a tridentate tert-butyl-substituted terpyridine ligand (tttpy) significantly promotes the selectivity of borylation, now forming monoborylated compound 3a in 83% yield together with small amount of bisboronate ester 2a (entry 11). The selectivity toward the bis(borylated) product 2a was much improved simply by conducting the borylation at low temperature (40 °C) (entry 14), also indicating that the ddbpy-mediated bisborylation occurs faster than the tttpy-mediate mono-borylation. Added LiCl slightly slowed down the monoborylation, resulting in the formation of 2a in 85% yield (entry 15)68,69 (The details are listed in Supplementary Tables 1–7).

Synthetic scope of selective bis and monoborylation reactions

The catalytic conversion of carboxylic esters to bis- or monoboryl alkanes via boryloxy Cr-carbene shows a considerable synthetic scope, and the resulting boron compounds serve as useful starting materials for further synthetic elaborations. Fig. 2 illustrates the scope of the preparation of the geminal benzyl bisboronate motif by the ddbpy-mediated reaction. The same set of carboxylic esters as those shown in Fig. 2 afforded the geminal bisboronates 2b–e in good yield and selectivity. Biaryl-containing methylene bisboronate motifs 2f–h were also prepared in good yield. The ortho-substituent showed minimal influence on the bisborylation (2i–l). Chloride (2m), phenol (2n), silyloxy (2o), phenoxy (2p), and boronate ester (2q) survived the reaction. Oxygen- and sulfur-containing heteroaryl-substituted boronate esters were synthesized from the respective aryl carboxylic esters (2t–y). Notably, the Cr-catalyzed bisborylation system can be extended to the synthesis of geminal bis(boronate ester) products containing nitrogen-based functional groups, including amino, morpholinyl, and indolyl substituents (2z–ab). Furthermore, the bisborylation using alkyl carboxylic esters such as substituted methyl acetates smoothly proceeded, furnishing the alkylated 1,1-bis(boronate) derivatives 2ac–af. These differ from those of the cobalt-catalyzed deoxygenative triborylation with B2pin2, which typically affords 1,1,2-triborylalkane compounds58. The practicality of this bisborylation was further highlighted by applying it to carboxylic esters derived from bioactive molecules. Specifically, derivatives of the commercial drugs Ibuprofen (2ag) and Mefenamic acid (2ah) were smoothly converted to the corresponding borylated products.

In the presence of the tttpy ligand, both electron-rich and electron-deficient aryl carboxylic esters gave monoboronate esters 3b–d in good yield (Fig. 3). Cyclohexyl-, silyl-, and phenoxy-substituted benzyl boronate esters were synthesized from the corresponding aryl carboxylic ester precursors (3e–h). The presence of a phenol group in aryl carboxylic esters did not disturb the reaction and formed a (borylmethyl)phenol 3i. The tttpy-Cr-catalyzed reaction produced a boryl benzyl boronate ester 3j. The presence of ortho-methyl, ethoxyl, benzyl, and methylphenyl groups did not interfere with the reaction (3k–p). Naphthylmethylenyl, benzofuranylmethylenyl, and heteroaryl-containing benzyl boronate esters were synthesized in good yield (3q–v). The reaction system is tolerant of nitrogen-bearing functional groups, including amino, morpholinyl, and indolyl, enabling the formation of N-containing monoboronate ester derivatives 3w–y. Furthermore, aliphatic carboxylic esters, such as substituted methyl acetates, were successfully coupled with HBpin to furnish alkyl boronate esters 3z–ac in moderate to good yields. Beyond pinacolborane, 4,4,6-trimethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborinane also served as an effective boron source for the monoborylation of carboxylic esters, affording the monoborylated products 3ad and ae. The chemodivergent formation of two geminal bis- or monoboronate products from one common aryl carboxylic ester precursor summarized in Figs. 2 and 3 attest to the complementarity of the monoboronate ester synthesis to the Miyaura borylation of organic halides70, and the bisboronate ester synthesis to the cross-coupling of a halogen-containing geminal bisboronates with organometallic reagents71,72.

Mechanistic studies

We found a catalytic cleavage of the C–O bond of the ester 1a with CrCl2 and Mg without HBpin leads to a benzil compound 4, suggesting a critical role of a low-valent Cr species for the C–O bond cleavage and the possible involvement of η2-acyl chromate intermediate (Fig. 4a)73,74. When methyl 2-allylbenzoate (5) reacted with HBpin and ddbpy-Cr or tttpy-Cr species, we noted the formation of a hydroxyl cyclopropane product 6 in 19% and 13% yields, respectively, in addition to small amounts of borylation products by couplings with carboxylic ester (Fig. 4b). Without Cr species, the cyclopropanated compound 6 did not form. This observation is consistent with the formation of a plausible boryloxy Cr–carbene intermediate that undergoes an intramolecular cyclopropanation to afford 675,76. Furthermore, we used the isolated Fischer carbene complex 7 as precursor to react with HBpin, and found that 7 smoothly undergoes the bis- or monoborylation in a manner similar to the one observed in the catalytic reaction. In the presence of the ddbpy ligand, we obtained the geminal bis(borylated) compound 2a as the major product (Fig. 4c). In contrast, the monoborylated compound 3a was selective formed when we used the tttpy ligand. This selectivity parallels the selectivity observed under the catalytic conditions, providing support that a chromium carbene complex is a likely intermediate in the catalytic reaction and that HBpin drives the conversion of the carbene complex into bis and monoboryl products. The stoichiometric reaction with 1 equivalent of HBpin, followed by quenching with D2O, afforded deuterated benzyl alcohol 8-d in 35% yield with 82% deuterium incorporation at the benzylic position (Fig. 4d). The reaction of the in-situ formed η2-acyl chromate (complex I, Fig. 5g) with HBpin is considered, which may generate the hydrided α-boryloxy Cr–carbene species (III) and undergo hydride elimination in giving a boryloxy (alkyl)Cr (IV), by subsequent quenching to afford 8-d.

A plausible intermediate connecting the Cr–carbene complex (e.g., 7) and the borylated products 2a and 3a is a 1-boroxy-1-boryl compound 9. We propose that the bis- and monoboryl selectivity observed in the catalytic system originates from a bifurcation of the pathways at the stage from 9 to 2a and 3a, depending on the choice of the ligand between ddbpy and tttpy, respectively. Thus, the treatment of 9 with HBpin in the presence of ddbpy reductively borylated the boroxyl group to produce bis(borylated) product 2a, while that in the presence of tttpy afforded the monoborylated product 3a (Fig. 4e). The data indicated that the effects of ddbpy and tttpy originated from the bifurcation at the state of 9. Notably, Mg(OMe)2, a plausible byproduct originating from metallic magnesium, was found not to be a bystander but to promote the product formation. Repeating the tttpy-mediated reaction in Fig. 4e and quenching with D2O afforded a 63%-monodeuterated compound 3a-d, attesting to the intermediacy of a chromate intermediate V (Fig. 4f).

In an attempt to obtain more direct evidence of the formation of a chromium carbene complex, we performed mechanistic experiments to probe and trap this reactive species. We repeated the reaction in Fig. 4a in the presence of HBpin, hoping for the generation of boryloxy Cr–carbene via the deoxygenative borylation. A mixture of CrCl2 and ddbpy was reduced by Mg(0), was then reacted with a mixture of 1a and HBpin (Fig. 5a). Analysis of the mixture with high-resolution mass spectrometry analysis revealed signals consistent with species assignable to a boryloxy Cr–carbene formulation (In-a) or its hydridochromate tautomer (In-b) (HRMS, [M + H]+, found, 643.3368). (see Supplementary Fig. 8). While not definitive, these data support the presence of carbene-like intermediates under the reaction conditions. When this mixture was reacted with styrene, the cyclopropanol compound 10 could be detected by HRMS measurement (see Supplementary Fig. 9), suggesting the formation of boryloxy Cr–carbene species that reacts with styrene by cyclopropanation and followed by hydrolysis in giving 1077,78. It was noteworthy that the further reaction with HBpin and Mg could lead to the formation of borylated products 2a and 3a.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements provided a support of the formation of a chromium(II) intermediate in the first reaction in Fig. 4b. The peaks with a binding energy of 576.29 eV (assigned as Cr 2p3/2) and 585.45 eV (assigned as Cr 2p1/2) can be ascribed to the Cr(II) state79. We performed the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements of the Cr(II) species at X-band (9.85 GHz), showing a weak and sharp resonance at g = 2.006 (ΔH = 17.7 G) (Fig. 5c). Although it does not show additional hyperfine splitting, the EPR spectra is typical for an intermediate-spin Cr(II) complex80. The magnetic susceptibilities of the polycrystalline powder sample of Cr(II) intermediate were studied at temperatures from 300 to 10 K under 1000 Oe dc field. The effective magnetic moment was measured to be 2.82 μ at 300 K (Fig. 5d). It is in accordance with the expected value of 2.83 μ for Cr(II) with S = 1 and g = 2.0, suggesting a triplet Cr(II) species for In-b81. Upon cooling, the effective magnetic moment decreases slightly down to a minimum value of 2.32 μ at 10 K, indicating some magnetic anisotropy.

We followed the time course of the decrease of the starting material 1a and the formation of 2a and 3a with ddbpy. The bisborylation of 1a occurs rapidly, providing the bisboronate 2a in 82% yields within 2 h (Fig. 5e). The reaction was initially slow to give 3a in 3% yield after 2 h, indicating the existence of an inductive period in the reaction (Fig. 5f). We explored the kinetic behavior of deoxygenative monoborylation by using reactive Cr that was formed in situ by reducing CrCl2/tttpy with Mg. The logarithm plot and linear fitting of the data of the initial coupling rates are consistent with a first-order kinetic behavior in aryl carboxylic ester 1a, HBpin, and the tttpy Cr catalyst (see Supplementary Figs. 17–19).

DFT studies on boryloxy chromium–carbene formation and bis and monoboryl bifurcation

The experimental data combined with the conventional wisdom of organometallic chemistry suggested a catalytic cycle shown in Fig. 5g. In contrast to Zhao’s triborylation, which is initiated by carbonyl addition and proceeds via β-H/β-O elimination through alkenyl ether intermediates51, the reaction is proposed to start with oxidative addition of a Cr(0) species with the aryl ester to generate an acylchromium (II) species I, which then reacts with HBpin to generate an unstable hydridochromium(II) carbene complex III via the transition state II. Intramolecular hydride transfer reaction of III produces IV, which reacts with another molecule of HBpin to give stage to convert 9 to α-borylated (alkyl)Cr species V, from which the ddbpy/tttpy-dependent bifurcation of the path occurs. The stoichiometry of the reaction requires the generation of dihydrogen, which we confirmed by GC-MS analysis for the borylation of 1a. Although plausible, this catalytic cycle is complex, and we therefore sought computational support using DFT modeling, as detailed below.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the first step of the catalysis, the cleavage of acyl C–O bond of 1a is calculated to produces an (acyl)Cr(II) IN2 with a barrier as small as ~3 kcal mol–1 either via triplet or quintet Cr(0) path. The reaction of IN2 with HBpin is computed to affords a triplet boryloxy hydridochromium–carbene complex IN6 through a series of low-energy intermediates in triplet or quintet states, such as a boryl-bound intermediate IN4. The most important step in the first half of the catalysis is the conversion of IN6 into IN7, where the hydride transfer from the Cr(II) center to the carbene carbon converts a thermodynamically unstable Cr = C bond to a stable Cr–C bond. The conversion of IN7 to a mixture of a borylated boryloxide product 9 and hydrochromate(II) IN9 occurs with the largest energy barrier in the whole catalytic cycle because of the energetically costly σ- bond metathesis between the Cr–C bond in IN7 and H–B bond in HBpin. Loss of dihydrogen from IN9 produces a reactive Cr(I) complex (IN10), which reductively cleaves the C–OBpin bond of the borylated boryloxide intermediate 9 to generate a stable Cr(III) intermediate IN11. We propose that metallic magnesium takes part in the catalytic cycle at this stage to reduce the chromium center to generate α-borylated (alkyl)Cr(II) intermediate (IN12) and thermodynamically stable Mg(OBpin)2(THF)2 that provides the driving force of the catalysis.

The DFT analysis proved useful for rationalizing the ddbpy/tttpy-dependent bifurcation between bis- and monoborylation. According to the calculations, the selectivity originates from a subtle difference in the energy barriers of the quintet (for ddbpy) and triplet (for tttpy) pathways—a feature that could be addressed with the aid of high-level theoretical calculations. We can imagine that the bifurcation occurs at the stage of the reaction of HBpin and the last ddbpy-complex IN12 in Fig. 6a, as illustrated in Fig. 6b. σ-Bond metathesis between Cr(II)–OMe and H–B places the Bpin on the methoxy group via TS12-13 and produces the monoboryl compound 3a. Alternatively, σ-bond metathesis between Cr(II)–C and H–B places the Bpin on the carbon atom via TS12-9 and produces the bisboryl compound 2a. While both pathways should be intuitively feasible through either triplet or quintet path, the calculations indicated that the path to 2a occurs with 11.6 kcal mol–1 activation energy, which is favored over the path to 3a by 1.4 kcal mol–1. This difference would correspond to a product ratio of approximately 1:9.5, which is consistent with the experimental ratio of 1:9.4.

The reaction of HBpin with tttpy-chromium(II) complex IN15 in Fig. 6c was a little more complicated than that of the ddbpy-counterpart IN12 in Fig. 6b. The Cr(II)–C/H–B metathesis step (TS15-16) leading to the bisboryl compound 2a is calculated to have the highest energy (23.6 kcal mol−1), and the Cr-to-carbon hydride migration step (TS18-19) producing the mono boron compound 3a is lower in energy (22.0 kcal mol−1). The energy difference is 1.6 kcal mol−1, translating to a 9.8:1 ratio favoring the monoboryl compound 3a. (Source data are provided as a Source Data file).

Concerning the key factor that governs the ligand-controlled chemoselectivity, we suggest that it might be the steric effect. As shown in the Fig. 6b, the ddbpy ligated Cr species is inclined to deliver bisborylation product, whereas this tendency is reversed for tttpy ligated Cr species catalyzed pathway. Intriguingly, this phenomenon can be rationalized by analyzing the ligand state of the two chemoselective determining transition states via tttpy ligated Cr-species. As shown in the Fig. 6c, the coordination numbers of the transition state (TS15-16) leading to diborylation product is six, which is larger than that (five) of TS18-19 leading to monoborylation one. The larger coordination number of TS15-16 corresponds to a more hindered coordination environment around Cr center, thus disfavoring the event leading to diborylation product. Consequently, we proposed that steric effect might serve as one of the factors contributing the observed ligand-controlled chemoselectivity. Notably, the higher energy of the tttpy-mediated formation of monoboronate 3a is consistent with our experimental observation that this reaction occurs at much higher temperature (80 °C, 12 h; Fig. 3) than the ddbpy-mediated formation of bisboronate 2a (40 °C, 3 h; Fig. 2). (see Supplementary Figs. 20–22). We acknowledge that definitive structural characterization of the proposed oxycarbene intermediate remains challenging under catalytic conditions. Nevertheless, the convergence of reactivity patterns, isotope incorporation, spectroscopic signatures, and theoretical modeling collectively supports its intermediacy as a plausible species.

Scalability and downstream functionalization

The Cr-catalyzed bis- and monoborylation reactions of carboxylic esters are scalable. The ddbpy-promoted reaction on a five-gram scale afforded 7.97 g of the bisboronate 2a and the tttpy-promoted reaction afforded 5.05 g of the monoboronate ester 3a (Fig. 7a). Examples of downstream functionalization of geminal bisboronate 2a are shown in Fig. 7b. The reaction of 2a with benzophenone afforded a boronate containing tetrasubstituted olefin 11 in 90% yield, and 2a smoothly reacted with trifluoromethyl alkene to form a difluoro allylic benzyl boronate ester (12). Arylation at the benzylic carbon of 2a with an aryl iodide provided quick access to diarylmethene (13). In the presence of LiOt-Bu, the reaction of geminal bisboronate with cyclohexyl carboxylic ester furnished the corresponding ketone 14. Fig. 7c illustrates further conversion of the monoboronate ester 3a into a naphthyl compound (15) by Pd-catalyzed Suzuki coupling, and a thiophene 16 by C–H coupling. Alternatively, the Kumada coupling with alkenyl Grignard reagent gave an allylic benzene 17, and the boronate group was oxidized into a benzyl methyl ether 18.

In summary, we have developed a strategy of bora Brook-type reaction for generating boryloxy chromium–carbene complexes catalytically from simple aryl carboxylic esters, enabled by a Cr–Mg–HBpin system. This transformation represents an example of ester-to-carbene conversion, proceeding via initial acyl C–O bond activation followed by boryl transfer to form non-stabilized Fischer-type Cr–carbene intermediates. Depending on the choice of bidentate or tridentate polypyridine ligands, these intermediates enable divergent pathways toward selective bis- or monoborylation. The ligand-dependent bifurcation is proposed to arise from distinct electronic and spin-state preferences, as indicated by DFT calculations. The resulting geminal bis- or monoboronate products were obtained with high selectivity and scalability, using readily available esters as alkylating reagents. Experimental studies, including cyclopropanation and deuterium quenching, provide support for the involvement of a reactive Cr–carbene species. These findings highlight an alternative disconnection in metal–carbene chemistry and open avenues for the broader application of esters as carbene precursors. Ongoing efforts are directed toward stabilizing and expanding the utility of these boryloxy Cr–carbene intermediates.

Methods

General procedure for the deoxygenative cross-electrophile bisborylation of aryl carboxylic esters by Cr catalysis

In a dried Schlenk tube were placed ester 1 (0.2 mmol), HBpin (116 μL, 4 equiv., 0.8 mmol), CrCl2 (2 mg, 10 mol%), ddbpy (6 mg, 10 mol%), Mg (14 mg, 3 equiv., 0.6 mmol), LiCl (9 mg, 1 equiv., 0.2 mmol). A freshly distilled THF (1 mL) was then added by a syringe under atmosphere of nitrogen, and the mixture was stirred at 40 °C for 3 h. After cooling down to room temperature, the mixture was quenched with a saturated aqueous solution of NH4Cl (2 mL), and the aqueous layer was extracted with dichloromethane. The organic phases were collected and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by column chromatography to afford the related products.

General procedure for the deoxygenative cross-electrophile monoborylation of aryl carboxylic esters by Cr catalysis

In a dried Schlenk tube were placed ester 1 (0.2 mmol), HBpin (145 μL, 5 equiv., 1.0 mmol), CrCl2 (2 mg, 10 mol%), tttpy (8 mg, 10 mol%), Mg (19 mg, 4 equiv., 0.8 mmol). Subsequently, a freshly distilled THF (1 mL) was added by a syringe under atmosphere of nitrogen, and the mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 12 h. After cooling down to room temperature, the mixture was quenched with a saturated aqueous solution of NH4Cl (2 mL), and the aqueous layer was extracted with dichloromethane. The organic phases were collected and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by column chromatography to afford the related products.
