Research on intercity travel mode recognition and network structure characteristics based on complex network and random forest classification
Authors: Wanping Zhang, Zhanfu Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to address the issue of identifying diverse intercity travel modes in high population density areas and analyzing their network structure characteristics over different time periods. Consequently, a management model combining complex network theory (CNT) with a random forest classification (RFC) algorithm—referred to as the CNT-RFC model—is proposed. The study utilizes publicly available migration data from the National Bureau of Statistics and transportation departments from January 2021 to December 2023. Network structure features are extracted through node degree distributions, edge connections, centrality metrics, and community detection algorithms. Integrating RFC enables precise identification of travel modes and uncovers the spatio-temporal heterogeneity of intercity travel patterns. Experimental results demonstrate that the optimized model outperforms comparative methods on key metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Specifically, for leisure travel identification, the CNT-RFC model achieves an accuracy of 0.947, precision of 0.928, and F1 score of 0.947, surpassing advanced models such as the Funnel Tabular Transformer and Graph Transformer for Node and Edge Representation Learning. Paired sample t-tests confirm that these improvements are statistically significant (p < 0.05) with a very large effect size (Cohen’s d > 3.5). Network structure analysis reveals a decline in the small-world coefficient to 0.58 during holiday periods, an increase in average travel distance to 25 km, and a rise in average adaptation cost to 0.30, indicating significant structural reconfiguration. Sensitivity analysis related to the pandemic further validates the model’s robustness, showing only a slight accuracy decline of 0.0101 in 2022, while the centrality of the high-speed rail network decreased by 0.0720, confirming the pandemic’s asymmetric impact across transportation network layers. This study provides robust scientific evidence and effective solutions for regional transportation planning and policy formulation, thereby effectively addressing traffic congestion, optimizing travel routes, and improving intercity transportation efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19392-x.

Introduction

With the acceleration of global urbanization and population concentration in metropolitan areas, intercity travel demands—such as commuting, business trips, and tourism—have grown significantly. These demands have become critical variables influencing regional coordinated development, resource allocation efficiency, and the resilience of transportation systems1,2. Guided by national strategies such as China’s 14th Five-Year Plan and regional integration policies, promoting regional transportation integration and optimizing travel structures have emerged as macro-level policy priorities3. In practice, intercity travel exhibits pronounced heterogeneity across temporal, spatial, and structural dimensions. Accurately identifying distinct travel modes and understanding their underlying network characteristics has become a central challenge for transportation governance and regional policymaking. Existing studies predominantly rely on traditional methods—such as questionnaire surveys, origin-destination matrix statistics, and rule-based classification—to identify intercity travel types. While these approaches provide some reference value, they face significant limitations in handling complex travel behaviors, large-scale data volumes, and nonlinear relationships, often resulting in low computational efficiency and insufficient classification accuracy4. In academic research, the structural analysis of intercity travel networks remains contested. Some studies have applied complex network theory (CNT) to reveal centrality and clustering characteristics of urban nodes, offering fresh perspectives on intercity interactions. Meanwhile, machine learning algorithms—particularly random forest classification (RFC)—have demonstrated strong capability in recognizing urban travel behaviors5. However, few existing studies have integrated network structure modeling with travel behavior identification within a unified analytical framework. Moreover, empirical validation of such integrated models across multiple regions and time periods remains limited.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an integrated model combining complex network analysis with RFC, targeting two core challenges:

How to identify multiple types of intercity travel modes based on structural network characteristics;How these travel modes exhibit centrality, community structure, and spatio-temporal evolution within the network.

Distinct from existing hybrid approaches, this study introduces three main innovations. Methodologically, complex network indicators—such as degree centrality and clustering coefficients—are incorporated as input features into the RFC algorithm, enabling a unified framework for structure-behavior identification. Empirically, a multi-source migration dataset spanning three years and multiple time periods is constructed to capture behavioral differences across holidays, weekdays, and weekends. Practically, the study proposes policy recommendations for regional coordination and transportation optimization, bridging the gap in the “structure identification–behavior classification–policy response” chain.

In summary, this study systematically investigates the identification of intercity travel modes and the analysis of network structures in high-density regions, establishing an integrated analytical framework with both theoretical significance and practical applicability. The objectives include developing and validating the CNT-RFC integrated model, identifying typical intercity travel types and their key influencing factors, analyzing intercity network structures, community divisions, and spatio-temporal evolution patterns, and ultimately offering targeted policy recommendations for traffic optimization. This study addresses existing literature gaps and provides new analytical tools and decision-making pathways for regional transportation governance.

The structure of the study is as follows. First, the relevant literature and theoretical foundations are reviewed to clarify the current state and limitations in travel mode identification and network analysis research. Next, the CNT-RFC model framework is developed, integrating complex network structures with travel behavior factors to design the algorithmic process. Subsequently, model evaluation and comparative experiments are conducted using real-world data, including performance testing, network structure analysis, spatio-temporal heterogeneity analysis, and data sensitivity verification. Finally, the study discusses the model’s performance and research findings, addressing controversies and gaps identified in existing studies.

To better manage complex urban transportation systems, community detection algorithms have increasingly been applied to uncover intercity connectivity patterns. Boeing (2022) employed community partitioning methods to analyze public transportation networks, revealing that network-based zoning facilitated more refined transportation policy management9. Similarly, Basciftci and Van-Hentenryck (2023) proposed a label propagation algorithm with high stability and accuracy for identifying transportation network communities10. Building on this, Liu et al. Nevertheless, most community detection studies remain at the structural analysis and have yet to integrate these network insights into travel behavior classification frameworks.

Complementing structural approaches, RFC has shown strong performance in travel behavior identification due to its nonlinear modeling capabilities and robustness. Xiao and Xu (2024) applied RFC to classify travel patterns among Beijing residents, finding it outperformed traditional statistical methods in both accuracy and robustness12. Durán-Micco and Vansteenwegen (2022) used RFC on large-scale transportation datasets to distinguish commuting, business, and leisure travel modes, confirming its stability under complex classification tasks13. Taecharungroj (2022) further improved classification accuracy by tuning RFC parameters, demonstrating its adaptability and laying the groundwork for integrating complex network metrics into classification models14. However, most RFC-based studies focus exclusively on behavioral dimensions and lack structural perspectives—such as intercity node interactions and transportation linkage intensity—making it difficult to capture the multidimensional, heterogeneous nature of cross-regional datasets.

Overall, prior studies have significantly advanced intercity travel research from both structural and behavioral perspectives. Yet, a deeply integrated framework that unifies structural features with behavioral recognition has not been fully developed. Existing work often remains confined to single cities or specific modes of transport, with limited systematic analysis of cross-regional, multi-temporal travel patterns. Moreover, complex network features are rarely embedded in machine learning classification pipelines, reducing structural interpretability, while classification results seldom feed back into network optimization or transportation planning.

The proposed CNT-RFC model addresses these gaps through both methodological and empirical contributions. Methodologically, structural indicators such as centrality and modularity are incorporated into the RFC model as input variables, enabling an integrated structure–behavior modeling process. Empirically, the study leverages publicly available migration data from multiple regions and time periods to evaluate model adaptability and stability across varied spatio-temporal contexts on a national scale. Furthermore, by analyzing structural distributions and community evolution patterns based on classification results, the model generates targeted policy recommendations for regional transportation planning and collaborative governance. This approach bridges the research gap between “structure identification,” “behavioral classification,” and “policy application,” offering methodological innovation alongside practical, cross-regional empirical insights.

However, most applications of CNT in this domain focus on static network properties, with limited integration of dynamic travel behavior data for multimodal identification17,18. Building on this foundation, the present study extends CNT-based approaches by incorporating structural indicators—such as node centrality, clustering coefficients, and path efficiency—into a travel behavior classification model. These indicators serve as input features for identifying intercity travel modes, including commuting, business, and leisure travel, with a particular focus on high-density areas.

Extended application and integrated innovation of the RFC algorithm in transportation data analysis

RFC has been widely validated in travel behavior research for its strong ability to handle nonlinear relationships and high-dimensional transportation data. However, most existing studies employ RFC solely as an independent behavioral recognition tool, without integrating complex structural variables23,24. In this study, RFC is combined with CNT-derived indicators to construct a “structure–behavior collaborative identification model” for classifying three major intercity travel modes—commuting, business, and leisure—while also validating the model’s generalization capability across different temporal scales.

The computational process of the random forest algorithm primarily involves several key steps. First, sample data are extracted from the overall dataset through random sampling, generating multiple subsets of data, each of which is used to train an individual decision tree. For each subset, feature selection is performed to choose a portion of features for model construction. Based on the selected features, each decision tree is progressively built by splitting decision nodes. Each tree independently learns and classifies according to different feature combinations and training samples. After all decision trees are independently constructed, the model proceeds to the result integration phase. At this stage, all trees evaluate the input data individually and generate classification outcomes that form a voting mechanism. Each tree’s classification result counts as one vote, and the system aggregates these votes. The final output of the RFC model is the classification category receiving the highest frequency of votes, regarded as the most probable classification decision. This ensemble learning mechanism significantly enhances the model’s accuracy and robustness when handling high-dimensional, nonlinear problems, effectively mitigating the sensitivity of single models to noisy data or local optima.

Management model optimization based on CNT and RFC algorithm

The optimization of the management model in this study integrates CNT and the RFC algorithm to leverage their respective strengths—CNT for revealing network structure and nodal significance, and RFC for accurate classification and prediction in complex, high-dimensional datasets. This integration supports a unified framework for traffic system analysis, planning, and policy formulation.

The transportation management model framework constructed in this study encompasses key processes, including data collection, network metric extraction, feature engineering, and model optimization. In the data collection and preprocessing phase, multi-source migration and transportation data are obtained from agencies such as the National Bureau of Statistics and the Ministry of Transport. Data preprocessing involves cleaning (removing duplicates and outliers), standardization (unifying temporal formats and spatial units), and integration (merging data across multiple time periods and regions) to ensure accuracy and consistency for subsequent analyses. The processed data are then structured into a complex network, where cities serve as nodes and population migration flows constitute weighted edges. Based on this network, core structural metrics are extracted, including degree distribution (measuring node connectivity), clustering coefficient (reflecting local clustering characteristics), path length (measuring the shortest distance between nodes), and centrality indicators (such as degree centrality, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality). These metrics form the critical feature inputs for model training. During feature engineering, the aforementioned network structure metrics are combined with spatio-temporal attributes to construct a high-dimensional feature set, which is subsequently partitioned into training and testing subsets. In the model construction and optimization phase, the random forest algorithm is employed for training, with ensemble learning techniques applied to enhance the model’s performance. Model performance is evaluated and tuned through cross-validation to ensure stable identification capability and predictive accuracy across multiple time periods and regions. Finally, the trained model is applied to practical transportation management tasks, enabling precise identification of different travel modes and classification of travel patterns. This provides data support and a decision-making foundation for urban traffic optimization and policy formulation.

In summary, the optimized management model based on CNT and the random forest algorithm effectively integrates the strengths of both methodologies. It adeptly processes intricate traffic data, accurately identifies travel patterns, predicts traffic flow, and offers a robust scientific foundation and practical solutions for optimizing and managing traffic systems. This approach not only enhances the operational efficiency of traffic systems but also addresses the increasing demands and complexities associated with modern transportation.

Analysis of network structure characteristics

To reveal the mechanisms influencing intercity travel across different time periods, this study introduces two analytical dimensions: push–pull factors and intermediate barriers. This theoretical framework explains the driving forces and impediments individuals face during migration. Push–pull factors refer to the attractive or repulsive influences of the origin and destination—such as economic conditions, population, employment, and service provision. In contrast, intermediate barriers include resistive factors along the migration path, such as geographical distance, administrative boundaries, cultural differences, and information barriers. Although originally developed in the context of international migration and tourism research, this framework has recently been applied to urban mobility, commuting, and travel behavior to explain the drivers of travel willingness and the impediments affecting route choice. Since intercity travel similarly involves destination selection and path constraints, this study extends the framework to transportation behavior analysis. By quantifying push–pull factors—such as population size and urban hierarchy—and intermediate barriers—such as geographic distance, administrative divisions, and cultural adaptation costs—this approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the spatio-temporal heterogeneity of intercity travel across different time periods.

The experiment conducts a comprehensive analysis of network structural characteristics, including central city analysis, community structure analysis, and exploration of modularity and clustering patterns. Central cities play pivotal roles within intercity travel networks, exerting significant influence over network dynamics. Degree centrality is used as a key metric for assessing city connectivity. Six representative cities are selected for analysis: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Hangzhou, and Chengdu, hereafter referred to as City1 through City6. These cities lie at the core of China’s major metropolitan regions and are characterized by large populations, high travel densities, and frequent intercity connections, making them both representative and generalizable. The edge weights between cities are standardized based on three years of population migration flows, with the strength of connections between network nodes reflecting actual transportation interaction frequency. In terms of community detection, City1 (Beijing) and City2 (Tianjin) are classified into Community1, reflecting the high integration level of the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region. City3 (Shanghai) and City4 (Hangzhou) form Community2, representing the Yangtze River Delta urban cluster. City5 (Guangzhou) and City6 (Shenzhen) constitute Community3, corresponding to the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area.

Prior studies indicate that the pandemic had a significantly greater impact on intercity high-speed rail networks compared to localized urban rail transit or conventional rail systems. Therefore, a stratified analysis by transport mode was conducted, comparing key structural indicators of the high-speed rail and conventional rail networks before and after the pandemic. This analysis revealed that during the pandemic, the average degree centrality of the high-speed rail network declined from 0.684 to 0.612—a decrease of 10.5%—while its modularity dropped from 0.4260 to 0.3980, reflecting a marked weakening of connections among city nodes. Discussion

Overall, the proposed optimized model demonstrates significant advantages in handling high-dimensional, structurally complex, and heterogeneous intercity travel data. This superiority is evident not only in its leading performance across individual evaluation metrics but also in its robust adaptability across three distinct travel scenarios. Compared with other advanced models—such as Transformer-based structured modeling approaches and graph neural network frameworks—the optimized model benefits from integrating complex network structural indicators with spatiotemporal features. This integration allows it to more effectively capture critical characteristics and node interrelations in travel mode classification, thereby improving both classification accuracy and stability. Furthermore, the model achieves a balanced performance between precision and recall, mitigating classification bias that can result from overemphasis on any single metric. This stability holds practical importance for traffic management and travel demand forecasting, as it ensures reliable recognition across diverse travel behaviors and supports informed decision-making within dynamic urban transportation systems.

Although this study demonstrates community partitioning, core city distribution, and node clustering structures through complex network analysis, it lacks a deeper exploration of the underlying socio-economic implications. For instance, the high centrality observed for City1 and City2 may stem not only from network metrics such as node degree and betweenness but also from external factors including regional economic dominance, infrastructure density, and policy orientations. Future research could enhance the practical utility of network indicators by incorporating social-spatial variables such as urban functional hierarchy, industrial distribution, and levels of public service provision, thereby improving their relevance for governance and policy formulation. Relatedly, prior literature has proposed a multi-level complex network analysis framework that integrates high-speed rail, intercity rail, conventional rail, and urban transit within a static spatial Durbin model to quantify the impact of multi-tier rail networks on regional economic equity28. In contrast, the CNT-RFC model developed in this study builds upon this multi-layer network concept while embedding dynamic modularity analysis to capture network structural reconfigurations during key periods such as holidays and weekends. This holiday-oriented network “remodeling” mechanism reveals the asymmetric effects of non-structural shocks—such as capacity saturation and policy interventions—on node centrality and travel mode evolution. By doing so, it addresses the limitations of static spatial analyses in managing short-term disturbances. For example, the observed “long-distance migration tendency” during holidays, which superficially reflects increased travel willingness, is reinterpreted through joint modeling of travel costs, average distances, and adaptation costs. This behavioral change appears to be driven more by structural migration caused by capacity saturation in short-distance corridors and policy guidance (e.g., free highway tolls, staggered traffic restrictions) rather than pure leisure travel demand. This dynamic explanation of abrupt network disturbances combined with social behavioral mechanisms represents a key innovation distinguishing the CNT-RFC model from existing static network models. Regarding model performance comparison, although this study experimentally benchmarks several state-of-the-art models, further investigation is warranted into the mechanisms underlying the relatively poorer performance of some models. For instance, deep neural network (DNN) models often struggle to capture complex nonlinear relationships without adequate feature engineering and parameter tuning, limiting their classification effectiveness. In contrast, the CNT-RFC model effectively addresses the uncertainty inherent in “black-box” learning by integrating network attributes, geographic features, and node behavior labels, thereby enhancing both interpretability and robustness.

In summary, while the CNT-RFC model demonstrates strong classification performance and structural recognition capabilities in this study, certain limitations remain. These include the need for improved model interpretability, unverified generalizability across different regions and extended time frames, and insufficient characterization of underlying causal mechanisms. Future research could incorporate transfer learning, causal graph modeling, and structural equation modeling to further enhance the model’s adaptability and explanatory power. Additionally, integrating insights from classical multi-level transportation network studies may help advance urban network research from static association analysis toward a paradigm focused on dynamic mechanism identification.

Conclusion

This study develops an optimized model, CNT-RFC, for intercity travel mode identification and complex network structure analysis, systematically evaluating its performance across multiple travel types, time periods, and network layers. By integrating complex network features with push–pull factors and intermediary resistance mechanisms, the proposed model not only achieves superior performance metrics but also enhances the interpretability of real-world travel behavior mechanisms. First, the optimized model demonstrates significant advantages and stability in identifying multiple travel modes. Experimental results show that the model’s accuracy exceeds 0.91 across three travel scenarios, with leisure travel achieving an accuracy of 0.947, precision of 0.928, recall of 0.922, and an F1 score of 0.947, outperforming traditional models such as FT-Transformer (accuracy 0.917) and Graphormer (F1 score 0.918). Further significance testing confirms statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) across all four evaluation metrics, with Cohen’s d values exceeding 3.5, indicating a large effect size and substantial practical improvement. Second, network structure analysis reveals dense intra-urban cluster connectivity alongside notable spatiotemporal heterogeneity. The model maintains robust performance despite pandemic disturbances, although different transportation network layers show varying levels of impact. Comparison of model metrics between the high-impact pandemic period of 2022 and the relatively normal periods of 2021 and 2023 shows changes in accuracy and F1 scores of less than 0.01, demonstrating temporal stability of the recognition system. However, layered analysis reveals that the average degree centrality of the high-speed rail network declined from 0.684 to 0.612, and modularity dropped by 0.028, while the conventional rail network experienced minimal structural change (centrality declined by only 0.013). These findings highlight the pronounced constraints on high-frequency long-distance travel during the pandemic and underscore the differential resilience of transportation system layers in the face of sudden public health emergencies.

While this study has achieved notable accomplishments, it also has inherent limitations. Although CNT and RFC algorithms yielded promising results, they may encounter challenges when applied to more intricate travel behavior patterns and larger datasets. Future research will explore advanced analytical methods and technologies, such as deep learning and big data analytics, to enhance the model’s performance and applicability. These approaches are expected to provide improved analytical capabilities and accuracy, particularly for managing larger datasets and addressing complex travel behaviors. Leveraging these advanced methodologies, future studies aim to further refine models to better understand and predict the complex dynamics within transportation systems.
