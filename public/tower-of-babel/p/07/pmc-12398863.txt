Effect of particle size variation of stabilized wheat germ on dough processing characteristics and steamed bread quality
Authors: Gongqi Zhao, Miaomiao Pan, Xianling Zhang, Gengqing Liao, Yizhou Wu, Yanxia Yang, Liping Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wheat germ is a by-product of wheat flour milling and processing, and is valued for being the nutrient-rich center of wheat grains. In this study, hot air-stabilized wheat germ with five different particle sizes (coarse, medium, fine, ultrafine, and very fine) was prepared and mixed with wheat flour (WF) in a ratio of 8:92. The study focused on the effect of particle size of stabilized wheat germ on the thermomechanical, dynamic rheological, and fermentation properties of wheat flour and the quality of steamed bread. The results revealed that the particle size of wheat germ affects the secondary structure of gluten proteins, as the contents of β-sheet and α-helix structures in dough containing very fine wheat germ particles increased to 29.16 % and 25.07 %, respectively. The addition of wheat germ significantly lower starch digestibility of steamed bread. In conclusion, the quality of steamed bread can be improved by controlling the particle size of wheat germ; the incorporation of very fine wheat germ enhanced the quality of steamed bread, while ultrafine grinding weakened the quality. This study serves as a reference for the development of wheat germ-steamed bread and contributes to the promotion of deep processing of wheat germ.

Introduction

Steamed bread is a staple food in Asia, accounting for 40 % of flour consumption, and is gaining popularity in North America, Europe, and South Africa (Shao, Yi, & Li, 2022). In addition, steamed bread contains lower levels of acrylamide and experiences less loss of soluble amino acids than traditional bread. However, in the past, as consumer preferences for product color and taste improved, steamed breads were often made from refined white wheat flour (WF), resulting in significant nutritional loss due to excessive processing. Consequently, conventional steamed bread made from WF has struggled to meet the criteria for a balanced diet. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore methods to enhance the nutrient content of steamed bread to satisfy the consumer demand for healthier food options.

Wheat germ, the nutritional core of wheat grains, is abundant in proteins and unsaturated fatty acids and contains a variety of trace elements, including minerals, flavonoids, essential amino acids, dietary fiber, and vitamin E. These components are known for their antioxidant, cholesterol-lowering, anti-hyperlipidemic, and anticancer properties (Boukid, Folloni, Ranieri, & Vittadini, 2018). However, wheat germ is primarily utilized as an animal feed, and its application in the food industry remains limited. This limitation arises from the high levels of lipase and lipoxygenase present in wheat germ, which readily react with lipids, leading to deterioration and adverse effects on storage and processing characteristics. Moreover, the high dietary fiber content of wheat germ may negatively affect the textural, microstructural, and sensory properties of food products. Therefore, it is essential to implement techniques for the pretreatment of wheat germ.

Based on our previous studies, hot-air treatment has been identified as the preferred stabilization method for wheat germ because it effectively inactivates lipase and lipoxygenase (Zhao et al., 2024). Although hot-air treatment enhanced the storage life of wheat germ, the adverse effects of wheat germ addition on dough processing characteristics and product quality remained significant. Tebben, Shen, and Li (2018) also reported that compared with WF, whole WF negatively affected the product attributes, resulting in decreased textural firmness and fermentation heights. This, in turn, leads to an increase in hardness and deterioration in color and flavor. The incorporation of additional raw materials significantly influences the processing characteristics of dough, including the diluting effect of fibers on gluten proteins and competition for water among proteins, fibers, and starches (Bilal et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). These factors ultimately impact product quality.

Several studies have reported that reducing the particle size of additives can enhance product quality (Heinio et al., 2016; Sun, Bu, Qiao, Drawbridge and Fang, 2023; Yin et al., 2024). Sun et al. (2023)found that the addition of large particle-size bran reduced the gluten strength of the dough, whereas flour with smaller particle-size bran additions produced harder bread. Jin et al. (2020) found that ultra-micromilling treatment enhanced the surface area of bran, resulting in stronger interactions with water and protein molecules within the dough matrix. This improves the smoothness of the bran dough and increases its extensibility. Qin et al. (2021) found that rice flour with finer particles exhibited improved pasting characteristics and rheological parameters, which subsequently enhanced leavening properties and adhesion in rice bread making. Most studies examining the effects of particle size on gluten network structure have focused on bran, rice, and whole wheat products. Ultra-fine grinding is often used for bran and whole wheat, which are high in dietary fiber and hard, to improve the processing characteristics and taste of the product. Whether finer particles can improve dough processing characteristics and product quality remains to be further investigated. Moreover, there is a notable lack of research on how the particle size of wheat germ affects the rheological properties of dough and texture of baked goods. It is hypothesized that the particle size of wheat germ flour may influence the quality of steamed bread and the smaller the particles, the more conducive it is to improving the processing characteristics of the dough.

Therefore, wheat germ flour with varying particle sizes was prepared by controlling milling time and method. The effects of wheat germ particle size on dough processing characteristics and steamed bread quality were systematically investigated in terms of the rheological, textural, and digestive properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

The high-gluten WF and yeast used in this study were purchased from local supermarkets, whereas wheat germ was generously provided by Anhui Fengbao Cereal and Oil Foodstuff (Group) Co., Ltd. (Anhui, China). The basic components were analyzed using methods approved by the American Association of Cereal Chemists (AACC, 2003; Tolouie, Mohammadifar, Ghomi, Yaghoubi and Hashemi, 2018). The results revealed that the protein, fat, ash, moisture, and crude fiber content of wheat germ were 35.57 %, 11.97 %, 4.93 %, 2.47 %, and 2.10 %, respectively. In contrast, the protein, fat, ash, moisture, and crude fiber content of WF were 20.01 %, 0.13 %, 0.04 %, 12.14 %, and 0.09 %, respectively.

The following compounds were used in this study: Porcine pancreatic α-amylase (40–60 U/mg) (1266151) and amyloglucosidase (100,000 U/g) (87572152) were purchased from Shanghai Original Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All reagents were purchased from China National Pharmaceutical Group Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China): anhydrous ethanol (702); potassium bromide (253877); urea(1176); guanidine hydrochloride(5742); ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (6254); trichloroacetic acid (6421); Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminoethane (15712952); glycine (750); sodium acetate (517045); 3 5-Dinitrosalicylic acid (11873). All reagents were of analytical grade.

Sample preparation

The wheat germ was treated in an electric blast drying oven (MD-86 L506, Hefei Valin Co., Ltd., Hefei, China) at 120 °C for 30 min, following a previously reported method (Zhao et al., 2024). After cooling to room temperature, wheat germ was ground using a high-speed multifunctional pulverizer (RHP-600, Ronghao Industry & Trade Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China) for 5, 10, 15, and 25 s. The grinding duration was determined through pre-testing to ensure that the resulting samples could pass through 20, 40, 60, and 100 mesh sieves and were categorized as coarse wheat germ (CWG), medium wheat germ (MWG), fine wheat germ (FWG), and very fine wheat germ (VWG), respectively. Smaller wheat germ particles were produced using a high-energy nanoimpact mill (CJM-SY-B, Qinhuangdao Bomao New Material Technology Co., Ltd., Hebei, China), and the resulting samples were passed through a 300-mesh sieve and labeled ultrafine wheat germ (UWG).

Particle size distribution analysis

The particle size distributions of wheat germ and WF were determined using a Master 2000 laser diffraction particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) following the method described by Yang et al. (2022).

Preparation of mixed flour

Five blends were obtained by mixing CWG, MWG, FWG, VWG, and UWG with WF at a rate of 8 g per 100 g. They were labeled CWGF, MWGF, FWGF, VWGF, and UWGF. WF was used as the control.

Water retention capacity (WRC)

The WRC of the flour mixture was tested using the method described by Yang et al. (2022). The sample (3 g) was mixed well with 30 mL of distilled water in a centrifuge tube and maintained at 30 °C for 30 min. After centrifugated at 4000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatant was removed. Weight was used to calculate the WRC according to the following formula:(1)WRCg/g=W1−W2/W2where W1 is the weight of the flour and water after centrifugation and W2 is the weight of the flour sample.

Pasting analysis

The viscosity of the mixed flour was determined using a rapid viscosity analyzer (RVA, Super3 Newport Co. Ltd., Australia). The 3 g of flour (dry basis) and 25 mL of distilled water were placed in an aluminum sample container and mixed thoroughly. The heating program was as follows: the sample was held at 50 °C for 10 s, heated to 95 °C at a uniform rate of 12 °C/min, cooled to 50 °C at a uniform rate of 12 °C/min, and held at 50 °C for 2.5 min.

Thermo-mechanical properties analysis of mixed flours

The thermomechanical properties of dough were evaluated using the method described by Jiang, Wang, and Zhou (2023). A 75 g sample of dough was prepared by mixing blended flour with distilled water using a Mixolab analyzer (MIXOLAB2, Chopin Technologies France, Paris, France) and subjected to secondary mixing at 80 rpm. The thermomechanical properties of the dough were assessed and the relevant parameters were recorded.

Preparation of dough

The mixed flour prepared as described in Section 2.4 was combined with distilled water in a separate process, maintaining a flour-to-water ratio of 2:1. Yeast was incorporated at a concentration of 1 g per 100 g of flour. The mixture was thoroughly blended to create smooth and homogeneous dough. The dough was then placed in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (LHP-160; Sanfa Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 37 °C and 75 % humidity for 30 min. After incubation, the dough was pressed to remove air and divided into 50 g portions to form steamed bread embryos. A portion of the fermented dough was further divided into 10 g sizes and frozen in a refrigerator (MD-86 L506, Hefei Valin Co., Ltd., Hefei, China) at −80 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were dried in a freeze-dryer (LGJ-10FD, Songwon Freeze-Drying, Beijing, China) for 48 h for use in the subsequent experiments. The doughs prepared with CWGF, MWGF, FWGF, VWGF, and UWGF were designated CWGD, MWGD, FWGD, VWGD, and UWGD, respectively. The dough made from WF was labeled WD.

Dynamic rheological analysis of dough

Dough prepared without yeast using the method described in Section 2.7, was tested according to the method described by Kuang, Ma, Pu, Huang and Xiong, 2021. Five grams of dough were placed on the platform of a rheometer (HAAKE RheoStress 6000, Thermo Scientific, USA). The fixture was lowered to a height of 2 mm and any excess dough was removed. Sweep tests were conducted at 25 °C within a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz.

Fermentation characteristics

Dough fermentation characteristics were assessed using an F4 rheological fermenter (RheoF4; Chopin Technologies, Villeneuve, France). The parameters were as follows: 315 g of dough prepared according to Section 2.8, fermented at 30 °C for 1.5 h, and with a resistance weight of 2 kg. The maximum height of fermentation (Hm), maximum height of gas production (H'm), volume of gas production (Vt), and volume of gas retention (Vr) were recorded and the gas retention coefficient was calculated.

Protein secondary structure analysis of dough

Data were recorded after measurement using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR 850, Tianjin Gangdong Science and Technology, Tianjin, China). The secondary structure of the protein was analyzed according to the methodology outlined by Wang, Gou et al. (2023), with slight modifications. Freeze-dried dough, prepared as described in Section 2.8, was ground and passed through a 100-mesh sieve. The mass ratio of the sample to potassium bromide was set to 1:100, and the scanning wave number ranged from 4000 to 400 cm−1. The amide I band spectrum (1600–1700 cm−1) was selected to characterize the secondary structure of the protein. Gaussian smoothing, baseline calibration, inverse convolution processing, and second-order derivative fitting were conducted using Peakfit 4.12 software to determine the content of the secondary structure.

Analysis of free sulfhydryl groups and disulfide bonds (S—S) in dough

The free sulfhydryl and S—S contents of the fermented dough were determined using the colorimetric method with Ellman's reagent as described by Wu et al. (2022), with some modifications. Lyophilized fermented dough (15 mg) was mixed with 1 mL Tris-glycine buffer (pH 8), followed by the addition of 4.7 g of solid guanidine hydrochloride. The volume of the mixture was adjusted to 10 mL using Tris-glycine buffer. To determine the free sulfhydryl and total sulfhydryl content, 1 mL of the prepared solution was mixed with 4 mL of an 8 mol/L urea-guanidine hydrochloride solution and 0.05 mL of mercaptoethanol (4 mg/mL) in a volumetric flask. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (SP-752, Shanghai Spectrum Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).(2)FreeSHμmol/g=73.53A412×D1/C(3)TotalSHμmol/g=73.53A412×D2/C(4)S−Sμmol/g=TotalSH−FreeSH/2where A412 represents the absorbance at 412 nm, D represents the dilution factor (D1 is 5.02 and D2 is 10), and C represents the sample concentration (mg/mL).

Microstructure analysis

A small piece of lyophilized fermented dough was placed in a sample holder and subjected to gold sputtering for 10 min. The microstructure of the dough was examined using scanning electron microscopy (EVO-18, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany), and micrographs were captured at a magnification of 2000 × .

Preparation of steamed breads

Steamed bread embryos prepared in a 2.8 % solution were allowed to rise in a constant temperature and humidity incubator at 37 °C and 75 % humidity for 10 min. Steamed bread was cooked in a steamer for 30 min to obtain the final sample. The steamed breads prepared using CWGD, MWGD, FWGD, VWGD, and UWGD were labeled CWGSB, MWGSB, FWGSB, VWGSB, and UWGSB, respectively. The steamed bread prepared using WD was labeled as WSB.

Quality analysis of steamed bread

The specific volume of steamed bread was calculated according to the methodology outlined by Zhao, She, et al. (2024) and was expressed as the ratio of volume (mL) to weight (g) of steamed bread. A texture analyzer (CT3-10 K, Brookfield, Massachusetts, USA) was used to assess the textural properties of steamed bread using a TA 4/1000 cylindrical probe.

In-vitro digestion of steamed bread

The method described by Wang, Gou et al. (2023) was used with slight modifications. The sample (0.1 g) was first mixed with 15 mL of sodium acetate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 5.2) and placed in a conical flask. After stirring, the sample mixture was completely gelatinized in a boiling water bath for 30 min. The samples were then cooled to 37 °C, and 5 mL of a mixed enzyme solution (290 U/mL α-amylase and 15 U/mL saccharase) was added to the conical flask. The samples were subsequently placed in a water bath shaker at 37 °C. An aliquot of 500 μL of the hydrolyzed solution was taken at intervals of 0, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 180 min, transferred into a 5 mL centrifuge tube containing 2 mL of anhydrous ethanol solution, and immediately mixed to inactivate the enzyme. The absorbance of glucose was measured at 540 nm using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid method.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation of three replicate measurements. All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 27 and graphically plotted using Origin version 2025. Duncan's multiple range test was conducted at a significance level of p < 0.05 for analysis.

WRC

Fig. 1 illustrates the WRC of wheat germ flour mixtures. The incorporation of wheat germ enhanced the WRC of the WF. This improvement is primarily attributed to the higher number of hydrogen bonds present in the dietary fibers of wheat germ, which facilitates greater water retention through interactions with these bonds (Yang et al., 2022). Notably, as the particle size decreased, the WRC initially increased and then decreased. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that a reduction in particle size leads to the fragmentation of dietary fibers, which exposes more hydrogen bonds and enhances water interaction, thereby increasing WRC. However, with a further reduction in particle size, the dietary fibers in the UWG sample became significantly damaged, resulting in a decrease in WRC (Zhu, Huang, Peng, Qian, & Zhou, 2010).Fig. 1Water retention capacity (WRC) of wheat germ flour mixture. Pasting properties

Table 2 presents the pasting characteristics of the wheat germ mixed flours. The addition of wheat germ increased the viscosity of the mixed flour compared with that of WF. This enhancement may be attributed to the abundant proteins and fats in wheat germ, which interact with starch during heating to form a network structure, thereby increasing the peak viscosity. CWGF exhibited the highest final viscosity of 2098.00 cP. This can be explained by the fact that larger particles require a longer time to gelatinize, resulting in a higher viscosity than smaller particles (Hasjim, Li, & Dhital, 2013). In contrast, UWGF exhibited the lowest peak viscosity at 1484.50 cP and final viscosity at 2074.00 cP. This reduction may be attributed to the disruption of the protein structure during the ultra-micromilling process. The breakdown value of the blended flour was lower than that of WF. This phenomenon may occur because proteins in wheat germ are highly sticky and adhere to the surfaces of discrete starch granules during heating to form a sticky film, which can inhibit starch granule fragmentation (Kuang et al., 2021). The setback values of the mixed flours were all elevated, indicating an increased likelihood of retrogradation of the starch paste and a decrease in storage stability. A similar phenomenon was reported by Zhao, She, et al. (2024), in which the setback value increased with decreasing particle size. The starch paste with MWGF exhibited the lowest setback value, indicating that it was more stable. In addition, the incorporation of wheat germ increased the pasting temperature of the WF. This increase may be due to the inhibition of starch granule swelling by the complexes formed between the fat in wheat germ and the straight-chain starch. Thermo-mechanical properties

Table 3 presents the thermomechanical properties of wheat germ mixed flour. The particle size of wheat germ flour did not significantly affect the WF water absorption. However, the incorporation of wheat germ influenced the stabilization time. The stabilization time for WF was 8.85 min, whereas the addition of wheat germ flour reduced the dough stabilization time to between 4.68 and 3.55 min, indicating a decreased ability to withstand dough mixing. Notably, no further reductions in stabilization time were observed with smaller particle sizes in the UWGF samples. The dietary fiber present in wheat germ can positively affect the mechanical strength of dough through hydration, which can partially counteract the gluten dilution effect caused by the addition of wheat germ (Jiang et al., 2023). However, finer particles exerted a less detrimental effect on gluten network formation within the dough. C1 refers to dough formation time, which was found to decrease with the addition of wheat germ. This reduction may be attributed to competition for moisture between the proteins and dietary fibers in wheat germ and WF, leading to a shorter dough formation time. The incorporation of wheat germ-diluted gluten proteins resulted in decreased dough strength and C2 values. Finer particles had a lesser impact on the structure of the gluten network. C3–C4 values indicate the stability of the thermogel and endogenous amylase (Yang et al., 2022). A lower C3–C4 value signifies a reduced disintegration of starch and a more stable starch gel. The stability of the starch paste improved as the size of germ particles decreased. The C3–C4 value for VWGF was only 0.10 N/m, indicating a more stable starch paste. However, the C3–C4 value of UWGF increased to 0.15 N/m, indicating a decrease in the stability of the starch gel. This shows that the conclusion proposed in 3.1, that smaller particle sizes are better for dough processing characteristics, is not absolute. C5 represents the maximum torque during the cooling phase, which is influenced by starch aging. Smaller particle sizes correspond to lower C5 values. Dynamic rheological properties

The results of the frequency scan tests presented in Fig. 2 indicate that G′ consistently exceeded G″, with the tan δ value remaining <1 throughout the frequency scan interval, thus demonstrating solid-like properties. Compared with wheat dough, mixed dough exhibits greater viscoelasticity, likely owing to the binding of protein molecules from wheat germ to starch polymer chains (Kuang et al., 2021). Among the samples, MWGF exhibited the highest apparent viscosity, which facilitated the formation of a stable and ordered gluten network. However, the G′ and G″ values of the UWGF samples decreased. This reduction may be attributed to the increased surface area of the particles, which enhances interactions with the gluten network, thereby negatively affecting the gluten network formation. The tan δ value, defined as the ratio of G″ to G′, was higher for the mixed flours than for WF, indicating that the addition of wheat germ diminished the strength of the wheat dough and resulted in a homogeneous, high-fluidity system that improved dough stability. The UWGF samples demonstrated an increase in dough strength similar to that of WF, suggesting a tendency to form a more elastic system and enhance dough tensile properties. More suitable for products that require high extensibility, such as noodles and dumpling wrappers. Nevertheless, tan δ increased for all doughs as the frequency increased, indicating that viscous characteristics predominated in the doughs, whereas elastic characteristics diminished at higher frequencies (Iuga, Boestean, Ghendov-Mosanu, & Mironeasa, 2020). The addition of wheat germ loosens the gluten network, making the dough more fluid, while elasticity cannot effectively store energy at high frequencies due to reduced cross-linking.Fig. 2Dynamic rheological properties of mixed dough. Rheological properties of fermentation

Table 4 presents the dynamic rheological properties of the dough. The Hm is a critical criterion for evaluating the quality of a fermented dough. The incorporation of wheat germ, regardless of particle size, significantly decreased dough Hm (P < 0.05). This reduction may be attributed to the presence of proteins and fats in wheat germ, which disrupt the formation of the gluten network and inhibit the ductility of the dough (Huang et al., 2019). For MWGD, the results showed that the incorporation of wheat germ with medium particle size significantly reduced the Hm of wheat dough from 35.90 mm to 26.65 mm. However, the adverse effects of wheat germ on dough fermentation characteristics diminished as the particle size decreased. The VWGD sample exhibited the highest Hm value of 30.15 mm. This may be due to the increased intensity and duration of milling, which releases nutrients, such as proteins and carbohydrates, thereby providing more energy for yeast activity. In addition, wheat germ proteins hydrolyze, unfold, and subsequently crosslink through hydrogen bonds, which enhance the formation of the gluten network (Marti et al., 2014). The decline in Hm observed in the UWGD sample, which reached its lowest level (26.05 mm, aligns with the deterioration in dough rheology. As the particle size decreased, the surface area of the particles increased, leading to a greater number of interactions with the gluten network and resulting in a weaker gluten structure. The gas retention factor is defined as the ratio of Vr to Vt. The CWGD sample exhibited the highest gas retention factor (76.55 %). However, this does not necessarily indicate superior fermentation characteristics, as the high retention of dietary fiber in the large wheat germ, combined with proteins and lipids, forms a film on the dough surface. Protein secondary structure

Fig. 3 illustrates the secondary structure of dough protein. Deconvolution and quadratic derivation of the FTIR spectra revealed the protein secondary structure of the dough within the amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1). Specific regions of the amide I band were assigned to distinct protein secondary structures: α-helices (1651–1660 cm−1), β-sheets (1610–1640 cm−1), β-turns (1660–1700 cm−1), and random coils (1640–1650 cm−1) (Wang et al., 2017). As depicted in Fig. 3, the addition of wheat germ with varying particle sizes had a minimal effect on α-helices and random coils, whereas it significantly influenced β-sheets and β-turns in the mixed flour dough compared with the control group. The formation of stable secondary structures is contingent upon the degree of gluten protein polymerization, which is influenced by flour composition, hydration level, and specific mixing process. β-sheets are the preferred structure for the hydration and water diffusion of wheat gluten proteins and are regarded as the most stable protein conformation. However, the incorporation of wheat germ diminished the β-sheet content of WF. This reduction may be attributed to competition for water between wheat germ and gluten proteins, which inhibits the hydration and water diffusion of wheat gluten, leading to the transformation of β-sheets into other non-covalently bonded structures (Wang, Guo, et al., 2023). Notably, as the particle size of wheat germ decreased, the β-sheet content of the VWGD sample increased to 29.16 %. This increase may result from a reduction in water competition between wheat germ and gluten proteins owing to the smaller particle size. Proteins in wheat germ and gluten were weakened with a decrease in particle size. In addition, proteins in wheat germ promoted increased formation of S—S bonds, which facilitated the development of β-sheet structures in the glutenin structure. These findings suggest that cross-linking wheat gluten with very fine wheat germ particles modifies the protein secondary structure of dough and enhances the internal dense structure of the gluten network. The α-helix maintained a relatively stable conformation, and among all samples, the VWGD samples exhibited the highest relative content of β-sheets and α-helices, indicating the most stable gluten network structure. However, with a further reduction in wheat germ particle size (UWGD), the beneficial effect of wheat germ on the mixed flour dough diminished, likely because the smaller particles increased the number of interactions with the gluten network, which compromised the orderliness of the gluten structure.Fig. 3Protein secondary structure of mixed dough. Free sulfhydryl groups and S—S bonds

Fig. 4 illustrates the free sulfhydryl groups and S—S bonds present in the dough. S—S bonds are essential for maintaining the viscoelasticity of dough and stabilizing the gluten network structure (Wu et al., 2022). The incorporation of wheat germ resulted in decreased disulfide bond content and increased free sulfhydryl content of WF. Specifically, the S—S bond content of the CWGD samples was reduced to 21.99 μmol/g. This reduction may be attributed to the presence of large wheat germ particles, which likely make the S—S between the glutenin chains more susceptible to damage, leading to their conversion into free sulfhydryl groups (Fan et al., 2024). However, as the particle size of the additive wheat germ gradually decreased, the free sulfhydryl content remained relatively unchanged, whereas the content of S—S bonds exhibited fluctuations. MWGD samples demonstrated the highest disulfide bond content, measuring 25.57 μmol/g. S—S bonds are the dominant factor determining whether gluten networks can form and maintain their three-dimensional framework, while protein secondary structures affect the extensibility of gluten networks (Wu et al., 2022). Interestingly, despite the increase in S—S bond content in MWGD samples, there was a decrease in fermentation characteristics and destruction of the gluten network. This phenomenon may be due to the fact that although the MWGD sample has a high S—S bond content, which forms an elastic three-dimensional network, the orderliness of the protein secondary structure is reduced, hindering the extensibility of the gluten network and reducing gas retention capacity, ultimately leading to a decline in dough fermentation performance. In contrast, the VWGD samples exhibited increased S—S bond content, enhanced dough stability, and improved fermentation performance. This improvement may be due to the increased proportion of β-sheets and α-helical-ordered structures within the mixed doughs, which are connected by S—S bonds, resulting in a more stable and organized gluten network structure.Fig. 4Free sulfhydryl and S—S bond contents of the mixed dough. Microstructure of dough

As illustrated in Fig. 5, a gluten-protein film with a protein-starch matrix was affixed to the surface of WF dough. The pores in the air chamber result from the lack of ductility in the protein-starch matrix and surface tension of the liquid film during fermentation (Zhao et al., 2024). The proteins and fats in the CWGD samples interacted with the starch granules, leading to the firm embedding of the starch granules within the gluten network. However, the ductility of the gluten network was inadequate, resulting in non-uniform pore sizes. In the MWGD samples, the gluten matrix was significantly ruptured, likely because of increased moisture competition between medium-sized wheat germ and WF. This finding aligns with the WRC and the MIXOLAB experimental results. Nevertheless, the disruption of the gluten network by wheat germ gradually decreased as the particle size decreased, leading to a denser gluten network structure, a reduction in dough pore size, and a more complete encapsulation of starch, particularly in the VWGD sample. Conversely, the UWGD samples exhibited an increased number of interactions between the wheat germ particles and the gluten network, which adversely affected the gluten network and ultimately resulted in decreased ductility of the gluten matrix (Yang et al., 2022).Fig. 5Structure of the gluten network in mixed dough. Quality of steamed bread

As shown in the data presented in Table 5, the addition of large wheat germ particles decreased the specific volume of the mixed steamed breads to 1.87 mL/g. This reduction may be attributed to the adverse effects of wheat germ particles on the stomatal stability of steamed bread, leading to the rupture of stomata, and consequently, a decrease in the specific volume. Conversely, the specific volume of the FWGSB increased to 1.95 mL/g as the particle size decreased. VWGSB and UWGSB exhibited the largest specific volumes, both reaching 2.03 mL/g. This improvement may be due to an increase in the degree of cross-linking between the particles and the gluten network after particle size reduction, enhancing the ability to retain gas and thereby increasing the specific volume (Gómez & Román, 2018). The hardness, cohesiveness, springiness, chewiness, and gumminess of steamed bread are critical indices for evaluating its quality. Variations in the texture test data indicated changes in the structure of the steamed bread. The incorporation of wheat germ increased hardness, chewiness, and gumminess, whereas cohesiveness and springiness decreased in steamed wheat bread. This phenomenon may be attributed to the dietary fiber present in wheat germ, which disrupts the internal structure of steamed bread and competes with wheat starch for water, thereby reducing the degree of hydration. Initially, the hardness of the steamed bread increased but subsequently decreased as the particle size decreased. Elasticity is a vital index for characterizing the air-holding capacity of steamed bread (Zhao et al., 2021). As the particle size of the added wheat germ gradually decreased, the hardness of the VWGSB samples decreased to 5619.00 g, whereas the elasticity increased to 23.94 mm. This change was positively correlated with the degree of order and fermentation characteristics of the dough. In-vitro digestion

Fig. 6 illustrates the rate of starch hydrolysis in wheat germ-blended steamed bread with various particle sizes. Glucose is released during food digestion in the stomach, which provides the body with a substantial source of energy. However, excessive and rapid glucose intake can lead to health issues, such as obesity and diabetes. Consequently, slow-digestion foods have gained in popularity (Wang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 6, the incorporation of wheat germ significantly reduced the rate of starch hydrolysis and glucose release from the steamed wheat bread. The hydrolysis rate of starch and glucose release content of the mixed flour steamed bread gradually decreased as the particle size decreased. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that smaller particle sizes increase the specific surface area of wheat germ particles, thereby exposing more enzyme-binding sites. This increased exposure inhibited the ability of α-amylase to bind to wheat starch. In addition, dietary fibers exhibit an adsorptive effect on glucose, further inhibiting the rate of starch hydrolysis (Guo et al., 2022). Interestingly, the starch hydrolysis rate and glucose release content of VWGSB and UWGSB gradually increased as the particle size of wheat germ decreased. This may be because smaller particle sizes facilitate the hydrolysis of wheat germ particles, whereas the reduced glucose adsorption capacity associated with smaller particles leads to an increased rate of starch hydrolysis. However, the overall starch hydrolysis rate and glucose release content of the mixed steamed bread with added wheat germ were lower than those of plain wheat steamed bread.Fig. 6In vitro digestion of steamed bread. Conclusion

This study examined the effects of varying the particle size of wheat germ on gluten network structure during steamed bread processing. Scanning electron microscopy observations indicated that the incorporation of larger wheat germ particles disrupted the gluten network structure of the dough. This disruption gradually diminished as the particle size decreased. In the case of VWGD, the stability of the secondary structure of dough proteins improved with an increase in the β-lamellae and α-helical conformations. This enhancement suggested that the degree of order and hydration within the dough improved. Concurrently, the content of S—S bonds in the dough also increased, indicating a more homogeneous gluten network, which facilitated the extension of gluten network strands during fermentation. The improved resilience and chewiness, along with the reduced digestibility of the VWGSB samples, enhanced consumer acceptance and were beneficial to human health. These findings suggest that the relationship between wheat germ flour particle size and product quality is complex. The research results proved that the hypothesis was not entirely correct. When the wheat germ particle size was reduced to very fine, the dough and steamed bread quality was optimal, but when it was reduced to ultrafine, the dough and steamed bread quality declined. In conclusion, this study offers new insights into enhancing the processing characteristics of dough and the quality of steamed bread made from wheat germ. In addition, it provides a new perspective on the application of wheat germ as a functional ingredient in alternative grain systems.
