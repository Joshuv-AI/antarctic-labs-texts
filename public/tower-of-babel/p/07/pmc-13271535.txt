Human Intestinal Spirochetosis Shows Higher Positivity in Sessile Serrated Lesions Than in Tubular Adenomas With Preferential Colonization of Absorptive Epithelium
Authors: Hisanori Matoba, Mai Iwaya, Chifumi Fujii, Masatomo Kawakubo, Noriyasu Kobayashi, Haruka Kuribayashi, Jun Nakayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFactors underlying differential colonization by human intestinal spirochetosis (HIS) of various colorectal neoplastic lesions remain unclear. We compared HIS positivity and colonization patterns between sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) and tubular adenomas (TAs) and examined the relationship between spirochetal distribution and epithelial differentiation. HIS positivity was significantly higher in SSLs than TAs at both lesion (26/590 [4.4%] vs. 3/536 [0.6%], p < 0.001) and case (16/421 [3.8%] vs. 1/171 [0.6%], p = 0.03) levels. Because the SSL and TA main cohorts covered different periods, nine HIS‐positive SSL cases harboring TAs diagnosed outside the TA cohort period were separately analyzed for within‐case comparison, confirming significantly higher HIS positivity in SSLs than TAs (11/12 [91.7%] vs. 6/23 [26.1%], p = 0.003). Among HIS‐positive lesions, both SSLs and TAs showed reduced colonization at lesion sites compared to surrounding mucosa, with greater reduction in TAs. GS‐II staining demonstrated reduced terminal βGlcNAc on HIS bacterial bodies at SSL lesion sites. HIS preferentially colonized absorptive epithelium, although less evidently in TAs with limited absorptive differentiation. All cases harbored only Brachyspira aalborgi. These results indicate that HIS exhibits universal affinity for absorptive epithelium and that higher HIS positivity in SSLs reflects their greater abundance of absorptive epithelium.

Introduction

Human intestinal spirochetosis (HIS) is a colorectal infection caused by Brachyspira aalborgi or B. pilosicoli. Although HIS is often discovered as an incidental finding [1, 2], it can cause colitis and diarrhea in some individuals [3]. The relationship between HIS and colorectal neoplastic lesions remains controversial. Studies suggest decreased HIS colonization in tubular adenomas (TAs), possibly due to changes in microvilli, which serve as HIS attachment sites [4, 5, 6, 7]; however, systematic analysis is still lacking. For sessile serrated lesions (SSLs), one Japanese study reported higher HIS positivity [8], while an Australian study found no association [9]. These conflicting findings may partly reflect methodological differences, as both studies focused only on the presence or absence of HIS without quantitatively assessing colonization patterns.

Griffonia simplicifolia‐II (GS‐II) is a lectin specific to terminal α/βGlcNAc residues [10]. Using GS‐II‐horseradish peroxidase (HRP) staining and anti‐αGlcNAc immunostaining, we previously reported that both SSL mucins and HIS bacterial bodies possess terminal βGlcNAc, whereas TAs show limited βGlcNAc glycosylation restricted to the Golgi apparatus without secreted mucin positivity [11, 12]. Therefore, these differences in epithelial composition and glycosylation between SSLs and TAs may underlie distinct HIS colonization patterns.

Here, we systematically analyzed HIS colonization in SSLs and TAs by comparing HIS positivity at both lesion and case levels and quantitatively assessing spirochetal distribution not only at lesion sites but also in the surrounding mucosa. Our goal was to define mechanisms underlying differential HIS colonization by integrating our findings with observations of epithelial differentiation and bacterial glycosylation.

Materials and Methods

Case Selection

SSLs diagnosed between April 2016 and March 2024 and TAs diagnosed between November 2023 and March 2024 were retrospectively identified in the pathology archive of North Alps Medical Center Azumi Hospital, Japan. The main cohort included a total of 590 SSLs from 421 cases and 536 TAs from 171 cases. Histological diagnosis of SSLs and TAs was made following WHO Classification of Tumours, 5th edition, Volume 1, Digestive System Tumours [13]. Because none of the HIS‐positive TAs in the main cohort were obtained from cases concurrently harboring SSLs, we also analyzed HIS‐positive SSL cases diagnosed before the TA main cohort period to identify cases harboring both SSLs and TAs. This supplementary analysis identified nine cases with coexisting SSLs and TAs, including 12 SSL lesions and 23 TA lesions, which were used for within‐case comparison of HIS colonization between the two lesion types.

The study protocol, including the use of retrospective tissue samples obtained from North Alps Medical Center Azumi Hospital, was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shinshu University (no. 6193; June 2024). All procedures for studies using human tissues were in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of archival tissue samples. The privacy rights of human subjects were observed, and all data were anonymized.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and GS‐II‐HRP Staining

Immunohistochemistry for anti‐Treponema and anti‐αGlcNAc (clone HIK1083), and GS‐II‐HRP staining were performed as previously described [11, 12]. Antibodies and reagents are listed in Supplementary Table 1. HIS positivity was initially screened by morphological identification of spirochetes on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Alcian blue/periodic acid‐Schiff (AB/PAS) staining; cases identified as positive or equivocal were then subjected to anti‐Treponema immunostaining for confirmation. In most cases, spirochetes formed a characteristic PAS‐positive false brush border. In cases with low bacterial load and lacking distinct fringe formation, positivity was established when both PAS positivity and anti‐Treponema immunoreactivity were present on the epithelial surface. HIS positivity was evaluated at lesion and case levels. At the lesion level, HIS colonization at lesion sites and surrounding mucosa was evaluated separately. At the case level, a case was judged to be HIS‐positive when at least one lesion of the corresponding type showed HIS positivity. Case‐level HIS positivity was evaluated separately for SSLs and TAs. In HIS‐positive lesions, the proportion of epithelium colonized by spirochetes and the proportion of GS‐II‐positive bacterial bodies were quantified. HIS‐positive lesions were further classified according to the distribution pattern of spirochetes at the lesion site. Lesions were categorized as “typical” when ≥ 70% of spirochetes were located on absorptive epithelium. Within this category, two variants were recognized: “overflow,” defined as a high bacterial load (≥ 80% of the lesion site colonized) with spillover onto adjacent mucinous epithelium, and “sparse,” defined as a low bacterial load (≤ 10% of the lesion site colonized). Lesions were categorized as “other” when spirochetes showed no clear preferential colonization of absorptive epithelium, or when limited absorptive differentiation within the lesion precluded reliable assessment. Two authors (H.M., M.I.) reviewed staining and reached a consensus.

DNA Extraction, PCR Amplification, and Direct Sequencing of NADPH Oxidase (nox) Genes

DNA extraction from formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue sections and nested PCR amplification of B. aalborgi‐ and B. pilosicoli‐specific nox genes were performed as previously described [12]. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Sanger sequencing of PCR products was performed by Azenta Life Sciences (Tokyo, Japan) to determine the infecting Brachyspira species.

Statistical Analysis

Clinicopathological characteristics were compared using Fisher's exact tests (categorical variables) or unpaired t‐tests (age). HIS positivity between SSLs and TAs was compared using Fisher's exact tests at lesion and case levels. For within‐case comparisons, mixed‐effects logistic regression was used with lesion type as a fixed effect and case as a random effect. HIS positivity between lesion sites and surrounding mucosa within TAs was compared using exact McNemar tests. In HIS‐positive lesions, the proportion of epithelium colonized by spirochetes and the proportion of GS‐II‐positive bacterial bodies were compared between lesion sites and surrounding mucosa using paired t‐tests. Lesions without evaluable surrounding mucosa were excluded from these analyses. The differences in the proportion of colonized epithelium between lesion sites and surrounding mucosa in SSLs and TAs were compared using unpaired t‐tests.

Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.37 [14] (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, version 3.4.1).

Results

HIS Positivity in SSLs and TAs

Clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Cases with TAs were significantly older than those with SSLs (mean age: 69.1 vs. 65.6 years, p = 0.002), and SSLs were predominantly right‐sided (cecum, ascending, and transverse colon: 72.2%). At the lesion level, HIS was detected significantly more frequently in SSLs than in TAs (26/590 [4.4%] vs. 3/536 [0.6%], p < 0.001) (Table 2A). At the case level, HIS positivity was also significantly higher in SSLs than in TAs (16/421 [3.8%] vs. 1/171 [0.6%], p = 0.03) (Table 2A). HIS positivity in SSLs did not differ significantly among months (p = 0.31) (Supplementary Table 3A) and fiscal years (p = 0.92) (Supplementary Table 3B). Among the 16 HIS‐positive SSL cases, 12 (75.0%) were male, which was not significantly different from HIS‐negative cases (252/405 [62.2%], p = 0.43) (Supplementary Table 3C). HIS‐positive cases were significantly younger than HIS‐negative cases (mean age 57.6 vs. 65.9 years, p = 0.010) (Supplementary Table 3C). The anatomic distribution of HIS‐positive and HIS‐negative SSL lesions did not differ significantly (p = 0.20) (Supplementary Table 3D). For TAs, only one case was HIS‐positive in the main cohort, precluding meaningful analysis of case characteristics. The TA main cohort spanned a shorter period than the SSL main cohort, and no HIS‐positive TAs were found in cases that also harbored SSLs during this period. We therefore analyzed nine cases with HIS‐positive SSLs that also harbored TAs diagnosed outside this period for within‐case comparison (12 SSL and 23 TA lesions; Table 2B). In these cases, mixed‐effects logistic regression analysis demonstrated significantly higher HIS positivity in SSLs than TAs (11/12 [91.7%] vs. 6/23 [26.1%]; odds ratio 31.2, 95% CI 3.3‐295.3, p = 0.003) (Table 2B). In the main cohort, all HIS‐positive lesions with evaluable surrounding mucosa showed colonization at both lesion sites and the surrounding mucosa (SSL: 23/23; TA: 2/2). One additional TA, classified as HIS‐negative, showed HIS colonization in surrounding mucosa only without lesion site colonization. In contrast, among TAs in cases harboring both SSLs and TAs, HIS was detected significantly more frequently in surrounding mucosa than at lesion sites (16/23 [69.6%] vs. 6/23 [26.1%], p = 0.002) (Table 2C). Ten of 23 TA lesions (43.5%) showed HIS colonization in surrounding mucosa but not at lesion sites. This ‘surrounding mucosa‐positive and lesion site‐negative’ pattern was observed exclusively in TAs and not in any SSL lesions within these cases.

These results indicate that HIS positivity is higher in SSLs than in TAs, and that TA lesion sites are selectively unfavorable for HIS colonization.

The Proportion of HIS‐Colonized Epithelium in HIS‐Positive Lesions

We next analyzed the proportion of epithelium colonized by spirochetes in HIS‐positive lesions. That analysis included 23 SSLs and 8 TAs (combining the main cohort with concurrent cases) with evaluable surrounding mucosa.

In both SSLs and TAs, the proportion of HIS‐colonized epithelium was consistently higher in the surrounding mucosa than at the corresponding lesion sites (SSLs: p < 0.001; TAs: p = 0.001), as demonstrated by paired comparisons within individual lesions (Figure 1B,E,H,K; Figure 2A). To compare the magnitude of the decrease in colonization between lesion types, we calculated the difference in the proportion of colonized epithelium between lesion sites and surrounding mucosa (Δ = lesion site − surrounding mucosa) for each lesion and found that the decrease in colonization at lesion sites was significantly greater in TAs than in SSLs (p < 0.001) (Figure 2B).

The Proportion of GS‐II‐Positive Bacterial Bodies in HIS‐Positive Lesions

Given our previous finding that HIS bacterial bodies possess terminal βGlcNAc [12], we examined the proportion of GS‐II‐positive bacterial bodies in HIS‐positive lesions using the same 23 SSLs and 8 TAs.

In SSLs, the proportion of GS‐II‐positive bacterial bodies was significantly lower at lesion sites than in surrounding mucosa (p = 0.02) (Figure 1C,F; Figure 2C). In contrast, TAs showed no significant difference (p = 0.17) (Figure 1I,L; Figure 2C). Anti‐αGlcNAc immunostaining was negative in all cases, confirming terminal βGlcNAc on bacterial bodies.

These results indicate a significant reduction in the proportion of GS‐II‐positive bacterial bodies at lesion sites in SSLs, but not in TAs.

Preferential HIS Colonization of Absorptive Epithelium in SSLs and TAs

HIS reportedly attaches to microvilli of the colonic surface epithelium [6, 7]. We therefore asked whether HIS shows preferential colonization of absorptive epithelium identified by eosinophilic cytoplasm and well‐developed microvilli. Based on the proportion of spirochetes distributed on absorptive epithelium and the bacterial load at lesion sites, HIS‐positive lesions were classified into two broad distribution patterns, either “typical” or “other” (Table 3). In SSLs, 22 of 26 lesions (84.6%) showed the “typical” pattern, demonstrating preferential colonization of absorptive epithelium, as characterized by eosinophilic cytoplasm and GS‐II negativity (Figure 3A–C; see also Figure 1D–F). Of these, two lesions with very high bacterial load showed notable extension onto the mucinous epithelium (“overflow”) (Figure 3D–F), and one had a low bacterial load (“sparse”). The remaining four SSL lesions were classified as “other”: three showing prominent mucinous differentiation with spirochetes extending onto mucinous epithelium (Figure 3G–I), and the other showing fragmented spirochete distribution, which precluded reliable assessment. In TAs, fewer lesions were classified as “typical” (3/9, 33.3%) (Figure 3J–L), and “other” was the most frequent category (6/9, 66.7%). All six of the “other” lesions showed low bacterial load (≤ 10%) combined with limited absorptive differentiation, making it difficult to assess preferential distribution (Table 3; Supplementary Table 4).

Molecular Identification of Two Brachyspira Species

We subjected one representative lesion per HIS‐positive case to molecular analysis of B. aalborgi and B. pilosicoli by PCR amplification and direct sequencing of species‐specific nox gene regions (Supplementary Figure 1). In cases where HIS‐positive TAs coexisted with SSLs, the SSL lesion was analyzed as representative of both lesion types. All 18 lesions examined were infected exclusively with B. aalborgi, with no evidence of B. pilosicoli infection in any lesion examined.

Discussion

This study reveals significantly higher HIS positivity in SSLs than in TAs at the lesion level, case level, and within‐case comparisons. Among HIS‐positive lesions, the proportion of colonized epithelium was reduced at lesion sites compared to surrounding mucosa in both SSLs and TAs, with TAs showing a greater decrease. Importantly, in cases harboring both lesion types, a substantial proportion of TAs showed HIS colonization in surrounding mucosa but not at lesion sites, whereas no SSL exhibited this pattern. We further observed that HIS preferentially colonized absorptive epithelium in both SSLs and TAs. These findings suggest that HIS has universal affinity for absorptive epithelium, and that differences in absorptive epithelial abundance account for the higher HIS positivity seen in SSLs compared to TAs (Figure 4). In SSLs, absorptive epithelium coexists with mucinous epithelium (Figure 3) but is less abundant compared to surrounding mucosa, resulting in decreased HIS colonization at lesion sites. In TAs, absorptive epithelial differentiation was more severely limited (Figure 3), and the epithelium instead showed non‐specialized features with altered microvilli [6, 7], which may further restrict colonization sites (Figure 4). The ‘surrounding mucosa‐positive and lesion site‐negative’ pattern observed only in TAs further supports this mechanism (Table 2), indicating that TA lesion sites are less permissive for HIS colonization even when infection is established in the same patient. This reduced permissiveness most likely reflects the limited absorptive differentiation in TAs, but an active microenvironmental contribution cannot be excluded. For example, conventional adenomas frequently show Paneth cell differentiation with defensin expression, which is uncommon in SSLs [15] and could render the lesional environment locally antimicrobial. Whether this affects HIS colonization remains to be determined.

Schematic model of human intestinal spirochetosis (HIS) colonization in surrounding mucosa, sessile serrated lesions (SSLs), and tubular adenomas (TAs). (A) In surrounding mucosa, abundant differentiation to absorptive epithelium facilitates HIS colonization. (B) In SSLs, HIS preferentially colonizes absorptive epithelium within lesions that also contain abundant mucinous epithelium. Mucinous epithelium secretes mucins with terminal βGlcNAc, which may be associated with reduced bacterial surface βGlcNAc, as previously hypothesized [12]. (C) In TAs, limited differentiation to absorptive epithelium restricts HIS colonization.

As noted, a previous Japanese study reported a markedly higher HIS positivity in SSLs (52.6%) than in controls (8.1%) [8], whereas an Australian study reported only 1% positivity in SSLs [9]. Our observed positivity of 4.4% is closer to the latter and consistent with the prevalence reported in general colorectal specimens, especially in developed countries [2, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. A critical difference between these studies lies in diagnostic criteria used to define HIS positivity. The Japanese study classified cases as HIS‐positive based on immunostaining, including cases with immunoreactivity within mucus or crypt lumina without epithelial surface localization [8]. The Australian study also used immunostaining but distinguished between “positive” and “focally positive” cases; only the positive cases showed a false brush border on H&E, whereas focally positive cases lacked spirochetes based on electron microscopy analysis, suggesting that they may be false positives [9]. Differences in Brachyspira species biology may also contribute to variation across studies. HIS can exist in two states: an attached state with epithelial fringe formation, and a floating state within the mucus layer [23]. The floating pattern is reportedly more common in B. pilosicoli than B. aalborgi [23], suggesting that histology‐based approaches relying on epithelial attachment may underestimate B. pilosicoli infections. In our study, HIS positivity required morphological evidence of epithelial surface localization on H&E and AB/PAS staining, with anti‐Treponema immunostaining for confirmation, and was validated by PCR with direct sequencing. This approach excludes non‐specific immunoreactivity, providing a conservative estimate of epithelial colonization. Conversely, our exclusive detection of B. aalborgi may partly reflect this diagnostic emphasis on epithelial attachment (Supplementary Figure 1). However, given the known predominance of B. aalborgi in human HIS [20, 24, 25, 26, 27], this limitation is unlikely to substantially alter our overall findings. Collectively, the wide variation in reported HIS positivity among studies likely reflects differences in diagnostic stringency, particularly whether epithelial attachment is required, although geographic or biological factors may also contribute. The existence of both attached and floating states also suggests that the mucus layer functions as a reservoir for spirochetes. Alterations in mucin glycosylation, including βGlcNAc enrichment in SSLs [11], may influence bacterial persistence within this compartment. The reduced proportion of GS‐II‐positive bacterial bodies at SSL lesion sites suggests adaptive modulation of bacterial surface glycosylation in response to the βGlcNAc‐rich environment (Figure 4). Terminal βGlcNAc is an aberrant glycan absent from normal colonic epithelium [11] and can be an immunogenic carbohydrate determinant [28]. Therefore, its reduction on the bacterial surface may help the spirochetes avoid recognition of this glycan by the host immune system at SSL lesion sites, favoring their persistence in the neoplastic environment. However, the relationship between mucus glycosylation and bacterial glycosylation, and the balance between attached and floating states remain to be elucidated.

Beyond colonization patterns, HIS‐positive cases showed a male predominance (although not significant) and were significantly younger than HIS‐negative cases. Both features are directionally consistent with Japanese HIS cohorts, in which a male predominance [20, 21, 29] and a younger age (59.3 vs. 64.1 years, p < 0.01 [29]; 57 vs. 66 years [20]) have been repeatedly documented. Although these studies examined unselected colorectal populations, the same patterns appear in our SSL‐restricted comparison, suggesting that they reflect HIS infection itself rather than the SSL lesion; their underlying basis, however, remains undefined. The higher HIS positivity in SSLs is unlikely to be explained by the older age of the TA cohort (Table 1), as it persisted in the within‐case comparison of lesions from the same patients.

We acknowledge some study limitations. First, the concurrent cases analyzed for within‐case comparison were identified based on SSL HIS‐positivity, potentially introducing selection bias. However, the main cohort analysis confirmed the SSL > TA difference independent of this bias, and the comparison between lesion sites and surrounding mucosa within TAs (Table 2C) was similarly unaffected. Moreover, identifying cases by an HIS‐positive SSL enriches for patients with established colonic HIS colonization, reflected by frequent HIS positivity in the mucosa surrounding their TAs (16/23, 69.6%); this would be expected to raise rather than lower HIS positivity in the accompanying TAs, so the relatively low positivity at TA lesion sites (6/23, 26.1%) supports the interpretation that TA lesion sites are less permissive for HIS colonization rather than reflecting an artifact of the selection criterion. Second, the low bacterial load in many TA lesions limited detailed characterization of bacterial surface glycosylation. Third, as a single‐center retrospective study, our findings require validation in independent cohorts. Fourth, we classified absorptive and mucinous epithelium morphologically rather than by quantitative immunohistochemistry; marker‐based quantification of the absorptive‐to‐mucinous ratio within and between lesions would further substantiate the proposed mechanism and is an important direction for future work.

In conclusion, HIS exhibits universal affinity for absorptive epithelium, and higher HIS positivity in SSLs reflects their greater abundance of absorptive epithelium. Further studies incorporating direct assessment of absorptive epithelial differentiation will help clarify HIS colonization mechanisms in colorectal lesions.
