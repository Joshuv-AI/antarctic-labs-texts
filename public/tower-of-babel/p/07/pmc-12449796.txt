Effectiveness of open reduction internal fixation versus revision arthroplasty around Vancouver type B2 periprosthetic femoral fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fei Tan, Yongjie Qiao, Yingjia Zhou, Cuixian Yang, Xiaoyang Song, Peijie Li, Jiankang Zeng, Jiahuan Li, Zhiqiang Lin, Peng Liu, Shuo Ye, Jianan Ji, Kaipeng Zhuang, Shenghu Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWhen orthopedic surgeons encounter periprosthetic femoral fractures after hip arthroplasty, the task they face becomes challenging. Controversy continues to surround the fixation of Vancouver type B2 fractures in particular. The most discussed and successful method is open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) versus femoral stem revision arthroplasty (RA). Therefore, this article discusses both methods in order to compare the indications for both approaches.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science (from January 1, 2006, to July 30, 2024) and included all studies comparing the outcomes of open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) versus revison arthroplasty (RA) techniques. The primary outcome measures assessed were: injury to surgery time (ITST, days), union time (UT, months), length of hospital stay (LOS, days), surgical time (ST, minutes), and the Harris Hip Score (HHS). Complication metrics included: first-year mortality (FYM), blood transfusion rate (BT), as well as rates of revision, refracture, loosening, infection, dislocation, nonunion, subsidence, reoperation, total complications, and the proportion of patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification ≥ 3. Quality assessment (using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale [NOS] for cohort studies) and data extraction were independently performed by two reviewers.ResultsThis review included 34 studies comprising a total of 5137 patients.The injury to surgery time (days) was significantly shorter in the ORIF technique compared to the RA technique [Mean Difference (MD)=-0.66, [95% CI -1.16 to -0.15]; I2 = 65%, P = 0.01].In terms of the union time(days) (MD=-0.92, 95% CI -3.49-1.65, P = 0.57), surgical time (minutes) (MD=-0.87, 95%CI -2.80-1.07, P = 0.38), Harris hip score (MD = 0.66, 95% CI -3.44-4.77, P = 0.75) were superior in the ORIF technique compared to the RA technique.However the length of hospital stay (days), there were no significant differences observed. The ORIF technique exhibited a lower incidence of revision [Odds Ratio(OR)=-0.42, 95%CI 0.24–0.74, P = 0.0003] infection(OR = 0.61, 95%CI 0.41–0.92, P < 0.00001),dislocation(OR = 0.28, 95%CI 0.17–0.47, P < 0.00001) and nonunion(OR = 0.34, 95%CI 0.13–0.90, P = 0.03) compared to the RA technique alone.However the subsidence (OR = 0.48, 95% CI 0.23–0.99, P = 0.05) ,reoperation (OR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.35–1.53, P = 0.41), loosening (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.58–1.94, P = 0.86), and total complications (OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.70–1.31, P = 0.79),there were no significant differences observed. Remaining complications, such as refracture rate(OR = 1.81, 95%CI 1.11–2.97, P = 0.02),the RA technique was superior than ORIF technique.In the anesthesia risk assessment of patients, more patients in the ORIF group had an ASA ≥ 3 than in the RA group, suggesting that patients in the former group were in worse general condition.ConclusionsORIF poses a low risk to patients because it requires less surgical time; results in better postoperative union and functional recovery; and has lower rates of revision, dislocation, nonunion, and infection than other methods. ORIF that leads to fracture healing without the need for subsequent revision is advantageous, as reduced surgical time and complexity benefit patients. Finally, avoiding the use of long-stem implants during periprosthetic fracture fixation can benefit young patients who may require further revisions in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09052-6.Keywords: Periprosthetic fracture, Fracture fixation internal, Osteosynthesis fracture, Vancouver B2, Revision arthroplasty

Introduction

The incidence of periprosthetic femoral fractures (PFFs) is increasing annually, primarily due to the rising number of total hip arthroplasty procedures [1–3]. Additionally, the trend toward younger patients undergoing joint replacement and their high activity demands contribute to the increased fracture rates. According to the Swedish National Hip Arthroplasty Registry, PFFs are the third most common reason for revision surgery, following infection and dislocation [3]. Periprosthetic fractures around the hip joint are categorized into acetabular and femoral fractures, with femoral fractures being the most common. For femoral fractures, the most widely used classification system is the Vancouver system [4, 5], which considers the fracture location, implant stability, and surrounding bone stock. This system divides fractures into types A, B, and C [6], with Vancouver type B being the most prevalent. Type B fractures are further subdivided into three subtypes: B1, B2, and B3. In B1 fractures, the femoral stem is fixed. In B2 fractures, the femoral stem is loose. In B3 fractures, the femoral stem is loose with insufficient bone stock. Therefore, in terms of implant stability, Vancouver B1 fractures are considered “stable,” whereas B2 and B3 fractures are “unstable“ [7].

Historically, B1 fractures were treated with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), whereas B2 and B3 fractures were treated with revision arthroplasty (RA). However, with long follow-up periods and increased patient lifespans, complications following RA for B2 fractures have risen over time. As a result, several studies have questioned the optimal treatment approach for Vancouver B2 fractures [8–10]. In terms of postoperative complications, one study reviewing 203 patients with B2 fracture reported no difference in survival rates or reoperation rates between those treated with ORIF or RA [11]. From the perspective of surgical difficulty and cost, ORIF is less invasive for patients compared with RA, as it is a faster and less complex procedure, and plate fixation is cost-effective [8]. Considering patient health status, patients with PFF are often elderly with multiple severe comorbidities, making them less likely to tolerate lengthy and complex surgeries such as RA compared with their counterparts.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether ORIF can be recommended over RA for the treatment of Vancouver B2 fractures, explore the indications for internal fixation, and provide clinicians with enhanced treatment options.

Methods

The prospero registration number is CRD42024584044.

Search strategy

This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA reporting guidelines. We performed an extensive literature search in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase for English-language articles. Key search terms incorporated Medical Subject Headings (MESH) such as “Periprosthetic fracture,” “Fracture Fixation, Internal,” “Osteosynthesis, Fracture,” “Vancouver B2,” and “Revision arthroplasty.” The search encompassed all records available up to July 30, 2024, with no language limitations applied. Furthermore, we manually examined paper-based resources, grey literature, and the bibliographies of retrieved articles to locate additional pertinent studies.

Selection criteria

The criteria for inclusion were established as follows

Population: Studies exclusively involving Vancouver type B2 periprosthetic fractures. (2) Intervention: Direct comparison of open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) versus revision arthroplasty (RA). (3) Study Design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), case-control studies (CCS), or retrospective/prospective clinical studies. (4) Outcomes: Reporting of ≥ 1 relevant outcome: Injury-to-surgery time (ITST), union time (UT), length of hospital stay (LOS), surgical time (ST), or Harris Hip Score (HHS). Additionally, documented complication rates including first-year mortality (FYM), blood transfusion (BT), revision, refracture, loosening, infection, dislocation, nonunion, subsidence, reoperation, total complications, and counts of patients with ASA classification ≥ 3. (5) Data Availability: Studies providing complete or extractable data

The exclusion criteria were as follows

(1)Publication Type: Non-peer-reviewed sources (e.g., conference abstracts, theses, preprints).(2)Study Design: Non-human studies, case reports, case series, review articles (systematic or narrative), letters/commentaries, editorials, recommendations, guidelines, and non-comparative observational studies (e.g., cohorts without a control group comparing ORIF vs. RA).(3) Data Accessibility: Studies where the full text was inaccessible or where essential outcome/complication data were incomplete or irretrievable.

Data extraction

(1)Independent Review & Adjudication: Two researchers independently performed the literature review. Discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer.(2)Systematically Extracted Data: The following data were extracted from each included study: (3)Study Characteristics: First author, study period, country of origin, study design, sample size, patient age.(4)Perioperative Outcomes: Injury-to-surgery time (ITST), union time (UT), length of hospital stay (LOS), surgical time (ST), Harris Hip Score (HHS).Complication Metrics: First-year mortality (FYM), blood transfusion rates(BT), revision, refracture, loosening, infection, dislocation, nonunion, subsidence, reoperation, total complications, number of patients with ASA classification ≥ 3.Refer to Supplementary Material 1 for detailed extracted data.

Study quality assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated the methodological quality of included studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [12]. This validated tool assesses cohort and case-control studies, covering key aspects like case definition adequacy, cohort selection and comparability, and outcome ascertainment. Studies achieving a NOS score ≥ 6 (out of a maximum 9 points) were deemed high quality.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager (Cochrane Collaboration) and Stata 17 (Stata Corp).Effect Measures: Continuous outcomes (ITST, UT, ST, HHS): Pooled weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).Dichotomous outcomes (FYM, BT, revision, subsidence, reoperation, refracture, loosening, infection, dislocation, nonunion, total complications): Pooled odds ratios (OR) with 95% CI.Data Synthesis: Effect sizes were combined using inverse-variance weighting. Logarithmic transformation was applied to ORs prior to pooling. Weighted averages were calculated based on individual study sample sizes, presented as numerical values or proportions. Pooled estimates are reported as percentages with 95% CIs.Heterogeneity Assessment: Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic: I² < 50%: Low heterogeneity (fixed-effects model), I²= 50–75%: Moderate heterogeneity and I² >75%: High heterogeneity.A random-effects model was used for moderate/high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 50% or P < 0.05).Bias Assessment: sensitivity analyses were performed where appropriate.Publication bias was assessed visually via funnel plots and statistically using Begg’s test (to supplement funnel plot interpretation).Forest plots illustrated individual and pooled effect estimates.

Results

Search results

The initial search yielded a total of 5137 articles, with 3684 retrieved from PubMed, 161 from Web of Science, 1260 from Cochrane Library, and 32 from Embase. Furthermore, the manual search identified two studies that met the inclusion criteria. After removing duplicates, a total of 1935 studies remained. Among these, 1837 were excluded based on abstract and title screening. Subsequently, we retrieved the remaining 98 full-text papers for a more comprehensive analysis. Out of these, 64 papers were excluded due to various reasons, including outcome does not meet the request (n = 30), inability to calculate results from available data (n = 9), and missing data on key outcome indicators (n = 25). The meta-analysis finally included 34 articles (Fig. 1). Fig. 1 shows the flowchart of the study selection process.

Outcomes analysis

Injury to surgery time (ITST)

The ITST was recorded in thirteen studies [10, 17–20, 22–24, 26, 28–30, 33, 36–39, 42–44]. Data were analyzed using a random-effects model, and forest plots showed high heterogeneity (I2 = 65%, P = 0.01), revealing a significant difference between the two techniques (MD=−0.66, [95% CI −1.16 to −0.15]; I2 = 65%, P = 0.01). A meta-analysis of these thirteen studies demonstrated that the ORIF exhibited a shorter ITST compared to the RA (Fig. 3A). This finding holds clinical significance as it indicates that the injury time to surgery time was shorter in the ORIF than in the RA. A visual assessment of the funnel plot indicated the presence of a slight publication bias (Fig. 2A). However, the Begger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.732) (Table 3).

Comparison of postoperative outcomes between ORIF and RA procedures

Union time (UT)

We performed a meta-analysis comparing the UT in patients with the ORIF versus the RA, including nine studies [10, 18, 19, 30, 33, 36–39]. The random-effects model analysis showed a significant difference between the ORIF and RA(MD = 0.93, [95%CI 0.07 to 1.79]; I2 = 90%; P = 0.03). This finding holds clinical significance as it indicates that the union time was shorter in the ORIF technique than in the RA. (Fig. 3B).

Length of hospital stay (LOS)

Fourteen studies [8, 14, 20, 23–26, 29, 31, 38, 39, 41, 42, 44] reported HOS, including 807 patients. There were 415 patients in the ORIF and 392 patients in the RA. Heterogeneity analysis showed that there was no significant statistical heterogeneity between these studies (MD=−0.11, 95%CI −1.02 to 0.80; I2 = 0%, P = 0.82). The results of the data showed no significant difference in LOS between the two techniques (Fig. 3C).A visual assessment of the funnel plot indicated the presence of a slight publication bias (Fig. 2B). However, the Begger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.189).

Surgical time (ST)

Fourteen studies [8, 10, 13, 14, 17, 18, 24, 29, 31, 36–39, 44] involving 757 patients (364 in the ORIF and 393 in the RA) reported the ST. The pooled results demonstrated that there was a significant difference in ST between the two treatment techniques (MD=−43.34, [95%CI −57.79 to −28.90]; I2 = 87%; P<0.00001) with high heterogeneity (Fig. 3D). This finding holds clinical significance as it indicates that the surgical time was shorter in the ORIF than in the RA.A visual assessment of the funnel plot indicated the presence of a slight publication bias (Fig. 2C). However, the Begger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.956).

Harris hip score (HHS)

Fourteen studies [15, 16, 18, 24, 26–28, 30, 31, 33, 34, 37, 41, 43] involving 802 patients (464 in the ORIF and 338 in the RA) reported the HHS. The pooled results demonstrated that there was a significant difference in HHS between the two treatment techniques (MD=−3.68, [95%CI −7.26 to −0.11]; I2 = 51%; P = 0.04) with high heterogeneity (Fig. 3E). This finding holds clinical significance as it indicates that the harris hip score was greater in the ORIF than in the RA.A visual assessment of the funnel plot indicated the presence of a slight publication bias (Fig. 2F). However, the Begger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.913).

The statistical data for complications

First-year mortality (FYM)

Twenty studies [15–18, 20–24, 26, 31, 32, 34, 36, 38, 40–44] compared the first year mortality in patients with the ORIF versus the RA. Meta-analysis of these 20 studies showed that the first year mortality was not significantly in the ORIF than that in the RA(131/640 vs. 110/661; OR 1.34, [95% CI 1.00 to 1.81]; I2 = 0%; P = 0.05) (Fig. 4A).A visual assessment of the funnel plot indicated the presence of a slight publication bias (Fig. 2D). However, the Begger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.626).

Funnel plots show the results of the injury to surgery time(A), length of hospital stay(B), surgical time(C), first year mortality(D), blood transfusion (E) and harris hip score(F).

Blood transfusion rate (BT)

A total of ten studies [8, 10, 15, 23, 24, 28, 31, 38, 41, 42] provided data comparing the BT rate in patients with ORIF versus the RA. The meta-analysis revealed that patients with the ORIF don’t increase the risk of BT compared to patients with the RA(112/317vs.135/334;OR 0.77, [95%CI 0.54 to 1.11]; I2 = 36%;P = 0.16) (Fig. 4B). A visual assessment of the funnel plot indicated the presence of a slight publication bias (Fig. 2E). However, the Begger’s test was not statistically significant (P = 0.721).

Subgroup analysis

When total heterogeneity is high and within-group heterogeneity is low, this indicates that the grouping factor is significant for heterogeneity.First, the types of the PFF in the article were categorized as B2, B2/B3, and B2/C. In order to exclude the confounding factors mentioned above, the authors performed subgroup analyses of ITST, UT, and HHS. The results showed that the three different fracture types were not significant factors in their heterogeneity. Secondly, we conducted subgroup analyses of ITST, UT, and HHS according to different regions, and the results showed that the articles published in different regions were also not significant factors in their heterogeneity in this study (Table 4). Finally, there may be other reasons for the source of heterogeneity, such as the fact that the literature was all retrospective and the inclusion of the literature itself.

Discussion

This review included 35 studies with a total of 2,592 patients. Compared with RA, ORIF had a shorter time interval from injury to surgery. In terms of union time, operative time, and Harris Hip Score, ORIF outperformed RA. However, we found no statistically significant difference between the two groups in the time from initial arthroplasty to periprosthetic fracture or in length of hospital stay (LOS). Compared with RA, ORIF was associated with lower rates of revision, infection, dislocation, and nonunion. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of stem subsidence, reoperation, loosening, or total complications. For other complications, such as re-fracture rates, RA had fewer occurrences than ORIF. In the assessment of patient anesthesia risk, the number of patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score ≥ 3 was higher in the ORIF group than in the RA group, indicating that patients in the former group generally had poorer health conditions but were more suitable for ORIF due to its less invasive nature than those in the latter group.

Age and ASA score

From the perspective of age, when patients are older, surgeons tend to prefer ORIF over RA.

We also found that the average age in the ORIF group was higher than that in the RA group (81 years vs. 78.4 years). We speculated that ORIF was chosen because elderly patients with PFF often have multiple comorbidities, which typically exclude highly complex surgeries such as RA.

Our study found that the number of patients with an ASA score ≥ 3 was higher in the ORIF group than in the RA group, indicating that patients in the former group generally had poorer health conditions but were more suitable for ORIF due to its less invasive nature than those in the latter group. However, Martinov [14] et al. found no significant difference in ASA scores between the ORIF and RA groups.

Injury to surgery time

In our study, injury to surgery time was shorter in the ORIF group than in the RA group, and postoperative recovery improved. Surgical delay is closely associated with increased mortality in patients with hip arthroplasty [45–47]. Longer waiting times are associated with poorer outcomes, such as increased transfusion rates, LOS, and mortality. Bhattacharyya [20]et al. found that a surgical delay of more than 2 days after admission in patients with periprosthetic fracture is also associated with increased mortality within 1 year. When controlling for confounding factors, surgical delay remained an independent risk factor. As with other fractures in the elderly population, early surgery is recommended for periprosthetic fractures, provided the patient is stable, to prevent complications related to fixation.

Intraoperative surgical time and blood loss

Solomon [18]et al. found that the ORIF group had shorter surgical times than the RA group, but the RA group had longer surgical times and more complications than the ORIF group. Our study also showed shorter surgical times in the ORIF group than in the RA group, but we found no significant difference in complications between the two groups. Additionally, Martinov [14] found significant differences in blood loss and surgical time. As demonstrated by Manara [48] et al., these two metrics are interrelated. For every additional minute of surgical time, the probability of significant blood loss increases by 3%. A twofold reduction in surgical time resulted in a threefold reduction in blood loss, which cannot be overlooked. These factors are critical for patient tolerance of surgery and improved postoperative recovery.

Length of hospital stay

Our study compared the LOS between the two groups and found no significant difference, which was consistent with Jain [23]et al.‘s findings. This finding was unexpected because RA allows for full weight-bearing postoperatively, while some surgeons may restrict weight-bearing after ORIF, which could lead to differences in LOS between the two groups. However, LOS is considered complex and multifaceted, involving many patient-related factors or aspects of care. Additionally, Marino [41]et al. found that the number of patient comorbidities affects LOS, with patients having more than 5 comorbidities experiencing longer LOSs and higher total costs compared with those with 0–2 comorbidities [49].

First-year mortality

In terms of first-year mortality, patients with PFFs are often frail, elderly, and have multiple comorbidities, resulting in a high first-year mortality rate of 13–17%. Therefore, the two surgical approaches, with their differing complexities, may have some impact on mortality due to.

surgery time and blood loss [50]. However, our study showed no significant difference in first-year mortality between the two groups, which differed from previous studies. Furthermore, studies on survival rates beyond 10 years postoperatively are not meaningful, as most patients die within the first 10 years after surgery.

Reoperation and revision rates

In a paper analyzing reoperation rates after periprosthetic fractures, 126 cases of Vancouver.

B2 fractures were analyzed, with a 2-year reoperation rate of 13%. The most common reasons for.

reoperation were infection, followed by re-fracture, dislocation, and loosening [51]. Additionally, the study showed that patients treated with ORIF had higher reoperation rates than those treated with RA. They speculated the following reasons. First, the local biological environment may be affected by the pre-existing intramedullary implant. Second, tissue damage and blood supply disturbances caused by previous surgeries may slow the healing process of PFFs. Finally, the beneficial effects of weight-bearing on fracture union have been well-documented. In the treatment of PFFs, only RA or retrograde intramedullary nailing bridging the femoral stem can provide sufficient stability and weight-bearing potential, whereas ORIF requires restricted weight-bearing for 6 weeks. These three factors may lead to nonunion and increased reoperation rates. However, our study showed no difference in reoperation rates between the two groups.

In this study, the revision rate in the RA group was much higher than that in the ORIF group.However, Lindahl [35]et al. reviewed 1,049 periprosthetic fractures in the Swedish National Hip Arthroplasty Registry and reported that the revision rate in the ORIF group was much higher than that in the RA group, which contradicted our findings.

Total complication rate

Joestl [8] et al. found that the risk of recurrent hip dislocation increases after RA, which was consistent with our findings. They attributed this to the weakening of the joint capsule and muscles around the hip due to repeated surgeries through the same or different approaches.Therefore, ORIF may be performed for patients with poor muscle conditions and low postoperative walking demands. Additionally, some researchers [9] believe that ORIF for periprosthetic fractures has a lower total complication rate than RA, but ORIF carries a higher risk of nonunion and plate loosening than RA [52].

Harris hip scores and mobility

Flury [24]et al. used the Harris Hip Score as the primary functional outcome measure. They found that patients with ORIF have slightly elevated scores, although the difference was not statistically significant [24]. However, in our study, ORIF outperformed RA, and the difference was statistically significant. Although patients with ORIF were older than patients with RA, postoperative hip function recovery was still better in the former group, indicating that ORIF is less traumatic and results in better postoperative recovery compared with RA. At the same time, ORIF can achieve adequate fracture stability and restore patients’ mobility to levels similar to those before the fracture. Additionally, maintaining long-term implant stability can reduce surgical time, transfusion requirements, bone loss, and complication rates [53]. Therefore, Niikura [10]et al. suggested that RA may not be the best treatment for all B2 fractures.

Limitations

However, this study had some limitations. First, we could not obtain detailed information on other confounding factors, such as preoperative femoral stem type and the reason for initial hip arthroplasty. Second, some studies from over a decade ago may have used surgical concepts different from those used today. Third, the included studies were primarily retrospective cohort studies and that patient selection is a factor that must be taken into account. For example, it is possible that revision arthroplasty was performed in patients who actually had a loose stem, i.e., Vancouver B2 or B3, whereas in patients where one or more fragments were found to be well-fixed to the stem during surgery (Vancouver B1), fixation was chosen instead. We may therefore be looking at different patient groups, and the generalizability to Vancouver B2 fracture patients cannot be properly established.Finally, there are only two prospective cohort studies in this article. More prospective studies are needed in the future to control confounding factors in order to evaluate the clinical efficacy of ORIF and RA.

Conclusion

ORIF poses a low risk to patients because it requires less surgical time; results in better postoperative union and functional recovery; and has lower rates of revision, dislocation, nonunion, and infection than other methods. ORIF that leads to fracture healing without the need for subsequent revision is advantageous, as reduced surgical time and complexity benefit patients. Finally, avoiding the use of long-stem implants during periprosthetic fracture fixation can benefit young patients who may require further revisions in the future.
