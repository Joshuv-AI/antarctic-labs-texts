Emphysematous endometritis following postpartum uterine artery embolization: A case report
Authors: David Nai, Nazanin Kiani Fard, Elza Matrova, Matthew Smetko, William Kobak, André Kajdacsy-Balla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emphysematous endometritis is a rarely described clinical entity. The present report describes the case of a 44 -year-old woman who presented 2 weeks postpartum with abdominal pain, malodorous vaginal discharge, fever, and paresthesia in the setting of uterine embolization. Imaging showed emphysematous changes in the wall of the uterus. Histology of the hysterectomy sample showed acute suppurative myometritis, subserosal neutrophilic infiltrates, and emphysematous air pockets; these were accompanied by embolic material, bacterial clumps, and granulation tissue. Her recovery from the hysterectomy was without further complications.

Introduction

Postpartum acute endometritis is a major cause of morbidity in women, especially after Cesarean birth. Its incidence ranges from 1% to 3% in patients without risk factors after a normal spontaneous vaginal delivery, increasing to about 5% to 6% with risk factors [1]. Among patients who receive antibiotic prophylaxis, which is now standard practice, the incidence of postpartum endometritis is about 7.0% for cesarean sections performed after the onset of labor and 1.5% for scheduled cesarean sections. In comparison, the incidence rates without antibiotic prophylaxis are approximately 18.4% and 3.9%, respectively [2]. Bacterial vaginosis increases the risk several-fold. Other risk factors include prolonged labor, prolonged rupture of membranes, multiple examinations of the cervix, and manual placenta removal [3]. When postpartum acute endometritis occurs, the infection is usually polymicrobial and consists of vaginal flora. Symptoms and signs comprise fever, lower abdominal pain, tachycardia, purulent lochia, chills, malaise, headache, and anorexia [1].

Emphysematous endometritis is a rare entity; the authors could find only five reports in the English-language literature [4], [5], [6], [7]. These used the terms “pneumopolycystic endometritis” and “endometrial pneumatosis.” A 1960 article by Perkins described a 32-year-old woman postpartum who was found by X-ray to have innumerable gaseous cysts outlining her.

uterus [4]. Histologically, the endometrial lining was composed of “multiple empty and variably sized cystic spaces present within edematous stroma” [4]. Val-Bernal et al. report the case of a 49-year-old woman who had hypermenorrhea and irregular menses for one year [5]. The patient was found by ultrasound to have “diffusely thickened endometrium and a 15 mm anechoic cystic structure within the posterior uterine wall.” Histology showed a proliferative endometrium and simple endometrial hyperplasia with gas-filled cysts in the endometrial stroma [5]. Chua et al. describe the case of a 43-year-old woman who underwent hysterectomy and bilateral salpingectomy for a symptomatic uterine leiomyoma [6]. The anterior and posterior endometrium of the uterus had a cobblestone appearance and were lined with innumerable cystic spaces that, when punctured under water, released air. Histologically, the appearance was of “multiple, irregular and partly collapsed vesicular spaces within the superficial endometrial stroma extending close to the surface epithelium. No distinct epithelial or endothelial lining was identified, and immunostaining for cytokeratin, CD31, CD34, FLI-1, and D2-40 was negative in several representative blocks.” [6] An article by Sbihi et al. discusses the case of a 56-year-old woman, a known case of stage III, high-grade, poorly differentiated glassy cell endometrial cancer on chemotherapy, presenting to the emergency department with a one-week history of abdominal pain and distention and vaginal discharge complicated by hypovolemic shock and sepsis. Computerized tomography (CT) revealed air in the endometrial cavity with a small locule adjacent to the uterus, indicative of emphysematous endometritis [7].

Case Presentation

A 44-year-old woman (G7P6006) was admitted in labor at 39 weeks and 4 days. She was referred from the clinic because of advanced dilation. The patient denied pain and vaginal bleeding. Her pregnancy had been characterized by a late entry into medical care, at 24 weeks. Besides a cholecystectomy at 33 years of age, she had no other medical conditions and took no medications except a prenatal vitamin; her family history included ischemic heart disease in her mother and diabetes mellitus type 2 in her sister. She was a homemaker and did not smoke, drink alcohol, or use recreational drugs.

The next morning, the patient progressed to complete dilation and + 1 station. After pushing for 7 min, she had a spontaneous vaginal delivery of a 3345 g male infant (Apgar scores of 9 and 9). She subsequently developed postpartum hemorrhage. A cervical laceration was identified and repaired with 2–0 Vicryl. The patient received 1000 μg of misoprostol, 0.2 mg of methergine, and 250 μg of carboprost (Hemabate). The estimated blood loss (EBL) was 1300 mL. Endometrial curettage was then performed using a Banjo curette under ultrasound guidance, during which an additional 700 mL of blood loss was estimated (total EBL 2000 mL). A Bakri balloon was placed afterward. She received 3000 mL of crystalloid and 500 mL of albumin at the time of curettage. No blood products were administered at this time, as there was no evidence of coagulopathy, although her hemoglobin decreased from 11.9 to 8.5. The patient remained hemodynamically stable. These measures were unsuccessful, necessitating bilateral uterine artery embolization performed by interventional radiology. The bleeding ultimately resolved. On postpartum day 1, the patient developed preeclampsia with severe features, with a blood pressure of 160/90 mmHg and a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 0.41, for which magnesium sulfate was administered for maternal seizure prophylaxis. She was discharged on postpartum day 3.

On postpartum day 14, the patient returned for a routine postpartum exam with abdominal pain and malodorous vaginal discharge that had been increasing for 3–4 days, as well as fever and paresthesia of her right leg, left forearm, and hand. She was sent to the obstetric and gynecologic emergency room for endometritis evaluation. Vital signs were as follows: temperature 39.4 °C, pulse 133, BP 100/65 mmHg. On exam, the patient was severely tender in the abdomen, especially over the fundus, and had bilateral adnexal tenderness. She also had a headache and fatigue. Her white blood cell count was 34,300/ml. An intravenous fluid bolus was given, and gentamicin/clindamycin was started. Her hemoglobin level at admission was 6.7 g/dL. She was transfused with 2 units of packed red blood cells. The next day, a CT scan was done of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast.

The imaging (Fig. 1) showed an enlarged, irregular uterus measuring 12.6 × 14.8 × 21.1 cm, surrounded by inflammatory fat stranding. The wall of the uterus showed small areas of peripheral enhancement in the myometrium/subserosa. No retained products of conception were identified. There was extensive soft-tissue thickening and emphysematous changes throughout the submucosal regions of the uterus. There was also air noted within the endometrial canal. No focal fluid collections were seen. The patient required an exploratory laparotomy with supracervical hysterectomy. The uterus was found to be fluctuant and necrotic, but without spillage of contents; the adjacent bowel and omentum were very inflamed. Endocervical wound culture from the day of admission grew Morganella morganii, Porphyromonas, Prevotella melaninogenica, and enterococcus (non-VRE). Based on the culture results, the antibiotics were changed to levofloxacin and metronidazole.

The patient was successfully discharged after four days, during which no further complications arose. On the postpartum day 28, she presented for staple removal and was noted to be doing well.

On gross examination, the uterus measured 18.5 cm by 12.7 cm by 12.0 cm. The serosa was tan-pink with necrotic pouches. The fundus and endometrial canal were replaced by tan-brown soft necrotic tissue extending into the myometrium, where there was a rim of fibrous tan-green subserosal tissue. The myometrium measured up to 6.0 cm in greatest thickness. The microscopic examination showed transmural coagulative necrosis in some areas, acute suppurative myometritis, subserosal neutrophilic infiltrates, and emphysematous air pockets (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). These changes may have been due to either infarction or infection. Embolization material, calcifications, colonies of cocci and bacilli, and granulation tissue were observed. Discussion

Postpartum endometritis is usually a polymicrobial infection involving both aerobes and anaerobes from the genital tract. Of the microbial species involved in the present case, Porphyromonas and Prevotella melaninogenica are anaerobic bacteria [8], [9], while Enterococcus (non-VRE) and Morganella morganii are facultative anaerobes [10], [11]. Cesarean delivery is the greatest risk factor [12]. Only 15% of postpartum endometritis cases present more than a week after delivery. The most common symptoms are uterine tenderness, midline abdominal pain, fever, and tachycardia. Differential diagnoses include Cesarean surgical site infections, pyelonephritis, aspiration pneumonia, infection from neuraxial anesthesia, and mastitis [1].

Surgical pathology findings include edematous, hyperemic endometrium with marked neutrophilic inflammatory infiltrates. Acute endometritis is defined by the presence of ≥5 neutrophils per 400 X high power field in the endometrium. It is frequently characterized by microabscess formation and neutrophil invasion of the superficial endometrial epithelium, gland lumina, and uterine cavity. Myometrial and parametrial invasion may be present, along with necrosis or thrombosis [13].

Septic shock and its complications, which may include death, are rare. Infections caused by Group A streptococci or Clostridium species are more frequently linked to sepsis. Additionally, streptococcal or staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome can develop.

Antibiotic treatment is usually initiated with clindamycin and gentamicin or other regimens covering beta-lactamase-producing anaerobes [14]. To overcome clindamycin resistance in Bacteroides fragilis or Group B streptococci, ampicillin and sulbactam may be used instead. Blood cultures are positive in only 6.5% of cases with post-cesarean acute endometritis [15].

The patient reported here had emphysematous endometritis most likely as a sequela of uterine necrosis. Despite being one of the rarest complications of uterine artery embolization to treat postpartum hemorrhage, twenty-one published cases of uterine necrosis were found in the literature. According to Poujade et al., four pathophysiological factors that determine the risk of uterine necrosis are the presence of anastomotic vasculature, the size and nature of the embolizing agent, and the embolizing technique used. Embolization using gelatin sponge fragments was implicated in most cases of uterine necrosis [16]. These authors postulate that this is because gelatin fragments are too small and obliterate distal collateral vessels, including the utero-ovarian and cervico-vaginal arteries [16]. Signs and symptoms of uterine necrosis include fever, abdominal pain, menorrhagia, and leukorrhea in a minority. According to Kwon, involvement of the bladder or adnexa also occurs [17]. The average time between embolization and the diagnosis of necrosis was 21 days [17]. The author did not mention any case of emphysematous endometritis or endometrial pneumatosis.

Morganella morganii is a Gram-negative member of the Enterobacteriaceae. It was formerly classified in the genus Proteus. It infrequently causes urinary tract infections in humans [11]. Although relatively uncommon, it has been implicated in severe infections such as sepsis, abscesses, and chorioamnionitis, with notable associated mortality in vulnerable patients. Rising antimicrobial resistance, including the acquisition of multiple resistance genes in addition to its intrinsic β-lactam resistance, makes M. morganii a clinically important organism that requires targeted antibiotic therapy [11].

Conclusion

Emphysematous endometritis is a rare but serious complication of uterine artery embolization for postpartum hemorrhage, presenting with delayed abdominal pain, fever, leukocytosis, and gas within the uterine wall on imaging. Prompt recognition of this entity, coupled with broad-spectrum antibiotics and timely surgical management, can prevent further septic complications and lead to full recovery even in the setting of extensive uterine necrosis.
