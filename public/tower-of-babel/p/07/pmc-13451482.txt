lncRNA-mRNA Co-expression Network Unveils Neutrophil Metabolic Reprogramming in Human Sepsis
Authors: Peng Zhang, Fupeng Wang, Kang Zhao, Zeqing Miao, Yang Yu, Aiqing Wen, Huang Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a life-threatening inflammatory syndrome driven by dysregulated immunity. Metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation are now recognized as critical mechanisms underlying persistent immune cell alterations in sepsis. Neutrophils play a central role in sepsis by forming NETs, releasing inflammatory cytokines, and phagocytosing pathogens. During these processes, neutrophils undergo metabolic adaptation that supports their effector functions. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have been implicated in multiple facets of sepsis progression, including the regulation of immune cell functions and organ injury. Recent studies further indicate that lncRNAs modulate metabolic reprogramming across various cell types during sepsis, highlighting extensive crosstalk between metabolic and epigenetic pathways. Nevertheless, the relationship between lncRNAs and metabolic reprogramming in neutrophils remains poorly understood. To better characterize the regulatory interplay between metabolic alterations and lncRNAs in neutrophils during sepsis, we performed RNA sequencing of neutrophils isolated from septic patients. We identified a set of hub differentially expressed genes enriched in amino acid metabolic processes and uncovered potential lncRNA networks that may regulate these genes. Our study delineates a broader landscape of neutrophil metabolic reprogramming in sepsis and provides a foundational co-expression network, offering new directions for investigating lncRNA-mediated amino acid metabolic alterations in neutrophils during sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02532-4.Keywords: Sepsis, Neutrophil, lncRNAs, Metabolism

Introduction

In 2016, the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) redefined sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Sepsis is increasingly recognized as a syndrome of profound immune dysregulation, characterized by concurrent and often biphasic imbalances between hyperinflammation and immunosuppression [2, 3]. A detailed understanding of the underlying immune alterations is therefore essential. Notably, recent studies have identified major metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming events, which drive dramatic shifts in transcriptional profiles, as central mechanisms in the induction and maintenance of sepsis-induced immune dysfunction [4–7]. It is now established that nearly all cells undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming during sepsis, involving pathways such as glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid metabolism [8]. These findings indicate that studying sepsis-associated metabolic alterations and their regulatory mechanisms may yield potential therapeutic targets.

Neutrophils, the most abundant immune cells in peripheral blood, serve as essential first responders recruited to sites of infection [9]. Numerous studies have established their critical roles in sepsis, including cytokine release via pyroptosis, formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), phagocytosis, and degranulation [10, 11]. Accumulating evidence indicates that neutrophils undergo profound metabolic reprogramming throughout sepsis [12]. Although primarily reliant on glycolysis for ATP generation, septic neutrophils exhibit a dramatic surge in oxygen consumption, which drives the respiratory burst to generate abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS) via NADPH oxidase activation [11]. Another key finding is that increased GLUT1 expression enhances glucose uptake in neutrophils, a process critical for sustaining aerobic glycolysis and redirecting metabolic flux toward the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) [13]. These metabolic adaptations are essential for supporting chemotaxis, oxidative burst, and the formation and release of NETs [8]. Although neutrophil metabolic rewiring is now considered crucial to sepsis progression, the specific molecular details and regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated.

As pivotal components of the epigenome, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) participate in diverse physiological processes, including DNA replication, damage repair, transcriptional and epigenetic regulation, mRNA processing, and microRNA sequestration as molecular sponges [14]. Recent studies have identified specific lncRNAs that regulate immune cell function during sepsis. For instance, lncRNA GSEC controls neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation and inflammatory activation [15], while lncRNA Nron and its functional motif modulate macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [16]. Beyond immune regulation, numerous lncRNAs contribute to sepsis-associated multiple organ dysfunction, including lncRNAs GAS5 [17], Neat1 [18], rPvt1 [19], and TCONS_00016233 [20], which have been implicated in sepsis-induced intestinal barrier injury, encephalopathy, cardiomyocyte damage, and acute kidney injury, respectively. Notably, emerging evidence reveals that lncRNAs directly influence cellular metabolism during sepsis. For example, cardiomyocyte-expressed lncRNA Cpat maintains mitochondrial metabolic homeostasis and mitigates myocardial injury in septic cardiomyopathy [21], and lncRNA IGF2-AS modulates nucleotide metabolism in endothelial progenitor cells via HMGA1 [22]. Furthermore, lncRNA GSEC promotes neutrophil inflammatory activation by enhancing PFKFB3-mediated glycolytic metabolism [23]. These findings collectively underscore a potential mechanistic crosstalk between metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation in the pathogenesis of sepsis.

Although initial studies have reported the role of lncRNAs in glycolytic and lipid reprogramming within neutrophils during sepsis [23], whether lncRNAs regulate alterations in other metabolic pathways in septic neutrophils remains largely unexplored. To address this, we performed RNA sequencing on peripheral blood neutrophils from septic patients and identified a set of key metabolism-associated genes, which are primarily involved in amino acid metabolism, along with potential lncRNAs that may regulate them. Our study aims to elucidate broader metabolic reprogramming in neutrophils during sepsis and its underlying regulatory network, thereby providing potential directions for future sepsis research.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and RNA Extraction

Adult patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) who fulfilled the consensus definition of sepsis were included in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients had clear evidence of infection, confirmed by bacterial culture; (2) Patients presented secondary organ dysfunction or acute exacerbation of primary organ dysfunction; (3) The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was ≥ 2. Exclusion criteria included age under 18 years, pregnancy, moribund status with a life expectancy of less than 24 h, and patients with comorbidities such as HIV infection or autoimmune diseases. Healthy individuals undergoing routine physical examinations were enrolled as controls.

Peripheral blood samples were collected within 24 h after ICU admission using disposable EDTA blood collection tubes. Neutrophils were isolated from the peripheral blood of patients and healthy subjects within 2 h after collection as previously described [24]. Histopaque gradient centrifugation was used to separate and purify neutrophils from whole blood. Equal volumes of Histopaque-1077 and Histopaque-1119 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were layered to form a double gradient. Neutrophils were collected and purified at the lower interface between these two gradient media. Neutrophil viability was assessed by flow cytometry using Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) (C1062, Beyotime, China), and purity was determined by detecting CD11b (301330; Isotype ctrl: 400110, BioLegend, USA) and CD66b (305118; Isotype ctrl: 401613, BioLegend, USA) expression. Total RNA was then extracted from each sample using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA).

RNA-seq Library Preparation and Sequencing

Total RNA’s integrity and quantity were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. For each sample, 1 µg of total RNA was subjected to ribosomal RNA depletion, followed by strand-specific library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. Libraries were qualified by Qubit 2.0, Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and qRT-PCR, then pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 150 bp mode. Raw FASTQ reads were processed by in-house Perl scripts to remove adapters, poly-N, and low-quality bases, yielding clean reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (hg38) using Hisat2 v2.0.5, and gene-level expression was quantified as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) using StringTie v1.3.3b.

Identification of Differentially Expressed mRNAs and lncRNAs

RNA-seq data were processed and analyzed with R. Principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out with the plotPCA function from the “limma” R package, and the PCA plot was created using “ggplot2” R package. Differentially expressed genes of mRNAs and lncRNAs were identified utilizing “limma” R package with a significant threshold of p.adjust < 0.05 and absolute fold change > 2 (|log2FoldChange| > 1). Volcano plots and heatmaps were visualized using “ggplot2” and “pheatmap” R packages, respectively. The expression levels of metabolic mRNAs were also visualized with “ggplot2” R package.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

For selected mRNAs, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package to identify significantly enriched biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), cellular components (CC), and signaling pathways.

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

Co-expression networks were constructed using the “WGCNA” R package to identify modules highly correlated with sepsis. After preprocessing and outlier removal, the expression matrix was analyzed with WGCNA functions to obtain modules and their eigengenes. A soft-thresholding power of 12 was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion. The cutreeDynamic function was used for dynamic tree cutting with a minimum module size of 200 to define co-expression modules, and highly correlated modules (minimum height for merging modules = 0.3) were merged. Module–trait relationships were assessed using Pearson correlation between module eigengenes (MEs) and clinical traits. Module membership (MM) was defined as the correlation between gene expression and the module eigengene, and gene significance (GS) as the correlation between the gene and the trait. Hub genes were defined by |MM| and |GS|.

Analysis of mRNA-lncRNA Relationships

Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) and adjusted p value (p.adjust) were calculated between selected mRNAs and lncRNAs based on their expression profiles. Pairs with |PCC| > 0.9 and p.adjust < 0.05 were considered to exhibit co-expression relationships. Genomic annotations were used to determine positional relationships: pairs located on the same chromosome within 1e + 06 bp were classified as cis-acting, and all others as trans-acting. Co-expression networks were visualized using the “circlize” R package.

Quantification of mRNA and lncRNA by qRT-PCR

Total RNA from neutrophils was extracted using TRIZOL reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA). Furthermore, RNA was transcribed into cDNA via a reverse transcription kit (RR036A, Takara, Japan). Real-time qPCR was performed using iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (L000915B, Bio-Rad, USA) with CFX96 Real-Time system (Bio-Rad, USA). β-Actin was used as a reference gene for normalization. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

LncRNA Knockdown in Neutrophils

The antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting PLIN3-AS1 were obtained from Sangon (Shanghai, China), the sequences of ASOs are shown in Supplementary Table 3. ASOs were transfected into isolated neutrophils using Amaxa Nucleofector (Lonza, USA). At 12 h post-electroporation, total RNA was collected to assess the knockdown efficiency of the target lncRNA. Subsequently, neutrophils were treated for 2 h with or without 1 µg/mL LPS (L2630, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) to simulate the sepsis model, each group contained three replicates.

Results

Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes in Neutrophil During Sepsis

To investigate transcriptional alterations in neutrophils during sepsis, we collected peripheral blood from four sepsis patients and four healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). Neutrophils were isolated, and flow cytometry confirmed viability > 90% (Figure S1a) and purity > 90% (Figure S1b). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol and subjected to RNA-seq, yielding expression data for 14,267 mRNAs and 7,174 lncRNAs. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between septic and healthy neutrophils based on both mRNA and lncRNA expression profiles (Fig. 1a), indicating pronounced transcriptional differences between these groups. To further analyze these alterations, we conducted differential expression analysis using a threshold of p.adjust < 0.05 and an absolute fold change > 2 (|log2FC| > 1). This approach identified 404 significantly differentially expressed mRNAs, including 220 upregulated and 184 downregulated mRNAs. Additionally, 64 lncRNAs were significantly differentially expressed, with 11 upregulated and 53 downregulated (Fig. 1b–c). Collectively, these findings establish a comprehensive profile of altered mRNA and lncRNA expression in septic neutrophils, providing a foundation for subsequent investigations.

Neutrophils Undergo Metabolic Reprogramming During Sepsis

To elucidate the functional roles of differentially expressed mRNAs in neutrophils, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on all 404 differentially expressed mRNAs. These genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to neutrophil activation and inflammatory signaling, including myeloid leukocyte activation and positive regulation of NF-κB transcription factor activity. In addition, metabolic pathways such as pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis and starch and sucrose metabolism were also enriched (Fig. 2a-b). Moreover, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on up- and down-regulated mRNAs, especially focused on alteration of upregulated mRNAs. Our results demonstrate that upregulated mRNAs are primarily involved in neutrophil activation and neutrophil-mediated immune responses. Furthermore, we identified substantial enrichment of genes associated with intracellular metabolic processes, including glycolytic process via carbohydrate catabolic process, and glucose catabolic process, consistent with previously published findings. Notably, we also observed alterations in additional metabolic pathways, including nucleoside phosphate catabolic process, pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis, ribonucleoside diphosphate catabolic process, ADP metabolic process and biosynthesis of amino acids (Fig. 2c-d). Conversely, downregulated mRNAs showed significant enrichment in GO biological processes related to cell migration and signal transduction (Figure S2a-b).

Neutrophils undergo metabolic reprogramming during sepsis. (a) Top 30 GO and (b) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of all differentially expressed mRNAs . (c) Top 30 GO and (d) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of upregulated mRNAs in sepsis

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that during sepsis, neutrophils not only participate in anti-infection immune responses but also undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming. Notably, these metabolic changes extend beyond canonical energy-generating pathways such as glycolysis, reflecting a global remodeling of neutrophil metabolism that involves lipid and amino acid metabolism, as well as cofactor and nucleotide metabolic pathways.

WGCNA Reveals Hub mRNAs in Sepsis

While previous GO and KEGG enrichment analyses focused on functional annotation of differentially expressed genes, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on RNA-seq data from 14,267 mRNAs to identify genes closely associated with sepsis. A soft-thresholding power of 12 was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion (Figure S3a). Hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting (minimum module size = 200) were applied to define co-expression modules, and highly correlated modules (r > 0.7; dissimilarity < 0.3) were merged (Fig. 3a). This process grouped all genes into 14 co-expression modules (Figure S3b). Module-module correlation analysis and module–sepsis correlation analysis identified the MEred module as strongly correlated with sepsis (Fig. 3b, Figure S3c), thereby narrowing the candidate gene pool for further investigation.

To further validate the association between these module genes and sepsis, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on genes within these modules. Functional annotation revealed that the MEred module was significantly enriched in pathways including myeloid leukocyte activation, C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway, Endocytosis, RNA processing, Pentose phosphate pathway and Glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis - chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate (Fig. 3c-d), indicating its involvement in energy generation, metabolism, inflammatory signaling, and RNA processing. These analyses confirm that MEred module is indeed closely associated with neutrophil functional responses in sepsis.

To identify key mRNAs within these modules, we defined hub genes. Based on gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) with thresholds of |GS| > 0.8 and |MM| > 0.8 within the MEred module (Fig. 3e), we identified 724 hub mRNAs that were strongly associated with sepsis. We then intersected these hub mRNAs with differentially expressed mRNAs, yielding 177 hub differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs). To further validate our analysis, we measured the expression changes of these genes in a public dataset (GSE28750) [25]. Among the 177 hub DEmRNAs, 96 showed significant alterations in this dataset (|log2FoldChange| > 0.5 and p.adjust < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 4). In conclusion, these results indicate that these hub genes may represent key regulatory molecules governing neutrophil function during sepsis progression.

Sepsis-related Metabolic mRNAs are Co-expressed With Hub DElncRNAs

Recent studies have established that immune cells participate broadly in various pathophysiological processes through metabolic reprogramming, including during sepsis. In sepsis, diverse cell types undergo multiple metabolic alterations, such as the respiratory burst, glycolysis, and lipid remodeling in neutrophils. To further characterize metabolic reprogramming in septic neutrophils, we retrieved all genes annotated under the “metabolism” category in the KEGG database, yielding 1,227 metabolism-associated genes. By intersecting these with differentially expressed genes and hub genes, we identified ten metabolism-associated hub DEmRNAs (Fig. 4a). Notably, these genes were implicated not only in lipid metabolism (INPP5B, INPP5K, PLD4, VAPA) and carbohydrate metabolism (PGAM1, PYGL, UAP1), but also in amino acid metabolism (GSR, DGLUCY, IDO1). We next examined their expression patterns, which revealed that VAPA, PGAM1, PYGL, and GSR were upregulated in septic neutrophils, whereas INPP5B, INPP5K, PLD4, UAP1, DGLUCY, and IDO1 were downregulated (Figure S4a). Generally, the expression of these genes is tightly regulated, and lncRNAs are known to serve as key regulators of gene expression through multiple mechanisms. To identify potential lncRNAs that may regulate these metabolism-associated hub mRNAs, similarly we performed WGCNA on RNA-seq data from 7,174 lncRNAs to identify lncRNAs closely associated with sepsis. A soft-thresholding power of 12 was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion (Figure S4b). Hierarchical clustering and dynamic tree cutting (minimum module size = 200) were applied to define co-expression modules, and highly correlated modules (r > 0.7; dissimilarity < 0.3) were merged. This process grouped all lncRNAs into 10 co-expression modules (Fig. 4b). Module- module correlation analysis and module–sepsis correlation analysis identified the MEbrown module as strongly correlated with sepsis, with a correlation coefficient of 0.86 (Fig. 4c, Figure S4c). Mirroring the strategy applied to mRNA modules, we computed GS and MM within these lncRNA modules, identifying 17 hub lncRNAs (|GS| > 0.9 and |MM| > 0.9) (Fig. 4d). To prioritize candidates, we intersected the hub lncRNAs with differentially expressed lncRNAs, yielding 6 differentially expressed hub lncRNAs, consisting of 1 up-regulated and 5 down-regulated lncRNAs. To identify putative interactions, we performed correlation analysis between differentially expressed hub mRNAs and hub lncRNAs. Significant relationships were defined using a p.adjust < 0.05 and |r| > 0.9. Cis-acting relationships were assigned to pairs within 1 Mb on the same chromosome; all others were considered trans-acting. The most highly correlated pairs were IDO1-ENST00000647691 and PYGL-ENST00000624682. Notably, these correlations are all trans-acting (Fig. 4e-f). In summary, we have identified differentially expressed hub mRNAs and lncRNAs in septic neutrophils and constructed a co-expression network linking lncRNAs to metabolism-associated mRNAs. Our findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying metabolic reprogramming in neutrophils during sepsis, with particular emphasis on amino acid metabolic alterations.

PLIN3-AS1 Knockdown Attenuates GSR mRNA Upregulation in LPS-stimulated Neutrophils

Previous results indicate that neutrophils undergo amino acid metabolic reprogramming during sepsis. Among the three identified amino acid metabolism-related genes, GSR was upregulated, whereas DGLUCY and IDO1 were downregulated. We focused on the upregulated gene GSR and its potential regulatory relationship with the upregulated lncRNA ENST00000591657 (PLIN3-AS1). To validate this co-expression relationship, we collected neutrophils from an independent cohort of 10 healthy controls and 27 septic patients and performed RT-qPCR. The results were consistent with the RNA-seq data: both GSR and PLIN3-AS1 were significantly elevated in septic neutrophils (Fig. 5a). We next isolated neutrophils from healthy peripheral blood and treated them with or without LPS to establish an in vitro sepsis model. LPS treatment significantly increased TNF-α expression, confirming successful model induction. Consistent with the clinical phenotype, LPS stimulation also upregulated both PLIN3-AS1 and GSR (Fig. 5b). We then electroporated neutrophils with ASO targeting PLIN3-AS1 and assessed knockdown efficiency by RT-qPCR. PLIN3-AS1 expression was reduced to 0.4-fold or less compared with the scrambled control (Fig. 5c). Cells were then treated with or without LPS (1 µg/mL), knockdown of PLIN3-AS1 by ASO significantly suppressed LPS-induced upregulation of GSR, and also reduced TNF-α release from neutrophils (Fig. 5d). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PLIN3-AS1 regulates neutrophil function during sepsis by modulating the amino acid metabolism gene GSR mRNA expression, providing preliminary evidence for a regulatory axis between PLIN3-AS1 and GSR.

Discussion

Neutrophils serve as primary sentinels in host defense, employing ROS production, phagocytosis, and NETosis to combat pathogens [9, 26, 27]. Under steady-state conditions, neutrophils have a short half-life [28]; however, during sepsis, inflammatory mediators such as LPS, C5a, TNF-α, and FasL [29–32] prolong their survival, enabling extended tissue activities. To meet heightened bioenergetic demands, neutrophils undergo profound metabolic reprogramming [33]. Sepsis shifts neutrophil metabolism toward an oxidative burst fueled by NADPH oxidase assembly, while both glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) are concurrently upregulated, underscoring the multifaceted nature of this metabolic adaptation [34]. Collectively, these findings highlight that elucidating metabolic reprogramming in neutrophils is critical for advancing our understanding of sepsis pathogenesis [35–40]. Our transcriptomic analysis reveals that sepsis induces broad metabolic alterations in neutrophils. Beyond expected pathways such as glycolysis and lipid metabolism, we observed significant changes in amino acid metabolism. For instance, Glutathione-disulfide reductase(GSR), a glutathione metabolism gene critical for bacterial defense [41] and essential for neutrophil respiratory burst [42], was upregulated. GSR has also been implicated in neutrophil NETs formation [42], suggesting its involvement in multiple fundamental neutrophil functions. Moreover, our results demonstrate that indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) is also closely associated with neutrophils in sepsis. IDO1 is a critical enzyme that regulates the catabolism of tryptophan by converting it into kynurenine, a core regulatory factor in immune suppression [43]. Recent studies have shown that IDO1 regulates neutrophil NETs formation to promote Echinococcus multilocularis infection [44], and similarly, IDO1 has also been associated with inflammation in other cell types [45, 46]. D-glutamate cyclase(DGLUCY), an amino acid metabolism enzyme that converts D-glutamate to 5-oxo-D-proline, has previously been reported only in the context of diabetic foot ulcers [47]. Our results also demonstrate that DGLUCY participates in metabolic reprogramming of neutrophils during sepsis. Despite these observations, however, the precise regulation of amino acid metabolism-associated hub mRNAs in septic neutrophils remains poorly understood.

Over the past decade, accumulating evidence has established that lncRNAs are widely expressed and play key roles in gene regulation. LncRNAs function through specific subcellular localization and molecular interactions with DNA, RNA, and proteins, ultimately reprogramming gene expression profiles and modulating protein activities [48, 49]. Although numerous studies have established that lncRNAs are intricately linked to metabolism [50, 51], the relationship between lncRNAs and amino acid metabolic reprogramming in neutrophils under inflammatory conditions remains poorly characterized. We therefore calculated the co-expression relationships between mRNAs and lncRNAs in order to identify potential co-expression pairs. Ultimately, we focused on the amino acid metabolic enzyme GSR and the lncRNA PLIN3-AS1, and found that PLIN3-AS1 significantly regulates GSR expression in LPS-treated neutrophils, targeting PLIN3-AS1 ultimately reduced TNF-α release from neutrophils. These findings provide new mechanistic insights into the metabolic alterations of septic neutrophils, particularly in amino acid metabolism, and point toward future research directions.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. The sample size for RNA-seq may not be sufficiently large; although we validated our conclusions in an independent cohort, it remains possible that some key genes were excluded due to high variability. Moreover, while we demonstrated that PLIN3-AS1 regulates GSR expression, direct evidence for regulatory relationships between other differentially expressed hub mRNAs and lncRNAs is lacking. Additionally, lncRNAs are known to regulate mRNA expression through multiple mechanisms, including epigenetic modification, transcriptional regulation, post-transcriptional processing, and translational regulation [49, 52, 53]. However, how PLIN3-AS1 specifically regulates the expression of GSR remains unclear, and this will be the focus of our future investigations.

In summary, our study identifies a putative lncRNA–mRNA network associated with immunometabolic alterations in sepsis, orchestrated by a precisely coordinated lncRNA–mRNA network. These results establish lncRNAs as important regulators of neutrophil metabolism and function in sepsis. Future work should focus on elucidating their precise mechanisms and guide future mechanistic studies.
