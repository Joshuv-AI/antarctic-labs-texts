North American “warming holes” and European heat during abrupt Holocene cooling events in the Atlantic
Authors: Bryan N Shuman, Ioana C Stefanescu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent decades, Europe has warmed rapidly while a prominent ‘warming hole’ has muted changes in eastern North America. Paradoxically, the pattern coincides with cooling in the North Atlantic, even though ocean cooling is commonly expected to chill Europe, not North America. The regional departures from global trends and expectations may have informative precedents in millennial-scale climate anomalies during the Holocene. Here, circum-Atlantic records reveal that counter-intuitive European warming developed amid rapid cooling of the North Atlantic and North America from 5.8-4.8 ka and after 2.2 ka. The anomalies track rapid reductions in the flow of North Atlantic Deep Water southwest of Iceland and appear in independent but highly correlated records of mean sea-surface temperatures, continental temperatures, and hydroclimate changes from both sides of the Atlantic. The patterns confirm the importance of millennial climate variability during the Holocene and the risk of climate disruptions around the North Atlantic today.Subject terms: Palaeoclimate, Palaeoceanography

Introduction

As current anthropogenic climate trends increase the risk of rapid climatic state shifts, unusual patterns such as ‘warming holes,’ intense heatwaves, and extreme droughts differentiate regional responses1–5 (Fig. 1). The patterns of interglacial climates may provide useful analogs for understanding such regional departures from global trends6,7. Looking beyond the slow, global evolution of Holocene climates can reveal controls on abrupt shifts and regional differences possible during warm Earth system states8. Holocene climate changes were likely part of a power-law spectrum of variability, including significant millennial variability, particularly at regional scales7,9. Mismatches between paleoclimate reconstructions and orbitally-forced Holocene climate simulations underscore the need to re-examine any potential millennial-scale dynamics10,11 by identifying and evaluating benchmark patterns useful for testing numerical and conceptual models12,13.

21st century anomalies reveal the paradox of faster warming in Europe than in North America amid North Atlantic Ocean cooling.

Annual (January-December) sea-surface temperature (SST) anomalies (A) show cooling in the central North Atlantic15 and near Florida, but warming in other areas, such as the Labrador Sea. June-August air temperature (TJJA) anomalies at 1000 mb (B) show rapid warming in Europe but cooling in North America’s mid-latitude ‘warming hole,’ including negative summer daily maximum temperature trends2,4,5. Annual precipitation (Pann) anomalies (C) include increases in eastern North America but drought in Europe and California1. The anomalies show 2001–2020 CE compared to 1951–2000 CE as represented in NCAR/NCEP Reanalysis I56.

The challenge is not simply recognizing that millennial changes took place during the Holocene, but rather to develop synoptic perspectives aimed at predicting their spatial patterns, regional impacts, and implications for the future. To do so, we examine a geographically gridded network of pollen-inferred summer warmth reconstructions, representing growing degree days above 5 °C (GDD5)13, as well as an updated network of sea-surface temperature (SST) reconstructions spanning the North Atlantic Ocean. The SST reconstructions, updated from the Temp12ka dataset14, include all available reconstructions from 8.0–0.8 ka to maximize geographic coverage. We use the data to evaluate correlations among continental climates and broad oceanic changes within the western and central North Atlantic, although sparse data coverage prevents analyses of some relevant spatial patterns, such as those directly related to the Gulf Stream15.

Here, the network of records allows us to study millennial climate variations that were sufficiently large and extensive to coordinate impacts in disjunct ecosystems across two continents. We examine the hypotheses that interglacial instability affected North Atlantic climates and that North Atlantic cooling, such as that induced by slowed Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), can chill Europe amid a globally warm climate. Models are uncertain about such risks during interglacial climates12,16. We focus on summer patterns of the past 8 ka after the influence of the northern hemisphere ice sheets diminished and find patterns consistent with current North Atlantic cooling, the summer ‘warming hole’ in North America, coincident extreme heat in Europe, and affiliated hydroclimate changes (Fig. 1).

Results

Opposite sign millennial changes in Europe and North America

Summer temperature (GDD5) changes over the past 8000 years include millennia with different direction anomalies across northwest Europe and mid-latitude eastern North America (ENA; Fig. 2). Over the long term, summer warmth declined across northwest Europe (orange line, Fig. 2A) and, to a lesser degree, mid-latitude ENA (blue line, Fig. 2A). Previous work showed that a stepwise increase in temperature interrupted the trends in the mid-Holocene13. However, the mid-Holocene increase developed primarily in Europe as ENA cooled from 5.8-4.8 ka (blue bar, Fig. 2A). Abrupt warming in Europe and cooling in ENA was then repeated at 2.2 ka (gray bars, Fig. 2A). Both events increased the thermal difference between the continents in summer. The changes after 2.2 ka warmed Europe but cooled ENA and the North Atlantic during the Roman Warm Period (RWP) and Medieval Climate Anomaly (MCA), demonstrating large-scale reorganization but not universal warmth17.

Mean SSTs across the central and western North Atlantic declined rapidly as Europe warmed at 5.8 and 2.2 ka (black line, Fig. 2B). The mean rate of central-western SST change (from southwest of Iceland to the Gulf of Mexico, as defined by the large inset box in Fig. 2D, E) correlates negatively with the European rate of GDD5 change per millennium (Fig. 2C; Pearson product moment correlation, r = −0.39, 95% CI: −0.58 - −0.15, df = 61). Maps show expansive North Atlantic cooling, as well as warming like that today (Fig. 1C) west of Greenland and along the Labrador coast (Fig. 2D). Linear regression indicates that for every 1 °C of cooling in the central-western North Atlantic mean, northwest European warmth increases by the equivalent of 1 °C over ~100 days per year: ΔGDD5 = (−101 ± 28 °C days °CSST−1 x ΔSST) - 31 ± 7 °C days where Δ represents the rate of change per 1000 years (adjusted R2 = 0.16, df = 61, p = 0.0006). Other analyses of SSTs affirm the rapid mid-Holocene cooling8,18,19, although individual records commonly differ from the means because of spatial variability typical of the surface ocean.

The temperature changes after 5.8 and 2.2 ka also correspond to effective moisture changes (Figs. 3, 4), which are spatially similar to those that exist today (Fig. 1). The oceanic changes (Fig. 3A, B) and reduced ENA temperatures (Fig. 3C) correlate significantly with an increase in mean effective moisture across ENA (increased lake levels, reduced dust, Fig. 3D)20. The increase likely facilitated cooling there by reducing sensible heating2 (Fig. 3C). Reduced effective moisture may have produced the opposite outcome in other regions, such as California (Fig. 3D) and Europe (Fig. 4). Trees submerged in Sierra Nevada lakes have been interpreted as evidence of severe droughts in California from 5.8–4.5 ka and after 2.2 ka21,22 (histogram, Fig. 3D). From 4.8–3.5 ka, the reverse hydroclimate pattern developed with drying common in ENA20 (Fig. 3D) and sedimentary evidence of pluvial conditions in the Sierra Nevada23. Likewise, a previous synthesis of lake-level records from Europe24 indicates a strong correlation between summer warmth and effective moisture (Fig. 4A, B; Pearson product-moment correlation, r = -0.77, 95% CI: −0.88 - −0.60, df = 35). Low effective moisture extended furthest across Europe during peak warmth from 5.8–4.6 ka. Afterwards, lakes rose widely except during renewed warming and drying from 2.2–1.4 ka (Fig. 4B). The correlations among European and North American temperature changes (Fig. 2A), with North Atlantic SSTs (Fig. 3B, C), and with the sedimentary evidence of hydroclimate changes (Figs. 3, 4) show no significant lags in the pollen response underlying the temperature reconstructions and indicating rapid terrestrial ecosystem consequences.

Context of AMOC reorganization

The millennial temperature anomalies during the Holocene appear related to AMOC reorganization, even if a large-scale shutdown did not take place (blue bar, Fig. 3). North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) flow rates declined, at least in some areas, when much of the North Atlantic Ocean cooled (Fig. 3A, B). The flow rate inferred from the mean size of sortable silt from southwest of Iceland25 (core MD2251, Fig. 3A) correlates significantly with our mean SST reconstruction (Figs. 2B, 3B)(Pearson product moment correlation, r = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.67–0.86, df = 61). The correlation exceeds the 95% range of 1000 random surrogate time series with the same spectral properties (see Supplementary Information) and suggests that the MD2251 record could represent a broad North Atlantic dynamic. The detailed sortable silt record from core MD225125 linearly predicts not only the mean SST history (Fig. 3B) but also changes in North American temperature and hydroclimate (Fig. 3C, D) and the timing of the GDD5 changes in northwest Europe (Fig. 4).

Other mineralogical indicators of NADW flow26 and an additional set of sortable silt records from near Iceland27 confirm the correlation with SSTs (Fig. 5A, B) expressed in core MD2251 (Fig. 3A). They indicate that Iceland-Scotland (I-S) overflow, a component of the AMOC, slowed at 5.8 and 2.2 ka, especially at water depths of <1.6 km27, even though overflow flow remained persistently high elsewhere28,29 (see Supplementary Information). The continuous flow rates in these other areas agree with 231Pa/230Th records that indicate negligible Holocene changes in deep-water residence times30. However, carbon isotopes show limited NADW penetration near Bermuda31 and Ireland32 from 5.8–4.8 ka (see Supplementary Information). Changes afterwards involved a rapid acceleration of Labrador Sea Water flow, another component of AMOC, off eastern Canada33. The AMOC flow signals appear reproducible (Fig. 5B), but dependent on location, which reveals spatial variability and Holocene proxy differences tied to diverging flow, penetration, and residence time variations, which have analogs in variability among sites and proxies during Pleistocene abrupt changes34 and today35.

Discussion

The cool North America, warm Europe pattern at ca. 5.8–4.8 ka and after 2.2 ka (Fig. 2) resembles the 21st century anomalies (Fig. 1), including warm water near Labrador and southwest Greenland but large areas of ocean cooling in the central-western North Atlantic (Fig. 2D). Correlations in the Holocene datasets indicate that the extensive Atlantic cooling (Fig. 3A) was consistently associated with reductions in I–S overflow (Fig. 3B), changes in continental temperatures (Fig. 3C), and an alteration of hydroclimates (Figs. 3D, 4B). The consistency emphasizes that similar linkages likely connect continental anomalies with ongoing AMOC and SST changes today. Our analysis indicates that the modern patterns may not be a chance alignment of stochastic anomalies, but rather a commonly recurring set of interconnected changes. Sensitivity simulations show that inclusion of accurate SSTs in climate models can reproduce the observed summer cooling and increased precipitation in ENA, extreme heat and drought in Europe, and severe drought in California2,3 (Fig. 1). Similarly, CMIP6 models do not accurately reproduce the modern pattern of oceanic cooling and, as a result, fail to generate today’s continental temperature and precipitation outcomes2. Paleoclimate simulations may also lack the relevant regional dynamics.

Potentially, as during recent extreme years today, a cold anomaly in the central North Atlantic favored increased wind speeds and heat flux into Europe3. Furthermore, heat fluxes to Europe may have increased on millennial scales if proximal sectors of the AMOC compensated for reductions in others28, such as took place over the past 3 ka east and west of Iceland36. The state of the North Atlantic may have been most important, however, for determining precipitation changes on the continents2. The effective moisture increase in ENA (Fig. 3C) and decrease in Europe (Fig. 4B) after ca. 5.8 ka may have determined the continental temperature difference by changing energy partitioning: cooling North America while warming Europe. Common Era land use likely amplified the effects by further warming Europe and cooling North America after 2.2 ka37.

Understanding the ultimate drivers of the abrupt changes and millennial anomalies will require further consideration of at least three potential mechanisms. First, high-frequency external forcing by volcanic and solar variability could be a candidate for creating millennial departures from orbitally forced trends38,39. Second, even without such forcing, internal ocean and atmosphere variability may have affected circum-Atlantic pressure gradients and attendant regional climate anomalies by stimulating changes in the subpolar gyre40 or in the frequency or mean state of processes such as the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO)41–43. Feedbacks related to these dynamics could either drive or develop from AMOC changes, including by altering meltwater fluxes from Greenland44 or wind stress over the North Atlantic45. NAO-like dynamics can alter the temperature differences between the continents today46, and may also explain some of the additional spatial patterning in the mapped anomalies (Fig. 2D, E). Finally, Earth system thresholds, such as related to ocean-atmosphere-vegetation feedbacks and the weakening of the west African monsoon during the mid-Holocene, could have either caused or amplified rapid changes to atmospheric pressure gradients and wind fields connected to the North Atlantic and adjacent regions8,47 (Fig. 5).

Related circulation changes at ca. 5.8 ka may explain cooling in northern and eastern areas of Greenland (Fig. 5C, D) as well as extensive dust changes in North America and Africa (Fig. 5E, G). Dust fluxes from the Great Plains48,49 declined during a moist period of soil formation from 5.8–4.8 ka (Fig. 5E), consistent with the moisture increases across ENA (Fig. 3D) and a westward retreat of the eastern margin of the prairies50 (Fig. 5F). Cooling the North Atlantic likely favored the increase in Great Plains precipitation2, while also shifting sub-tropical precipitation southward8. The dynamic could have increased dust fluxes relative to precessional forcing at ca. 5.8-4.8 ka by drying parts of west Africa from 19-23°N51,52 (Fig. 5G) while increasing precipitation to the south in Ghana53 (Fig. 5F). In model simulations, these abrupt changes represent a climatically-coherent response of the sub-tropical Easterly Jets to northern cooling, which reduced precipitation in the western Sahel8 and does not require that all other African changes were synchronous8,53. Together, however, the changes around the North Atlantic help to define a “mid-Holocene transition”54 as a replicated feature of multiple, representative reconstructions connecting AMOC to high- and mid-latitude changes and the end of the African Humid Period (Fig. 5); the step changes may represent an Earth system threshold, but could equally well represent constructive interference of long-term trends and millennial events (e.g., Fig. 5E, F), including in Africa (see Supplementary Information).

The rapid changes point to risks today that AMOC reorganization could underlie and thus continue to amplify regional extremes today, from intense European heatwaves to California droughts (Fig. 1). The Holocene changes were no less significant for warming rather than cooling Europe when NADW flow slowed. For example, the major tree genus, Ulmus (elm), declined widely in association with warming and drying in Europe at ca. 5.8 ka, thus reinvigorating the hypothesis of climatic causality for this much debated biogeographical event55 (Fig. 4C). In North America, growing-degree days declined substantially in the midwestern USA, the current agricultural center of the continent (Fig. 2C)20,41. At the same time, severe drought reduced critical western water supplies (Fig. 3D)21,22. The RWP and MCA represent a similar dynamic, underscoring the importance of low-frequency changes during the Common Era that produced regionally varying outcomes17 (Figs. 2, 3). Submerged trees in California from these intervals provide a stark warning about the potential for regional outcomes to become far more extreme than expected based on historic droughts21,22. The Holocene hydroclimate changes also confirm that soil moisture and latent heating could play an important role in creating regional departures from global temperature trends today (Fig. 1). In the context of the warm interglacial climate, North Atlantic cooling and overturning reorganization facilitated moistening and cooling in North America (Fig. 3C, D) while drying and warming Europe (Fig. 4A, B) despite expectations that the opposite would have occurred. Diagnosing such changes during the not-so ‘stable’ Holocene will help anticipate future regional outcomes over large portions of the globe.

Methods

Modern climate trends

Maps of modern climate trends around the North Atlantic Ocean (Fig. 1) were generated using the NCAR/NCEP Reanalysis I56. The differences between CE 1951–2000 and 2001–2020 were calculated using online tools provided by the NOAA Physical Sciences Laboratory, Boulder, Colorado, USA, and obtained as NetCDF files from their website at https://psl.noaa.gov/. The focus on 21st century differences from the second half of the 20th century avoids effects of the warm, dry CE 1930s on the ‘warming hole’ and related North American temperature statistics2,4,5.

Holocene temperatures

The 2° x 2° latitude/longitude grid of growing degree day base 5 °C (GDD5) reconstructions from Marsicek et al.13 were extracted from the NOAA Paleoclimate database (https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/paleo-search/study/22992) and mapped using R version 4.3.157. The reconstructions include 100-year time steps spanning the Holocene based on GDD5 reconstructions from 584 pollen records that passed a significance screen based on comparisons of each reconstruction with a distribution of random null models for each site13. We map the changes for the 5.8 and 4.8 ka intervals (Fig. 2) as well as 2.2 ka (Fig. SI2) based on the change over 600 years by subtracting an initial interval (6.0, 5.3, or 2.5 ka) from a post-change interval (5.4, 4.9, or 1.9 ka, respectively). The changes were mapped after classifying the anomalies into zero change (−50 to 50 °C days), moderate change (50–100 °C days magnitude) and large changes ( > 100 °C days magnitude).

To produce European and North American GDD5 time series, grid cells from the target regions were extracted and averaged per century. For each century, beginning at 9 ka, 75% of the available grid cells in each region were randomly selected and averaged using base functions in R. The process was repeated 100 times to generate a distribution of mean GDD5 reconstructions for each region, which accounts for spatial variability and differences in time and reconstruction uncertainty among the grid cells13; the time series in Fig. 2A show the median and 95% range of the regional means. The CE 1950 (0 ka) value was subtracted from each of the regional means prior to plotting as anomalies. GDD5 units represent the mean annual sum of degree days, which are total daily mean increases in degrees (°C or K) above 5 °C per year.

To produce time series of North Atlantic Ocean sea-surface temperatures (SSTs), all SST reconstructions from the Temp12ka database14 were combined with additional SST reconstructions from the region that spanned from 8.0–0.9 ka43,58–60 (Table SI1). The additional records were either too new or did not span a sufficient duration of the Holocene for inclusion in the Temp12ka dataset. All data were obtained from either the PANGAEA or NOAA Paleoclimate databases, and most derived from two sources: Temperature 12ka Database (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0445-3)14 and the database of Holocene temperature reconstructions for northeastern North America and the northwestern Atlantic (10.1594/PANGAEA.905293)58. Additional individual records were then identified and added43,59,60.

For consistency with the GDD5 data, each SST record was interpolated to 100-yr time steps using base functions in R in accordance with recommendations61. Maps show their differences based on the same 600-yr time intervals as used for the GDD5 data. Likewise, all SST reconstructions from southwest of Iceland to the Gulf of Mexico (south of 65°N and west of 15°W) were averaged using the same iterative approach, based on 100 iterations of 75% of the records to create a mean SST time series and 95% confidence interval. The CE 1950 (0 ka) value was again subtracted from the mean prior to plotting as anomalies. To confirm the spatial extent and coherence of the anomalies, the same approach was used with the prcomp function in R to conduct a principal components analysis (PCA), except that all 77 SST records spanning from 8.0–0.9 ka north of 20°N were included (Fig. SI3). All SST reconstructions were included regardless of seasonality to ensure maximum geographic coverage and because the magnitudes of millennial anomalies are consistent across seasons, but the Supplementary Information includes versions of the analysis using only annual (Fig. SI1, n = 26 in our core study region) and only summer SST reconstructions (Fig. SI2, n = 7 in our core study region).

Rates of change and correlations

Rates of change (Fig. 2C) were calculated by subtracting the change in the regional mean GDD5 and SST reconstructions over 1000-year intervals, centered on the 500-year mid-point for each interval. Time series in Fig. 3 compare our mean SST and the previously published a) NADW flow rate reconstruction based on sortable silt size data from core MD2251 from the central Gadar Drift, south of Iceland25, b) eastern North America mean temperature of the warmest month (MTWM) reconstruction based on well-resolved fossil pollen stratigraphies between Wyoming and Maine20, and c) eastern North America mean effective moisture based on the average z-scores of dust and lake-level records from Wyoming to Massachusetts20. The correlations of the NADW flow rate with the SST, MTWM, and moisture reconstructions were compared with those calculated based on 1000 random surrogate time series with a power-law spectrum with the same spectral slope as the actual reconstructions; the surrogates were generated and correlations compared using the corit package in R61. The correlations were compared based on both the raw (Fig. SI4) and detrended timeseries (see Fig. SI5). To compare the different sign GDD5 changes in Europe and North America, the absolute values of the 1000-yr rates of change were also compared (Fig. SI6).
