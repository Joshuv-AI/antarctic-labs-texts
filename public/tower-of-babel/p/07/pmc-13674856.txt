Cellular Dynamics and Dentine Bridge Formation Following Direct Pulp Capping With MTA and Biodentine in a Murine Model
Authors: Amnah Sajdeya, Joshua Milgram, Nurit Kot‐Limon, Itzhak Abramovitz, Amnon Sharir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimFormation of a mineralized dentine bridge is the hallmark of successful direct pulp capping (DPC). Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) and Biodentine are widely used capping materials, but in vivo evidence comparing their regenerative potential remains inconclusive. This study used a controlled murine model to compare dentine bridge formation and pulp response to MTA and Biodentine.MethodsDPC was performed on maxillary first molars of mice using either MTA or Biodentine. Reparative dentine bridge formation was assessed by micro‐computed tomography (bridge volume, mineral content, density); histology (morphology, collagen deposition); immunohistochemistry and RNAscope in situ hybridization (odontoblast activity); TUNEL assay and EdU labelling (apoptosis, proliferation, cell retention); and fluorochrome incorporation (dentine formation dynamics).ResultBoth MTA and Biodentine supported dentine bridge formation. Biodentine induced significantly greater bridge volume and mineral content at 28 and 56 days, while mineral density was similar. Histological analysis showed more complete bridges with fewer tunnel defects in Biodentine‐treated teeth. TUNEL assays indicated a trend toward lower apoptosis in Biodentine‐treated pulps, while EdU labelling showed similar proliferation rates. Label‐retaining cell analysis revealed reduced pulpal cell retention in MTA‐treated teeth. Biodentine‐treated samples displayed higher dentine sialophosphoprotein expression at Day 8, thicker predentin at Day 14, and more extensive collagen deposition at Day 28. Fluorochrome labelling confirmed earlier and more robust dentine deposition with Biodentine.ConclusionsIn a controlled murine DPC model, Biodentine promoted greater dentine bridge formation and more favourable pulp regeneration than MTA. These effects may be explained by reduced apoptosis, greater pulpal cell retention, and enhanced odontoblast activity. The findings support Biodentine as a stronger stimulator of dentine regeneration and underscore the value of murine models for mechanistic studies of pulp healing.Keywords: Biodentine, dentine bridge, direct pulp capping, MTA, odontoblasts, tertiary dentine

Introduction

Direct pulp capping (DPC) seeks to preserve pulp vitality by enabling controlled healing and promoting the formation of a mineralized dentine bridge that protects against bacterial irritants and prevents inflammation or necrosis (Hanna et al. 2020; Kakehashi et al. 1965). Contemporary bioceramic materials have transformed DPC outcomes by providing improved biocompatibility, reduced cytotoxicity, and favourable interactions with pulpal cells compared to calcium hydroxide (Silva et al. 2023). These materials induce less necrosis and inflammation, sustain deeper pulp tissue viability, and create more stable, durable seals (Hanna et al. 2020; Tran et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2023; Yaemkleebbua et al. 2019).

Mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) has long served as the standard bioceramic material in vital pulp therapy and is strongly supported by clinical and histological evidence demonstrating reliable dentine bridge formation and favourable pulpal responses (Komora et al. 2024; Kundzina et al. 2017; Nair et al. 2008). Despite these advantages, MTA's long setting time, handling challenges, and discoloration associated with bismuth oxide limit its clinical convenience (Fasoulas et al. 2023; Tran et al. 2012). Biodentine, a tricalcium silicate–based cement containing zirconium oxide, was designed to overcome these limitations. It sets faster, handles more predictably, minimizes discoloration risk, and releases high levels of calcium ions that may enhance mineralization and promote odontoblast differentiation (Kim et al. 2020). Dentine bridges produced by bioceramic materials are typically thicker and less porous than those formed with calcium hydroxide (Tran et al. 2012).

Although both MTA and Biodentine show a high success rate in DPC (Cushley et al. 2021; Katge and Patil 2017; Linu et al. 2017; Nowicka et al. 2013), more sensitive assessments, such as CBCT and histology, indicate that Biodentine forms a thicker, more developed dentine bridge (Hoseinifar et al. 2020; Nowicka et al. 2015). In vitro studies report similar effects on pulp cell viability (Attik et al. 2014; Corral Nuñez et al. 2014; Sismanoglu and Ercal 2023), although some show higher viability, migration, and proliferation in Biodentine‐treated cells (Abuarqoub et al. 2022; Birant et al. 2021; Tomás‐Catalá et al. 2018). In vivo studies in pigs and rats show comparable inflammation and dentine formation. However, most comparative studies focus on structural endpoints, such as dentine bridge continuity, thickness, and the presence of tunnel defects or cellular inclusions (Minic et al. 2021; Shayegan et al. 2012; Tziafa et al. 2014), and rely largely on two‐dimensional (2D) sections. As a result, the three‐dimensional (3D) architecture of dentine bridges remains unexplored. These features provide useful static information but do not explain the cellular or molecular mechanisms that drive healing. The biological processes underlying reparative dentinogenesis, including the balance between early cell death, proliferation of pulpal cells, the recruitment or retention of progenitor‐like cells, and the activity of odontoblasts, remain insufficiently explored. Only a limited number of in vivo studies have investigated how different capping materials influence these processes (Kim et al. 2016; Paula et al. 2019; Yaemkleebbua et al. 2019), and even fewer have incorporated temporal tracking of dentine deposition or have integrated molecular and three‐dimensional imaging.

Understanding these cellular events is essential for interpreting differences in dentine bridge formation and for refining biomaterials to optimize clinical outcomes. Cell death directly affects the preservation of functional pulp tissue (Kitamura et al. 2003), while early proliferation contributes to repopulation of the exposed site (Babb et al. 2017; Tran et al. 2012). Label retaining cells (LRCs), a population associated with long lived or slowly cycling progenitor like cells, may play a role in reparative dentinogenesis by surviving the initial insult and contributing to later odontoblast‐like differentiation (Saito et al. 2013). Moreover, markers of odontoblast activity, such as dentine sialophosphoprotein (DSPP), reflect functional matrix secretion, and may advance our understanding of material dependent effects on reparative processes (Wang et al. 2023). Temporal mineralization studies using fluorochrome labelling further clarify the dynamics of bridge formation and the rate of tertiary dentine deposition (Pautke et al. 2007; Vidovic et al. 2017).

In an attempt to address these gaps, this study aimed to compare the pulpal response to MTA and Biodentine with a particular focus on the cellular events underlying dentine bridge formation. We examined apoptotic and proliferative activity, the persistence of pulpal LRCs, and the presence of functional odontoblasts through DSPP expression, together with imaging and histological assessments of dentine bridge formation, collagen matrix deposition, and the occurrence of tunnel defects and cellular inclusions. Using a controlled murine model and integrating μCT, histology, RNAscope, and fluorochrome‐based temporal labelling, this study sought to define material‐dependent differences in the early healing cascade and to clarify how these mechanisms contribute to the superior regenerative performance observed with Biodentine.

Methods

Experimental Design

The description of this animal study follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Endodontic Animal Studies (PRIASE) 2021 guidelines. C57BL/6JRCCHSD mice were housed in a specific pathogen‐free facility at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all procedures (protocol #MD‐22‐16 871‐4).

Direct Pulp Capping (DPC)

Direct pulp capping was performed on the right maxillary first molar at 8–10 weeks of age. Mice were anaesthetised by intraperitoneal (ip) injection of ketamine (Clorketam, 75 mg/kg, Vetoquinol, Magny‐Vernois, France) and medetomidine hydrochloride (Domitor, 1 mg/kg; Orion Pharma, Espoo, Finland) and anaesthesia was reversed at the end of the procedure with atipamezole (1 mg/kg; Orion Pharma, Espoo, Finland) (Arras et al. 2001). Mice were placed in a supine position, and the mouth was opened with a custom‐made retractor. Local infiltration anaesthesia (mepivacaine, 7.5 mg/mL, Septodont Saint‐Maur‐des‐Fossés, France) was administered, and the tooth was isolated with cotton pellets. The DPC procedure was performed under a surgical microscope at 10× magnification (Weck Surgical Systems, North Carolina, USA). Access was obtained with a high‐speed handpiece (200 000 rpm) and round carbide bur (FG1/4; Strauss Diamond Instruments, Raanana, Israel) under sterile air–water spray coolant. Drilling stopped when the pulp was visible, and a #10 K‐file (Mani, Japan) was used to expose the pulp. The pulp was irrigated with sterile saline, and bleeding was controlled with light pressure from a sterile saline–moistened cotton pellet. MTA (Dentsply Tulsa Dental, Tulsa, USA) or Biodentine (Septodont, Saint‐Maur‐des‐Fossés, France) was mixed according to the manufacturer's instructions and applied to the exposed site. A light cure glass ionomer liner (3M Vitrebond, Minnesota, USA) was placed over the capping material, followed by acid etching (3M ESPE Scotchbond, Neuss, Germany), adhesive application (3M ESPE Single Bond, Neuss, Germany), and composite restoration (Filtek Z250; 3M, St. Paul, MN, USA). The opposing mandibular molar was reduced with a fine separation bur (889/008; Strauss Diamond Instruments, Natanya, Israel). Postoperatively, mice received analgesics (buprenorphine, 0.05 mg/kg, Richter Pharma) and soft food for 3 days.

Micro‐Computed Tomography (μCT) Analysis

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation (μCT and fluorochromes) or intracardiac perfusion (cell kinetics and RNAscope) with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). After euthanasia, the right maxillary bone was dissected, cleaned of soft tissue, fixed overnight (PFA), dehydrated in 70% ethanol and imaged by ex vivo μCT (MicroXCT400, Zeiss). The source was set at 40 kV and 74 μA. 1200 projections were acquired over 360° using a 4‐x objective with 2‐s exposure. Volumes were reconstructed using XMReconstructor 8.2.2720 (Zeiss) with the Feldkamp filtered‐back projection algorithm, yielding 3D images with a 3.4 μm3 isotropic voxel. A 1200 mg/cm3 hydroxyapatite phantom was used to convert attenuation values to mineral density. 3D imaging and dentine morphometry were performed using Dragonfly software (version 2022.2; Object Research Systems Inc., Montreal, Canada). Pulp exposure consistency was assessed in five samples on the day of DPC. Axial images were used to measure the distance from the buccal surface to the exposure sites and from the exposures to the distal contact points of the first and second molars (Figure 1A,B). Depth was measured from the cusp tips to the exposure. Dentine bridge volume, and mean and total mineral content were quantified at 28‐ and 56‐days post treatment (Figure 1E–G).

Tissue Preparation and Histological Analysis

Euthanized mice were perfused with PBS followed by 4% PFA. The right maxillary bone was dissected and fixed with 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. Samples were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8) for 28 days, dehydrated to 70% ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Blocks were mesiodistally sectioned at 5 μm. Masson's Trichrome staining (HT152; Sigma‐Aldrich) was used to assess collagen fibre formation. H&E staining was used to classify dentine bridge formation as either partial, that is, newly formed dentine does not span the exposed site, or complete, that is, a continuous bridge across the defect.

Cell Kinetics Experiment

EdU pulse‐chase labelling was used to assess cell kinetics in the dental pulp. To compare pulpal cell survival between Biodentine‐ and MTA‐treated teeth, mice received three ip injections of 20 mg/kg 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine (EdU) (A10044; ThermoFisher, Massachusetts, USA) on postnatal Days 5, 6 and 7 to label proliferating cells and were analysed for label retention at 3 days after DPC. To compare proliferation between Biodentine‐ and MTA‐treated teeth, mice received three consecutive EdU injections at 24, 12 and 3 h before harvest. Molars were processed for histology as above and stained using the Click‐iT Plus EdU Alexa Fluor 555 Kit (C10638; Invitrogen, California, USA). Slides were counterstained with DAPI (D9542; Sigma, Missouri, USA) and mounted using Hydromount (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Pennsylvania, USA). EdU+ cells were counted in the exposed site, distal root, and adjacent gingiva.

TUNEL Assay

Apoptosis was assessed using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (TMR red, 12156792910; Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and incubated with proteinase K for 30 min at 37°C. Sections were then incubated with 50 μL of TUNEL reaction mix for 60 min at 37°C in a humidified dark chamber. Slides were counterstained with DAPI as above.

RNAscope In Situ Hybridization

In situ hybridization was performed on paraffin sections using the RNAscope 2.5 Assay with RED HD (Catalogue # 322350; Advanced Cell Diagnostics, USA) and a probe for DSPP (Catalogue # 448301; Basel, Switzerland). The target retrieval was replaced by Pepsin reagent treatment.

Evaluation of Dentine Deposition by Fluorochromes

Dentine deposition was evaluated using calcein and alizarin complexone labelling as described (Pautke et al. 2007). Calcein (C0875; Sigma, Missouri, USA, 7.5 mg/kg) and alizarin complexone (A3882; Sigma, Missouri, USA, 7.5 mg/kg) were administered intraperitoneally at 14 and 21 days post‐DPC, respectively. Fluorochromes were dissolved in 2% NaHCO3 and filtered before injection. Mice were harvested 42 days after the final injection, and samples were prepared as above, without decalcification. Teeth were then dehydrated and immersed in a clearing solution (Hong et al. 2019) before imaging.

Microscopy

Bright‐field and fluorescent images were captured using an upright microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2) equipped with DS‐Ri2 and Flash4.0LT C11440 cameras, respectively. Representative confocal images were obtained using an A1R+ confocal mounted on a Nikon Ti2 fluorescent microscope. Cleared teeth were imaged with a Yokogawa W1 Spinning Disk (Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan), equipped with ORCA‐FusionBT digital camera C15440 (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). Images were processed with NIS‐Elements AR 5.42.01 (46‐bit) and analyzed using Photoshop (2024) or ImageJ (2023). Representative images were selected after quantitative analysis of all samples and correspond to measurements closest to the group mean, ensuring that images shown reflect the quantified dataset.

Statistics

Bar chart data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons between the two groups, normally distributed data were analysed using an unpaired Student's t‐test. Data that were not normally distributed (EdU proliferation for Biodentine and MTA at Days 3 and 8; tunnel assay at Day 1). Significance was defined as p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. Statistical and graphics were performed using Prism (GraphPad9.5.1, San Diego, CA).

Results

Biodentine Promotes Superior Dentine Bridge Formation Compared to MTA Following Direct Pulp Capping

To assess reparative bridge formation after pulp capping of mouse maxillary first molars, we first determined the consistency of the location and depth of the exposure using μCT scans immediately after DCP. The results confirmed high consistency of the exposure location, with a coefficient of variation (CV) of 2% in the sagittal plane and 9% in the horizontal plane. Depth variability was slightly higher (CV = 18%) due to natural cusp attrition prior to the procedure (Figure 1A,B).

Examination at 28 days post‐DPC revealed mineralized tissue consistent with formation of a dentine bridge beneath the exposed site in both MTA‐ and Biodentine‐treated teeth. Bridge volume was 3.0 and 2.8 times greater in Biodentine‐treated teeth than in MTA‐treated teeth at 28 and 56 days, respectively (Figure 1C–E). Likewise, total mineral content was 2.8 and 3.7 times greater in Biodentine‐treated teeth at 28 and 56 days, respectively (Figure 1F). Mean mineral density was similar between groups at both time points (Figure 1G).

During reparative dentinogenesis, morphologically distinct populations of odontoblast‐like cells were observed adjacent to the dentine bridge in both MTA‐ and Biodentine‐treated teeth. Flattened cells were located beneath the globular regions of the bridge, whereas columnar, polarized cells with palisading nuclei were found near areas of tubular dentine (Figure 1H, yellow inserts). These features are similar to odontoblast‐like cells previously described during reparative dentinogenesis beneath the dentine bridge (Azimi et al. 2014), and may reflect different stages in odontoblast differentiation during tertiary dentinogenesis as suggested in earlier studies. In this context, columnar cells are associated with regions of tubular dentine, whereas flatter cells are found adjacent to globular reparative dentine (Tziafa et al. 2014).

Embedded osteocyte‐like cells were observed in newly formed dentine in both groups, indicating cellular inclusions within osteodentin (Figure 1H, yellow insert). The bridges exhibited a globular structure with reduced tubular organization, characteristic of reparative dentine (Figure 1H). Complete bridges composed of both tubular dentine and atubular osteodentin were observed in 4 of 5 Biodentine‐treated teeth, compared to 1 of 5 MTA‐treated samples. Tunnel defects were seen in several incomplete bridges in MTA‐treated teeth (Figure 1H, blue insert).

These results indicate that while both materials support dentine bridge formation, Biodentine consistently induces greater bridge volume, mineral content, and structural integrity, suggesting enhanced regenerative potential compared to MTA.

Higher Apoptosis and Reduced Cell Retention in MTA‐Treated Teeth

To explore the cellular mechanisms underlying differences in dentine bridge formation between the two capping materials, we first examined cell proliferation and apoptosis at the exposed site during the initial stage of recovery using EdU and TUNEL labelling, respectively. Adjacent gingival cells were used as internal controls.

As expected, no EdU‐positive cells were observed 24 h after treatment in either Biodentine‐ or MTA‐treated teeth (Figure 2B, yellow inserts). By Day 3, EdU‐positive cells, indicating pulpal cell proliferation, were detected in both groups and persisted through Day 8 (Figure 2A, yellow inserts). No statistically significant differences in proliferation were found between the groups (Figure 2C).

TUNEL‐positive apoptotic cells were detected in the exposed site at 24 h and 3 days post‐treatment (Figure 2B). Although the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.08, 0.09, respectively; Figure 2D), most MTA‐treated samples exhibited more apoptotic cells than those treated with Biodentine. At 24 h, MTA‐treated teeth showed an average of 12 apoptotic cells, compared to 7 in Biodentine‐treated teeth. By Day 3, these numbers declined to 7 and 4, respectively, indicating that the apoptotic response is transient (Figure 2D). This trend may have been influenced by inter‐sample variability and outliers, likely contributing to the lack of statistical significance. Notably, EdU‐positive proliferative cells and TUNEL‐positive apoptotic cells were distributed in a mutually exclusive pattern. TUNEL‐positive cells were situated directly beneath the capping material, whereas EdU‐positive cells localized deeper in the pulpal horn.

Pulpal cell survival was assessed by evaluating the presence of label‐retaining cells (LRCs) 3 days after DPC (Figure 2E,F). MTA‐treated teeth exhibited fewer LRCs in the treated region compared to Biodentine‐treated teeth (Figure 2E). This reduction was localized, as similar numbers of LRCs were observed in the mesial root, gingiva (Figure 2F and Figure S1), and untreated control teeth in both groups. These results suggest that although cell proliferation was comparable between groups, MTA‐treated teeth exhibited a trend toward greater apoptosis and reduced retention of pulpal cells, which may contribute to differences in healing.

Increased DSPP Expression and Collagen Matrix Formation in Biodentine‐Treated Teeth

We subsequently characterized the exposed site histologically and molecularly. Consistent with TUNEL staining, 3 days after DPC the mechanical exposure produced a localized zone of tissue destruction with both capping materials. This zone was characterized by cellular disorganization, scattered dentine particles, and areas of pulp damage as revealed by H&E and Masson's trichrome staining. The odontoblast layer, directly beneath the capping material and along the pulp chamber floor, appeared disrupted and discontinuous, with no obvious differences observed between the two capping materials (Figure 3A,C).

In H&E staining a light pink region of predentin was visible, on Day 14 after DPC for both capping materials, on the lateral wall of the exposed site adjacent to the odontoblast layer. However, this predentin layer tended to be thicker in Biodentine‐treated than in MTA‐treated teeth (Figure 3A). Collagen formation was observed in the exposure site, extending toward the adjacent pulp horns at 28 days post‐treatment in both materials. A larger portion of the former coronal pulp space was filled with collagen in Biodentine‐treated teeth than in MTA‐treated teeth, with more pronounced collagen formation in the bridge area (Figure 3B). This is consistent with μCT results at Day 28, which showed triple the bridge volume for Biodentine compared to MTA (Figure 1C,E).

Because successful reparative dentinogenesis at the injury site involves matrix formation by newly formed or recruited odontoblasts, we next examined the presence of odontoblasts as well as early histological changes. To determine odontoblast response and collagen deposition in the two capping materials, we performed RNAscope in situ hybridization for dentine sialophosphoprotein (DSPP). Results showed that at Day 8, Biodentine‐treated teeth had more DSPP + cells in the exposed site when compared to MTA‐treated teeth (Figure 3C,D).

These results indicate that enhanced dentine formation in Biodentine‐treated teeth is due to the presence of more functional secreted odontoblasts at Day 8 when compared to MTA‐treated teeth.

Biodentine Promotes Earlier and Faster Dentine Formation Compared to MTA

The greater number of odontoblast‐like cells in Biodentine‐treated teeth might contribute to a faster rate of dentine production. To test this possibility, we monitored dentine production during healing by labelling newly formed dentine with fluorochromes at 14 and 21 days post‐treatment.

In untreated molars, two distinct fluorescent bands were visible beneath the central cusp, whereas both treatment groups exhibited poorly defined and less intense fluorescent bands (Figure 4A). Notably, the labelled bands were wider in the Biodentine group than in the MTA group (Figure 4B). This finding was corroborated by μCT images from the same samples at 6 weeks, which showed larger size of the dentine bridges in Biodentine‐treated teeth (Figure 4C). Together, these findings suggest that Biodentine induces earlier and greater dentine formation compared to MTA.

Discussion

Although the choice of capping material has a significant impact on the outcome of DPC, in vivo evidence regarding the regenerative potential of Biodentine and MTA remains limited. In this study, we compared the ability of these two materials to promote pulp healing and dentine bridge formation following DPC in a controlled murine model with healthy pulps. This model enables investigation of the reparative properties of capping materials without the confounding effects of tissue inflammation or pulp degeneration. In all analyses, including μCT, histology, molecular staining, and fluorochrome labelling, Biodentine outperformed MTA in bridge volume, mineral content, odontoblast activity, collagen deposition, and dentine formation rate. These advantages likely reflect less apoptosis and more pulpal cell retention in Biodentine‐treated teeth. Collectively, the findings suggest that Biodentine has a superior regenerative potential and elicits more favourable cellular responses when compared to MTA in this model.

μCT analysis demonstrated that Biodentine resulted in greater bridge volume and total mineral content when compared with MTA, while no significant difference was observed in mineral density. This indicates that both materials induced production of similar quality dentine, with Biodentine generating a greater volume. The consistent difference in bridge volume at two different time points suggests that the beneficial effect of Biodentine is sustained over time, rather than a transient effect at initiation of repair. Thus, Biodentine appears to accelerate and maintain the rate of dentinogenesis.

To explore the cellular basis for these differences, we examined the sequence of events after DPC. MTA‐treated samples tended to show more apoptotic cells when compared to Biodentine‐treated samples, while Biodentine‐treated teeth retained more pulpal LRCs at Day 3 than MTA‐treated teeth. These observations align with reports that Biodentine supports higher cell viability than MTA (Birant et al. 2021; Tomás‐Catalá et al. 2018). In contrast, proliferation rates were similar between groups, consistent with an earlier rat study using PCNA immunolabelling (Tran et al. 2012). Mesenchymal stem cells differentiate into odontoblast‐like cells under appropriate conditions (Li et al. 2021). In this study, Biodentine‐treated teeth exhibited more DSPP‐positive cells at Day 8, consistent with a prior in vitro report of DSPP upregulation at Day 7 (Sismanoglu and Ercal 2023). This enhanced early differentiation contributed to greater dentine volume at later timepoints (28 and 56 days; Figure 1).

Fluorochrome labelling demonstrated the temporal progression of dentinogenesis. Untreated controls showed two distinct fluorescent bands beneath the cusp, reflecting orderly sequential deposition of physiologic dentine similar to findings in developing embryos (Gonzalez Lopez et al. 2023), and untreated adult mice (Pautke et al. 2007). In contrast, MTA‐ and Biodentine‐treated teeth displayed irregular, fused, and less distinct bands, characteristic of tertiary dentine, which mineralizes in a globular rather than linear pattern (Vidovic‐Zdrilic et al. 2019). Similar irregular labelling has been reported in reparative dentine bridges in mice (Vidovic et al. 2017). Histological analysis confirmed globular calcification, with fusion of discrete calcospheric foci, producing rapid but disorganized mineralization within the collagen matrix.

Both materials produced reparative dentine bridges with atubular, tubular, and globular regions, which indicates a mixed healing response. Biodentine samples, however, contained more cellular inclusions, likely due to faster dentinogenesis, entrapping odontoblast‐like cells within the matrix. Similarly, a rat study reported engulfed inclusions within the matrix (Kim et al. 2016). Biodentine also generated more complete dentine bridges with fewer tunnel defects compared to MTA, suggesting enhanced dentinogenic potential. Mechanistically, in cell culture, Biodentine has been shown to induce greater TGF‐β1 release, upregulate anti‐inflammatory and downregulate pro‐inflammatory cytokines in dental pulpal stem cells and macrophages, promote DPSC migration, and polarize macrophages toward the anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype (Abuarqoub et al. 2022; Laurent et al. 2012). Although our study did not test these pathways, future mechanistic experiments should determine whether similar effects occur in vivo.

Conclusion

Biodentine elicited a more favourable pulpal healing response than MTA, characterized by greater cell survival, enhanced odontoblast differentiation, and earlier, more robust mineralized matrix deposition. These findings highlight the importance of early cellular events in shaping dentine bridge formation. The controlled murine model enabled precise evaluation of these mechanisms but represents a limitation, as it reflects healing in healthy teeth under standardized conditions and may not fully replicate human clinical presentations or inflammatory states.
