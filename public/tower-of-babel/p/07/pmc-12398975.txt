First primary care visit of the newborn and its implications for breastfeeding
Authors: Paloma Comino-Vázquez, Marta Carballal-Mariño, Josep Vicent Balaguer-Martínez, César García-Vera, Juana María Ledesma-Albarrán
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe first primary care visit of the newborn aims to assess neonatal feeding, promote breastfeeding, and identify potential health issues. This study examines the relationship between the timing of the first visit and the prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) at one month of life.MethodsThis prospective, observational, analytical study was conducted in Spain through a paediatric research network. Data were collected from newborns at both the first visit and the one-month check-up. Selected variables were compared with those from a 2014 study by the same network to estimate EBF rates at the first visit. The primary outcome was EBF prevalence at one month, measured using 2024 data. EBF was defined as receiving only breast milk in the past 24 h. To analyse factors associated with EBF over time, generalised estimating equation models with binomial distribution and logit link function were used in univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsA total of 1952 cases were collected in 2024, with the first visit at 9.3 ± 6.1 days after discharge, compared to 2047 cases in 2014 at 10.7 ± 6.3 days. EBF prevalence at the first visit was 63.1% in 2014 and 62.6% in 2024. In 2024, EBF prevalence at one month was 55.0% (95% CI 52.7, 57.3). Previous maternal breastfeeding experience was the strongest predictor of EBF at one month (AOR 14.61, 95% CI 5.04, 42.33). Having the first visit within 7 days of life was associated with higher EBF maintenance at one month in univariate analysis (OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.17, 1.53), but not in multivariate analysis (AOR 0.90, 95% CI 0.47, 1.74).ConclusionsThe first primary care visit of the newborn occurs late in Spain. Rates of EBF have declined over the past decade, highlighting the need for targeted interventions that address modifiable determinants. The strongest predictor for EBF at one month is having previous breastfeeding experience. Early postnatal follow-up of newborns in primary care appears to have a beneficial effect on EBF maintenance at one month, although other determinants may mediate or influence its impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13006-025-00758-w.Keywords: Newborn, Breast feeding, Postnatal care, Primary health care, Ambulatory care facilities

Background

The first primary care visit of the newborn aims to assess newborn nutrition, promote breastfeeding, prevent and detect health problems, supervise adaptation to family life, and ensure access to health resources [1–4]. Recommendations regarding the timing of the first primary care visit vary across countries according to their differing strategies and public health plans [5–10].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that the first primary care visit be performed between 24 and 72 h of life [6], whereas the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative proposes the visit between 2–4 days after hospital discharge [9]. Furthermore, the American Academy of Pediatrics offers individualized recommendations depending on the duration of hospital stay: for stays of less than 48 h, the first visit should be performed within 2 days after hospital discharge, and for stays of more than 48 h, the visit should be performed 3–5 days after hospital discharge [7].

In Spain, the Ministerio de Salud (Spanish Ministry of Health) recommends the first primary care visit between 48 and 72 h after hospital discharge [5], and the Comité de Lactancia of the Asociación Española de Pediatría (Spanish Paediatrics Association Lactation Committee) recommends that the visit occur within 24–48 h after hospital discharge [11]. However, at the regional level, some autonomous communities recommend simply that the first visit be performed before 10 days of life [12]. Considering this variability, further research is needed to determine the optimal timing of the first primary care visit [13].

The length of hospital stays after delivery has progressively decreased in recent decades [14, 15], further highlighting the importance of the first primary care visit. In Spain, the normal hospital stay for healthy newborns is 24–48 h for vaginal deliveries and at least 72–96 h for caesarean deliveries [5].

Given the ample evidence regarding the benefits of breastfeeding—including improving the mother and newborn’s health and offering substantial economic savings for health systems [16, 17] — support for breastfeeding should be considered a universal public health intervention [18]. However, the promotion of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) is often limited to the hospital setting with few interventions in primary care [9]. Moreover, although primary care is an optimal context for effective intervention, few studies have assessed the programs aimed at prolonging the duration of breastfeeding [11, 19–22].

According to the 2017 report from the Instituto Nacional de Estadística in Spain (Spanish National Institute of Statistics) [23], the EBF prevalence at 6 months was 39%, similar to the 35% 6-month EBF prevalence reported in the LAyDI study (EBF was defined as receiving only breast milk in the last 24 h) [24]. However, these numbers still fall far from achieving the WHO’s 2030 a global EBF prevalence of at least 70% at 6 months [25].

Furthermore, Spain has a public and universal healthcare system, nonetheless primary care and hospital practices have developed independently, often with little communication between them. As a result, first primary care visit may be delayed or not performed at an optimal time for detecting health problems or breastfeeding difficulties [5]. In addition, health competencies are the responsibility of the autonomous communities and therefore organizational differences in newborn care may be present despite the national, scientific evidence-based, regulations [5, 11, 26, 27].

The main objective of this study was to determine whether the time between hospital discharge and the first primary care visit is related to the prevalence of breastfeeding at one month of life. Further objectives included data comparison between 2024 and 2014 to determine if the first visit is performed within the recommended timeframes, if the time to the first visit has decreased over the last ten years, and if there are differences in the first visit between neonates born in public versus private hospitals.

Methods

Data source and design

This research was based on an observational, prospective, analytical design. Data collection was conducted in Spain between 1 November 2023 and 31 January 2024, through a network specifically designed for paediatric research. Study participants were newborns under one month of age who attended initial appointments with collaborating paediatricians distributed nationally.

To ensure that the sample was nationally representative, the sample size for the current data collection process was based on 2022 data (329 251 newborns born in Spain), the most recent data available during the study design process. Thus, it was determined that a sample size of 980 individuals would be considered representative, based on a confidence of 95%, an estimated prevalence of breastfeeding at 15 days of 85% [23, 28], a precision of 0.025 and a potential loss of 20%.

The inclusion criteria were completion of the initial primary care appointment, a one-month visit with a collaborating paediatrician during the study period, and age of less than one month at the time of the first appointment. The exclusion criteria were premature birth before 36 weeks, birth weight under 2,200 g [29], maternal and/or neonatal stay for more than 5 days before hospital discharge, syndromes, metabolic diseases or congenital malformations that may affect food-related decisions during the study period, and home or out-of-hospital delivery.

Data collection was carried using a dynamic database created ad hoc in REDCap (version 10.6.15, 2023, Vanderbilt University) by collaborating paediatricians in the Red de Investigación en Pediatría de Atención Primaria (PAPenRED) (Primary Care Paediatrics Research Network). This network, founded in 2013, operates in all Spanish autonomous communities through the Asociación Española de Pediatría de Atención Primaria (AEPap) (Primary Care Paediatrics Association of Spain). Proportional numbers of paediatricians in each autonomous community ensures that data obtained through the network are nationally representative. Data for this study was collected during the first primary care visit at the primary care facility after signed parental consent. Current data was compared with other data obtained 10 years ago—collected between 1 October 2014 and 31 December 2014—by the same network of paediatricians (PAPenRED). Identical inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied at both data collection times.

Variables

The main variable assessed in the 2024 study was the prevalence of EBF at one month of age. EBF was defined as receiving only breast milk in the last 24 h. The main independent variable was the time elapsed, in days, from birth to the first primary care visit performed by paediatricians or paediatric nurses. The same variables were used in both the 2014 and 2024 study periods with the exception of the following, which were only registered in 2024: type of feeding on the first day of life, newborn age at hospital discharge, type of feeding at hospital discharge, mother with previous experience in breastfeeding and type of feeding at the one-month visit and 24 h before.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 27.0. A descriptive study of the collected variables was conducted. Qualitative variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies, expressed as percentages, and quantitative variables by measures of central tendency and dispersion—mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data, or median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed data. The chi-square test was used to compare proportions, and Student's t-test was used to compare means between groups.

To assess factors associated with EBF over time, generalised estimating equation (GEE) models with binomial distribution and logit link function were applied in both univariate and multivariate analyses. This approach was chosen given the longitudinal nature of the data, with repeated measurements of EBF at different follow-up times: birth, hospital discharge, first visit in primary care, and at one month of life. GEE allow the estimation of population average effects by controlling for dependence between within-subject observations. An independent correlation structure was assumed, since the observations were equally spaced in time and no complex dependence structure was identified. Variables included in the multivariate model were those that showed statistical significance in the univariate models, and whose independence was confirmed by auxiliary linear regression to assess collinearity using variance inflation factors and tolerance. Statistical significance was considered when p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The PAPenRED project was approved by the regional ethics committee of Aragón (favourable opinion, 19/2013; CP-CI PI13/00154). For the second phase of the study (2024), approval was granted by the respective ethics committees of the Hospital Virgen Macarena and the Hospital Vigen del Rocío (CEI_06/2023; CP RN-2023 and CI 0921-N-23). In addition, permission was granted by the ethics committees of each autonomous community.

Discussion

Newborns whose mothers had previous breastfeeding experience showed statistically significant differences in favour of breastfeeding maintenance at one month of life, being the strongest predictor for EBF (AOR 14.61, 95% CI 5.04, 42.33). This reflects the central role of maternal experience and self-confidence. Prior research by Hackman et al. supports this finding, reporting that multiparous mothers had a longer duration of EBF and significantly greater rates of EBF compared to primiparous mothers [30].

The temporal factor plays a crucial role in this study. The probability of sustaining EBF decreases over time, with a notable decline observed by one month of age (AOR 0.28, p = 0.001).

A negative correlation was observed between consultation with the healthcare system—either in hospital emergency departments or primary care centres—and EBF rates at one month. This may be indicative of neonatal complications or other circumstances that compromise EBF.

Other variables, such as type of delivery, type of hospital management, previous evaluation by a primary care midwife, having received indications about when to attend primary care consultation at hospital discharge and performing first primary care visit within the indicated time frame were not statistically significant after multivariate adjustment. However, due to their clinical relevance, the variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis are subsequently discussed.

Women who underwent a caesarean section were significantly less likely to maintain EBF at one month compared to those who had a vaginal delivery, after adjusting for timing. This factor has gained considerable attention in the literature as a key determinant of EBF maintenance at one month [31, 32].

The type of hospital management where the birth occurs is also significantly associated with the likelihood of maintaining EBF. Women who gave birth in private or joint hospitals show a 17.4% reduction in the odds ratio of maintaining EBF compared to those who gave birth in public hospitals. This association is statistically significant, although of moderate magnitude.

Prior midwife visits positively influence the likelihood of maintaining EBF. Women who had a midwifery consultation before giving birth were 20.3% more likely to maintain EBF compared to those who did not, after adjusting for timing. This finding underscores the importance of professional antenatal care and education, particularly when combined with other interventions.

Hospital referral for an early first primary care visit has a significant protective effect on EBF. Mothers who received hospital indications for a check-up in the first week of life are 29.5% more likely to maintain EBF than those who did not receive such indication, adjusting for timing.

Early postnatal follow-up of the newborn in primary care has a beneficial impact on the maintenance of EBF. Performing the first primary care visit within the first 7 days of life is associated with a 33.6% increase in the odds of maintaining EBF compared to not having a medical visit in that period, adjusted for time. This variable shows one of the strongest protective effects in the study. A significant association was also found between performing the first primary care visit within 72 h of hospital discharge and a greater likelihood of maintaining any breastfeeding (i.e., EBF or mixed breastfeeding). The odds of maintaining any breastfeeding were 32.2% higher in infants who received the visit within 72 h of discharge compared to those who did not, and this association was statistically significant. However, these associations did not remain statistically significant in the multivariate models adjusted for clinical and contextual factors. These findings suggest that while early postnatal follow-up may contribute to breastfeeding support, its impact could be mediated or influenced by other determinants. Therefore, strategies aimed at promoting breastfeeding should adopt a multifactorial approach. The WHO recommends breastfeeding promotion across the spectrum of maternal and child health care [9], especially in the prenatal and puerperal periods. Primary care centres are an ideal place for this initiative.

The 2024 data shows that the first primary care visit is performed at an average of 9.3 ± 6 days following hospital discharge, which is considerably later than the timelines recommended by national guidelines and most international organizations [3, 5]. By comparison, the 2014 study reported a mean time of 10.7 ± 6.3 days to the first primary care visit. Although the time to first visit has decreased by an average of 1.4 days, there is still substantial room for improvement to reach the recommended standards.

This decrease may be attributed to the fact that more hospitals now recommend a first primary care visit within the first week of life (74% in 2024 compared to 62.7% in 2014). A significant relationship was identified between hospitals recommending the first visit within the first week of life and higher rates of breastfeeding (both EBF and mixed feeding) at one month of life.

Despite the reduction in the recommended time elapsed before for the first primary care visit, our data shows a decline of 0.5 percentage points in the overall rates of EBF at the first visit. This decline may be attributable to a variety of factors, as there are many determinants that influence breastfeeding at one month of age, including biological, socioeconomic, educational, and health-related factors [24, 32–34].

This decrease in breastfeeding rates is of interest and requires further investigation, especially considering the complex interplay among all other factors. Maternal experience, age, education level, attendance of prenatal classes, contact with support groups, emotional support and self-efficacy have been identified as key factors in the initiation and duration of breastfeeding [28, 31, 35–38].

The prevalence of EBF on the first day of life was 72.6%, which decreased to 64% at the time of hospital discharge. These values reflect a relatively low initial prevalence compared with other European countries, such as Germany where the EBF prevalence at birth is 97% [39]. The results of our study show a 55% EBF prevalence in the first month of life, which is much lower than the 73.9% EBF prevalence at 6 weeks of life reported by the most recent (2017) survey published by the Instituto Nacional de Estadística (National Institute of Statistics of Spain) [23]. Our results are more similar to those published in the LAyDI study [24], showing an EBF prevalence of 62.3% at the first month and 35.2% at six months of life. Considering that the WHO has established a goal of a global EBF prevalence of 50% at six months of life in 2025 and 70% in 2030 [25, 40], the 55% EBF prevalence at one month indicates that Spain is far from reaching that goal.

Regarding breastfeeding promotion, early neonatal visits represent direct government savings. Specifically, increasing breastfeeding rates at hospital discharge and at 6 months postpartum (from 85%/15% to 95% /50%, respectively) is estimated to save the Spanish health-care system at least 197 million euros per year [41]. Breastfeeding also generates indirect savings through intellectual, social and emotional benefits [42, 43]. Given these positive benefits, breastfeeding research should be a public health priority. Furthermore, breastfeeding promotion and support are needed to address the advantages for both the mother and child, and for society in general.

This study has many strengths, such as the use of a considerably large sample which thereby increases the statistical power and the ability to detect statistically significant associations. In addition, data collection through PAPenRED—a network specifically designed for paediatric research which has a wide variety of patients throughout the country with different geographical, socioeconomic, educational, and cultural contexts—enabled us to obtain a nationally representative sample.

Nonetheless, this study has certain limitations. Selection bias may have occurred due to the pressure associated with limited time to provide care during appointments, possibly influencing sample recruitment. To minimize this bias, all newborns delivered during the study period were consecutively registered. Another possible selection bias is the paediatricians’ voluntary participation in PAPenRED. This bias was addressed by including a large number of collaborating researchers, which strengthens data validity. Although most variables were collected identically in both periods, some could not be compared because they were not recorded in the first period. This limits data comparability but does not affect the variables analysed consistently. In addition, this study exhibits the typical limitations of an observational study, which does not allow the direct establishment of cause‒effect relationships.

In summary, first primary care visit is particularly important for primiparous mothers, as it helps mitigate the challenges they face compared to multiparous mothers. As previously mentioned, prior maternal breastfeeding experience is the strongest protective factor associated with continued breastfeeding at one month of age. Early postnatal follow-up of newborns in primary care appears to have a beneficial effect on EBF maintenance at one month, although other determinants could mediate or influence its impact. Additionally, receiving a hospital referral for first primary care visit and a having a prior visit with a midwife may also contribute positively to EBF at one month. Therefore, strategies to promote breastfeeding should adopt a multifactorial approach that includes professional support, given that the first days of life constitute a window of opportunity for the correct establishment and maintenance of breastfeeding.

The first primary care visit occurs late in Spain, at 9.3 ± 6 days after hospital discharge. Although this value has improved by 1.4 days comparing 2024 and 2014 data, substantial efforts are still needed to reach national and international recommendations. Furthermore, EBF rates at the time of the first primary-care visit have declined over the last 10 years, from 63.1% in 2014 to 62.6% in 2024.

Future research should examine what modifiable determinants can promote early first primary care visit, given the evidence of its positive influence on breastfeeding overall and enhanced EBF rates. Research about the specific primary care interventions that influence EBF during the first year of infancy, such as receiving a hospital referral for first primary care visit, performing first visit within the indicated time frame, prior visits with a midwife, and special support for first-time mothers, could strengthen the role of primary care as a pillar in the promotion and support of breastfeeding, consequentially improving health outcomes.
