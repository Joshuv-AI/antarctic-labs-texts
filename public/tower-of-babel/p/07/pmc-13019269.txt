Flower-Like CNTs@MoS2 Core–Shell Nanostructure for NH3 Gas Sensor at Room Temperature
Authors: Nobpon Seniwong-Na-Ayuttaya, Sarawut Kondee, Tanatsaparn Tithito, Chatchawal Wongchoosuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ammonia (NH3) is a hazardous gas requiring rapid detection at room temperature for practical environmental and agricultural monitoring. Herein, we report a flower-like CNTs@MoS2 core–shell heterostructure synthesized via a facile hydrothermal process for room-temperature NH3 sensing. Structural analyses confirm uniform MoS2 nanosheets anchored on the CNT networks, forming well-defined p–n heterojunction interfaces that facilitate efficient charge transport. This architecture effectively combines the high surface activity and gas adsorption capability of MoS2 with the superior electrical conductivity of CNTs, promoting rapid charge transfer and enhanced sensing performance. The optimized sensor achieves a high response of 94% toward 500 ppm of NH3 with rapid response and recovery times of 36 and 68 s, respectively. Additionally, humidity-assisted interfacial charge transfer further improves the sensor response under high relative humidity. Long-term stability tests demonstrate ∼80% response retention after 150 days. This study elucidates the dominant role of MoS2 and the synergistic charge modulation enabled by CNTs@MoS2 heterostructures, providing a clear design strategy for high-performance NH3 gas sensor at room temperature.

Introduction

Gas sensors are vital tools for monitoring and detecting various gases in the environment. By sensing changes in electrical properties when exposed to specific gases, gas sensors help in identifying hazardous and pollutant gases, which is critical for safety and environmental monitoring. − These sensors have become an integral part of daily life, contributing to safety in homes, workplaces, and public spaces. For instance, in residential areas, gas sensors detect leaks of gases like methane or LPG, providing early warnings to prevent accidents. In workplaces, particularly industrial and agricultural settings, gas sensors are essential for managing air quality, detecting hazardous substances, and ensuring regulatory compliance.

In industrial facilities and livestock farms, gas sensors are used for leak detection and quality control. They play a significant role in maintaining safe working conditions and preventing environmental contamination by monitoring gases such as formaldehyde, methanol, toluene, ethanol, acetone, ammonia (NH3), and propanol. , The presence of such reactive gases in industrial and agricultural environments necessitates reliable detection to safeguard human and animal health. For example, formaldehyde is a commonly encountered industrial VOC with known adverse health effects upon prolonged exposure. Among these gases, NH3 is particularly critical due to its continuous emission in confined livestock facilities and its strong impact on respiratory health. NH3, widely found in livestock farms from animal waste and organic decomposition, can irritate the respiratory tract at concentrations above 25 ppm and poses serious health risks for both humans and animals at higher levels of 300 ppm, where it becomes immediately dangerous to life and health. − In livestock environments, prolonged exposure to NH3 can lead to respiratory diseases in animals, reduce productivity, and impact the overall farm environment, underscoring the importance of reliable and sensitive gas detection.

In recent years, extensive efforts have been devoted to the development of chemiresistive gas sensors capable of operating with high sensitivity and low power consumption at room temperature. , Among various sensing technologies, chemiresistive gas sensors based on low-dimensional materials, particularly transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) and their hybrid nanostructures, have attracted considerable attention due to their simple device architecture, high surface-to-volume ratio, and tunable electronic properties, making them well suited for practical applications such as environmental monitoring, industrial safety, and indoor air-quality control. , Hybrid sensing materials that integrate semiconducting TMDs with conductive nanomaterials have been widely explored to enhance gas-sensing performance by improving charge transport, lowering operating temperature, and enabling room-temperature operation. Accordingly, such low-dimensional and hybrid chemiresistive gas sensors are increasingly regarded as promising candidates for distributed sensing platforms, including smart agriculture and IoT-based environmental monitoring systems, where low power consumption and room-temperature operation are essential.

Despite substantial advances in chemiresistive gas sensors, achieving reliable and stable NH3 detection at room temperature remains challenging. Many NH3 sensors based on metal oxides or TMDs still require elevated operating temperatures to activate surface reactions and attain sufficient sensitivity and fast response, leading to increased power consumption and limited applicability in portable or distributed sensing platforms. At room temperature, NH3 sensing is often restricted by sluggish adsorption–desorption kinetics and inefficient charge transfer, resulting in prolonged recovery and unstable baseline behavior, particularly under humid or fluctuating environmental conditions.

Molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) has been investigated as a promising material for gas sensors due to its advantageous properties, including high surface-to-volume ratio, tunable electronic structure, and strong responsiveness to gases such as NH3 and formaldehyde. , In particular, the layered structure of MoS2 provides abundant exposed surface sites that facilitate effective interaction with electron-donating gases like NH3, leading to noticeable modulation of electrical resistance in resistive-type sensing systems. Although MoS2 demonstrates effective gas adsorption and detection capability, it still suffers from certain drawbacks such as long recovery time, relatively high intrinsic resistance, and in some cases the requirement for elevated operating temperatures. , These limitations are generally associated with slow adsorption–desorption kinetics and restricted charge transport within the pristine material. To overcome these issues, MoS2 is often hybridized with other materials, such as metal oxides (e.g., ZnO) or carbon-based materials (e.g., graphene), which enhance its gas sensing characteristics by improving charge transport pathways and accelerating response–recovery dynamics. Such surface and interface engineering strategies have been widely discussed in recent reviews on semiconductor-based gas sensors.

In this work, we aim to improve the performance of MoS2 by combining it with carbon nanotubes (CNTs). CNTs were selected due to their remarkable physicochemical properties, including a large specific surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and robust chemical stability. For examples, Liu et al. demonstrated that CNT-dispersed TiO2 nanoparticles exhibited a 3-fold enhancement in photocatalytic activity compared to conventionally synthesized TiO2, which was attributed to improved dispersion and increased specific surface area. Shoa et al. reported that incorporating CNTs into MOF-derived CuO nanosheets significantly enhanced glucose sensing performance, achieving approximately three times higher sensitivity and an ultrafast response time of 0.45 s due to accelerated electron transfer and improved conductivity. In the field of gas sensing, Bittencourt et al. showed that dispersing oxygen plasma-functionalized MWCNTs into WO3 matrices markedly improved gas detection at ambient temperature, owing to increased surface defects and enhanced charge transport pathways. The incorporation of CNTs into MoS2 is expected to reduce the overall resistance of the sensing film and accelerate charge carrier transport leading to shorter response and recovery times. Consequently, the developed CNTs@MoS2 hybrid material holds great potential for reliable detection of hazardous gases, particularly in industrial and agricultural environments where rapid and accurate monitoring is essential for safety and environmental protection at room temperature.

Experimental Details

Synthesis of CNTs@MoS2 Nanocomposite

CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite was synthesized using a hydrothermal method as shown in (Figure a). Initially, CNTs were growth using a thermal CVD process with acetylene as a carbon source based on our previous conditions. Afterward, 0.01 g of obtained CNTs, 0.5 mL of hydrochloric acid, and 10 mL of deionized water were mixed and subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min. Sequentially, 0.48 g of sodium molybdate dihydrate and an additional 40 mL of deionized water were added to the solution under moderate magnetic stirring at room temperature for 30 min. Next, 0.96 g of l-cysteine was introduced into the mixture with further moderate stirring at room temperature for another 30 min. The prepared solution was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 12 h. Finally, the resultant CNTs@MoS2 powder was obtained by freeze-drying. It should be noted that the MoS2-to-CNTs ratio has a significant influence on the initial resistance of the gas sensor which directly affects the sensing performance. Specifically, an excessive CNTs content leads to overly low resistance and weakened modulation upon gas exposure, whereas an excessive MoS2 content results in excessively high baseline resistance, thereby suppressing the sensor response. Based on preliminary trials, this compositional ratio was selected to achieve an optimal balance between electrical conductivity and gas sensitivity. In addition, this behavior is consistent with our previously published work on SnO2–CNTs gas sensors. In addition, CVD method provides superior control over CNTs morphology, crystallinity, and defect density while MoS2 was subsequently deposited via a hydrothermal process to form CNTs@MoS2 heterostructures. This combined approach synergistically integrates the defect control of CVD-grown CNTs with the scalability and material tunability of hydrothermal synthesis, thereby providing a balanced strategy for gas sensor fabrication.

Sensor Fabrication and Measurement

The fabrication procedure of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor is schematically illustrated in (Figure b). Interdigitated silver electrodes with a finger width of 1 cm, finger length of 2 cm, and an interelectrode gap of 0.1 mm were first deposited onto a flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrate using a screen-printing technique, followed by drying under ambient conditions to ensure electrode adhesion and electrical continuity. The synthesized CNTs@MoS2 powder (10 mg) was dispersed in 1 mL of ethanol and ultrasonicated for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous dispersion. Subsequently, 15 μL of the as-prepared CNTs@MoS2 dispersion was drop-cast onto the active area covering the interdigitated electrodes using a calibrated micropipette. The device was then maintained at room temperature for 24 h to allow solvent evaporation and the formation of a stable and uniform sensing film. The resulting CNTs@MoS2-coated device was used directly for subsequent gas-sensing measurements without further thermal treatment.

The gas-sensing characteristics of the fabricated CNTs@MoS2 sensor were evaluated using a laboratory-built dynamic gas-flow measurement system. All measurements were carried out at room temperature (26 ± 2 °C). The sensor device was mounted inside a custom-designed Teflon chamber with an internal volume of approximately 36 mL. The target gases were introduced into the chamber by precisely controlling the gas flow rates using mass flow controllers, with dry air serving as the carrier gas. Prior to each sensing cycle, dry air purified through silica gel was continuously supplied to the chamber to stabilize the baseline resistance of the sensor. Subsequently, the test gases were introduced at predetermined concentrations by adjusting the flow-rate ratio between the target gas and the carrier gas, while maintaining a constant total flow rate of 1000 sccm to ensure stable and reproducible sensing conditions. Various volatile organic compounds (VOCs), including NH3, methanol, ethanol, formaldehyde, propanol, toluene, and acetone, were employed to evaluate the selectivity of the CNTs@MoS2 sensor at room temperature. The electrical resistance of the sensor was measured using a voltage divider configuration under a constant applied voltage of 10 V, and the resistance signals were recorded at 1 s intervals using a data acquisition system (NI USB-6008) controlled by a LabVIEW-based program. The sensor response (S) was calculated according to eq : 1 S(%)=|Ra−RgRa|×100

where Ra and Rg represent the sensor resistance in dry air and upon exposure to the target gas, respectively. The response and recovery times were defined as the durations required for the sensor resistance to attain 90% of its maximum variation during target gas exposure and to recover to 90% of its original baseline following gas removal, respectively.

For humidity-dependent gas-sensing measurements, the relative humidity (RH) inside the sensing chamber was controlled using a bubbling-based humidification system combined with mass flow controllers. Dry air was initially passed through silica gel and introduced into the chamber to stabilize the baseline resistance. Humidified air was then generated by passing a portion of the carrier gas through a water-filled bubbler and subsequently mixed with dry air by adjusting the flow-rate ratios, while maintaining a constant total flow rate of 200 sccm. The RH level was monitored in real time using a commercial SHT15 humidity sensor embedded in the sensor chamber.

Results and Discussion

XPS Analysis of CNTs@MoS2 Nanocomposite

The surface chemical composition and oxidation states of the synthesized CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite were determined via XPS analysis. The survey spectrum and high-resolution spectra for Mo 3d, S 2p, C 1s, O 1s, and S 2s regions are shown in Figure . The XPS survey spectrum (Figure a) confirms the presence of the main elements in the CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite including Mo, S, C, and O with no other noticeable sharp peaks. It indicates the high purity of the synthesized CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite and suggests a low density of structural defects which is beneficial for enhancing its electronic and sensing performance. In the high-resolution Mo 3d spectrum (Figure b), the peaks at 228.9 eV (Mo 3d5/2) and 232.2 eV (Mo 3d3/2) are attributed to the 2H phase of MoS2 while the peaks at 230.1 eV (Mo 3d5/2) and 233.3 eV (Mo 3d3/2) correspond to the 1T phase indicating the coexistence of a mixed-phase structure. Additionally, the peak at 235.0 eV suggests the presence of Mo6+ species due to surface oxidation. − The S 2s peak observed at 226.6 eV further confirms the presence of sulfur in MoS2, , verifying the successful formation of MoS2 within the composite. In S 2p region (Figure c), the peaks at 161.8 and 163.7 eV are attributed to S–C bonding while those at 163.0 and 164.9 eV correspond to C–S–C linkages. Oxidized sulfur species, such as S–O, are identified at 168.8 eV (S 2p3/2) and 170.0 eV (S 2p1/2), further confirming functionalization on the composite surface. , In the O 1s spectrum (Figure d), peaks at 530.5, 531.7, 532.7, and 533.8 eV correspond to C–OH, Mo–O, C–O, and CO, respectively. These groups indicate oxygen adsorption and functionalization. The C 1s spectrum (Figure e) reveals peaks at 285.0 eV (CO), 286.6 eV (C–O), and 288.5 eV (CC or C–C), confirming the integration of carbon nanotubes and their oxygenated functional groups into the composite matrix. Such chemical states not only validate the successful integration of MoS2 with CNTs but also imply enhanced interfacial interactions which are expected to improve charge transfer and sensing performance.

SEM Analysis

The surface morphology and elemental distribution of the synthesized CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite were analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). As shown in Figure a–b, the FESEM images exhibit a well-integrated structure of MoS2 are uniformly anchored onto the tubular surfaces of CNTs, confirming the effective formation of the hybrid structure during the hydrothermal synthesis process. In addition, EDS elemental mapping images presented in Figure c–f illustrate the spatial distributions of Mo, S, O, and C, respectively. All four elements are homogeneously dispersed across the observed large area which indicates the successful incorporation of MoS2 into the CNTs matrix. Furthermore, the quantitative elemental composition obtained from EDS analysis is shown in Figure g, which reports atomic percentages of Mo: 4.84%, S: 9.32%, O: 13.38%, and C: 72.45%. It should be noted that the atomic ratio of Mo to S obtained from EDS analysis is slightly lower than the ideal stoichiometric ratio of MoS2. This deviation can be attributed to the surface-sensitive nature and semiquantitative limitation of EDS analysis, as well as the possible presence of sulfur-deficient regions in the composite. Such sulfur deficiency has been widely reported in MoS2-based materials synthesized via hydrothermal routes and is commonly associated with sulfur vacancies, which can act as additional active sites for gas adsorption and facilitate charge transfer during gas-sensing processes.

TEM Analysis

The microstructural features of the synthesized CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposites were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) as shown in Figure . The TEM images in Figure a–b clearly reveal that MoS2 adopts a nanosheet-like morphology uniformly anchored onto the outer surfaces of the CNTs to form a distinctive core–shell structure in which CNTs act as the core and MoS2 sheets resemble a flower-like shell. This configuration indicates the strong interfacial interaction between the MoS2 sheets and CNTs, suggesting a successful integration of the two components during synthesis with our conditions. Furthermore, the high-resolution TEM images in Figure c–d display well-defined lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.64 nm which is in good agreement with the (002) plane of MoS2 and interlayer spacing of 0.36 nm corresponding to CNTs. These results collectively demonstrate the formation of a well-crystallized MoS2 structure intimately coupled with the CNTs network.

Gas Sensing Performance of CNTs@MoS2 Sensor

The gas sensing characteristics of the CNTs@MoS2 sensors were investigated using seven VOCs including NH3, methanol, ethanol, formaldehyde, propanol, toluene, and acetone as shown in Figure a. Each analyte was introduced at a fixed concentration of 500 ppm under room temperature. Among these, the sensor exhibits the highest response toward NH3 (∼94%), followed by methanol (∼25%) and ethanol (∼24%). In contrast, the responses to formaldehyde, propanol, toluene, and acetone were negligible under identical conditions. This selective response pattern suggests a strong interaction between NH3 molecules and the CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite which can be attributed to the high density of active sites and the efficient charge-transfer pathways provided by the CNTs network. Although methanol and ethanol exhibit moderate responses, other VOCs induce only negligible changes in sensor resistance, further confirming the material’s selectivity toward NH3. These findings demonstrate that the CNTs@MoS2 is a promising candidate for selective NH3 detection, particularly in applications that require sensitive and reliable monitoring at room temperature.

(a) Gas responses of CNTs@MoS2 gas sensors toward various VOCs (500 ppm) at room temperature. (b) Dynamic resistance response of CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor toward 500 ppm of NH3 at room temperature. (c) Responses of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor toward NH3 at varying concentrations (50–500 ppm) under room temperature. (d) Linear fitting curve of gas responses (%) versus NH3 concentrations.

To assess the stability and repeatability of the CNTs@MoS2 sensor, cyclic measurements were performed by exposing the sensor to 500 ppm of NH3 for six consecutive cycles. As shown in Figure b, the dynamic resistance of the CNTs@MoS2 sensor consistently decreases upon NH3 exposure and returned to its baseline level in clean air, confirming full reversibility of the sensing reaction. The sensor exhibits highly reproducible response profiles with no noticeable signal drift throughout the repeated cycles. The standard deviation of the response across six loops was less than 5%, indicating excellent operational stability. In addition to repeatability, the CNTs@MoS2 sensor demonstrates rapid response and recovery times of approximately 36 and 68 s, respectively. These fast switching dynamics suggest efficient adsorption and desorption of NH3 molecules on the active sites of the MoS2 nanosheets while the CNT framework provides accelerated charge transport pathways, enabling quick resistance modulation. Such synergistic behavior between MoS2 and CNTs confirms the advantages of the hybrid architecture.

To evaluate the concentration-dependent behavior of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor, its response to NH3 was tested at varying concentrations of 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, and 500 ppm under room temperature. The corresponding response values (%S) are presented in Figure c. As illustrated, the sensor’s response increases proportionally with the rise NH3 concentrations, indicating a clear and consistent trend of enhanced sensitivity at higher gas levels. As shown in Figure d, the response exhibits a good linear relationship with the NH3 concentration in the range of 50–500 ppm, following the fitting equation y = 6.2167 + 0.1945x, with a correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.96. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated based on the 3σ/slope method, where σ represents the standard deviation of the baseline signal and the slope was obtained from the linear calibration curve. The calculated LOD is 10.33 ppm. demonstrating the capability of the CNTs@MoS2 sensor to detect low concentrations of NH3 with high sensitivity at room temperature. This strong linearity indicates that the CNTs@MoS2 sensor can provide reliable quantitative detection of ammonia based on its response magnitude, confirming its potential for practical quantitative gas sensing applications. In addition, the concentration–response data were also analyzed using a Langmuir-type adsorption model, assuming that the sensor response is proportional to surface coverage. The fitting yields an adsorption equilibrium constant of K = 1.81 × 10–3 ppm–1. The calculated KC value remains below unity across the investigated range (0–500 ppm of NH3), approaching 1 only at the highest concentration when C is NH3 concentration. This indicates that the sensing behavior predominantly occurs within the quasi-linear region of the Langmuir isotherm and the slight deviation at 500 ppm can be attributed to the onset of surface saturation.

To elucidate the synergistic effect of the hybrid architecture, the gas-sensing performances of pristine MoS2 and pristine CNTs were systematically evaluated under 500 ppm of NH3 at room temperature. As shown in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information, the CNTs@MoS2 sensor exhibits a significantly enhanced response of approximately 94%, which is markedly higher than that of pristine MoS2 (61%). The moderate response observed for MoS2 can be attributed to its abundant adsorption sites for NH3 molecules. However, its relatively high intrinsic resistance and limited charge transport pathways restrict the overall resistance modulation. In contrast, CNTs alone display negligible response despite their excellent electrical conductivity. The substantial enhancement in the CNTs@MoS2 sensor clearly demonstrates a synergistic coupling effect between the two components. The MoS2 nanosheets provide chemically active adsorption sites for NH3 molecules, while the conductive CNTs network facilitates rapid electron transport and amplifies resistance variation upon gas adsorption. This interfacial charge-transfer mechanism significantly improves signal transduction efficiency, thereby enabling a superior sensing response at room temperature.

To investigate the humidity effects, the sensing response of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor toward 200 ppm of NH3 was evaluated under different relative humidity (%RH) levels ranging from 55% to 85% as shown in Figure S2 of the Supporting Information. As displayed in Figure a, the responses remain constant about ∼54% until 65% RH, then gradually increase to 59% and 64% at 70% and 75% RH, respectively. At higher humidity levels, the response further increases to 72% and 83% at 80% and 85% RH, respectively. This enhancement is attributed to the adsorption of water molecules on the CNTs@MoS2 surface. At higher humidity levels, H2O molecules are adsorbed and partially dissociate into protons (H+) and electrons, which facilitate charge carrier generation and improve electrical conductivity. Consequently, the redox interaction between NH3 molecules and preadsorbed oxygen species is promoted leading to more pronounced resistance variations and enhanced sensing response. In addition, humidity can promote molecular dissociation and surface reactions by lowering the activation energy for interfacial charge transfer. This observed sensing behavior is consistent with previously reported humidity-assisted NH3 sensing mechanisms in room-temperature MoS2-based gas sensor.

(a) Effect of various %RH values on the NH3 sensing response of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor. (b) Long-term stability of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor monitored from Day 30 to Day 150.

The long-term stability of a gas sensor is a critical parameter for practical applications. To assess the stability of the synthesized CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor, its response performance was monitored over 150 days as presented in Figure b. The CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor exhibits a gradual decrease in its response over time. Specifically, the response decreased from 94% at Day 30 to 89% at Day 90, corresponding to a loss of approximately 5.3% relative to the initial response. This decline could be attributed to minor alterations in active sites, surface contamination, or the slow degradation of the sensing material. Beyond Day 90, the response continued to decrease gradually, reaching 86% at Day 105, 83% at Day 120, and eventually stabilizing around 80–81% from Day 135 to Day 150. Overall, this represents a total loss of about 14.9% from the initial measurement. Despite this gradual decrease, the sensor demonstrates excellent long-term operational stability after reaching its steady-state performance. The ability of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor to maintain a nearly constant response around 80% from Day 135 to Day 150 underscores its potential for reliable and durable operation in gas-sensing applications. Sensing Mechanism

The gas sensing mechanism of the CNTs@MoS2 sensor toward NH3 is primarily governed by surface adsorption and redox interactions between NH3 molecules and preadsorbed oxygen species on the sensor surface (Figure ) facilitated by the formation of p–n heterojunctions between p-type CNTs and n-type MoS2 at room temperature. When the p-type CNTs and n-type MoS2 come into contact, a p–n heterojunction is formed at their interface due to the difference in work functions. This difference causes electron transfer from the n-type MoS2 to the p-type CNTs until their Fermi levels reach equilibrium leading to the formation of an electron depletion layer in MoS2 and a hole depletion region in CNTs. These interfacial charge redistributions effectively modulate the overall charge carrier concentration of the hybrid structure. As a result, the CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposite exhibits a resistance that is lower than that of pristine MoS2 but higher than that of pristine CNTs. It provides an optimal baseline resistance for gas sensing at room temperature.

Schematic illustration of the sensing mechanism of the CNTs@MoS2 gas sensor.

To clarify the respective roles of each component in the CNTs@MoS2 hybrid sensor, the sensing mechanism can be further interpreted by considering the intrinsic functions of MoS2, CNTs, and their interfacial CNTs@MoS2 junctions with toward 500 ppm of NH3 at room temperature as displayed in Figure S1 of Supporting Information. Based on these gas-sensing responses, it is clear that MoS2, as an n-type semiconducting material with abundant surface-active sites and defect states (e.g., sulfur vacancies), plays the predominant role in NH3 adsorption and molecular interaction. The adsorption of NH3 primarily occurs on the MoS2 surface, where charge transfer processes are initiated through interactions with preadsorbed oxygen species. In contrast, CNTs do not act as the primary adsorption sites for NH3 but function as a highly conductive charge-transport network that facilitates efficient carrier collection and signal transmission throughout the hybrid structure. Meanwhile, the p–n heterojunction formed at the CNTs@MoS2 interface serves as a charge-modulation region, where variations in the depletion width induced by NH3 adsorption on MoS2 are effectively converted into amplified resistance changes. Therefore, although the overall sensing response exhibits n-type semiconducting behavior dominated by MoS2, the CNT network and interfacial heterojunction synergistically enhance the sensitivity, response dynamics, and signal stability of the composite sensor.

Upon exposure to NH3, which acts as a reducing gas, the adsorbed NH3 molecules donate electrons to the surface, recombining with the adsorbed oxygen species (O2 –) as described in eq : 2 4NH3(gas)+5O2(ads)−↔4NO(gas)+6H2O+5e−

These electrons recombine with holes in the p-type CNTs and reduce the width of the depletion region at the p–n interface. Consequently, the charge carrier concentration increases leading to a decrease in the resistance of the CNTs@MoS2 sensor. This reversible decrease in resistance upon NH3 exposure and recovery in clean air (see Figure b) confirms that the sensing mechanism is dominated by surface-controlled charge transfer processes combined with the synergistic modulation of the p–n heterojunctions at the CNTs@MoS2 interface.

Conclusion

In summary, a flower-like CNTs@MoS2 core–shell nanostructure was successfully synthesized via a hydrothermal method and employed as a high-performance NH3 sensor operating at room temperature. Structural and morphological characterizations using XPS, SEM, and TEM confirmed the successful formation of CNTs@MoS2 nanocomposites. The CNTs@MoS2 sensor exhibits high responses of 94% to NH3 (500 ppm) with high selectivity over various VOCs such as methanol, ethanol, formaldehyde, propanol, toluene, and acetone. The CNTs@MoS2 sensor shows a fast response/recovery times (36/68 s) and reliable long-term stability with maintaining ∼80% of its initial response after 150 days. The superior sensing performance originates from interfacial charge modulation at the p–n junction between n-type MoS2 and p-type CNTs. When NH3 donates electrons to the hybrid surface, the depletion region narrows and resistance rapidly decreases, while the CNT framework ensures fast charge transport and recovery dynamics. This work provides fundamental insights into the charge-transfer dynamics and interfacial modulation mechanisms of CNTs@MoS2 hybrids, highlighting their potential as scalable, energy-efficient, and durable sensing materials for real-time NH3 monitoring in environmental, agricultural, and industrial applications at room temperature.
