Taxonomy, Host Associations, and Life Cycles of Four Rust Fungi Associated with Prunus spp. in Korea
Authors: Yoo Kyeong Lee, Jae Sung Lee, Seong-Jin Lee, Seung-Yeol Lee, Hee-Young Jung, Yongho Jeon, Hyeon-Dong Shin, Young-Joon Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRust fungi associated with Prunus spp. in Korea have been reported under different genera, yet their taxonomy, host associations, and life cycles have remained insufficiently clarified. In this study, rust specimens collected from Prunus species and their putative alternate hosts were examined using morphological observations and molecular phylogenetic analyses based on internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and large subunit (LSU) rDNA sequences. Four species belonging to three genera were identified: Blastospora makinoi, Blastospora radiata, Leucotelium semiaquilegiae, and Tranzschelia discolor. Blastospora radiata and L. semiaquilegiae are newly recorded in Korea. In addition, Prunus salicina and Semiaquilegia adoxoides are reported for the first time in Korea as hosts of rust fungi. Field investigations combined with sequence data clarified the heteroecious life cycles of Blastospora and Leucotelium species in Korea, confirming host alternation between Prunus spp. and alternate hosts including Se. adoxoides and Smilax spp. Historical records of rust fungi on Prunus in South Korea were taxonomically reassessed in light of these results. The congruence between molecular phylogenies and morphological characteristics provides an updated taxonomic framework for rust fungi associated with economically important Prunus species.

Introduction

The genus Prunus L. (Rosaceae) includes economically important fruit crops, such as almond, apricot, cherry, peach, plum, as well as numerous ornamental and wild species widely distributed in temperate regions. The fruits of Prunus are drupes with a single hard-coated seed and contain polyphenols, which significantly relax blood vessels and improve endothelial function [1,2]. Especially, peach (Prunus persica (L.) Batsch) is rich in phenolics, carotenoids, and vitamins and is widely consumed worldwide [3]. Although global peach and nectarine production reached 27.1 million metric tons in 2023 [4], production has declined in many countries since 2013 [5]. Fungal diseases are major factors affecting peach cultivation. Rust disease on Prunus spp. may cause premature defoliation and reduce fruit quality and yield, thereby affecting agricultural production and ornamental value [6]. Despite their economic importance, the taxonomy, diversity, and life cycles of rust fungi associated with Prunus remain unresolved in many countries, including Korea.

Rust fungi (Pucciniales) include about 7000 species and represent the largest group of plant-pathogenic Basidiomycota [7]. They are characterized by complex life cycles that may produce up to five spore stages [8]. Many species are heteroecious, alternating between unrelated host plants, which complicates their identification based solely on morphology and host association. Reliable species identification therefore requires an integrated approach combining morphology, host association, and molecular phylogenetic data.

In East Asia, rust fungi associated with Prunus have been placed in several genera, including Caeoma Link, Blastospora Dietel, Leucotelium Tranzschel, and Tranzschelia Arthur [9,10]. Species of Blastospora alternate between Prunus (aecial host) and Smilax L. (telial host) [11–13], whereas Leucotelium species typically alternate between Ranunculaceae (aecial host) and Prunus (telial host) [14,15]. Tranzschelia discolor (Fuckel) Tranzschel & M.A. Litv. has been widely reported on Anemone L. (aecial host) and Prunus (telial host) worldwide [16], but in some (sub)tropical countries, it has been suggested to overwinter without an alternate host [17,18]. Recent molecular studies have clarified generic concepts and nomenclature of Prunus-associated rust fungi [13,19], but regional confirmations using DNA sequence data remain limited.

In Korea, rust fungi on Prunus have been recorded under three different genera, Blastospora, Caeoma, and Tranzschelia [10]. However, their host–fungus associations remain unclarified, and some Prunus hosts (e.g., P. mume (Siebold) Siebold & Zucc.) have been attributed to different rust taxa [13]. In addition, Prunus salicina Lindl. and Semiaquilegia adoxoides (DC.) Makino have not been documented previously in Korea as hosts of rust fungi. These issues highlight the need for an updated taxonomic account based on integrative evidence.

The present study aims to clarify the taxonomy and diversity of rust fungi associated with Prunus spp., to document previously unreported rust species and host plants, and to complete their life cycles in South Korea through integrating field observations with morphological and molecular phylogenetic analyses based on the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and large subunit (LSU) rDNA sequences.

Materials and methods

Collection of rust samples

Rust-infected samples of Prunus spp. were collected at various sites in Korea between 1994 and 2026. As several rust fungi associated with Prunus are known to be heteroecious, field surveys also targeted potential alternate hosts, including Smilax spp. (Smilacaceae) and Se. adoxoides (Ranunculaceae), growing near infected Prunus trees. All collected samples were air-dried and preserved as herbarium specimens. A total of 25 specimens were deposited in the Kunsan National University Herbarium (KSNUH) and the Korea University Herbarium (KUS-F). Morphological analysis

Symptoms and sori were examined under a stereomicroscope (M205C; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a digital camera (Dhyana 400DC; Tucsen, Fuzhou, China). For observation of spermogonia and internal tissue structures, cryosections were prepared using a cryostat (CM1860; Leica). Small pieces of infected leaf tissue were placed on specimen disks and embedded in frozen section compound. After freezing at low temperature, the specimens were mounted onto the specimen holder and sectioned at a thickness of 50 µm. Sections were mounted on glass slides and examined under a differential interference contrast (DIC) microscope (Axio Imager 2 + Axiocam 512 color; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). For morphological observation, sori (aecia, uredinia, and telia) and spores (aeciospores, urediniospores, and teliospores) were mounted in lactic acid on glass slides, gently heated, and examined using a DIC microscope. Measurements of at least 30 structures per structure type (sori and spores) were taken to determine size ranges and averages. Surface ornamentation of rust structures was examined with a scanning electron microscope (SEM; S-4800 + EDS; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Samples were mounted on aluminum stubs, sputter-coated with platinum, and observed at 3–5 kV.

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from rust-infected leaves using the MagListo 5 M Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea). The ITS region was amplified using the primer set ITS5u/ITS4rust [20,21], and LSU was amplified using LRust1R/LRust3 [21]. For some specimens, the primer pair Rust2inv/LR6 was additionally used to obtain longer LSU fragments [22,23]. PCR products were purified with the AccuPrep® PCR/Gel Purification Kit (Bioneer) and bidirectionally sequenced by a DNA sequencing service (Macrogen, Seoul, Korea). Sequences were assembled and edited using DNASTAR Lasergene v7.1 (DNASTAR Inc., Madison, USA) and aligned with reference sequences retrieved from GenBank (NCBI). Phylogenetic trees were constructed with the maximum likelihood (ML) method based on the Tamura 3-parameter model in MEGA 7 [24]. The robustness of phylogenetic relationships was evaluated by bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates.

Results

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

ML phylogenetic analyses based on ITS and LSU rDNA sequences produced congruent tree topologies and consistently resolved three well-supported genera comprising four species associated with Prunus spp. in Korea: Blastospora, Leucotelium, and Tranzschelia (Figures 1 and 2). All major clades received high bootstrap support values (above 87%) in both phylogenetic trees, although there were weak supports (60–70%) for two Blastospora species.

The Blastospora clade comprised two species-level lineages corresponding to Blastospora makinoi (Kusano) Jing X. Ji, Okane & Kakish. and Blastospora radiata (Shirai) Jing X. Ji, Okane & Kakish. Korean specimens grouped with reference sequences of both species from Japan and China. The sequences of B. makinoi exhibited five nucleotide differences (ITS: 4, LSU: 1) from B. radiata. Among B. radiata samples, the sequences from Japan and Korea exhibited two nucleotide variations in ITS and one in LSU. Leucotelium semiaquilegiae (Dietel) Kakish., K. Hosaka & Hosoya formed a monophyletic clade, including Korean samples from Se. adoxoides, P. persica, and P. salicina, together with a Japanese Prunus sample. Korean specimens identified as T. discolor clustered within a globally distributed clade containing sequences from Brazil, China, Iran, New Zealand, Oman, Taiwan, and the USA, originating from various Prunus species.

Morphology

Morphological characteristics observed in Korean specimens supported the recognition of three genera: Blastospora, Leucotelium, and Tranzschelia (Figures 3–6). Species of Blastospora produced yellow aecia on Prunus, with uredinia and telia on Smilax. Aeciospores were ornamented, urediniospores globose to subglobose and echinulate, and teliospores one-celled with thin, smooth walls. B. makinoi was characterized by verrucose aeciospores and rounded telia, whereas B. radiata showed completely echinulate urediniospores and telia distinctly confined by leaf veins.

Leucotelium semiaquilegiae was distinguished by hypophyllous, aecidium-type aecia. Aeciospores were verrucose and subglobose to oblong, and uredinia produced reddish brown, echinulate urediniospores. Host associations and life cycles

Field surveys combined with ITS and LSU sequence data enabled documentation of heteroecious life cycles in three species (Figure 7). In Blastospora, Prunus spp. served as the aecial host and Smilax spp. served as the telial host. For B. makinoi, specimens collected from P. mume and Sm. sieboldii var. inermis Nakai were placed within the same phylogenetic clade, supporting host alternation between these species. Similarly, B. radiata appears to complete its life cycle between Prunus sp. and Sm. nipponica Miq., with specimens from both hosts forming a single clade in the phylogenetic analyses.

Life cycles of four rust species (Blastospora makinoi, B. radiata, Leucotelium semiaquilegiae, and Tranzschelia discolor) associated with Prunus spp. and their alternate hosts in South Korea.

Leucotelium semiaquilegiae was documented on Se. adoxoides and P. persica and P. salicina. Specimens from these hosts clustered together in a well-supported monophyletic clade, supporting a heteroecious life cycle alternating between Ranunculaceae and Prunus. In Korea, only the uredinial stage of T. discolor was observed on P. persica, and neither telia nor alternate hosts were discovered. The heteroecious life cycles of Blastospora and Leucotelium species are confirmed here for the first time in Korea.

Note: The Korean specimens exhibited echinulate aeciospores and more distinctively echinulate urediniospores, and vein-limited telia. Previously, Blastospora itoana Togashi & Onuma was described as the teleomorph of Caeoma radiatum Shirai and was later treated as a synonym of B. radiata [12,13]. Prunus spp. and Smilax spp. have been recorded as its hosts in China, Japan, Nepal, Taiwan, the Himalaya, and USSR [9] (Table 3). Previously, B. itoana was reported on Sm. nipponica in Korea [28], but the record remained uncertain due to the lack of detailed description and no preserved specimen. The present study provides voucher-based morphology and ITS and LSU sequence data, supporting the occurrence of B. radiata in Korea and documents its heteroecious life cycle between Prunus sp. and Sm. nipponica.

Discussion

Rust fungi associated with Prunus in Korea have been recorded in limited taxonomic accounts (Table 2), and most previous identifications were based primarily on morphology and host association [10,26,27,30,31,34]. Given the morphological similarities of rust taxa and the complexity of heteroecious life cycles, such records require verification using molecular data. In the present study, ITS and LSU phylogenies were combined with detailed morphological observations and host data to clarify the taxonomic status of four rust species associated with Prunus in Korea.

The taxonomic history of these fungi reflects long-standing difficulties in rust classification. Aecidium semiaquilegiae Dietel was originally described as the anamorph of Leucotelium pruni-persicae (Hori) Tranzschel and later treated as L. semiaquilegiae [14,19]. Likewise, as Caeoma makinoi was demonstrated to present the aecial stage of Blastospora smilacis Dietel, the name B. makinoi was formally adopted under the Shenzhen Code [11,13]. Recent molecular studies in Japan and China have clarified generic boundaries within these rust fungi and the distinct host-alternation systems of Blastospora and Leucotelium [11,12,14]. The Korean specimens examined in this study are well congruent with those East Asian lineages.

As many rust fungi require two unrelated hosts to complete their life cycles, understanding these cycles is essential for assessing risk and epidemiology, particularly in economically important crops [7,8]. The present study confirms host alternation in Blastospora and Leucotelium species, strengthening the biological interpretation of these rust systems in temperate fruit trees. Recent integrated studies in East Asia have indicated the value of combining molecular phylogenetics and field data to resolve rust life cycles in woody crops. For example, the heteroecious cycle of Nyssopsora cedrelae (Hori) Tranzschel alternating on Aralia elata (Miq.) Seem., an economically important tree species, and Toona sinensis (Juss.) M. Roem. was clarified using multilocus sequence analyses and pathogenicity assay [114].

For T. discolor, Anemone spp. have been reported as alternate hosts in several regions outside East Asia (Table 3) [9]. Ellison et al. [17] showed that urediniospores of T. discolor can overwinter on infected leaf litter and infect Prunus spp. the following spring in New South Wales, Australia, and the species has been suggested to overwinter as mycelia in twig infections, forming twig cankers that serve as sources of urediniospores in California, USA [18]. Given that neither the present study nor previous observation [31] detected telia on infected leaves [31] in South Korea and that this species was only found on the southern island of South Korea, a similar overwintering mechanism may contribute to the survival of this species without any alternate host in Korea.

The distribution of alternate hosts represents a key ecological determinant of heteroecious rust fungi, because shifts in alternate host distribution may influence the establishment, persistence, and spread. Climate change can further shape rust epidemiology by altering temperature regimes, precipitation patterns, host phenology, and the distribution ranges of alternate hosts [7]. In Korea, ecological modeling has demonstrated that northward expansion of Corydalis incisa (Thunb.) Pers. under climate change is associated with increased risk of rust disease on Wisteria floribunda (Willd.) DC., an economically important ornamental vine [115]. Similarly, Se. adoxoides, an aecial host of L. semiaquilegiae in Japan [14] and Korea, is recognized as a climate-sensitive biological indicator species [116], and its recent expansion in Korea [117] provides ecological context for the occurrence of L. semiaquilegiae on Prunus cultivations. More broadly, increasing incidence of subtropical rust pathogens such as Puccinia polysora Underw. in the Korean Peninsula further illustrates how environmental change may facilitate the establishment and persistence of rust fungi beyond their previously recognized distribution ranges [118]. These climate-driven shifts in alternate host distribution may therefore influence the long-term dynamics of heteroecious rust fungi associated with Prunus in Korea.

The present study refines the taxonomy of rust fungi infecting Prunus in Korea through integrated morphological and molecular evidence and documents their host associations. It clarifies species boundaries and heteroecious life cycles, providing a systematic foundation for future ecological, epidemiological, and phytosanitary research under ongoing climate and environmental change.
