Exploring a novel voice-guided artificial intelligence platform for real-time colonoscopy documentation: a pilot study
Authors: Mahsa Taghiakbari, Timothy Wong, Rohini Gaikar, Azar Azad, Robert Battat, Mickael Bouin, Benoit Panzini, Roupen Djinbachian, David Armstrong, Daniel von Renteln
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate and consistent documentation during colonoscopy is essential for optimal patient care and therapeutic decisions. Traditional manual documentation is time-consuming and subject to variability. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted tools offer potential improvements by standardizing report generation in real-time. We developed a novel AI-driven, voice-guided reporting platform that uses natural language processing (NLP) and real-time image capture for endoscopy documentation.MethodsThis prospective pilot study was conducted at the Centre Hospitalier de l‘Université de Montréal between October 2023 and May 2024. A total of 95 patients undergoing elective endoscopy were recruited, with 57 procedures included in the final analysis. Endoscopists provided real-time verbal dictations during procedures, which the AI-assisted report generation tool transcribed and linked to captured images. The system’s performance was evaluated based on documentation completeness, transcription accuracy, and user engagement.ResultsThe AI-assisted report generation tool successfully documented key procedural parameters when verbal annotations were provided, achieving an 87.5% detection rate for ileocecal valve identification, and 100% detection rate for procedure indication, Boston Bowel Preparation Score, withdrawal time, and polyp characterization. However, the transcription word error was 10.07%, with errors primarily in medical terminology. User engagement varied, with some procedures lacking dictated annotations.ConclusionOur AI-assisted report generation tool demonstrates potential in standardizing colonoscopy documentation through AI-assisted, real-time NLP for generating reports. While effective, its performance depends on endoscopist engagement. Future improvements in NLP capabilities and structured reporting prompts can enhance completeness and usability, contributing to more efficient and accurate endoscopy documentation.Keywords: artificial intelligence, documentation, colonoscopy, endoscopy, natural language processing

Introduction

Accurate and consistent documentation during colonoscopy is important for optimal patient management, influencing surveillance intervals and therapeutic decisions. Traditional documentation methods are often subject to variability, leading to inconsistencies in reporting critical details such as polyp characteristics and bowel preparation quality. Artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy in endoscopy, particularly in lesion detection through computer-assisted detection (CADe) and characterization. Studies indicate that AI systems can increase adenoma detection rates, avoid unnecessary histopathology examinations, streamline workflow, and standardize key quality indicators. The AI in gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy holds tremendous promise to augment clinical performance, better treatment plans, and improve patient outcomes.1 However, the application of AI in standardizing endoscopy documentation through real-time report generation remains underexplored. Building on these advancements, the Artificial Intelligence Decision-support for REal-time Medical Image Analysis (AIDREA) platform is a novel AI-driven, voice-guided reporting tool designed to enhance colonoscopy documentation by standardizing report elements in real-time. Unlike other AI systems primarily focused on visual detection, the AIDREA leverages natural language processing (NLP) to transcribe and analyze the endoscopist’s verbal input (Figure 1). This enables comprehensive and consistent documentation of essential elements—including patient demographics, sedation details, indications, procedural findings, and follow-up recommendations—without the need for manual entry. This prospective pilot study evaluates the efficacy of the AIDREA in generating comprehensive reports by integrating real-time verbal dictation from the endoscopists with endoscopic images during procedures.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective pilot study at the Centre Hospitalier de l‘Université de Montréal (CHUM), between October 2023 and May 2024, enrolling 81 adult patients aged 28-80 years undergoing elective colonoscopy. Approached patients received information about AI and the AIDREA systems through a consent form, with research staff answering questions. All study participants signed a written informed consent before the procedures, which the research ethics committee approved at the CHUM (CER# 2023-11159). We excluded all hospitalized patients, patients in need of an emergency endoscopy, and those with American Society of Anesthesiologists status >3.

Procedures and data collection

Three staff endoscopists and one trainee performed all colonoscopies using Fujifilm (CAD-EYE; Fujifilm Co., Tokyo, Japan) or Olympus systems (Olympus colonoscopes 190 series; Olympus Corp, Center Valley, PA, United States). Before the procedure began, endoscopists were instructed to provide key elements of a colonoscopy report through verbal dictation during the procedure. During the colonoscopy procedures, the AIDREA system was activated via voice commands (“AIDREA START,” “AIDREA CAPTURE” and/or “AIDREA,” if no photodocumentation is intended, such as dictating patient characteristics or procedure indication or recommendations) or through pressing the image capture button of the endoscope that will document a photo along with the verbal dictations. The platform allows real-time documentation of procedural data, including procedural time described as the time between endoscope insertion to and removal from the rectum, procedure indication, bowel preparation quality according to the Boston bowel preparation score, ileocecal valve (ICV) identification, withdrawal time described as the time between cecum identification and endoscope removal from the rectum (feature added for the last 20 procedures), and polyp characteristics such as size, location, morphology, polypectomy techniques, and specimen retrieval without the need for manual data entry by the endoscopist. Endoscopists and GI technicians received training for AIDREA using dummy models, which was simulation-based training. Initially, they trained to connect the AIDREA device to the endoscopy systems, connecting the microphone to the AIDREA device. Once the setup was ready, they recorded complete colonoscopy procedures using the AIDREA device. While performing the colonoscopy, they used AIDREA voice commands to start recording the procedure, to capture an image, and provided annotation for the image using verbal dictation. The work instructions were shared with the users for recording the key parameters during the procedure.

Outcome measures

The AIDREA device can successfully document the observation of key parameters if they are provided by the clinicians during the procedures. The key parameters identified were the ICV observation, procedure indication, Boston bowel score, withdrawal time calculation, and polyp/lesion observations during the colonoscopy procedure. The primary outcome was the detection rate of documentation for key parameters, presented as the percentage of accurately documented data. The AIDREA detection rate was defined as the number of colonoscopy procedures that correctly documented key parameters using the provided verbal dictations divided by the total number of colonoscopy procedures having the key parameters in verbal dictations.

There were 2 scenarios when AIDREA could not report the key parameters observation. Firstly when the user forgot to report the observation in verbal dictations, and secondly when the automatic speech recognition (ASR) model incorrectly transcribes the words provided in verbal dictations to AIDREA. Considering these 2 scenarios we reported 2 secondary outcomes: (1) the user engagement ratio with the AIDREA device, defined as the number of colonoscopy procedures satisfying the criteria to provide key parameters in verbal dictations divided by the total number of colonoscopy procedures, and (2) missed detection by AIDREA in percentage defined as the number of colonoscopy procedures missed the reporting of key parameters out of total colonoscopy procedure having key parameters provided in verbal dictations.

AI-driven, voice-guided reporting tool (AIDREA)

The AIDREA platform (A.I. VALI Inc., Toronto, Canada) is an agnostic, modular AI system and can be connected to all endoscopy platforms’ monitors. The AIDREA platform offers features such as automatic real-time image capture, adding voice annotation to the captured image, voice-to-text annotation, and expedited standardized colonoscopy procedure data capture for the patient reports (eg, calculating withdrawal time). The platform was designed as a laptop connection and is compatible with any scope system or computer-assisted detection (CADe) software (eg, CADEYE), requiring no additional built-in feature (Figure 2). Compared to the traditional reporting method of gastroenterology procedures, the AIDREA is a smart, AI-driven voice-guided reporting tool that enables real-time documentation of findings during the procedure.

AIDREA platform adopted in a clinical setting.

The AIDREA works in 3 sequential stages of data processing (Figure 1):

In the first stage, AIDREA begins the procedure recording with the voice command “AIDREA START.” Voice commands are recognized through the live microphone stream using an optimized conformer ASR architecture,2 enhance with Legendre memory units3 to improve performance, memory usage, and inference speed. Throughout the procedure, endoscopists trigger image captures either with the endoscope or by saying the voice command “AIDREA Capture.” Each image capture initiates a verbal dictation, which is linked to the image, allowing the endoscopist to describe observations such as abnormalities or anatomical landmarks. The audio recording stops with the voice command “AIDREA” or after 5 seconds of silence.

In the second stage, the verbal dictations associated with the captured image are processed to generate raw text using an ASR model. The word error rate (WER), the ratio of errors in a transcript to the total words spoken, is the evaluation metric commonly used in evaluating ASR models, transcription software, and even in some NLP applications. A lower WER in speech-to-text means better accuracy in recognizing speech. We utilized the pretrained state-of-the-art Whisper ASR model for speech recognition, as it met the memory requirements to run locally on the AIDREA device.4 The pretrained Whisper ASR model was validated on the in-house custom proprietary gastroenterology endoscopy procedure dataset. The Whisper ASR generated transcriptions (raw or unstructured text) from the endoscopists’ verbal dictation audio samples. The postprocessing rules were applied to the generated transcriptions using search-and-replace for misspellings or typographical errors, based on prior test errors before this study.

In the third stage, after completion of the video recording, the unstructured text is processed using the named entity recognition (NER) approach. The set of rules was developed to identify endoscopy landmarks, treatments, interventions, polyp findings, adverse events, and the Boston bowel preparation score for each segment of the colon from the unstructured text. The entities extracted in NER processing are converted to a structured data format to prepare the procedure report. This structured NER extraction and transcribed dictations are displayed on the AIDREA interface as part of the endoscopist’s review and report completion. Endoscopists could edit the report using a keyboard or voice input. After the review, a PDF report is created, accompanied by images, dictation audio, and the full procedure video as part of the procedure package (Figure 3). The procedure package is stored in the AIDREA device’s local database and could be transferred to other locations using a secure file transfer protocol.

Results

To complete this study successfully, endoscopists were instructed to dictate information related to the patient’s demographics, sedation drugs used, dosage, and postcolonoscopy follow-up recommendations in English. A total of 81 consecutive patients were recruited for the study (Supplemental Figure 1). Two patients were initially excluded from the study because the user forgot to turn on AIDREA during their colonoscopy procedures. Later, we excluded 22 colonoscopy procedures due to reported data being in French or a mix of French and English, leaving 57 colonoscopy procedures recorded in English for the final analysis. Thus, AIDREA successfully recorded colonoscopy procedures in 57 (males: 27, females: 30) patients, with a mean age of 64.77 years (SD = 10.77 years), with a mean procedure time of 22.19 minutes (SD = 11.10 minutes) (Supplemental Table 2). To evaluate AIDREA’s performance, a statistical power analysis was conducted. We set the minimum acceptable detection rate of key parameters 70% and an expected detection rate of key parameters of 90%. This test used a significance level of 𝛂 = 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80. This analysis showed that at least 47 samples are required to ensure the validity of the evaluation outcomes. A total of 57 colonoscopy cases were roughly 21.27% than required to account for potential data loss and increase reliability. Out of 57 colonoscopy procedures, 10 were performed with an intact colon, showing no polyps or abnormal findings. Although the AIDREA device was turned on, users failed to record the verbal annotations for some key parameters during the colonoscopy. Only 19 out of 57 colonoscopy procedures had all key parameter observations provided in verbal dictations. Thus, the colonoscopy procedure completeness rate of the user, provisioning of all required key parameters, was 33.33% (n = 19/57).

When information was provided to AIDREA in verbal annotations, AIDREA successfully documented the key parameters. The detection rate of AIDREA for key parameters documentation, when dictated in verbal annotations for each criterion was: ICV identification (87.50%, n = 42/48), procedure indication (100%, n = 19/19), Boston bowel preparation scores (100%, n = 34/34), Withdrawal time (100%, n = 20/20), and Polyp/lesion information extraction (100%, n = 39/39) (Table 1).

We also evaluated the performance of the ASR model. The pretrained Whisper ASR model validated on an in-house endoscopy dictation dataset generated a transcription WER of 32.56%. The transcription WER was reduced to 10.07% for the verbal annotations provided for the captured images using the postprocessing rules for correction of misspellings or typographical errors.

Discussion

Over the past 10 years, the number of endoscopic procedures has significantly increased, resulting in reduced time and cost efficiency of screening programs due to a shortage of resources.5,6 Endoscopy documentation is an important component of the endoscopy workflow, ensuring accurate records of findings, interventions, and recommendations to optimize patient outcomes.7 The integration of AI in endoscopy documentation aligns with the need for the appropriate use of limited resources and high-quality, cost- and time-effective endoscopy services. In this pilot study, our AI-assisted report generation tool showed potential for standardizing endoscopy documentation through real-time verbal reporting. It could accurately capture key procedural components, with an accuracy of 87.5%-100%, when verbalized by the endoscopist, ensuring consistent and efficient documentation.

Computerized endoscopic medical records improve accuracy, efficiency, and standardization in endoscopy documentation.8,9 These systems improve efficiency through automated scheduling, interoperability (eg, seamless data exchange with databases, pathology interface), automated scoping (eg, patient monitoring during the procedure), and structured documentation. Electronic reports must include key elements for effective interdisciplinary communication, practice audit, and quality assurance.7,10 Manual documentation is prone to human error and variability, particularly in tracking quality metrics like adenoma detection rates. One study found that endoscopy centers mainly documented procedure volume, wait time, and no-show/cancellation procedures but inconsistently documented completeness of colonoscopy (ie, cecum identification), BPPS, withdrawal time, and adverse events. Our AI-assisted report generation tool efficiently records procedure details, ensuring standardized documentation, reducing manual errors, and supporting quality assessment and clinical decision-making.

Efficient management of pre- and postprocedure wait time is important for screening program efficiency and patient satisfaction.11 Manual report finalization prolongs intervals between procedures and increases the risk of data loss, as endoscopists may forget key details. For instance, a speech recognition-based endoscopy reporting system using predefined templates reduced report generation time, but voice commands delayed input capture, and even a foot switch alternative could disrupt endoscopist concentration.9 Our AI-based tool presents a solution by automating the transcription of procedural data with minimal delay, only by a simple voice command, allowing endoscopists to focus on patient care, reduce fatigue, and improve procedural throughput. Additionally, automating traditional documentation with our AI-based tool would lower operational costs and optimize resource use. One study showed that replacing traditional endoscope fault reporting with a digital system to arrange repairs saved over 4 hours of staff time, equal to £178 in efficiency gains.12

Photodocumentation is crucial for quality assurance, training, and longitudinal patient monitoring, as it enables objective assessment of mucosal abnormalities, facilitates second opinions, and supports adherence to standardized reporting guidelines.13 A key feature of our AI-based report generation tool is its ability to document endoscopy photos and correctly match them with corresponding information by seamlessly integrating real-time image capture with AI-assisted voice-guided reporting, ensuring accurate documentation of critical visual findings without disrupting workflow.

Our AI-based report generation tool offers distinct advantages over other existing automatic documentation systems. It is designed to integrate seamlessly into existing “endoscopic workstation,” requiring minimal disruption during implementation.14 Additionally, the system is user-friendly as it can be activated via simple voice commands or image capture, providing intuitive and flexible options for endoscopists during procedures. It would automatically record a wide range of procedural data, including indications, bowel preparation quality, anatomical landmarks, and polyp characteristics, with a high recognition rate and without the need for manual input, ensuring thorough and detailed documentation. It can integrate pathology databases for tracking outcomes and automatically measure quality metrics, such as adenoma detection rates, which are directly linked to interval cancer risk and patient outcomes.

Our AI-based report generation tool faced some challenges that could be partially mitigated over time. While using Whisper-based ASR on AIDREA, we identified several areas to improve the model. First, the whisper could not handle the long speech recordings of more than 30 seconds. In clinical settings, this is difficult to monitor the duration of time and restrict the verbal dictation to less than 30 seconds. Therefore, we derived a method to pass audio to ASR in chunks of less than 30 seconds. We identified the silent pauses in the speaker’s speech to prepare the audio in chunks. Second, the ASR was improved by setting up postprocessing rules for the commonly misspelled words for the key terminologies. Therefore, AIDREA successfully detected all key parameters when provided in the verbal annotation, except the ICV. The ASR made some transcription errors that postprocessing rules failed to handle, resulting in the ICV detection rate of 87.50%. Third, in actual clinical settings, the performance of Whisper ASR was affected by the speaker’s pace and dialect. This issue can be mitigated by optimizing the system for diverse speech patterns, incorporating adaptive language models, and providing endoscopists with guidance on optimal speaking techniques to improve transcription accuracy. Fourth, it had difficulty in accurately interpreting medical terminologies due to hard and misapplied words and variations of terminologies used by endoscopists. This can be addressed by enhancing its language model with specialized medical datasets, integrating domain-specific vocabulary, and allowing for continuous learning from expert corrections. Additionally, incorporating user feedback and refining speech-to-text algorithms can further improve accuracy in recognizing complex medical terms. The minimal standard terminology usage can also help in simplifying electronic report generation,15 but needs endoscopists’ training for proper adherence. Notably, the need for correction of medical terminologies manual entry was more related to postcolonoscopy surveillance recommendations in almost all procedures, and the missing report of the Boston bowel preparation score for one or more colon segments. Fifth, this pilot study’s small sample size limits the generalizability of findings. Other challenges included logistical failures such as setting up the microphone, filtering out background noise in the endoscopy room, and ensuring endoscopists consistently used the tool for important documentation. This user variability in input data (eg, not providing annotation or diagnosis via microphone) affected the consistency of report completeness. Although the AI-based report generation tool shows significant promise, its effectiveness in documenting complex cases—like multiple polyp findings and the negative implications associated with such findings, as well as adverse events—largely relies on how clinicians engage with the platform. We observed that when endoscopists were fully engaged in dictating procedure characteristics and actively using AIDREA, the system consistently generated accurate reports. This finding underscores that AIDREA’s performance is significantly influenced by the endoscopists’ active participation and proper microphone usage, emphasizing the importance of user training for optimal outcomes.

In conclusion, our AI-based report generation tool effectively addresses the challenges of time and cost consumption, and variability in traditional endoscopy documentation. Its real-time transcription improves efficiency, reduces operational costs, and ensures accurate, consistent procedural records, ultimately improving patient care and workflow. The system’s success depends on endoscopist engagement and precise verbal input. Future refinement of NLP warrants better medical terminology recognition, comprehensive and quality reporting of key endoscopic findings, and patient outcomes.
