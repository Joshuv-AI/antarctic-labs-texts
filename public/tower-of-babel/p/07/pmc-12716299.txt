Using stereo‐electroencephalography data to model the optimal intracranial venous sinus location for an endovascular seizure detection device: A feasibility study
Authors: Thanomporn Wittayacharoenpong, Gil Rind, Martin Hunn, Matthew Gutman, Matt Hudson, Zhibin Chen, Joshua Laing, Terence J O'Brien, Nicholas Opie, Andrew Neal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe investigated the theoretical optimal venous location for an endovascular seizure detection device using stereo‐EEG (SEEG) data.MethodsSix venous sinus segments from consecutive SEEG patients: anterior, middle, and posterior sagittal sinus (SS‐A, SS‐M, SS‐P), straight sinus, and the ipsilateral/contralateral (relative to primary epileptogenic zone) transverse sinus (ITS, CTS). 25 mm was selected as optimal propagation distance, satisfying two criteria: (1) >80% of seizures propagated to the reference point and (2) >90% of patients had at least one electrode within this range. A sinus was classified as “detecting” a seizure if SEEG contacts within a 25 mm radius showed ictal activity at <5 s, <10 s, or any time from onset. We analyzed data at seizure level (focal‐preserved‐consciousness seizure (FPC), focal‐impaired‐consciousness seizure (FIC), and focal‐to‐bilateral‐tonic‐clonic (FBTCS)) and epilepsy level (temporal/extratemporal (TLE/ETLE)). Multilevel regression and pairwise comparisons were performed to assess detection rates across sinuses.ResultsWe analyzed 113 seizures (FPC: 30.1%, FIC: 50.4%, FBTCS: 19.5%) from 40 patients (TLE: 50%). At the optimal propagation distance, 86.11% of seizures propagated to the reference point. Additionally, 92.5% of seizures were detectable from at least one sinus location at any time point, with an average minimum sinus‐to‐contact distance of 9.38 mm. Seizure level: ITS had the highest seizure detection rate at any time point (FPC: 71.43%, FIC: 95.56%, FBTCS: 100%) and was superior or non‐inferior to other sinuses for FPC and FIC. No significant differences were observed for FBTCS between sinuses. Epilepsy‐level: ITS detected the most seizures at any time point (TLE: 93.75%, ETLE: 82.35%). ITS outperformed other sinuses for TLE, except the straight sinus. For ETLE, only SS‐P was significantly less effective than SS‐M and SS‐A.SignificanceBased upon SEEG seizure propagation modeling, over 90% of seizures were detectable from venous sinus locations, supporting the feasibility of endovascular seizure detection devices. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders, affecting ~50 million people worldwide. The unpredictable nature of seizures often has a significant impact on the quality of life. 1 , 2 , 3 The development of technologies to enable accurate and timely seizure detection, counting, and prediction would greatly improve the management of epilepsy, including the effectiveness of anti‐seizure medication. 4 , 5

Traditional methods for recording seizures, such as seizure diaries, rely on patients' self‐reporting; however, they are very subjective and unreliable, especially for patients who might not recognize their seizures. 6 While wearable devices like smartwatches offer noninvasive monitoring, their effectiveness is limited by individual variability in physiological responses, which may differ from person to person, leading to inconsistent performance. 7 , 8 Due to this limitation, there is increasing interest in the role of implantable devices to record epileptic activity. Several subcutaneous long‐term monitoring systems are currently being investigated (e.g., UNEEG, Neuroview, Epios, EASEE, and Epi‐Minder); these devices offer continuous and long‐term EEG recordings; however, they have some inherent limitations. 6 , 9 , 10 , 11

In recent years, endovascular electrocorticography devices have gained attention as promising alternatives for detecting epileptiform activity. 12 These devices offer several advantages to existing long‐term implantable devices, including being minimally invasive, providing extended recording periods, and being in close proximity to the cortex. 13 , 14 , 15 An endovascular stent‐electrode array which self‐expands upon deployment can be inserted through the jugular vein and placed within intracranial vessels. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Intravascular electrodes have been shown to detect both low‐ and high‐frequency cortical rhythms. 20 , 21 , 22 Additionally, animal studies have shown that superior sagittal sinus electrodes can detect epileptiform discharges simultaneously observed in adjacent implanted subdural grids, 20 , 21 but not recorded on scalp EEG. 21

Despite these promising features, there is a lack of literature examining the sensitivity and effectiveness of an endovascular electrode array to detect seizures in focal epilepsy and the optimal sinus deployment location. Due to the potential risks associated with implantation and long‐term use, further feasibility data are needed before proceeding to human epilepsy studies. This retrospective study aimed to utilize stereo‐electroencephalography (SEEG) data to model the neurophysiological feasibility of detecting seizures from intracranial venous locations and to therefore model the optimal placement of an endovascular device. We examine the propagation of seizures to cortical regions adjacent to intracranial venous sinuses where endovascular electrodes could theoretically record the same ictal rhythm. We hypothesized that the optimal venous location for an endovascular seizure detection device would need to be individualized for different seizure and epilepsy types.

METHOD

Patient selection

Consecutive patients who underwent SEEG implantation at Alfred. SEEG implantation was tailored to each patient based on the hypothesized epileptogenic zone (EZ), as determined by a multidisciplinary team using noninvasive data, including semiology, EEG, neuroimaging, and neuropsychological assessments. 23 , 24

Image processing

Post‐SEEG implantation CT scan was co‐registered with the preoperative MRI using Brainlab Elements Stereotaxy software (iPlan Stereotaxy, Brainlab, Feldkirchen, Germany). The AC‐PC line standardized the brain position for all patients, with the anterior commissure serving as the original point (X = Y = Z = 0). We identified four prominent venous sinuses (superior sagittal, straight, and right and left transverse sinuses) on pre‐implantation T1‐weighted images with contrast. These sinuses were selected and considered accessible by an endovascular device based on the following criteria: (i) vessel diameter larger than 1 mm and (ii) accessible from either jugular vein. 16 , 17 The sagittal sinus was equally segmented into three sections: anterior, middle, and posterior (SS‐A, SS‐M, and SS‐P, respectively) (Figure 1A1). The transverse sinuses were defined as ipsilateral (ITS) and contralateral (CTS) based on the primary epileptogenic hemisphere from SEEG. Coordinates were recorded at 10 mm intervals along the center of each venous sub‐sinus, which runs parallel to the diameter of the vessel.

Electrode contact to sinus distance calculation

The SEEG implantation utilized microdeep intracerebral electrodes (DIXI Medical, Beaucanson, France, number of contacts 8–18, size 0.8 × 2 mm). Target and entry point coordinates of the individual electrode were collected from the same MRI sequence as venous sinuses. The contact position was estimated by interpolation methods between the target and entry point for each electrode. We only included intraparenchymal contacts.

Seizure analysis

SEEG signals were recorded with Profusion neXus 360™ software at a sampling rate of 2 kHz. A maximum of three seizures was analyzed per patient. Where possible, one of each seizure type was analyzed: focal‐impaired‐consciousness seizure (FIC), focal‐preserved‐consciousness seizure (FPC), and focal‐to‐bilateral‐tonic‐clonic seizures (FBTCS). 25

Each seizure was visually analyzed by an epileptologist (AN) and neuroscientist (TW). All intracranial contacts were examined in a bipolar montage. For each bipolar channel (e.g., A1–2), reviewers determined if an ictal rhythm involved that channel at some point in the seizure. An ictal rhythm was defined as a sustained rhythmic discharge that displays evolution in frequency (within that channel) and space (to nearby or connected brain regions). 26 , 27 We also measured the time taken for a bipolar channel to have an unequivocal ictal rhythm from the seizure onset. The seizure onset was defined as the first channel that displays an ictal rhythm. 27 The time component was classified into three different time points: (i) t < 5 s, (ii) t < 10 s, and (iii) any time during seizures (Figure 1B1).

Optimal location analysis

Step 1: Electrode level analysis to determine the optimal propagation distance

To estimate the “optimal propagation distance” distance, we analyzed seizure propagation along individual SEEG electrodes. A reference point (RP) was set at 3.5 mm from the inner table of the cranium to approximate the position of an intracranial sinus. The distance between each electrode contact and the RP was calculated in 5 mm intervals from 10 to 65 mm. Each electrode was treated as an independent data point.

At each distance, we calculated the proportion of seizures that propagated from that distance to the RP (e.g., modeling propagation from a cortical depth to venous sinus).

We then defined the “optimal propagation distance” for our study based on two criteria:

>80% of seizures propagated from a proposed “optimal propagation distance” to the RP.

>80% of seizures propagated from a proposed “optimal propagation distance” to the RP.

>90% of patients had at least one electrode contact within the “optimal propagation distance” to at least one sinus.

>90% of patients had at least one electrode contact within the “optimal propagation distance” to at least one sinus.

Step 2: To determine the optimal venous sinus location

In this study, we assumed that the majority of seizures detectable in gray matter within an “optimal propagation distance” from a sinus would likely propagate to the cortical structures adjacent to that sinus and be detected by an EEG array within the sinus (i.e., any seizure detected within the optimal propagation distance of a sinus will be detectable by a device at that sinus location).

An individual venous sinus segment was included in the analysis if there was at least one electrode contact located within the optimal propagation distance. If at least one bipolar channel within optimal propagation distance of the sinus segment could detect the ictal rhythm, this sinus location was classified as being able to “detect the seizure.” This process was repeated for all seizures and time segments.

Statistical analysis

We utilized Stata version 17 (StataCorp, TX, USA). The analysis was separated into two parts: (1) determining the impact of time, seizure type, and epilepsy type on seizure detection, and (2) comparing the percentage of seizures detectable at each sinus.

Data was divided into two levels: seizure level (FIC, FPC, and FBTCS) and epilepsy level (temporal (TLE) and extratemporal epilepsy (ETLE)).

To prevent data bias in which seizures are counted without electrodes present for that vessel, we calculated the percentage of seizures that each sinus location could detect by determining the number of seizures that each sinus location could detect, divided by the total number of potential seizures where there was at least one contact within the “optimal propagation distance” to a specific sinus location. In this study, we excluded sinuses that had electrode contacts within the optimal distance from less than three patients from the analysis in each seizure and epilepsy type.

We performed multilevel mixed‐effects linear regression (MLR) with random intercept by seizure or epilepsy type with Gaussian distribution to compare the percentage of seizures that were involved at each time epoch, seizure type, and epilepsy type. Following this, we performed pairwise comparisons with the Holm‐Bonferroni method from the outcome at any time from seizure onset.

RESULTS

Baseline information

Forty SEEG patients were included in the analysis (average age 37.56 ± 10.48 years) (see Table S1). The median number of seizures was 5.00 [IQR 2.00–8.50] per patient during admission. Fifty percent of patients were diagnosed with TLE. About 113 seizures were included in this study (FIC: 50.44% (N = 57), FPC: 30.09% (N = 34), and FBTCS: 19.47% (N = 22)). A total of 522 electrodes (6707 contacts) were included in this study.

Determining “optimal propagation distance”

About 522 electrodes were included in this initial electrode level analysis. We analyzed seizure propagation along each individual electrode. An average of 2.99 seizures per electrode was analyzed (1561 electrode level seizure propagation analyses = “electrode‐seizures”). Of these, 77.19% of “electrode‐seizures” (1205/1561) were detected at any propagation distance, with 989 seizures propagating to the RP.

At 5 mm from RP, 1048 electrode‐seizures were detected, 94.27% (988) of which propagated to the RP. The proportion of propagated seizure declined with increasing distance from RP, decreasing below our 80% threshold at 40 mm (Figure 2A).

We next analyzed the proportion of patients who had at least one electrode contact from the “optimal propagation distance” to at least one major sinus location and could therefore be included in the subsequent analyses. 25 mm was the minimum distance to allow >90% of patients to meet these criteria (Figure 2B).

To balance seizure propagation rate with comprehensive coverage of patients and sinuses, we selected 25 mm as the optimal propagation distance for identifying suitable venous sinus locations for endovascular seizure detection. At this distance, 86% of seizures propagated to the RP and 92.5% of patients had at least one electrode within 25 mm of any proposed sinuses.

The effect of time, seizure type, and epilepsy type

There were 1116 contacts that were located within 25 mm from at least one sinus (Figure 3). The mean minimum distances between contacts recording an ictal rhythm and a nearby sinus were 9.38 mm overall; 8.99 mm (SS‐P), 11.79 mm (SS‐M), 11.52 mm (SS‐A), 3.99 mm (straight), 8.84 mm (ITS), and 11.17 mm (CTS) (see Table S2).

Out of 113 seizures, 7 were excluded due to the absence of electrode contacts within the optimal propagation distance. Of the remaining 105 seizures, 92.45% (98/106) propagated to at least one venous sinus at any time points from seizure onset, with detection rates of 70.76% at <5 s, 80.19% at <10 s, and 92.45% at any time point from seizure onset. Eight seizures were not detectable from any sinus; consisting of 7 FPCs and 1 FIC (Table S3), most of which originated from mesial temporal or insular‐opercular EZs. The proportions of FPC, FIC, and FBTCS detected by at least one sinus location were 46.15%, 53.28%, and 78.95% at t < 5 s; 49.23%, 63.11%, and 81.58% at t < 10 s; and 55.38%, 88.52%, and 100% at any time from seizure onset, respectively.

The percentage of detectable seizures at each venous sinus segment's location was significantly influenced by seizure type, with FBTCS having the highest percentage of detectable seizures compared with other seizure types (FPC: coeff = 0.385, 95% CI [0.341, 0.429], p < 0.001, FIC: coeff = 0.198, 95% CI [0.154, 0.242], p < 0.001). In addition, FIC had a significantly higher percentage of being detectable than FPC (coeff = 0.187, 95% CI [0.143, 0.230], p < 0.001). There was also a significant difference observed between epilepsy types (coeff = 0.122, 95% CI [0.076, 0.168], p < 0.001), with TLE showing a higher rate of detectable seizures than ETLE.

At both seizure and epilepsy levels, the percentage of detectable seizures at a sinus location increased over the duration of the seizure. The percentage of detectable seizures at any time point during the seizure was significantly higher compared with t < 5 s (seizure level: coeff = 0.227, 95% CI [0.83, 0.270], p < 0.001; epilepsy level: coeff = 0.262, 95% CI [0.151, 0.374], p < 0.001) and t < 10 s after seizure onset (seizure level: coeff = 0.180, 95% CI [0.136, 0.224], p < 0.001; epilepsy level: coeff = 0.191, 95% CI [0.079, 0.302], p = 0.001). For example, in FIC, ITS detected 60.00% of seizures at t < 5 s, 73.33% at t < 10 s, and 95.56% at any time from seizure onset. In TLE, the ITS detected 64.58% of seizures within t < 5 s, 77.08% within t < 10 s, and 93.75% at any time.

Overall, both seizure and epilepsy type significantly influenced the percentage of seizures detectable from potential venous sinus locations at different time points. FBTCS showed fast propagation of seizures to cortical areas close to sinus locations, with most sinuses detecting 100% of seizures within t < 10 s from seizure onset. Moreover, seizures from TLE patients showed higher seizure detectability from any sinus compared with ETLE patients.

Focal‐aware‐seizures (FPC)

CTS was excluded due to only two patients having at least one electrode near the sinus. Among the remaining sinuses, SS‐M demonstrated the highest percentage of detectable seizure from the early phase (t < 5 s) of seizure onset (71.43%), followed by ITS, which detected 50% of seizures. Pairwise comparisons showed that SS‐M had a significantly higher percentage of seizures detected compared with other sinuses, although there was no difference between SS‐M and ITS. However, SS‐P exhibited a significantly lower seizure detection rate compared with most sinuses except the straight sinus.

Focal‐impaired‐awareness‐seizures (FIC)

For this seizure type, the straight sinus showed the highest percentage of detectable seizures from the early seizure phase (68.42%), increasing to 94.74% at any time point during seizure. ITS showed a slightly lower detection rate within the first 5 s from seizure onset (60%), but it increased to the highest rate at any time point during seizure (95.56%). However, there was no significant difference between these two sinuses. Additionally, CTS and SS‐M also showed a significantly lower percentage of detectable seizures compared with other sinuses at any time point from seizure onset.

Focal to bilateral tonic‐clonic seizures (FBTCS)

The highest percentage of detectable seizures within 5 s was at SS‐A (84.61%); however, this was very similar to ITS (83.33%) and straight sinus (83.33%). However, all sinuses detected 100% of seizures at any time point from seizure onset. Pairwise comparisons found no significant differences between sinuses at any time point.

Overall, at the seizure level, the optimal venous sinus varies depending on seizure type and timing. SS‐M, straight sinus, and SS‐A provided the highest seizure detection rate at times <5 s from seizure onset in FPC, FIC, and FBTCS, respectively. However, ITS exhibited high seizure detection rates for detecting all seizure types, with no statistically significant differences in pairwise comparison to SS‐M, straight sinus, and SS‐A at any time point.

Temporal epilepsy (TLE)

For TLE group, 53 seizures were included, consisting of 11 FPC, 37 FIC, and 5 FBTCS. Within this group, ITS demonstrates the highest percentage of detectable seizure at all‐time segments (64.58%: t < 5 s, 70.80%: t < 10 s, and 93.75%) at any time point from seizure onset. Additionally, ITS significantly outperformed most sinus locations in detecting seizures at any time point, except for the straight sinus, where there was no statistical difference. SS‐A exhibited a significantly lower seizure detection rate compared with most sinuses except SS‐M.

Extratemporal epilepsy (ETLE)

For ETLE group, there are 45 seizures from 20 patients included at this level, consisting of 13 FPC,18 FIC and 14 FBTCS. The type of ETLE is consisted of 10% insular, 32.5% Frontal, 5% parietal and 2.5% occipital. For ETLE, CTS was excluded due to only one patient having at least one electrode near the sinus. SS‐A was able to detect the highest percentage of seizure within 5 s from seizure onset (73.53%), increasing to 79.41% at any time from seizure onset. However, at any time point from seizure onset, ITS showed the highest seizure detection rate (82.35%), increasing from 47.06% in the early phase of seizure. Pairwise comparison demonstrates that SS‐P displayed a significantly inferior detection rate from SS‐A (p = 0.002) and SS‐M (p = 0.001). However, there is no significant difference among other sinuses.

Overall, at the epilepsy level, ITS exhibited high seizure detection rates for detecting all epilepsy types, with no statistically significant differences in pairwise comparison to straight sinus for TLE and SS‐A and SS‐M for ETLE at any time point.

DISCUSSION

This study utilizes SEEG data to determine theoretically optimal venous sinus location for an endovascular seizure detection device. Our findings indicate that both epilepsy and seizure type influence the optimal venous location of future endovascular electrode implantation.

Feasibility of venous sinuses as implantation target

Prior study with sub‐scalp EEG and endovascular electrodes in animal models has demonstrated that spikes and other neurophysiological signals can be recorded with amplitudes and waveforms comparable to subdural grids in the same subject. 20 , 21 , 29 However, no study has clearly defined the minimum effective distance between the brain region and the venous sinus, which is essential for designing future endovascular seizure detection devices.

In our study, the average minimum distance from the venous sinus to detectable seizure contacts was 9.38 mm, with the shortest being 3.99 mm, near the straight sinus (see Table S2). In the first stage of analysis, we evaluated the distance between the first seizure contacts and the reference point (RP) along the same trajectory. A 25 mm threshold, identified as the “optimal propagation distance,” represented a minimum distance that balanced high‐seizure detectability with broad patient coverage, determined by systematically evaluating distances from 5 to 65 mm along the same electrode trajectory from the RP. Detection rates at 25 mm were comparable to 5 mm (94.28% vs. 86.11%), supporting the potential of seizure propagation from the deep cortex to the area close to the sinus.

Existing data support the feasibility of implanting the superior sagittal sinus, which has been implemented as a location for brain‐computer interface studies. 16 , 20 , 21 , 30 However, there remains limited literature on selecting eligible venous sinuses for seizure detection devices. We focused on four main venous vessels (six segments) with a diameter >1 mm, consistent with other endovascular studies. We found that 92.45% of seizures were detectable from at least one venous segment at any time point, increasing to 100% for disabling seizures (FIC and FBTCS). While this does not confirm the ability to detect seizures from each venous sinus, it provides anatomical and physiological support for the development of a future endovascular seizure detection device.

Relationship between seizure and epilepsy type and optimal venous sinus location

Our results indicate that the optimal venous sinus location to “detect” a seizure is strongly influenced by seizure type, epilepsy type, and the time from seizure onset, reflecting the anatomical relationship between sinuses and common epileptogenic zones associated with different seizure semiologies. The ITS location had high yield in detecting seizures across all seizure and epilepsy types, though accurate lateralization of the EZ is required.

For FBTCS, we found no appreciable difference in the percentage of seizures detected across sinuses. FBTCS are characterized by rapid propagation to widespread brain areas from the originating EZ and are supported by studies incorporating functional imaging comparing signal patterns between individuals with and without FBTCS. 31 Given the seizure propagation pattern in FBTCS, it was not surprising that there is no difference in the optimal venous location to detect seizures, distinguishing it from FPC and FIC, where most seizures remain more focal. These FBTCS results can potentially be translated to patients with diffuse onset seizures or generalized epilepsies.

For FIC, a straight sinus would be an optimal venous sinus, offering early seizure detection and insensitivity to EZ lateralization, making it suitable for multifocal or bilateral epilepsy. However, there remains a question regarding the suitability of this sinus for implantation in patients who experience more than one seizure type.

Additionally, our findings highlight the importance of understanding the underlying epilepsy type, particularly in patients whose predominant seizure types are FPC or FIC. The primary epilepsy type of patients who had FPC was ETLE, and the main epilepsy type of patients in the FIC group was TLE. This relationship may explain the overlap in optimal venous sinus locations observed between FPC and ETLE, as well as between FIC and TLE.

At the epilepsy level, ITS displayed characteristics of being an optimal location for both epilepsy types. In TLE, ITS was a more promising optimal location, which is consistent with its proximity to EZ. Compared with other selected sinuses, the contralateral transverse sinus (CTS) was less suitable, emphasizing the importance of identifying the primary epileptogenic hemisphere. Based on our findings, the straight sinus could be a secondary option for TLE, reflecting the close location between the straight sinus and mesial‐lateral structures and its potential utility in cases where lateralization is unclear or in bitemporal lobe epilepsy. In this study, 14 patients underwent bilateral implantation, of whom only five were identified as having bitemporal epilepsy. We hypothesize that the seizure detection rate for CTS may have been influenced by this subgroup, particularly within the TLE group. This may partly explain the increased seizure detection rates observed in both the TLE and FIC groups.

For extratemporal epilepsy, the majority of patients had a fronto‐insular epileptogenic zone (32.5% frontal and 10% insular), reflecting the finding of sagittal sinus segments showing a high proportion of detectable seizures at the early phase. The ETLE group in our study is quite heterogeneous. Half of the epilepsy cases categorized as ETLE are in the insular or parietal area, which is closer to the temporal area and transverse sinus. Moreover, the limited number of cases in the posterior quadrant may restrict the generalizability of the results for this cohort. However, due to the limited number of patients in each subgroup, larger cohort analyses should be considered in future studies.

According to the result of this study, SS‐A and SS‐M showed the lowest potential to detect seizures in FIC and TLE, while SS‐P had the lowest percentage of detectable seizures for FPC and ETLE. These findings highlight the importance of individualizing the optimal location for endovascular electrodes, as epilepsy and seizure type influence the optimal location. SS‐A and SS‐M are located further from temporal epilepsy, which was the primary epileptogenic zone location for FIC seizures. In addition, SS‐P is also distantly located from the frontal or insula lobe, which were the main epileptogenic areas for ETLE.

Limitations and future research

These preliminary findings support the anatomical and neurophysiological feasibility of using venous sinuses for seizure detection, though no endovascular electrodes were implanted. Incorporating a time component into the data (e.g., t < 5 s, t < 10 s, anytime) supports future research on seizure detection (requiring seizure recording from any time point) and the development of responsive, closed‐loop stimulation using implanted endovascular devices.

Our cohort included surgical candidates with presumed focal epileptogenic zones, including a high proportion of complex focal cases (70% MRI‐negative, 55% offered surgery, 20% proceeding to neuromodulation), enhancing generalizability to patients who may benefit from detection or prediction devices.

The main limitation is the assumption that if the cortex in close proximity (within 25 mm) to a venous sinus can record ictal rhythms, then an EEG array within the venous sinus can detect seizures. Extraparenchymal contacts (e.g., dura mater, bone, scalp) were excluded, though their proximity to sinuses may enhance signals via volume conduction through volume conduction. Additionally, SEEG electrodes in this study were placed for surgical evaluation rather than optimized for sinus proximity. Eight seizures were undetectable by the sinuses, likely due to very focal onset (majority FPCs), deep sources (mesial temporal or insular‐opercular), or limited electrode coverage (Table S3).

Additionally, we used a linear space approach to estimate the rate of seizure propagation from a given distal distance to a venous sinus location. However, we acknowledge that seizure propagation is not linear and is influenced by structural and connectivity factors. Future studies should incorporate network‐based propagation models to improve the modeling of seizure detection from endovascular locations. The 25 mm threshold analysis was conducted independently, but we acknowledge the potential for analytic circularity. Future validation in larger cohorts may support the use of a closer distance (i.e., 5 mm) from the sinuses and is essential to improve generalizability.

Lastly, this study examined only six sinus segments. Further exploration of additional venous structures and subgroup analyses in extratemporal epilepsy is warranted to expand these findings. Potential risks, such as sinus thrombosis, which is potentially life‐threatening due to (a) smaller caliber and (b) because it drains the deep venous system, remain a system. 32 However, this is not the focus of this article, where future studies, either in vivo or in vitro, should be considered.

CONCLUSION

In a focal epilepsy cohort, over 90% of seizures propagate to SEEG contacts located within close proximity to at least one venous sinus at any time point, supporting the feasibility of endovascular seizure detection. Determining the optimal sinus depends on both seizure and epilepsy type. Ipsilateral transverse sinus (ITS) and straight sinus were optimal sinuses for TLE and FIC, while the sagittal sinus location would be a promising target for ETLE and FPC. Overall, ITS consistently demonstrated high seizure detectability across seizure and epilepsy types, though it requires prior identification of the primarily epileptogenic hemisphere. Future research is warranted to further explore the safety and effectiveness of endovascular seizure detection devices.
