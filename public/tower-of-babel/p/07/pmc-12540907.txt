Sustainability evaluation of the steel industry in belt and road countries using an ESG-MI and obstacle analysis framework
Authors: Binyu Ren, Qunhao Wang, Hongling Ge, Jianming Xu, Xiaomin Hao, Haiping Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a fundamental raw material industry in the industrial sector, the steel industry is the largest emitter of carbon among the 31 manufacturing sub-sectors. Assessing the sustainability level of the steel industry is thus of critical importance for advancing carbon reduction efforts. In 2024, crude steel production in Belt and Road Initiative countries reached 1.43 billion tons, accounting for 75.8% of global output. However, significant disparities exist in the development of steel industries across these countries, necessitating the construction of a robust model to evaluate their relative sustainability. This study proposes an ESG-MI evaluation model that integrates environmental, social, governance, resource, and industrial dimensions, and applies it to panel data from 64 BRI countries between 2000 and 2023 to assess the sustainability level of their steel industries. Furthermore, an obstacle degree model is employed to identify key hindering factors. The study yields the following key findings: (1) From 2000 to 2023, the sustainability level of the steel industry along the BRI showed a steady upward trend, with an average annual growth rate of 1.04%. (2) Industrial development and governance are the main drivers of sustainable development in the steel sector, while environmental performance remains a major weakness. Additionally, the resource supply chains in BRI countries are fragile and vulnerable to external shocks. (3) The sustainable development of the steel industry along the BRI follows a geographical transmission pattern of “Europe → East Asia → Middle East → Southeast Asia → South Asia.” (4) Based on obstacle degree analysis, industrial value added (X20), crude steel output per capita (X11), and the proportion of ores and metals in total exports (X12) are identified as the top three obstacles to sustainable development in the steel industry, with significantly greater hindering effects than other indicators.

Introduction

Introduction

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) was first proposed and advocated by the United Nations, requiring micro-level entities to incorporate environment, social responsibility, and corporate governance into economic decision-making, thereby establishing a market mechanism connecting “investors-enterprises-sustainable development”, and playing an increasingly important role in global sustainable development. Data show that in 2020, the ESG investment scale of major developed countries increased to 35.3 trillion US dollars, accounting for 35.9% of their total investment scale1,2. In recent years, with climate change, the international community has paid more and more attention to the ESG system. As the market matures, it is expected that by 2030, the scale of ESG investment may reach 40 trillion US dollars3,4. At present, ESG has begun to extend from the enterprise field to the industrial field, but there is still no unified understanding of ESG evaluation at the national industrial level.

As a basic raw material industry in the industrial field, the steel industry consumes 7% of global energy and emits 9% of global carbon dioxide, making it the industry with the highest carbon emissions among the 31 manufacturing sectors5. The “Belt and Road” region is an important area for global steel production and consumption. According to the World Steel Association, in 2024, the crude steel output of countries along the “Belt and Road” was 1.43 billion tons, accounting for 75.8% of the global total6. In 2023, the total crude steel consumption of countries along the “Belt and Road” reached 1.33 billion tons, accounting for 75.4% of the world6. Five of the world’s top ten steel-producing countries are located along the “Belt and Road”, with a total capacity of 1.29 billion tons, accounting for 68.7% of the global total6.

According to the World Bank7, in 2023, the total economic volume of countries along the “Belt and Road” reached 36.5 trillion US dollars, with an average annual growth rate of 4.5% since 2013, 1.6 times that of non-“Belt and Road” countries. The “Belt and Road” region holds 61.5% of the world’s population and is one of the most economically dynamic regions in the world. In order to achieve ESG goals, the steel industry along the “Belt and Road” is actively promoting green and low-carbon transformation. For example, JSW Steel has actively responded to economic development and social demands by formulating a corporate climate action plan (Table 1)3, seeking to reduce carbon dioxide emission intensity by 42% by 2030 through increasing the use of renewable energy and scrap steel, improving energy efficiency, and optimizing production processes; achieving 100% solid waste recycling; and reducing freshwater consumption by more than 39%.

It can be observed that the “Belt and Road” region places significant emphasis on carbon reduction and the green sustainable development of the steel industry. Currently, numerous steel enterprises are advancing carbon reduction and sustainable development governance through the ESG framework. Some scholars, from an industrial perspective, have studied the impact of the steel industry on carbon emission reduction and the promotion of social development. However, the green transformation actions in the steel industry are primarily driven by corporate initiatives, and there is a lack of systematic evaluation of the sustainable development levels of the steel industry in the countries along the Belt and Road from an industrial perspective. Moreover, there is an absence of unified evaluation standards, making it impossible to compare and measure the differences in sustainable development of the steel industry across the 64 Belt and Road countries.

Therefore, to address these shortcomings, this study attempts to examine the current status, disparities, and influencing factors of the sustainable development of the steel industry in the 64 countries along the Belt and Road from an ESG perspective. The study mainly includes the following aspects: First, it constructs a sustainable development evaluation model for the steel industry by leveraging the ESG framework and incorporating the characteristics of the steel industry; second, it systematically evaluates the temporal and spatial characteristics of the sustainable development of the steel industry in the 64 Belt and Road countries; third, it quantitatively identifies the factors influencing the development of the steel industry along the Belt and Road.

Literature review

The academic research on ESG is extensive, and overall, the related literature primarily focuses on the following aspects. First, the relationship between ESG performance and corporate financial returns. Egorova, (A) A. (2022)8 studied the impact of ESG factors on the performance of information technology (IT) companies. The results showed that IT companies are not currently leaders in ESG ratings, but advancing ESG practices will improve their position and have a positive impact on performance. Kalia, D. (2023)9 conducted an empirical analysis of data from 468 healthcare companies in the fiscal year 2020. The results indicated that ESG activities positively affect the performance of healthcare companies in developed economies, while in developing economies, this relationship may be negative or insignificant. Additionally, studies on the relationship between ESG and corporate investment have been conducted by Kotsantonis, S. (2019)10, Ionescu, G. H. (2019)11, Cornell, (B) (2021)12, Zumente, I. (2021)13, and Rau, P. R. (2024)14, indicating that the application of ESG data in investment decisions is continuously growing, with companies having higher ESG ratings benefiting from lower capital costs and being able to attract more investment. Henisz, W. (2019)15 explored the impact of ESG ratings on asset al.location, finding that ESG strategies have become an important reference for institutional investors. Furthermore, many scholars focus on how to enhance corporate ESG. Fang, M. (2023)16 collected annual reports and financial data from 1,130 listed companies in China’s A-share market between 2012 and 2020. The baseline regression analysis indicated that digitalization significantly improved the ESG scores of companies. Meanwhile, Albitar, K. (2020)17, Huang, D. Z. (2021)18, Asif, M. (2023)19, David, L. (2024)20, and other scholars studied ESG governance mechanisms, showing that differences in national systems, cultures, and regulations can significantly influence corporate ESG practices.

Regarding the green low-carbon development of the steel industry, this has become a research hotspot in the academic community, mainly summarized in the following aspects. First, reducing carbon emissions. Bhaskar, A. (2020)21, Xu, R. (2023)22, and Hebeda, O. (2023)23 found that if the global steel industry fully deploys low-carbon technologies such as electric arc furnaces + hydrogen reduction + CCUS, it can reduce carbon emissions by 70%, with technological advancements contributing over 60% of the reduction. Second, recycling scrap steel. Johnson, J. (2008)24 pointed out that increasing secondary recycling of scrap steel can reduce energy consumption by 33% and carbon dioxide emissions by 32%. Broadbent, (C) (2016)25 found that recycling 1 kg of scrap steel saves 1.5 kg of carbon dioxide equivalent emissions and 1.4 kg of iron ore. Guo, J. (2018)26 encouraged the resource utilization of by-products such as steel slag and dust, exploring the use of steel slag in cement production, roadbed construction, etc. Third, steel industry environmental regulation. Wu, R. (2022)27 and other scholars studied the impact of environmental regulation on the green development of the steel industry, showing that there is a U-shaped relationship between environmental regulation and energy-environmental performance. Lower environmental regulation intensity inhibits the improvement of energy-environmental performance, but as regulation intensity increases, it helps improve performance. Zhu, X. (2019)28 calculated the effects of current and proposed environmental regulations in China’s steel industry from 2006 to 2013, suggesting that the implementation of environmental regulations will improve the material efficiency of steel use in the long term. Fourth, international cooperation. Hu, R. (2015)29 and Zhang, X. (2021)30 pointed out that the steel industry should align with industrial evolution trends and actively promote regional steel industry chain restructuring to balance and reduce carbon emission intensity in the steel industry.

Currently, there is limited systematic research on the ESG framework for the steel industry. Existing studies mainly extend the previous research on corporate social responsibility (CSR) and sustainable development. Some scholars, such as Kaur, N. (2021)31, argue that corporate social responsibility helps enhance corporate revenue. Haider, S. (2020)32 and Sharma, M. (2023)33 contend that steel companies, through the practice of CSR, can reduce carbon emissions. Acharya, J. (2018)34 developed an analytical framework that defines transformation strategies for different levels of communities from the perspectives of production, organization, governance, institutions, and relational culture, and outlines how CSR policies can guide companies to achieve sustainable community development. Misani, N. (2017)35 examined the relationship between CSR and financial performance from the environmental, social, and governance dimensions, with inconsistent results.

In summary, current research applying ESG frameworks to the industrial level remains limited, with a notable lack of studies specifically focused on ESG systems in the steel industry. As the global economy and society undergo a transition toward low-carbon development, future research on industry-level ESG is expected to increase, particularly in the following areas: (1) grounding in the concept of sustainable development, theoretical analysis of the connotation, framework, and influencing factors of industry-level ESG; (2) expanding the practical application of ESG by developing evaluation models and indicator systems tailored to the characteristics of different industries; (3) conducting systematic evaluations of sustainable development within specific industries and comparing differences across countries; and (4) comprehensively assessing the factors influencing industrial sustainability among different nations to optimize industrial layout and enhance sustainable development.

As the foundation of the modern economy, the steel industry not only needs to meet its own ESG development needs but also has a broad impact on the development of other industries and companies. Therefore, constructing and studying the ESG system for the steel industry has profound theoretical and practical significance. In view of this, this paper systematically evaluates the sustainable development level of the steel industry in countries along the Belt and Road. The logic of this paper is as follows: In the second section, the logical connotations of ESG are systematically outlined, and an ESG evaluation indicator system for the steel industry is constructed; In the third section, the specific calculation process for the steel ESG evaluation indicator system is introduced; In the fourth section, the steel ESG index for 64 countries along the Belt and Road is calculated, and spatial differences between the indices of different countries are compared, with a detailed analysis of the influencing factors of the system; Finally, recommendations are proposed to provide decision-making support for the green and low-carbon development of the steel industry in the Belt and Road countries.

Innovation and academic contribution

The marginal contributions and innovations of this paper are as follows:

(1) For the first time, the ESG framework is applied to the study of industrial sustainable development, analyzing the connotations and logic of sustainable development in the steel industry. The paper comprehensively considers five core elements—resources, environment, industry, resources, and governance—and constructs the ESG-MI model (where MI stands for Mineral Resources and Industries) for sustainable development in the steel industry.

(2) The paper constructs an ESG-MI evaluation indicator system for the steel industry and, for the first time, systematically evaluates the sustainable development level of the steel industry in 64 countries along the Belt and Road, providing a comprehensive understanding of the spatial correlations of steel industry sustainability in these countries.

(3) The paper identifies the key factors influencing the sustainable development of the steel industry along the Belt and Road, further elaborating on their operational mechanisms. This provides valuable insights for the green and low-carbon transformation of the steel industry in these countries and offers decision-making references for regional cooperation.

Model construction

Research approach

ESG is a framework that evaluates the credit quality, social responsibility performance, risk management capabilities, and sustainability levels of companies across various industries worldwide (Fig. 1). ESG is a crucial driver for achieving corporate sustainability in today’s world and has become an international universal language on par with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), with global applicability. This concept profoundly influences the interaction between businesses and their external environment and indirectly promotes the deep adjustment of production relations. The ESG concept aligns closely with China’s new development philosophy and “dual carbon” goals, in line with the country’s strategies for building a beautiful China, achieving high-quality development, and upgrading its energy structure. All these strategies are committed to achieving sustainable economic, social, and environmental development.

Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the logical relationship between environment, society, and governance.In terms of overall ESG ratings, there are currently various ESG evaluation models both domestically and internationally. A representative one is the Runling Global ESG Sustainable Development Rating System, which includes four aspects: E (Economy), S (Society), N (Nature), and G (Governance). Overall, these models have not deeply considered the local steel industry’s resource base, industrial status, and policy environment, which results in a lack of comprehensiveness in their evaluations. This paper, based on the ESG analytical framework, combines the resource base and industrial characteristics to construct a steel sustainable development evaluation model, providing a comprehensive assessment of the production environment of countries along the Belt and Road.

Model construction

The steel industry, as a complex system that involves multiple elements such as environment, economy, and society, requires the construction of a comprehensive, relevant, and scientific indicator system for its sustainable development evaluation. In 1997, the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) established the Global Reporting Initiative (GRI), formally defining sustainable development as consisting of three components: E (Environment), S (Social), and G (Governance). Subsequently, with the promotion of the United Nations Principles for Responsible Investment (UN-PRI), ESG rapidly developed on a global scale36. ESG is a thinking model used to describe complex systems, detailing the evolutionary feedback mechanisms between industrial development and influencing factors14,37. Currently, ESG has gained widespread attention from businesses, governments, and the public, and is extensively applied in corporate governance38. ESG is used for comprehensive analysis of environmental issues and their relationship with social development, which provides a framework for the sustainable development evaluation of the steel industry.

The steel industry is a capital-intensive economic activity with characteristics such as long investment cycles, strong resource dependence, and significant environmental impacts, all of which are influenced by resource endowment, policy planning, economic development, and environmental protection. Therefore, based on the ESG thinking model, this study incorporates Mineral Resources (M) and Industries (I) into the steel industry’s sustainable development evaluation model, constructing the Belt and Road ESG-MI Model for the sustainable development evaluation of the steel industry in countries along the Belt and Road (Fig. 2). This model provides a systematic evaluation of the sustainable development level of the steel industry in these countries.

Research methodology

Global entropy method

The entropy method, as an objective weighting approach, makes full use of the information provided by the evaluation indicator data to avoid subjective judgment, ensuring that the evaluation results are relatively objective. In this paper, the global entropy method is applied to the dynamic evaluation of the sustainable development level of the steel industry along the Belt and Road. Compared to the traditional entropy method that establishes a two-dimensional data table based on “indicators-time” or “indicators-region,” the global entropy method builds a three-dimensional data table of “indicators-region-time,” allowing for both horizontal regional comparison and vertical temporal comparison of the evaluation indicators, thus possessing dynamic analysis attributes. Exploratory spatial data analysis (ESDA)

Exploratory Spatial Data Analysis (ESDA) is a set of methods used to describe and visualize the spatial distribution of data. In this study, global spatial autocorrelation and local spatial autocorrelation are applied to analyze the spatial association and clustering characteristics of the sustainable development levels of the steel industry along the Belt and Road. Data sources

In this study, the “Belt and Road” refers specifically to the “Silk Road Economic Belt” and does not include the “21st Century Maritime Silk Road.” Accordingly, the sample covers only the 65 countries initially involved in the proposal. However, due to data unavailability, Palestine is excluded from the analysis. Data on steel production and consumption are sourced from the World Steel Association. Economic, social, and demographic statistics are obtained from the World Bank. Indicators such as the prevalence of clean energy consumption, sustainable economic growth, and social equity are drawn from the SDI Indicators related to water stress, air pollution, and other environmental metrics are retrieved from the Environmental Performance Index (EPI). Trade-related data are obtained from the United Nations Commodity Trade Statistics Database (UN Comtrade). In the Economic Freedom Index, data is missing for several countries including Maldives, Bhutan, Afghanistan, Syria, Iraq, Montenegro, and Serbia. For cases where the data gap is less than or equal to two years, linear interpolation is applied. For longer periods of missing data (exceeding three years) and where no stable trend is evident, a trend-fitting approach based on similar countries is employed. Regional similarity is determined based on a combination of factors, including geographical proximity, economic system, and historical patterns in the fluctuation of freedom scores.

To evaluate the accuracy of the interpolation, we calculate the Mean Squared Error (MSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Bias. The analysis shows that interpolation errors for most countries are relatively small, with MSE values mostly ranging from 1 to 2.5 and average Bias values close to zero, indicating the overall robustness of the interpolation method.

Spatiotemporal evolution of sustainable development

Macroscopic analysis

Based on the global entropy method, the SDI of the steel industry in 64 countries along the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) from 2000 to 2023 was calculated, as shown in Fig. 3. In 2023, the SDI for the steel industry was 14.097, reflecting a 26.8% increase compared to 2000. The growth was primarily driven by improvements in the governance and industrial sub-indicators. Specifically, the governance sub-index increased by 1.560, a growth rate of 65.1%, the highest among the five indicators. The industrial index grew by 0.976, an increase of 58.5%, ranking second in terms of growth. The resource and social indices increased by 0.375 and 0.365, respectively, reflecting growth rates of 25.3% and 13.3% compared to 2000. In 2023, the environmental index was 2.530, showing a decline of 10.5% from 2000, making it the only sub-index with a decrease among the five dimensions.

The resource dimension index grew by 25.3% from 2000 to 2023, a higher growth rate than both the social and environmental dimensions. During the 2009 financial crisis, the resource index experienced a sharp drop of 16.0%, significantly more than the other four dimensions, highlighting the vulnerability of the resource supply chain along the BRI, which is highly susceptible to external impacts. In 2023, the governance dimension index reached 3.956, having increased by 65.1% since 2000, ranking first among all five dimensions in terms of growth.

In summary, from 2000 to 2023, the industrial (I) and governance (G) dimensions have been the main drivers of sustainable development in the steel industry across the BRI. The environmental (E) dimension has become the primary constraint, while the resource (M) dimension has been highly susceptible to external shocks. The social (S) dimension has shown steady progress.

National-level analysis

The Sustainable Development Index (SDI) of the steel industry in each country from 2000 to 2023 is divided into five levels, from low to high, namely I, II, III, IV, and V, corresponding to low, relatively low, medium, relatively high, and high levels, respectively. Visualization analysis for the years 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020, and 2023 was performed using ArcGIS 17.0 software. As shown in Fig. 4, the overall trend indicates that the level of sustainable development of the steel industry along the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) has generally shown an upward trajectory. Specifically, the number of countries in the low-level zone decreased directly from 21 in 2000 to 6 in 2023. The number of countries in the medium-level zone remained at 12 from 2000 to 2023, though there were significant changes, with the countries concentrated in South Asia, Southeast Asia, Central Asia, and the Caucasus region by 2023. During the study period, the number of cities in the high-level zone increased by 14. From the dynamic ranking, some countries in the Middle East and Southeast Asia, such as Oman, Bahrain, UAE, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Thailand, Vietnam, and Myanmar, have relatively advanced levels of sustainable development in the steel industry. In terms of the evolution trajectory, the countries in the high-level zone have gradually spread from Europe to economically underdeveloped regions, while the number of countries in the lower levels has gradually decreased, existing only in regions like Turkmenistan, Afghanistan, and Syria.

Regional-level analysis

Based on geographical location, the 64 countries along the Belt and Road are classified into two primary regions: China and non-China regions. The non-China region is further subdivided into Northern Asia, Southeast Asia, South Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and Africa. Using the SDI scores of the constituent countries within each subregion, the steel industry’s sustainable development level is calculated at the regional level. The results are shown in Fig. 5.

Overall, in 2023, China’s steel industry SDI reached 0.520, ranking first among all regions along the Belt and Road, and was 2.4 times higher than the average for non-China regions. Northern Asia ranked second with an index of 0.261, followed by Europe (0.239), the Middle East (0.225), Southeast Asia (0.207), and Africa (0.203), while Central Asia ranked the lowest.

In terms of growth, the sustainable development levels of the steel industry across all Belt and Road regions showed an upward trend from 2000 to 2023. China’s index increased by 0.316 during this period, marking a 154.3% growth—the highest among all regions. This was followed by the Middle East (34.2%), Europe (24.6%), Southeast Asia (21.1%), South Asia (19.2%), and Africa (16.2%). Northern Asia (10.7%) and Central Asia (10.5%) experienced relatively smaller increases.

The high-performance group mainly includes China, Bahrain, and the Slovak Republic, while the low-performance group comprises Yemen, Afghanistan, and Turkmenistan.

China, as the initiator of the Belt and Road Initiative, possesses a triple advantage—policy, investment, and technology—in the green transformation of its steel industry. As early as 2009, China launched the Steel Industry Adjustment and Revitalization Plan, explicitly targeting the elimination of outdated capacity, improvement of resource efficiency, and promotion of cleaner production. These policies have since evolved. For instance, the Ultra-Low Emissions Retrofit Plan for the Steel Industry (2019) required steel enterprises in key regions to complete ultra-low emission retrofits by the end of 2020. Further, the 14th Five-Year Plan for the Development of the Steel Industry (2022) emphasized carbon peaking and neutrality goals, and advocated the adoption of cutting-edge technologies such as hydrogen metallurgy and electric arc furnace short-process steelmaking. China is one of the earliest and largest investors in hydrogen metallurgy globally, and places strong emphasis on the intelligent upgrading of energy-saving equipment, promoting real-time monitoring and feedback regulation of energy consumption and pollutant emissions. Driven by this combination of policies and technologies, China’s SDI rose from 0.2046 in 2000 to 0.5203 in 2023, with its global ranking improving from 16th to 1st.

In contrast, low-performance countries such as Yemen and Afghanistan face long-standing institutional, resource, and technological challenges in developing their steel industries. On the institutional front, political instability has resulted in a lack of investment in basic industry, and the absence of green policies tailored to the steel sector. Relevant regulatory frameworks and policy documents are virtually nonexistent. For example, although Yemen’s Ministry of Industry proposed the “Industrial Modernization Support Project” in 2014, it was halted due to the outbreak of civil war; to date, the country still lacks industry standards on energy conservation or emission control. Technologically, most steel enterprises in these countries operate with manual processes and small-scale scrap recycling, heavily relying on outdated imported equipment. In Afghanistan, for instance, major steel firms are limited to basic scrap melting and rolling, lacking capabilities in raw material preprocessing, exhaust recovery, and energy efficiency management. As a result, they suffer from high energy consumption per unit of output and heavy pollution, leading to extremely low green performance. These issues are reflected in the SDI: between 2000 and 2023, Afghanistan’s SDI rose marginally from 0.0674 to 0.0841—an increase of less than 0.017—placing it at the bottom among all sample countries.

Robustness analysis

To verify the reliability of the comprehensive evaluation results obtained by the entropy weight method, this study conducts a comparative test using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and the equal-weight method.

The Spearman correlation coefficient between the entropy weight results and the first principal component (PC1) is -0.860, which is statistically significant at the 1% level. This negative correlation is mainly due to economic indicators such as industrial value added (X20) having higher weights in the entropy method but weaker contributions to PC1 (Table 5). In contrast, environmental indicators such as water stress (X7) have strong loadings in PC1 but lower weights in the entropy method. This pattern aligns with this study’s emphasis on the economic sustainability of the steel industry in Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) countries. Given that the absolute value of the correlation coefficient exceeds 0.8, the sample rankings produced by the two methods are considered highly consistent.

Further analysis of the third and fourth principal components shows that the entropy method results are only weakly correlated with PC3 (ρ = -0.169, p < 0.001) and PC4 (ρ = 0.209, p < 0.001), which is consistent with their relatively low cumulative explained variance (less than 15%). This supports the conclusion that the entropy-based results reflect the dominant patterns in the data.

In addition, the Spearman correlation coefficient between the entropy method and the equal-weight method reaches 0.914, also significant at the 1% level, indicating a high degree of agreement between the two evaluation methods and further validating the rationality of the entropy weight method.

Spatial correlation characteristics of sustainable development

Based on the spatial weight matrix, spatial autocorrelation of the SDI for the steel industry in 64 Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) countries from 2000 to 2023 was tested. The results are shown in Table 9. An examination of Moran’s I statistic reveals that between 2000 and 2019, the p-values of Moran’s I were consistently below 10%, indicating strong spatial clustering of steel industry SDI levels within the BRI region during this period. Moran’s I remained positive throughout 2000–2019, with a value of 0.092 in 2019, representing a decline of 0.158 compared to 2000—a reduction of 63.2%. This suggests that the spatial agglomeration effect of SDI within the BRI has gradually weakened over time. From 2020 to 2023, the p-values exceeded 10%, indicating that spatial clustering of SDI was no longer statistically significant across BRI countries. A closer look reveals that during 2000–2015, Moran’s I generally ranged between 0.14 and 0.29, with statistically significant positive Z-values and p-values below 0.05, suggesting clear positive spatial autocorrelation and a pattern of “high–high” and “low–low” clustering. However, beginning in 2016, Moran’s I steadily declined and even turned negative by 2023, signaling a diminishing spatial correlation in the sustainable development levels of national steel industries. These findings suggest increasing differentiation among BRI countries in terms of development, with the traditional advantage of geographic proximity exerting a diminishing influence on industrial sustainability. The driving forces behind sustainable development are increasingly shaped by domestic governance capacities, green transition policies, and transnational cooperation.

Conclusions and future directions

Conclusion

This study employs the entropy method to construct a SDI for the steel industry across countries along the BRI. Utilizing the entropy weight method and spatial autocorrelation analysis, the research examines the spatiotemporal evolution of sustainable development in the steel sector, and further applies the obstacle degree model to identify key factors influencing regional disparities. The main findings are as follows:

(1) From 2000 to 2023, the overall SDI of the steel industry along the BRI exhibited a fluctuating upward trend, rising from 11.118 to 14.097 with an average annual growth rate of 1.04%. Among the five dimensions of sustainable development—Industrial (I), Governance (G), Environmental (E), Resource (M), and Social (S)—the Industrial and Governance indices have increased by more than 50% since 2000, serving as the primary drivers of sustainable development. In contrast, the Environmental index is the only one that declined, representing a key bottleneck. The Resource index reveals a fragile resource supply chain highly susceptible to external shocks. Meanwhile, moderate progress has been made in the Social dimension, though the transition to clean energy remains lagging.

(2) The SDI of the BRI steel industry is undergoing dynamic changes. The number of countries classified as low-level declined from 21 in 2000 to 6 in 2023, while the number of high-level countries increased from 6 to 20. Since 2000, the spatial pattern of sustainable development in the steel industry has gradually shifted along the trajectory of “Europe → East Asia → Middle East → Southeast Asia → South Asia.” Among the 64 BRI countries analyzed, 59 have shown improvements in their SDI. Notably, eight countries have experienced an increase exceeding 50%, including China (154.3%), Bahrain (131.5%), Oman (90.3%), Montenegro (72.6%), the United Arab Emirates (69.5%), Saudi Arabia (54.1%), Serbia (53.6%), and Iran (51.5%).

(3) From 2000 to 2019, the Moran’s I values of the SDI for the steel industry along the BRI countries were statistically significant at the 10% level (P < 0.10). During this period, the Moran’s I index decreased by 63.2%, indicating a gradual weakening of the spatial agglomeration effect in the sustainable development of the steel industry. Since 2016, the Moran’s I value has continued to decline, and by 2023, it even turned negative, suggesting that the spatial correlation has tended toward randomness.

(4) From 2000 to 2023, the constraint factors affecting the sustainable development of the steel industry in BRI countries exhibited similar patterns, decreasing in severity in the order of “Industry → Resources → Environment → Society → Governance”. In 2023, the industrial constraint index reached 22.892, followed by the resource constraint at 21.432, and the environmental constraint at 13.907. The social and governance constraint indices were relatively low at 1.750 and 4.019, respectively. Together, the industrial and resource constraints accounted for 69.3% of the total, indicating that the pace of technological upgrading in the steel industry lags behind the demand for industrial expansion. As a result, the sustainable development of the steel industry across the BRI is subject to dual constraints stemming from both resource limitations and technological insufficiencies.

(5) In terms of barrier degree values, industrial added value (X20), per capita crude steel output (X11), and the proportion of ore and metal exports (X12) represent the most significant constraints to the sustainable development of the steel industry along the Belt and Road, far exceeding other indicators. The structure of barriers to sustainable development in the steel industry of Belt and Road countries is gradually shifting from a pattern of “uneven development and weak foundations” to a new stage characterized by “ecological pressure and governance challenges.”

Recommendations

Given that scrap steel recycling and hydrogen metallurgy technologies are recognized as key pathways for the green transformation of the steel industry, it is recommended that countries along the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) strengthen cooperation in the following aspects:

(1) Establish a regional cooperation mechanism for scrap steel recycling and trade to ensure efficient circulation of recycled resources and mutual recognition of standards;

(2) Promote interoperability and mutual recognition of green technology standards, and encourage the establishment of a “hydrogen metallurgy pilot country cooperation framework”;

(3) Encourage enterprises to disclose their Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) performance and integrate it into government procurement, financing, carbon trading, and other incentive systems, thereby enhancing the green governance capabilities across the entire industry.

Future directions

Although some scholars have assessed industrial sustainability using the ESG framework, most existing studies fail to adequately account for the impact of resource endowments and are unable to quantify the dynamic differences in sustainable development of the steel industry across countries. To address this gap, this study extends the traditional ESG model by incorporating Resource (M) and Industrial (I) dimensions, thereby constructing an “ESG + MI” framework. Drawing on authoritative international databases such as the World Bank and the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD), panel data for 25 indicators from 64 Belt and Road countries over the period 2000–2023 were compiled. Based on this data, the entropy weighting method was employed to calculate the SDI of the steel industry for each country, allowing for cross-national comparisons of sustainable development levels within the BRI region. However, due to data availability constraints, this study does not incorporate indicators such as secondary scrap recycling or digital transformation. Future research may consider integrating indicators related to resource circularity and transnational policy coordination to further enrich the “ESG + MI” model.
