Risk of cancer induced by human papillomavirus in patients with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases: a nationwide matched cohort study
Authors: Clothilde Gros, Tiphaine Goulenok, Jean-François Timsit, Karim Sacre, Arthur Mageau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWomen with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs) are at an increased risk of human papillomavirus (HPV)-related cervical cancer. Our study aimed to assess the risk of HPV-induced cervical, vaginal, vulvar, anal and penile cancer in patients with IMIDs.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide matched cohort study comparing patients with IMIDs with a control group using the French nationwide hospital medical information database between 2016 and 2023. The following IMIDs were considered: systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren’s syndrome, systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue disease and rheumatoid arthritis. The primary outcome was the occurrence of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia.ResultsA total of 122 316 patients with IMIDs and 316 047 matched controls were analysed over an 8-year study period, with a mean follow-up of 4.11 (2.47) years. IMIDs were associated with an increased risk of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia (age-adjusted HR 1.99 (95% CI 1.80 to 2.20)) in all patients with IMIDs compared with matched controls. An increased risk was observed in patients with IMIDs for all types of HPV-induced cancer except penile cancer. In the multivariable competing risk analysis, the risk factors for HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia in the IMIDs population were female (HR: 5.88 (95% CI 4.01 to 8.61)), SLE (HR: 2.08 (95% CI 1.70 to 2.55)), age under 65 years (HR: 2.09 (95% CI 1.70 to 2.57)) and chronic kidney disease (HR: 1.44 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.77)).ConclusionPatients with IMIDs are almost two times as likely to develop HPV-associated cancer or severe dysplasia, particularly at cervical, vulvar, vaginal and anal sites, compared with matched controls.Keywords: Autoimmune Diseases, Epidemiology, Prevalence

Introduction

Nearly 80% of sexually active men and women are infected with human papillomavirus (HPV) during their lifetime.1 At least 14 HPVs are defined as high-risk for carcinogenesis (HR-HPV), and HPV 16 and 18 are implicated in 70% of cervical cancers.2 While 80% of HPV-induced cancers are cervical cancers, HPV infection can also lead to cancers of the vulva, vagina, anus, penis and oropharynx.1

In patients with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs), an inadequate protective immune response, mainly caused by immunosuppressive therapies, can impair the natural clearance of HPV, resulting in chronic infection that in turn increases the risk of dysplasia and cancer.3 4 Previous studies have shown that women with IMIDs, particularly systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), are at increased risk of HPV-induced cervical cancer.4,9 However, no nationwide cohort study has evaluated the risk of multiple HPV-related cancers, such as vaginal or anal cancers, in patients with IMIDs. We aimed to assess the frequency of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia of the cervix, vagina, vulva, anus and penis, and the associated risk factors, in patients with IMIDs.

Materials and methods

Data source

We used data from Programme de Médicalisation des Systèmes d'Information (PMSI), a French nationwide hospital medical information database, collected between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023. PMSI is an inpatient database that provides information on patients’ diagnoses and medical conditions on their discharge from French healthcare facilities. Data for each structure that has been standardised and made anonymous are compiled at the national level. Administrative data include information such as age, sex, the year and duration of the stay and the location of the hospital. Hospital deaths are also reported. Medical data include diagnoses and procedures. All diagnoses are coded according to the 10th International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). Procedures are coded according to the Classification Commune des Actes Médicaux (CCAM, French common classification of medical procedures). The reliability of PMSI has been assessed in the past.10,13

Study population

We conducted a matched cohort study comparing patients with IMIDs (exposed) with controls (unexposed). To select our IMIDs population, we first extracted from PMSI all records of patients for whom at least one of the following ICD-10 diagnoses was reported during the study period: SLE (ICD-10 code M32), Sjögren’s syndrome (SS; ICD-10 M35.0), systemic sclerosis (SSc; ICD-10 M34.X), mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD; ICD-10 M35.1) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA; ICD-10 M05). Our study focused on this predefined group of connective tissue diseases for which previous studies had specifically suggested increased susceptibility to HPV infection and HPV-related neoplasia.9 To select our control population, we extracted all records of patients for whom cholecystectomy was reported during the same period. There were three reasons for selecting patients undergoing cholecystectomy as the control group: the need to select hospitalised patients; the known association between female sex and the procedure (as our analysis focused on IMIDs which are much more prevalent in women); and the fact that patients undergoing this procedure are not known to be at an increased risk of HPV-induced cancers compared with the general population. All diagnoses were identified on the first day of the stay, when the relevant ICD-10 or CCAM codes were recorded for the first time. Patients who were recorded with both diagnoses (IMIDs and cholecystectomy) between 2016 and 2023 were considered to be in the first-diagnosis group and were censored on the date of the second diagnosis. We excluded patients younger than 16 years old, patients with a diagnosis of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia before the diagnosis of IMIDs or cholecystectomy and patients with history of HIV infection, haematological malignancy or kidney transplant (online supplemental figure S1). All diagnoses codes are listed in online supplemental tables S1 and S2.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia in patients with IMIDs compared with controls during the study period. HPV-induced cancer was defined by at least one of the following diagnoses: cervical, vulval, vaginal, anal or penile cancer; cervical or penile in situ carcinoma; vulvar or vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia. Severe dysplasia included cervical, vulvar or vaginal dysplasia (online supplemental table S1). The ICD-10 codes used in our study identify cancers and dysplastic lesions occurring in anatomical areas commonly associated with HPV infection. However, they do not permit direct confirmation of HPV attribution at the individual level. Since the PMSI codes used for diagnosis did not distinguish between HPV-induced and non-HPV-induced oropharyngeal cancers, oropharyngeal cancers, which are more closely related to alcohol and tobacco consumption, were not included in our analysis.

Matching procedure

A propensity score for HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia was constructed using multivariate logistic regression including age, sex, year of diagnosis and comorbidities (such as high blood pressure, cardiovascular history, obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic respiratory disease and liver diseases) as covariates. Confounding variables were chosen a priori. A random, without replacement, 1:3 matching of exposed and unexposed patients was performed based on the propensity score. Matching accuracy and efficacy were assessed by calculating the standardised mean differences (SMD) for the matching variables between the two populations. SMD<0.1 was considered an indicator of a good balance between the groups being compared.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Quantitative variables are presented as mean (SD) or median (first quartile to fourth quartile). The first day of index stay for IMIDs (exposed) or controls (unexposed) was defined as time 0. Censoring was performed at death or on 31 December 2023, whichever came first. The incidence rates of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia were calculated in the IMIDs population by dividing the total number of patients with cancer during follow-up by the patient-years at risk. HRs of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia-free survival between exposed and unexposed patients and their respective 95% CIs were estimated in the matched population with a marginal Cox proportional hazards model.14 To account for remaining differences between groups despite matching, the estimated HRs were adjusted for age at baseline. Cumulative incidence curves were used to present survival free from HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia, with death treated as a competing risk. To identify risk factors for HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia in the IMIDs population, subdistributional HR (sHR) of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia-free survival and their respective 95% CIs were estimated in the IMIDs population at baseline using a Fine and Gray model15 to address the issue of competing risks, with death considered as the competing event. Adjustment was performed for age, sex and comorbidities. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to present the survival rate of patients free from HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia according to the diagnosed IMIDs. Statistical tests were two sided with a p value of <0.05 considered as statistically significant. Data management and most of the analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 software. Survival analyses, Kaplan-Meier curves and forest plots were generated using R V.4.4.1 software (Survival, Survminer, ggplot and gridExtra R packages).

Patient and public involvement statement

Patient and the public were not involved in any way.

Results

HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia in patients with IMIDs

From January 2016 to December 2023, a diagnosis of IMIDs (ie, exposed) and cholecystectomy (ie, unexposed controls) was reported in respectively 123 479 and 698 025 patients (online supplemental figure S1). The mean follow-up time for IMIDs and controls was 4.34 (2.57) and 3.88 (2.35) years, respectively. During the follow-up period, the incidence rate of HPV-induced cancers/severe dysplasia was 1.28 per 1000 patient-years (95% CI 1.18 to 3.37) compared with 0.61 per 1000 patient-years (95% CI 0.59 to 0.64) in controls.

HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia occurred in 693 (n=693/123 479; 0.56%) patients with IMIDs. Among them, 665 (n=665/693; 95.96%) were female and the median age at diagnosis of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia diagnosis was 49.00 years (38.00–63.00). HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia was diagnosed in 72.58% (n=503/693) of cases between the ages of 30 and 65, in 21.21% (n=147/693) of cases over 65 and in 6.20% (n=43/693) of cases under 30. The median interval between the diagnosis of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia and the diagnosis of an IMID was 1.73 years (range 0.01–7.86 years). Patients with IMIDs with HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia had SLE (n=266/693; 38.38%), RA (n=233/693; 33.62%), SS (n=135/693; 19.48%), SSc (n=122/693; 17.60%) and MCTD (n=37/693, 5.34%). Of the different cancer types, 487 (n=487/693; 70.27%) patients with IMIDs had cervical cancer, 93 (n=93/693; 13.42%) had anal cancer, 76 (n=76/693; 10.97%) had vulvar or vaginal cancer, and 11 (n=11/693; 1.59%) had penile cancer. 26 (n=26/693; 3.75%) patients had cancer at multiple sites. Among patients with anal cancer, 19.35% (n=18/93) were male. Online supplemental table S3 provides details of the types of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia according to each IMID.

HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia-free survival analysis

Overall, 686 (n=686/122 316; 0.56%) patients with IMIDs and 893 (n=893/316 047; 0.28%) controls developed HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia (details of the type of cancer in patients with IMIDs and controls are given in online supplemental table S5). IMIDs were significantly associated with an increased risk of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia with an age-adjusted HR of 1.99 (95% CI 1.80 to 2.20) in all IMIDs compared with matched controls (figure 1). An increased risk was observed in patients with IMID for all types of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia except penile cancer (table 2). Excess risk of HPV-induced cancer/severe dysplasia was higher for SLE (HR 3.65 (95% CI 3.17 to 4.21)), MCTD (HR 2.51 (95% CI 1.81 to 3.49)) and systemic sclerosis (HR 2.23 (95% CI 1.85 to 2.70)) (figure 1). Data on immunosuppressive treatment were unavailable and were therefore not included in the multivariable model.

Discussion

Our nationwide matched cohort study showed that the risk of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia of the cervix, vagina, vulva and anus was almost two times as high in the IMIDs population as in the control group. The excess risk is even more than three times higher in patients with SLE.

Our results are consistent with international studies that have reported an increased risk of HPV-induced cancer or high-grade lesions in patients with IMIDs. In 2020, a retrospective cohort study conducted in Australia compared a group of patients with various IMIDs with a control group free of IMIDs16 and showed an excess risk (adjusted HR of 1.47 (1.03–2.09)) of high-grade cervical abnormalities in the subgroup of patients with SLE/MCTD, compared with the control group. A Chinese cohort study evaluating the risk of cancer for five IMIDs, including SLE, RA, SSc, SS and idiopathic inflammatory myositis, found an increased risk of cervical and vulvar cancer (standardised incidence ratio (SIR) of 5.44) in patients with IMIDs.17 In the UK, a study of almost 500 000 patients showed a multivariable-adjusted HR of 1.90 (1.41–1.97) for HPV-associated diseases in patients with various IMIDs (80 types), compared with controls.18 More recently, a cohort study investigating the risk of HPV-induced cancer and precancerous conditions in multiple sites in patients with SLE revealed a higher risk of cervical, vulvar, vaginal and anal precancerous conditions, as well as oropharyngeal cancer, compared with the general population (SIR 2.5 (0.9–4.9)).19

The selection of the control group was crucial to ensuring the reliability of our study. As we used the PMSI database, we needed hospitalised patients for the controls. Cholecystectomy is usually performed for symptomatic gallbladder disease, including acute cholecystitis.20 Risk factors for this common diagnosis at hospital admission include age over 40 years, female sex and metabolic syndrome.21 Patients undergoing this procedure are not known to be at a higher risk of HPV-induced cancers than the general population.22 Based on this clinical knowledge, we considered that patients admitted for cholecystectomy could be used as controls for the IMIDs population, even though a matching procedure using a propensity score was used to minimise differences. Although matching accuracy between the two populations was good, we subsequently adjusted for age in the survival analysis because the SMD for age remained relatively high.

Patients with IMIDs who are under 65 years old are at a higher risk of HPV-induced cancer, as are patients with IMIDs who also have CKD. SLE appears to be the IMID associated with the highest risk of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia compared with other IMIDs, as previous reports have suggested.1623,25 The prevalence of HPV infections is up to three times higher in patients treated for SLE than in the general population.26 However, the underlying mechanisms linking HPV and lupus more strongly than with other IMIDs remain unclear. One possible explanation is that chronic HPV infection results from a major defect in 'natural’ HPV elimination in patients with SLE. Persistent infection with HR-HPV is crucial for the development of cervical cancer. In patients with SLE, HPV infection has been reported to persist for 6 months or longer in almost 50% of patients with incident infection, compared with 20% reported in the general population.27 A study of 65 patients over the age of 30 found that the rate of persistent HPV infection was as high as 80% in those with SLE. This was found to be associated with exposure to long-term steroid and immunosuppressant drugs.3 Overall, a B-cell defect consistent with a ‘common variable immunodeficiency-like’ immune phenotype has been reported in SLE and may explain the high incidence of severe infections and poor immune responses to vaccines observed in these patients.28,37 Anti-IFNα autoantibodies have been associated with an increased susceptibility to viral infections.38 Up to 15% of patients with SLE test positive for anti-IFNα autoantibodies.39,42 However, no significant correlation has been reported between anti-IFNα autoantibodies and HPV infection. The 2025 updated recommendations of the American Society of Gynecology stipulate that patients with SLE, whether or not they are on immunosuppressive therapy, should benefit from the same close monitoring as women living with HIV.43 However, the cervical cancer screening rate remains dramatically low among patients with SLE,44 and efforts should be made to raise awareness of this issue among medical professionals and patients with SLE.

This study has several limitations. First, the PMSI database only includes in-hospital diagnoses, procedures and deaths. Second, the reliability of the codes during initial hospitalisation determines the recorded diagnoses and procedures in the PMSI database. Third, censoring was defined as either in-hospital death or the end of the follow-up period. This assumes that patients neither moved out of France nor died at home during the follow-up period. Fourth, information bias cannot be excluded, as IMIDs could have been diagnosed in outpatients prior to the index admission or during inpatient stays before 2016. Fifth, we did not have access to information on patients’ treatments, which could have helped to explain the excess risk of cancer associated with IMIDs. Sixth, more effective cancer screening for patients with IMIDs could lead to an increased incidence of HPV-related cancers. However, despite national and international recommendations, current cervical cancer screening rates among patients with IMIDs are low. Previous reports have shown that only half of patients with SLE undergo cervical cancer screening.944,46 Seventh, individual-level vaccination data were unavailable within the PMSI database. However, HPV vaccination coverage has been reported to be less than 15% in patients with IMIDs,944,46 which supports the need for enhanced HPV vaccination strategies, particularly for patients with SLE. Eighth, oropharyngeal cancer was not included in our analysis as the diagnosis codes did not distinguish between HPV-induced and non-HPV-induced oropharyngeal cancers. Ninth, the interpretation of our findings may also be influenced by the fact that the IMIDs included in this study are strongly female-predominant diseases. Consequently, the exposed population was enriched for women, who bear the greatest burden of HPV-related malignancies. This characteristic may have contributed to the magnitude of the observed association independently of disease-related immune mechanisms. Although a sensitivity analysis restricted to women yielded similar results (online supplemental table S7), residual sex-related confounding cannot be completely excluded. Furthermore, our findings may not be generalisable to other IMIDs, particularly male-predominant conditions such as ankylosing spondylitis or psoriatic arthritis. Tenth, the rate of non-HPV cancer among patients with IMID with HPV-related cancer was high. Patients with a history of non-HPV malignancy may have undergone more intensive medical surveillance, which could have increased the opportunities for the detection of HPV-related dysplasia or cancer. However, as information regarding outpatient follow-up, cancer treatment history and screening practices was unavailable, the potential impact of surveillance bias could not be assessed directly. Eleventh, we used patients who had undergone cholecystectomy as controls, but these patients do not constitute a random sample of the general population. They are more likely to be female, over 40 years old and have metabolic syndrome. While the propensity score partially corrects for these factors, residual confounding from selection into hospital care may persist. Finally, it was not possible to adjust for factors associated with HPV infection, such as socioeconomic factors, lifetime sexual history and tobacco exposure.

In conclusion, this large nationwide study found that each IMID (SLE, SS, systemic sclerosis, MCTD and RA) was associated with an increased risk of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia at cervical, vaginal, vulval and anal sites. Without treatment data, it is impossible to determine whether the elevated risk is driven by the immune dysregulation of the disease itself, its treatment or both. Particular attention should be given to encouraging patients with IMIDs to get vaccinated against HPV and to undergo regular cervical cancer screening, especially those with SLE.
