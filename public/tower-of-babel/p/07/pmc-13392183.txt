Understanding Autistic Adults’ Psychological Experiences of Anxiety Problems: A Qualitative Interview Study
Authors: Rebecca J Roberts-Davis, Francesca G Happé, Alex Lau-Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur understanding of psychological experiences of anxiety in autism could be improved if it were shaped by autistic individuals. However, anxiety research in autism often begins with cognitive conceptualisations drawn from neurotypical or non-autistic individuals. This qualitative study aimed to explore psychological experiences of anxiety using semi-structured one-to-one interviews with a diverse sample of 20 U.K.-based, autistic adults, aged 18–55 years. Transcripts were analysed using two complementary methods: conceptual content analysis, where existing theories were tested against the data with predefined codes; and reflexive thematic analysis, where meaning was constructed from the data while considering research subjectivity. Our sample highly endorsed existing cognitive constructs: intolerance of uncertainty (100% of participants), repetitive negative thinking (100%), attentional biases (70%) and intrusive mental imagery (70%). Some autism-specific characteristics were indicated. The endorsement of imagery is particularly striking given claims that autism is often associated with weak imagery. Autistic adults described the way in which being autistic interacted with anxiety, through psychological and lived experiences. Existing support for anxiety problems was deemed insufficient and may be improved by further refining our understanding of anxiety in autism through future quantitative and qualitative research.Lay AbstractAnxiety is a top priority for research for the autistic community. We could increase our understanding of what might contribute to anxiety problems in autism by speaking to autistic people and learning about the different experiences they have had. We interviewed 20 U.K.-based autistic adults (aged 18–55 years) and asked them about their anxiety problems, including how they felt about well-known ideas of what might be involved in anxiety in autism. We looked at the answers to the interviews in two ways. The first way considers what autistic people think about the well-known ideas. The second way looks at similarities in the answers that autistic people gave. All participants’ anxiety problems involved repeated negative thoughts and struggling with uncertain situations. Most also said that they tend to focus on noises or other sensory experiences within their environment and imagine situations in their mind when they are anxious. Being autistic came with challenges that could make them more anxious, including struggling with changes to routine and being bullied. Autistic people described how anxiety made their life difficult and ways in which current support for anxiety is not helpful enough. Introduction

Anxiety is a paramount issue for autistic adults. A recent meta-analysis found anxiety disorders co-occur in about 20% of autistic adults (Lai et al., 2019), and a matched cohort study found the incidence rate ratio was almost double for autistic adults without intellectual disability compared to that for non-autistic counterparts (O’Nions et al., 2024). In addition, in a large, diverse sample of autistic adults from the United Kingdom, Cage et al. (2024) found their top research priority is mental health.

Cognitive mechanisms have been implicated in the development and maintenance of anxiety disorders. In neurotypical adults, cognitive models of anxiety disorders (e.g. Beck & Rush, 1985; Clark, 1986) were developed bottom-up, whereby detailed case studies of patients informed a thorough understanding of clinical phenomenology. This generated testable theories of the mechanisms maintaining the disorder and effective procedures to manipulate them. This resulted in the development of the cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) – efficacious in treating anxiety in neurotypical adults, although only around 50% of individuals fully recover (Carpenter et al., 2018). Mechanisms of anxiety theorised in neurotypical populations have recently been explored in autistic individuals, particularly intolerance of uncertainty (IU), repetitive negative thinking (RNT), attentional biases (AB) and intrusive mental imagery (MI).

IU is “a cognitive bias that affects how a person perceives, interprets, and responds to uncertain situations” (Dugas et al., 2004). As an established transdiagnostic construct across anxiety disorders in neurotypical adults and children (Mahoney & McEvoy, 2012), IU is considered a primary explanation for higher levels of anxiety in autistic populations (Boulter et al., 2014). Scores on questionnaire measures of IU have a well-established relationship with anxiety in autistic children and adults, explaining around 38% of the variance in anxiety (Jenkinson et al., 2020). Treatments addressing IU appear to also improve anxiety in autistic children (Rodgers et al., 2023), but these are yet to be tested in autistic adults.

RNT is also a transdiagnostic cognitive process, broadly described as recurring, passive and/or relatively uncontrollable thoughts focused on negative content such as distressing life events (Ehring & Watkins, 2008). In anxiety, RNT typically manifests as “worry,” defined as an attempt to solve problems with potentially negative outcomes (Borkovec et al., 1983). RNT may be more prominent in autistic individuals as a reflection of repetitive behaviours – a diagnostic feature of autism (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Compared to non-autistic peers, autistic adults report increased RNT, especially in relation to depression, as captured by questionnaire (Cooper & Russell, 2025) and diary methods (Cooper et al., 2022). A mixed-method study also found autistic adults worried significantly more than non-autistic adults and reported worry significantly impacted their ability to engage in daily life (Black et al., 2024).

AB towards fear-provoking stimuli have been found in neurotypical individuals with anxiety disorders (MacLeod & Mathews, 1988; Puliafico & Kendall, 2006). However, these biases have not been reliably detected in autistic individuals. May et al. (2015) found autistic children with clinically significant anxiety did not show facilitated attention to, or difficulties disengaging from, threatening stimuli (an angry face), relative to autistic and neurotypical groups without anxiety. Studies with autistic adults are lacking. Overall, the nature and relevance of AB in anxiety in autism remains unclear.

Finally, there is increasing recognition that intrusive MI is important in the development and maintenance of anxiety disorders (Brewin et al., 2010) but has only been explored in autistic individuals across two studies. Anxious autistic adolescents rate spontaneous MI significantly more negatively than those with low anxiety (Ozsivadjian et al., 2017), and the frequency and emotional impact of spontaneous future-oriented MI (flashforwards) were associated with symptoms of anxiety in autistic adolescents (Lau-Zhu et al., 2024). These studies challenge existing notions that autistic people have reduced “imagination” (Hatakeyama, 2024; King et al., 2024), but the role of MI in anxiety in autistic adults is yet to be fully explored.

The efficacy of CBT in targeting anxiety problems in autistic adults remains mixed. A systematic review by Menezes et al. (2022) identified only 13 studies investigating the efficacy of CBT in treating anxiety in autistic adults, which all had small, predominantly male samples. Four studies failed to find significant improvements in anxiety symptoms. Those that did, however, failed to outperform waitlist controls. The effectiveness of CBT in treating anxiety in autism may depend on how it is adapted for this population (Prasanna & Nayak, 2024; Spain & Happé, 2020). It may be that existing procedures for autism-adapted CBT insufficiently target the mechanisms already theorised as being relevant. Alternatively, it may be that the discovery of novel mechanisms, or novel aspects of existing mechanisms, is needed to adapt CBT to be more effective.

Qualitative research can provide rich, detailed data without the top-down restrictions of quantitative research, such as hypotheses and the experimental design usually taken from work conducted with neurotypical populations. Qualitative research has explored lived experiences of anxiety in autism, with minimal discussion of psychological experiences (phenomenological accounts of internal states) or cognitive mechanisms (components of these experiences with plausible causal significance). Existing studies have shown support for the role of IU and RNT in anxiety in autism. Trembath et al. (2012) interviewed 11 autistic young adults (aged 18–35 years), as well as parents and practitioners, who described uncertainty, fearful anticipation, unexpected change, disappointment and obsessive thoughts as sources of anxiety. Similarly, Robertson et al. (2018) interviewed 10 autistic adults (aged 19–60 years), as well as their parents and partners, who identified uncertainty, change and unpredictability as contributors to anxiety. Halim et al. (2018) compared 10 autistic adolescents and adults (aged 15–36 years) to 10 non-autistic adults (20–34 years) and found that the relationship between IU and anxiety was described distinctly across these groups, with autistic individuals linking IU to catastrophising and ability to cope.

Limited psychological insights from prior qualitative research may be partly because of the researchers focusing on external causes and the impacts of anxiety across both the interview and the analysis process. Moreover, the reliance on focus groups previously may have also made autistic participants less comfortable discussing private psychological experiences. Spain et al. (2020) individually interviewed six autistic adults (aged 23–52 years) about social anxiety and identified several psychological factors – negative self-beliefs (e.g. related to internalised ableism) and unhelpful thinking styles (e.g. absolutist thinking and a tendency to paranoia). The lead researcher and interviewer’s background in CBT may have drawn their attention to participants’ views on cognition, leading to more psychological insights arising. Thus, both the interview context and the researchers’ views and positions can impact the nature of the data obtained.

This study aimed to increase our understanding of anxiety in autistic adults by exploring their endorsement of established cognitive mechanisms in (neurotypical) anxiety disorders, as well as their first-person accounts of lived experience of anxiety problems, which could reveal novel and autism-specific insights. To investigate autistic adults’ endorsement of existing mechanisms, we used conceptual content analysis (Elo & Kyngäs, 2008), a deductive method assessing whether data is in agreement with existing theoretical frameworks. To explore autistic adults’ lived experiences of anxiety in a bottom-up manner, we used reflexive thematic analysis (TA; Braun & Clarke, 2019, 2021, 2023; Braun et al., 2022) while acknowledging the role of the researchers’ backgrounds in data interpretation. We adopted a subjectivist epistemology within an interpretivist paradigm, actively constructing meaning from a contextual and interpretative understanding of participants’ lived experience, which was influenced by our reflexive engagement with the data.

We individually interviewed autistic adults across several accessible formats to facilitate private discussion without the pressure of a group setting. Participants were recruited if they identified as having anxiety problems, rather than requiring a clinical diagnosis, to allow for participants who experience anxiety outside of traditional criteria (Kerns et al., 2014). Our sample was diverse across age, gender, sexuality, ethnicity and socioeconomic backgrounds to maximise diverse and representative views.

Methods

Participants

Twenty autistic adults were recruited. Eligibility criteria were (a) self-identification as having anxiety problems; (b) a formal diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), American Psychiatric Association, 2013; or the International Classification of Diseases, World Health Organization, 2022); (c) aged between 18 and 55 years; (d) living in the United Kingdom and (e) able to speak or write in English. Diagnosis of autism was verified using reports shown to the lead researcher, which was necessary due to recent issues of fraudulent participation in autism research (Pellicano, Adams, et al., 2024).

Participants were recruited from an existing participant database, as well as from a network hosted by Autistica – a U.K. charity for autism research. Among the responses from the database and Autistica’s network, participants were purposely contacted to generate a diverse sample across age, gender, sexuality, ethnicity and socioeconomic backgrounds (using parental education as a proxy measure). This strategy was adopted to address the historical underrepresentation and underreporting of diverse groups in autism research (Maye et al., 2022; Rødgaard et al., 2022; Steinbrenner et al., 2022). Purposive sampling was considered achieved when the sample reflected balanced representation across key demographics or aligned with U.K. population ratios (Office for National Statistics, 2022).

We used the five principles of information power by Malterud et al. (2016) to determine the final sample size. Our study had (a) broad research questions, aimed to collect a (b) diverse sample, used a (c) cross-case analysis strategy, but applied (d) established theory, and we deemed (e) the quality of the dialogue as high, due to the in-depth, varied discussions that took place. Sampling and data collection took place iteratively, with ongoing assessment of information power (Malterud et al., 2016). We judged information power to be sufficient when interviews consistently yielded rich, relevant data addressing each research question, supported by in-depth, detailed accounts and adequate coverage across the purposively sampled characteristics.

Measures

Semi-Structured Interview Guide

This was designed by the lead researcher who is autistic, in collaboration with the two other researchers who are both experienced in conducting cognitive and mental health research with autistic adults. Two autistic adults (outside of the sample) provided feedback on the wording and accessibility of the questions, after which further amendments were made. Supplemental Appendix A contains the items of the interview schedule.

The interview guide began with a short introduction about its purpose. Emphasis was made that there were no wrong or right answers.

The first section explored their experiences of anxiety, physiologically, emotionally and cognitively. Questions were anchored to participants’ recent, worst and earliest experiences of anxiety to explore potential differences across situations and times.

The second section explored the relevance of four established cognitive constructs related to anxiety within the clinical, cognitive literature: (a) RNT, (b) AB, (c) IU and (d) MI. For each construct endorsed, further questions explored specific characteristics previously documented in the clinical literature (e.g. whether RNT felt uncontrollable).

Self-Report Questionnaire Measures

Participants completed two self-report standardised measures of anxiety – the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory–Form Y Trait Items (STAI-T), a measure of participants’ tendency to experience anxiety (Spielberger et al., 1983), and the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), a measure of anxiety symptoms over the past month (Beck et al., 1988). Participants also completed a self-report standardised measure of depressive symptoms over the past 2 weeks, the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8; Kroenke et al., 2009), and a measure of trait imagery vividness, the Plymouth Sensory Imagery Questionnaire (PSI-Q; Andrade et al., 2014). Further details are included in Supplemental Appendix B.

Procedure

Participants were contacted via email with the information sheet and completed an online consent form and screening form (via the platform Gorilla).

Interviews were all conducted by the lead researcher RRD, an autistic doctoral researcher and senior support worker for autistic people. In line with Cridland et al. (2015), the interview guide was used flexibly, paramount where the interviewer recognised the questions were challenging or distressing for the participant.

Eighteen participants were interviewed online, via Microsoft Teams. One participant was interviewed in person at a university premise. All interviews were transcribed verbatim from the Microsoft Teams recording. A remaining participant completed the interview asynchronously in a written format via email exchanges. They were provided an initial set of questions via a Microsoft Word document with follow-up questions on the same document in one further exchange. This document was used as the transcript for the interview. All participants completed standardised self-report questionnaires within 24 hours prior to the interview (except the asynchronous interview participant who completed these after submitting their final answers).

Data Analysis

Transcription was conducted manually, immediately following interviews, as part of data familiarisation. Transcripts were stored under participants’ pseudonym (of their choosing if they had a preference). Analyses were performed in printed forms of the documents and with physical aids (i.e. sticky notes).

Autistic adults’ endorsement of cognitive mechanisms (data from the second section of interview) were analysed using the Conceptual Content Analysis method, as outlined in Elo and Kyngäs (2008). The lead researcher (RRD) went line-by-line, assigning nodes to each segment of the transcript. Some segments were given multiple nodes based on a range of meanings. Nodes went on to be assessed for correspondence with or exemplification from the identified categories in an unconstrained matrix of analysis, developed based on prior cognitive accounts of anxiety. If nodes made up an additional category within the matrix, this was added. If nodes did not fit within the matrix, they were added to the data considered for the reflexive TA.

In addition, all experiences of anxiety (from the data from the first section of interview) were analysed using the reflexive TA method (Braun & Clarke, 2019, 2021, 2023; Braun et al., 2022). RRD went line-by-line, assigning nodes to each segment of the transcript. Some segments were given multiple nodes based on a range of meanings. Nodes were grouped into codes, and themes were constructed from the codes (rather than from existing theoretical models). After initial themes were developed by RRD, a preliminary theme map and set of theme definitions were presented to co-researchers ALZ and FH. An in-person discussion was held to discuss these initial themes and how well they captured shared meaning in the data. This led to theme names being changed to be more closely aligned with participants’ own words to ensure their meaning was preserved. A final version of the themes and sub-themes, including definitions and example quotations, were sent to participants to ask for their reflections. In line with reflexive TA, we emphasised the constructive nature of our themes, caveating these were not “the truth” but “our truth.” Therefore, we incorporate their feedback in our discussion but not in our construction of themes/subthemes in the results (Braun & Clarke, 2024; Tracy, 2010).

Through analytic notes and regular in-person discussions, each researcher reflected on how their views and positions shaped the meaning constructed in this study. The aim was not to achieve a consensus of meaning but to explore multiple interpretations of the data at a semantic level. For example, discussions were held on diagnostic versus dimensional approaches, “atypical” presentations of anxiety in autism incompatible with current diagnostic frameworks (DSM-5th edition; American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and the value of different sources of expertise, including academic, clinical and lived experience.

In addition, a pre-registration on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/2wpz5/overview) was used to document our methodological decisions prior to data collection. Thoughts on personal, interpersonal and methodological reflexivity noted throughout the study are highlighted in the statement below.

Positionality Statement

As the lead researcher, I, RRD, acknowledge that my beliefs and experiences, both academically and personally, shaped this research. I believe being autistic comes with strengths and challenges, but it does not need to be “cured.” I also believe anxiety problems may, particularly in autistic people, not fit within traditional clinical criteria. My diagnoses of autism and anxiety and years of working with autistic people made me feel confident in designing the interview guide and speaking with participants.

I chose not to declare my diagnosis in the opening dialogue with participants as I feel it can be disingenuous for a researcher to try immediately build a relationship off a shared diagnosis. However, if participants asked, and many did, I was happy to disclose that I am autistic.

My perspective may have, in many ways, enriched the meaning I constructed from their lived experience; in other ways, it may have restricted it. Moreover, I recognise how my privilege as a White ciswoman based primarily in an academic setting is likely to have limited my understanding of some of the experiences described.

As co-authors (ALZ and FH), our perspectives were informed by a combined clinical and academic background in cognitive approaches to autism. This background shapes our interest in understanding and treating anxiety problems in autism, particularly the potential value of cognitive perspectives alongside the predominant behavioural focus of current guidelines and interventions.

Intolerance of Uncertainty

All of our participants endorsed IU as a predominant cause of their anxiety, though one participant felt IU was more relevant to their anxiety in the past than currently. To manage uncertainty, most relied on routine and predictability or attempted to resolve uncertainty through information seeking. This often involved researching uncertainties before events, a process they were “overwhelmed by.” When having to seek information from others, participants described feeling “embarrassed” due to concerns about appearing “immature” or “weird.” Autistic adults reported avoiding uncertain situations to cope with anxiety when younger, but in adulthood, they did so only when these could not be resolved through information seeking.

Repetitive Negative Thinking

All participants endorsed RNT, and the majority tended to catastrophise, feeling “life is gonna get worse, [they’re] not gonna be able to cope.” Around half reported experiencing RNT in verbal form (i.e. internal dialogue), while the remainder experienced it in imagery form (i.e. imagining in different senses) or both.

Attentional Biases

Around three quarters of our participants recognised AB during anxiety, though many required follow-up prompts or examples. AB were mostly towards auditory stimuli, followed by visual stimuli. AB was typically towards threatening stimuli, for example, unknown noises while home alone, the sound of stinger insects, sirens of emergency services and so on. Others described AB towards more neutral stimuli present in a generally anxiety-inducing environment, for example, the sounds in a pub during a social event, potentially due to increased sensory sensitivity during anxiety. Several also reported hyperfocusing on sensory stimuli to self-soothe or distract during anxiety.

Mental Imagery

Over two-thirds (70%) of our participants endorsed experiencing involuntary, intrusive MI during their anxiety. Quotations exemplifying their imagery are included in Supplemental Appendix C.

MI was across various sensory domains but was visual and/or auditory most frequently. A minority expressed they are “less likely to start imagining stuff than normal” when they felt anxious, as they are “more in the moment when [they’re] feeling really panicky.”

Of those reporting visual MI, some said the image was like “looking through [their] eyes”; some experienced an outside perspective like they could “see [themself] in that moment” and some experienced a mixture of both perspectives. The vividness of MI varied vastly, described as static or “patchy,” versus “clear” and “detailed.” Those who experienced anxiety-related MI mostly could not change the MI, especially where it related to a past experience, although a few felt distraction could make MI go away.

The majority of our autistic adults experienced MI directly when recalling or relating to a past distressing experience (i.e. intrusive memories). Many found MI distressing, even though they “knew it wasn’t real . . . it still reinforced the negative thoughts/feelings” they have about themselves, others and the world. Where MI was related to a potential future event, they often still felt “they come from past events . . . they feel like something that I could imagine happening again” rather than having MI of something completely novel and fictional.

Themes and subthemes.

Anxiety and Autism Are Very Strongly Linked

Elevated anxiety in autistic individuals was described as closely linked to the autistic experience. Participants described a mutually reinforcing relationship between anxiety and autistic traits or autism-related traits (e.g. sensory processing and masking), as well as greater exposure to distressing events, furthering anxiety vulnerability.

“It Feels Like a Vulnerability.”

Autistic adults described how being autistic could make them vulnerable to experiencing anxiety. Autistic traits (e.g. differences in social communication and interaction) could induce distress and, therefore, provoke anxiety (e.g. in social situations). Similarly, an autistic preference towards restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviour meant many participants reported experiencing high levels of anxiety from uncertainty or “anything unexpected like changes to routines.”

“A Chain Reaction.”

Autistic adults described “a chain reaction” between anxiety and their unique experiences as an autistic person. For example, high-intensity sensory environments were described as provoking greater anxiety, and a state of anxiety was described as causing more sensory hypersensitivity.

Similarly, autistic adults reported as “your anxiety rises, that ability to mask without having to think about it, lessens and lessens and lessens,” and the more anxious they get, “the more mental energy you’re having to put into masking.”

Actual Negative Experiences

Autistic adults closely linked moments of anxiety to negative past experiences such as others being “threatening, abusive.” These experiences were commonly described as resulting from their vulnerabilities as autistic individuals.

Anxiety was linked to current societal stressors, including employment, financial and housing insecurities and lack of day-to-day support. All of these stressors have been shown to disproportionately affect autistic individuals (Pellicano, Hall, & Ying Cai, 2024).

Consistent Part of Life

Anxiety problems were described as a ubiquitous, chronic feature of the day-to-day life of autistic individuals, including in the form of an ongoing “undercurrent,” accompanied by “intense” peaks.

“There’s This Undercurrent.”

Autistic adults described anxiety as an “undercurrent” with daily experiences “generally being a little bit more distressing than for other people.” This meant some found it “difficult to pinpoint a single event” because “as an autistic person . . . anxiety is something that you experience so frequently due to just how society is structured.”

A Very “Intense” Feeling

Autistic adults described intense external experiences of anxiety, such as physiological symptoms, panic attacks, meltdowns and shutdowns. These could be very distressing, to the point of having “wanted to end [their] life because of how painful it felt, how intense the feeling was, how extreme it was.”

Participants also reported intense internal experiences during anxiety, describing a “blind panic,” total loss of “rational thought,” intrusive thoughts and suicidality. Many experienced uncontrollable rumination, which they linked to an autistic tendency to fixate or problem solve. Catastrophising was similarly seen as being caused by the autistic ability to recognise patterns in the likelihood of experiencing negative events, particularly those that had previously occurred.

“It’s Not Letting People to Get Into This Chronic Part of It.”

Autistic adults described varying success in managing anxiety to prevent it from becoming chronic. Some described better management of their anxiety through self-advocating for reasonable adjustments; using sensory aids and deep breathing and avoidance of anxiety-provoking situations. Many felt their anxiety had been improved by a greater understanding of themselves, for example, through research they had done. For several, receiving an autism diagnosis at a later age was “validating,” helping them contextualise their anxiety in light of “the sensory overload, the mental exhaustion.”

For those who felt their anxiety had become more chronic, this was often due to the intersectional impact of gender, ethnicity and societal stressors. Several autistic women discussed how menopause and increasing familial responsibilities had worsened their anxiety. An autistic person explained their anxiety had been affected by cultural expectations (from a British Chinese background) related to their academic performance and professional career, as well as by economic insecurities caused by living in social housing.

Failed by the System

Autistic individuals perceive limitations in the current support provision, across psychological and pharmacological approaches, while highlighting a desire for a more relational approach to care.

“Hesitancy to Be on Medication.”

Autistic adults described hesitancy towards taking psychiatric medications primarily due to the side effect of feeling “very foggy and zombie-like” and “oblivious to danger.” Many reported having reduced anxiety from medication, but side effects made taking them “unsustainable.”

Neurotypical Bias in Therapies

CBT is the most-frequently used psychological intervention in the National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom and was described by autistic adults as being delivered through a “neurotypically focused” framework. This aligned with feeling frustrated with therapists “trying to break it down into ‘it’s just thoughts’ when a lot of the time it’s actually [their] reality.” During CBT, autistic adults felt they were “having a lot of responsibility put on [them] for the way [they were] feeling.”

Overburdened, Non-relational Healthcare System

Challenges experienced by autistic adults with current treatment options were compounded by problems with the wider healthcare system, described as overburdened and non-relational. Treatments were often perceived as suboptimal due to a limited number of sessions, group formats and the inconsistent involvement of multiple practitioners. Participants disliked that “anything through the NHS is always task related; they always want results; they always want your score to come down.”

Discussion

This study aimed to (a) investigate autistic adults’ endorsement of theorised cognitive mechanisms in anxiety while (b) exploring their lived experience of anxiety with a focus on psychological experiences. We individually interviewed 20 autistic adults, diverse across age, gender, sexuality, ethnicity and socioeconomic backgrounds, using a semi-structured interview guide and a hybrid analytical approach combining conceptual content analysis and reflexive TA. In our content analysis, we had complete endorsement for IU (100%) and RNT (100%), and strong endorsement for AB (70%) and MI (70%). Our study is the first to explicitly explore autistic adults’ endorsement of existing cognitive models of anxiety, showing endorsement of AB and MI. Both IU and RNT were also spontaneously referenced during the TA section of the interviews, suggesting these have high face validity as cognitive drivers of anxiety for autistic adults. Themes focus on how being autistic interacts with anxiety, through psychological and lived experiences, and the impacts, management and treatment of anxiety.

This study reinforces the case for IU as a potential explanation for higher levels of anxiety in autistic populations. This is in line with previous theories (Boulter et al., 2014), quantitative (Jenkinson et al., 2020) and qualitative research (Halim et al., 2018; Robertson et al., 2018; Trembath et al., 2012). Avoidance was viewed as a primary strategy for dealing with uncertainty at a younger age, but as adults, most moved to resolving uncertainty through information seeking. The participant who identified IU as a historical (rather than current) aspect of their anxiety felt they had minimised the negative effect of uncertainty by seeking out accepting spaces and people. Implementing strategies for managing uncertainty, as well as countering unhelpful strategies, may be effective for reducing anxiety in autistic populations (Rodgers et al., 2023). The high resonance of IU accounts with lived experience of anxiety suggests stronger causal evidence is warranted, with a shift from cross-sectional correlational studies to longitudinal and experimental designs.

Similarly, our study corroborates RNT as a key psychological experience in anxiety in autism, as participants recognised their tendency to repeatedly catastrophise during anxiety. Imagery-based RNT was prevalent, at odds with models arguing RNT is a verbal-based strategy to avoid distressing intrusive images (Borkovec et al., 1998), but in line with recent empirical data demonstrating comparable effects on affect between both modalities of RNT (Lawrence & Schwartz-Mette, 2019). Whether imagery-based RNT is a unique feature of anxiety in autism also warrants further research. During anxiety, imagery was both involuntary (spontaneous, repetitive images) and deliberate (active construction of worse-case scenarios and attempted problem-solving). Although anxiety treatments can reduce RNT in neurotypical populations (Monteregge et al., 2020), this evidence is lacking for autistic adults.

Most of our sample endorsed experiencing AB during their anxiety, linking threatening triggers to their negative past experiences. It is perhaps surprising AB was endorsed highly as it is typically considered an unconscious process, though it may be experienced consciously in autistic populations through a heightened awareness of their sensory sensitivities. Alternatively, the heightened awareness of AB in our sample may be due to prior experience of psychological therapies. A previous quantitative study suggests AB might be absent in anxious autistic children (May et al., 2015), indicating further objective measures are needed to confirm the presence of AB in autistic individuals.

Seventy percent of our sample endorsed experiencing intrusive MI during anxiety, a rate comparable to neurotypical populations (Brewin et al., 2010). This aligns with emerging literature (Lau-Zhu, Chan, et al., 2024; Lau-Zhu et al., 2024; Ozsivadjian et al., 2017) challenging the view that most autistic people have weak MI (Hatakeyama, 2024; King et al., 2024). Participants often experienced memory-based intrusive MI during anxiety, rather than the future-based imagery characteristic of neurotypical anxiety (Brewin et al., 2010). This aligns with the prominence of past negative experiences throughout the interviews. Emerging evidence suggests autistic individuals may perceive a wider range of negative events as “traumatic,” potentially contributing to an increased likelihood of developing memory-based, intrusive MI (Lau-Zhu et al., 2026; Rumball et al., 2020). MI was experienced in different strengths and modalities, and not all autistic adults who were capable of MI, generally, experienced intrusive MI during anxiety. Heterogeneity, inherent to the autism spectrum, will be important for fully understanding MI’s role in anxiety in autism (Happé & Frith, 2020). How we assess emotional imagery in autism also seems crucial, as many participants did not recognise sensory-based memories as “imagery,” perhaps because the term “imagery” is often associated with fictional or visual experiences.

Autistic adults described autistic traits as having a reciprocal relationship with anxiety, for example, being anxious could heighten inflexibility around routine, and being highly inflexible around routine could provoke anxiety. Similarly, autistic cognition was seen as exacerbating anxious cognition – an autistic tendency to analyse, problem solve and recognise patterns was perceived as leading to RNT and catastrophising. External stressors of anxiety, shown to disproportionately affect autistic individuals (Pellicano, Hall, & Ying Cai, 2024), were also seen as interacting with anxious cognition – bullying and abuse were content sources of MI, and being highly intolerant to uncertainty made financial, housing and employment insecurities more anxiety-inducing. Sensory hyperreactivity, a highly prevalent co-occurring difference in autistic individuals (Liss et al., 2006), was linked to anxiety in line with existing models (Green & Ben-Sasson, 2010; South & Rodgers, 2017).

Improvements in anxiety could reduce the impacts of anxiety, with earlier, more effective interventions minimising the likelihood of anxiety becoming chronic and/or debilitating. Current interventions were perceived as limited by an overburdened health system; for example, autistic adults reported being prescribed medications for long periods of time, which they felt had unsustainable side effects. They said they received small numbers of therapy sessions, in group formats and with constant change in practitioners. Existing therapies, particularly CBT, were perceived as following a neurotypically focused framework, especially in discussing and attempting to reframe thoughts.

This study was strengthened by our methodological approach. We developed the interview schedule with autistic adults, and interviews were led by an autistic interviewer, likely ensuring its relevance and potentially allowing for greater shared understanding. While discussing meaning in the data, we considered perspectives from our different sources of expertise, including academic, clinical and lived experience. Autistic adults had shared and diverging views, in line with the heterogeneity inherent to the autistic spectrum (Happé & Frith, 2020). This may have been enhanced by our diverse sample and individual interviews, differing from previous qualitative studies.

While this study expanded our understanding of anxiety in autism, it does carry important limitations. We focused our inclusion criteria on autistic adults with a diagnosis of autism, residing in the United Kingdom, aged 18–55 years. Thus, our understanding of anxiety in autism from this study may not transfer to other autistic populations, such as those without a formal diagnosis, of other developmental stages, living in other cultural contexts or those with an intellectual disability. We did not have a non-autistic control group given our focus on phenomenology. Standardised questionnaires of anxiety, depression and MI were used only to characterise our sample. STAI-T in our modest sample (N = 20) should be interpreted cautiously given the poor internal consistency (α = .54), which may reflect limitations of using standardised questionnaire measures in autistic populations (Stacey & Cage, 2023).

Our questions regarding psychological experiences of anxiety in autism did not provide novel theories of cognitive mechanisms (although some potential autism-specific characteristics were indicated). Data instead broadly aligned with the existing models discussed in the interview. One interpretation is that the underlying mechanisms are broadly shared with neurotypical populations but expressed or experienced differently in autistic individuals, supporting the adaptation of existing cognitive behavioural approaches. However, an alternative explanation is that because most autistic participants had received CBT, their accounts of cognitive experiences were shaped by dominant cognitive frameworks, potentially calling into question the validity of these constructs for this population. To better distinguish these two interpretations, more explicit probes for “new” or “different” ideas would be useful in future studies, as well as interviewing treatment-naïve adults with anxiety problems in both autistic and non-autistic populations.

However, importantly, it may be that existing therapies focus on relevant cognitive mechanisms but are neurotypically biased in other aspects of the delivery, for example, group settings. This would support further adaptation of existing therapies beyond the target mechanisms, perhaps particularly in the language around the purpose of these interventions which autistic adults described as making them feel “blamed.”

To sum, this study reaffirms the prominence of IU and RNT in anxiety in autism and demonstrates, for the first time, that most autistic adults endorse AB and intrusive MI as relevant aspects of anxious cognition for autistic adults. The imagery findings are particularly important considering the higher reported rates of weak/reduced imagery (aphantasia) in autism. Autistic traits are deemed to have a reciprocal relationship with anxiety, as well as exacerbating the impact of external and internal stressors on anxiety. Greater support for anxiety is needed with interventions that consider unique caveats of autistic cognition and are informed by the role potentially traumatic experiences may play. Future quantitative and qualitative research is needed to address the fundamental challenge of heterogeneity in autistic cognition for developing an improved understanding of anxiety phenomenology in autism.
