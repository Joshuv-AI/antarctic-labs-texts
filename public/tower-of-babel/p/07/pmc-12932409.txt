Discover personalized drug opportunity and optimized tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy for rare cancer: an organoid-based whole-journey clinical mapping study in a neuroendocrine cancer patient
Authors: Xinyu Gao, Zhenjiang Liu, Zhaoting Xu, Ke Liu, Yiming Zhang, Jing Yu, Xu Wang, Xinghua Cheng, Chao Ni, Pin Wang, Yarong Liu, Xinfang Hong, Jingwei Sun, Bing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRare cancer patients face dire prognoses due to high malignancy and scarce treatment options, underscoring an imperative for personalized strategies. This study aims to evaluate the potential of precision medicine to extend survival in rare cancer patients by leveraging personalized tumor organoids (PTOs) as a discovery platform to comprehensively explore and generate new treatment opportunities.MethodsWe performed whole-journey clinical mapping in a neuroendocrine cervical cancer (NECC) patient with chemo-resistant metastases. Longitudinal PTOs from three recurrent lesions and autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were established to correlate clinical responses and discover new therapies. A co-culture system integrating PTOs and autologous TILs was developed to replicate clinical responses and optimize TIL therapy through rebuilding tumor antigen microenvironment.ResultsPTOs recapitulated tumor evolution and resistance imprints, enabling identification of a highly effective targeted therapy candidate post-chemotherapy failure. The co-culture system faithfully mirrored clinical immunotherapy responses, validating its potential as a companion diagnostic. Utilizing PTOs to rebuild the autologous tumor antigen microenvironment during TIL production generated a personalized, optimized TIL population. These optimized TILs exhibited enhanced anti-tumor ability and their administration correlated with clinical tumor regression in the patient. Generalizability of this PTO-based optimization strategy was validated across both patients and cancer types.ConclusionThis study establishes that survival for rare cancer patients hinges on integrated, personalized companion diagnostics and therapy optimization. By bridging iterative tumor models with clinical interventions, we provide a scalable conceptual framework to overcome therapeutic nihilism and unlock precision-driven survival benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-026-01172-y.Keywords: Whole clinical journey case study, Rare cancer, Organoids, Tumor evolution, Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy, Clinical trial, Immune co-culture

Introduction

Globally, there are more than 200 types of rare cancers, with an annual incidence rate of approximately 6 per 100,000 as defined by the European Society For Medical Oncology (ESMO) [1]. Yet in stark contrast, rare cancers collectively account for about 20% of all cancer cases and contribute to one-fourth of cancer-related deaths, underscoring their significant overall impact [1, 2]. Patients with rare cancers often face dire prognoses [3]. For too long, their survival needs have been neglected. The underlying reasons for this situation include: (1) profound tumor heterogeneity that complicates research, and (2) small patient populations that stymies clinical trials [4, 5].

Because rare cancers are often highly aggressive, each and every treatment opportunity for a patient is precious. However, there are scarcely any effective treatment options available for these patients [6]. In many cases, physicians are often forced to rely on case studies or borrow regimens from more common cancers with similar characteristics. Yet differences in molecular mechanisms and significant patient heterogeneity often render these treatments ineffective, which leads to increased health burdens and mortality [7, 8]. While some studies have reported high response rates for targeted therapies in rare cancers, the cautious approach and limitations of approved drug indications frequently discourage physicians from adopting these therapies [9, 10]. As a result, patients may lose treatment opportunities after developing resistance to chemotherapy [11–13]. In end-stage scenarios, patients with rare cancers are often prioritized for enrollment in clinical trials of new technologies, such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy, as the last resort. Despite this, rare cancers exhibit extremely low success rates compared to common cancers [14]. Therefore, it is vital to select and optimize personalized therapies to broaden treatment choices and improve clinical outcomes for rare cancer patients.

Organoid models have become powerful tools in precision oncology, enabling drug screening and translational research across various common cancer types [15, 16]. By integrating with anti-tumor modalities, including immune cells, the potential of organoid models in evaluating novel therapies has been further expanded [17, 18]. Recent studies have also begun to explore the application of organoids in optimizing immunotherapy [19, 20]. However, rare cancer patients with urgent clinical needs have yet to fully benefit from the organoid-guided precision medicine revolution [13]. The unique disease mechanisms and rapid evolution of diseases make it difficult for them to benefit from existing high-throughput tumor organoid cohort studies. Therefore, personalized and sustained attention to these patients remains a critical necessity.

Capturing longitudinal tumor samples throughout a patient’s disease course is exceptionally challenging, especially in rare cancers. This study capitalized on a unique opportunity to track the entire clinical journey of a patient with neuroendocrine cervical cancer (NECC), who underwent multiple rounds of chemotherapy and immunotherapy. We established personalized tumor organoids (PTOs) from three recurrent metastases and, critically, obtained lesion-specific tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs). Notably, clinical data on TIL therapy in rare cancers remain scarce. This patient achieved a partial response (PR) to TIL therapy, providing a valuable clinical anchor for our experimental work. This allowed us to leverage the PTOs as a discovery platform to exhaustively explore and generate new treatment opportunities, probing the potential of precision medicine to extend survival.

These longitudinal PTOs preserved the molecular signatures corresponding to specific evolutionary stages of tumor progression, capturing correlated clinical responses and acquired resistance profiles. Moreover, the identification of a highly effective targeted therapy candidate following metastatic chemoresistance highlighted the crucial potential of targeted therapies in treating rare cancers.

We further established a co-culture platform integrating lesion-specific TILs with matched PTOs, effectively recapitulating the patient’s clinical response to immunotherapy. Building upon this system, we developed an optimized strategy for expanding tumor-specific TILs by reconstructing the autologous tumor antigen microenvironment using PTOs. This approach enabled the generation of personalized TIL populations with significantly enhanced antitumor activity. The reliability of these optimized TILs was validated at the clinical level, while their generalizability was demonstrated by consistent results across two additional independent cases.

This study provides a proof-of-concept for personalized therapeutic companion diagnostics and optimization throughout the whole clinical journey by making the most of PTOs, highlighting the significant potential to expand therapeutic opportunities to maximize the clinical benefits for rare cancer patients.

PTOs were established from three metastatic lesions (Fig. 1a). These PTOs formed within two weeks and shared similar morphological characteristics, presenting as grape-like cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Transcriptome data, however, supported the different proliferation rates they showed, suggesting varying degrees of malignancy (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Histopathological analysis demonstrated that PTOs faithfully duplicated the histological features and pathological protein expression of their parental tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The PTOs were composed of a homogeneous cell population characterized by ovoid or somewhat spindled hyperchromatic nuclei and scant cytoplasm, while the cell population was also accompanied by extensive crush artifacts, nuclear fragmentation, and necrosis [21]. Positive staining for Chromogranin A (CHGA) and Synaptophysin (SYP) confirmed their neuroendocrine characteristics. Additionally, PTOs exhibited positive staining for P16INK4a and a high Ki-67 proliferation index, indicating sustained HPV infection and high-grade malignancy, which was consistent with their parental tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1c) [8, 21].

Bulk RNA-seq analysis was conducted to determine whether PTOs retained gene expression profiles similar to their parental tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). We detected expression levels of typical neuroendocrine differentiation markers (CHGA, SYP, NCAM1) and neuroendocrine lineage transcription factors (INSM1, ASCL1, POU2F2, POU3F2, NEUROD1, NEUROD2) in PTOs. These expression levels resembled those observed in their parental tumors and aligned with expression trends in other neuroendocrine tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1d) [22, 23]. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) clustered PTOs with patient’s tumor tissue, revealing that the transcriptomic profile of NECC distinctly varied from those of cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and other neuroendocrine carcinomas (NEC). This finding suggested the unique expression characteristics of NECC tumor of this patient (Supplementary Fig. 1e) [23–25].

To determine whether PTOs recapitulated the genomic landscape of their parental tumors, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on the primary lesion and three pairs of metastatic tumor-PTO samples (Fig. 1b; Table S2). The PTOs exhibited high concordance with their parental tumors in terms of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertion-deletion mutations (Indels), while preserving distinctions among different lesions (Fig. 1b). In comparison to databases such as COSMIC and previously reported neuroendocrine-related cancer genes, 43 shared non-synonymous mutational genes were identified across all tumors and observed in cancer-associated genes such as ACVR2A, KMT2C, TNFAIP3, GATA3, MUC16, and BCORL1 [26–29]. Additionally, the metastatic lesions exhibited an additional 20 mutations in genes such as FUBP1, FAT1, FGFR2, RNF213 and CIITA, suggesting their potential relevance to tumor metastasis and malignant progression. Moreover, the study identified 4, 12, and 21 unique mutational genes among the metastatic tumors M1, M2, and M3, respectively, but few known cancer genes, excepting LRIG3 in M2, and TTN in M3 (Table S2). In addition, consistency of copy number alterations was found between PTOs and their parental tumors, meanwhile demonstrating inter-tumoral differences (Fig. 1c and Table S3).

Overall, these findings illustrate the genomic consistency between PTOs and their parental tumors at the genetic level, underscoring PTOs’ ability to preserve genomic integrity across different stages of tumor progression.

Furthermore, considering the heterogeneity of tumors, this study examined the subclonal architecture across all samples to understand their evolution during treatment. Clonal analysis identified a total of 12 distinct mutational clusters, with 8 clusters showing significant changes during tumor progression (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 1d, the proportions of clusters c1, c2, c3, c5, c7, and c10 continuously increased over time, implying their contribution to malignant evolution. Cluster c4 and c11, which were major clones in the primary lesion, exhibited a considerable reduction after initial TC (Paclitaxel and Carboplatin) treatment (M1) yet recovered during subsequent EP and VEC (EP: Etoposide and Platimum; VEC: Combination of Vinorelbine, Epirubicin, and Cyclophosphamide) alternating chemotherapy (M2), indicating that these clones may respond to the initial TC treatment but are insensitive to the second-phase treatment. Additionally, they maintained high proportions even after the second TC treatment (M3), suggesting that these clones may have developed acquired resistance to TC therapy. The subclonal structure analysis of PTOs displayed that proportions of clones and trends of change were mostly consistent with those of their corresponding tumor tissues (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Thus, PTOs accurately reflected the complex genetic composition of their parental tumors.

Moreover, mutational signatures analysis uncovered that PTOs preserved numerous single-base substitution signatures (SBSs) found in the tumors (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2b). The platinum chemotherapy treatment-associated signatures (SBS31 and SBS35) were enriched in both the M3 tumor and the PTOs, consistent with the patient’s ongoing clinical exposure to platinum-based therapy. Notably, these platinum-associated signatures also arose in PTOs derived from M1 and M2 lesions, despite being absent in the corresponding tumors (Fig. 1f). Further analysis of the mutational signatures of each cluster unveiled that clusters c3, c10, and c11 were primarily responsible for the features correlated with platinum chemotherapy treatment (Fig. 1g). Increased proportions of these clusters in PTOs manifests that PTOs may have enriched resistant tumor cell populations (Fig. 1e). Combined with the changes in their proportions during the treatment process, it may suggest that they exacerbate malignant progression.

In conclusion, our data indicate that within the same patient, PTOs from different lesions can precisely retain the molecular characteristics specific to different evolutionary stages.

Subsequently, to assess the fidelity of PTOs in companion diagnostics for chemotherapy, PTOs from M1 to M3 metastatic lesions were utilized for the retrospective examination of their drug sensitivity to clinical therapies. Throughout the treatment process, the patient underwent various chemotherapy regimens and showed varying responses (Fig. 1a). The time window from the initiation of chemotherapy to the discovery of new lesions served as an evaluation metric. The initial TC therapy was 25 months, followed by 4-month of EP/VEC alternating chemotherapy received after the first recurrence and 7.5-month of resumed TC therapy received after the second recurrence. After the third recurrence, the patient received XELOX therapy (Capecitabine and Oxaliplatin), whereas treatment interruptions precluded a meaningful assessment.

Based on data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database for CESC, we categorized the response of PTOs to different drugs into three grades (Fig. 2a) [30]. Grade 1 includes Paclitaxel and Epirubicin, indicating high sensitivity of PTOs to these drugs. Grade 2 includes Vinorelbine, Capecitabine and Etoposide, and Grade 3 includes platinum-based drugs and Cyclophosphamide. PTOs exhibited the highest sensitivity to Paclitaxel, consistent with the complete remission (CR) achieved with initial TC treatment in clinical settings. Following the second recurrence, TC treatment, although not curative, still showed significant efficacy (Fig. 1a). The treatment failure after the first recurrence may be attributed to the use of relatively insensitive drugs (Etoposide and platinum-based drugs) in alternating chemotherapy. Although the XELOX therapy after the third recurrence was not evaluable, our drug sensitivity data suggested a poor response (Fig. 2a). These results collectively demonstrate that PTOs exhibit drug sensitivity responses consistent with clinical outcomes.

Remarkably, PTOs derived from lesions that recurred during drug treatment demonstrated relative resistance compared to PTOs from particular treatment-naïve tumors. Specifically, PTOs from the second metastasis (M2) displayed higher IC50 values for most drugs involved in EP and VEC therapies compared to those from the first metastasis (M1) (Fig. 2b). Similarly, PTOs from the third metastasis (M3) exhibited significant tolerance to Paclitaxel or Carboplatin compared to the second recurrence (Fig. 2c). ATAC analysis also reported the acquisition of resistance signatures in PTOs, with M2 and M3 PTOs respectively showing enrichment of ABC transporter pathway and platinum drug resistance pathway, suggesting the development of drug resistance (Fig. 2d). These findings highlight the capability of PTOs to retain drug resistance imprints acquired through clinical treatment.

Given the clinical failure of the final chemotherapy, we employed PTOs to explore additional therapeutic options for the patient. Genomic analysis disclosed substantial amplification of ERBB2 in the patient’s tumors (Fig. 2e). Upon validation, patient’s samples exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of ERBB2 (Fig. 2f). PTOs derived from all metastases collectively showed prominent enrichment in ERBB2 downstream Ras and MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 2d). Transcriptome analysis further indicated heightened expression of ERBB and MAPK, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways in NECC compared to CESC (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3a). These findings suggest the potential for investigating pathway-specific targeted therapies.

Therefore, we evaluated the sensitivity of PTOs to three drugs: (1) Afatinib, a pan-ERBB inhibitor approved for non-small cell lung cancer; (2) Trametinib, an MEK inhibitor approved for lung cancer treatment; and (3) Alpelisib, a PIK3C inhibitor approved for breast cancer. Results indicated that M3 PTOs, compared to another ordinary cervical cancer organoid line, exhibited higher sensitivity to Afatinib and Trametinib, but unaffected by Alpelisib, suggesting that the malignancy of these tumors is more dependent on the ERBB-MAPK pathway (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 3b).

We selected Afatinib for further validation at an in vivo level. Compared to the vehicle control group, clinical TC therapy, which demonstrated a relatively favorable response in patient treatment, failed to achieve complete tumor control. This finding aligned with the clinical development of resistance to TC therapy. Nevertheless, once-daily oral Afatinib monotherapy completely inhibited tumor growth and even induced tumor regression in one instance (Fig. 2i, j, and Supplementary Fig. 3c). Additionally, evidence confirmed that Afatinib did not induce acute or chronic toxicity in animal models (Supplementary Fig. 3d). These results demonstrate that the patient’s tumor exhibits extremely high sensitivity to Afatinib, indicating significant therapeutic potential.

However, due to the cautious yet rigorous approach and drug approval status, the patient was not eligible to receive this drug clinically. In light of the failure of multi-drug therapy, clinicians opted for novel treatment modalities to address the challenges posed by tumor progression in subsequent treatments.

Immunofluorescent staining and mRNA transcriptome sequencing data revealed significant T cell infiltration within the tumor, rendering the implementation of adoptive transfer of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) as the current treatment strategy (Fig. 3a, b). Consequently, the patient received the GT201 TIL product.

During the GT201 preparation, the number of TILs increased 512-fold within 2 weeks compared to the initial cell count, and reached clinically relevant dose (Fig. 3c). Subsequent analysis of activation/exhaustion-related signatures indicated that the TIL product predominantly exhibited a double-negative phenotype for CD39 and CD69 in both CD4 and CD8 subgroups (Fig. 3d), which correlates with tumor-specific TILs and is associated with prolonged clinical benefits post-infusion [31]. These findings underscore the potent proliferative activity and potential for improved clinical efficacy of GT201 TILs expanded from this patient.

The baseline information and treatment details of the patient upon enrollment are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a. Treatment efficacy was demonstrated by a partial response (PR), with computed tomography (CT) scans presenting a 69% reduction in the size of the left clavicular lymph node 4 weeks after the treatment (Fig. 3e). Immunophenotype tracking of the patient’s peripheral blood showed peak levels of GT201 TILs approximately 3 days after infusion and indicated persistent serum interferon (IFN-γ) levels (Fig. 3f). Post-infusion tracking results revealed a predominance of effector memory (Tem) and terminally differentiated effector memory (CD45RA+ Effector Memory CD8+ T Cells, Temra) cells in the CD8 subset, while central (Tcm) and effector memory T cells were predominant in the CD4 subset (Fig. 3g). These observations denote an enhanced antitumor immunity, which align with the clinically observed positive treatment outcome.

To validate that our PTOs and TILs can serve as companion diagnostics for clinical immunotherapy outcomes, we established a co-culture system of lesion-specific TILs and PTOs, as illustrated in Fig. 3h. Specifically, TILs and PTOs were labeled with live-cell dyes beforehand, and then co-cultured under suspension conditions. The tumor-specific markers of immune cells, along with the disintegration and apoptosis of tumor organoids, were used as indicators of TILs’ cytotoxic capabilities.

When co-cultured with PTOs, imaging analysis demonstrated that our cultured TILs effectively induced disintegration of PTOs and killed tumor cells (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Consistent with the response of GT201 TILs to in situ tumor cells, immunological analysis revealed elevated IFN-γ release from TILs upon PTO stimulation (Fig. 3j, k). These data suggest specific cytotoxicity of TILs, aligning with the patient’s clinical outcomes and supporting the utility of this system for treatment response evaluation. Notably, TILs also exhibited significant cytotoxicity and tumor specific responses against PTOs derived from the first two metastatic lesions (M1, M2) (Supplementary Fig. 4b-d). Given their shared mutation profiles, this observation indicates that TILs may exert broad-spectrum cytotoxic effects against metastatic tumors.

However, although several lines of evidence confirm that TILs exert antitumor effects, the patient did not achieve complete remission (Fig. 1a). Given the tumor’s high malignancy and uniqueness, the limited efficacy likely stems from constraints of standard treatment protocols designed for common cancers.

To maximize therapeutic benefits for rare cancer patients in clinical trials, the potential of TILs must be fully harnessed. Thus, this study attempts to personalized optimize TILs of this patient, providing critical insights for future management of similar cases.

During the conventional expansion process of TILs, detachment from the tumor antigen microenvironment silences tumor-specific signals, thus complicating the monitoring of effective TIL expansion. Additionally, competition from bystander TILs often leads to the elimination of effective TILs during production. To address these challenges, the study employed an optimization strategy by introducing lesion-specific PTOs as an autologous tumor antigen microenvironment before large-scale TIL expansion. This approach trains TILs to regain tumor-specific signals, enabling the selective expansion of tumor-specific TILs through further sorting (Fig. 4a).

CD137, a sensitive cell surface marker for T cell activation, is widely used independently or jointly to identify and sort antigen-specific cells [32, 33]. In tumor immunology studies, CD137 has been reported to be more effective than other tumor-specific markers in indicating tumor-specific TILs [34]. In preliminary experiments, CD137 expression on TILs significantly exceeded baseline levels after incubation with matched PTOs, indicating its utility as a molecular hook for extracting tumor-specific TILs. Notably, CD137 expression progressively declined during extended incubation, leading us to establish the 24-hour incubation period as optimal for CD137-based sorting (Supplementary Fig. 5a).

To develop an optimized TIL therapy, pre-rapid expansion procedure TILs (preREP TILs) were routinely expanded and subsequently trained with autologous PTOs. TILs were sorted based on their CD137 expression profile at the aforementioned time point and then highly-expanded. Subsequently, the anti-tumor function of post-expanded TILs was assessed in co-culture system (Fig. 4a).

Image analysis revealed that CD137+-sorted TILs induced significantly enhanced dissociation and specific cytotoxicity against M3 PTOs, which was consistent with tumor apoptosis observed by Incucyte real-time imaging (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 5b). FACS quantification of residual tumor cells further confirmed the enhanced cytotoxic efficacy of CD137+-sorted TILs in the co-culture system, showing a marked reduction in tumor cell survival (Fig. 4d). Critically, addition of an MHC-blocker (MHC-B) specifically inhibited the cytotoxicity and infiltration capacity of CD137+-sorted TILs, confirming their tumor antigen-dependent specificity (Fig. 4c, d).

Additionally, immunophenotypic analysis demonstrated that PTO-trained CD137+-sorted TILs exhibited enhanced tumor-specific responses upon M3 PTO stimulation, as evidenced by elevated expression levels of CD137 and CD69 in the CD8+ and CD4+ subgroups, respectively, along with enhanced cytotoxic function, which positively correlated with the percentage of CD8+IFN-γ+ and CD8+CD107a+ TILs (Fig. 4e). Notably, these TILs retained precise specificity against autologous in situ tumor cells, with significantly increased IFN-γ+, CD107a+, and TNF-α+ populations (Fig. 4f).

These findings confirm that CD137 expression identifies putative tumor-specific TILs following PTO incubation. PTO training enriches TIL subsets with bona fide anti-tumor activity, thereby offering a strategy to optimize current TIL therapies.

Furthermore, CD137+-sorted TILs mediated near-complete eradication of PTOs derived from all metastatic sites (M1-M3), with the most potent efficacy against M3 (Supplementary Fig. 5c-e). This suggests that PTO-guided enrichment captures TIL clones recognizing conserved antigens across the evolving tumor landscape. The pan-lesional efficacy implies that despite subclonal diversification during disease progression, a core immunogenic antigen repertoire persists, enabling unified targeting of heterogeneous metastases.

To validate the generalizability of this PTO-based TIL optimization strategy, we replicated the process in TILs derived from an additional NECC case, whose PTOs recapitulated the histochemical and genomic features of the original tumor tissue (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Co-culture assays similarly confirmed that after PTO-training, CD137+-sorted TILs exhibited enhanced tumor antigen-specific responses against both PTOs and tumor cells. These included increased cytokine production and elevated expression of activation/killing markers (IFN-γ, Granzyme B; Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Furthermore, in a separate lung cancer case, CD137+-sorted TILs also demonstrated heightened specificity toward PTOs (Supplementary Fig. 6e). These findings collectively underscore the potential of PTO-guided TIL optimization across diverse patients and cancer types.

To define the molecular identity of PTO-primed tumor-specific TILs, we performed integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing on TILs after autologous PTO incubation (Fig. 4g). This identified a dominant tumor-reactive T cell cluster (C6, Supplementary Fig. 7a-c) characterized by high expression of CD137 and established effector genes (IFNG, TNF, MYC, and GZMB), consistent with known tumor-specific TIL biomarkers [35].

TCR clonotype tracking delineated the functional transition of these cells (Fig. 4h, i). Notably, the tumor-specific clonotypes originated from an exhausted (Tex) population in the baseline tumor (Fig. 4j), as evidenced by their significantly elevated expression of exhaustion markers including PDCD1, TIGIT, LAG3, and TOX (Fig. 4j), aligning with known associations between tumor-specific TILs and exhaustion markers [36–38]. Critically, during PTO-guided in vitro expansion (preREP TILs and TIL product), these same clonotypes shifted from an exhausted state into proliferative, stem-like states. This shift was marked by their broad distribution across proliferating (C2/C4/C8) and Trm-like Tem clusters (C0) and the upregulation of genes related to proliferation and stemness, such as MKI67, CDKN2A, E2F2, and KLF2 (Fig. 4h-j). This functional reversal indicates durable anti-tumor function, aligning with evidence that stem-like TILs underpin long-term tumor control and T cell persistence post-infusion [31, 39].

To clinically validate the efficacy of these putative tumor-specific TILs, we tracked their clonotypes in the patient’s peripheral blood before (D-6) and after (D7) infusion. The results demonstrated significant enrichment of these TCR clonotypes post infusion, correlating with the clinical tumor reduction observed in the patient (Fig. 4k, l). This suggests that tumor clearance was highly likely attributed to the putative tumor-specific TILs, which could be effectively expanded through our PTO-guided optimization strategy.

Collectively, these findings validate the clinical translation potential of this approach and suggest the great value of fully leveraging PTO to achieve personalized therapy optimization.

Discussion

In summary, our study establishes a comprehensive conceptual framework for whole-lifespan precision medicine using the PTO model. We reveal that PTOs can function as both companion diagnostics and optimization platforms throughout the clinical course of rare cancer patients. PTOs derived from multiple metastatic sites not only captured genomic and transcriptomic heterogeneity but also preserved resistance signatures. This enables recapitulation of the patient’s clinical outcomes and forecasting of effective targeted therapies after chemotherapy failure. Moreover, by integrating PTOs into TIL expansion workflows, we successfully enriched tumor-specific T cell populations with enhanced anti-tumor activity, demonstrating that organoid-based training can improve immunotherapy efficacy. This approach opens new avenues for personalized immune treatments in rare cancers.

Research on rare cancers is often hindered by challenges in sample collection and a lack of suitable models [12]. Nevertheless, the advancement of organoid technology presents a promising opportunity for studying these cancers [29, 40]. In the context of neuroendocrine cancers, Kenta Kawasaki et al. and Talya L. Dayton et al. have independently established organoid libraries derived from gastrointestinal and other tissue sources, providing valuable insights into their oncogenic mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets [29, 40]. Given the inherently limited sample sizes of specific cancer subtypes and the rapid tumor evolution during therapy, personalized and sustained patient management is particularly crucial. To address this, we longitudinally established PTOs from distinct spatial and temporal tumor sites, enabling dynamic tracking of disease progression rather than relying on static snapshots (Fig. 1a). Our findings revealed that these tumors exhibited markedly different molecular characteristics (Fig. 1b-g). Furthermore, their corresponding PTOs demonstrated distinct therapeutic responses to the same treatment regimens, depending on the stage of disease progression (Fig. 2b, c). These observations highlight the importance of carefully considering both the timing of organoid derivation and the patient’s specific clinical context when employing organoid models for therapeutic prediction or guiding treatment decisions.

The development of therapeutic strategies for rare cancers largely relies on international collaborations, small case series, or retrospective analyses [5, 41]. However, the actual clinical benefit patients derive from these approaches remains minimal [6, 42]. Clinicians also tend to adopt a cautious stance toward targeted therapies for rare cancers, further contributing to the therapeutic void faced by these patients [9, 10]. For instance, although international guidelines recommend the EP regimen for treating NECC, our case clearly exhibited a limited response to this treatment (Fig. 2a) [43, 44]. Even though the patient initially responded well to paclitaxel, resistance ultimately emerged (Fig. 2c). In our study, the pan-ERBB inhibitor afatinib demonstrated exceptional therapeutic potential against metastatic, chemotherapy-resistant tumors, even suggesting the possibility of complete tumor regression (Fig. 2h-j). These results challenge the perception of therapeutic nihilism in rare cancers, underscoring that effective options may still exist even after resistance to conventional chemotherapy, and sometimes capable of inducing complete tumor eradication.

In recent years, organoid technology has significantly expanded the repertoire of drugs available for cancer treatment; however, patients with rare cancers have yet to benefit meaningfully from this advance. Constrained by ethical considerations and translational barriers, rare cancer patients’ urgent survival needs have long been overlooked. Patients with rare cancers face fewer therapeutic opportunities and a substantially narrower pool of drug options compared to those with more common tumor types. This makes it imperative to maximize the value of every available treatment opportunity. Encouragingly, recent regulatory shifts (such as FDA and NIH approvals allowing organoid models to replace animal testing) combined with emerging international efforts to launch regulatory fast-track programs for rare cancer precision therapies are beginning to dismantle these long-standing obstacles [45, 46]. These changes point to a reduction in the translational cost, positioning rare cancer patients as those most likely to benefit from next-generation precision medicine.

Patients with rare cancers often have a higher likelihood of being included in clinical trials for new therapies during the advanced stages of their disease, providing valuable treatment opportunities. As a last-line therapy, the patient received GT201 TIL immunotherapy, which resulted in demonstrable interim therapeutic effects (Fig. 3e). The patient achieved a confirmed partial response following TIL infusion. Given the highly aggressive nature of this rare malignancy, this represents a significant and encouraging outcome. However, the patient did not achieve complete remission. Given the tumor’s aggressive and unique nature, standard TIL protocols may be insufficient for such rare cancers. To fully capitalize on the final therapeutic opportunities that clinical trial participation may offer rare cancer patients, it is essential to optimize innovative therapies specifically tailored to such cases. Accordingly, we implemented a personalized optimization strategy for the patient’s TILs.

Conventional TIL expansion protocols often lead to dilution of effective TILs due to loss of the tumor antigen microenvironment [34, 47]. Here, we introduced lesion-specific PTOs into the TIL manufacturing process to recreate an autologous tumor antigen microenvironment (Fig. 4a). This strategy reactivated silent tumor-specific TILs, transiently inducing CD137 expression (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Following sorting and selective expansion of CD137+ cells, we obtained optimized TILs with markedly enhanced tumor-specific cytotoxicity. These PTO-trained TILs not only showed enhanced killing of restimulated PTOs but also exhibited bona fide antitumor activity against autologous tumor cells (Fig. 4b-f). Notably, this approach demonstrated generalizability across patients and tumor types, as evidenced by consistent findings in additional NECC and lung cancer cases (Supplementary Fig. 6). Although a valuable marker, CD137 may not fully capture all tumor-reactive TILs. Exploring other markers or their combinations therefore represents a promising avenue for future research. Thus, PTOs represent a robust and scalable platform for the discovery and production of high-quality tumor-specific TILs. By overcoming clinical limitations in tumor cell availability and enabling large-scale selection and expansion, this platform can improve the potency of adoptive T-cell therapies.

To support future clinical translation of optimized TIL products, we performed integrated scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq to explore the molecular dynamics of PTO-trained TILs throughout tumor and manufacturing processes (Fig. 4g). Upon incubation with autologous PTOs, tumor-reactive TILs exhibited upregulation of CD137 along with key effector molecules such as IFNG, TNF, and GZMB (Fig. S7). TCR lineage tracing revealed that PTO-based enrichment not only reverses T cell exhaustion but also promotes a persistent anti-tumor response, highlighting the therapeutic potential of this optimization strategy (Fig. 4h–j). Furthermore, by longitudinally tracking the identified tumor-specific TCR clonotypes before and after infusion, we gained insights into their kinetics in patient blood and their potential correlation with tumor response (Fig. 4k and l). The specificity of these putative tumor-specific TCRs is currently under validation, and their cognate tumor antigens will be thoroughly investigated to better understand the interactions between the immune system and the tumor, as well as the evolution of tumor antigens during disease progression. This approach may provide insights for implementing patient-based TIL therapy.

Our study has several limitations. The co-culture system lacks incorporation of stromal and other microenvironmental components that critically influence drug response and immune regulation, warranting further model refinement. Additionally, as a retrospective single-case analysis, we were unable to perform real-time clinical intervention despite strong ex vivo predictive results, highlighting the gap between experimental diagnostics and bedside application. To truly benefit rare cancer patients, larger prospective cohort studies with multidisciplinary collaboration are urgently needed.

In conclusion, this work comprehensively addresses current therapeutic challenges and opportunities for rare cancer patients and provides proof-of-concept for PTO-guided longitudinal diagnostics and immune cell optimization. By integrating advanced organoid technology, multi-omics profiling, and novel therapeutics, we propose a conceptual framework for personalized, adaptive treatment in rare cancers. Future efforts should focus on integrating emerging technologies with personalized therapeutic optimization and long-term patient monitoring. Collectively, these strategies hold promise to transform rare cancer patients into true beneficiaries of precision oncology.

Materials and methods

Patient samples processing

For NECC, fresh tumor tissue from patients’ cervical primary lesion was not obtained to construct PTOs, while paraffin-embedded tissue was utilized for genomic sequencing and histopathological evaluation. M1 and M2 Tumor tissues were used for PTO culture, with paraffin-embedded tissues again serving for genomic sequencing and histopathological evaluation. M3 tumor tissue was divided into six portions according to the required amount, which were used for PTO culture, TILs culture, combined single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing, genomic sequencing, transcriptomic sequencing, and histopathological evaluation.

For the additional NECC case, the tissue was divided into three portions for PTO construction, TIL culture, and genomic sequencing, respectively. For the additional lung cancer case, the tissue was divided into two portions for PTO construction and TIL culture.

Post-surgery, the tissue designated for PTO culture was preserved in tumor tissue preservation solution (bioGenous), while tissue for TILs culture was stored in T-cell preservation solution (Stemcell). Both were transported on ice and processed within 24 h for culture and culture. Tissue samples intended for genomic sequencing were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, those for transcriptomic sequencing were placed in RNAlater solution and stored at -80 °C, and tissues for histopathological evaluation were immersed in 4% PFA solution for subsequent procedures. Tissue intended for single-cell sequencing was digested into single cells and generated libraries within 2 h.

For the longitudinally tracked NECC patient with multiple metastases, tissue acquisition complied with the protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Ethics Committee of the Obstetrics & Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University and the IRB of the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Chest Hospital. The collection and processing of samples from the additional NECC case and the lung cancer case were conducted under the same IRB approvals.

Tumor organoid culture

Tumor tissues were minced into 1–3 mm³ fragments in a 6 cm cell culture dish using surgical scissors or scalpels, then digested at 37 °C in a 15 mL conical tube with 400 U/mL collagenase I (Thermo), 10 U/mL dispase type I (Roche), 10 µM Y-27,632 (bioGenous), and 1:500 Primocin (Invitrogen) in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco). The mixture was pipetted vigorously every 15 min until it could pass through 5 mL pipette tips. Digestion was terminated by adding FBS to a final concentration of 2%, and the mixture was filtered through a 100 μm cell strainer. The filtered cells were centrifuged at 200 g for 3 min at 4 °C. If a visible red pellet formed, it was resuspended in 2 mL of Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (Solarbio) at 37 °C for 3 min, then washed twice with Advanced DMEM/F12. The pellet was resuspended in Matrigel at approximately 10,000 cells per 25 µL and plated in drops of ~ 25 µL onto 24-well cell culture plates. The plates were incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ for 20 min to allow the Matrigel to solidify, and then the organoids were overlaid with conditioned culture medium. For NECC, the culture medium contained 1x Advanced DMEM/F12, 100 U/mL Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco), 1x GlutaMax (Gibco), 10 mM HEPES (Gibco), 1x B27 supplement (Gibco), 1.25 mM N-Acetylcysteine (Sigma), 5 mM nicotinamide (Sigma), 500 ng/mL human recombinant R-spondin1 (bioGenous), 100 ng/mL human recombinant Noggin (bioGenous), 25 ng/mL human recombinant FGF-7 (bioGenous), 100 ng/mL human recombinant FGF-10 (bioGenous), 50 ng/mL EGF (bioGenous), 500 nM A83-01 (bioGenous), 500 nM SB202190 (bioGenous), and 5 µM Y-27,632 (bioGenous). Organoids typically formed within two weeks and were passaged every 1–2 weeks by incubating in Organoid Dissociation Solution (bioGenous) at 37 °C for 1–2 min to induce mechanical disintegration, followed by washing and replating in Matrigel. Organoids were cryopreserved in Organoid Cryopreservation Medium (bioGenous), and only organoids with fewer than 10 passages were used for experiments.

TILs preparation

Tumor tissues from the patient were processed and TIL product was expanded as previously described [48]. GT201 is an autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy product that is genetically engineered to express a membrane-bound IL-15Rα/IL-15 complex to further promote the persistence and cytotoxicity of TIL product.

Histological analysis and immunofluorescence study

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by dehydration and embedding in paraffin. To prepare organoids for pathological identification, those cultured in Matrigel were washed and collected at the bottom of tubes, then fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h. After fixation, organoids were washed with 1x PBS and resuspended in 50 µL of warm 3% agarose, followed by dehydration and embedding in paraffin. Sections were cut and hydrated before staining. Standard protocols for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunofluorescent staining were employed. For immunostaining, samples were incubated overnight with primary antibodies: Synaptophysin (abcam), Chromogranin A (abcam), Ki-67 (BD Biosciences), and P16INKa (abcam). Secondary antibodies labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 115-545-003, 3 µg/mL) or Cy3 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111-165-003, 6 µg/mL) were incubated for 1 h. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (ASGB-BIO). Images were captured using the cellSens system of a brightfield microscope (Guangmi, XSP-9CP) or a confocal microscope (Leica, DMi8).

Grading of drug sensitivity

NECC PTOs were digested and filtered through a 70 μm strainer. A small portion of the total sample was dissociated into single cells to estimate cellular density. The remaining cell clusters were measured and resuspended in Matrigel at a density of 5000 cells/µL. Subsequently, 3 µL drops of the cell cluster suspension were dispensed into 96-well plates, followed by the addition of 100 µL of culture medium to each well. Different drugs were added to the organoid cultures after 7 days of growth. After an additional 7 days of drug treatment, cell viability was assessed using the CCK8 Assay (APExBIO). All drugs were dissolved in DMSO, except for carboplatin and oxaliplatin, which were prepared in PBS, as DMSO would inhibit their biological activity. A consistent final DMSO concentration of 0.1% (v/v) was maintained across all experimental conditions. As cyclophosphamide and capecitabine are prodrugs, we directly used their bioactive metabolites (phosphoramide mustard and 5-FU) in experiments. Key trends were reproduced in two independent repeats, using PTOs from relatively early-stage (P1-P5) and relatively late-stage (P5-P10) of in vitro culture, respectively.

Results were normalized to vehicle controls (100%) and baseline controls (without adding culture medium). Drug sensitivity curves were generated and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration values (IC50) were calculated using GraphPad Prism 8 software with non-linear regression. Subsequently, the IC50 values were normalized by the geometric mean IC50 of cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC) in the GDSC database. A -log10 transformation of these normalized IC50 values was performed to obtain a drug sensitivity score. Scores greater than 2 were classified as grade 1; scores between 1 and 2 were classified as grade 2; and scores below 1 were classified as grade 3.

Xenotransplantation of tumor organoids

Female NOG mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The mice were housed in a temperature- and light-controlled specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with a 12-hour light/dark cycle, in individually ventilated cages alongside mice of similar age. The mice used in this study were 6 to 8 weeks old.

On the day of transplantation, NECC M3 PTOs were released from Matrigel, and a small portion of the total sample was dissociated into single cells to estimate the cellular density. Approximately 100,000 cells were suspended in 100 µL of medium mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio, and this mixture was subsequently injected into the right axilla of the NOG mice. The mice were monitored twice a week for tumor growth. Tumor volumes were estimated using the formula: tumor volume = (length × width²) / 2, where length represents the largest tumor diameter and width represents the perpendicular tumor diameter. Tumors were harvested and passaged to another batch of NOG mice once their sizes reached approximately 500 mm³. Tumors that underwent two additional passages were stored in liquid nitrogen for future experiments.

For the treatment of PTOX tumors, 6-8-week-old female NOG mice with similar-sized tumors were randomly divided into groups of four. They received treatment with vehicle (0.9% injectable saline, 1 mg/kg, i.p. b.i.w.), a TC regimen (Paclitaxel, 10 mg/kg and Carboplatin, 50 mg/kg, i.p. q.w.), or Afatinib (20 mg/kg, p.o. q.d.). After three weeks of treatment, the tumors were excised, and the mice were euthanized in accordance with the protocols of the Laboratory Animal Research Center at Fudan University. Subsequently, the harvested tumors were used for volume and weight measurements, as well as histopathological staining.

PTO-TIL co-culture system and tumor cells detection

Before co-culture, PTOs were stimulated with 200 ng/mL IFN-γ for 24 h. The next day, PTOs were isolated from Matrigel using 2 mg/mL Dispase and filtered through a 70 μm strainer. A portion of the PTOs was then dissociated into single cells and counted using a hemocytometer, allowing for the accurate determination of tumor cell numbers for co-culture with TILs at a specific effector: target (E: T) ratio.

To assess the cytotoxic activity of TILs against PTOs via flow cytometric analysis, PTOs were stained with carboxyfluorescein succinimidylester (CFSE) (Yeasen) in PBS for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by blocking with human serum and washing with PBS. TILs were then seeded into U-bottom 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and co-cultured with PTOs at a 2:1 E: T ratio in T cell medium. After 24 h of co-culture, both PTOs and TILs were collected, dissociated into single cells using TrypLE Express, and stained with Annexin V-647/PI apoptosis staining solution for 15 min. In some cases, TILs were labeled with BV421 Mouse Anti-Human CD3 antibody (BD Pharmingen). For accurate quantification, 7 μm 123count™ eBeads Counting Beads (Invitrogen) were added before recording on a Becton Dickinson Fortessa.

To evaluate the infiltration and cytotoxicity of TILs against PTOs using bright-field and fluorescent imaging analysis, PTOs were stained with CellTrace-Red CMTPX (Yeasen), while TILs were labeled with CellTrace Violet (Invitrogen) in PBS for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by blocking with human serum and washing with PBS. TILs were seeded into glass-bottom 384-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 104 cells per well and co-cultured with tumor organoids at a 1:1 E: T ratio in T cell medium. After 48 h of co-culture, SuperView 488 Caspase-3 substrates (Yeasen) were added to visualize cells undergoing apoptosis. Bright-field and fluorescent images were captured using the cellSens system of a brightfield microscope (Guangmi, XSP-9CP) or a confocal microscope (Leica, DMi8).

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of TILs against PTOs using IncuCyte, autologous PTOs were incubated with TILs at an E: T ratio of 1:1 in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 5% human serum for 24 h. Images were captured every 90 min, and the number of apoptotic cells per mm² was quantified using the SuperView™ 488 Caspase-3 Assay Kit for Live Cells in conjunction with the IncuCyte® S3 Platform (Sartorius, Germany). The reagent was cleaved by activated Caspase-3/7 upon target cell apoptosis, resulting in the release of the dye and green fluorescent staining of nuclear DNA. The imaging data were analyzed using the Basic Analyzer of IncuCyte software.

These assays were independently repeated in three expansion batches for the index patient.

Immune cells detection of TILs

After the expansion stage of TILs, tumor reactivity of TILs was defined as follows: PTOs embedded in Matrigel or dissociated tumor single cells were stimulated overnight with 20 ng/mL human recombinant IFN-γ (ACROBiosystems). The next day, TILs and IFN-γ-treated dissociated PTOs or tumor single cells were collected and washed twice with RPMI 1640 containing 5% human serum, and then co-cultured at a ratio of 4:1 in RPMI 1640 with 5% human serum for 6 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. In the context of Intracellular Staining (ICS), Golgi Stop (BD), Golgi Plug (BD), and anti-CD107a BV421 (BioLegend) were added during co-culture. Cells were stained with two antibody panels as follows: CD137 Panel: CD3 Percp (BioLegend), CD4 AF750 (eBioscience), CD8 BV785 (BioLegend), eBioscience™ Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (eBioscience), CD137 PE (BioLegend), CD69 BV421 (BioLegend), and PD-1 PE-Cy7 (BD); ICS Panel: CD3 Percp (BioLegend), CD4 AF750 (eBioscience), CD8 BV785 (BioLegend), eBioscience™ Fixable Viability Dye eFluor™ 780 (eBioscience), IFN-γ PE (BD), TNF-α PE-Cy7 (BD), and CD107a BV421 (BioLegend). After co-culture, 100 µL of supernatant from each well of the CD137 Panel was collected for further cytokine analysis via Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells from both panels were collected and stained following the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometric data were acquired using a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (BECKMAN), and FACS data were analyzed with FLOWJO Version 10.4.0 software (TREESTAR). These assays were repeated in more than one expansion batch in the patient.

Optimization of TIL expansion by PTO training

PTOs embedded in Matrigel were stimulated overnight with 20 ng/mL human recombinant IFN-γ (ACROBiosystems). The following day, PTOs were dissociated into single cells using Organoid Dissociation Solution (bioGenous) and incubated with TILs at an E: T ratio of 50:1 in X-VIVO15 (Lonza), supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, IL-2 (100 U/mL) (ACROBiosystems), IL-15 (10 ng/mL) (ACROBiosystems), IL-21 (10 ng/mL) (ACROBiosystems), 1:100 penicillin/streptomycin, and 10% male human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. TILs without PTO training served as a control. On day 2, PTO-specific CD137 expression was confirmed via flow cytometric analysis, and CD137+ cells were isolated using a CD137 MicroBead kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi). Sorted CD137+/− TILs were rested and expanded in expansion medium (X-VIVO15 supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, IL-2 (1000 U/mL) (ACROBiosystems), IL-15 (10 ng/mL) (ACROBiosystems), IL-21 (10 ng/mL) (ACROBiosystems), 1:100 penicillin/streptomycin, and 10% male human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich) for 7–14 days at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until functional assessment. Unsorted TILs after incubation were expanded as a control.

TILs infusion

Before cell infusion, the patient underwent a non-myeloablative, lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen consisting of cyclophosphamide (300 mg/m²) and fludarabine (25 mg/m²) administered over 3 days. Following this, the patient received a single infusion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, followed by interleukin-2 administered every 12 h. Following TIL infusion, adverse events (AEs) were recorded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Efficacy was evaluated using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1.

Patient peripheral blood and serum analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients to monitor the following TIL subsets: CD45+CD3+, CD3+IL-15α+, CD4+CD137+, and CD8+CD137+ at baseline (6 days prior to infusion), 1-day pre-infusion, and at 3-, 7-, and 21-days post-infusion. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC Lysis Buffer (BD). TILs or peripheral blood immune cells were counted with the NucleoCounter NC-200 (ChemoMetec) for absolute number calculations. Flow cytometric data were acquired using a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (BECKMAN) and analyzed with FLOWJO Version 10.4.0 software (TREESTAR).

The concentration of serum cytokines, including IFN-γ, was measured at baseline, 1-day pre-infusion, and at 3-, 7-, and 21-days post-infusion using three commercially available kits: V-PLEX Proinflammatory Panel (human) 1 Kits, V-PLEX Cytokine Panel (human) 1 Kits, and V-PLEX Chemokine Panel (human) 1 Kits.

Whole-genome sequencing and data analysis

To align the DNA sequences to the human reference genome, Bowtie2 (v.2.3.5.1) analysis was performed with the --very-sensitive option, and GRCh38 was used as the human reference genome [49]. To generate high-quality initial whole-genome sequencing data, the GATK (v.4.5.0.0) pipeline was used; after running gatk MarkDuplicates, gatk BaseRecalibrator, and gatk ApplyBQSR, we generated recalibrated BAM files (BQSR.bam) for further analysis.

To identify somatic mutations, gatk Mutect2 was used with the PBMC sample serving as the normal reference, allowing us to call somatic mutations in the tumor samples [50]. To ensure high-confidence variant calls, we applied gatk FilterMutectCalls and retained variants labeled as PASS with a minimum allele fraction greater than 0.1. Additionally, somatic mutations were called using Strelka (v.2.9.10) with the same tumor/normal sample set and reference genome [51]. The gatk Mutect2 and Strelka results were then intersected, and mutations within exonic regions were extracted for extra reliability. Finally, these filtered mutations were annotated with gene information by gatk Funcotator (dataSources.v1.7.20200521s). Mutations were compared to COSMIC, SMG, BERT, and NE-associated cancer gene databases [26–29]. Genes that shared in tumor-PTO pairs were combined into a gene list for generating an oncoplot.

To detect and analyze copy number variations across samples, copy number variations were analyzed with CNVkit (v.0.9.6) using the batch command for each sample, with the PBMC sample serving as the reference [52]. Whole-genome sequencing was specified by parameter: -m wgs, while refFlat.txt provided by the official website was used as the gene annotation database.

To study the clonal evolution of cancer, allelic counts, copy number, and purity data were gathered. First, interval files from each sample’s filtered Mutect2 VCF result were generated by using gatk PreprocessIntervals, then the intervals were merged into a unified BED file. Next, we ran gatk CollectAllelicCounts to count reference and alternate allele counts upon this specific region. PURPLE analysis was performed to obtain major and minor copy number as well as the purity of each sample. Afterwards, we ran a custom script to combine these data across all samples into a single input file for PyClone-VI (v.0.1.4) [53]. The parameters used were: -r 10, -c 30, -g 100, and -d binomial.

To identify mutational signatures, SigProfilerExtractor (v.0.0.5.77) was applied to the aforementioned, intersected VCF file of each tumor sample or cluster separately, generating SBS96 signature decomposition plots [54].

Bulk RNA sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA of M1 PTOs, M2 PTOs, M3 PTOs, and M3 Tumor were isolated using the RNAprep Pure Micro Kit (Tiangen Biotech) and then sent for sequencing (Novogene). High-throughput sequencing was performed using Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. The raw sequencing data quality was checked by FastQC. HISAT2 was then used to align clean reads to the human reference genome (GRCh38) with default parameters. Bam files were sorted by Samtools and count matrices were generated by StringTie.

The RNA-seq data of CESCs were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The RNA-seq data of NECs were obtained from the EBI ArrayExpress database following the accession number E-MTAB-11,599. The RNA-seq data of SCLCs were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database following the accession number GSE60052. Downstream analysis of RNA-seq from our experiments and RNA-seq of 9 CESCs, 9 NECs, and 7 SCLCs were processed in R v4.2.1. The expression files underwent the transformation into transcripts permillion (TPM), normalization, and PCA. Visualization of log10-transformed TPM was generated by pheatmap (version 1.0.12). The immune-related and stromal scores were predicted by ESTIMATE (Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data).

Single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing and data analysis

Combined single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing was performed on four samples (including fresh M3 tumor tissue, GT201 preREP TILs, GT201 TIL product and PTO-trained TILs).

The scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the ChromiumTM Single cell 5′ Reagent Kit of Chromium platform (10x Genomics, California, USA) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Generated scRNA-seq libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform. The CellRanger software (version 3.1.0) was used for the preprocessing of the PE150 Illumina sequencing reads. The “Seurat” package (version 4.3.0.1) was used to process and analyze single cell RNA sequencing data. We filtered cells with > 15% mitochondrial gene content and those with < 200 genes. We also discarded cells with expression of CD3D = 0. After filtering outlier cells, the remaining cells from 4 samples were utilized for PCA and unsupervised clustering.

The CellRanger software (version 3.1.0) was used for the preprocessing of the PE150 Illumina sequencing reads. The “scRepertoire” package (version 1.6.0) was used to combine scTCR-seq data to “Seurat” object of scRNA-seq data.

Bulk TCR sequencing and data analysis

We used MixCR to investigate bulk RNA-seq data by reconstructing T cell repertoires. The TCR sequences were filtered by following steps: (a) sequences coding the same CDR3 protein sequence were pooled together. (b) out-frame sequences were filtered out. (c) sequences with only 1 single read were filtered out. The “immunarch” package (version 0.7.0) was used to analyze the remaining TCR sequences.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests and one- or two-way ANOVA (GraphPad Prism 8). RNA-seq normalization, PCA, and heatmap visualization were implemented in R (v4.2.1). Flow cytometry data were quantified using FLOWJO (v10.4.0). Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated, with significance thresholds set as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
