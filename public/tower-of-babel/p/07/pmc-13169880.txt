Anti-Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) antibodies in EBV-associated gastric carcinoma
Authors: Chihaya Koriyama, Lusheng Song, Ji Qiu, Joshua LaBaer, Athira Nandakumar, Suminori Akiba, Charles S. Rabkin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAbout 10% of gastric carcinomas (GC) harbor monoclonal Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). Anti-EBV antibody profiles distinguished patients with EBV-associated GC (EBVaGC) from those with EBV-negative GC (EBVnGC) in a previous study. Therefore, we examined these markers in a larger cohort with patient samples from Japan, a high gastric cancer-risk population.MethodsMulti-microbial Nucleic Acid Programmable Protein Array was used to test stored sera from 102 EBVaGC and 131 EBVnGC patients.ResultsAmong 89 IgG antibodies against EBV-specific antigens, 32 (36%) showed at least 10% seroprevalence in either EBVaGC or EBVnGC patients. Seropositivity of ten anti-EBV antibodies (LF2, BDLF2, BXLF1, BRLF1, BKRF3, BHRF1, BORF2, BZLF1, BNRF1, and BALF2) were significantly higher in EBVaGC patients than those with EBVnGC, with a maximal odds ratio of 22 (95% confidence interval: 5.2, 195) for anti-LF2. The ten antibodies collectively had an area under the receiver operating curve of 0.747 (95% CI: 0.683, 0.811). Five of these markers (LF2, BRLF1, BXLF1, BORF2, and BALF2) were among the seven discriminatory antibodies identified in the previous study. These differences in serological reactions were seen for proteins in the lytic infection phase, but not for those in the latent infection phase, consistent with the previous results.ConclusionThe present study confirms the utility of anti-EBV serology for non-invasive detection of EBVaGC.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-026-00753-8.

Introduction

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a ubiquitous virus, has been causally associated with some malignant diseases, including Burkitt lymphoma, undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), Hodgkin lymphoma, and immunosuppression-related lymphoma [1]. Although at least 90% of individuals worldwide acquire infection with this virus in childhood, mainly through salivary contact, most remain asymptomatic carriers through life. An in situ hybridization (ISH) assay of EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER), which became available in the early 1990s, has elucidated the presence of EBV in some cases of gastric carcinoma (GC) [2]. Gastric cancer tissue showing homogenous EBER expression in tumor cells is considered to represent EBV-associated GC (EBVaGC) and about 10% of GCs harbor EBV [3]. The etiological involvement of EBV in gastric carcinogenesis has been suspected from monoclonality of the viral DNA and the uniform expression of EBER in gastric cancer cells but not in the surrounding normal epithelial cells [4]. Unlike EBV-positive lymphomas, EBVaGC rarely exhibits deletions of viral genes [5], emphasizing that EBV infection itself is critically important from the earliest stages of tumorigenesis.

Although the detailed mechanism of EBVaGC carcinogenesis is not clear yet, its unique clinico-pathological features indicated a distinct entity of GC. EBVaGC is predominant in male cases and tumors of non-antrum and diffuse type [3]. EBV-positivity is particularly high with lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (LELC) histology, more than 90% [6].

Song et al. [7] have reported distinctive EBV-specific humoral immune responses in EBVaGC patients but not in EBV-negative GC (EBVnGC) patients, which were mainly responses to the lytic cycle immediate-early and early antigens. These findings suggest that some EBV-specific IgG antibodies distinguish between EBVaGC and EBVnGC. Furthermore, the results in EBVaGC were different from the antibody response in patients with other EBV-related cancers such as NPC and lymphoma where humoral response is primary directed against late lytic antigens [8–10]. Compared to the search for EBER using gastric cancer tissue, antibody testing in blood may be simpler, faster, and more useful for screening for EBVaGC. Thus, utility of this antibody test warrants further investigation using blood samples from other populations.

In this study, we examined EBV-specific humoral immune response signatures using long-term stored serum obtained from Japanese patients with EBVaGC and EBVnGC.

Study subjects and methods

Study subjects

The subjects of this study were GC patients who participated in previous studies in Kagoshima, Japan during the 1990s [11, 12] and for whom blood samples had been stored (Supplementary Table S1). The inclusion criteria were patients with the information of EBER status in GC specimens by ISH and sufficient serum volume remaining for antibody testing. We were able to retrieve blood samples from 102 EBVaGC and 131 EBVnGC patients. Of the 233 patients, 164 (70.4%) were male and 69 (29.6%) were female. The median (range) age was 67 (29–89) years for EBVaGC patients and 66 (28–94) years for EBVnGC patients. The aliquoted serum specimens had been stored at −80 degrees until use. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Kagoshima University Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences.

Histology

Gastric carcinomas were originally classified according to the classification scheme of the Japanese Research Society for Gastric Cancer [13]. Briefly, histologic patterns were considered as follows: well-differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma (tub1), moderately-differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma (tub2), solid poorly-differentiated adenocarcinoma (por1), non-solid poorly-differentiated adenocarcinoma (por2), signet-ring cell carcinoma (sig), and mucinous carcinoma (muc). For the present analysis, we considered tub1, tub2, and muc histologies to represent intestinal-type tumors and por1, por2 and sig histologies to represent diffuse-type tumors, according to the Lauren classification [13].

The location of tumor defined as the predominant site was divided into the following three sites: cardia or upper third part, middle part, and antrum or lower third part according to the guidelines of the Japanese Research Society for Gastric Cancer [14]. The depth of invasion was classified as mucosal, submucosal, muscularis propria, subserosal, serosa externa, and serosal invasion. A tumor which is confined to the mucosal or submucosal tissue is defined as early cancer. A tumor invading beyond the submucosa is considered to be advanced cancer [14].

ISH assay to detect EBER

EBV status in gastric tumors was determined by ISH in previous studies. The ISH assay of paraffin-embedded tissue samples obtained from the main tumor was conducted using a digoxigenin-labeled EBER-1 oligonucleotide probe as described before [2]. A case was considered to be EBER-positive based on an intense nuclear dark purple signal under microscopy. In every ISH assay, lymph node section from a patient with infectious mononucleosis and a sense probe for EBER-1 were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Detection of EBVaGC-associated antibody by NAPPA

The procedure for detecting antibodies using NAPPA is the same as that reported in previous studies [15, 16]. In brief, 89 EBV open reading frames (ORFs) from 85 total unique proteins for type-1 EBV (B95-8) and 203 ORFs from H. pylori, along with 35 ORFs from several other microbes were included [17]. As with the previous study, there were ORFs from immediate-early lytic (n = 2), early lytic (n = 31), late lytic (n = 32), and latent (n = 12) phases, and 12 ORFs are unknown phase [7]. The antibodies against latent proteins examined in this study were as follows: LMP-1, LMP-2A, LMP-2B, EBNA-LP, EBNA-1, EBNA-2, EBNA-3A, EBNA-3B, EBNA-3C, BARF0, RPMS1, and A73. All clones were obtained from DNASU in a NAPPA compatible pANT7-cGST expression vector [18, 19]. Details of the antibody assays were reported elsewhere [7]. During the measurement process, the EBER status of each subject was blinded.

Antibody responses on NAPPA were analyzed as Median Normalized Intensity (MNI) by dividing by the median signal intensity of all proteins in each array. Seropositive responses among Japanese GCs were defined as MNI > 1.4 [16]. Antibodies that showed at least 10% seropositivity in either EBVaGC or EBVnGC were assessed for discrimination between these groups as reported in the previous study [7].

Statistical analyses

The difference in quantitative antibody response on NAPPA and differences between numbers of seropositive antibodies in EBVaGC and EBVnGC were assessed by the Mann-Whitney U test. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by logistic regression models.

Results

Positive frequency of EBV-specific antibodies in EBVaGC and EBVnGC

The NAPPA contains 89 antibodies against EBV-specific antigens, and among them, 32 antibodies (36%) showed at least 10% seropositivity in either EBVaGC or EBVnGC (Supplementary Table S2). Twenty-seven of these 32 antibodies were detected in both EBVaGC and EBVnGC. Four additional antibodies, against BXLF1, BVRF1, BDLF2, and LF2, were found to present more than 10% seropositivity only for EBVaGC and one, anti-BILF2, only for EBVnGC.

Odds ratio of EBVaGC by presence of specific anti-EBV and H. pylori antibodies

Among 327 antibodies including other pathogens, 81 antibodies (24.8%) showed at least 10% seropositivity in either EBVaGC or EBVnGC. Discussion

The present study analyzed the humoral immune reaction among Japanese patients with GC using long-term stored blood sera. EBVaGC patients were more likely to be positive for several EBV-specific antibodies compared to EBVnGC patients. The current study confirmed the usefulness of five of the seven discriminatory antibodies identified in the previous study [7] among GC patients with a different ethnic background. Furthermore, these serological reactions were observed for immediate-early lytic, early lytic, late lytic and unknown phase proteins, consistent with the previous results in Latvian GCs [7]. These findings strongly support the hypothesis that EBV lytic infection occurred in EBVaGC patients although EBVaGC cells are well known to express viral products of latent infection. More notably, evidence has been reported to suggest that lytic replication proteins are also involved in tumor development and progression [20–22].

The present study found ten EBV-specific antibodies (against BRLF1, BZLF1, BALF2, BHRF1, BORF2, BXLF1, BDLF2, BNRF1, BKRF3, and LF2) which had significantly higher positive rates in EBVaGC cases than EBVnGC. ROC analysis revealed that the combination of these ten EBV-specific antibodies was able to predict EBVaGC and its accuracy was moderate (AUC = 0.747) even using long-stored sera.

Five of the above ten antibodies (specifically, anti-LF2, -BRLF1, -BXLF1, -BORF2, and -BALF2) were also included among a total of seven discriminatory IgG antibodies in the previous study [7]. Interestingly, it has also been reported that antibodies to BRLF1, BXLF1, and BALF2 are also increased in the patients with NPC, another EBV-associated epithelial tumor but not in patients with lymphoma [8, 9, 23].

In EBV‑associated lymphomas, abnormalities including deletions, disruptions, and rearrangements of immediate-early/early/late lytic gene regions occur with notable frequency, in contrast to epithelial tumors such as NPC and EBVaGC, where these alterations are seldom observed [24]. This difference may be influencing the blood antibody response. A recent study reported “East-Asian antibody signature of EBV-positive classic Hodgkin lymphoma” and BALF2 is the one of the core signatures in the East-Asian population [25]. In another study among Chinese NPC patients, antibodies to BALF2 were significantly elevated in patients with advanced-stage tumor compared to those with early-stage tumors [22]. However, no such trend was observed in our Japanese EBVaGCs (data not shown).

In analyses conducted by tumor location, the seroprevalence of EBV-specific antibodies was higher for EBVaGC than for EBVnGC in all sites, with the trend being more pronounced for tumors in the non-antrum region (Supplementary Figure S1). By histological types, EBVaGC with diffuse type of tumors showed higher seropositivity than those with intestinal type of tumors (Fig. 3). However, regarding tumor depth, EBV-specific antibody positivity rates in EBVaGC cases were higher than those of EBVnGC in both early-stage and advanced, but no difference was observed between early-stage and advanced tumor. These results except by tumor depth are consistent with previous studies reporting that non-antrum, diffuse type, and early stage of tumors are clinico-pathological features of EBVaCG [3, 26].

Burke et al. [27] first reported the detection of EBV genomes in GC tissue with LELC, a characteristic histological type of NPC. Subsequent research in the early 1990s using ISH method revealed that LELC tissue specimens were almost always EBER-positive [6], and it was suspected that EBV is involved in carcinogenesis, especially in LELC among EBVaGCs. In the present study, five patients with LELC were included, and all of them were male patients with EBER-positive tumors in non-antrum sites. The LELC cases had higher seropositive rates than EBER-positive non-LELC for nine of the above ten antibodies, but not anti-BHRF1 (Supplementary Figure S3). In particular, anti-BZLF1 was positive in all five LELC GCs and three other antibodies (against BALF2, BORF2 and BNRF1) were positive in four of the five cases (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, the case with the highest seropositivity rate (9 out of 10) was the oldest case who was diagnosed in 1983.

The ten antibodies in this study performed with high specificity. For eight of the ten antibodies, except against BZLF1 and BHRF1, the specificity ranged from 67% to 98% (Table 1). Among the antibodies against products of immediate-early lytic stage, only the BRLF1 antibody demonstrated high specificity. On the other hand, BZLF1, another antibody against protein of immediate-early stage, was the most sensitive (80%) despite its low specificity (32%). BZLF1 and BHRF1 are proteins expressed in immediate-early lytic and early lytic phase, respectively. Ant-BZLF1 (ZEBRA) IgG, is one of the candidate markers that was investigated as a possible link to GC [8]. To investigate the reason of low specificity against BZLF1 among Japanese patients, we compared the amino acid sequences of BZLF1 from 95-8 cell line, which was used in NAPPA, and the Akata cell line, which was derived from Japanese individuals (Supplementary Table S4). Amino‑acid substitutions were concentrated in the N‑terminal low‑complexity P/G‑rich region—known to form linear epitopes and to exhibit strain‑dependent antigenicity—and in the C‑terminal basic region. As a consequence of these substitutions, the B95‑8–derived antigen appears to exhibit diminished discrimination by antibodies generated in response to Akata‑strain infection.

It has been reported that early-stage EBVaGC and submucosal invasive cancer have fewer lymph node metastases [28], which may expand the indications for endoscopic submucosal dissection treatment in such cases. Furthermore, PD-L1 and PD-L2 have been found to be amplified in EBVaGCs [29], and immune checkpoint inhibitors may be effective. Antibody testing in blood is simpler and faster than the search for EBER using gastric cancer tissue and may be useful for screening EBVaGC in clinical practice.

In the present study, 32 out of 89 antibodies (36%) showed at least 10% seropositivity in either EBVaGC or EBVnGC. This seroprevalence in Japanese GCs was less than that in the previously reported Latvian GC patients (41 out of 89, 46%) even though the cut-off point was lowered in the present study (2.0 and 1.4 for Latvian and Japanese GC patients, respectively) [7]. However, seroprevalence of individual antibodies was highly correlated between Japanese and Latvian GC groups, in both EBVaGC (r = 0.903) and EBVnGC (r = 0.890) cases (data not shown). Thus, the results of this study are consistent with those of previous studies. Another possible explanation for the sensitivity difference between Latvian and Japanese patients is the antigen used in NAPPA. To assess whether the antigen origin affects sensitivity, the BRLF1 amino acid sequences were compared between B95-8 and Akata cell lines. Only three amino‑acid substitutions were identified between the Akata and B95‑8 BRLF1 (Supplementary Table S5), all located within the C‑terminal low‑complexity region. Because these substitutions lie outside the known functional and immunodominant domains, their impact on antigenicity is expected to be limited; however, minor modulation of epitope presentation cannot be excluded. Furthermore, it has been reported that while the EBV European/American strain is similar to B95-8 [30], the Asian strain exhibits high diversity, including in the early lytic region, and differs significantly from B95-8 [31]. Consequently, EBV antibody testing using the B95-8 antigen may have exhibited reduced antibody detection sensitivity in Asian populations.

Although the present study examined a relatively large number of GC patients, there are some limitations. First, the overall sensitivity was low, perhaps because the sera had been stored for more than 30 years. Nevertheless, blood specimens obtained from both EBVaGC and EBVnGC were preserved under the same conditions. Therefore, we believe that internal validity is secured. Second, the blood specimens were obtained after clinical diagnosis, and we cannot deny the possibility that the antibody titer in the blood was affected by the clinical stage of the disease. However, when limited to early-stage GC, six of the ten antibodies identified in this study still showed a high positive rate in EBVaGC cases, so the effect of clinical stage may be limited. Lastly, we could not verify whether the EBVaGC patients had a history of other EBV‑associated cancers because these are patients more than 30 years ago. Although some patients may have had cancers at other sites, the likelihood that these were EBV‑associated is low in Japan. Thus, it is highly unlikely that the elevated antibody titers observed in EBVaGC patients were influenced by other EBV‑associated malignancies.

Conclusion

The present study confirms the usefulness of the discriminating EBV-specific antibodies identified in the previous study in GC patients with a different ethnic background. However, antibody specificity may vary with the distribution of prevalent strains. Furthermore, these antibodies are directed against proteins expressed during lytic infection, indicating that lytic infection occurs in patients with EBVaGC, a result that strongly supports the involvement of EBV in the carcinogenic process in the stomach.
