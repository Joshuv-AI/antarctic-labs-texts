Reconstruction of complex distal humeral bone loss with osteoarticular massive allograft, latissimus dorsi flap, and ligament–tendon allograft reconstruction: a technical note
Authors: Ammar Ghabi, Cyril Guetari, Diane Di Gioia, Thibaut Battut, Hugo Caso, Laurent Mathieu, Bernard de Geofroy
Published: 2026

Massive bone loss and severe soft tissue damage of the distal humerus following ballistic trauma represent a major challenge in limb reconstructive surgery.3,12 The complexity of these injuries lies in the combination of extensive bone destruction, a high likelihood of contamination, skin-coverage defects, and associated ligament damage, which often limits reconstruction options.1,6 Conventional strategies such as internal fixation, total elbow arthroplasty, or arthrodesis are often unsuitable for young, active patients because of the high risk of mechanical failure or insufficient bone stock,13 infection,11 or functional limitation.5 In such cases, biological reconstruction remains the preferred option when preservation of limb length and joint mobility is desired. Osteoarticular allografts restore bone stock and joint anatomy, but their success depends on optimal ligament reconstruction in cases involving articular damage. Combining the allograft with a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap provides reliable coverage for extensive soft tissue defects, which are consistently present in war-related injuries. This report presents the first description of a single-stage reconstruction of ballistic distal humeral bone loss using a massive allograft, ligament reconstruction, and a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap, along with early outcomes in 2 patients.

Description of the technique

Débridement, necrosectomy, and bacteriological sampling

The first step in the context of war-related trauma is extensive débridement of all nonviable bone and soft tissues, along with bacteriological sampling, followed by a 3-week antibiotic-free interval2 (Fig. 1).

Patient positioning and preparation

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position, with the arm resting on a sterile support. A midline posterior incision, extended proximally, allowed full exposure of the distal humerus, olecranon, and radial head.

Preparation of the allograft

A fresh-frozen, nonirradiated distal humerus allograft from the national bone bank was thawed in sterile saline (37°C, 30 minutes). Its size was adjusted according to pre-operative computed tomography measurements of the contralateral limb. A step-cut osteotomy was performed to ensure a precise and stable fit with the proximal humerus. An allogenic Achilles tendon was prepared for triceps reconstruction and for reinforcement of the medial and lateral ligamentous structures (Figs. 2 and 3).

Preparation of the recipient site

After thorough débridement of necrotic tissues and debris, the healthy bone ends were exposed. The ulnar, radial, and median nerves were identified and protected. The humeral stump was recut following the same osteotomy pattern as the allograft.

Fixation

The allograft was inserted using a primary press-fit technique. Compression was achieved with 2 3.5-mm screws, supplemented by dual locking plates spanning at least 10 cortices on each side of the graft–host junction.

Ligament and extensor mechanism reconstruction

A fresh-frozen allograft (Achilles) was used for all ligament reconstructions. The anatomical footprints of the collateral ligaments were identified on the osteoarticular allograft at the medial and lateral epicondyles. Suture anchors were placed at the corresponding humeral isometric points. The lateral collateral ligament complex was reconstructed first, fixed proximally at the lateral epicondyle and distally at the supinator crest using anchors. Fixation was performed with the elbow at 30° of flexion in neutral rotation, applying slight valgus stress to restore posterolateral stability. The medial collateral ligament was then reconstructed and fixed to the medial epicondyle and the sublime tubercle of the ulna. Tensioning was adjusted at 60° of elbow flexion under varus–valgus stability testing. Triceps reconstruction involved distal fixation to the olecranon through transosseous tunnels with nonabsorbable Krackow sutures at approximately 60° of flexion.

Soft tissue coverage

A pedicled latissimus dorsi flap was harvested through a postero-axillary incision, preserving the thoracodorsal pedicle. The flap was tunneled subcutaneously to the elbow, wrapped around the graft–host junction, and sutured to the reconstructed triceps to restore the extensor mechanism and provide immediate vascularized soft tissue coverage.

Post-operative course

Patient 1 required a hinged external fixator for 6 weeks due to immediate post-operative instability. Patient 2 was immobilized at 90° of flexion for 7 days, followed by protected mobilization with an articulated brace. Active motion began at 6 weeks, and muscle strengthening started at 10 weeks once early callus formation was observed.

Results

Both patients were reviewed at a mean follow-up of 14 months (12 and 16 months). In both cases, complete osseous union at the graft–host junction was achieved between 8 and 10 months, with no evidence of lysis or nonunion. Patient 1 experienced immediate post-operative lateral instability due to transosseous anchor avulsion of the lateral collateral ligament reconstruction, requiring a hinged external fixator for 6 weeks. Patient 2 developed an early post-operative infection, effectively managed with 2 surgical débridements and prolonged antibiotic therapy, without graft loss. No neurological complications or flap necrosis were observed. The final range of motion ranged from 50° to 130° of flexion, with both patients reporting satisfactory functional recovery (Table I and Fig. 4).

Discussion

Rationale and comparison with the literature

Reconstruction of massive distal humeral bone loss following ballistic trauma represents a major challenge due to the combined destruction of bone, joint structures, and soft tissues. Conventional strategies such as internal fixation, arthrodesis, or total elbow arthroplasty are generally not applicable in the cases presented. Amputation remains a last-resort option in situations of uncontrollable infection or non-reconstructible soft tissue loss, but it is often poorly accepted by young patients.9 Osteoarticular allografts have been reported as an alternative for distal humerus reconstruction after tumor resection or closed trauma, allowing restoration of bone stock and joint anatomy while preserving limb length and retaining a degree of functional potential.4,10 However, no published work has reported cases related to war-related trauma with concomitant soft tissue loss. To our knowledge, this is the first description of a single-stage reconstruction combining a massive osteoarticular allograft with a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap for ballistic distal humeral bone loss.

Advantages and limitations

The main advantage of this approach lies in the ability to perform bone, ligamentous, and soft tissue reconstruction in a single operative stage. The pedicled latissimus dorsi flap is particularly well suited for elbow coverage, providing substantial tissue volume, high vascular reliability, and minimal donor-site morbidity. This technique also preserves humeral bone stock, articular congruence, and the potential for functional recovery. However, it is a technically demanding and lengthy procedure that requires close coordination between orthopedic and plastic surgery teams. Its main limitations include the risks of infection, instability, graft resorption, or secondary lysis, as well as late degenerative changes related to chondrocyte necrosis and the partial revascularization of the allograft. It must be emphasized that osteoarticular allografts do not fully incorporate biologically: osseous union is typically achieved at the graft–host junction, while the central portion of the graft undergoes only partial revascularization.8 Moreover, the articular cartilage remains nonviable and may progressively deteriorate over time, potentially leading to secondary degenerative changes. In our series, an early post-operative infection occurred but was successfully managed with 2 surgical débridements, without graft loss. Long-term follow-up is necessary to assess the durability of the osteogenic junction and the evolution of the elbow articular cartilage. Nevertheless, the small sample size and short follow-up period limit the generalizability of these results. An alternative in selected cases is pedicled bone grafting or arthrodesis, which, although functionally less “brilliant,” may provide a more durable biological solution with permanent joint fusion once solid bony union is achieved.7

Clinical relevance

This combined approach is particularly relevant in high-energy ballistic injuries with massive bone loss in young patients with high functional demands. In such cases, this reconstruction may be considered a temporary functional preservation strategy, aimed at maintaining joint motion and bone stock for as long as possible. However, this strategy should not be regarded as definitive. Given the biological limitations of osteoarticular allografts and the risk of long-term degenerative changes, secondary surgical procedures (such as arthrodesis or arthroplasty) may eventually be required. Importantly, the reconstruction described does not preclude or compromise potential future salvage procedures. Residual limitations in extension may occur and are likely multifactorial, related to extensive soft tissue reconstruction, post-operative fibrosis, and the prioritization of stability in this complex setting.

Conclusion

Single-stage reconstruction of massive distal humeral bone loss using a combined osteoarticular allograft, ligament reconstruction, and a pedicled latissimus dorsi flap offers a reliable option for young patients with complex ballistic injuries. In our 2 cases, this biological approach provided stable bone union, relatively satisfactory elbow motion, and durable soft-tissue coverage despite post-operative challenges. Although longer follow-up and larger cohorts are required, this technique appears to be a valuable reconstructive alternative when preservation of limb length and function is desired.
