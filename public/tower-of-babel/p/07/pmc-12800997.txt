Lower Trapezius Transfer: Technical Tips for Tendon Harvesting and Arthroscopic Achilles Allograft Fixation
Authors: Abdul-ilah Hachem, Luis Rojas, Pablo Marcet, Betlem Fargues, Xavi Rius, Bassem Elhassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArthroscopy-assisted lower trapezius transfer is increasingly recognized as a standard approach for addressing irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears. A posterior incision is made along the medial margin of the scapula to identify and dissect the lower trapezius. The Achilles allograft is arthroscopically fixed to the greater tuberosity with a knotless double-row “transosseous-equivalent” repair and to the trapezius using Pulver-Taft technique sutures. The arm should remain immobilized in abduction and neutral rotation for 6 weeks. This procedure enables a dynamic tendon transfer, restoring torque biomechanics and normalizing glenohumeral joint function.

Irreparable rotator cuff tears (RCTs) cause disability and pain, and they restrict daily activities.1 There is no consensus on the best treatment for these lesions.1, 2, 3 The lower trapezius transfer (LTT) has shown multiple advantages over other tendon transfers.2,4,5 This technique provides technical tips and landmarks for dissecting and harvesting the lower trapezius (LT), along with transosseous-equivalent (TOE) double-row fixation, to improve graft healing, enhance footprint contact and compression, and increase load to failure.

Preoperative Planning

Conservative options must be exhausted. Active patients under 65 to 75 years old with disabling shoulder pain, passive flexion >80° to 90° regardless of the lag sign, Hamada <3, and Goutallier ≥3 for the infraspinatus are indications for LTT.2,3,6 Lesions of the spinal accessory nerve, axillary nerve, deltoid deficiency, or uncertain recovery adherence contraindicate LTT.2,3,6

LT Dissection and Preparation

A thorough understanding of the LT’s anatomy is essential (Fig 1). The patient is placed in the beach-chair position with a head and arm holder. Proper placement of the arm holder is crucial, along with identifying skin landmarks. Positioning the arm holder in a protracted position and dissecting the LT tendon less than 1 cm medial to the medial border of the scapula prevents damage to the spinal accessory nerve during tendon harvesting. An incision parallel to the inferior border of the scapular spine is made (Fig 2 A and B). The LT “axilla” is identified by dissecting the subcutaneous and fat tissue, using the finger peel technique or a swab (Fig 3 A and B). The LT tendon is detached from the upper part of the scapular spine tubercle and bluntly dissected from the middle trapezius toward the T4 spinous process, taking care to maximize the tendon’s vertical amplitude (Fig 3C). The tip of the LT tendon is prepared with a Krakow suture using an ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene SutureTape (ST) of 1.3 mm (Arthrex), creating a rip-stop suture (Fig 3D).

Achilles Tendon Allograft Preparation and Fixation

A tendon allograft (AT) provides a broad surface and achieves a TOE double-row fixation. The AT is prepared with 2 STs in different colors. Ultimately, 4 sutures of 2 different colors remain (Fig 4).

Achilles tendon allograft preparation. An Achilles tendon measuring 15 to 20 cm in length provides a broad surface. Using 2 different-colored 1.3-mm SutureTapes (Arthrex), starting at the extremely lateral narrow end of the graft, 2 entry sutures and 2 exit sutures are placed in a Krakow fashion. Ultimately, 4 sutures of 2 different colors remain on the narrow lateral end of the graft, resulting in 4 Krakow sutures of 2 different colors, both on the acromial surface (A) with free sutures on the bone surface (B) of the Achilles allograft.

Concomitant Lesions, Greater Tuberosity, and Tunnel Preparation

The standard posterior portal is used to examine the glenohumeral joint. Debridement of the subacromial bursa is performed, and then any reparable subscapularis tear must be addressed. The long head of the biceps may be preserved, tenotomized, or tenodized using the eyelet hole of the anteromedial anchors placed later. The rotator cuff footprint is prepared using a curette by removing any remaining debris and adding microfractures. The first anchor, preloaded with 2 different-colored ST-4 tails, is placed on the posteromedial (PM) area of the footprint, close to the humeral cartilage, through the Neviaser or the posterolateral (PL) portal (Fig 5A).

AT Passage and Tuberosity Fixation

Four 4.75-mm knotless biocomposite SwiveLock (Arthrex) screw anchors are used (Fig 7A). The Neviaser or the PL (Fig 5A, Fig 7C and D) is used to place the first anchor, preloaded with 2 different-colored ST-4 tails on the PM area of the footprint (Fig 7E). These tails are kept through the Neviaser portal, allowing the AT sutures (Fig 7B) to pass laterally when introduced into the SS (Fig 7F). With the arthroscope through the lateral portal, the free sutures from the AT are grasped and pulled into the SS between the deltoid and ISF using a grasping clamp through the PL portal (Fig 7G). An anterolateral portal (AL) is located 1 to 2 cm lateral to the anterolateral corner of the acromion, while an anteromedial portal (AM) is positioned near the acromioclavicular joint. The AT medial tails are retrieved from the AM portal, and the AT lateral tails are also obtained through the AL portal. The AT is guided into the joint by pulling the sutures (Fig 7G and H). The second anchor, loaded with AT medial sutures, is introduced through the AM portal to place it within the medial bone area of the footprint, behind the bicipital groove and 5 mm lateral to the cartilage (Fig 5B, Fig 7H and I). The eyelet hole of the second anchor can be used for biceps tenodesis. The third anchor, introduced through the AL portal and loaded with the AT lateral sutures and 2 PM anchor tails, is placed in the outer footprint area, posterior to the bicipital groove (Fig 5C, Fig 7J and K). These third anchor tails are cut once the first diagonal cord is secured in the TOE configuration. The fourth anchor is passed through the PL portal, carrying the remaining 2 ST tails from the AM and PM anchors (Fig 5D, Fig 7L), completing a TOE fixation (Fig 7M).

Achilles and LT Tendon Fixation

Positioning the shoulder in abduction and maximum external rotation (Fig 8A), the AT allograft is split longitudinally (Fig 8B-D), and two 1.3 ST sutures are placed on the most lateral part underneath the ISF, one superiorly and the other inferiorly. Then, the AT is rolled over and passed through the LT’s small split hole (Fig 8E), while pulling the allograft along its longitudinal axes. A Pulver-Taft technique fixation is then completed (Fig 8F and G). Additional ST sutures should be utilized to enhance the connection between the AT and LT (Fig 8H and I). The remaining graft can be expanded and used to reinforce the LT (Fig 9A and B). A final subacromial inspection shows the AT’s TOE fixation (Fig 9C).

Postoperative Care

The shoulder stays immobilized in abduction and external rotation for 6 weeks (Fig 10). Immediately, patients begin isometric contractions of the deltoid and scapular retraction exercises. Formal physical therapy is typically initiated between 6 and 8 weeks, incorporating tension band exercises. Between 10 and 12 weeks, patients are allowed to lift heavier weights through the entire range of motion. Most can return to sports and work activities after 5 to 6 months.

Right shoulder. Postoperative care requires 6 weeks of shoulder immobilization in a sling, with a pillow placed at 45° of abduction to support neutral or slightly external rotation, while preventing graft elongation.

Discussion

Since Elhassan proposed LTT for irreparable RCT, it has gained popularity due to its biomechanical advantages.2,5,7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 It has been suggested that LTT can restore range of motion and external rotation strength, even with advanced fatty infiltration of the teres minor.10 Multiple modifications to the original technique have been developed. However, the type of graft fixation to the cuff footprint depends on the surgeon’s preference.

Valenti and Werthel6 used a tunnel to shuttle a cortical button previously sutured to the lateral end of the graft or employed anchors to secure the tendon to the footprint.14 Stone et al.15 described a double-row construct with 4 anchors on the greater tuberosity. Although these studies reported the use of a double-row fixation, neither one described a TOE fixation for the LT graft on the cuff footprint. Ek et al.16 used a suspensory button attached to the AT allograft, with a calcaneal block in a transosseous humeral tunnel, for graft fixation. Baek et al.17 described a double-row suture bridge technique utilizing a fascia lata autograft in an arthroscopic-assisted LTT. The advantages of the double-row/TOE technique in terms of biomechanical stability and healing have already been shown,18, 19, 20, 21 including an increased anatomic contact area, high pressure at the repaired RCT footprint, and a greater ultimate load to failure.22,23 TOE also provides better stiffness distribution across the tendon24 and more effective prevention of synovial fluid leakage than single- and double-row techniques.25,26 TOE also has the lowest retear rate, especially in massive RCTs.27,28 Wang et al.29 assessed 5 suture configurations, referring to the TOE as the “optimal treatment strategy.

The AT fixation technique was modified with a TOE double-row construct for the footprint to improve graft healing by creating a broad, stable contact surface between the graft and bone.
