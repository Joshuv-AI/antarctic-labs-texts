Gluteus Maximus-Tensor Fascia Lata Transfer With Double-Row Repair and Bioinductive Scaffold Augmentation for Chronic Abductor Tendon Tears
Authors: Jorge Sánchez-Mateos, Felipe Casanova, M. Agustina Olaran, Juan Bernardo Villarreal-Espinosa, Melissa L. Carpenter, Germán Escribano, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hip abductor tendon tears are a common cause of lateral hip pain and gait disturbance, especially in older adults. While conservative treatment is first-line, high failure rates often necessitate surgical intervention. Chronic, retracted tears with poor tendon quality pose a surgical challenge. In such cases, transfer of the anterior portion of the gluteus maximus and tensor fascia lata is a viable option. This Technical Note describes the surgical approach for managing chronic, retracted tears combining gluteus maximus-tensor fascia lata transfer and abductor tendon double-row repair with bioinductive collagen scaffold augmentation.

Hip abductor tendon tears, with an incidence of 1.8 per 100,000 person-years, are a significant cause of lateral hip pain, abductor weakness, and gait disturbance, particularly in patients older than 60 years.1,2 Although conservative treatment is standard, failure can still be seen in up to 34% of cases.3 A great proportion of treatment failure occurs in chronic tears, as the remaining tendon tissue is often inadequate for primary repair. In such cases, reconstruction of the tendon is warranted.4 When reconstruction or augmentation is required, allograft or gluteus maximus-tensor fascia lata (TFL) transfer is among the suitable options.2, 3, 4, 5 Furthermore, biologic augmentation with scaffolds or dermal allografts has also been shown to support tendon healing and improve tissue quality via bioinductive mechanisms.6

This Technical Note details the preferred surgical technique of the senior author (J.C.) for surgical management of chronic hip abductor tears. In such cases, the senior author performs a concomitant gluteus maximus and TFL transfer plus a double-row abductor tendon repair augmented with a bioinductive collagen scaffold.

Surgical Technique

A detailed description of the procedure is provided in Video 1.

Patient Positioning

The patient is positioned in a lateral decubitus position on the operating table, secured with a beanbag, while appropriately padding the pressure points of the dependent leg. Hip range of motion is then assessed to confirm unrestricted movement in the established position (Fig 1A). The operative site is prepped and draped in a sterile fashion. A sterile sleeve is placed over the distal surgical limb, followed by the application of an iodine-impregnated surgical drape (Ioban 2 Antimicrobial Incise Drape; 3M). Additionally, a sterile Mayo stand is positioned to assist with leg abduction throughout the procedure (Fig 1B).Fig 1Patient positioning and preparation prior to right-sided hip surgical intervention. Surgical Exposure

Following patient positioning, anatomic landmarks, including the greater trochanter (GT) and femoral shaft, are palpated and marked to guide the surgical approach. The incision site is then marked accordingly (Fig 2A). A longitudinal skin incision measuring 6 to 8 cm is made, centered over the GT and extending distally in the axis of the femoral shaft (Fig 2B).Fig 2(A) Right hip posterior view: anatomic landmarks, including the anterior, posterior, and tip of the greater trochanter (GT) and femoral shaft, are identified. Electrocautery is used for dissection deep toward the iliotibial band. A Cobb elevator is then used to carefully develop the plane between the subcutaneous adipose tissue and the underlying fascia, allowing precise palpation and identification of the borders of the GT. The TFL is identified anteriorly, while the gluteus maximus tendon is visualized over the posterior aspect of the GT. Once the fascial plane is fully developed, the planned flap can be outlined (Fig 3).Fig 3Right-side hip anterior view once the fascial plane is developed. Triangular Flap Dissection

After identifying the gluteus maximus and TFL, the tip and the anterior and posterior margins of the GT are marked. Distally, the apex of the planned flap is defined as a point approximately 1 cm proximal to the insertion of the vastus lateralis. A triangular flap with a proximally based design is then outlined. The anterior edge of the flap is defined by the TFL and its fascia, extending through the most anterior point of the trochanter. The posterior edge extends through the most posterior aspect of the trochanter and continues posteriorly toward the gluteus maximus (Fig 4). Special attention is given to preserve the transverse branch of the lateral femoral circumflex artery, which penetrates the vastus lateralis and curves around the femur just inferior to the GT, anastomosing on the back of the thigh with the medial femoral circumflex artery.Fig 4Illustration of gluteal flap planning in a right hip: line A represents the anterior edge of the gluteal flap, formed by the tensor fascia lata and its fascia, passing through the most anterior point of the greater trochanter. Line B marks the posterior edge, extending through the most posterior aspect of the trochanter and continuing posteriorly toward the gluteus maximus.

Once the flap is marked, an incision is made using a fresh No. 15 blade. At this stage, a portion of the TFL and gluteus maximus, along with their respective fascia, is excised, creating a full-thickness flap. Upon completion of the flap dissection, the distal insertion of the vastus lateralis, the anterior insertion of the gluteus minimus, and the exposed footprint of the gluteus medius are clearly visible. A subfascial Charnley frame is then placed. Tissue releases are performed to mobilize the detached and retracted tendon (Fig 5A). To ensure a healthy, well-vascularized surface for optimal biological healing of the transferred flap, the exposed GT is decorticated using a rongeur and high-speed bur (Fig 5B).Fig 5Right-side hip anterior view once the flap dissection is complete. Graft Fixation

A Kocher clamp is then used to secure the distal aspect of the flap, ensuring accurate positioning over the GT. After preparing the trochanteric surface, 3 double-loaded suture anchors (Knotless 2.6 FiberTak RC; Arthrex) are placed to form the proximal row. The anterior anchor is positioned at the anterior edge of the superior aspect of the trochanter, while the posterior anchor is placed at the posterior edge. A third anchor is positioned midway between the anterior and posterior anchors, at the tip of the trochanter (Fig 6A). The suture limbs from these anchors are then sequentially shuttled through the tendon and transferred flap.Fig 6Right-side hip anterior view. (A) Proximal row double-loaded suture anchor placement. (D) Final construct after the transfer and secure fixation of the flap. Abduction and internal/external rotation of the hip were then tested to confirm the strength of the repair.

A bioinductive collagen scaffold (Regeneten; Smith & Nephew) is applied over the transfer flap at the footprint insertion. The patch is held in place by the loops generated through the suture-tensioning mechanism of the knotless technology (Fig 6B).

Next, all the sutures are brought into the distal row and linked to 3 knotless PEEK (polyether ether ketone) anchors (4.75 mm SwiveLock C Anchor; Arthrex) (Fig 6C). The distal-row anchors are placed on the more distal aspect of the trochanter, 1 cm proximal to the proximal insertion of the vastus lateralis (Fig 6D).

To achieve optimal suture tension, the leg is abducted over a sterile Mayo stand while maintaining neutral rotation. The adjoining edges of the transferred flap are sutured to the fascial layer with a No. 1 absorbable, braided suture (Vicryl; Ethicon). The subcutaneous tissue is closed with a No. 2-0 absorbable, monofilament suture (Monocryl; Johnson & Johnson) in a layered fashion.

Postoperative Care

Nonweightbearing is recommended for the first 6 weeks following surgery. During this period, a postoperative abduction brace should be worn during ambulation and while sleeping for the initial 2 weeks. Passive range of motion is limited to 0° to 90° starting on postoperative day 1, with strict precautions to avoid excessive abduction or external rotation. At 6 weeks, progression to weightbearing begins with partial weightbearing, advancing to full weightbearing by 8 weeks. Formal physical therapy is initiated at 6 weeks, with an emphasis on gait training, gradual progression from passive to active range of motion, introduction of passive hip external and internal rotations, and proprioception enhancement. At 12 weeks, progressive active range-of-motion exercises, strengthening, and dynamic balance training are incorporated. Full strength and functional recovery are typically achieved by 1 year postoperatively.

Discussion

This Technical Note outlines a reproducible technique for open reconstruction of chronic hip abductor tears using gluteus maximus–TFL transfer, double-row abductor tendon repair, and scaffold augmentation. The approach addresses limitations of traditional repairs, especially in cases with poor tissue quality and retraction.

It is well established that chronic lesions of the abductor apparatus, particularly those associated with fatty atrophy and tissue retraction, typically result in poor postoperative outcomes when repaired.7 However, significant clinical improvements have been observed following gluteus maximus transfer, as shown by Lemme et al.,8 who reported promising results at both 6 months and 1 year.

Similarly, Maldonado et al.2 described a technique for transferring the gluteus maximus and TFL in patients with irreparable tears characterized by extensive retraction or atrophy, as well as poor scores on the scales for pain and satisfaction. Their use of a single-row anchor technique yielded favorable short-term results. More recently, in 2023, Inclan et al.9 proposed a double-row transosseous suture technique for anchoring the gluteus maximus and TFL transfer, claiming improved tensile strength of the reconstruction. However, there is currently no evidence regarding the clinical significance of this technique.

While endoscopic10 and open9,11 techniques have shown success in pain relief, they often fail to correct the Trendelenburg gait.9 The outlined technique offers a viable option for treating chronic abductor deficiency, especially in elderly patients with lateral hip pain and gait dysfunction. By integrating mechanical stability and biological enhancement, it may optimize both healing and long-term outcomes.

This technique has several limitations. The high cost of implants may limit their use, and the demanding postoperative protocol—including 6 weeks of nonweightbearing and the need for an abduction brace—can be particularly challenging for elderly patients. Rehabilitation is lengthy, with full recovery often requiring up to 1 year. Moreover, long-term clinical outcomes remain unreported.
