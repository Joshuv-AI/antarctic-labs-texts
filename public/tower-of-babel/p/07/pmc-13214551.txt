Differential polarization on assembled graphene electrodes for sweat metabolites detection
Authors: Yongtao Tang, Yuanyuan Wu, Jianghai Zhu, Yang Liu, Yonghuan Chen, Tangyue Xue, Wenyan Yue, Fengyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryReal-time sweat analysis is crucial for health and sports monitoring; yet, enzyme-based sensors suffer from instability and frequent recalibration. Here, we present an enzyme-free strategy that exploits differential polarization on structurally distinct graphene electrodes. Direct-written graphene oxide inks were dried to yield a dense graphene film (GF) or porous graphene aerogel (GA), then chemically reduced. Electrochemical characterization revealed two regimes: GA with low charge-transfer resistance and high surface area and GF with higher resistance and lower area, corresponding to mass-transport/adsorption-dominated versus charge-transfer-dominated behavior. Differential pulse voltammetry combined with linear discriminant analysis achieved 100% classification of 12 sweat-relevant analytes and accurately classified human cohorts using a wearable prototype. For quantification, symbolic regression mapped GA/GF features to concentration with near-unity slopes (R2 ≈ 0.999) over 0.001–0.10 M for representative analytes. This work demonstrates accurate, enzyme-free identification and quantification of sweat metabolites and outlines a scalable route toward wearable biochemical monitoring.

Introduction

Human sweat is a noninvasively and continuously accessible biofluid rich in biomarkers such as sugars, amino acids, and organic amines.1,2,3,4 These analytes carry valuable physiological and metabolic information for real-time health monitoring, early disease warning, and assessment of exercise status.5,6 Current analytical approaches for sweat include enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), liquid chromatograph mass spectrometer (LC-MS), and electrochemical sensing.7,8,9 Although ELISA and LC-MS offer excellent sensitivity and accuracy, they require complex sample preparation, expensive instrumentation, and are not well suited for on-site and real-time analysis.10,11,12,13 In contrast, electrochemical sensing features high sensitivity, rapid response, facile miniaturization, and easy system integration, making it attractive for wearable and portable applications.14,15 Enzyme-based electrodes provide specificity but suffer from complicated fabrication and calibration and declining enzyme activity over time.16,17 Such enzyme “lock-and-key” specificity is achieved through biorecognition; yet, enzymes are prone to denaturation and performance drift under variations in pH, temperature, and storage/usage conditions, which limits long-term reliability in wearable settings.18 These issues motivate the development of enzyme-free electrochemical platforms that remain stable and reliable in complex matrices. Differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) suppresses capacitive currents and background noise through pulsed and differential strategies, enabling trace detection with high signal-to-noise ratio in complex samples and electrode materials critically determine sensitivity, selectivity, and stability.20,21 Graphene, with its excellent electrical conductivity, large specific surface area, and processability, is a strong candidate for high-performance enzyme-free electrodes.22,23 Recent progress in graphene materials and device engineering has further underscored its promise for flexible and wearable sensing platforms, as demonstrated by graphene-based electronic/sensor architectures and ultrafast large-area graphene growth strategies.24,25 However, in complex sweat matrices, DPV with a single, nonselective electrode often produces partially or fully overlapping peaks because multiple electroactive species have proximate redox potentials and the matrix induces background currents, which compromises quantitation and specificity and limits applicability to complex samples.26,27,28

In this work, a differential-polarization strategy is proposed: distinct dominant polarization mechanisms are introduced within the same enzyme-free graphene system by engineering two structurally different electrodes so that they generate complementary electrochemical responses that can be fused and amplified by pattern recognition (Figure 1). Specifically, graphene electrodes are fabricated by direct writing and subsequently processed by natural drying or freeze-drying to obtain GF and GA, respectively (Figure S1). The dense, layered GF facilitates stable charge injection and charge-transfer kinetics, exhibiting predominantly electrochemical polarization, whereas the 3D porous GA enhances adsorption/desorption and diffusion, showing predominantly concentration polarization. By fusing the complementary DPV responses of GA and GF across 12 sweat metabolites, LDA-based pattern recognition achieves 100% discriminative accuracy. The method further proves robust in classifying real-sweat samples from diverse individuals, and symbolic regression models the key feature-concentration relationships to enable quantification. Overall, this establishes a closed-loop route spanning manufacturing-structure-mechanism-algorithm.Figure 1Polarization-differentiated binary graphene electrodes for multianalyte sweat recognition

Compared with previously reported graphene-based or enzyme-free sweat sensors, which predominantly target one or a limited number of predefined analytes using analyte-specific electrode architectures, ion-selective interfaces, or functional membranes (Table S4), the present platform emphasizes a fundamentally different performance dimension by enabling broad-spectrum analyte discrimination within a single non-enzymatic system.29,30,31 Rather than optimizing the analytical figures of merit for individual targets, a single graphene ink is structurally engineered into two microstructures (GA and GF) that encode distinct polarization regimes. These structurally encoded contrasts yield high-dimensional, complementary DPV fingerprints that can be decoded by multivariate pattern recognition to achieve multiplexed identification of 12 organic metabolites and differentiation among multiple real-sweat cohorts using the same dual-electrode pair. This mechanism-level engineering expands the accessible electrochemical information space in complex sweat matrices, representing the core advantage of the proposed platform and distinguishing it from conventional target-specific graphene sweat sensors.

Results and discussion

As shown in Figure 2A, GF appears as a continuous and compact film at low magnification, with largely aligned lamellae and slight surface wrinkling; at higher magnification (Figure 2B), densely stacked layers with narrow interlayer slits are observed, and the cross-section reveals a regular lamellar architecture in which submicron slit-type voids dominate. In contrast, GA displays a 3D interconnected porous network at low magnification (Figure 2C), where pore walls interconnect to form continuous pathways; higher-magnification views show overlapped graphene sheets with a rough surface and secondary porosity, while cross-sections reveal through-thickness channels and local necked throats, indicative of a hierarchical micro/meso/macro-porous structure (Figure 2D). Collectively, GF is characterized by a dense, lamellar texture with limited open pores, whereas GA features high porosity and good connectivity. In Figure 2E, the I–V curves are nearly ohmic and linear within ±0.9 V with negligible hysteresis, and both current and slope increase markedly after reduction, with GF > GA; this is because oxidation introduces O–H, C=O, and C–O–C groups, enlarges the interlayer spacing, and disrupts the sp2/π-π conjugation, which suppresses conductivity, whereas chemical reduction attenuates oxygenated features, contracts the interlayer spacing, and reconstructs a continuous sp2 network to enhance charge transport32,33; additionally, GA’s porous junctions and tortuous pathways introduce extra series/contact resistances, yielding lower macroscopic conductance than the more continuously stacked GF (Figures S2 and S5–S8). Figure 2F (mercury intrusion-extrusion) reveals markedly different pore architectures: GA displays a larger intrusion volume, a broader pressure window, and a more pronounced hysteresis loop, signatures of a highly porous, interconnected network with hierarchical ink-bottle pores characterized by neck and cavity features; GF shows a smaller intrusion volume and a smoother profile, consistent with limited slit-like interlayer voids in a dense film.Figure 2SEM images of the graphene electrodes(A) low-magnification overview and (B) top/cross-section details of GF; (C) low-magnification overview and (D) top/cross-section details of GA; (E) I–V characteristics of GF and GA; and (F) mercury intrusion-extrusion (MIP) curves of GF and GA.

Figure 3A shows DPV traces of four amino acids on GA and GF. The contrast lies mainly in peak current (Ip) and peak width, while Ep is generally stable with only slight, analyte-dependent offsets between GA and GF. This arises because amino acid electrooxidation on carbon is typically irreversible and multistep (Ep governed by intrinsic barriers and charge-transfer kinetics), whereas GA’s porous connectivity chiefly alters local concentration and diffusion flux, amplifying Ip. Figure 3B presents four saccharides, which are weakly electroactive and require higher overpotentials. GA increases residence time and induces partial thin-layer transport, yielding higher effective concentration, manifested as Ip enhancement and slight peak broadening, while peak positions remain broadly consistent, albeit with modest shifts for certain sugars. GF provides a smoother baseline and more uniform peak shapes. As shown in Figure 3C, aromatic amines exhibit stronger π-π/cation-π interactions with graphene and benefit from GA’s higher accessible surface area, typically giving larger Ip and for some aromatic amines, visibly shifted peak positions, reflecting their stronger adsorption/heterogeneous kinetics coupling on GA. Aliphatic polyamines are governed more by diffusion and protonation, showing pronounced Ip gains with comparatively smaller changes in peak position. Overall, Ip values are generally higher on GA than on GF (most pronounced for organic amines, followed by saccharides), whereas amino acids mainly differ in Ip amplitude with Ep remaining largely unchanged in a qualitative sense (Figure 3D and Table S2). Mechanistically, 3D connectivity and higher accessible surface area of GA enhance local concentration and flux, boosting Ip, while GF’s compact lamellae favor charge-transfer control with narrower peaks and more stable peak shapes.Figure 3Differential pulse voltammetry response of reduced graphene oxide to (A) four amino acids, (B) four sugars, (C) four organic amines, and statistical chart of peak currents (D)Error bars represent SD, n = 5 independent replicates.

As shown in Figure 4A, GF has a dense lamellar structure in which interfacial reactions are primarily limited by electron-transfer kinetics and the near-interface concentration gradient is weak, thus electrochemical polarization predominates. In addition, GA features a 3D interconnected porous structure with pronounced pore-wall adsorption and in-pore diffusion, making it easy to establish concentration gradients and supply lag at the interface/inside pores, hence concentration polarization predominates. In the EIS Nyquist plot of Figure 4B, GF (Rct = 540 Ω) shows a dominant high-frequency semicircle and a relatively inconspicuous low-frequency 45° Warburg segment, indicating a response governed by charge transfer. In contrast, GA (Rct = 20 Ω) presents a steeper low-frequency diffusion slope and a larger apparent capacitance/constant-phase element (Cdl/CPE) contribution, implying a higher proportion of mass-transfer and surface charge-storage processes.Figure 4Differential polarization characterization(A) Schematic of interfacial reactions on graphene film (GF) and graphene aerogel (GA) electrodes.(B) Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS, Nyquist) of GA and GF with equivalent-circuit fitting.(C) Finite-element simulated cyclic voltammograms of GA and GF electrodes.(D) BET N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of GA and GF electrodes.

Figure 4A schematically summarizes how microstructure dictates the dominant polarization mechanism on GF and GA. In this schematic, green spheres represent reactant sweat analytes in the bulk electrolyte, whereas yellow spheres represent the electro-oxidation products formed at the interface or within pores; blue paths indicate electron transport through the graphene network. On the GF side, the compact lamellar stacking provides continuous electronic pathways but limited open pores, so reactants reach the surface with minimal local accumulation and weak near-interface concentration gradients. Therefore the interfacial process is predominantly charge-transfer/electron-injection controlled, as emphasized by the large blue curved arrow, corresponding to electrochemical polarization. On the GA side, the 3D interconnected porous framework offers abundant accessible surface and pore walls that promote adsorption/desorption and in-pore diffusion. Reactants can be locally enriched inside/near pores, while products diffuse out more slowly, enabling the build-up of concentration gradients and supply lag; this mass-transfer/adsorption-controlled flux is highlighted by the large red curved arrow, corresponding to concentration polarization.

Consistent with this mechanistic contrast, the EIS Nyquist plot in Figure 4B shows that GF exhibits a dominant high-frequency semicircle with a large charge-transfer resistance (Rct = 540 Ω) and an inconspicuous Warburg region, indicating charge-transfer-governed behavior. In contrast, GA presents a much smaller semicircle (Rct = 20 Ω), a steeper low-frequency diffusion slope, and a larger apparent Cdl/CPE contribution, reflecting stronger mass-transfer and surface charge-storage participation. This mechanistic distinction is further supported by scan-rate-dependent CVs of glucose (Figure S12), where GF shows a diffusion-controlled slope (≈0.4) while GA exhibits a mixed diffusion-adsorption slope (≈0.6), consistent with their charge-transfer-dominated and mass-transport/adsorption-modulated regimes, respectively. Scan-rate CVs of a strongly adsorbing aromatic amine, tyramine, on GA (Figure S13) further show broad, quasi-capacitive waves with disproportionately increasing currents at high scan rates compared with GF, a response characteristic of adsorption-enhanced, thin-layer-like behavior and consistent with an adsorption-modulated polarization regime for amines on GA. From BET isotherms (Figure 4D), GA possesses a ∼10× higher accessible surface area than GF (1.629 vs. 0.162 m2 g−1), providing the structural basis for adsorption-assisted transport and concentration-gradient sensitivity. Accordingly, simulated CVs (Figure 4C) show a kinetics-limited character for GF (narrower peaks), whereas GA exhibits a transport-influenced character with higher quasi-capacitive baselines and broader peaks, in line with a concentration-polarized regime.

The adsorption-desorption and charge-transfer processes on GA and GF can be further rationalized within a Langmuir-Butler-Volmer framework.34 For a surface-confined redox couple A ⇌ Aads ⇌ B ⇌ B, the adsorption rate can be written as the following equation:(Equation 1)rads=ka1cA(1−ΘAads−ΘBads)−kd1ΘAadswhere ka1 and kd1 are the adsorption/desorption rate constants and ΘAads, ΘBads denote surface coverages. The local faradaic current density is then described by the Butler-Volmer expression:(Equation 2)iloc=k0ΓF(ΘBadse0.5ηFRT−ΘAadse−0.5ηFRT)where k0 k0 is the heterogeneous electron-transfer rate constant, Γ is the surface density of active sites, and η is the overpotential. For small overpotentials, linearization of this relation leads to an interfacial resistance scaling approximately as the following equation:(Equation 3)Rct∝1k0Γ

The highly porous GA provides a much larger population of electroactive sites and stronger analyte adsorption (effectively larger ka1, smaller kd1 and higher steady-state Θads), thereby increasing the effective product k0Γ compared with the dense GF film. This accounts for the markedly lower Rct and higher DPV peak currents observed on GA, whereas GF, with fewer accessible sites and weaker adsorption, remains limited by charge-transfer kinetics and exhibits higher Rct and narrower peaks. Thus, the contrast in porosity and specific surface area between GA and GF is directly reflected in the kinetic parameters ka1, kd1, k0, Γ, which in turn modulate both charge-transfer resistance and peak-current response.

Figures 5A–5C present the LDA-based identification results for the 12 analytes. Figure 5A shows the LDA score plot obtained from the GF electrode, in which analyte clusters appear relatively compact, reflecting limited discriminability when relying on a single electrode. In contrast, Figure 5B displays the LDA result for the GA electrode, where clusters are more dispersed in the 3D space, though partial overlap persists. By integrating signals from both GA and GF, Figure 5C reveals well-separated clusters for all 12 analytes, with no inter-class overlap and tight intra-class grouping. These results confirm that the combined use of graphene film and aerogel electrodes significantly enhances discriminating ions among amino acids, saccharides, and organic amines. The marked improvement in separation underscores the complementarity of the two electrode types in capturing distinct yet synergistic features for multicomponent identification. Figures 5D–5F present the calibration of predicted versus actual concentrations obtained by the symbolic regression model for lysine, glucose, and tyramine. The data points for all three analytes nearly coincide with the unity line; the corresponding coefficients of determination are R2 = 0.9994, 0.99980, and 0.9991, respectively, indicating excellent linearity and quantitative accuracy over 0.001–0.10 M range. With slopes near 1 and interceptions approaching zero, and no observable systematic bias, the model demonstrates that symbolic regression effectively maps the combined electrochemical features from GA and GF to analyte concentration, enabling consistent and precise quantification across diverse analyte classes.

Symbolic regression in this study is not restricted to linear forms. The MEP-based evolutionary search permits nonlinear compositions of the selected operators, variables, and constants, and therefore, nonlinear concentration-feature relationships would be retained whenever they yield lower validation error. The near-linear optimal expressions indicate that, within the investigated domain (0.001–0.10 M in PBS), the dominant DPV descriptors from GA/GF electrodes scale quasi-linearly with analyte concentration. To enhance reliability and reduce overfitting, an internal validation split is maintained during evolution for candidate selection and early stopping, and multiple independent runs are performed to verify reproducibility and stability of the selected expression (Table S1). The present SR model is validated within the above concentration and matrix range; broader operational-variability and multi-cohort datasets will be necessary to further evaluate and extend generalization to complex real-world sweat conditions.

Here the complementarity between GA and GF should be understood as information enrichment via differential polarization, rather than mutual compensation. Owing to its dense lamellar architecture, GF operates in a charge-transfer-dominated regime, where electron-transfer kinetics govern the DPV response, producing narrower peaks and relatively stable Ep. By contrast, the 3D porous GA introduces extensive adsorption sites and short diffusion pathways, giving rise to mass-transfer/adsorption-dominated (concentration-polarized) responses characterized by enlarged Ip, quasi-capacitive baselines, and transport-influenced peak broadening. Because sweat analytes differ in kinetic barriers, adsorption strengths (e.g., π-π/cation-π interactions for aromatic amines), and diffusivities, GA and GF accentuate distinct reaction descriptors (primarily Ip-sensitivity vs. Ep/shape-stability). The resulting DPV features from the two electrodes are therefore more orthogonal in the feature space, and their fusion significantly increases class separability in LDA, as evidenced by the transition from partial overlaps in single-electrode scores to fully separated clusters in GA + GF scores.

Figure 6 summarizes real-sweat testing with the wearable sensor based on GA/GF electrodes. The optical design images (Figures 6A and 6B) indicate that, via direct-writing, electrode geometries can be customized to practical needs. Using electrochemical features extracted from participants’ sweat, which were centered/standardized prior to LDA, the 3D LDA projection (Figure 6C) shows well-separated clusters with tight intra-class grouping and no overlap among the six cohorts (male-no exercise, male-exercise, male-thin, male-fat, female-thin, female-fat); this real-sweat cohort test under natural sweat-matrix variability supports that the clustering originates from GA/GF differential-polarization patterns rather than uncontrolled scaling effects. The corresponding confusion matrix (Figure 6D) contains 5s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere, indicating 100% classification accuracy for this dataset. In summary, we established an enzyme-free, wearable sweat-analysis platform. And it built on a binary graphene architecture (GF and GA) fabricated by direct-writing and chemically reduced to restore π-π conjugation, thereby enhancing charge transport and enabling stable transduction; leveraging an integrated structure-function toolkit that spans morphology, EIS, BET surface analysis, and finite element modeling, we identified complementary operating regimes in which GF is predominantly controlled by charge transfer, whereas GA is governed by mass transport and adsorption. These regimes yield mutually informative DPV signatures that, when embedded in principle component and linear discriminant spaces, enable separation of multiple analytes and discrimination between individuals in wearable applications.

Prior carbon-nanomaterial sensor arrays combined with machine learning generally rely on increasing the number of sensing elements or exploiting uncontrolled inter-electrode variability to obtain multichannel fingerprints. In comparison, the GA/GF pair in this work is not a larger array but a mechanistically engineered dual-electrode system, where microstructure is used to intentionally modulate the dominant polarization pathway. This controlled mechanism shift yields predictable, analyte-dependent DPV descriptors that expand the feature space for LDA and symbolic regression, thereby enabling robust discrimination and quantification in complex sweat matrices.

Limitations of the study

Key areas for further development include strengthening quantitative performance under operational variability (e.g., sweat rate, dilution, temperature, and pH) and reducing reliance on frequent calibration, ideally with explicit uncertainty quantification to support on-device decision-making. Such variability, together with unexpected interferents or atypical sample matrices, may introduce nonlinear distortions in concentration-feature mapping; therefore, expanded training domains and uncertainty-aware updates of the symbolic-regression model are needed to further improve reliability beyond the current validation range. Generalization could be improved through multi-center, longitudinal cohorts and judicious use of domain adaptation, and the pipeline would benefit from leveraging additional physiological modalities (skin temperature, heart rate, and acceleration) to enhance stability and context. On the hardware side, integrating microfluidics for controlled sampling and evaporation management, more durable antifouling/long-adhesion interfaces, and lower-power edge computation would be advantageous. Addressing these points should enhance robustness, transferability, and long-term wearability in practical deployments.

Method details

Chemicals and apparatus

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical reagent grade unless otherwise specified. Expanded Graphite was purchased from Jinrilai Marble Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Potassium persulfate (GR), phosphorus pentoxide, hydroiodic acid, potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6]), potassium ferrocyanide (K4[Fe(CN)6]), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, 10×) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Potassium permanganate, hydrochloric acid and anhydrous ethanol were obtained from Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory (China). In addition, Tyramine, β-phenylethylamine, histamine, spermine, D-galactose, D-glucose, maltitol, lactose, threonine, leucine, lysine, and glutamic acid were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Direct writing was carried out using a WND-019 micro-nano structure direct writing instrument (Tianjin Winner Nano Material Technology Co., Ltd., China). The morphology of the printed structures was characterized by an SU1000 scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Electrochemical measurements were performed on a CHI660E electrochemistry workstation (Shanghai CH Instruments Co., Ltd., China).

Preparation of GO ink

GO ink was synthesized using a modified Hummers’ method. First, 40 mL of concentrated H2SO4 was mixed with 3 g of expanded graphite under stirring, followed by the addition of K2S2O8 and P2O5, and the mixture was heated at 80 °C for 5 h for pre-oxidation. After cooling, the product was slowly diluted with 500 mL of ice water, left to stand for 12 h, filtered, washed with deionized water until neutral, and dried under vacuum to obtain the pre-oxidized material. Subsequently, 120 mL of concentrated H2SO4 was cooled in an ice bath, the pre-oxidized material was added, and 9 g of KMnO4 was slowly introduced. The suspension was stirred at 35 °C for 2 h, cooled to room temperature, diluted with ice water, and treated with H2O2 until the solution turned yellow. The resulting product was collected and repeatedly washed by centrifugation with deionized water, 10% HCl, and again with deionized water until neutral. It was then dispersed in 500 mL of water and dialyzed (MWCO 14,000) until the dialysate became neutral, yielding an aqueous GO dispersion with a concentration of approximately 5 mg/mL. Finally, to meet the viscosity requirement for subsequent direct-writing, the dispersion was concentrated by solvent evaporation at 60 °C under stirring, with the concentration monitored gravimetrically, resulting in printable GO ink with a final concentration of ∼20 mg/mL.

Preparation of GF/GA electrode

The GO ink was loaded into a 10 mL syringe for direct-writing and extruded through a 200 μm inner-diameter nozzle onto polyethylene terephthalate (PET) substrates using a micro/nano-structure direct-writing system, as illustrated in Figure S1. The printing pressure was set at 7.5 psi and the printing speed at 10 mm/s, producing rectangular patterns with dimensions of 0.7 × 1 cm2. The pattern was designed as an STL model in CINEMA 4D and sliced in Ultimaker CURA software to generate the printing path. To accelerate fabrication, array printing was implemented in CURA to enable batch preparation of patterned GO electrodes.

The printed GO patterns were divided into two groups: one was frozen and subsequently subjected to freeze-drying to obtain GO aerogels, while the other was air-dried under ambient conditions to obtain GO films. For reduction, the as-obtained GO aerogels and GO films were placed in a glass dish, followed by the slow addition of 200 mL of hydroiodic acid (15 wt %). The samples were then heated at 95 °C for 3 h in a vacuum oven. After cooling to room temperature, the residual hydroiodic acid was decanted, and the samples were rinsed three times by immersion in deionized water for 20 min each, followed by additional washing with anhydrous ethanol to remove acid residues. Finally, the aerogel samples were frozen and freeze-dried, while the film samples were air-dried at room temperature, yielding reduced GA and GF electrode, respectively.

Electrochemical measurements

In a standard three-electrode configuration, GA and GF were used as working electrodes, a 1 × 1 cm2 platinum plate served as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode was used as the reference electrode. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was conducted in the potential range of −0.3 to +1.2 V at a scan rate of 50 mV/s, using 0.1 mol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) as the electrolyte. DPV was performed in the potential range of −0.5 to +1.0 V at a scan rate of 50 mV/s. For DPV analysis, 0.1 mol/L PBS buffer (pH 7.4) was used as the supporting electrolyte, into which tyramine, β-phenylethylamine, histamine, spermine, D-galactose, D-glucose, maltitol, β-lactose, L-threonine, L-leucine, L-lysine, and L-glutamic acid were individually dissolved to prepare 12 electrolyte solutions at a concentration of 1 mM each. These solutions were used for DPV detection and analysis.

Real sweat experiments

Real human sweat was collected from a small cohort of healthy adult volunteers. In total, five participants (three males and two females, age range 27–29 years) were enrolled. Resting sweat was collected under sauna/steam-bath conditions, where perspiration was induced without deliberate physical exercise. For one male participant, both resting and exercise sweat were obtained; the exercise sweat sample was collected immediately after a long-distance running session once sweating became profuse. All real-sweat samples were subsequently subjected to DPV measurements on the GA and GF electrodes following the same protocols as for the model analytes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Graphene-based enzyme-free electrodes have been shown to yield enhanced and stable voltammetric signals by promoting interfacial electron transfer, validating the reliability of graphene DPV peak descriptors as quantitative features.35 Moreover, graphene interfaces can support surface-confined redox/recognition motifs to generate analyte-dependent electrochemical signatures in complex bio-relevant settings, highlighting the value of multivariate voltammetric readouts beyond single-peak assays.36 Recent high-resolution, enzyme-free discrimination of closely related amino acid isomers via physical/electrostatic asymmetry further illustrates that electrochemical fingerprint features can resolve subtle molecular differences when coupled with appropriate data analysis.37

DPV measurements for each analyte and each electrode type were performed with five independent replicates (n = 5). Here, n denotes the number of independently repeated electrochemical measurements used for statistical summarization and for constructing the LDA feature matrix. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and error bars represent SD. DPV features (peak height, half-peak width, and peak position) were assembled into a feature matrix and mean-centered/standardized. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was performed by constructing within-class and between-class scatter matrices and projecting the features onto up to K−1 discriminant axes for classification. Model performance was assessed by leave-one-out cross-validation, reporting the confusion matrix, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. All LDA analyses were implemented using the Custom computer code listed in the key resources table (KRT).

Symbolic regression was performed using Multi Expression Programming (MEP) in MEPX (2024.4.14.0-beta) on a Windows 10 (64-bit) workstation. The input was a CSV dataset with a header row of predictor names and the target variable in the last column. For each analyte and each concentration level, electrochemical measurements were independently repeated five times (n = 5), the dataset was randomly partitioned into training, validation, and test subsets with an 8:1:1 ratio. The evolutionary search adopted a standard pipeline comprising sub-populations, tournament selection, crossover, and mutation to evolve candidate closed-form expressions from a predefined function/variable/constant set. During evolution, only the training and validation subsets were used: the validation subset was employed for candidate selection and early-stopping decisions, while the test subset was kept completely held out for final performance assessment. To further guard against overfitting, multiple independent runs were executed with a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility, and after convergence the best individual—judged by the lowest validation error across runs—was exported as the final analytical model and used for downstream evaluation and interpretation. The symbolic regression model generated a concentration prediction for each replicate, and the reported concentration values represent the mean of the five predictions, with error bars indicating the standard deviation (SD).The high coefficient of determination (R2 ≈ 0.999) reported in the main text arises from a linear fit between symbolic-regression predictions and reference concentrations over the investigated gradient, while the detailed SR parameter settings are summarized in Table S1 and the test-set performance metrics (R2, RMSE, MAE, MBE) for representative analytes are listed in Table S3.
