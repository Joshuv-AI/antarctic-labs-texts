Synchronous quantification of arterial, venous, and cerebrospinal fluid flow dynamics using real-time phase-contrast MRI
Authors: Leonardo A Rivera-Rivera, Bruce Collick, Tomas Vikner, Sterling C Johnson, Kevin M Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurofluid flow dynamics are frequently studied from asynchronous blood and CSF flow measurements from real-time imaging using separate phase contrast (PC) MRI scans. Asynchronous measures can be influenced by changes in heart rate, respiration, and other physiological processes, obfuscating neurofluids assessment. Here we present an approach for synchronous measures of neurofluids using simultaneous real-time 2D PC MRI and investigated the effects of different breathing patterns on synchronous and asynchronous blood and CSF flow in a group of healthy participants.MethodsInterleaved dual-velocity encoding 2D PC MRI with retrospective real-time reconstruction was utilized for synchronous neurofluid measures during free breathing, paced breathing and breath holds. Data were collected on a clinical 3.0T scanner at the level of C1/C2 vertebrae in 10 participants. From real-time images, flow rates repeated measures, and cardiac and respiratory flow power were assessed using Bland-Altman, power spectral analyses, and breathing pattern group differences. Neurofluids coupling from cross-correlation between arterial and venous blood and CSF flow signals were quantified from synchronous and asynchronous measures. Real-time images were re-binned to the cardiac cycle and compared to high-temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images in terms of flow rate, pulsatility index, and stroke volume.ResultsFlow repeatability was highest in free breathing scans and in arteries and spinal canal compared to veins from Bland-Altman and repeatability coefficients. Significant differences were measured in cardiac and respiratory flow power across breathing patterns in various vessel segments and spinal canal (P ≤ 0.006). Synchronous blood and CSF cardiac coupling were significantly higher than asynchronous results in all vessels (P = 0.002). For example, free breathing synchronous cardiac couplings ranged from [0.81, 0.93], compared to asynchronous range [0.49, 0.53]. Synchronous cardiac coupling showed significant differences across breathing patterns in most vessels (P = 0.002). Comparison between real-time cardiac re-binned images and high-temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images showed high correlations for flow rate and spinal canal stroke volume (ρ ≥ 0.95) and lower for pulsatility index (ρ = [0.45, 0.88]).ConclusionsBreathing patterns induced significant responses across neurofluids including in flow rates, flow power, and coupling parameters. Background

The dynamics and interactions of arterial, venous, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow are driven by a combination of cardiac-induced pulsations, systemic blood pressure changes (e.g. from respiration), and neuronally evoked responses. Within the rigid cranium, these neurofluids interact with brain tissue and vasculature to regulate cerebral perfusion and stabilize intracranial pressure [1]. Disruptions to the neurofluid system are commonly observed in pathological conditions such as hydrocephalus, stroke, and Chiari malformations, often resulting in elevated intracranial pressure and other serious complications [2]. Moreover, impaired neurofluid circulation has been hypothesized to hinder the clearance of metabolic waste products, including beta-amyloid plaques implicated in Alzheimer’s disease and other neurodegenerative disorders [3]. These established and emerging insights into the physiological and pathological significance of neurofluids underscore the need for methodologies capable of characterizing the coupling and driving forces of neurofluid flow dynamics.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains a leading imaging technology for non-invasive evaluation of human neurofluid flow dynamics. For example, human brain clearance and neurofluid flow transport can be assessed with intrathecal and intravenous injections of Gadolinium agents [4, 5], and CSF motility with diffusion based methods [6, 7]. In the clinic, cardiac-resolved phase contrast (PC) MRI velocimetry is the standard imaging approach for the assessment of blood and CSF velocities, providing both magnitude and direction of coherent flows [8–10]. More recently, advances in hardware, data acquisition, and reconstruction have enabled reliable real-time imaging (i.e. gated to absolute time) with 2D PC MRI, enabling detailed assessment of cardiac and respiratory effects on neurofluid dynamics [11–14]. Using real-time 2D PC MRI, the influence of breathing patterns on arterial, venous, and CSF flows have been studied in healthy and disease states (e.g. Chiari malformation) [15–18]. Furthermore, motivated by evidence of slow oscillatory neuronal activity during sleep driving large blood volume and CSF oscillations [19], recent studies have interleaved real-time 2D PC MRI acquisitions with fMRI to quantify CSF flow and cortical BOLD relationships [20].

The quantitative assessment of blood and CSF flow with PC MRI requires the selection of a velocity sensitizing parameter (venc) which must be set tailored to the expected fluid velocity of interest [21]. Thus, measures of arterial, venous, and CSF flow, which span a large velocity dynamic range, are commonly acquired asynchronously in separate scans. Consequently, the comparison of blood and CSF flow measures and the study of their coupling are influenced by variations in heart rate, respiration, and the repeatability of external challenges (e.g. respiratory maneuvers, exercise, neurostimulation) [18, 22, 23]. This physiological variability can confound the evaluation and repeatability of real-time 2D PC MRI based neurofluid dynamics [9, 24]. To address this, various methods have been proposed to collect multiple vencs in a single scan [25–28]; however, these approaches are limited by the intravoxel velocity distribution to approximately doubling the dynamic range. Thus, while these advances have provided methods to improve simultaneous arterial and venous blood flow imaging, they have not been extended to imaging CSF and blood flow synchronously and enable real-time support.

Ideally, PC MRI would simultaneously collect both blood (arterial, venous) and CSF flows for synchronous characterization of their couplings. Some progress has been made to achieve this with multi-venc acquisitions using multi-slice encoding [29, 30] (e.g. SMS) and interleaved spiral sampling [31]. These techniques provide methodologies to synchronously quantify cardiac driven arterial, venous, and CSF flows, removing some physiological state dependencies in flow measures. However, they have not yet been applied to real-time imaging, where they hold significant potential for quantifying dynamic interactions among cardiac, respiratory, and neuronal driving forces. This limitation has partially been placed by the high spatiotemporal resolution needs of real-time imaging, requiring reconstruction from undersampled multi-venc datasets. Recent advances on model-based reconstructions have facilitated high frame rate real-time imaging of asynchronous scans from extremely undersampled datasets, exploiting compressed data representations [14, 32].

In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of synchronous real-time imaging of neurofluid dynamics at the cranio-spinal level and investigated the effects of different breathing patterns on flow dynamics in a group of healthy participants. To accomplish this, we leveraged interleaved dual-venc 2D PC MRI and model-based reconstruction support for real-time imaging. We studied the impact of free breathing, paced breathing, and breath holds on neurofluid flow rates including cardiac and respiratory oscillatory flow power, coupling between blood and CSF flow from synchronous and asynchronous measures, and conducted test-retest repeatability. Finally, real-time images re-binned to the cardiac cycle were compared against standard high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images to study the technique’s ability to depict high frequency cardiac oscillations.

Materials and methods

Study participants

MRI data were collected in healthy human participants (N = 10, age range 22–49 y, mean 28 ± 7 y, 6 female) in a single MRI session. The University of Wisconsin Institutional Review Board approved all study procedures and protocols following the policies and guidance established by the campus Human Research Protection Program. Informed, written consent was provided by each study participant before participation.

Imaging protocol

MRI data were acquired on a clinical 3.0T system (SIGNA Premier, GE Healthcare) (maximum gradient strength = 80 mT/m, maximum slew rate = 200 T/m/s) using a 48-channel head-coil (GE Healthcare). T2-weighted and time-of-flight scans were utilized to localize region of interest. Sagittal 3D CUBE T2-weighted images were acquired with an imaging volume of 25 × 25 × 20.5 cm3 and 1 × 1 × 1.6 mm3 spatial resolution. Axial 3D time-of-flight images were collected with an imaging volume of 24 × 24 × 4 cm3 and 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 spatial resolution. 2D PC MRI scans were collected with a single slice prescribed at the level of the C1/C2 vertebrae to capture arterial inflow (internal carotid arteries (ICAs) and vertebral arteries (VAs)), venous outflow (internal jugular veins (IJVs)), and bidirectional CSF flow (spinal canal) within the same imaging slice.

To enable synchronous assessment of blood and CSF flow, interleaved dual-venc 2D PC-MRI scans were collected [31]. In this approach, flow encodings were collected in an interleaved fashion with identical k-space sampling for flow compensated, followed by high and low venc encodings. K-space was collected continuously with a center out 2D golden angle spiral trajectory to enable flexible and efficient data collection with short echo times and retrospective reconstruction. 2D PC MRI scans were prescribed axially with: field of view of 22 × 22 cm2, slice thickness = 4 mm, in-plane resolution = 0.68 × 0.68 mm2, flip angle = 8°, receiver bandwidth = ± 125 kHz, vencs = 75 and 8 cm/s (optimized for arterial blood and spinal canal CSF flow), TR/TE = 10.1/4.2 ms, scan time = 61s, number of spiral arms = 2000 and sampling window ~ 3.7 ms. Cardiac cycles were collected for each participant from a photoplethysmogram on a pulse oximeter (GE Healthcare) worn on the participant’s finger during the MRI exam. Respiratory waveforms were collected from a respiratory belt (GE Healthcare) placed on the participant’s abdomen.

2D PC MRI scans were collected during three breathing states: free breathing (FB), paced breathing (PB) and a breath hold maneuver (BH). Breathing patterns were selected to evaluate the capacity to measure changes in physiology from cardiac and respiratory physiological fluctuations. For free breathing, patients were instructed to breathe naturally. For paced breathing a 4-7-8 s pattern was used throughout the duration of the scan (i.e. 61s). Participants were instructed to inhale through their nose for 4s, hold their breath for 7s, and exhale through their mouth for 8s continuously after receiving a flashlight signal from the MRI control room. Scans started during inspiration after 1 cycle of paced breathing was completed as monitored from the consoled displayed respiratory bellow signal. For the breath hold maneuver, participants were instructed to perform a deep inspiration followed by a breath hold which lasted 20s. Participants were instructed to attempt pushing air out while maintaining the mouth closed to induce a stronger physiological response, akin to a Valsalva maneuver. Participants received a flashlight signal from the MRI control room to start performing the breath hold maneuver, which began ~ 20s after scan start, then a second light signal was played indicating to release the breath hold and breath normally ~ 40s after scan start.

2D PC MRI scans were repeated for test-retest evaluation with participants remaining in the same position and using identical scan plane prescriptions. The acquisition order was as follows: (1–2) two back-to-back free-breathing scans, (3) first paced breathing scan, (4) first breath hold scan, (5) second paced breathing scan, and (6) second breath hold scan. The protocol was designed to prioritize the collection of a complete dataset, ensuring at least one scan under each respiratory condition. The time interval between free-breathing scan repeats was minimal (approximately 3–5 s, corresponding to the time required to initiate the next acquisition), whereas the interval between paced breathing and breath hold scan repeats was approximately 1.5 min, accounting for the in-between scan and participant instruction.

Image reconstruction and post-processing

Real-time magnitude and velocity images were retrospectively reconstructed from 2D PC MRI data to 500 time points using the absolute time of the acquisition and iterative SENSE with a local-low rank temporal constraint with adaptative thresholding and block size = 8 × 8. [32–35] Four spiral arms were used per reconstructed flow encoding image time point for a temporal resolution of ~ 122ms (i.e. 4 arms x 3TR (referenced, high, and low venc flow encodings)). Images were reconstructed using spiral trajectories measured with a dynamic field probe camera system (Skope Magnetic Resonance Technologies AG), which lead to fewer artifacts compared to images reconstructed with the nominal spiral trajectories, which were more susceptible to errors from uncompensated Eddy currents (see Supplemental Fig. 1). Post-reconstruction data corrections included 2nd order polynomial fit of static tissue phase to remove background phase offsets, and single step Laplacian correction for phase anti-aliasing [36].

Flow measurements were performed using FIJI ImageJ (v1.54f) [37]. Regions of interest (ROIs) extraction and flow processing were conducted with a semi-automatic adaptive thresholding segmentation approach of vessels, using both magnitude and velocity images from the high venc flow encoding. This approach dynamically adjusted ROIs over time to account for respiratory-induced changes in vessel shape and position. The spinal canal was segmented from the low venc flow encoding using the standard deviation of the velocity images. Images were visually inspected for motion corruption. All ROIs were visually inspected to verify and correct alignment of the vessel of interest and spinal canal across the different scans. Real-time flow waveforms were estimated from the segmented ROIs area and spatial mean in the velocity time series. ROIs included left and right ICAs, VAs, IJVs, and spinal canal.

Parameters derived from the extracted real-time flow waveforms included: flow rates (mean) for vessels and root mean square (RMS) flow for spinal canal to quantify amplitude of CSF oscillations since mean CSF flow rate is ~ 0 (i.e. assuming negligible CSF production in the 61s scan). To study the power of cardiac [0.70–1.5] Hz and respiratory [0.05–0.50] Hz oscillations in blood and CSF, Welch’s power spectral density was estimated (MATLAB R2019b pwelch() function) from demean flow rates and reported both as raw power and normalized power to the total band frequency content. Circular cross-correlation between blood and CSF flow signals from the same scan (synchronous) and different scans (asynchronous) were quantified to study neurofluids coupling. To enable asynchronous measures, high venc flow encoding data were compared to low venc flow encoding data from the repeated scan. Cross-correlations were estimated for cardiac band-pass filtered [0.7–1.5] Hz and respiratory low-pass filtered [≤ 0.50] Hz demeaned signals (MATLAB R2019b designfilt() function). Finite impulse response filters were applied twice (forward and backward) to ensure a zero-phase (zero-delay) filtering [14]. For the cardiac band-pass filtered signals, we used a FIR filter with an order of 62 (i.e. 63 coefficients), and both the lower and upper transition bandwidths were set to 0.20 Hz. For the respiratory low-pass filtered signals, a FIR filter with an order of 49 and a transition bandwidth of 0.25 Hz was applied. After filtering, maximum cross-correlation was estimated from the normalized correlation between circular shifted signals (MATLAB R2019b corr(blood(), circshift(csf(), time point())) functions).

For free breathing scans, real-time reconstructed image series (temporal resolution ~ 122ms) were re-binned to the cardiac cycle and compared to a high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved image reconstruction of the same data (30 cardiac phases, temporal resolution ~ 31ms) using cardiac triggers. Data were compared using the same set of ROIs, and cardiac-resolved parameters included flow rates (mean), pulsatility index (flow range over mean flow) and stroke volume (ΔV), defined as the range of the cumulative integral of the demeaned flow waveform [38].

Statistical analyses

Differences in flow parameters across breathing patterns were assessed using the Friedman test, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To control Type I errors (i.e. family-wise error rate), Holm-Bonferroni corrections were applied to the statistical tests. Bland-Altman analyses were performed to estimate mean differences, agreement intervals, bias, and repeatability coefficients (RPC) between parameters for repeated scans [39]. RPC was defined as (1.96)*100 where is the standard deviation of the differences (d = y1-y2) each expressed as a proportion of their mean (m=(y1 + y2)/2). [40] Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were estimated for cardiac-resolved image parameters comparison. Statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB. P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance before Holm-Bonferroni correction.

Results

Real-time images were successfully reconstructed for all datasets in all volunteers (N = 10), and no evidence of bulk motion corruption was observed.

Comparison of real-time cardiac re-binning and high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images

Summary of cardiac-resolved flow waveforms for the spinal canal and right ICA are displayed in Fig. 7. From visual inspection of cardiac waveforms, real-time images re-binned to the cardiac cycle (right column) showed lower cardiac pulsatile amplitudes compared to high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images (left column). A quantitative summary of flow rates (a), pulsatility index (b) and stroke volume (c) is shown in Fig. 8. Estimated flow rates were similar between real-time and cardiac-resolved reconstructions, with only a significant difference in ICAR (P = 0.004). In contrast, pulsatility index measures from the high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved reconstruction were significantly higher (ICAR, ICAL, VAR, VAL, all with P = 0.004; IJVR, IJVL with P = 0.008). The percentage loss of pulsatility index from the high temporal resolution cardiac reconstruction compared to real-time images with cardiac re-binning was as follows: ICAR = 55 ± 6%, ICAL = 63 ± 7%, VAR = 75 ± 8%, VAL = 77 ± 8%, IJVR = 56 ± 13%, and IJVL = 60 ± 12%. Stroke volume (ΔV) was also significantly higher from high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved reconstructed images (P = 0.004). Stroke volume comparisons for arterial and venous segments can be found in Supplemental Fig. 11. Table 2 summarizes Spearman’s rank correlations between flow parameters derived from both types of reconstructions. High correlations were measured for flow rates and CSF stroke volumes (ρ ≥ 0.95), and lower for pulsatility index (ρ ~ 0.45–0.88).

Discussion

Previously, we introduced an interleaved multi-venc 2D PC MRI technique that enables the synchronous assessment of blood and CSF flow and validated its capability to characterize cardiac dynamics in healthy participants [31]. In this follow-up study, we applied advanced model-based reconstruction methods [14, 32] to achieve high frame rate, real-time imaging using interleaved dual-venc 2D PC MRI. This approach was employed to investigate both respiratory and cardiac dynamics and evaluate the effects of various breathing patterns, including free breathing, paced breathing, and breath holding, on synchronous and asynchronous measurements of cranio-spinal blood and CSF flow.

The main findings of the study were a significantly higher blood and CSF flow cardiac coupling in the synchronous cross-correlation measures as compared to asynchronous measures. Furthermore, synchronous cross-correlation revealed significant cardiac coupling differences across breathing patterns not observable from asynchronous data. Respiratory cross-correlations were also higher in synchronous compared to asynchronous measures. Cardiac and respiratory blood and CSF flow power were significantly different across breathing patterns. Flow rate repeatability was highest during free breathing compared to paced breathing and breath holds scans, and higher in arteries and spinal canal compared to veins. Similar flow rates and high CSF stroke volume correlations were measured in real-time re-binned cardiac resolved images compared to high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images; however, real-time imaging underestimated cardiac pulsatility index. To our knowledge, this is the first study which evaluates synchronous measures of neurofluids using dual-venc real-time 2D PC MRI. Together, these findings indicate that synchronous measures can significantly benefit the evaluation of neurofluid dynamics and couplings during breathing paradigms.

Results support neurofluid coupling assessment from real-time imaging can benefit from synchronous measures of blood and CSF flow. Asynchronous measures can be more susceptible to changes in heart rate, respiration, and other physiological processes, which could obfuscate the assessment of blood and CSF flow couplings. Synchronous cardiac coupling results showed a significantly higher coupling during free breathing compared to breath hold scans, likely reflecting a reduction in cardiac pulsations during the breath hold. Neurofluids cardiac couplings were higher than respiratory couplings. Respiratory couplings did not show significant differences across breathing patterns in synchronous measures and only in one vein (IJVL (PB vs. BH)) in asynchronous measures. Respiratory coupling correlations were on average higher in paced breathing scans compared to breath hold scans across all vessel segments, but not significantly different after Holm-Bonferroni corrections, likely influenced by the small participant sample size of the study (N = 10).

Power spectral density analyses showed significant differences in flow power during breathing patterns for both cardiac and respiratory frequencies in blood and CSF measures. Generally, cardiac flow power was highest in the free breathing scans and lowest in the breath hold scans. However, respiratory power was significantly higher in paced breathing scans and lower in free breathing scans. Veins displayed lower normalized cardiac flow power compared to arteries, but higher respiratory power especially during paced breathing. Our results are in line with a previous real-time single venc 2D PC MRI study, which evaluated flow power in blood and CSF during free breathing, paced breathing and deep (paced and forced) breathing [18]. In that study, researchers also observed the highest cardiac flow power in the free breathing scans and highest respiratory flow power in the deep breathing scans.

Generally, higher flow rates were associated with free breathing scans compared to paced breathing and breath hold scans, agreeing with a previous study which also observed mean flow rate decrement in paced and forced breathing scans compared to free breathing using real-time single venc 2D PC MRI [18]. Bland-Altman analyses support high repeatability of blood and CSF flow measures, especially during free breathing and in CSF and arteries compared to veins. However, a negative bias was measured in arteries and CSF during paced breathing and breath hold scans. We attribute these changes to an influence from the order the scans were acquired. While free breathing scans were acquired in a back-to-back fashion, a breath hold scan was collected between the paced breathing repeated scans. This data collection strategy attempted to prioritize collecting a complete dataset of breathing patterns scans over collecting repeated measurements. However, a strong physiological response was observed after the breath hold. During breath hold scans, the heart rate was significantly higher (group avg. ~67 BPM) before (initial ~ 20s of scanning) than after the breath hold (last ~ 20s of scanning) (group avg. ~60 BPM) (P = 0.039), highlighting a strong physiological response during the scan. To induce a strong physiological response during the breath hold scan, participants were instructed to attempt to push air out, likely leading to effects similar to a Valsalva maneuver. Heart rates were not significantly variable during the free breathing and paced breathing scans; however, the breath hold scans effects could had impacted the repeated paced breathing scan. Furthermore, when group-wise breathing pattern flow rate comparisons were performed by averaging over repeated measures, no significant differences were observed between breathing patterns. However, when considering flow rates from repeated scans separately, significant flow rate differences across breathing patterns were observed (Supplemental Fig. 7). The lack of a significant negative bias in venous outflow during the repeated paced breathing and breath hold scans is likely related to the greater variability observed in venous measurements across different breathing patterns. This is in part due to inconsistent cycle timing between different respiratory maneuvers, as seen in Fig. 3 for paced breathing. In addition to venous flow being more sensitive to respiratory-induced pressure changes [13], the high velocity encoding (venc = 75 cm/s) was optimized for arterial flow, likely resulting in higher venous measurement variability and reduced sensitivity.

Comparison of real-time images re-binned to the cardiac cycle against high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images revealed similar flow rates and high correlation with CSF stroke volume; however, vessel pulsatility index and stroke volume were significantly lower in real-time re-binned images. Previous studies evaluating cardiac oscillations from single venc 2D PC MRI real-time and cine imaging in vessels and spinal canal also found similar flow rates, high correlations with stroke volume, and smaller pulsatility index in the real-time acquisitions [11, 12]. In our study, the temporal resolution of real-time images was significantly lower than the high resolution cardiac-resolved images (122ms vs. 31ms), likely significantly decreasing accuracy of cardiac waveforms including the depiction of the systolic peaks. Another study using real-time single venc 2D PC MRI to measure CSF flow in the cerebral aqueduct, found an underestimation of cardiac oscillations on real-time images [14]. In that study, lower cardiac oscillations were speculated to be related to spatiotemporal resolution tradeoffs and the use of radial sampling which undersamples higher frequencies of k-space. Like our study, they used model based iterative reconstructions (e.g. compressed sensing, local low rank) to generate real-time images from undersampled datasets. These types of reconstructions that exploit data sparsity have a tendency for spatiotemporal smoothing which may also impact depiction of high frequency cardiac oscillations during real-time imaging [32]. This is further supported by the magnitude of the observed reductions in pulsatility index and stroke volume. The percentage loss in these measures was greater than previously reported in studies using CINE and EPI-PC methods [11, 41], with the most pronounced reduction observed in the vertebral artery pulsatility index (~ 75%). This decrease may be attributed to the combined effects of lower temporal resolution and the smaller size of the vertebral arteries, which makes them more susceptible to spatiotemporal smoothing effects from model-based iterative reconstructions.

This study has several limitations. Real-time cardiac oscillations were significantly reduced compared to high temporal resolution cardiac-resolved images which may have impacted cardiac result quantification. A breath hold scan was acquired between the two paced breathing scans, resulting in a longer interval between repeats. This extended interval, along with the breath hold scan itself, likely affected the participant’s physiological state during the second paced breathing scan, thereby reducing the repeatability of the flow measurements. Participants were visually and verbally instructed on how to perform the breathing patterns paradigms and were monitored by the respiratory bellow signal to ensure performance of the task. While this study investigated technique feasibility, a more rigorous approach may be necessary to ensure repeatability of breathing task across participants. For example, a manometer can be used to objectively measure and verify the expiratory effort of a breath hold like Valsalva maneuver. Neuronally evoked responses were not quantified in this study and could assert influence in neurofluids flow. The respiratory frequency band used for cross-correlation analysis was defined as ≤ 0.50 Hz of the demeaned signal. This range also encompasses the slower neurogenic fluctuation band (< 0.1 Hz) and the variations induced by breathing maneuvers. Consequently, respiratory cross-correlations were likely influenced by non-respiratory components. This limitation stems from the challenges of applying a low-pass filter, given the constraints of our scan duration and imaging during respiratory maneuvers. Further investigation is required to assess the efficacy of the proposed measurements in disease-specific contexts.

Conclusions

Characterization of blood and CSF flow dynamics and couplings during breathing patterns paradigms can benefit from synchronous neurofluid measurements facilitated by interleaved dual-venc 2D PC MRI. Breathing patterns can enact significant effects on cardiac and respiratory flow rates, power, and arterial- and venous-CSF couplings. Some of these relationships can be obfuscated in neurofluids assessment from asynchronous measurements.
