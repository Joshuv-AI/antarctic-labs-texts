General practitioners’ views on inappropriate prescribing for older patients: a qualitative study through focus groups
Authors: Libert Gaël, Kacenelenbogen Nadine, De Breucker Sandra, Bensliman Rachida
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInappropriate prescribing (IP) is a common problem in the older population. Despite numerous attempts to tackle this issue, it remains a public health concern. In most European countries, general practitioners (GPs) are responsible for global primary care and are thus gatekeepers for the adequacy of medicines, specifically for older people. An in-depth analysis of the situation is necessary to understand why this phenomenon is still prevalent and to identify solutions that could help avoid IP in primary practice.MethodA qualitative study based on five focus groups (FG) comprising nine to thirteen general practitioners was conducted in the French-speaking part of Belgium. Participants were recruited among the Local Medical Evaluation Groups (LMEG) and selected to guarantee heterogeneity in working organisations, years of professional experience, gender and age. All interviews were recorded with prior agreement and transcribed in verbatims. The transcripts were coded and analysed to highlight the primary themes, considering dominant and marginal discourses.ResultsFifty four GPs participated in the study, with an average experience of 30 years. IPs are perceived as a significant problem in the older population, leading to dangerous health situations. The issue is associated with polypharmacy and multimorbidity, and GPs stated to manage IP using their clinical experience. Most of the study participants faced difficulties managing IP, mostly due to the specificity and complexity of elderly care. Indeed, managing an older patient is challenging due to medical complexity, poor adherence to drug regimes, or low medical literacy. In addition, the medical environment of general practice is challenging, with multiple providers, lack of time, prescribing routine and the absence of effective communication with specialists or other care providers. Additionally, the tools and support available to help medication management in primary care are inadequate. Enhancing collaboration with pharmacists is perceived as a strong potential facilitator.ConclusionThis article addresses the complex management of IP in the old age, from the point of view of GPs. Interesting clues were highlighted, like the need to clarify roles of healthcare providers, the better fit of tools to facilitate medication’s review with particularities of GPs needs and the empowerment of pharmacist collaboration.Keywords: Inappropriate prescribing, Polypharmacy, General medicine, Older patients, Qualitative study

Introduction

In 2021, the proportion of persons aged 65 years and over represented 21% of the European population (18% ten years ago), with projections showing a constant yearly increase in Europe and worldwide [1, 2]. These numbers imply that healthcare systems will need to deal a significant increase in the number of older patients in the next few years [2].

Older adults are more vulnerable than the young population due to specific patterns of physiological aging, including changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, atypical clinical presentation of diseases [3–5] and the higher prevalence of polypharmacy(PP) [5] resulting from multimorbidity [5–7]. Approximately 30% of individuals are affected by inappropriate prescribing (IP) [6, 8] and are, therefore, more likely to develop adverse effects from the treatments they receive [9]. Inappropriate prescribing (IP) is defined as a prescription that do not meet the patient’s needs and is likely to have harmful effects. The concept covers mis-prescription, over-prescription and under-prescription [10–12].

The IP’s problem is observed throughout Western Europe [6, 7, 13] with prevalence of IP among the over-65 population estimated to be between 12 and 60%, depending on the research settings (e.g., nursing home-dwelling vs. community-dwelling older adults). IP leads to a significant increase in hospital readmission rates after discharge [5, 14, 15], medical consultations [5, 15] and falls [5]. The issue is also responsible for an altered general health condition [8] and a higher mortality risk [8]. All these issues lead to increasing costs to national healthcare systems [15].

In Western Europe, general practitioners (GPs) are generally responsible for the overall healthcare management and continuity in care of patients [16]. However, studies have shown that various factors can affect this fundamental function in regard to medication management, particularly in the old age, including the fear of going against patients’ wishes [17], a conflict between prescribing routines and guidelines [17–20], GPs reluctance to modify a complex treatment [17–20], insufficient consultation time [18–20] and difficulties interacting with other healthcare providers [18–20].

During the past decade, several interventions have been implemented to deal with those difficulties [21–23]. Among these, the most cited are medication software improvements, medication reviews by multidisciplinary teams, implementation of supporting tools (e.g., lists or scales), specific training, and the implication of the pharmacist in medication counselling and medication reviews.

To our knowledge, there is no recent data on how GPs manage IP in Western European countries, in parallel with these interventions. This study aims to identify and analyse GP’s views of IP in patients over 65 years make recommendations for improvement.

Methodology

To compare different fields of research, we set up five focus groups comprising family doctors practicing in the French-speaking part of Belgium. The FG methodology was chosen because it allows thorough examination of a subject in the field, encourages interaction between participants and the analysis of familiar situations and courses of action. As we did not have studies of this design to refer to the sample was limited to GPs to assess the general situation of IP management in primary care. Indeed, in Belgium these physicians are responsible for the overall care of older adults, whether community-dwelling or in nursing homes.

Recruitment procedures

To be accredited, GPs in Belgium must participate in continuing professional education activities [24]. These activities occur primarily within the Local Medical Evaluation Group (LMEG). Accordingly, we emailed the doctors in charge of the LMEGs in the French-speaking part of Belgium, inviting them to participate in the study. During the recruitment phase, we tried to maximize the group’s diversity in terms of the participants’ engagement in a specific type of organisation (private practice, doctors’ association and multidisciplinary teams), their geographical area of practice (urban, rural or mixed), their experience in family medicine, their age, and gender. To avoid recruitment bias, we limited the presence of so-called “expert doctors” (doctors with academic positions, long-term care coordinators or GPs working in geriatric wards in hospitals), assistant doctors, or pensioned doctors who followed continuing education.

Conduct of the interviews

The doctors willing to participate were invited to meet in a specified location and signed the informed consent. To facilitate interactions, meetings were organised in the same location where they follow their continuing education. The sessions lasted one to two hours. The interview protocol, prepared based on the literature review [5], consisted of four themes:

Participation in the study was voluntary and unfunded. Focus group sessions were conducted until thematic saturation was obtained. To assess saturation, topics were divided from the most important to the least relevant: “main topic”, “sub-topic”, and “notions”. Saturation was achieved in the fourth session when no new sub-topic appeared during group analysis.

Data processing and data analysis

Each FG was recorded, transcribed, and made anonymous by attributing a determined identifier to each participant (codes FG 1 to FG 5 were attributed to the groups and each doctor was identified by a D followed by a number). Grounded theory [25] was chosen to analyse verbatim speech to facilitate the emergence of new perspectives. Thus, the data processing consisted of segmentation into units of meaning, coding, and definition of the main categories of analysis, considering dominant and marginal discourses. To facilitate thorough analysis, the researchers worked manually, without software.

I) General practicioners’ views of IP

The interviewees viewed IP as a frequent problem, strongly linked to polypharmacy and various underlying conditions such as the presence of multimorbidity, social isolation and language barriers (this element was pointed out by GPs working in Brussels, where patient sometimes does not speak French).

“Let us consider a diabetic patient with macrovascular complications. If, on top of that, he/she has another illness, he/she will immediately find themselves with a very high number of medications. So, of course, it increases the risk of iatrogenicity, but it is also related to the comorbidity itself. That’s not a reason to qualify his/her medication as not appropriate” (FG1-D2)

The most frequently cited drugs during the FGs were those affecting the central nervous system, statins, antihypertensives drugs, and anti-inflammatory drugs. Adverse effects and complications mentioned included drug interactions, hypotension, falls, and delirium.

While age is a factor, GPs often believed that the specific health conditions of frail older patients were the primary cause of IP.

“The focus should not be on age, but on the health of the person. (FG5-D3)

“In fact, the older you get... there is another normality that takes over. They are not the same standards as when you are young...” (FG1-D3)

The participants also mentioned multimorbidity as an independent cause of IP, with a risk of cumulative prescriptions. In this context, they reported using their experience and knowledge of the respective patients they have been following for years to customize the treatment.

“For example, when you are called by a patient in a nursing home and you see 15 medications, with multiple interactions and multiple side effects, with one medicine prescribed to treat the side effect of the first one…” (FG3-D1)

“It is true that as general practitioners we have the advantage of knowing our patients, sometimes for several decades. There’s information there that you can’t find in books.” (FG4-D4)

II) Patient-related difficulties

Participants experienced difficulties with some patients’ dependence on certain drugs, limiting their ability to make therapeutic adjustments. Controlling the use of prescribed drugs by patients is also challenging, and there is a risk of inducing an unsuitable change of dosage, for example.

“People are used to taking this little pill for sleeping. Eventually they don’t have too many problems with it because they have been taking it for 30 to 40 or 50 years, and finally I feel a little troubled forcing them to come off.” (FG2-D1)

“So we give them the treatment... It doesn’t work. We increase the dosage, it doesn’t work either. But they don’t take it, so obviously we’ll increase the dose again... and the day they start taking it, they’ll take a dose that’s four times too high...” (FG5-D2)

The GPs also noted that patients do not always understand the purpose of their medicines, impacting their ability to be compliant.

“I’m struck by the total lack of understanding of some patients... There is a lot of information being communicated, which you think people understand, but it is not the truth, it is difficult to get a grip on that.” (FG1-D2)

III) Environment-related difficulties

This theme relates to the doctor’s own ability to change patient’s medication. Most participants noted the time-consuming nature of a medication review in a context where other care must be provided in a defined and limited time schedule.

“I’m sorry, but I just can’t bring myself to consider taking the time to check all the treatments of my elderly patients. Sometimes they call you: ‘Quick, hurry! You must go and see them’. And at the same time you need to review their entire treatment.” (FG2-D8)

Many of the interviewees felt the need to treat their patients’ concerns with medicines, involving the addition of new, sometimes unnecessary drugs, resulting in a domino effect. This effect seemed more frequent in collaborative work in nursing homes. In contrast, some doctors reported that certain medical situations resulted in IP (e.g.., there was a dissociation between theory and clinical feeling).

“ Let’s say that the problem is that if a patient is constipated, the nurses (of the nursing home) call us. If the patient is ill at the same time, nurses also want everything to be sorted out separately, whereas it turns out that it is constipation that explains everything else.” (FG2-D2)

“We can’t use neuroleptics, but sometimes we use them anyway. Because the situation is such that... We try to make a prescription that corresponds to the patient’s needs, but also that minimises secondary effects.” (FG1-D3)

The need to treat also includes prescribing convenience for care staff, particularly in nursing homes situations, where it seems difficult to communicate effectively with nurses and use new electronic prescription tools.

“We are asked to prescribe one medicine per prescription for organisational reasons (in nursing homes)... and finally, when we receive a prescription for the next three, we have 40 prescriptions to sign... Who can check 40 prescriptions?” (FG5-D7)

“In the nursing homes now, we are planning ahead for prescriptions. In other words, we ask you: ‘In case your patient has a fever, what would you give them?’” (FG5-D7)

Furthermore, a phenomenon of routine seems to develop over time, with doctors no longer aware of medication errors, especially when new medicines are added. Several providers reported that a second opinion was helpful in their prescribing routine, independent of whether the provider was an assistant, a colleague, or the patient’s pharmacist, and this occurred more often when the provider was operating in a working association.

“It’s also if you only see patients for acute problems every time and you don’t schedule a consultation to review the chronic treatment. We don’t have a view on overall health and we only treat the acute aspects all the time.” (FG3-D6)

The GPs spoke repeatedly about their responsibility towards their patients and their medication. This notion has a causal component, as many medicines are prescribed by GPs themselves. Therefore, it seems that the GPs cannot fully guarantee the chronic care management of patients as they are potentially implied in the root cause of IP.

“We blame the specialists a lot... But even as general practitioners, we have prescribed medicines to young patients, whom we have not seen grow old. And we didn’t necessarily doubt our prescription. Neither in terms of its use, nor in its form, nor in its dosage, nor in the possible interactions with new illnesses or medicines that have come along”. (FG1-D2)

Discussing this topic, GPs said they had difficulties working effectively with specialists. Thus, contrary to general medicine, specialists would not allow a comprehensive treatment of the patient, favoring a limited and targeted treatment of the relevant condition.

“Each specialist has his own view, and that’s it: ‘The patient’s diabetes, hypertension...’ or ‘kidney problem’ are very important. And so they each treat their own little bit without really looking at each other’s medicines.” (FG3-D2)

This difference is linked to the intrinsic character of specialized medicine, and GPs recognised that they were not entitled to act as the coordinator is was not entrusted to them in daily practice.

“They have seen specialists who have said ‘certain things’... We must dare to go against them.” (FG2-D2)

“I don’t think it’s a problem of challenges. It is a matter of trust. Will the persons we treat have enough confidence to accept that someone says to them: ‘Look, your cardiologist says “this”, I say “something else”?’” (FG2-D1)

Having an abundance of healthcare providers also makes medication management difficult, especially in nursing homes. The pivotal moment for GPs was the discharge of the patient from the hospital.

“When they come in with Perindopril® and in hospital they were given Coversyl®, they come out from hospital with Coversyl®. So they go home, and they continue to take their Perindopril®, and they add the Coversyl®...” (FG1-D7)

“I followed a patient taking Sintrom® (Acenocoumarol). She was used to taking two pills once a day for a long time and was perfectly fine. And then in hospital, probably as a result of small diet changes, she was discharged with the following daily dosage: ‘2 – 2 – 3, 2 – 2 – 3’. I was called because she had terrible bruises. And in fact, she took 2 in the morning, 2 at noon and 3 in the evening because it was written on her box: ‘2 – 2 – 3’.” (FG4-D2)

IV) Considerations to avoid inappropriate prescribing

When GPs were asked which solutions might improve the appropriateness of prescriptions in older patients, the majority relied, amongst others, on their practical experience to reassess the chronic treatment, using their acquired knowledge and the quality of the relationship with their patients. When they had to review a prescription, they used either paper media (belgian compendium, medical reviews, etc.) or electronic media (applications, software, etc.).

Validated tools such as STOPP&START list were poorly known and infrequently used by GPs. STOPP&START was commonly mentioned by a nursing home medical coordinator or a doctor with an academic job function, but not by doctors who were not involved in such activities.

The GPs perceived collaboration with pharmacists positively, with a particular interest in the role of the reference pharmacist. Many people recognised that the pharmacist’s competence concerning medicines could counterbalance their own limits and that many errors could be avoided through efficient teamwork. However, physicians also reported on the challenges of such collaborations, particularly in relation to communication difficulties and lack of habits.

“It should be their responsibility to look at all these interactions, because that’s their real skill. And, support in ensuring compliance.” (FG3-D9)

“Yes, or about dosages, half-life times of medicines... Well, things we don’t know anymore...” (GF3-M6)

“With regard to the pharmacist, I still have very little interaction to discuss the patient’s treatment. It’s for prescription errors, switching medicines... It would be interesting to have the opinion of a pharmacist who really knows pharmacopeia, but I have more “shop” types of relations with the pharmacist.” (FG3-D6)

The collaboration with geriatric day hospitals (GDHs) was discussed as a means of helping GPs improve medication appropriateness and was regarded as valuable due to the convenience and information centralisation of GDHs. However, there were differences between geographical regions: the collaboration between GPs and GDHs was more favorable in the Brussels region than in the Walloon region. More generally, GPs used GDHs primarily to obtain swift, complex assessments of frail patients rather than for the preventive aspects they could offer.

“However, what would be interesting is the possibility of sending a patient, at our suggestion, to do a number of exams that would take 5 or 6 appointments. This would be interesting.” (FG5-D3)

Discussion

This field research analyses the practice of family doctors in relation to IP, using grounded theory applied in focus group. We have collected interesting data related to the way on how IP was perceived and in particular how GPs used to manage it. We pointed out clue elements appeared to make GPs’ task difficult. This includes inadequate communication with specialists especially after hospital discharge, difficulties in collaborating with nursing home staffs and the lack of easy-to-use tools to conduct medication reviews. On the other hand, the involvement of the community pharmacist in medication reviews instead represents a potential source of improvement.

General considerations regarding inappropriate prescribing

GPs regularly face IP issues with older patients. Similar to Bokhof et al., Ailabouini et al., and Xu et al. [18–20], we found that IP was perceived as standard in older adults. Indeed, these patients are more challenging to help during medical consultations due to a combination of social, personal, and medical elements related to their age. It is not new, for example, that poor adherence or lack of understanding can affect the willingness to start a deprescribing process [19]. On the other hand, older people tend to present complex medical syndromes that affect, and potentially worsens pre-existing comorbidities [5, 19]. The GPs in our sample showed the tendency to focus on “misprescription” and did not mention under/overprescribing as a possible definition of IP. Likewise, they did not use the STOPP/START list, which may mean new post-university training is required in Belgium to promote medication regime adequacy.

Interprofessional collaboration: myth or reality?

We also found that several elements related to multidisciplinary teamwork were likely to increase the risk of IP. The complexity of caring for older people [18, 20], the number of care providers they must rely on [18] and time constraints [18–20] were identified in the literature. Our study underlines GPs’ difficulties in dealing effectively with specialists and nursing home staff, especially during hospital discharge [19, 26, 27]. At this key moment, the transmission of information is often not adequate in meeting primary care needs. More generally, GPs faced issues dealing with specialised, and sometimes conflicting, advices. In this case, poor communication of medical information leads to clinical inertia [20] and does not ensure continuity of treatment, leading to fragmentation of care. This element has been previously highlighted by Bokhof et al., and Xu et al. [18, 20] and reinforces the importance of better integration of the different healthcare in the decision making process, including GPs. Bokhof et al., and O’Reilly et al. [18, 28] highlight that interprofessional collaboration is not easy, especially for doctors, although most of them seem to be in favour of it [28]. The lack of seamless healthcare [18, 20] and the lack of involvement of GPs in geriatric health care pathways in Belgum might explain this finding. GPs seem unable to act as coordinators and ensure continuity of care. They are rarely involved in decision-making when multidisciplinary care is provided to patients, especially when there are significant ambient changes (e.g., hospitalisation) or treatments require multiple providers with the same role (e.g., doctors). Moros et al. [29] underline these issues, emphasising the importance of clarifying providers’ roles in collaborative situations and organising the patient experience through multiple healthcare providers. At the end, there is a lack of clarity in providers’ roles in the treatment of older patients, giving rise to questions such as whether a GP can question or adapt a prescription made by a specialist or whether a nurse can make suggestions to facilitate medicine intake.

Inadequacy of tools or supportive structures within clinical practice

There was a large discrepancy between the availability of tools or support for improving the prescribing process and its use in clinical practice. For example, like Clyne B et al. [27] we found that only a few GPs knew the STOPP/START list, except the “expert” doctors in our sample. For that reason, only few doctors use it in their practice. However, its implementation in medical computer programs used to date could integrate and ease its use more systematically [30]. These observations align with Bokhof et al., and Xu et al. [18, 20], who have pointed out that applying recommendations or guidelines to improve integrated care in primary care can be challenging. Tools and supports must be adapted to primary care medicine for example, OptiMeds tools for medication review in the nursing home [31].

A key role for the pharmacist

Of the solutions proposed by the GPs, a better integration of the pharmacist in the medication management processes seemed the most common. This element has been highlighted in research performed by Ailabouini et al. [19] and Palagyi et al. [26] in different countries. Pharmacists have already been involved in medication reviews [32–35] and direct counselling in hospitals and follow-up after discharge, and in settings linking hospitals and external pharmacies [32, 33]. Furthermore, pharmacists show an interest in this type of collaboration, nevertheless, its implementation must be guided, and each of the experts’ roles must be clarified [29]. Indeed, some authors reported it can be hard for doctors and pharmacist to cooperate with equity due to status differences [36]. Further analysis is required to support the involvement of community pharmacists in medication reviews and collaborate with physicians.

Strengths and limitations of the study and perspectives for future research

The main strength of this study is its field- and practice-based nature and the comparison between the data from five groups of GPs. This approach enabled us to address many issues related to prescribing medicines in first-line healthcare. However, to address this issue in an integrated manner, the insights of other healthcare providers who are involved in the care for older people should also be considered. Researchers should conduct a qualitative study involving everyone involved in prescribing practice in addition to GPs (e.g., geriatricians, pharmacists, hospital nurses, etc.). Furthermore, the study focused on the French-speaking part of Belgium and might not be generalisable. It would be insightful to conduct a similar study in the Flemish region of Belgium to compare results and reinforce our conclusions.

Conclusion

This study addresses the complex situation of GPs in their day-to-day practice regarding IP in older people. The focus groups’ analyse give insights into the various factors making it difficult to face this problem. It highlights considerations regarding the way health care providers collaborate in multidisciplinary network. Among them, adequate communication and effective interactions between providers were pointed out as facilitators. It also emerges that clarifying roles and limits of competence for each provider is fundamental, in particular with a better integration of GPs as primary healthcare’ coordinators. The pharmacist’s involvement as an expert in biochemistry to collaborate with physicians and review prescriptions also seems to be crucial, and its growing importance should be further integrated in the care for older patients. Moreover, it appear that large scale’s spread lists and tools to help physicians in their practice, are not enough tailored to face specific GPs’ needs.

Further analysis is needed to improve multidisciplinary collaboration. This includes enhancing lists/tools’ adequacy with needs in primary care physicians to facilitate medications reviews, in addition of comprehensive research to clarify roles of health care providers, specifically in the context of IP’s management in older patients.
