Dietary choline intake during pregnancy and birth weight in a Chinese population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jiaomei Yang, Ziqi Xiao, Wanting Hu, Baligen Rekemubieke, Yue-Hua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies on dietary choline and fetal growth are limited and inconsistent, and the impacts of choline subtypes on fetal growth remain unexplored. We aimed to investigate the associations of maternal intakes of dietary choline and choline subtypes during pregnancy with birth weight, low birth weight (LBW), and small for gestational age (SGA).MethodsWe used a multi-stage stratified cluster random sampling method to recruit 7310 women in Shaanxi China. Maternal diets during pregnancy was collected using a validated food frequency questionnaire. Multilevel linear or logistic regression models were applied to assess the associations. Dose-response relationships were evaluated using restricted cubic spline curves.ResultsThe mean total choline intake was 331.0 (SD: 162.4) mg/d, and the mean birth weight was 3263.7 (SD: 440.1) g. Each 100 mg increase in total choline, free choline, glycerophosphocholine, phosphocholine, phosphatidylcholine, and sphingomyelin was associated with an increase in birth weight of 18.7 (95% CI = 8.8–28.6), 25.0 (3.4–46.6), 51.1 (0.6-101.7), 128.3 (7.5-249.2), 15.3 (2.2–28.4), and 105.4 (33.5-177.3) g, respectively. Comparing the highest with the lowest tertiles of total choline, phosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, glycerophosphocholine, and phosphocholine intakes, participants had lower odds of LBW and SGA, with the ORs for LBW ranging from 0.50 (0.30–0.81) to 0.62 (0.45–0.87) and for SGA from 0.71 (0.57–0.88) to 0.81 (0.68–0.96). There were L-shaped relationships between total choline and choline subtypes intakes and the odds of LBW.ConclusionsHigher maternal intakes of dietary choline and choline subtypes during pregnancy was associated with higher birth weight and lower odds of LBW and SGA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01339-1.

Introduction

Birth weight, a critical indicator of neonatal development, exerts lifelong impacts on cardiovascular and metabolic health [1–3]. Adverse birth weight related outcomes including low birth weight (LBW) and small for gestational age (SGA) have imposed a substantial burden on global public health over the past decades [3]. Each year worldwide, approximately 20 million infants are born with LBW [4], and more than 23 million are born SGA [5]. In China, the prevalence of LBW is reported as 4.5% [6], and the prevalence of SGA is estimated at 4.6% [7]. Moreover, LBW and SGA infants exhibit short-term vulnerabilities, such as neonatal infections and hypoglycemia, and long-term risks of cardiometabolic complications, such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and kidney disease [1, 2, 8]. Therefore, implementing targeted interventions to address modifiable determinants of birth weight and reduce the incidence of adverse birth outcomes may yield benefits for human health throughout the whole life cycle.

Maternal nutrition in pregnancy, an important modifiable factor, is crucial for fetal growth [9–13]. Choline, a conditionally essential nutrient, exerts critical biological functions during pregnancy possibly through the mechanisms of cell membrane synthesis, neurodevelopment, lipid homeostasis, and epigenetic regulation [14–19]. While several previous studies have investigated the role of circulation choline biomarkers in birth weight [20, 21], it is crucial to distinguish these measures from dietary intake. There is currently no validated single biomarker of choline status, and circulating concentrations do not always correlate strongly with dietary intake [16]. However, the direct evidence about the association between choline intake and birth weight among humans is limited [22]. To our knowledge, only one recent study has specifically examined this association, reporting a positive association between maternal choline intake and birth weight [22]. Moreover, existing related researches among pregnant women typically focus on total choline, without considering different subcategories of choline. Choline consists of water-soluble forms, including free choline, glycerophosphocholine (GpCho), and phosphocholine (PCho), as well as lipid-soluble forms such as phosphatidylcholine (PtdCho) and sphingomyelin (SM) [23, 24]. Dietary choline subtypes differ in absorption (e.g., lipid-soluble forms bypass hepatic first-pass metabolism, whereas water-soluble forms do not) and function (e.g., membrane synthesis, neurotransmission) [16]. Examining only total choline intake may obscure subtype-specific relationships with birth weight. It is crucial to address this knowledge gap by investigating the associations between different choline subtypes during pregnancy and birth weight, thereby informing both optimal dietary choline intake and targeted prenatal supplementation strategies to promote fetal growth.

Therefore, we conducted a population-based study to investigate the associations of maternal intakes of total choline and its subtypes (free choline, GpCho, PCho, PtdCho and SM) during pregnancy with newborn birth weight as well as LBW and SGA among pregnant women in Northwest China.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was based on data from a population-based cross-sectional study conducted in Shaanxi Province, Northwest China between August and November 2013. This population-based cross-sectional study aimed to assess maternal nutritional status during pregnancy and identify the determinants of birth outcomes. The study design has been previously described in detail [9, 11]. Briefly, a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method was employed to enroll 30,027 women who were pregnant during 2010–2013 across 30 district-level administrative units in Shaanxi Province. The sampling procedure was conducted as follows: twenty counties and ten districts were randomly sampled in Shaanxi, China based on the proportion of urban to rural populations, overall population size, and fertility rate; within each selected county, six townships were chosen at random, and from each township, six villages were further randomly sampled; similarly, in each selected district, three streets were randomly identified, with six communities selected from each; 30 eligible women were randomly recruited from each village and 60 from each community. Among the participants, 7,750 women who had delivered infants within the preceding 12 months were interviewed to report diets in pregnancy. We further excluded participants who had multiple gestation (n = 87), lacked data on offspring birth weight (n = 65), or had an implausible total energy intake (less than 500 kcal/day or greater than 5000 kcal/day) (n = 288). Finally, we included 7,310 eligible pregnant women in the analyses.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University Health Science Center (No.2012008), and all participants signed written informed consent.

Dietary assessment

We collected the dietary data of participants during pregnancy by a 107-item semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) at 0–12 months (median: 3; 10-90th percentiles: 0–7) after delivery [9, 11]. This FFQ was adopted from a previously validated FFQ designed for pregnant women in Shaanxi, China [25]. The FFQ was validated against six 24 h dietary recalls for a range of nutrients, including protein (r = 0.49), folate (r = 0.47), and vitamin B₂ (r = 0.46) [23]. Choline intake was not specifically assessed in the validation study; however, food items in the FFQ capture the primary dietary sources of choline in this population, and FFQ assessment of choline intake is a common approach in nutritional epidemiology [26–28]. The frequency scales of five food items (vegetable oils, animal oils, sugar, salt, and sauce) were open-ended. The remaining 102 food items were assessed using an eight-category frequency scale, ranging from never to more than twice daily, with portion sizes estimated by a standardized visual atlas. Although evidence regarding the longitudinal stability of dietary patterns during pregnancy is mixed [29–31], diet assessment during the entire pregnancy at one time was reasonable, convenient, and economical for large epidemiological survey [9, 11, 32]. Due to the lack of relevant data on choline content in Chinese foods, daily choline intake were derived by the food composition from the United States Department of Agriculture [33–35], which has been widely applied in nutritional studies involving Chinese and other western populations [26–28]. Total choline intake was calculated as the sum of free choline, GpCho, PCho, PtdCho, and SM. Dietary supplements during each trimester of pregnancy were also gathered in the survey; however, no choline supplements were reported among the study participants.

Birth outcomes assessment

In this study, the primary outcome was birth weight, and the secondary outcomes were LBW and SGA. The neonatal information including birth date, sex, birth weight, and gestational age was obtained by reviewing birth certificates. Birth weight was measured after delivery by the midwives using a baby scale, with the precision to the nearest 10 g. Gestational age at delivery was calculated according to self-reported last menstrual period and confirmed by ultrasound scans. Birth outcomes were ascertained by examining medical records including physical examinations, clinical diagnosis, and medical history. LBW was defined as birth weight < 2500 g [36]. SGA was defined as birth weight below the 10th percentile of the gestational age- and sex-specific international reference for fetal growth [37].

Covariates assessment

Trained research personnel administered structured questionnaires to systematically collect baseline characteristics through in-person interviews. The covariates included: (1) sociodemographic factors: geographic region (northern, southern, or central Shaanxi); urban-rural residence; maternal education (≤ primary, junior secondary, or ≥senior secondary); maternal occupation (farmer or nonfarmer); maternal age (< 25, 25–29, or ≥ 30 years); and parity (nulliparous or multiparous); (2) health-related behaviors during pregnancy: alcohol drinking (yes or no); passive smoking (yes or no); antenatal care visit frequency (< 6 or ≥ 6); anemia (yes or no); medication use (yes or no); and folate and/or iron supplements use (yes or no). Alcohol drinking included any one of alcoholic beverages (beer, wine, and liquor) consumed during pregnancy. Passive smoking was quantified as involuntary inhalation of environmental tobacco smoke for ≥ 15 min/day. Maternal anemia was clinically confirmed through venous blood hemoglobin measurement < 110 g/L. Medication exposure was defined as any medication use during pregnancy. Folate and/or iron supplementation was defined as the consumption of dietary supplements containing either folate or iron at least 14 consecutive days.

Statistical analyses

The baseline characteristics according to tertiles of dietary total choline intake were expressed as percentages or means, and differences among groups were compared by χ2 test or analysis of variance, as appropriate. The household wealth index was constructed via principal component analysis using the items reflecting economic levels (housing, transportation, income diversity, and household appliance) and stratified into tertiles (poor, medium, and rich). To address multicollinearity among nutrients in regression models, we employed principal component analysis to extract the first component from the intakes of 13 key nutrients (protein, fat, carbohydrates, vitamin A, thiamine, vitamin C, folate, riboflavin, vitamin E, calcium, zinc, iron, and selenium), in which 68.9% of the total variance was explained. Nutrient variables were entered into the principal component analysis without prior energy adjustment, consistent with previous methodological recommendations [38]. The selected nutrients represent key dietary factors with established associations with fetal growth in prior literature, and were included based on complete data availability within our study.

Considering the stratified multistage random sampling design, multilevel models were applied to assess the associations. Multilevel linear or logistic regression models were used to estimate birth weight changes or ORs (95% CIs) for LBW and SGA in association with dietary choline. Following the execution of four-level null models structured at the county (district), township (community), village (street), and individual levels, we found that within-group variations at the village (street) level were not statistically significant (all P > 0.05), along with very low intra-class correlation coefficients (all below 0.001). Consequently, we used simplified multilevel models incorporating random intercepts at the county (district) and township (community) levels. In multilevel regressions, we stratified dietary choline intakes (total choline, free choline, GpCho, PCho, PtdCho and SM) by tertiles to avoid the possible influences of extreme values. Given the high correlation among choline subtype intakes (all correlation coefficients > 0.60), mutual adjustment was not performed to avoid multicollinearity in the statistical models. Covariates for the regression models were selected based on two criteria: (1) established associations with birth weight in previous research [9, 11, 12], and (2) a change-in-estimate criterion, whereby a covariate was retained in the final model if its inclusion altered the effect estimate by more than 10% [39]. Model 1 was adjusted for total energy intake in pregnancy and socio-demographic characteristics (geographic area, residence, education, occupation, household wealth index, childbearing age, and parity). Model 2 was adjusted for all variables in Model 1, health-related behaviors in pregnancy (alcohol drinking, passive smoking, antenatal check visit frequency, anemia, medication use, and folate/iron supplements use), and principal component score based on the nutrient intakes. Models were additionally adjusted for offspring sex and gestational age at delivery in the analyses for birth weight and LBW. P for linear trend was calculated using the median intake of each tertile as a continuous variable. To further explore the shapes of the associations of dietary choline intake with birth outcomes, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves with three knots were plotted, as this setup resulted in the lowest Akaike Information Criterion when compared to models with four or five knots. The threshold intake level for each nutrient was defined as the point on the RCS curve beyond which the OR plateaued.

To evaluate potential effect modification, we performed subgroup analyses by baseline characteristics (offspring sex, geographic area, residence, household wealth index, maternal age, maternal education, maternal occupation, and parity), and evaluated interactions through the likelihood ratio tests. In sensitivity analyses, we repeated the primary models using energy-adjusted choline intake by the residual method [40]. This approach, while algebraically related to the standard multivariable model, isolates the variation in choline intake independent of total energy intake, thereby assessing the robustness of our findings to alternative energy-adjustment methods. Sensitivity analyses were also performed by additional adjustment for dietary intakes of other one-carbon metabolism nutrients, including folate, vitamin B12, and betaine.

All analyses were performed with the use of STATA software (version 15.1; StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Status of choline intake

Table 1 shows the estimated daily choline intake from different sources during pregnancy among the study participants. The mean (SD) intake of total choline intake was 331.0 (162.4) mg/d. The mean (SD) intakes of water-soluble choline and lipid-soluble choline were 166.8 (77.5) and 164.2 (103.9) mg/d, respectively. Phosphatidylcholine was the major form of choline, with the mean (SD) intake 151.6 (94.6) mg/d. The mean (SD) intakes of free choline, GpCho, PCho, and SM were 109.4 (47.5) mg/d, 43.1 (27.3) mg/d, 14.3 (11.0) mg/d, and 12.6 (15.5) mg/d, respectively.

Baseline characteristics of participants

The baseline characteristics of participants by tertiles of total choline intake are present in Table 2. Participants consuming more choline in pregnancy tended to be in central Shaanxi, in urban areas, better educated, non-farmers, wealthier, aged more than 25 years at delivery, at the first delivery, have more than 6 antenatal check visits, and take folate/ iron supplements. Total choline intake was positively associated with the intakes of total energy and most nutrient, and negatively associated with the intakes of fat and vitamin E (all P < 0.001). In offspring, the mean (SD) of birth weight was 3263.7 (440.1) g, with 3.3% LBW and 10.1% SGA. Significant differences existed in birth weight, gestational age, LBW, and SGA among tertiles of total choline intake (all P < 0.05).

Association of choline intake during pregnancy with birth weight, LBW, and SGA

Birth weight changes associated with choline intake from different sources during pregnancy are shown in Fig. 1. Each 100 mg increase in total choline intake during pregnancy was associated with the birth weight increase of 18.7 (95% CI: 8.8–28.6) g. Per 100 mg increase in maternal intakes of free choline, GpCho, PCho, PtdCho, and SM during pregnancy was associated with an increase in birth weight of 25.0 (3.4–46.6) g, 51.1 (0.6-101.7) g, 128.3 (7.5-249.2) g, 15.3 (2.2–28.4) g, and 105.4 (33.5-177.3) g, respectively.

As shown in Table 3, compared with those in the lowest tertile of total choline intake, participants in the highest tertile had lower odds of LBW and SGA, with the ORs (95% CIs) of 0.50 (0.30–0.81) and 0.74 (0.58–0.95), respectively. For each 100 mg increase in total choline intake, the odds of LBW and SGA were reduced by 16% (OR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.72–0.98) and 11% (OR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.82–0.95), respectively. The odds of LBW and SGA were reduced with increasing tertiles of GpCho, PCho, PtdCho and SM intakes (all P for trend < 0.05). Comparing the highest tertiles with the lowest tertiles of GpCho, PCho, PtdCho and SM intakes, participants had lower odds of LBW and SGA, with the ORs for LBW ranging from 0.52 (95% CI: 0.38–0.71) to 0.62 (95% CI: 0.45–0.87) and the ORs for SGA ranging from 0.71 (95% CI: 0.57–0.88) to 0.81 (95% CI: 0.68–0.96). However, there was no significant associations of free choline intake during pregnancy with LBW or SGA.

An L-shaped relationship was found between total choline intake during pregnancy and LBW (P for nonlinearity = 0.015) (Fig. 2). The odds of LBW decreased with increasing intake of total choline during pregnancy and reached a plateau of total choline intake above 541 mg/d. The L-shaped relationships were also found between the intakes of GpCho, PCho, PtdCho and SM during pregnancy and the odds of LBW (all P for nonlinearity < 0.05) (Figure S1), with the lowest odds at the intakes of 79 mg/d, 27 mg/d, 245 mg/d, and 34 mg/d, respectively. No nonlinear associations were observed between the intakes of total choline, GpCho, PCho, and SM during pregnancy and the odds of SGA (Fig. 2 and Figure S2). An L-shaped relationship between PtdCho intake and SGA was observed with a plateau of the intake above 265 mg/d (Figure S2). We did not observe any significant nonlinear associations between free choline intake during pregnancy and the odds of LBW or SGA (Figure S1 and Figure S2).

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

In subgroup analyses, the associations of total choline intake during pregnancy with birth weight, LBW, and SGA were not significantly modified by geographic area, residence, household wealth index, maternal age, maternal education, maternal occupation, or parity (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Figure S3, Figure S4, and Figure S5). The associations of total choline intake during pregnancy with birth weight and LBW did not alter by offspring sex. However, the association between total choline intake during pregnancy and SGA seemed to be stronger among girls than boys (P for interaction = 0.001). Sensitivity analyses did not yield appreciably different results after using the residual energy-adjusted choline intake as exposure or additional adjustment for dietary intakes of folate, vitamin B12, and betaine during pregnancy (Table S1-S2).

Discussion

In this population-based study of Chinese pregnant women, we observed that higher choline intake during pregnancy was associated with higher birth weight and lower odds of LBW and SGA. The analyses for choline subtypes revealed that maternal intakes of free choline, GpCho, PCho, PtdCho, and SM during pregnancy were positivity associated with birth weight, and maternal intakes of GpCho, PCho, PtdCho, and SM during pregnancy were negatively associated with the odds of LBW and SGA. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the associations of dietary choline from different sources during pregnancy with birth weight and the related birth outcomes.

Current recommendations for choline intake during pregnancy vary across regions and countries [19]; however, studies have shown that most pregnant women fail to meet the adequate choline intake worldwide [19]. The present study found that the mean intake of dietary choline among the study population was 331.0 mg/d, which was below the recommended adequate intake for pregnant women in China (420.0 mg/d) [41]. Dietary choline intake in the current study was comparable to that observed among pregnant women in New Zealand (316.0 mg/d) [42] and Canada (338.0 mg/d) [43], and was higher than the intake reported among both omnivorous (267.8 mg/d) and vegetarian (204.4 mg/d) pregnant women in Germany [44]. Eggs, beef, chicken, milk, fish, and certain plant-based foods such as cruciferous vegetables and some legumes are recognized as good dietary sources of choline. In the present study, choline intake was primarily derived from cereals and tubers (38.6%) and eggs (22.9%), consistent with the dietary patterns characteristic of Northwest China, which are typified by a predominant reliance on cereal-based foods. These findings underscore the importance of region-specific dietary assessments and provide an evidence base for developing targeted nutritional interventions aimed at improving choline intake among women in Northwest China .

To date, human studies on choline and birth weight-related outcomes remain limited, and there is substantial heterogeneity in exposure assessment across studies. One recent study found that maternal choline intake was positively associated with birth weight [22], which is consistent with our finding. Other studies have focused on circulation choline concentrations in relation with birth weight [20, 21], with inconsistent results. For example, one study in Singapore found a positive association between maternal choline concentration and neonatal BMI-for-age z-score [21], while another study in China observed no significant associations of maternal choline concentration with offspring birth weight and SGA risk [20]. A positive association between choline status in umbilical cord blood and the risk of LBW was also observed among pregnant women in Netherlands [45]. Such discrepancies may be partly attributable to heterogeneities in study designs, methods of choline exposure assessment, and population characteristics such as baseline choline status, dietary habits and genetic backgrounds. In addition, the current study found that the odds of LBW reached a plateau when choline intake in pregnancy approached 541 mg/d and the odds of SGA continued to be reduced with the increasing of choline intake in pregnancy. These findings suggest that the optimal choline intake in pregnancy may exceed the current dietary recommended adequate intake of 420 mg/d in China when considering the impact on fetal growth. Previous studies have not assessed the dose-response relationships between choline intake and birth outcomes; thus, these findings make a valuable contribution to the limited evidence of choline intake in pregnancy.

Human data on the associations of choline subtypes with fetal growth are lacking in the published studies. Our study found that five choline subtypes (free choline, GpCho, PCho, PtdCho, and SM) intakes during pregnancy were positively associated with birth weight, and four choline subtypes (GpCho, PCho, PtdCho, and SM) intakes during pregnancy were negatively associated with the odds of LBW and SGA. While dietary choline forms differ in solubility and absorption (e.g., lipid-soluble phosphatidylcholine bypasses hepatic first-pass metabolism, whereas water-soluble free choline does not), these biological distinctions do not permit definitive causal inference. Subtype intakes were highly correlated with total choline intake, and FFQ-based estimation error is likely greater for individual subtypes than for total choline. Therefore, the subtype-specific findings are presented as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

The precise mechanisms for the effect of choline on birth weight remain unclear. However, several studies have proposed some plausible mechanisms. First, choline acts as methyl donor in one-carbon metabolism [22, 46], which is crucial for amino acid metabolism, DNA synthesis and many kinds of cellular functions such as the maintenance of cellular homeostasis [22]. Second, dietary choline that surpasses the intestinal absorption threshold can be conversed to trimethylamine by the gut microbiota and then catalyzed to trimethylamine N-oxide [47], which is reported to promote the development of the placenta and fetuses [48]. Third, maternal choline intake may attenuate the activation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [49], leading to the reduction of cortisol level and further promoting fetal growth [50, 51]. Maternal choline intake may also enhance fetal growth by modulating the epigenetic state of genes regulating hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis reactivity [52]. Lastly, maternal choline intake during pregnancy may help alleviate maternal inflammatory status, thereby promoting fetal growth and development [53].

The present study provided valuable evidence on the associations of dietary choline intake from different sources during pregnancy with birth weight and the related birth outcomes. Our study was performed in Shaanxi province of Northwest China by a stratified multistage random sampling method, with the large sample size representing 3% neonates in Shaanxi China. The findings of our study could be generalized to the broader Northwest China region due to the similarities in economy, culture and lifestyle, and could also partly reflect the national situation in China. Moreover, the present study had relatively accurate birth measurements through reviewing birth certificates and medical records. Although our analysis focused on absolute choline intake, interpreting these findings within the broader dietary context is important. Macronutrient composition and overall dietary patterns have been associated with birth weight outcomes in prior studies [3, 11], suggesting that the effects of individual nutrients like choline on fetal growth may be best understood as part of an integrated dietary matrix.

This study has inherent limitations. First, maternal information during pregnancy was retrospectively collected among mothers after delivery. Although previous researches indicated that mothers could recall nutrient intakes and events in pregnancy well after years [54–56], we cannot disregard the possibility of recall bias. To reduce potential bias, we have implemented some measures to assist mothers in providing accurate responses during the survey. For one thing, we used standard questionnaires and detailed supporting materials like food portion images and references calendars to enhance data accuracy. For another thing, we conducted a pilot study to evaluate the survey instruments and trained interviewers rigorously based on the detailed protocols prior to the formal survey. Second, although the FFQ had acceptable reproducibility and validity for overall diet in this population, it was not specifically validated for choline or choline subtypes, and FFQs are better suited to ranking individuals than to estimating absolute intake. Therefore, caution is needed when comparing the observed intake with dietary reference values. Third, dietary intake was assessed at a single time point during pregnancy. Although some studies suggest that maternal dietary patterns remain relatively stable across pregnancy [29, 31], other research indicates that dietary patterns can shift during gestation [30]. Therefore, a single dietary assessment may not fully capture potential changes in intake across gestation. Future studies incorporating repeated dietary assessments throughout pregnancy would be valuable to further elucidate the relationship between choline intake and birth weight. Fourth, choline intake was estimated using the USDA food composition database because no Chinese database was available, and this may introduce additional exposure misclassification. Fifth, blood samples were not obtained in this large-sample survey, restricting us to further consider objective biomarkers in association with birth outcomes. Finally, residual confounding from unobserved and unknown factors cannot be excluded even after adjusting for socio-demographic, health-related and dietary factors, and the real causal relationship cannot be revealed in the present observational study design.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study found that higher maternal choline intake during pregnancy was associated with higher birth weight and lower odds of LBW and SGA in this Chinese population. These findings were observed for total choline and were broadly consistent for several choline-containing compounds. Future prospective studies and randomized trials in diverse populations are needed to confirm these findings, improve exposure assessment, and clarify dose-response relationships and underlying mechanisms.
