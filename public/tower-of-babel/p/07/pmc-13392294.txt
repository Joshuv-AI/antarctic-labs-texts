Comparative assessment of urban plants carbon sink and storage estimation methods: an integrated AHP-fuzzy comprehensive evaluation framework
Authors: Chaoming Li, Haosong Lin, Yilun Cao, Shucheng Ai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amid rapid urbanization, urban plants measurably contribute to climate change mitigation by carbon sink and storage, rather than constituting a singular dominant solution. This necessitates a robust, standardized framework for method evaluation and selection. This study adopts the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation as its methodological core, developing a multi-level evaluation model with 9 specific indicators based on 3 primary criteria: Accuracy, Generality, and Advancement. The model was applied to systematically compare and comprehensively assess 8 prevalent methods used to estimate urban plants’ carbon sink and storage. Consistency checks (CR < 0.10) for all judgment matrices confirmed the statistical robustness of the evaluation data. The results revealed that among the evaluation criteria, Advancement carried the highest weight (52.00%), highlighting the importance of methodological innovation in the face of rapid technological progress. Accuracy ranked second (28.90%), while Generality was assigned the lowest weight (19.10%). In terms of comprehensive performance scores, the Optical Remote Sensing Method (Score: 97/100) outperformed others in carbon sink estimation. Similarly, the Remote Sensing Estimation Method (Score: 82/100) led the field in carbon storage measurement. In contrast, the Average Biomass Method received the lowest score (48/100), indicating significant performance disparities in addressing urban environmental complexity.

Introduction

Research background

Urban areas serve as key hubs for human activity that intensify energy demand and heat exposure; meanwhile, they concentrate energy consumption and contribute substantially to greenhouse gas emissions, particularly carbon dioxide. According to the IPCC Technical Summary, urban regions accounted for approximately 67–72% of global CO2 and methane emissions in 20201. As measurable components of urban ecosystems, plants contribute to carbon sink and help mitigate emissions2. Furthermore, they substantially contribute to alleviating the urban heat island effect, improving local microclimates, and enhancing thermal comfort for residents3,4. However, the complexity of urban environments poses dynamic challenges5. Recent studies indicate that flash droughts can exacerbate global vegetation loss and delay recovery, while the spatiotemporal dynamics of urban heat islands exhibit significant nonlinear characteristics in winter cities. Consequently, carbon estimation is not merely a static measurement but requires adapting to these rapid ecological shifts.

Precise quantification of carbon sink and storage within urban plants remains method-dependent and scale-sensitive. Unlike natural ecosystems, urban plants communities are shaped by anthropogenic design interventions and landscape management practices6, resulting in pronounced spatial heterogeneity and anthropogenic selection of species composition7. This structural complexity, exceeding that of natural systems, creates significant barriers to the accurate monitoring and evaluation of carbon sink functions. From individual trees to integrated green spaces, estimation methodologies often vary considerably in their standards, underlying assumptions, and applicability8. Consequently, despite growing recognition of this issue, there remains a lack of a unified, scientifically validated, and consensus-based framework specifically designed for carbon estimation in urban settings. While recent evaluations have addressed specific systems, such as the life cycle assessment of sponge campuses or refined frameworks for ecological security patterns, these studies often focus on singular metrics or specific scenarios. A generalized, mathematically formalized framework for comparing heterogeneous carbon estimation methods remains absent. This study addresses this gap by formalizing the evaluation as a Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) problem: selecting the optimal estimation strategy from a candidate set under constraints of data uncertainty and technological costs.

Furthermore, existing methods for quantifying carbon sink and storage vary considerably in their assumptions, data requirements, spatial granularity, and equipment dependencies. Consequently, selecting an appropriate approach constitutes an essential prerequisite for assessing urban plants’ carbon dynamics. Historically, quantitative comparisons among these methodologies have been scarce9. While literature reviews by some scholars delineate the mechanisms and procedures of mainstream techniques, few studies perform systematic quantitative evaluations across methods. Practical considerations such as urban-specific applicability remain infrequently discussed. Consequently, a significant gap persists in developing methodological frameworks that facilitate rigorous comparative analyses of carbon estimation approaches specifically tailored to urban plants10.

Literature review

To ensure precision and consistency throughout this analysis, we first define the core terminology used in this study. “urban plants” refers to all photosynthesizing vegetation within the urban boundary, including trees, shrubs, and lawns, distinct from “Urban Forests,” which often implies larger contiguous woodlands. Furthermore, a strict distinction is maintained between “Carbon Sink,” which denotes the annual rate of CO2 uptake (flux), and “Carbon Storage,” which refers to the total accumulated stock of carbon in biomass (stock).

Urban plants carbon sink estimation methods

A plants-based carbon sink refers to the process by which urban plants absorb atmospheric CO₂ through photosynthesis and sequester it in plant tissues and soil11. Direct measurement methods for plant carbon sink primarily include the Assimilation-based Method and the Micrometeorological Method. The Assimilation-based Method relies on physiological parameters related to leaf-level photosynthetic activity. Its core approach involves quantifying the net carbon assimilation capacity and carbon fixation efficiency by accurately measuring physiological indicators12. This method also incorporates structural variables such as the leaf area index (LAI) and three-dimensional green volume to construct a comprehensive assessment model of plant carbon sink potential.

The Micrometeorological Method, also known as the Carbon Flux Method, estimates plant carbon sink by continuously monitoring CO₂ fluxes over a given period using specialized instruments13. The approach operates by simultaneously measuring turbulence parameters in the near-surface atmosphere alongside the concentration gradients of target gases. This simultaneous measurement directly enables the derivation of material exchange fluxes at the interface between ecosystems and the atmosphere14. Owing to its ability to provide continuous and dynamic measurements, this technique has become a critical tool for analyzing the spatial-temporal patterns of carbon flux in urban plants and understanding the environmental drivers behind these dynamics.

Indirect quantification of vegetation carbon sink typically employs two primary approaches: process-based modeling and optical remote sensing techniques. The Model Simulation Method relies on tools such as CITYgreen and i-Tree Eco, which integrate field-measured parameters of urban trees to construct dynamic growth models15. By inputting plant attributes into these platforms, it becomes possible to estimate the carbon sink capacity of individual trees. When combined with remote sensing image interpretation, these models support quantitative evaluation of carbon sink functions at larger spatial scales16. The CITYgreen model is designed to assess ecological benefits using field survey data, and consists primarily of a database module and an ecological assessment module. The i-Tree model allows for the input of multiple plant types and can be applied to both small-scale plot-level studies and broader-scale ecosystem service assessments17.

Optical Remote Sensing Method is broadly used and requires calibration/validation with field data in the assessment of plant carbon sink18. It enables the estimation of carbon sink capacity by extracting key indicators such as plant cover and normalized difference plant index (NDVI)19. Among the various models applied, the Carnegie-Ames-Stanford Approach (CASA) Model is the most widely adopted. The core concept of CASA Model is that a plant’s net primary productivity (NPP) is calculated as the product of absorbed photosynthetically active radiation (APAR) and the light use efficiency (ε)20. Due to its operational simplicity and regional-scale applicability, the CASA Model has become a mainstream approach for estimating carbon sink in urban plants at large spatial scales.

Urban plants carbon storage estimation methods

Direct measurement methods for plant carbon storage include Dry Combustion and Wet Oxidation techniques21. The Dry Combustion Method quantifies total organic carbon by oxidizing plant samples at high temperatures, thereby breaking down organic matter. This approach is considered the standard laboratory procedure for determining carbon storage and is commonly applied but labor-intensive and site-limited in soil carbon storage research22. It is worth noting that soil carbon dynamics are complex; for instance, recent arable soil studies demonstrate that combining field measurements with aggregate turnover modeling provides more accurate storage assessments under climate scenarios. Similarly, global phosphorus patterns disrupted by silicate weathering suggest that biogeochemical cycles in urban soils may also require integrated estimation approaches beyond simple combustion.

Indirect methods for estimating plant carbon storage include biomass-based approaches, model estimation, and remote sensing estimation. The biomass approach can be further divided into two types: the Average Biomass Method and the Biomass Conversion Factor Method. The Average Biomass Method integrates mean biomass data obtained from field plots with the spatial distribution of corresponding green space types to construct a biomass estimation model for urban ecosystems23. Within this framework, direct measurement based on standard trees offers significant advantages in terms of accuracy24. The Biomass Conversion Factor Method is primarily used to estimate the total biomass of urban forests25. This method first calculates the total forest biomass by multiplying the stand volume by species-specific biomass conversion coefficients and wood density. This specific calculation enables data extrapolation from the plot level to broader spatial scales, although it inherently introduces certain upscaling uncertainties26.

In carbon storage estimation for urban plants, model-based approaches extend beyond the CITYgreen and i-Tree Eco platforms to include the InVEST model. While CITYgreen and i-Tree Eco are capable of calculating both carbon sink and storage, their core principle involves assessing the carbon accumulation of individual trees. By subtracting carbon storage values from different time periods, annual carbon sink can be derived.While acknowledging the established dual functionality of CITYgreen and i-Tree Eco in estimating both carbon storage and carbon sink, this study categorizes them specifically within the carbon sink evaluation set to maintain the structural independence of the comparative matrix and prevent analytical double-counting. This methodological choice is grounded in the fact that, unlike static biomass-based inventories, the primary scientific value of these models lies in their capacity for dynamic growth modeling and ‘what-if’ scenario simulations. By focusing on their ability to derive annual sequestration rates from multi-temporal storage baselines, the evaluation framework can more precisely assess their performance in reflecting the physiological dynamics of urban vegetation, which is the defining characteristic of carbon sink estimation. Their core theoretical principle involves assessing the baseline carbon accumulation of individual trees. Subsequently, they derive the annual carbon sink by subtracting these storage values across different time periods. In contrast, the InVEST model is primarily used for large-scale carbon storage assessments and is not suited for accurately measuring sink rates27, thus it is categorized here solely as a carbon storage estimation method. InVEST Model integrates remote sensing, soil data, and other geospatial inputs to simulate temporal changes in ecosystem carbon storage28. It also supports long-term land-use impact forecasting and is equipped with strong spatial visualization capabilities29. However, the model is highly sensitive to parameter inputs and requires substantial field data for calibration. Additionally, there is often a lag in data availability.

Remote Sensing-based Estimation Method relies on satellite or aerial imagery to rapidly acquire plant data at regional scales30. The principle of the Remote Sensing-based Estimation Method lies in the construction of a carbon storage prediction model through the extraction of remote sensing parameters as well as spectral information.This kind of methods support spatial mapping of carbon storage and enable large-scale estimation31. A key advantage is that they substantially reduce ground-sampling effort; however, calibration/validation with field data remains essential, making them suitable for extensive monitoring and capable of supporting dynamic updates over time. Nevertheless, their effectiveness depends heavily on accurate remote sensing data, and the spatial resolution of remote sensing imagery significantly influences estimation outcomes32. Conventional two-dimensional remote sensing is limited in its ability to differentiate the contributions of multi-layered plant structures to total carbon storage33. Consequently, supplementary technologies such as 3D point cloud scanning are often required.

Summary of existing research methods

The scientific quantification of carbon sink and carbon storage of urban plants faces a range of multidimensional challenges. These include the physiological characteristics of plant, disturbances specific to urban environments, and inherent limitations in the available technical approaches. From a technical perspective, a preliminary review of current estimation methods reveals 3 primary difficulties commonly encountered when comparing different approaches.

The data requirements and measurement accuracy differ markedly, reflecting scale-sensitivity and data heterogeneity across estimation methods. For example, in the context of urban plants carbon sink assessment, the assimilation-based method requires detailed physiological parameters of plants and is typically suited for small-scale studies, with spatial resolutions ranging from 1 to 10 m. In contrast, optical remote sensing depends on large-scale imagery, where spatial resolution may exceed 100 meters34. Not all research methods are appropriate for estimating carbon sink and carbon storage in urban plants. For instance, Dry and Wet Combustion are destructive laboratory techniques that rely heavily on specialized equipment, rendering them unfeasible for extensive in-situ applications within complex urban environments. Although these combustion techniques are consequently excluded from the candidate ranking matrix to preserve the practical focus of the evaluation framework, their omission admittedly constrains the absolute completeness of the methodological comparison. Nevertheless, it must be emphasized that such direct measurements remain the fundamental ground-truth calibration standard upon which all indirect estimation techniques ultimately depend. Considering the unique characteristics of urban plants, this study selects 8 representative methods for urban plants carbon sink and storage assessment to be included in the comparative analysis. The selected carbon sink estimation methods include the Model Simulation Method (CITYgreen and i-Tree Eco), the Micrometeorological Method, the Optical Remote Sensing Method, and the Assimilation-based Method. For carbon storage, the chosen methods comprise the Remote Sensing Estimation Method, the Biomass Conversion Factor Method, the Average Biomass Method, and the Model Estimation Method (the InVEST Model).

In synthesis, the literature reveals three measurable gaps that directly motivate the selection of the nine specific evaluation indicators. Specifically, the inherent trade-off between scale resolution and measurement precision necessitates the establishment of the Generality and Accuracy criteria. Concurrently, the high financial barriers associated with dynamic monitoring substantiate the inclusion of the Equipment Cost metric, while the prevailing lag in data processing workflows underscores the need for the Advancement dimensions. Consequently, the proposed evaluation framework is systematically grounded in addressing these identified methodological bottlenecks.

Despite the growing recognition of urban ecosystem complexity, a critical research gap persists in methodological evaluation. Existing studies often focus on single-metric assessments in specific scenarios, such as the life cycle assessment of sponge campus systems38, the identification of key restoration areas for ecological security patterns39 or specific urban green space40,41. However, there is a lack of a unified, mathematically formalized framework to systematically compare multiple carbon estimation methods. Furthermore, extreme climate events like flash droughts can severely exacerbate global vegetation loss and delay ecological recovery42. Simultaneously, urban heat islands exhibit highly complex spatiotemporal dynamics. Consequently, it is crucial to select an estimation method capable of adapting to these rapid environmental changes43. This study aims to bridge this gap by converting qualitative methodological attributes into standardized quantitative metrics.

Although existing studies have detailed the mechanisms of individual methods, a significant methodological hiatus remains. First, regarding the lack of cross-dimensional comparison, previous AHP-based environmental evaluations have predominantly focused on ranking static physical indicators for spatial planning or ecological risk assessment. In contrast, this study extends existing meta-evaluation frameworks in environmental decision sciences by applying AHP to evaluate the underlying scientific methodologies themselves, moving beyond mere qualitative reviews or enumeration of methods. Second, concerning the neglect of decision uncertainty, existing selection strategies often fail to quantify the ambiguity and subjective bias inherent in expert judgments. Consequently, by formalizing the selection of urban vegetation carbon estimation methods as a Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) problem, the primary novelty of this research lies in constructing a computational bridge using the AHP-Fuzzy coupled model. This bridge simultaneously processes quantitative technical parameters, such as data accuracy and measurement precision, alongside qualitative operational constraints, including equipment dependency, multi-scenario adaptability, and long-term technological advancement.

Research questions and objectives

To systematically address the aforementioned research gaps, this study formalizes the selection process of urban vegetation carbon accounting methods as a Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) problem. The evaluation model is defined as V = f(W, R), where W is the weight vector derived from AHP, and R represents the fuzzy evaluation matrix. Specifically, under a set of urban task constraints C (e.g., accuracy requirements, cost limits, and automation needs), it aims to solve for the optimal evaluation vector to identify the most suitable method set. Based on this formalization, a progressive logical framework consisting of “status identification, dimensional formalization, and quantitative assessment” is established, focusing on two core research questions:

Materials and methods

Construction of evaluation system

In response to the research questions and challenges, this study developed an evaluation framework based on exactly three core dimensions, namely Accuracy, Generality, and Advancement. The selection of these criteria is theoretically grounded in established multi-criteria environmental assessment frameworks. Traditional frameworks typically balance the precision of scientific measurement, represented by Accuracy, with practical operational feasibility, denoted by Generality. To explicitly address the unique dynamic complexities and rapid technological shifts inherent in modern urban ecosystems, this study introduces Advancement as a crucial third fundamental dimension.

The Accuracy dimension focused on data reliability and error control44, which formed the basis for judging the relative performance of different carbon sink and storage estimation methods. Planning of urban plants requires high-accuracy data to support decision-making. The methods differ in technical principles, data sources, and model parameters.

The Generality dimension was primarily assessed in two aspects: (1) Multi-scale Adaptability: Directly comparing localized plot-level techniques with broad regional approaches inherently introduces scale mismatch biases. To systematically mitigate this discrepancy, this indicator explicitly evaluates a method’s flexibility to bridge diverse spatial boundaries. Most existing methods are tailored to a single spatial scale, such as individual trees or regional areas, whereas comprehensive urban plant monitoring must cover multiple levels ranging from single trees to communities. (2) Technical Barriers and Cost Constraints: The Micrometeorological Method, for instance, involves high equipment costs. The Assimilation-based method requires leaf-level measurements, making large-scale application difficult45.

The Advancement dimension aligned with the long-term requirements of carbon neutrality governance46. It evaluated: (1) the level of intelligence and automation, including data acquisition methods, algorithm autonomy, and real-time monitoring capabilities; (2) integration with emerging technologies and dynamic simulation capabilities; and (3) the compatibility of results with open-source data systems.

Building upon the multidimensional framework encompassing Accuracy, Generality, and Advancement, we organized the indicators into a hierarchical structure to support task-fit method selection rather than mandatory parallel implementation. The evaluation hierarchy is formally defined as H ={G, C, I}, where G represents the target goal, C = {C1, C2, C3} denotes the criteria, and I = {I11,…, I33} represents the specific indicators. Specifically, this study established an evaluation system consisting of 1 target level, 3 criterion levels, and 9 indicator levels. The criterion levels represent the primary evaluation categories under the target, while the indicator levels served as secondary metrics associated with each criterion. This hierarchical system was designed to comprehensively capture the key elements relevant to assessing urban plants carbon sink and storage estimation methods. Calculation of indicator weights using AHP

Once the system was established, it was necessary to determine the weight of each indicator. The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), proposed by American operations researcher Thomas L. Saaty in the 1970s, is a multi-criteria decision-making method47. The selection of the AHP-Fuzzy coupled model over data-driven approaches such as machine learning, or purely qualitative methods like the Delphi technique, is grounded in two key formal rationales. First, regarding Small-Sample Adaptability, the scarcity of senior experts in the specialized field of urban carbon accounting limits data availability. AHP provides statistically robust results with small sample sizes (N < 20), thereby avoiding the overfitting risks associated with training machine learning models on limited datasets. Second, concerning the Handling of Ambiguity, fuzzy set theory converts linguistic variables including ‘Generality’ into quantitative membership degrees. This approach effectively resolves the information loss inherent in traditional Likert-scale scoring. It is particularly suitable for problems involving multiple evaluation factors and hierarchical decision structures, and for small expert panels; interpretability and traceability were prioritized over alternative MCDM tools. Specifically, compared to outranking methods like TOPSIS or PROMETHEE, the AHP-Fuzzy approach better handles the linguistic ambiguity of expert evaluations through mathematical membership functions. Furthermore, unlike entropy weighting, it does not strictly rely on large objective statistical datasets, which remain persistently scarce in this emerging interdisciplinary field.

To enhance objectivity under realistic constraints, 16 domain experts specializing in urban vegetation carbon sink and storage were invited to construct the pairwise comparison matrices. To ensure disciplinary diversity and representative validity, this panel comprised six urban ecologists, five remote sensing scientists, and five landscape planning practitioners. These experts were drawn from both academic institutions and municipal forestry bureaus, and all participants explicitly declared no conflicts of interest. Independent elicitation and geometric-mean aggregation were subsequently applied to reduce individual bias. All participating experts possess over 10 years of research experience in urban ecology or remote sensing monitoring. Potential heterogeneity in expert assessment data, arising from these diverse research backgrounds, was explicitly identified and addressed. To mitigate data uncertainty and heterogeneity, geometric-mean aggregation was employed during the judgment matrix construction process. Furthermore, missing values or logical inconsistencies were strictly handled through iterative screening; if an expert’s judgment matrix yielded a Consistency Ratio (CR) value exceeding 0.10, they were requested to re-evaluate the relative importance of indicators until all evaluation results passed the consistency check, thereby ensuring the scientific integrity and statistical robustness of the raw data. In addition, 4 co-authors facilitated matrix construction and clarified indicator definitions; expert judgments were used for weighting.

This study was granted an exemption from ethics review by the Ethics Committee of the School of Architecture, Southeast University. As the research solely involved expert questionnaire evaluations (without collection of sensitive personal or medical data), and all participants (including 16 external experts and 4 co-authors) provided written informed consent, the study was classified as non-interventional academic consultation with minimal ethical risk, meeting the criteria for exemption.

Step 2: Calculating indicator weights.

To derive the indicator weights, the geometric mean of each row in the judgment matrix was first computed to aggregate the pairwise comparison values. Subsequently, a strict vector normalization process was applied. This involved dividing each individual geometric mean by the total sum of the geometric means across all rows, ensuring that the final weight vector is normalized to unity49. This step effectively eliminates scale discrepancies inherent in the raw expert scoring.

Step 3: Consistency check.

To address potential cognitive biases in expert judgments, consistency tests were performed following Saaty’s criterion. The consistency ratio was calculated as CR = CI/RI, where CI = (λmax − n)/(n − 1), λ_max is the maximum eigenvalue of the judgment matrix, and n is the matrix order. If CR < 0.1, the matrix was considered logically consistent. If not, the judgment matrix was revised iteratively until the consistency threshold was met50. The maximum eigenvalue of the matrix was also computed to quantify deviation from perfect consistency. It is important to note that, unlike predictive machine learning models which require strictly separated training and testing datasets, the validation strategy for this evaluation model prioritizes logical consistency and structural robustness over predictive accuracy. Therefore, this study adopts the Consistency Ratio (CR < 0.1) as the primary validation metric to prevent ‘overfitting’—specifically, to ensure that expert scoring is not arbitrarily manipulated to fit a predetermined outcome—supplemented by sensitivity analysis to test model stability against parameter variations.

Calculation of score based on fuzzy matrix

In this step, the Fuzzy Comprehensive Evaluation Method was applied to convert qualitative indicators into measurable outcomes using fuzzy mathematics51,52. Specifically, the membership degree for each qualitative indicator was determined by expert scoring across five evaluation levels. These individual scores were subsequently integrated into the final comprehensive evaluation. This process transformed descriptive assessments of carbon sink and storage estimation methods into quantitative scores.

To ensure methodological rigor, the membership degree for each qualitative indicator was systematically elicited via an independent, single-round, standardized five-point Likert-scale questionnaire. Instead of conducting open workshops which might introduce dominant-personality bias, the sixteen experts were required to independently evaluate each estimation method against every qualitative indicator. They categorized their linguistic assessments strictly into five predefined evaluation levels, namely Excellent (I), Good (II), Average (III), Poor (IV), and Very Poor (V). To guarantee evaluation reliability, detailed descriptive rubrics defining the specific operational meanings of each linguistic level were provided alongside the questionnaires. Ultimately, the proportional frequency distribution of the experts’ selections for each level directly constituted the quantitative fuzzy membership vectors used in subsequent calculations. For example, when evaluating a specific estimation method under the indicator C23 Operational Complexity, if 60% of experts rate the method as excellent, 30% rate it as good, and 10% rate it as poor, then the corresponding vector for this indicator would be expressed as (0.6, 0.3, 0, 0, 0.1) across the five evaluation levels. Discussion

Results analysis

Based on the results, Advancement received the highest weight (52%) in evaluating carbon sink and storage estimation methods for plants. This result reflected the emphasis on technological intelligence and sustainability. As the demand for dynamic monitoring of urban green spaces and plant continues to rise, the capacity for long-term automated data acquisition and compatibility with open-source datasets have become key factors in improving methodological adaptability. This evaluation result provides strong empirical support for Hypothesis H1. In the digital context of urban carbon management, Advancement leads with a dominant weight of 52.00%. It is important to clarify that this high weighting primarily reflects a normative preference embedded in expert perception, indicating a collective anticipation of a paradigm shift toward automated monitoring. Rather than being an outcome derived purely from historical empirical evidence, this consensus highlights technological innovation as the aspirational core driver of future evaluation systems. Whether basic data collection is automated or relies on manual inspection directly affects the efficiency of large-scale carbon sink and storage estimations. Intelligent equipment reduces labor costs, while open-source data compatibility facilitates multi-source data integration and enhances analytical efficiency. This high level of compatibility not only reduces the pressure on researchers to acquire high-cost proprietary data but also facilitates horizontal comparability of carbon sink data across different cities through unified data interfaces, enabling the construction of a global benchmark database for urban carbon neutrality.

Accuracy (28.9%) ranks second in weight, which aligns with the practical requirements for accurate quantification of urban plants carbon sink and storage. In actual applications, carbon sink and storage estimation for urban plants supports a variety of application scenarios. Contexts such as carbon trading accounting and the design of ecological compensation mechanisms all require high-precision data as a foundation. Among the indicators, the Accuracy of measured carbon values (C12, 0.4269) ranked first, with its indicator weight (0.821) significantly exceeding that of the Accuracy of basic input data (C11, 0.179). This indicated that experts placed greater emphasis on the reliability of the final output data rather than solely on the accuracy of data acquisition. The primary ranking of indicator C12 (weighting 23.73%) demonstrates that in the context of carbon reduction performance accounting, the absolute deviation of final results directly affects the certification of carbon offset credits. This weighting reflects the academic priority placed on “data effectiveness” rather than mere “instrument precision.”

Generality (19.1%) carried a relatively lower weight. Nevertheless, within its indicator level, the contribution of multi-scenario applicability (C22) was the highest. This suggested an urgent need for carbon sink and storage estimation methods that can operate across multiple scenarios and spatial scales to accommodate the diversity of urban green spaces. The weights of operational complexity (C23) and equipment cost (C24) were comparatively lower, which may reflect a current research preference for efficiency and precision first.

Based on the evaluation model, the final results exhibited clear hierarchical differences: among the carbon sink estimation methods, the Optical Remote Sensing Method (97 points) performed the best, followed by the Model Simulation Method (67 points), the Micrometeorological Method (66 points), and the Assimilation-based Method (58 points), indicating task-fit selection: remote sensing for city-scale/dynamic assessments; micrometeorological flux for high-precision net exchange with limited generality; model simulation for scenario analysis subject to input-data quality. For carbon storage estimation methods, the Remote Sensing Estimation Method (82 points) ranked highest, followed by the Model Estimation Method (68 points), the Biomass Conversion Factor Method (60 points), and the Average Biomass Method (48 points), indicating task-fit selection: remote sensing for large-area inventories; biomass-based methods (conversion factor / average biomass) for small green-space inventories with simple operation and low cost.

Focusing on the scores of each method, the results revealed that the Remote Sensing Estimation Method achieved the highest overall score, indicating its significant advantages in large-scale and dynamic carbon estimation, particularly in the advancement dimension (with scores of 96 and 91), due to its strong capabilities in automated data acquisition and compatibility with open-source platforms. Its accuracy also performed relatively well among indirect measurement methods. Traditional two-dimensional remote sensing faces clear limitations in estimating carbon based on vertical plant structures. In contrast, newer active remote sensing techniques, such as UAV LiDAR point cloud fusion, can effectively reconstruct three-dimensional canopy structures. This three-dimensional reconstruction tightly controls carbon sink estimation errors and significantly improves overall accuracy. Empirical validation studies have provided quantitative evidence demonstrating that integrating such three-dimensional point cloud data significantly reduces carbon estimation errors compared to traditional two-dimensional optical modeling53,54. Compared to traditional 2D optical indices, LiDAR point clouds can penetrate the canopy to accurately capture leaf area index (LAI) and vertical stratification features. This physical measurement approach effectively mitigates the risk of underestimating carbon storage caused by shadow coverage and vegetation overlap in densely built-up areas.

While the Micrometeorological Method exceeds in Accuracy, its Generality is limited. Benefiting from its ability to directly monitor ecosystem net carbon exchange, it achieved an Accuracy score of 94 (the highest among all methods). Nevertheless, its Generality score was only 39 due to high equipment costs and poor adaptability to heterogeneous urban surfaces, making it suitable primarily for research-grade homogeneous green space monitoring. However, it must be emphasized that micrometeorological approaches remain absolutely indispensable for the baseline calibration and empirical validation of indirect techniques. This dynamic reinforces a complementary rather than purely competitive relationship among different methodologies.

The Biomass Conversion Factor Method and the Average Biomass Method received relatively lower scores, reflecting their dependence on empirical data and weak dynamic response capabilities. Despite its lower comprehensive score, the Average Biomass Method retains irreplaceable “baseline” value for informal urban green spaces where data are scarce or for initial surveys with highly restricted budgets. Its simplicity of operation enables rapid, low-cost estimations. Nonetheless, the Biomass Conversion Factor Method achieved high generality—for example, C21 “Dependence on measurement equipment”scored 96, as its non-reliance on specialized equipment and low estimation cost constitute its most prominent advantage, which effectively compensates for the limitations of remote sensing methods.

Theoretically, these findings prioritize Advancement over Generality. This signals an expert-perceived anticipatory shift toward digital-twin-based urban forestry, although this paradigm currently remains a methodological aspiration rather than a fully evidenced and widespread technological reality. Practically, the high score of LiDAR-integrated methods highlights their robust technical potential. However, suggesting them as an immediate universal benchmark for carbon trading certification requires cautious framing, as regulatory standards, cost tolerances, and technological access vary significantly across different jurisdictions.

Methods comparison

Before detailing the specific performance of each method, it is necessary to address the conceptual distinction between carbon sink and carbon storage estimations. Carbon sink measurement targets flux, focusing primarily on the dynamic rate of carbon uptake. Conversely, carbon storage measurement assesses the accumulated static stock. Although their measurement targets differ, continuous long-term carbon sinks fundamentally constitute carbon storage, demonstrating a strong quantitative complementarity. It is essential to clarify that the evaluation framework proposed in this study strictly assesses the methodological performance of these estimation techniques, such as their operational complexity and data accuracy, rather than cross-comparing the actual calculated values of carbon sinks and carbon storage. Consequently, the inherent temporal mismatch between flux and stock does not interfere with the unified methodological assessment. Furthermore, while all eight methods were evaluated using the same generic criteria dimensions, they were intentionally ranked strictly within their respective target categories, namely sink versus storage. This deliberate separation avoids arbitrary cross-category comparisons that would otherwise conflate fundamentally different physical mechanisms.

Based on the above calculation results and literature analysis, the application scenarios, advantages, and disadvantages of each research method can be further explained separately from the perspectives of Accuracy, Generality, and Advancement.

In terms of Accuracy, among carbon sink estimation methods, the Micrometeorological Method, as a direct measurement approach, serves as the authoritative benchmark due to its ability to directly monitor ecosystem net carbon exchange. However, the urban heat island effect and wind field disturbances caused by buildings can introduce systematic errors, requiring correction using microclimate models. The Model Simulation Method successfully supports dynamic prediction through multi-source data integration and mechanistic modeling. However, its precision depends heavily on the strict localization of input parameters. Furthermore, inevitable discrepancies between mechanistic model assumptions and the actual physiological responses of urban plants frequently lead to estimation biases. Despite being limited by the accuracy of input parameters, model simulation methods (such as i-Tree Eco) possess irreplaceable advantages in “what-if scenario analysis.” They can predict future carbon sink increments under different afforestation strategies or climate change scenarios, providing forward-looking decision support for urban planning. The Optical Remote Sensing Method excels in large-scale coverage. However, remote sensing methods face inherent technical limitations, notably atmospheric correction errors, severe shadowing effects within deep urban canyons, and optical sensor saturation in highly dense canopies. Furthermore, the mixed-pixel effect of two-dimensional remote sensing data and the structural simplification of vertical canopies limit its accuracy in fragmented urban green spaces. Therefore, this method requires strict optimization through the integration of LiDAR-based three-dimensional modeling and layered photosynthesis models to mitigate these overgeneralization risks.

For carbon storage estimation methods, the Biomass Conversion Factor Method is susceptible to species-specific variability and subjective errors due to its reliance on generalized conversion coefficients and manual measurements. Although the Remote Sensing Estimation Method offers relatively high accuracy, it currently lacks sufficient consideration of the three-dimensional structural characteristics of plants. The Model Estimation Method shows significant potential for dynamic prediction, but it needs to address the mismatch between mechanistic model assumptions and the actual light use efficiency of urban plants.

In terms of Generality, for carbon sink estimation methods, the micrometeorological method achieves dynamic carbon sink estimation by monitoring CO₂ flux variations at the atmosphere–plant interface, but its technical implementation is highly dependent on precision instruments and systematic equipment support. The Model Simulation Method requires significantly less equipment compared to the Micrometeorological Method, demonstrating a generally good level of equipment independence. Nevertheless, it fundamentally relies on key equipment for precise parameter localization and dynamic validation. The Optical Remote Sensing Method acquires plant spectral information through platforms such as satellites and unmanned aerial vehicles, offering relatively high generalizability. At the community and individual tree scales, the Assimilation-based Method requires substantial instrumentation support.

For carbon storage estimation methods, the Average Biomass Method has extremely low dependence on experimental equipment, requiring only basic measurement tools for core data collection without the need for complex instruments or high-precision sensors. The Remote Sensing Estimation Method demonstrates an average dependence on localized ground measurement equipment. By leveraging the wide-area coverage capabilities of satellites and unmanned aerial vehicles, this method is highly suitable for large-scale carbon storage assessments, although it remains limited by spatial resolution in small urban green space scenarios. The Biomass Conversion Factor Method is centered on ground surveys, offering simple operation and low cost, making it well-suited for small green space inventories. However, it cannot support dynamic monitoring or large-scale application. The Model Estimation Method, through multi-source data integration and mechanistic modeling, performs well in long-term carbon sink forecasting and multi-scenario simulation but relies on parameter localization validation and high-performance computing resources.

In terms of Advancement, at the carbon sink level, significant differences exist among the Optical Remote Sensing Method, the Micrometeorological Method, and the Model Simulation Method regarding the degree of intelligence in basic data acquisition. The Optical Remote Sensing Method demonstrates a high level of intelligence, supports long-term dynamic monitoring, and can significantly reduce manual intervention. The Micrometeorological Method requires manual involvement, resulting in a moderate level of intelligence. The Assimilation-based Method requires frequent manual operation and struggles to achieve long-term continuous automated monitoring, thus exhibiting an average level of data acquisition intelligence rather than full automation. The Model Simulation Method, relying on three-dimensional plant models, only requires inputting plant parameters to generate results, thereby achieving a relatively high level of intelligence.

At the carbon storage estimation level, the Average Biomass Method relies on manual ground surveys for data acquisition, resulting in a low level of intelligence. The Remote Sensing Estimation Method, based on satellite, UAV, or LiDAR data, features short processing times, a high degree of automation, and supports dynamic updates. The Model Estimation Method demonstrates potential in dynamic prediction through flexible data integration and model optimization but depends on manual input and professional maintenance. The Biomass Conversion Factor Method exhibits an average level of intelligence in data acquisition. Due to its strict reliance on manual labor and infrequent data updates, it is primarily suitable for static data supplementation or localized small-scale validation.

In summary, whether in the field of carbon sink or carbon storage estimation, each individual measurement method has its limitations. The integrated application of remote sensing technology and LiDAR enables precise, efficient, and intelligent data acquisition. Two-dimensional remote sensing demonstrates significant advantages in estimating urban-scale plant carbon sink and storage, as its wide-area coverage overcomes the spatial limitations of traditional ground surveys, making it particularly suitable for efficient surveys in heterogeneous urban surface environments.

At the single plant and plant community scales, LiDAR point cloud technology constructs three-dimensional plant models, allowing for precise analysis of canopy vertical structure and individual tree morphological parameters. Compared to traditional two-dimensional remote sensing, its penetrative sensing capability enables quantification of the layered carbon contributions of trees, shrubs, and ground cover within multi-layered plant, thus avoiding the underestimation of understory carbon storage. Future integration of large-scale satellite remote sensing with fine-scale 3D point cloud technologies will establish a technical framework coupling macroscopic dynamic monitoring with microscopic structural analysis. This synergy will address multi-scale operational requirements for urban plants carbon management through closed-loop data validation.

In summary, this study employed a hybrid approach combining qualitative expert knowledge with quantitative fuzzy logic. Just as recent research combined three-way decisions with DEA game cross-efficiency for procurement selection55, this hybrid paradigm effectively handles uncertainty in the decision-making process. Our results indicate that integrating remote sensing with modeling yields the highest scores, aligning with the trend toward high-fidelity simulations. For instance, future methodologies could incorporate high-performance multi-scale NWP-LBM coupling frameworks56 to correct micrometeorological method errors through real-time urban wind field simulation.

On the other hand, certain limitations still exist in this study. First, in the expert scoring stage, 16 experts were consulted. During the fuzzy evaluation process, the opinions of both the experts and the research team were referenced. Since it is difficult to recruit experts specializing in urban plants carbon sink and storage, and extensive discussions with experts on the strengths and weaknesses of each method were required, the sample size in this study remained limited. Additionally, the direct participation of co-authors in the matrix facilitation and weighting process may introduce unintended cognitive biases into the evaluation, representing a potential limitation in maintaining strict independence. In future research, the sample size can be expanded based on this framework to reduce subjectivity in the evaluation. Furthermore, although geometric mean aggregation was utilized to mitigate individual bias, this study did not conduct a dedicated sensitivity analysis to evaluate the specific impact of individual expert outliers on the final weight distribution. While the stability of the judgment matrices was mathematically ensured through the Consistency Ratio (CR), our current consideration of sensitivity remains strictly limited to a conceptual discussion of simulating subtle uniform fluctuations (± 10%) in overall expert preferences. Consequently, systematically investigating the influence of extreme subjective deviations beyond this ± 10% threshold represents a critical necessity for future methodological refinement. Current statistical robustness is primarily supported by the fact that all matrices satisfied the CR < 0.1 criterion, effectively minimizing variance in weight estimation. However, the assumption that AHP statistically resolves small sample issues must be nuanced. Expert elicitation uncertainty and subjective bias can still propagate significantly into the final evaluation results, even when consistency checks are mathematically satisfied. Building upon this, the present study acknowledges the epistemic uncertainty inherent in subjective expert judgment. A notable limitation of the current fuzzy evaluation process is the absence of a rigorous uncertainty propagation analysis. Future methodological refinements should therefore incorporate probabilistic techniques, including Monte Carlo simulations, to explicitly quantify how initial scoring ambiguities impact the stability of the final performance rankings.Additionally, integrating AI algorithms to simulate expert roles could be explored to further objectify the scoring process, although interpretability issues would need to be addressed. The scalability of this framework in practical deployment is constrained by its expert dependence. Re-organizing expert panels for AHP scoring when scaling the method to new urban contexts incurs high temporal costs. To address this scalability issue, future applications are recommended to establish a pre-trained weight database with preset parameters based on city typologies, thereby reducing reliance on real-time expert input.

The second limitation lies in the fact that although the methodology of this study partially fills the gap in comparative analysis of urban plants carbon sink and storage estimation methods, the research is still subject to the influence of subjective judgment. This is due to inherent limitations of the approach itself. Evaluation scores and assessments that rely on human judgment cannot entirely eliminate subjectivity. Crucially, it must be explicitly acknowledged that weights derived from the Analytic Hierarchy Process inherently encode the normative preferences and collective foresight of the selected experts. These weights serve as subjective prioritizations rather than purely objective absolute performance metrics. Assessments based on AI algorithms or multi-agent models may help overcome this limitation. Nevertheless, AI-based evaluation methods often suffer from issues of interpretability and require extensive training data. In future research, AI could be trained to simulate expert roles based on the evaluation objectives and indicators established in this study, allowing it to analyze the advantages and disadvantages of each method and imitate the expert scoring process. Additionally, the indicators used in this study can be further refined to enhance the systematic nature of method comparison.

While this framework addresses plant carbon estimation, we must acknowledge the governance gaps in urban decarbonization57. Moreover, this study primarily focused on above-ground biomass, whereas modeling soil carbon storage combining field measurements and climate scenarios58 is equally critical. The methodological evaluation framework constructed in this study currently focuses primarily on carbon accounting for above-ground biomass. Given the central role of urban soils in long-term carbon sequestration, future evaluation frameworks should further integrate complexity indicators for soil organic carbon monitoring. Future research directions must explicitly embed below-ground biomass models, integrating mechanisms such as aggregate turnover and root-soil exudate dynamics directly into the multi-criteria evaluation matrix to achieve a holistic assessment of the total ecosystem carbon pool. Just as silicate weathering disrupts global phosphorus patterns59, anthropogenic disturbances in urbanization may fundamentally alter carbon cycle patterns. Future work should also explore the greenhouse gas reduction potential of city recyclable material systems60 to integrate them into a broader carbon assessment system.

This study conducted a comparative evaluation of different research methods. However, this does not imply that methods with higher scores are always the optimal choice under all scales and application scenarios. Although mathematical models aim to approximate reality, they can never fully replicate the complexity of actual applications. Continuously analyzing the influencing factors of each method and continuously optimizing the models is essential to obtain the most reliable results possible. Model construction is always based on specific contexts and applicable scenarios, while the complexity and heterogeneity of urban green spaces result in highly variable carbon sink and storage estimation contexts. Thus, the core objective of this study is to quantitatively analyze various research methods and provide a reference for researchers when selecting appropriate methodologies. Moreover, the indicators within this framework account for technical costs and equipment dependency (C21, C24), ensuring the universal applicability of the evaluation system across cities with varying levels of economic development. It is particularly valuable for Global South countries undergoing rapid urbanization, assisting them in balancing costs and accuracy during technical selection.

For practitioners deploying this framework, we recommend applying a functional decision threshold. Specifically, aggregate score differences of less than five points between competing methods should be considered practically negligible. This margin allows decision-makers the flexibility to select either alternative based on localized administrative constraints or immediate budget availability. To achieve comprehensive research objectives, a coupled “macro-survey and micro-validation” path is highly recommended. Initially, researchers should utilize optical remote sensing to establish rapid city-wide coverage. This macro-level data must then be supplemented by micrometeorological stations on representative plots to ensure strict accuracy calibration. Finally, integrating 3D LiDAR point clouds is essential to accurately model complex multi-layered urban vegetation structures. Specifically, future research should explore the integration of edge computing with UAV-based LiDAR to enable real-time, species-level carbon sequestration monitoring in high-density urban canyons.
