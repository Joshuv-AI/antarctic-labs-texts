Impact of mucilage incorporation into xanthan gum on the physical and textural characteristics of gluten-free bread during short-term storage
Authors: Vhuhwavho Maphaha, Frederick Tawi Tabit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gluten-free bread often exhibits poor texture and low volume, necessitating the use of hydrocolloids such as xanthan gum and plant-derived mucilage to improve structural and sensory quality. This study investigated the effects of partially substituting xanthan gum with okra mucilage at 25, 50, and 75% levels on the physicochemical, textural, and sensory properties of gluten‑free bread during 72 h of storage. A quantitative study using a completely randomised design (CRD) evaluated four formulations (B1–B4) with three replicates each (n = 12), varying the mucilage‑to‑xanthan gum ratio. Physical, textural, and sensory properties were analysed to assess the influence of stabiliser ratios on bread quality. Moisture content increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) with mucilage incorporation, with formulation B4 (75% mucilage) showing the highest moisture retention (58–60%) compared to B1 (xanthan gum only, 44–46%). Specific volume improved significantly (p ≤ 0.05), increasing from 2.6 mL g⁻¹ (B1) to 4.8 mL g⁻¹ (B4), while mean air cell size decreased from 0.30 mm² in B1 to 0.15 mm² in B4. Bread hardness increased during storage for all formulations but remained consistently lower in mucilage‑rich breads (B4: 4–5 N vs. B1: 14 N at 48 h), while chewiness and gumminess were significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05). Springiness increased at higher mucilage inclusion levels, whereas resilience decreased at 50 and 75% mucilage substitution. Sensory evaluation revealed no significant differences among most formulations, although B4 exhibited a slight reduction in overall acceptance (p ≤ 0.05). Overall, partial substitution of xanthan gum with plant‑derived mucilage improved moisture retention and several physical and textural attributes associated with short‑term storage stability, including reduced firmness development and enhanced crumb elasticity. These findings indicate that okra mucilage is a promising natural hydrocolloid for improving the physical quality of gluten‑free bread when used in combination with xanthan gum, with relevance for clean‑label formulation strategies.

Introduction

Gluten intolerance, also known as celiac disease, is an autoimmune disorder triggered by the ingestion of gluten, a protein commonly found in grains such as wheat and barley1. Symptoms of Coeliac Disease (CD) often include diarrhoea, malabsorption, and abdominal pain2. A study by Karsas3 at Steve Biko Hospital, Pretoria, South Africa, found a prevalence of gluten intolerance of 10.2%, which was deemed associated with diabetes mellitus4. The availability of gluten-free product varieties in supermarkets is limited; thus, consumers have limited choice in gluten-free breads and where to purchase them5. Since the treatment of celiac disease primarily involves the consumption of gluten-free products, these products must be accessible to lactose-intolerant consumers6.

Gluten-free products are an economic burden to people with celiac disease since they are more expensive on average per 100 g compared to other products of a similar category. For example, in India, gluten-free products were found to cost 232% more than their gluten-containing counterparts7. Similarly, Guennouni et al.,8 found that gluten-free products in Morocco are almost 365% more expensive than their gluten-containing counterparts. This study also reported a significant price difference in products sold online and those sold in supermarkets. Gluten-free breads utilise alternative gluten-free grains, which necessitate a complex manufacturing process to produce a satisfactory product in terms of texture, taste and nutrients, resulting in additional expenditure9. Economically disadvantaged individuals on strict gluten-free diets face greater food insecurity and challenges in maintaining dietary compliance due to limited access and higher costs of gluten-free products10.

Gluten-free bread has a less stretchy crumb, hardens quickly, and can crumble easily due to the absence of gluten11. The lack of gluten reduces carbon dioxide retention during dough rising, preventing gluten-free dough from gaining volume and resulting in an unsatisfactory crumb12. Due to the lack of starch gelatinisation and water-holding capacity, gluten-free bread often has a small volume and can be very dense or complex; however, this can be improved by adding enzymes, hydrocolloids, and emulsifiers13. Wheat bread, compared to gluten-free bread, is known to be moist, soft, and spongy14. Gluten-free breads have different sensory properties compared to gluten-containing breads due to the absence and presence of gluten, respectively11.

Research on gluten-free bread has been growing in the market, aiming to improve bread quality as researchers discover new ways to enhance its structure, texture, acceptability, nutritional content, bioactive content, and shelf life15. Hydrocolloids, such as guar gum, xanthan gum, and carboxymethyl cellulose, effectively improve bread by modifying starch gelatinisation, increasing water-holding capacity, and increasing viscosity and gas retention16. The development of improved gluten-free bread properties has increased as the demand for gluten-free bread has risen over the years17. While the use of xanthan gum in gluten-free bread has been widely explored, mucilage, on the other hand, remains unexplored despite being found to improve the quality properties of gluten-free bread18. This research will provide empirical data on the combination of mucilage from okra (Abelmoschus esculentus (L.) fruits and xanthan gum, which may yield desirable structural and sensory qualities in gluten-free bread compared with xanthan gum alone, improving elasticity and crumb integrity14. Just like in the finding in which chia mucilage and flaxseed mucilage were found to enhance the quality of gluten-free pan bread, It is therefore hypothesised that a gluten-free bread stabilised with a combination of xanthan gum and mucilage will yield bread with improved textural and sensory properties, such as higher moisture retention, greater specific volume, enhanced elasticity, and better crumb integrity while maintaining acceptable sensory properties compared to gluten-free bread stabilised with gluten only19. This study aimed to investigate the effects of combining mucilage from okra (Abelmoschus esculentus (L.) fruits and xanthan gum on the physical, texture, and sensory properties of gluten-free bread, and to evaluate consumer acceptance of the formulated product. Due to practical constraints, this study focuses on the macro-structural and instrumental properties of the bread, providing a foundation for future molecular investigations into starch retrogradation.

Research methodology

Extraction of okra mucilage

Fresh okra (Abelmoschus esculentus (L.) fruits; 5 kg) were sourced from SwaTsakani Farm (Tzaneen, Limpopo, South Africa) and stored at 5 °C before processing. The pods were rinsed with distilled water, after which the seeds and calyces were removed. The fruits were then cut into uniform pieces (10 × 14 cm). The sliced material was blended with deionised water at approximately 19,000 rpm for 3 min using a Philips Series 3000 ProBlend 4 blender. The resulting slurry was heated at 80 °C for 90 min under continuous stirring and subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min (Beckman Coulter Avanti J-26 S XPI) to separate pulp and fibrous residues. Mucilage present in the supernatant was precipitated by the addition of 95% (v/v) food-grade ethanol, washed twice with 50% ethanol, and further reprecipitated with 95% ethanol until the supernatant became clear. The final precipitate was freeze-dried at − 56 °C for 24 h using a Harvest Right HRC100 freeze dryer (USA).

Research design

A quantitative approach was adopted using a Completely Randomised Design (CRD) with four treatments (B1–B4) and three replicates per treatment (n = 3), yielding 12 experimental units. The independent variable was the mucilage-to-xanthan gum ratio at four levels: 100:0, 75:25, 50:50, and 25:75, corresponding to formulations B1–B4. Dependent variables included physical properties (specific volume, moisture content, air cell size), textural attributes (hardness, chewiness, springiness, crumbliness, cohesiveness), and consumer acceptability. Bread samples were prepared under standardised conditions, and treatments were randomly assigned to minimise bias. This design enabled objective comparisons among formulations and supported statistical analysis to determine the effect of stabiliser ratios on gluten-free bread quality. The 100% mucilage treatment was excluded based on pilot studies, as it failed to produce a suitable dough, ensuring only feasible formulations were tested.

Preparation of gluten-free bread

The gluten-free breads were prepared following the method described by Franco et al. (2020), using rice flour (100 g), salt (2 g), dry yeast (6 g), crystal sugar (12 g), margarine (10 g), egg white (10 g), and water (150 g) as the main ingredients. Rice flour was used as a substitute for wheat flour. Mucilage (from Okra fruits) and xanthan gum were added in varying ratios. To prepare the bread, the dry ingredients, excluding the yeast, were mixed by hand in one bowl. Separately, the yeast was activated in warm water (35–40 °C). The oil, milk, and activated yeast were then combined in a separate bowl and subsequently incorporated into the bowl with the dry ingredients. The mixture was blended for approximately 9 min at a constant speed to ensure uniform kneading and form a smooth dough. The dough was then allowed to rise for 40–50 min at 30 °C and approximately 85% relative humidity. After rising, it was shaped and proofed, then baked in an oven at 180 °C for approximately 25 min, or until fully cooked. Once baked, the samples were left to cool before being subjected to analysis. A digital oven thermometer (SKU: THE0004; Wedoall Catering Equipment, South Africa), equipped with a stainless-steel probe and a measurement range of 0–300 °C, was employed to monitor internal temperature. Doneness was operationally defined as achieving a core temperature between 94 °C and 98 °C.

Texture profile analysis

The texture properties of gluten-free bread were evaluated using a TA. XT Plus texture analyser (Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK), equipped with a 50 kg load cell, following the method described by Chantieng et al.20. After baking and cooling to room temperature, each bread loaf was sliced into uniform pieces approximately 20 mm thick. The samples were stored at room temperature (approximately 22 ± 2 °C) before analysis. A cylindrical aluminium probe (P/36R, 36 mm diameter) was used to perform texture profile analysis. Each bread sample was placed on the platform of the texture analyser and subjected to a double-compression test to 50% of its original height (i.e., a compression distance equal to half the sample thickness). The probe speed was set at 2 mm/s, and a trigger force of 10 g was applied to initiate the test. The texture analyser software recorded the force-time curves, from which the following parameters were calculated: Hardness (peak force during the first compression), Cohesiveness (ratio of the area under the second compression curve to the area under the first compression curve), Gumminess (product of hardness and cohesiveness), Chewiness (product of gumminess and springiness). Texture measurements were conducted at four different time points after bread production: immediately after baking (0 h), and at 24, 48, and 72 h. Samples were stored in sealed containers at room temperature between measurements to simulate typical storage conditions and monitor staling effects. All tests were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation.

Specific volume determination

The specific volume of gluten-free bread was measured following the AACC (2000) standard method, using the millet (Panicum miliaceum) seed displacement technique as described by da Costa de Oliveira et al.21. Millet seeds were chosen for their small, uniform size and shape, which allow accurate filling of spaces around irregular bread surfaces without leaving gaps. To determine bread volume, a calibrated container was filled with millet seeds to a fixed level, after which the bread sample was gently placed inside, displacing the seeds. Bread volume (mL) was calculated based on the displaced seed volume, and bread mass was measured using an analytical balance. Specific volume (SV) was then computed as the ratio of bread volume (mL) to its mass (g) using the formula: SV = Bread Volume (mL) / Bread Mass (g).

Moisture content determination

Moisture content was measured following the method described by Stinton et al.,22. 1 g samples from the crust and crumb portions of the gluten-free bread were accurately weighed into pre-weighed aluminium foil boats. The boats containing the samples were then placed in an oven at 105 °C and dried for 16 h. After drying, the samples were removed and allowed to cool in a desiccator before being reweighed. The moisture content was calculated as the percentage weight loss using the following formula: Moisture (%) = (Initial Weight - Final Weight) / Initial Weight × 100.

Air cell size and cell density determination

The internal crumb structure of the gluten-free bread was analysed by digital image analysis to quantify air cell size and cell density. Slices of bread approximately 10 mm thick were cut from the central portion of each loaf. The slices were placed on a flat surface with consistent lighting conditions to minimise shadows and reflections. Photographs of the crumb structure were taken using a high-resolution digital camera fixed on a stand to maintain a consistent distance and angle for all samples. The camera settings (e.g., resolution, exposure, and white balance) were kept constant throughout the analysis to ensure uniformity. Before sample analysis, calibration and scaling were performed to convert pixel measurements to millimetres using a ruler included in the field of view, and ImageJ was used to determine the pixel-to-mm conversion factor. This step ensured that air cell areas were accurately reported in mm² and enhanced the reproducibility of the measurements. Captured images were imported into ImageJ software (version 1.44c). Images were first converted to grayscale, and contrast was adjusted to enhance the visibility of air cells. Thresholding was applied to differentiate air cells (void spaces) from the bread matrix using pixel intensity values, following the method outlined by Onipe et al.23. Individual air cells were identified automatically by the software, and their areas were measured in square millimetres (mm²). For each bread sample, three images from different regions were analysed to ensure representativeness. The mean air cell size was calculated and reported along with standard deviations.

Consumer acceptance test

Untrained consumer panellists were recruited through convenience sampling, based on accessibility, location, and time constraints. Recruitment was conducted through local networks, including a church group, work colleagues, and social media platforms. A total of 100 volunteers aged 18 years or older who regularly consume bread and report no allergies to gluten or eggs participated in the consumer acceptance test. The sample size was calculated using the one-sample mean sample size formula, statistically justified based on previous studies that estimated the standard deviation of hedonic ratings on a 9-point scale at 1.5, and a minimum meaningful difference of 0.5 units was considered. Using a 95% confidence level, the required sample size was calculated as approximately 35 panellists; however, the number increased to 100 to enhance reliability and account for potential dropouts or incomplete responses. This approach ensured that the hedonic data were robust and representative for analysis24. Before participating, each panellist received a verbal explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures. They were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained, and confidentiality of personal information was assured. Participants were thanked for their contributions at the conclusion of the tasting session. The sensory attributes assessed included appearance, colour, aroma, taste, aftertaste, and overall acceptability. Each participant received a 1 cm-thick slice of each bread sample, a glass of water, and a sensory evaluation form. Acceptability was measured using a nine-point hedonic scale, where 1 indicated “dislike extremely”, and 9 indicated “like extremely.” Sensory sessions were conducted in informal but controlled environments, depending on participant availability and comfort. Sessions lasted approximately 15–25 min and were held at the researcher’s workplace, the church, or the participant’s residence, if preferred25.

Data analysis

All measurements for each variable were performed in triplicate. Statistical differences were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparison test. For variables where the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene’s test, p < 0.05), Welch’s ANOVA followed by the Games-Howell post-hoc test was applied to ensure a more robust F-statistic and accurate pairwise comparisons. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied. Statistical analyses were executed using R Statistics software version 4.5.1. Results are presented using simplified graphical formats with consistent scaling and error bars to facilitate interpretation and comparison.

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the sensory evaluation. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, ingredient composition (including potential allergens), and their right to withdraw at any time. Confidentiality of responses was assured. This study was approved by the College of Agriculture and Environmental Sciences Research Ethics Committee, University of South Africa (Ref #: 2024/CAES_HREC/5202). All experiments involving human participants were performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Results and discussion

Physical and textural properties of gluten-free bread

Moisture content

The moisture content of the gluten-free bread increased significantly (p ≤ 0.05) with the incorporation of mucilage and xanthan gum, showing a progressive increase as the proportion of mucilage increased at both 0 and 24 h. Additionally, the presence of mucilage significantly (p ≤ 0.05) enhanced moisture retention after 24 h of storage when compared to bread formulated solely with xanthan gum (Fig. 1). There were no interactive effects between sample types and exposure time (Fig. 2). Mucilage exhibits greater water-holding capacity than xanthan gum because mucilage is a more highly branched, flexible with a highly hydrophilic polysaccharide network, unlike xanthan gum which has a rigid, viscous network that stabilises but does not trap as much water26. This property plays a crucial role in maintaining moisture in gluten-free bread27. Effective water retention in gluten-free bread decreases dryness and hardness, while enhancing cohesiveness, resulting in a softer texture, greater volume, and reduced crumbling13. Water retention is essential for extending shelf life, as it mitigates moisture loss over time and thereby preserves product quality28. Mucilage enhances bread quality by enhancing bread quality by improving moisture retention thereby contributing to reduced firmness development and maintenance of crumb softness, which are commonly used physical indicators of freshness Elsorady et al.29. For example, gluten-free bread enriched with linseed mucilage showed a gradual reduction in moisture over 3 days of storage, negatively affecting freshness and shelf life and leading to increased hardness and resilience30. Overall, mucilage serves as an essential additive that delays moisture evaporation and positively impacts key bread qualities, such as hardness, texture resilience, volume, and flavour, making it highly beneficial in gluten-free bread recipes31.

Specific volume

The specific volume of gluten-free bread significantly (p ≤ 0.05) increased with the increase in the amount of mucilage added to xanthan gum. Furthermore, all formulations with mucilage had significantly higher specific volumes (Fig. 2). The gel-like consistency of mucilage enables it to expand and trap carbon dioxide during fermentation, thereby increasing bread volume32. The incorporation of 2 to 10% chia seed mucilage has been shown to enhance the specific volume of bread33. Furthermore, mucilage improves the elasticity of gluten-free bread, resulting in a softer texture, reduced chewiness, and a more uniform crust34. Mucilage forms a highly hydrophilic, flexible, and branched polysaccharide network capable of extensive water binding and elastic deformation, allowing it to trap CO₂ bubbles during dough preparation, unlike xanthan gum, which produces a more rigid and viscous polymer matrix with limited elastic response, which constrains bubble expansion35.

Air cell size

The mean air cell area size of gluten-free bread did not change significantly with increasing amounts of mucilage added to xanthan gum (p > 0.05). However, formulations containing mucilage had significantly (p < 0.05) smaller air cells (Fig. 3) and fewer air cells compared to the formulation without mucilage (Fig. 4). This suggests that mucilage alters the crumb structure by enhancing the dough’s viscoelasticity and reducing both the size and number of air cells36. Mucilage, therefore, retains gases without forming a rigid network as observed with 100% xanthan gum16. B1 (100% xanthan gum) shows pores in a rigid and dense crumb, while the samples with mucilage exhibit low pore count, in a less dense and open structure. B4 (25% xanthan gum, 75% mucilage), displayed the highest porosity, indicating enhanced gas retention and crumb expansion at higher mucilage levels (Fig. 4). Increasing mucilage in xanthan gum–based gluten-free bread softens the dough matrix, allowing gas bubbles to expand and coalesce, resulting in a more open, porous crumb with higher gas retention and relatively fewer air cells, as seen in B437. Similarly, xanthagum gums, due to their highly viscous and rigid structure, stabilise air cells and restrict further expansion, thereby limiting bubble coalescence during baking38. B2 seems to maintain a balance between preserving air cell bubbles and uniform crumbs, given that the addition of more mucilage lowers viscoelasticity, leading to higher bubble coalescence and porous crumb structure39. The images captured were generated specifically for quantitative crumb structure analysis and were not intended to serve as representative photographic documentation of whole loaves; therefore, visual comparison at loaf level is not available and is acknowledged as a study limitation.

Instrumental texture profile and short-term storage stability

Hardness

Although the increase in bread hardness over storage time was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), a general trend of increasing firmness was observed across all formulations with prolonged storage (Fig. 5). In contrast, a consistent decrease in hardness was observed with increasing levels of mucilage incorporation into the xanthan gum system. Formulation B4 exhibited significantly lower hardness values than B1 across all storage intervals, indicating improved crumb softness at higher mucilage inclusion levels. The reduced hardness associated with increased mucilage content is strongly linked to mucilage’s high water‑holding capacity, which contributes to reduced firmness development during storage. Changes in hardness are commonly used as indirect physical indicators of storage‑related quality changes in bread systems, particularly in gluten‑free products. The lower hardness values observed in formulation B4 suggest improved maintenance of crumb softness during storage, although no direct molecular assessment of starch retrogradation was conducted in this study40. While xanthan gum has previously been reported to delay early firmness development in gluten‑free bread, its rigid and highly viscous network may become less effective at maintaining crumb softness during extended storage. In the absence of thermal analysis, the retention of crumb softness observed in mucilage‑containing breads is interpreted as a physical effect consistent with changes in water distribution within the crumb matrix rather than direct modification of starch retrogradation mechanisms41. Compared with xanthan gum alone, mucilage may promote a more hydrated crumb environment by limiting water redistribution within the bread matrix, which is reflected in a slower increase in firmness as measured by texture profile analysis42, 43. In addition, mucilage may contribute to improved crumb softness through physical interactions within the gluten‑free matrix, including associations with protein components that support a less compact structure during storage. Xanthan gum, by contrast, primarily increases viscosity through the formation of a rigid polymer network, which can restrict structural flexibility and contribute to firmer textures over time36. As increased hardness is a well‑established contributor to perceptions of staleness and undesirable crumbly textures, excessive firmness negatively influences consumer acceptance44. Therefore, the incorporation of mucilage as a natural hydrocolloid represents a promising formulation strategy for improving crumb softness and physical quality of gluten‑free bread during short‑term storage45.

Cohesiveness

The incorporation of mucilage into xanthan gum formulations led to a slight progressive increase in the cohesiveness of gluten-free bread, though not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6). The water retention capability from mucilage enhances the internal network formed by xanthan, leading to more moisture in the gluten free bread crumb even though the effect size could be small to produce a significant increase in the cohesiveness of gluten free bread46. Similarly, storage duration had no statistically significant effect on cohesiveness (p > 0.05), although a minor, consistent decrease over time was observed for all formulations. The minor reduction in cohesiveness is likely associated with typical moisture loss and crumb firming; however, further molecular studies are required to confirm the specific impact on the starch-hydrocolloid matrix47. Previous studies have shown that bread cohesiveness is negatively affected by extended storage, primarily due to moisture loss, which contributes to crumb firming and staling48. This reduction in cohesiveness is often perceived as a tougher, more crumbly texture, an undesirable sensory attribute44. These findings suggest that incorporating mucilage may help maintain cohesiveness, thereby preserving the textural integrity and overall quality of gluten-free bread during storage. The enhanced moisture-holding capacity of mucilage and xanthan gum has been reported to improve cohesiveness, resulting in a softer, spongier crumb structure in gluten-free bread formulations45. The observed minor increase in cohesiveness, rather than the increase in hardness, suggests that the weakening of the internal structure within the gluten-free bread crumb can be attributed to the interplay between moisture loss and the structural integrity of the hydrocolloid-starch matrix10.

Chewiness

Incorporating mucilage into xanthan gum formulations resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the chewiness of gluten-free bread (p ≤ 0.05) compared to formulations without mucilage. Chewiness values also remained lower in mucilage-containing samples after up to 72 h of storage (Fig. 7). Mucilage improves the water retention by forming a gel network that mimics gluten’s viscoelastic properties, which traps gas bubbles during fermentation, stabilizes crumb structure during baking, and helps retain moisture during storage—resulting in softer, less chewy loaves over time49. Gluten-free breads are often perceived negatively as dry, crumbly, or mealy rather than chewy; however, chewiness should always be gauged empirically through consumer testing to ensure that consumer expectations for some GF breads are met50. The combination of xanthan gums with other gums has been found to yield bread with softer crumbs than formulations with xanthan alone51.

Gumminess

Incorporating mucilage into xanthan gum formulations resulted in a statistically significant reduction in the gumminess of gluten‑free bread (p ≤ 0.05) compared to formulations without mucilage. Gumminess values also remained lower in mucilage‑containing samples throughout the 72 h storage period (Fig. 8). The reduction in gumminess observed in mucilage‑containing gluten‑free bread reflects the known effects of plant‑derived hydrocolloids on crumb texture, which are closely associated with changes in moisture retention and hydrocolloid network organisation18. The addition of plant mucilage to a xanthan gum system reduces gumminess primarily by altering water distribution and gel network formation within the crumb matrix. Mucilage incorporation appears to partially weaken the rigid, xanthan‑dominated structure, resulting in a softer crumb with lower resistance to deformation during compression testing52, 46. These physical changes are consistent with the high water‑binding capacity and flexible polysaccharide structure of mucilage, which promotes a more hydrated and less compact crumb structure during storage. Previous studies have reported that combining xanthan gum with other hydrocolloids can reduce crumb firming, limit excessive gumminess, and improve overall crumb structure during storage45. Similarly, the combination of xanthan gum and mucilage has been shown to improve crumb structure, enhance gas retention, and reduce the development of gummy textures during short‑term storage periods of up to 48–72 h53. In the present study, the observed reduction in gumminess aligns with these findings and suggests that mucilage contributes to improved physical texture by moderating the rigidity of the hydrocolloid–starch matrix. However, as molecular transformations were not directly assessed, these interpretations are limited to physical and instrumental observations derived from texture profile analysis.

Resilience

Incorporating mucilage into xanthan gum formulations resulted in a statistically significant reduction in resilience (p ≤ 0.05) only at 50% and 75% mucilage inclusion levels in gluten-free breads, compared to formulations without mucilage. Resilience values in mucilage-containing samples also remained lower after up to 72 h of storage (Fig. 9). The observed decrease in resilience at higher mucilage levels (50–75%) is likely attributed to modifications in the xanthan-dominated elastic network. It is possible that increased mucilage content competes for available water and limits xanthan chain extension and entanglement. This may shift the structure toward a more hydrated, mucilage-rich matrix with reduced contributions from xanthan–starch and xanthan–protein interactions, thereby lowering rebound capacity39. Previous studies have similarly reported that cactus mucilage, when combined with xanthan gum in gluten-free flatbreads, improves moisture retention and reduces staling rate (p < 0.05), although textural performance depends on the level of incorporation18. At lower concentrations, mucilage may interfere with the xanthan gum viscoelastic matrix, contributing to a softer and less elastic crumb, while higher concentrations may further weaken the gel network and reduce structural integrity after baking54. Overall, these findings suggest that although plant-derived mucilage enhances moisture retention, it may compromise structural resilience at elevated levels and should therefore be applied at moderate concentrations55.

Springiness

Incorporating mucilage into xanthan gum formulations resulted in an increase in the springiness of gluten‑free bread across all formulations and storage durations. This increase was statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05) at 50% (B3) and 75% (B4) mucilage inclusion levels (Fig. 10). The enhanced springiness observed at higher mucilage levels is associated with increased hydration and changes in the structural organisation of the dough matrix, which influence the viscoelastic response of gluten‑free bread systems26. Greater hydration within the crumb matrix may facilitate elastic deformation and recovery during compression, resulting in improved springiness values. The results indicate that mucilage contributes to a more flexible and hydrated crumb structure that supports elastic recovery under mechanical stress. This behaviour may be linked to mucilage’s water‑binding and gelling properties, which promote a less rigid hydrocolloid network and improve the capacity of the crumb to recover after deformation. Although weak physical associations and polymer entanglements among mucilage, xanthan gum, starch, and proteins may contribute to this behaviour, such interactions were not directly measured in the present study and are therefore discussed only in the context of physical texture responses56. Similar trends have been reported in previous studies, where the incorporation of plant‑derived mucilage was associated with improved dough elasticity and increased springiness in gluten‑free systems, primarily attributed to enhanced hydration and structural effects within the dough matrix57. In the present study, the increased springiness observed at higher mucilage inclusion levels supports the role of mucilage in improving the physical textural performance of gluten‑free bread during short‑term storage.

Consumer acceptance of gluten-free bread stabilised with mucilage and xanthan gum

The incorporation of mucilage into Xanthan gum did not result in any statistically significant differences in the acceptance parameters across most formulations. However, the formulation containing 75% mucilage (B4) showed a statistically significant decrease (p ≤ 0.05) in overall acceptance (Fig. 11). The sensory evaluation outcomes reveal that the partial replacement of xanthan gum with mucilage has negligible effects on overall acceptability across most formulations, indicating mucilage’s potential as a functional hydrocolloid substitute without compromising sensory quality58. Conversely, the formulation incorporating 75% mucilage (B4) exhibited a statistically significant reduction in overall acceptance, suggesting that elevated mucilage concentrations adversely influence sensory attributes, including texture, mouthfeel, and visual appearance of gluten-free breads37. In the absence of instrumental colorimetry (e.g., CIELAB), this study employed hedonic sensory scaling to evaluate colour. This approach prioritises consumer-relevant visual acceptability, a key determinant of market success for clean-label gluten-free products. Although instrumental colour measurements (such as CIELAB parameters) could complement sensory data, consumer perception remains the primary driver of product acceptance and was therefore prioritised. This methodological choice aligns with findings by Lee et al.59, who reported that while instrumental metrics such as lightness (L*) may correlate with acceptance, the influence of colour on overall consumer preference is indirect relative to the broader sensory experience.

Study limitations

The assessment of staling behaviour in this study relied on instrumental physical indicators, specifically texture profile analysis and moisture retention. While these provide robust evidence of crumb quality during storage, they do not provide direct thermal or molecular characterisation of starch retrogradation. The exclusion of techniques such as Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) was a deliberate boundary of this postgraduate research project, which focused on the macroscopic quality and consumer-relevant attributes of clean-label formulations within defined ethical and budgetary parameters. Furthermore, while the study prioritised sensory evaluation to capture the holistic consumer perception of colour and appearance—the primary driver of market acceptance—instrumental colour measurements (CIELAB) and standardised whole-loaf photography were not conducted. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as a physical and textural performance assessment of the hydrocolloid system, providing a baseline for future molecular-level investigations into the starch-mucilage matrix.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the partial substitution of xanthan gum with okra mucilage significantly enhances the physical and textural stability of gluten-free bread during short-term storage. The incorporation of mucilage improved moisture retention and specific volume while effectively moderating firmness development, gumminess, and crumb elasticity. These results confirm that okra mucilage acts as a potent natural hydrocolloid that improves physical storage stability by maintaining a more hydrated and flexible crumb structure. While higher mucilage inclusion levels (50–75%) yielded the most significant instrumental improvements in softness and volume, a clear trade-off was observed with sensory preference. The formulation with 50% mucilage (B3) emerged as the optimal balance, significantly improving crumb texture without the decline in overall consumer acceptance seen at the 75% level. Therefore, okra mucilage is a viable, clean-label alternative for enhancing the quality of gluten-free bakery products. Future research should build on these physical findings by utilizing thermal and molecular analysis to further elucidate the interactions within the starch-hydrocolloid network and to extend the evaluation to long-term shelf-life stability.
