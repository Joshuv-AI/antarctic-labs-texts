Introduction

Pulmonary infections are rare complications associated with bronchoscopy [1]. However, this can lead to severe abscess formation and a fatal clinical course [2,3]. Herein, we presented an extremely rare case of lung abscess following bronchoscopy associated with lung cancer that extended directly into the chest wall, and we presented the radiological features and surgical findings of this rare condition. This surgical case was reported in accordance with the SCARE 2020 guidelines [4].

Case presentation

A 49-year-old man with adenocarcinoma (cT2bN2M1a [malignant pericardial effusion], stage VIB) harboring an epidermal growth factor receptor-sensitizing mutation (L858R) (Fig. 1A) was treated with osimertinib as first-line treatment, which led to a partial response (Fig. 2 A-B, Fig. 3A-B). Seven months after osimertinib administration, progression of the primary lesion was observed (Fig. 2C, Fig. 3C-D). Endobronchial transbronchial rebiopsy of the primary lesion was performed via bronchoscopy. Hematoxylin and eosin staining indicated a transformation to poorly differentiated non-small cell lung cancer (Fig. 1B). Additionally, assessment of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression using the antibody 22C3 (Dako pharmDx) demonstrated a tumor proportion score of 100 %.

Six days after re-biopsy via bronchoscopy, a 2nd line treatment with atezolizumab, bevacizumab, carboplatin, and paclitaxel was initiated. Two days after the chemotherapy, the patient presented with chills and anterior chest wall pain. Physical examination revealed high fever (39.4 °C) and snowgrasping sensation in the left pectoral region. The patient's left upper chest wall was erythematous and tender upon palpation. Chest radiography revealed a large cavitary mass shadow with an air-fluid level in the left upper lung field and subcutaneous emphysema in the left hemithorax (Fig. 2D). Blood test results revealed leukocytosis (white blood cell count, 18,130 cells/mm3 with 87 % neutrophils) and high C-reactive protein (CRP) level (19.36 mg/dL). Peripheral blood cultures were negative for bacterial growth. Sputum culture revealed normal pharyngeal flora, including Streptococcus species, Neisseria species, and Haemophilus parainfluenzae. The patient was administered empirical meropenem and vancomycin. Despite 9 days of meropenem and vancomycin therapy, fever of approximately 39 °C persisted and CRP level increased (25.82 mg/dL). Moreover, the white blood cell count decreased (1690 cells/mm3 with 52.6 % neutrophils) because of myelosuppression caused by chemotherapy. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a gas-containing lung abscess, measuring 10 cm in the left upper lobe and subcutaneous emphysema (Fig. 4A-D). The coronal view of the CT indicated a continuous passage of air from the lung abscess to the subcutaneous emphysema beneath the pectoralis muscle (Fig. 5A-D). While pus was identified upon chest wall puncture, microbial cultures yielded negative results.

The patient was immediately transferred to our hospital for surgery. Based on the diagnosis of lung abscess-associated lung cancer and subcutaneous abscess of the left chest wall, emergent surgery was performed. Surgical access was obtained through a 12-cm anterior thoracotomy with video assistance. Surgical debridement of the subcutaneous abscess under the pectoralis muscle was performed, resulting in drainage of a large volume of purulent material (Fig. 6A). We confirmed that the lung abscess had directly extended to the chest wall (Fig. 6B-D) (Fig. 7A-B), and we performed segmentectomy of the upper division of the left lung. The bronchial stump was covered with a pericardial fat flap.

Postoperative inflammatory response resolved rapidly. Postoperative CT performed 6 days after surgery demonstrated that the residual left lung had fully expanded, and the subcutaneous emphysema had disappeared (Fig. 8A-B). Histopathological examination of the resected specimens revealed the presence of invasive adenocarcinoma at the margin of the abscess, and necrotic and inflammatory changes were observed adjacent to the cancer. The patient was uneventfully discharged from the hospital on the 7th postoperative day. Chemotherapy was safely resumed on the 20th postoperative day. No recurrence of lung cancer or infection was observed at 9 months after the surgery.

Discussion

Bronchoscopy is a useful diagnostic tool for pulmonary diseases, particularly lung cancer. Although it is generally safe, several major complications can occur, including bleeding, pneumothorax, and lung infections [5,6]. A lung abscess is an infectious pulmonary disease characterized by the presence of a pus-filled cavity within the lung parenchyma. Severe infectious complications, such as lung abscesses, following bronchoscopy are rare but potentially refractory and considerably serious [2,3]. The initial diagnosis of a lung abscess is usually made using chest radiography, which reveals a lung cavity with an air-fluid level. In the present case, the chest CT findings were consistent with a lung abscess associated with lung cancer (Fig. 4).

Thoracic empyema, which is defined as the presence of bacteria or pus in the pleural cavity, is a potentially fatal infection with high morbidity and mortality rates. Empyema necessitatis is a rare complication of empyema in which the pleural infection spreads outside the pleural space to involve the soft tissues of the chest wall [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. It can present as pleuritic chest pain. Most commonly, an enlarging soft tissue mass is present on the chest wall [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In the present case, continuous passage of air from the lung abscess to the subcutaneous emphysema under the pectoralis was observed on CT (Fig. 5A-D). This finding led to the suspicion of a lung abscess directly extending into the chest wall through the second intercostal space without any pleural effusion, which ruled out empyema necessitatis. A pulmonary abscess extending to the chest wall should be suspected in any patient presenting with subcutaneous emphysema and an underlying pulmonary infection.

The identified risk factors for lung abscess following bronchoscopy include cavitation, intratumoral low-density areas, and abnormal bronchoscopic findings (e.g., visible tumors, bronchial stenosis, and mucosal irregularities) [5,6]. Ishida et al. also reported that transbronchial lung biopsy of lung lesions measuring >30 mm with heterogeneous CT enhancement was predictive of the development of lung abscesses following bronchoscopy [2]. In the present case, a CT scan of the thorax revealed a 6 cm mass in the left upper lobe, characterized by heterogeneous CT enhancement (Fig. 4). In addition, both host and bacterial factors are thought to contribute to the progression of lung abscesses. Regarding host factors, the most common risk factors for progressive lung abscesses are systemic or local immunocompromised status, such as malignancy and diabetes mellitus. Our patient developed a lung abscess associated with lung cancer and was systemically immunocompromised after chemotherapy. Histopathological examination of the resected specimens revealed an invasive adenocarcinoma expressing high levels of PD-L1 adjacent to the lung abscess. PD-L1 is the ligand for the inhibitory receptor programmed death 1 (PD-1), which is expressed on various immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages. The PD-L1/PD-1 pathway suppresses immune cell function and plays an important role in evasion of tumor from the host immunity. Similarly, the activation of this pathway can attenuate host immune responses to pathogens, resulting in impaired pathogen clearance and increased mortality during acute and chronic infections [11]. Therefore, in the present case, high PD-L1 expression on tumor cells may have contributed to abscess formation and rapid progression of lung cancer.

Management of lung abscesses is usually based on prolonged antibiotic treatment [12]. Failure of conservative management may necessitate drainage with invasive techniques (percutaneous, endoscopic, or surgical) or open surgical removal of lung lesions [12]. Surgical intervention is the treatment of choice for rare complications of an extended local infection through the chest wall, as seen in empyema necessitates [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. In the present case, the lung abscess along with lung cancer extended directly to the chest wall (Fig. 6C-D). As conservative treatment failed, it was successfully treated with lung resection and surgical drainage of the chest wall. Lung abscesses associated with lung cancer are rare and life-threatening complications that may lead to significant delays in the commencement of oncological treatment and potentially worse long-term outcomes. Surgical therapy is the treatment of choice for this rare condition, providing rapid focus control and effective resolution; thus, it should be promptly initiated when conservative treatment fails.

Conclusion

The present study describes an extremely rare case of lung abscess following bronchoscopy, with lung cancer directly extending into the chest wall. The possibility that lung abscesses may extend into the chest wall should be considered during the differential diagnosis of infectious diseases, especially in patients presenting with subcutaneous emphysema. Successful treatment of this rare condition depends on prompt surgical intervention. Notably, the surgical treatment in this case was administered without any delay.
