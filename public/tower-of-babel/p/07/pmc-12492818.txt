Patient and mission characteristics of helicopter emergency medical services in the Republic of Ireland: a retrospective analysis
Authors: D Hennelly, S Masterson, C O’Donnell, P A Jennings, S McHugh, R Quinn, C Deasy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe National Ambulance Service (NAS) has operated a Paramedic-staffed Helicopter Emergency Medical Services (HEMS) in the Republic of Ireland since 2012. This study describes patient and mission characteristics, to provide insight into this service and inform future service development.MethodsA nationwide population-based retrospective study of HEMS missions was conducted from routinely collected health data over an 11- year period between 1 July 2012 and 30 June 2023.ResultsIn the study period HEMS in Ireland received 8,392 mission requests, Patient contact occurred in 60.1% of missions (n = 5,045) and 73.4% (n = 3703) were airlifted to hospital. Adults represented 90.3% (n = 4,535) of patients, predominantly male at 69.1%, with a median age of 55 years (IQR: 34 years) [35:69]. Medical patients accounted for 50.3% (n = 2,524), cardiac aetiologies (32.3%) and out of hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) (30.3%) were the most common medical presentation. Trauma patients account for 49.7% (n = 2,498). Road Traffic Collisions (RTCs) (31.4%) followed by farming and machinery incidents (17.4%) were the most common trauma presentation. HEMS teams significantly reduced pain in both trauma (ꭕ2 = 680, p < .001) and medical cases (ꭕ2 = 186.13, p < .001) during the care provided. The majority 73.1% (n = 6133) of total missions were conducted within a 20 min flight time radius of the HEMS base.ConclusionsOver the past decade, HEMS in Ireland has provided a valuable contribution to the care of seriously ill and injured patients, particularly in the west and southwest. Service use reflects HEMS base proximity, indicating potential benefits from geographic expansion. Current activation criteria appears to target an appropriate patient cohort, however sustained reassessment of dispatch criteria and hours of operation may enhance service availability. Continued development should focus on adopting evidence based-practices and advancing critical care capacity to align with international HEMS standards.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-025-01459-3.Keywords: Air ambulance, Emergency helicopter, Emergency medical service, Paramedics, Prehospital emergency care

Introduction / Background

Dedicated Helicopter Emergency Medical Services (HEMS) have operated in the Republic of Ireland since 2012. The evolution of the Irish aeromedical infrastructure has been previously described [1].

National health policies and service reconfigurations have led to increased centralisation of complex care such as major trauma, stroke and ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) [2–5]. Consequently, timely access to HEMS is increasingly essential for providing safe and efficient transport over longer distances to appropriate receiving facilities.

HEMS in Ireland is staffed by Advanced Paramedics (AP), who primarily deliver care consistent with ground based ambulance services [6], however they do offer extended scope of practice, notably for in-flight sedation or analgesia.

Internationally, HEMS teams often provide advanced prehospital critical care interventions including prehospital emergency anaesthesia (PHEA), the use of pre-hospital blood products and emergency surgical interventions [7–9] which is not currently available in the Irish HEMS model.

By global standards, Irish HEMS is still in an early development phase. This study aims to describe mission characteristics and patient profiles attended by HEMS and explore opportunities to enhance service delivery and clinical care as the health systems models of prehospital critical care and retrieval evolve.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective descriptive study examined mission profiles and patient characteristics of the Advanced Paramedic (AP) staffed HEMS in the Republic of Ireland over an eleven year period between 1st July 2012 and 30th June 2023. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [10].

Setting & population

The Republic of Ireland spans 70,273 km2 with a population over 5.2 million people and a growth rate of 1.3%. Population density at 73 per km2 is significantly lower than the European average, with the sparsest area to the west and border regions (33 per km2) [11].

The National Ambulance Service (NAS) is the primary provider of prehospital care responding to over 350,000 ambulance calls each year and employs over 2,000 staff across 102 locations [12]. Dublin Fire Brigade also provides Emergency Medical Services (EMS) for large parts of the population of Dublin.

HEMS responds to both medical and trauma emergencies, initially focusing on remote areas in the west. From 2012 to 2019, a single HEMS base operated centrally with the Irish Air Corps using an Agusta Westland 139. In 2019, a second base was added in the southwest, initially funded by a local charity and now state-supported, using an Airbus EC135 operated by Gulf Med Aviation Services (GMAS).

Both operators provide a dual pilot daytime only service. Daytime in Ireland ranges from 17 h in spring and summer to eight hours or less during the winter months. Operators are unified under a single governance and dispatch system. HEMS Missions are selected using a standardised HEMS Dispatch Criteria, incorporating Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Dispatches (S1).

Clinical crews collaborate with non-clinical aeromedical dispatchers to assess mission suitability considering patient acuity, aviation constraints (e.g., weather, daylight), and operational feasibility. Rapid Response Vehicles (RRVs) are used when aircraft response is impractical or outside of daylight operating hours, clinical crews work a 12 h shift (08:00–20:00), during the winter period they commonly respond by road after the hours of darkness. The system’s development, dispatch protocols, and clinical standards have been previously described in the literature [1].

Clinical standards

HEMS Paramedics are senior Advanced Paramedics (AP) who have extensive experience in resuscitation and major trauma care. They undergo further aviation specific training including Crew Resource Management (CRM), Helicopter Underwater Evacuation Training (HUET) and they participate frequently in clinical case reviews as a part of the clinical governance framework.

AP level care includes a broad spectrum of Advanced Life Support (ALS) interventions including intravenous (IV) and intraosseous (IO) access, non-drug facilitated endotracheal intubation, needle thoracocentesis, manual defibrillation, cardioversion and the use of multiple time critical medications (inotropes, antiarrhythmics, anticonvulsants, steroids, antibiotics) and multiple analgesic options (Methoxyflurane to Morphine, Fentanyl and Ketamine) [6] While HEMS Paramedic competencies generally align with ground based services, HEMS crews are authorised to administer enhanced in-flight sedation or analgesia to optimise safe and efficient flight. This incorporates the use intravenous Ketamine, Fentanyl, Morphine or Midazolam. However prehospital critical care interventions such as PHEA, the use of pre-hospital blood products and emergency surgical interventions are not currently available in the Irish HEMS model.

Data Sources / Participants

Data were collected using multiple routinely gathered NAS systems Medical Priority Dispatch System™ (MPDS®) [13], Computer Aided Dispatch (CAD) and Siren Field User [14], electronic Patient Care Reports (ePCR). Additional mission specific information was also collected via shift incident reports (SIR) for the ongoing governance, audit and quality improvement. HEMS specific datasets were created using standardised excel templates, completed by HEMS APs. Data incorporated demographic, physiological, treatment variables and mission profile data (Fig. 1).

Validation

Datasets were cross-referenced with CAD exports, HEMS SIR and ePCR data to ensure the maximal integrity and quality of data prior to being entered into the study database. Additional variables relating to patient characteristics following the introduction of a second HEMS platform were available for the period 1st Aug 2019– 30th June 2023.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe patient demographics, geographical spread, receiving facilities, mechanism/aetiology of disease or injury, vital sign trends, transportation decisions and short-term clinical outcomes. Statistical Package “R” was used for statistical analysis [15]. Chi squared test was used to assess statistical significance between categorical proportional variables. An omnibus McNemar test was used to test whether the numbers of patients in each category differed significantly between initial measurement and final measurement. Where the omnibus test indicated a significant result, post hoc tests, with correction for multiple comparisons, were carried out to determine which pairwise comparisons were statistically significant.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by the Health Service Executive Reference Research Ethics Committee Midlands Area and Corporate (RRECB0722DH).

Results

Operational mission characteristics

Between the 1st July 2012 and the 30th June 2023, the National Ambulance Service responded to 8,392 HEMS missions across eight health regions. Four regions accounted for the majority of missions: West (24.9%, n = 2092), Mid-West (16.8%, n = 1408), South-West (16.5%, n = 1385) and Midland (14.9%, n = 1248) accounting for 73.1% (n = 6133) of all missions.

Highest frequency of use was within a 20 to 30 min flight radius of each HEMS base, calculated at a flight speed of 120 nautical miles per hour (Fig. 2). Regions that were over a 30 min flight time from each base (North-West, North-East, South-East, East) accounted for only 26.9% (n = 2,259) of mission requests. Current distribution of missions suggests higher use of HEMS in rural populations (Fig. 3).

HEMS activity peaked in the afternoon (57.7%, n = 4,845), followed by morning (26.5%, n = 2,221) and evening missions (5.5%, n = 463). Time data was missing in 10.3% of cases. Seasonal variation showed 56.1% (n = 4,706) of missions occurred in spring and summer. Winter “declined before launch” rates (20.5%) were 2.6 times higher than in summer (7.8%).

Of all missions, 60.1% resulted in the attendance of the HEMS team at scene and patient contact defined as “completed” mission status (n = 5,045). HEMS teams were “stood down prior to launch” in 5.8% of cases (n = 467) and “stood down while airborne” in 19.8% of cases (n = 1168). Missions declined pre-launch due to clinical or aviation factors accounted for 12.6% (n = 1,061), and 1.7% (n = 151) were declined mid-flight, usually due weather (Table 1).

Mission characteristics for all calls 2012–2023

Among completed missions, 73.4% (n = 3,703) involved patient transport. Of these, 80% (n = 2,889) were transferred to four hospitals—University College Hospital Galway (37%, n = 1,323), Cork University Hospital (17%, n = 609), University Hospital Limerick (15%, n = 538), and Tallaght University Hospital (11%, n = 419). Non-transport cases (e.g., deceased on scene, transferred by road) accounted for 26.6% (n = 1,342).

Dispatch type data were available for 6,472 missions. Primary missions made up 64.7% (n = 4,190), secondary 34.7% (n = 2,247), and tertiary (interfacility transfers) 0.6% (n = 35).

Patient characteristics

Of the 5,045 completed missions, clinical disposition data were available for 5,022 patients. Medical cases accounted for 50.1% (n = 2,524) and trauma cases for 49.5% (n = 2,498).

Adults comprised 90.3% (n = 4,535) and paediatrics 9.4% (n = 472). Males represented 69.1% (n = 3,470). Median patient age was 55 years (IQR 34; range 35–69). Medical patients were significantly older than trauma patients (63 vs. 44 years; IQRs 23 and 36, respectively). Older adults (65–84 years) more often presented with medical issues, while trauma was more prevalent in younger adults (15–44 years). Paediatric trauma (11.2%) exceeded paediatric medical cases (5.4%) compared to adults (Table 2).

Patient and mission characteristics for completed calls 2012–2023

Additional variables relating to patient characteristics were available following the introduction of a second HEMS platform for the period 1st Aug 2019– 30th June 2023, this enhanced data collection and attributed to a better understanding of the patients served. In this period HEMS units were tasked to 3,647 missions and completed 2,512, complete data was available for 2,497 cases resulting in a missing data rate of 0.6% (n = 15).

Adults accounted for 89.8% (n = 2,256), with trauma representing 57.6% (n = 1,298) and medical cases 42.4% (n = 958).

Among paediatric cases (9.6%, n = 241), trauma predominated (69.3%, n = 167). Leading trauma mechanisms included road traffic collisions (RTCs) at 31.7% (n = 411), farming/machinery injuries (17.4%, n = 225), falls > 2 m (16.8%, n = 220), and equestrian incidents (8.1%, n = 105). Sports injuries, assaults, animal attacks (12.4%, n = 161), low falls (10.6%, n = 137), and burns (3%, n = 39) accounted for the remainder.

In patients aged 85+, RTCs, low falls, and farming incidents were most common. Equestrian injuries were notably frequent in the 15–34 age group (Fig. 4a).

Injury profile

Injury data were available for 1,205 trauma cases. Isolated musculoskeletal injuries were most common (43.9%, n = 529), followed by multisystem trauma without Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) (20%, n = 242), isolated TBI (8.3%, n = 100), and multisystem trauma with TBI (6.1%, n = 72). Burns (3.4%, n = 41), spinal cord injuries (3.9%, n = 47), and other wounds (14.4%, n = 174) constituted the remainder.

Patients with multisystem trauma (with or without TBI) and isolated TBI accounted for 34.4% (n = 414) of trauma cases and 11.3% of total HEMS case load from 2019 to 2023. Injury patterns varied significantly by age (p < .001): burns and TBI were more common in children, while musculoskeletal injuries and multisystem trauma predominated in adults (Fig. 5b).

The most common medical emergencies were cardiac in origin (32.3%, n = 334), a combination of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), (10.9% n = 113) and confirmed ST Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI), (21.4% n = 221). Out of Hospital Cardiac Arrest (OHCA) and /or return of spontanous crculation (ROSC) was the next most common medical mission attended, with neurological emergencies accounting for 16.6% (n = 171).

STEMI and ACS peaked in the 55–64 age group (48%, n = 75; p < .001). Poisoning/overdose and neurological conditions were more frequent in those ≤ 34 years, while drowning and respiratory conditions were most prevalent among children (0–14). Sepsis appeared disproportionately in the youngest (0–14) and oldest (85+) age groups, representing 8% in both, versus < 4% in all others (Fig. 6).

Pain was frequently reported across both adult and paediatric cases. However, full pain score data (initial and final) were available in 56% (n = 1,405) of completed missions.

Statistically significant pain reduction was achieved in both trauma (χ² = 680, p < .001) and medical patients (χ² = 186.13, p < .001). Over 60% of adult (n = 580) and paediatric (n = 56) trauma patients presented with severe pain (> 8). At final assessment, < 5% of adults (n = 42) and < 3% of children (n = 3) remained in the severe category. Most transitioned to mild or no pain (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of HEMS in the Republic of Ireland, detailing mission profiles, patient characteristics over a ten year period.

Mission characteristics

Accurate patient selection is key to effective HEMS use. Irish HEMS reported 60% completion rates and a 19.8% stand-down rate post mission acceptance consistent with established international benchmarks, although definitional variations exist across jurisdictions. Comparative analysis from Japan and the UK revealed stand down rates ranging between 20 and 35% [16, 17]. However significant variability exists internationally as evidenced by Berkeveld et al. 2021 [18] who analysed 18,638 dispatches in the Dutch HEMS setting reporting a cancellation rate of 54.5% (95% CI 53.8–55.3%).

Of the 5,045 cases for which the HEMS teams provided care, 73.4% (n = 3703) resulted in a HEMS transportation (i.e. the patient was flown to hospital), this appears higher than international comparators though cross-jurisdictional definitional variations must be acknowledged. These findings may reflect Ireland’s distinctive geographical challenges, including rural topography, limited access to specialist centres and the dispersed distribution of ground based emergency ambulances particularly in the western and south-western regions.

This is the first report of significant seasonal variation in HEMS utilisation, demonstrating a mission completion rate of 65.7% (n = 1526) in summer vs. 50.9% (n = 916) in the winter. Concurrently, mission declined rates increased from 7.8% in the summer to 20.5% in the winter, reflecting limitations of daylight-only service provision within a Northern European system. Prospective observational studies similar to the methodology employed by Curtis et al., who provided insights into the out of hour’s unmet need in a UK critical care HEMS unit may help assess unmet need in the Irish context [19].

Most missions occurred within a 20–30 min flight radius, supporting the principle that a targeted regional response, within 20–30 min/flight time is more likely to be utilized to support ground services compared to a longer range or more protracted response of 30–45 min. Opportunities exist for HEMS bases development to support underserved regions of the North West / North East of the country which has a large dispersed rural population. Cross border collaborations with HEMS providers in Northern Ireland may enhance service provision for these communities, however further research using methodologies similar to those employed by Jagtenberg et al. or Garner [20–22] is required to determine optimal HEMS base locations that can provide both an efficient and equitable population coverage.

Patient characteristics

Patient reported pain serves as a critical metric for prehospital service performance evaluation. Data suggests a statistically significant reduction in pain following HEMS team intervention, however data availability was low for this cohort at 56%. The reduction in pain potentially reflecting enhanced scope of practice and familiarity surrounding procedural sedation and analgesia (PSA), for the management of complex fractures and the reduction of limb threatening dislocations.

International research has demonstrated time saving benefits of HEMS for STEMI patients and the delivery of high performing teams to OHCA cases [23, 24] which represent the most prevalent medical cases attended. In the Irish context the introduction of HEMS in 2012 aligned with the health services ‘Optimal Reperfusion Strategy’ which seen the centralisation of Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PPCI) centres [4]. This strategic alignment enabled HEMS development concurrent with ACS program development streamlining dispatch processes, interventions and handovers to optimise STEMI care.

While HEMS teams’ resuscitation scope matches ground services, frequency of exposure, the delivery of experienced leadership focused on high performance team management and the ability to provide post-ROSC sedation, inotropic support and efficient transfer to a PPCI capable facility constitute enablers of a high performing care systems [25].

Our study revealed that the HEMS response to trauma in the older population focuses predominantly on road trauma, farming accidents and short falls. International evidence highlights the under triage of elderly patients and the late identification of occult major trauma [26, 27]. Ireland’s Major Trauma Audit (MTA) demonstrates that most Major Trauma patients are aged ≥ 65 with low falls as the leading cause of injury (62%), with a large proportion (89%) of patients aged ≥ 75 years having been injured in low-fall events [28].

Further research is required to ascertain if under-triage of older persons constitutes a systemic issue in the Irish context and if HEMS response provides a demonstrable benefit for this patient cohort.

In paediatric populations, isolated Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI), caused by RTCs and falls was most prevalent. The centralisation of Paediatric major trauma and specialist services will significantly impact the care requirements for this cohort of patients. Due to the infrequent nature and significant complexity of these patients frequent specialist training and closely managed standards are essential for safe care, as demonstrated in the literature [29, 30].

Some of the most complex trauma cases attended treated were multisystem trauma with or without TBI and/or isolated TBI. These cases made up 34.4% (n = 414) of all traumatic injuries reported by HEMS and 11.23% of the overall case load between 2019 and 2023. Further analysis of this cohort of critically injured trauma patients using validated assessment tools such as the Revised Trauma Score (RTS) or New Trauma Score (NTS) or National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) scores may enhance understanding of associated patient mortality [31–33]. The linking prehospital data to in-hospital datasets or national registries may also provide greater insights into the outcomes of patients and their specific care requirements, such as the work carried out by Alstrup et al. assessing one year mortality of Helicopter vs. ground transfer in the Danish HEMS Setting [34].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that the most critically injured patients benefit from ‘Roadside’ critical care competencies such as neuro-protective anaesthesia, chest thoracostomy or the use of pre-hospital blood products, combined with advanced diagnostics such as pre-hospital ultrasound and invasive haemodynamic monitoring [35–37].

The delivery of such interventions is not routinely available in the Irish prehospital setting and are outside the scope of Irish HEMS Paramedics. A recently published systematic review by Lavery et al. [38] demonstrated the benefits of prehospital critical care being delivered as part of interdisciplinary teams, the development of specialist roles such as Critical Care Paramedic (CCP) in Ireland will support the progression of both CCP and PHEM physician delivered care both in ground based services and in the HEMS environment.

Since the establishment of the service in 2012, there has been limited evolution of the clinical standards of care provided on HEMS in Ireland. Aligning with international standards requires strong leadership, stakeholder engagement, and systematic implementation of evidence-based practices [7–9, 24, 29, 30, 36–38]. The translation of this established research into practice in a stepwise approach is essential for the delivery of safe and effective prehospital critical care in the HEMS setting in Ireland.

Strengths and limitations

The decade-long study period revealed trends aligned with health system evolution. While recent data (2019–2023) included enhanced patient characteristics and demonstrated HEMS impact on pain reduction, the observational design limited illness/injury severity analysis due to the lack of historic data using validated scoring systems such as NACA / RTS or ISS. There was also limited data available for outcomes of patients not transported by HEMS including cases where resuscitation was commenced but ceased at scene.

Furthermore, this study lacked data on medium or long term survival, which restricted the evaluation of longer-term patient outcomes.

Data from 2019 to 2023 incorporates the Covid-19 pandemic era which may have influenced mission profile or call rates due to a more restrictive mission acceptance profile.

Conclusion

Over the past decade, HEMS in Ireland has provided a valuable contribution to the care of seriously ill and injured patients, particularly in the west and southwest. Service use reflects HEMS base proximity, indicating potential benefits from geographic expansion. Current activation criteria appears to target an appropriate patient cohort, however sustained reassessment of dispatch criteria and hours of operation may enhance service availability. Continued development should focus on adopting evidence based-practices and advancing critical care capacity to align with international HEMS standards.
