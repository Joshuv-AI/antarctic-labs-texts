Nuclear receptor coregulator NRIP1 R448G modulates T cell gut homing to control intestinal inflammation
Authors: Xiangjun Chen, Hee Seung Hwang, Bihua Li, Yanhua Zhao, Koushik Ghosh, Lei Deng, Elizabeth A. Creasey, Orr Ashenberg, Daniel B. Graham, Ramnik J. Xavier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAdvancements in human genetics revealed mechanisms of immune dysfunction underlying immune-mediated diseases. In this regard, genome-wide association studies can serve as unbiased discovery engines to identify genetic risk factors linked to human disease. Recent studies identified a missense variant in the nuclear receptor coregulator Nuclear Receptor-Interacting Protein 1 (NRIP1) and demonstrated its association with risk of developing inflammatory bowel disease. Here, we report the development of a mouse model of the NRIP1 R448G variant and demonstrate how it impairs mucosal homeostasis. We show that NRIP1 corepresses induction of a gut-homing transcriptional program in T cells mediated by retinoic acid signaling. Furthermore, we demonstrate that NRIP1 R448G inefficiently represses this program, leading to enhanced gut homing in models of colitis.

Nuclear receptors (NRs) are well-known sensors of nutrients, hormones, and specific metabolites. They are expressed ubiquitously across diverse immune cell populations and serve as important regulators that integrate metabolism and immune responses (1–3). As such, NRs are associated with immune-related diseases including autoimmune and inflammatory diseases and are attractive targets for therapeutic interventions (4–7). NRs belong to a family of transcription factors (TFs) whose activity can be modulated by coactivators and corepressors that bind to the receptors in a ligand-dependent or -independent manner. In this way, these regulators mediate the transcriptional activation or repression of NR downstream target genes in response to hormones, metabolites, and drugs (8–11).

Precise modulation of NR activities is crucial for maintaining intestinal homeostasis and resolving inflammation, as the gut is a complicated milieu of stimuli for NRs, particularly during infection or inflammation. NR-interacting protein 1 (NRIP1) interacts with multiple NRs including retinoic acid receptors (RARs), peroxisome proliferator activated receptors, and liver X receptors (12, 13), making it an important regulator of NR activities. NRIP1 is expressed at low levels in most tissues, with induction observed through activation of various NRs such as estrogen receptors and RARs (12, 14). In line with regulatory roles of these NRs in metabolism, NRIP1 plays a significant role in energy homeostasis by controlling metabolic gene networks and mitochondrial functions (12, 15, 16). Functions for NRIP1 were reported in the liver, adipose tissue, heart, and brain but remain unexplored in the gut.

NRIP1 has been recognized as an important contributor to inflammation, primarily through its effects on macrophages. Studies with NRIP1 knockout (KO) mice revealed the physiologic role of NRIP1 in skin and adipose inflammation (17, 18). NRIP1 has been suggested to act as a coactivator for NF-κB and to promote inflammatory cytokine production, thereby regulating macrophage activation and the M1-M2 phenotype switch as well as the establishment of endotoxin tolerance (19–21). More recently, NRIP1 has been associated with risk for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) by fine-mapping genome-wide association studies (GWAS) (22), and colocalization of these SNPs with an eQTL in CD4+ T cells (23). Additionally, the coding variant NRIP1 R448G (rs2229742) was identified as a risk factor for Crohn’s disease (CD) (odds ratio 1.09, P = 4.69 x 10-7) (24). While NRIP1 has been described as a corepressor for RAR signaling in T helper (Th) 9 differentiation (13), its regulation of T cell function and the specific involvement of the genetic variant in gut pathology remain unclear.

Here, we report that NRIP1 is upregulated in effector and regulatory CD4+ T cells during gut inflammation. NRIP1 deficiency promotes gut homing of activated CD4+ T cells through upregulation of the homing receptor CCR9. Meanwhile, NRIP1 KO and R448G increase the inflammatory signatures of CD4+ T cells with imbalanced functions. Thus, these synergistic effects drive gut inflammation, implicating NRIP1 as a potential therapeutic target for inflammatory diseases.

Results

NRIP1 Is Enriched in Intestinal CD4+ T Cell Subsets and Upregulated during Inflammation.

Fine-mapping GWAS identified several noncoding SNPs in linkage disequilibrium within a locus on chromosome 21 associated with IBD (22). Subsequent exome sequencing studies further pinpointed an independent signal in this locus, identifying a rare missense variant of NRIP1 (R448G) associated with risk for CD (24). Given that the IBD risk locus overlaps with an eQTL modulating NRIP1 expression in CD4+ T cells (23), we sought to define the phenotype of NRIP1 R448G in intestinal T cells. To study the role of NRIP1 in IBD pathogenesis, we first mapped its expression in single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) datasets from patients with CD (25) or ulcerative colitis (26). Expression levels of NRIP1 were observed across T cell subsets from intestinal biopsies but were notably upregulated in inflamed samples relative to healthy controls, suggesting dynamic regulation in disease settings (Fig. 1 A and B).

To systematically evaluate in vivo effects in physiological and disease conditions, we constructed Nrip1 KO and R448G mouse lines using CRISPR-Cas9-based genetic manipulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A) and validated Nrip1 deficiency and mutation, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C). We first immunophenotyped cells from the intestines of wild type (WT), KO, and R448G mice at steady state (Fig. 1 C–F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Notably, we found that KO mice had higher proportions of IFN-γ+ IL-17A+ and Rorγt+ CD4+ T cells in the lamina propria (LP) of both the colon and small intestine (Fig. 1 C–F). Similarly, R448G mice displayed increases in IFN-γ+ IL-17A+ and Rorγt+ CD4+ T cells in the colon (Fig. 1 C–F). There were no obvious changes in total Foxp3+ and Rorγt+ Foxp3+ regulatory T cell (Treg) subsets (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

NRIP1 Deficiency Enhances Gut Homing of CD4+ T Cells.

The marked skewing of T cell lineages in the intestines of KO and R448G mice prompted us to identify the underlying mechanism. Given that NRIP1 has been shown to modulate NR activity (12, 13), and the retinoic acid receptor plays a key role in T cell homeostasis, we first evaluated the effects of NRIP1 on RAR signaling responses to all-trans retinoic acid (atRA) (Fig. 2A). First, we engineered an RAR reporter cell line by introducing a fusion protein composed of the RAR ligand binding domain and the DNA binding domain of Gal4. In this line, stimulation with the RA analog AM580 induces transcription of a luciferase reporter gene under the control of a 9× Gal4 upstream activator sequence. Importantly, coexpression of NRIP1 suppressed AM580-induced transcriptional responses, indicating a key negative regulatory role for NRIP1, and the R448G variant exhibited a partial loss of function in this repressive activity (Fig. 2A).

During inflammation, RA levels in the intestine increase, which can promote gut homing of activated CD4+ T cells through receptors including CCR9 and α4β7 (27, 28). CCL25, which is the ligand for CCR9, is also markedly upregulated in the inflamed intestine (29). Consistent with this, the gut-homing receptor CCR9 was upregulated in both KO and R448G cells compared to WT cells after atRA treatment (Fig. 2 B–D). We therefore compared the gut-homing capabilities of activated CD4+ T cells with different genotypes. Activated CD4+ T cells were stimulated with atRA and labeled with cell trace dyes prior to transfer into T cell–deficient, recombinase-activating gene 1 (Rag1) KO recipient mice (Fig. 2 E–G and Dataset S1). We detected higher ratios of Nrip1 KO cells in the mesenteric lymph nodes, small intestine, and colon relative to spleen (Fig. 2 F and G), indicating enhanced gut homing. Collectively, these results demonstrate that NRIP1 is involved in RA-induced gut homing of activated CD4+ T cells, and its dysfunction leads to upregulation of the gut-homing receptor CCR9.

NRIP1 Is a Repressor of NR Signatures in CD4+ T Cells.

To illuminate the mechanism of how NRIP1 regulates CD4+ T cell phenotypes in gut inflammation, we performed bulk RNA-seq paired with Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease (CUT&RUN) analysis of RAR genomic occupancy of in vitro activated WT and KO T cells before and after RA treatment. Comparing KO to WT T cells treated with atRA, we observed a dramatic upregulation of the gut-homing program (Ccr9, Itgb7, S1pr1, and S1pr4) (Fig. 3A and Dataset S2). These results are consistent with our in vivo experiments demonstrating enhanced gut homing in KO and R448G T cells (Fig. 2 E–G). In CUT&RUN analyses, the number of RARα-bound sites in KO cells was much greater than in WT (15,253 vs. 2,782), suggesting that loss of Nrip1 repositions RARα occupancy throughout the genome. Within RARα-bound sites in KO cells, there was enrichment for the known TF binding sites of the RAR:RXR heterodimer (q val = 0.008). We next linked these changes in RARα occupancy to changes in gene expression and found that, of the 376 genes with increased expression between KO and WT T cells (FDR < 0.05), 300 genes were positioned in proximity (within 50 kb) to a RARα-bound site in KO T cells, and there was a significant number of RARα-bound sites near these genes (899 sites, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). In particular, we observed increased RARα occupancy in KO T cells for gut-homing receptors (Ccr9, Itgb7, S1pr1, and S1pr4) and NRs (Vdr and Nrip1) (Fig. 3C). These results indicate NRIP1 can regulate RARα activity within CD4+ T cells and drive programs of gut homing and inflammation.

To obtain a higher-resolution view of NRIP1 modulation of the gut-homing program, we performed a single-cell multiome (transcriptome and open chromatin) analysis of in vitro activated CD4+ T cells with or without atRA treatment. Clear transcriptional differences were observed between WT and KO T cells treated with atRA (Fig. 4A). Treatment with atRA induced specific gene signatures related to RAR signaling (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), consistent with previous reports (13). More importantly, Nrip1 deficiency led to dramatic upregulation of genes enriched in cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and NR signaling, as well as downregulation of genes involved in histone modification and DNA methylation (Fig. 4 B–D).

We next utilized Single-Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC+) as a method to infer enhancer-driven regulons (eRegulons) consisting of a TF and its candidate genomic enhancers linked to downstream target genes (30). Analysis of eRegulon activity in each condition (WT, KO, WT + atRA, and KO + atRA) recovered NRIP1-correlated TF activators (Fig. 4E), including regulons with activity specifically elevated in the KO + atRA condition. Several of these regulons were predicted to drive the expression of gut-homing receptors: the Vdr, Rarb, Smad3, and Runx1 regulons express Ccr9 as a downstream target gene, while the Smad3 and Klf3 regulons express Itgb7. Furthermore, target genes from the Vdr and Rarb regulons are enriched for retinol metabolism. Overall results from these regulons indicate a negative regulatory role for NRIP1 on RA signaling and T cell gut homing.

To obtain a more comprehensive view of NRIP1-regulated genes and their transcriptional regulation, without filtering for strong relationships with target genes, we performed further motif enrichment analysis on the single-cell open chromatin data using Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment (HOMER) (31). Accordingly, we identified RAR:RXR as one of the top TFs enriched in open chromatin regions in atRA-stimulated KO versus WT T cells, along with JunB and its heterodimer partner BATF (Fig. 4F), which have been shown to be important for CD4+ T cell differentiation and function (32, 33). This is striking, as BATF is required for RA-induced upregulation of Ccr9 by controlling binding of RARα to the regulatory regions of the Ccr9 gene (34). Moreover, Batf-deficient CD4+ T cells exhibit impaired Th17 function and imbalanced Treg induction (35–37). These conclusions are consistent with our findings that Nrip1 KO CD4+ T cells, in which BATF motif binding sites are enhanced, display elevated gut-homing receptor Ccr9 and skewed Th17 cytokine responses.

NRIP1 Dysfunction in CD4+ T Cells Promotes Intestinal Inflammation.

Having demonstrated that NRIP1 negatively regulates RA signaling and gut homing by T cells, we sought to contextualize these functions in a disease setting using the CD45RB-high T cell transfer model of chronic colitis. Naïve CD4+ T cells from WT, R448G, or KO mice were adoptively transferred into Rag1 KO recipient mice. Littermate controls were used in all experiments to mitigate effects of the microbiome. We observed significant body weight loss and shorter colons (Fig. 5 A and B) in mice receiving Nrip1 KO or R448G T cells compared to WT, indicating that NRIP1 dysfunction exacerbates intestinal inflammation. Histopathology of colon tissue sections revealed typical features of colitis in all groups of mice, including inflammatory infiltrates, crypt abscesses, edema, and alterations in crypt morphology (Fig. 5C). Mice receiving Nrip1 KO T cells displayed a trend toward increased histopathological scores that did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5D), so we refocused our characterization on more quantitative biomarkers of immune function. Further analyses of immune populations in the inflamed intestines showed larger numbers of CD4+ T cells (Fig. 5 E and F) and evident increases in IFN-γ+ IL-17A+ CD4+ T cells in the colons of Nrip1 KO and R448G T cell transferred mice (Fig. 5 G and H). A significant decrease in Foxp3+ Treg proportion was also observed in Nrip1 KO T cell recipient mice (Fig. 5 G and I). Consistent with this, we detected elevated IFN-γ and IL-17A concentrations as well as higher expression of Ifng and Il17a in colon tissue from Nrip1 KO and R448G T cell recipient mice (Fig. 5 J and K). Together, these results confirm a protective role of NRIP1 in intestinal homeostasis, and that NRIP1 dysfunction aggravates gut inflammation upon disease onset.

Discussion

As an important regulator of various NRs, NRIP1 expression is correlated with immune homeostasis as well as responses to infection and inflammation (12, 20). Inspired by the specific enrichment of NRIP1 in intestinal CD4+ T cell populations, we elucidated the impact of NRIP1 on T cell gut homing and function in the context of intestinal homeostasis and inflammation. NRIP1 is involved in several processes during intestinal inflammation. First, NRIP1 regulates RA-induced gut homing of activated CD4+ T cells by modulating Ccr9 and Itgb7 expression. Second, NRIP1 influences the cytokine profiles of Th cells in the presence of inflammatory stimuli. Importantly, the IBD risk variant NRIP1 R448G enhances homing of effector CD4+ T cells with proinflammatory signatures, which might synergistically impact the balance between effector CD4+ T cells and Tregs, ultimately exacerbating intestinal inflammation. Thus, NRIP1 R448G may impart T cell intrinsic phenotypes related to gut homing and inflammation, while potential extrinsic phenotypes related to regulation by Tregs and other immune cell types have yet to be explored.

NRIP1 has been demonstrated as a multifaceted coregulator that functions as either a coactivator or corepressor of target genes (12, 21, 38). In macrophages, NRIP1 was suggested to interact with NF-kB to induce inflammatory cytokine expression (21). In contrast, our results indicate a corepressor role of NRIP1 in CD4+ T cells. NRIP1 deficiency elevated Ccr9 expression through the RA–RAR pathway and increased inflammatory cytokines. These observations underscore the pleiotropic effects of NRIP1 on different immune cell populations during the development of inflammation.

In summary, our findings establish a connection between NRIP1 and T cell gut homing in intestinal inflammation and present a framework to understand the effects of NR coregulators on T cell responses and inflammation control. Our study provides insights into the intricate interplay between NRs and immune regulation as well as potential targets correlated with immune cell tissue homing for therapeutic interventions in inflammatory diseases. Given that inhibition of gut homing by blockade of α4β7 integrin has shown efficacy in treating IBD, our findings from human genetics and functional characterization of the NRIP1 R448G risk variant may suggest orthogonal approaches to target gut-homing pathways.

Materials and Methods

Mice.

Mouse experiments in this study complied with all relevant ethical regulations and were conducted according to protocols 2003N000158 and 2011N000126 approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Massachusetts General Hospital. The Nrip1 KO and R448G knock-in mouse lines were constructed by CRISPR/Cas9-based systems. C57BL/6 (JAX:000664) and Rag1 KO (JAX:002216) mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. All mice were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions and cohoused in a room with a 12-h light/dark cycle, temperature of 21 °C, and relative humidity of 30 to 70%. Male and female, 8 to 12 wk-old, age- and sex-matched mice were used for mouse experiments. Littermates were used as controls.

Nrip1 KO and R448G Mouse Generation.

CRISPR/Cas9-based systems were applied to generate Nrip1 KO and R448G mouse lines at Harvard University’s Genome Modification Facility. The single guide RNA (sgRNA) target sequence (5′-CAGCTTCCGGCTTTTCGATC-3′) was screened and selected from the mouse Nrip1 genome DNA sequence. sgRNA was in vitro-transcribed and purified by using the Guide-It sgRNA In Vitro Transcription kit (ClonTech) and MEGAclear kit (Thermo Fisher). The homology-directed repair template as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1A was ordered from Integrated DNA Technologies and PAGE-purified. Transfection-ready Cas9 SmartNuclease messenger RNA (mRNA) (Eukaryotic) was from System Biosciences (CAS500A-1). The founder mice harboring Nrip1 knock-out or R448G mutation were verified by sequencing the amplicon products (PCR primers: 5′-CACGAGCTATAAGAACTCACT-3′, 5′-CTGGCTGTATACAGTGGTGG-3′) with primer 5′-GAACGGGCACAGCCAGAAC-3′. Nrip1 KO mice have a 13 bp deletion near the sgRNA target sequence, which induces a frameshift to delete Nrip1 protein, as validated in SI Appendix, Fig. S1B. The founder mice were backcrossed to C57BL/6 for three generations before being used for experiments.

Immunoblot of Nrip1.

Purified mouse naïve CD4+ T cells were stimulated by plates coated with 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD3e and 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD28 antibodies, with or without 100 nM atRA supplement in full RPMI 1640 medium (10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS), GlutaMAX, penicillin-streptomycin, MEM nonessential amino acids, sodium pyruvate, and 2-mercaptoethanol) for 3 d. 1 × 106 cells were harvested and lysed in 100 μL RIPA buffer, and the supernatant of cell lysis was assayed for Nrip1 expression. Anti-RIP140 monoclonal antibody (targeting an epitope within 178 amino acids from the N-terminal half, clone 6D7, 1:2,000, Sigma) and β-Actin (13E5) rabbit monoclonal antibody (HRP conjugate, 1:10,000) were used for immunoblotting.

Immunophenotyping.

Mouse colons were harvested, cut into pieces, and then treated with epithelial strip buffer at 37 °C for 30 min with stirring, washed with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS), and digested with enzyme digestion buffer at 37 °C for 30 min with stirring. Epithelial strip buffer contained 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Invitrogen), 1 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma), 15 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) (pH 7.2 to 7.5, Gibco), and 5% FBS (Gibco) in HBSS buffer, calcium- and magnesium-free. Enzyme digestion buffer contained 0.167 mg/mL Liberase TL (Roche), 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma), 15 mM HEPES (pH 7.2 to 7.5), and 5% FBS in HBSS buffer. Cells in the supernatant were harvested and resuspended in 44% Percoll with carefully underlaid 67% Percoll. The lymphoid fractions at the interphase were collected and resuspended with full RPMI 1640 medium for use.

To detect cytokine-producing cells, cells were stimulated with 50 ng/mL PMA (Sigma), 1 μg/mL ionomycin (Sigma), and GolgiPlug (BD) at 37 °C for 2 to 3 h, then harvested for staining with cell viability dye (1:1,000) and antibodies (1:150–1:200) using Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD). To detect Foxp3+ Treg subsets, cells were stained with antibodies and cell viability dye using Foxp3/TF Staining Buffer Set (eBioscience).

NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (Agilent) with NovoExpress (v1.4.1) software was used for flow cytometry detection. FlowJo (v10.7.1) was used for data analysis. Antibodies used in this study for flow cytometry included BV785 rat anti-mouse CD45 (clone 30-F11), BV650 rat anti-mouse CD4 (clone GK1.5), PE-CF594 rat anti-mouse IL-17A (clone TC11-18H10.1), APC rat anti-mouse IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2), APC-Cy7 Armenian hamster anti-mouse TCRb (clone H57-597), FITC anti-mouse/human Helios (clone 22F6), PE-Cy7 anti-mouse CD199 (CCR9) (clone CW-1.2) from BioLegend; rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 (clone 2.4G2), PE-CF594 mouse anti-mouse Rorgt (clone Q31-378) from BD; PE rat anti-mouse Foxp3 (clone FJK-16s), and Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 506 from eBioscience.

Colitis Models.

For the T cell transfer model of chronic colitis, CD4+ T cells from spleens were isolated and enriched using EasySep™ Mouse CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL). Cells were blocked with anti-CD16/CD32 antibodies (1:100) and stained with Brilliant Violet 785 anti-mouse CD4 (GK1.5), PE anti-mouse CD25 (PC61), FITC anti-mouse/human CD44 (IM7), and APC anti-mouse CD62L (MEL-14) antibodies (BioLegend, 1:200) in magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS) buffer (PBS with 2.5mM EDTA, 2% FBS) at 4 °C for 20 min. After washing, cells were resuspended in MACS buffer with 7-AAD (BioLegend, 1:50) for sorting by BD FACSAria II with FACS Diva 6.1. Naïve CD4+ T cells (CD4+ CD25− CD44− CD62L+) were sorted and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For the T cell transfer model, 5 × 105 naïve CD4+ T cells were intraperitoneally injected per mouse. The body weight of mice was monitored weekly, and mice were sacrificed for phenotyping in week 7. To detect cytokines in the colonic tissue, 1 cm distal colon tissue was harvested and homogenized in PBS by the TissueLyser LT. Supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 15,000× g for 10 min, and cytokines in the supernatant were detected by ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Mouse IFN-γ and IL-17A kits (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Cross-sectional colon tissue sections were stained with H&E and scored for histopathology based on previously described features (39). Summed scores were reported based on the following criteria: degree of inflammation in the lamina propria (0-3), goblet cell loss (0-2), abnormal crypts (0-3), presence of crypt abscesses (0-3), mucosal erosion and ulceration (0-1), and submucosal spread to transmural involvement (0-3).

For dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis, mice were provided with sterile water containing 2.5% DSS (molecular weight 40,000 to 50,000; Affymetrix) for indicated days. T cells from different tissues were harvested as indicated.

Gut-Homing Assay.

CD4+ T cells from mouse spleens were purified using EasySep™ Mouse CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL). T cells were stimulated by plates coated with 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD3e and 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD28 antibodies, with 100nM atRA supplement in full RPMI 1640 medium for 4 d. Cells were harvested and then stained with different cell proliferation dyes (Invitrogen) for different genotypes following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were then mixed and transferred to Rag1 KO mice. Recipients were treated with 2.5% DSS for 3 d before the intravenous injection of labeled cells. At 18 h post–intravenous injection, labeled cells were detected from the spleen, mesenteric lymph nodes, and small intestine and colon LP.

T Cell Repolarization.

T cells were cultured in full RPMI 1640 medium and stimulated with plates coated with 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD3e and 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD28 antibodies. Cytokines were supplemented for different conditions: Th1, 20 ng/mL rmIL-12p70 (Peprotech); Th17, 25 ng/mL rmIL-6 (Peprotech), 20 ng/mL rmIL-1b (Peprotech), and 20 ng/mL rmIL-23 (R&D Systems). Cells were restimulated on day 3 by 50 ng/mL PMA (Sigma), 1 μg/mL ionomycin (Sigma), and GolgiPlug (BD) for 3 to 4 h, then harvested for intracellular cytokine staining using Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD).

NR Reporter Assays.

The luciferase reporter was generated in the HEK293 cell line by stable integration of the luciferase gene under the control of 9× Gal4 UAS (upstream activator sequence), along with Gal DBD-RARα LBD driven by the SV40 promoter. Cells were transfected in a six-well plate using 1.5 µg of either an empty vector or NRIP1 construct. Following transfection, cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 104 per well in a 96-well plate and treated with a RARα-specific agonist (AM580) or DMSO for 18 h. Reporter activity was assessed using the One-Glo luciferase assay kit.

Single-Cell Multiome ATAC and Gene Expression.

Mouse splenic naïve CD4+ T cells were purified using EasySep™ Mouse Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL). Cells were then stimulated on plates coated with 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD3e and 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD28 antibodies, with or without 100 nM atRA supplement in full RPMI 1640 medium. Cells were harvested on day 3, validated for upregulation of Ccr9 expression in atRA-treated groups, and submitted for 10× genomics single-cell multiome sequencing. Samples were prepared according to the Nuclei Isolation for Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression Sequencing protocol and Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC+ Gene Expression User Guide. In total, 12 libraries were prepared, with 3 replicates of each combination of genotype (Nrip1 KO and WT) and stimulation condition (with and without atRA).

Single-Cell Multiome Data analysis.

scRNA- and scATAC-seq data processing.

Raw sequencing reads were demultiplexed into FASTQ files using Cell Ranger ARC mkfastq (v2.0.1) (10× Genomics). Reads were aligned to the mouse mm10 genome. Barcoded gene unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrices were generated for gene expression data and barcoded fragment files for ATAC data using Cell Ranger ARC count (v2.0.1).

scRNA-seq data quality control.

All 12 filtered feature-barcode matrices generated by Cell Ranger were loaded as anndata (v0.8.0) (40) objects and subsequently merged into a unified dataset. We then filtered out low-quality cells and doublets with scanpy (v1.9.1) (41). We retained cells with at least 2,000 genes, at most 30,000 UMIs, and less than 20% of UMIs mapping to mitochondrial genes. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were removed. Doublets were filtered out using scanpy’s scrublet API (42) with sample ID as batch_key argument.

scATAC-seq peak calling.

We detected regions of open chromatin within each fragment file using MACS2 callpeak (v2.2.7.1) (43) with default parameters. Peaks were identified individually in each of the 12 samples, followed by a filtering process to select only those peaks present across all three replicates within identical genotype and stimulus categories (wt_w/RA, ko_w/RA, wt_w/oRA, and ko_w/oRA).

These 12 peak sets were union-merged by the above categories using bedtools mergeBed (v2.29.0) and default parameters, resulting in four category-specific peak sets. Depending on the requirements of downstream analysis, specific combinations of category-specific peak sets were union merged to preserve significant signals and minimize noise. For example, during the differential accessibility analysis looking at the effect of genotype on RA-treated cells, we union merged integrated peak sets associated with wt_w/RA and ko_w/RA categories to generate peak count matrices for corresponding samples.

To construct a peak count matrix, we supplied a fragment file and the peak sets described above to Seurat (v1.9.0) (44) and Signac (version 4.3.0) (45). In this step, Signac’s FeatureMatrix function computed the number of fragments overlapping a given peak window in the peak set for each unique cell barcode.

scATAC-seq data quality control.

We discarded low-quality cells from peak count matrices with Signac API. We computed the nucleosome signal and TSS enrichment score for each cell with NucleosomeSignal and TSSEnrichment functions. We then selected cells with between 2,000 and 60,000 peaks detected, between 4,000 and 150,000 total peak counts, nucleosome signal less than two, and TSS enrichment score greater than two. To maximize the number of quality cells in each modality, we used filtered cells from scRNA-Seq for differential expression analysis and filtered cells from scATAC-Seq for differential accessibility analysis. For multimodal gene regulatory network inference with SCENIC+, we used cells that passed quality control in both modalities to ensure that each cell had high-quality gene expression and ATAC data.

scRNA-seq data dimensionality reduction and visualization.

We used scanpy functions for normalization, dimensionality reduction, and visualization of scRNA-Seq data. Gene UMI counts were count normalized to 10,000 per cell and log transformed. We selected for highly variable genes with a z-score cutoff of 0.5. We scaled the expression of each gene to mean of zero and SD of one and performed principal component analysis. We constructed a nearest neighbor graph with n_neighbors=10 and top 40 principal components, followed by computation of 2-dimensional Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection embeddings for visualization.

scRNA-seq data differential expression analysis.

The differential expression analysis was performed using pegasus, a Python analysis package in the Cumulus framework (46). We imported gene expression data for RA-treated cells into pegasus and pseudobulked UMIs by adding all counts for each gene within the sample. We then used pegasus’s deseq2 API (47) with design=~genotype and contrast=(“genotype,” “KO,” “WT”) to find genes showing differential expression based on genotype, keeping genes with a false discovery rate less than 0.05.

scATAC-seq data differential accessibility analysis.

The differential accessibility analysis was also performed using pegasus. We loaded filtered peak count matrices for RA-treated samples into pegasus and pseudobulked counts for each peak by sample ID. We subsequently used the deseq2 function with design =~genotype and contrast = (genotype, KO, WT) to find regions differentially accessible based on genotype, keeping regions with false discovery rate less than 0.01.

Motif enrichment.

We performed TF motif enrichment analysis on the regions differentially accessible based on genotype within RA-treated samples. The regions were divided into two sets by the direction of fold change in the KO. Each set was reformatted into BED and inputted into Homer’s (v4.11) (31) findMotifsGenome.pl with size=200 flag explicitly provided.

Gene regulatory network inference.

We employed SCENIC+ (v1.0.1) (30) to derive category-specific (WT, KO, WT + atRA, KO + atRA) TF–target enhancer–target gene triplets, also known as eRegulons. Our methodology adhered to the workflow proposed in the SCENIC+ tutorials. Subsequent comparative analysis of eRegulon activity was carried out across genotype and stimuli, focusing on TFs exhibiting significant differences.

The execution of SCENIC+ workflow required a series of steps. First, we generated a consensus peak set utilizing pycisTopic. In this step, MACS2 peaks were called upon fragment files that had been pseudobulked by category. These peak sets were integrated using the get_consensus_peaks function. To identify candidate enhancer regions, we employed Latent Dirichlet Allocation topic modeling with n_topics=25 (selected based on visual assessment of four quality metrics provided by pycisTopic), followed by differential accessibility analysis on the consensus peak set. In the second phase, we quantified motif enrichment within these candidate enhancer regions with pycisTarget. The downstream analysis was then conducted with scenicplus API. We filtered for eRegulons whose TFs were 1) documented in the CIS-BP (48) mouse database and 2) expressed in high correlation (absolute value greater than 0.5) with target region enrichment score calculated by AUCell. Following this criterion led to the identification of 39 triplets.

RNA-seq.

Naïve CD4+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of Nrip1 WT and KO littermates using the EasySep™ Mouse Naïve CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies). Purified cells were stimulated for 3 d in full RPMI 1640 medium on plates coated with 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD3e and 2 μg/mL anti-mouse CD28 antibodies. Where indicated, 100 nM atRA was added to the medium. After 3 d of stimulation, cells were harvested, and CCR9 expression was confirmed by flow cytometry to validate atRA treatment efficacy. Subsequently, cells were processed for bulk RNA-seq and CUT&RUN assays.

Library preparation for bulk RNA-seq.

The bulk RNA-seq library preparation was performed according to our modified SmartSeq2 protocol. Briefly, the purified and treated/untreated cells described above were lysed in lysis/binding buffer (A33562, Invitrogen); the lysate concentration was approximately 1 × 105 to 106 cells in 50 to 100μL lysis buffer. Then the mRNA was isolated with the Dynabeads mRNA Direct Purification kit (Life Technologies, Cat#61006). RNA was reverse-transcripted with Maxima RT RNaseH-minus (Life Technologies, Cat# EP0753), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was amplified with KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready Mix (KAPA Biosystems, Cat#KK2602) according to the kit instruction, separately. After post–clean up with 0.8x AMPure XP SPRI beads (Beckman Coulter, Cat#A63881), the DNA was tagmented with Nextera XT DNA sample PreKit (Illumina, Cat# FC_131-1096) and Nextera XT Index Kit v2 SetA (Illumina, Cat# FC-131-2001). SPRI bead clean and gel purification were done to extract and recover the library fragments about 250 to 800 bps with the Zymo Gel DNA recovery kit (Zymo Research, Cat#D4008). The library was sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 system.

Bulk RNA-seq data processing.

We employed a publicly available bulk RNA-seq data processing pipeline (kco/bulk_rna_seq_pipeline/13) on Terra (https://app.terra.bio/) to generate a counts matrix from raw sequencing reads. First, the raw reads were demultiplexed into FASTQ files using Illumina’s BCL Convert (v3.10.5). Next, pseudoalignment to the mouse mm10 genome was performed with Kallisto, followed by aggregation of reads by replicate to create a single read counts matrix.

Bulk RNA-seq data differential expression gene analysis.

The bulk RNA-seq pipeline conducted a differential expression analysis on the counts matrix using EdgeR. After applying the gene expression threshold of 1 cpm, differentially expressed genes were identified for all possible pairwise comparisons between categories. The genes were considered significant at a false discovery rate of 0.05.

CUT&RUN.

T cells were prepared and stimulated as described above for bulk RNA-seq. The CUT&RUN assay was performed as described in Skene and Henikoff (49). Briefly, 0.5 to 1 million cells were captured with Concanavalin-coated magnetic beads (Bangs Laboratories, Cat#BP531) in binding buffer [20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM KCl, 1 mM CaCl2, and 1 mM MnCl2], rotating at room temperature about 10 min, then permeabilized and incubated with the following target-specific primary antibodies [diluted with digitonin containing buffer (Millipore, Cat#300410)], rotating for 10 to 20 min at room temperature: anti-RIP140 (Abcam, Cat# AB42126), anti-IgG (Abcam, Cat# AB171870), anti-H3K27me3 (Cell Signaling Tech, Cat# 9733s), and anti-RARα (Invitrogen, PA1-810A). Cells were washed 2 to 3 times with dig-wash buffer (20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM Spermidine, 1× Complete Protease Inhibitor EDTA-Free, and 0.05% Digitonin), and pA/G-MNase (Epicypher, cat# SKU: 15-1116) was added and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were washed again and prechilled to 0 °C in an ice-water bath. CaCl2 was added to a final concentration of 2 mM to activate pA/G-MNase for 30 min in the ice-water (exactly 0 °C). The reaction was stopped by addition of 2× STOP buffer (340 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 4 mM EGTA, 0.02% Digitonin, 5 μL/mL RNase A, 50 μg/mL glycogen and 2 pg/mL heterologous spike-in DNA). The DNA was released with Protease K digest, and DNA was extracted by the PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel NucleoSpin®, cat. no.740609.250) (for histone modification marker) or Phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol precipitation, followed by quantification and qualification with the Qubit fluorometer and Agilent 4200 TapeStation. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, barcoded libraries for Illumina sequencing with Tru-Seq adapters were prepared and followed paired-end Illumina sequencing.

CUT&RUN data processing.

Raw sequencing reads from the CUT&RUN experiment were demultiplexed into FASTQ files using Illumina’s BCL Convert (v3.10.5). We then employed a publicly available CUT&RUN data processing pipeline (https://nf-co.re/cutandrun/3.2.2/) to generate peak sets from FASTQ files. The pipeline aligned the reads to the mouse mm10 genome using Bowtie2. Peaks were then called for each replicate with Sparse Enrichment Analysis for CUT&RUN (SEACR) (50), using parameters “use_control=false” and “seacr_peak_threshold=1.00” to retain all identified peaks.

CUT&RUN data consensus peak calling.

For each category, we pooled the SEACR derived peaks from each replicate and selected for the top 1% of peaks by signal. These peaks were merged into a single peak set using bedtools mergeBed (v2.29.0). We then filtered the merged peak using bedtools intersect (v2.29.0) to include only those present in at least two of the three replicates.

Testing the association between bulk RNA-seq differentially expressed genes and CUT&RUN peaks.

We obtained the NCBI RefSeq Genes composite track for the mouse mm10 genome from the UCSC genome browser and identified the coordinates for 366 of 376 differentially expressed genes upregulated in KO versus WT under atRA treatment. To generate a null distribution, we randomly sampled 366 genes from the reference genome 1,000 times and counted the number of CUT&RUN RARα peaks within 50 kb of these genes using bedtools closest (v2.29.0) with options “-d” and “-t all.” We then compared this null distribution to the observed number of RARα peaks within 50 kb to the 366 upregulated genes (899 peaks).

Real-Time qPCR.

RNA from tissue or cells was extracted by using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) and then reverse-transcribed with iScript cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Relative mRNA levels were detected using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) and calculated using the ΔΔCt method with Actb as the internal control.
