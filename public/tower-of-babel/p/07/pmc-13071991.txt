Emergency medicine physicians treating urine cultures when it is not clear they should
Authors: Amanda Doodlesack, Clifford Marks, Nicolette Castaneda, Jonathan Edlow, Shamai A Grossman
Published: 2026

Emergency physicians make treatment decisions about positive urine cultures that result after patients are discharged from the emergency department (ED). The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) incorporates both symptoms and laboratory findings into the diagnosis of a urinary tract infection (UTI). Typical symptoms include dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain or tenderness, and flank pain, while confirmation of a UTI requires a positive urine culture with ≥100,000 colony-forming units (CFU) per milliliter of a single organism [1]. Although many cases involve patients who presented with clear symptoms of a UTI, a substantial proportion involve atypical presentations (e.g., confusion or a fall in an older patient) or patients without symptoms plausibly attributable to a UTI. Such positive culture results are considered asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) [1].

The IDSA recommends against antibiotic treatment for nearly all cases of asymptomatic bacteriuria other than in pregnant patients. Despite these recommendations, prescribers treat an average of 45% of ASB cases with antibiotics, according to a recent meta-analysis [2]. However, this and a more recent study addressing ASB in the emergency setting use conservative definitions, excluding cases with vague symptoms unless those symptoms are clearly attributable to another cause [2,3]. These scenarios, which emergency physicians frequently face, have been understudied. Our earlier paper examining the binary decision to treat with antibiotics demonstrated frequent deviation from IDSA recommendations both in these scenarios and in cases that fit a conservative ASB definition [4]. As a follow-up, this study aims to clarify how emergency physicians manage ASB by examining antibiotic selection and treatment duration following a positive culture result in ED patients who are asymptomatic or present with symptoms atypical for UTI.

We surveyed emergency medicine physicians based at an academic tertiary care medical center and its affiliated hospital network, which includes six community hospitals. The study used a cross-sectional design. An electronic questionnaire was emailed to all emergency physicians across the network and addressed nine common scenarios encountered when following up a positive urine culture, including several involving asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic bacteriuria. These scenarios were selected based on existing IDSA guidelines and to reflect the range of emergency practice [1,5–10]. ASB was defined as one or more bacterial species growing in the urine at ≥100,000 CFU/mL in the absence of signs or symptoms of a UTI [1]. Responses were collected over a 3-month period.

The survey was distributed to 305 emergency physicians, with 120 responses received, yielding a response rate of 39.3%. Of these respondents, 49 (40.8%) worked in academic medical centers with affiliated emergency medicine residency programs, 45 (37.5%) in large community hospitals (greater than 40,000 visits per year), and 26 (21.7%) in small community hospitals (less than 40,000 visits per year). Survey participants included 14 residents (11.7%), 33 attendings (27.5%) with less than 5 years out of training, 23 attendings (19.2%) with 5–9 years out, 37 attendings (30.8%) with 10–20 years out, and 13 attendings (10.8%) with more than 20 years out (Table 1).

A 10-day course was selected more often in all scenarios involving a male patient, including the 32-year-old asymptomatic man (scenario 3), the 76-year-old man with confusion after a fall (scenario 6), the 62-year-old asymptomatic man with an indwelling Foley catheter (scenario 7), and the 52-year-old man post–kidney transplant (scenario 9).

The emergency physicians surveyed demonstrated considerable variability in both the type and duration of antibiotics prescribed. Across all scenarios, most physicians selected either a 5- or 7-day treatment course, and the most common antibiotic choices were cefpodoxime and cephalexin. In the single scenario with clear IDSA recommendations for treatment, ASB in a pregnant woman, physicians largely followed established guidelines; 89.9% (107 of 119) selected a 5- or 7-day antibiotic course, which corresponds to the IDSA recommendation of 4 to 7 days of therapy [1]. In this scenario, all physicians selected either nitrofurantoin or a β-lactam antibiotic (cefpodoxime or cephalexin), again matching IDSA guidance [1].

Variability in practice patterns appeared most pronounced in scenarios involving vague symptoms that could plausibly be related to a UTI. For example, for the 76-year-old man with confusion and a fall (scenario 6), respondents selected all five antibiotic options, and treatment durations ranged between 5 and 10 days, indicating no consensus. Similarly, multiple respondents treating ASB in a kidney transplant patient selected various antibiotics and durations, reflecting the limited evidence available to guide decisions in these cases [11]. The frequent selection of cephalexin was particularly noteworthy because it generally requires dosing four times daily, which can make adherence more difficult.

For ASB scenarios without symptoms suggestive of UTI, antibiotic selections often diverged from current IDSA guidelines for cystitis treatment. The latest IDSA recommendations for women with cystitis advise a 5-day course of nitrofurantoin or a 3-day course of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole as first-line therapy, reserving β-lactams for situations in which first-line agents cannot be used [12]. Although physicians predominantly chose nitrofurantoin when treating a young woman with bacteriuria and no symptoms, they tended to favor β-lactams when the patient had diabetes or was older.

Physicians generally prescribed longer antibiotic courses for male patients, consistent with the traditional view that male UTIs are “complicated” because of anatomic considerations. However, recent studies challenge this assumption and suggest that 7-day courses may be adequate for men [13–15].

Ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole were rarely selected across all clinical scenarios. This pattern may reflect concerns about adverse events associated with fluoroquinolones and high community resistance rates to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [16].

The substantial variability observed in this follow-up study highlights the challenges emergency physicians face when balancing UTI management with antibiotic stewardship. Because patients are often lost to follow-up, physicians may favor broader-spectrum or longer-duration therapy even when evidence suggests such practices are unnecessary. Although patients with classic UTI symptoms and positive cultures require treatment to prevent complications such as pyelonephritis or urosepsis, overtreatment of ASB persists as a significant problem [6]. Inappropriate antibiotic use contributes to resistance at both individual and community levels [1,7]. Emergency physicians must therefore weigh risks and benefits carefully, tailoring choices about antibiotic type and duration to each clinical situation.

Overtreatment partly arises from a culture of excessive urinalysis and culture ordering in ED settings, driven by concerns about missing occult infection. One study of an academic emergency department found that urinalyses were ordered for one quarter of patients presenting with chest pain or headache [17]. Efforts to reduce mistreatment include requiring a second order for a culture after an initial urinalysis, canceling low-risk culture orders, and limiting reflex culture practices [18–21]. Some departments have also incorporated pharmacist review of cultures to guide antibiotic choice and duration and to decrease unnecessary treatment. As a result, studies have shown improved adherence to IDSA guidelines and, in one case, successful discontinuation of antibiotics for ASB [22–24]. However, the extent to which such initiatives are widely implemented is unclear, and inappropriate treatment of ASB, as demonstrated in our studies, likely remains common.

Limitations of this study include its relatively small sample size from a single network of emergency physicians, which may reduce generalizability. Nonetheless, the sample encompassed a wide range of physicians, from residents to those with more than 20 years of experience, as well as individuals practicing in small community, large community, and academic hospitals, and physicians currently practicing in other healthcare settings. Although the survey response rate was modest, the diversity of the network likely provides a reasonable cross-section of emergency physicians. Response bias is also possible because participation was voluntary, and self-reported practices may not perfectly reflect actual behavior.

In conclusion, this follow-up study demonstrates substantial variation in emergency physicians’ choices regarding antibiotic selection and duration. Most physicians treat positive urine cultures for 5 or 7 days and most frequently prescribe cephalexin. While timely treatment is necessary to prevent progression and complications, unnecessary antibiotic use can cause harm both to individual patients and to the broader community. Further research is needed to better inform treatment decisions for bacteriuria in patients with vague or atypical symptoms and to clarify the factors influencing management choices.
