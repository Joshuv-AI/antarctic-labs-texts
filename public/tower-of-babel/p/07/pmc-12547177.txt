Comparison of survey questions to define long COVID: Implications for prevalence and disparities
Authors: Jana L Hirschtick, Elizabeth Slocum, Blair Whittington, Michael R Elliott, Sameera Ahmed, Nancy L Fleischer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo understand whether variation in survey-based Long COVID estimates is partially due to how and when survey questions are asked.MethodsWe compared Long COVID prevalence using distinct questions within a population-based, longitudinal survey of adults with confirmed SARS-CoV-2 before June 2022 in Michigan.ResultsIn our sample (n = 3826), 17.0 % reported symptoms for 90+ days at baseline, a median of 4.4 months after COVID-19 onset. A median of 18.4 months after COVID-19 onset, 24.5 % reported ever experiencing Long COVID, 16.9 % reported current Long COVID, and 10.8 % reported diagnosed Long COVID. Among adults without 90-day symptoms at baseline, 17.3 % reported ever Long COVID at follow-up. Relatedly, among adults with 90-day symptoms at baseline, 31.1 % reported they never had Long COVID at follow-up. After adjustment for reinfection, respondents who were Hispanic (vs. White) or lower income (<$75,000 vs. $75,000+) had greater odds of reporting baseline symptoms but never experiencing Long COVID at follow-up. Conversely, Black (vs. White) respondents had greater odds of reporting ever Long COVID at follow-up without baseline symptoms.ConclusionSurveys should employ several questions to define Long COVID and interpret findings within the context of factors likely contributing to discrepancies, including reinfection, stigma, awareness, and care-seeking behaviors.Keywords: Post COVID condition, Post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2, Survey methodology, Population-based, Health disparities

Introduction

Some individuals experience delayed recovery after SARS-CoV-2 infection, a condition known as Long COVID. The National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine define Long COVID as “an infection-associated chronic condition that occurs after SARS-CoV-2 infection and is present for at least three months as a continuous, relapsing and remitting, or progressive disease state that affects one or more organ systems.” (Ely et al., 2024) Long COVID is characterized by diffuse, non-specific symptoms, including fatigue and brain fog, that may have delayed onset or fluctuate over time (Davis et al., 2021). Additionally, there is currently no diagnostic test for Long COVID, rendering it a diagnosis of exclusion. These factors complicate the identification of Long COVID in both research and clinical settings.

Given the challenges in accurately identifying Long COVID, prevalence estimates vary widely, even across nationally representative samples (Ford et al., 2024; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2023; Vahratian et al., 2024). Although this is likely due to differences in survey mode and design, it may also be attributable to respondent interpretation of the various survey questions used to capture Long COVID. Additionally, given the fluctuating nature of Long COVID symptoms, survey timing relative to acute COVID-19 illness also likely impacts point prevalence. However, due to the limited number of existing Long COVID survey questions and the cross-sectional nature of many surveys, it is often not possible to examine potential differences in response across questions and over time.

Accurate Long COVID prevalence estimates are needed for planning and resource allocation, particularly for groups disproportionately impacted by the pandemic. Given the subjective criteria used to define Long COVID, is it important to understand whether and how prevalence varies based on survey question and survey timing. In this study, we used longitudinal data to examine variations in Long COVID prevalence overall and by sociodemographic factors using survey questions with different wording and timing within the same sample of respondents.

Methods

Sample

We used data from the Michigan COVID-19 Recovery Surveillance Study, a population-based cohort of adults with polymerase-chain-reaction (PCR) confirmed SARS-CoV-2 in the Michigan Disease Surveillance System between March 1, 2020, and May 31, 2022. Participants completed a baseline survey a median of 4.4 months (IQR 3.4, 5.7) after COVID-19 onset and a follow-up survey a median of 18.4 months (IQR 15.0, 21.3) after COVID-19 onset with questions on COVID-19 illness and recovery. Baseline survey data was collected in sequential waves between 2020 and 2022, with follow-up data collected between 2022 and 2023.

Survey questions

We used several questions from the baseline and follow-up surveys to define Long COVID. The first definition (“90-day symptoms”) characterized Long COVID as a recovery period of at least 90 days after COVID-19 onset based on the following baseline questions: 1) “Have you recovered from COVID-19 to your usual state of health?”; 2) “How long did it take you to recover?” The remaining definitions were based on questions asked at follow-up, which were preceded by the following text: “The next set of questions ask you about potential long-term symptoms you experienced during your COVID-19 illness and your experiences seeking care for those symptoms. Persistent symptoms of COVID-19 are commonly referred to as Long COVID or Chronic COVID. For this section, we will refer to this condition as Long COVID.” The second definition (“ever Long COVID”) was based on a ‘yes’ response to the question “At any point since your diagnosis with COVID-19, have you experienced Long COVID?” The third definition (“current Long COVID”) was based on a ‘no’ response to the question “Have you recovered from Long COVID to your usual state of health?” for respondents who reported “ever Long COVID” at follow-up. Finally, the fourth definition (“diagnosed Long COVID”) was based on a ‘yes’ response to the question “Has your doctor or other health professional told you that you have Long COVID, or that you were experiencing long-term symptoms of COVID-19?” for respondents who reported “ever Long COVID” at follow-up.

Analysis

Accounting for the complex survey design, we calculated weighted prevalence estimates for each Long COVID definition by race and ethnicity (Hispanic, White, Black, American Indian/Alaska Native [AI/AN], Asian, Arab/Middle Eastern North African, another race or ethnicity), biological sex (female, male), and annual household income (low income, <$35,000; mid income, $35,000 - $74,999; high income, ≥ $75,000). Of the baseline respondents (N = 5521), 4100 completed the follow-up survey for a follow-up response rate of 80.5 %. To make comparisons across Long COVID definitions using the same respondents, we restricted the analytic sample to 4100 respondents who completed the follow-up survey and further removed 274 respondents (6.7 %) with missing information for any Long COVID definition. We handled minimal missingness of additional variables of interest using listwise deletion, resulting in a range of sample sizes from 3689 to 3826 across analyses.

In general, given the large number of resulting estimates and potential comparisons, we used non-overlapping 95 % confidence intervals as a conservative measure of statistical difference between the four Long COVID definitions. For specific prevalence comparisons across demographic groups, we used the Rao Scott chi-square test (Rao and Scott, 1981) to accommodate the complex sample design. For comparisons across Long COVID definitions within a single demographic group, we used the McNemar's paired test.

To explore differences between baseline report of persistent symptoms (“90-day symptoms”) and follow-up report of “ever Long COVID,” and the potential role of reinfection in discrepancies between these definitions, we used multinomial logistic regression to examine demographic differences in the following outcomes, adjusting for self-reported SARS-CoV-2 reinfection (yes/no) since the baseline infection: 1) Reported 90-day symptoms at baseline, but never having Long COVID at follow-up; 2) Reported ever having Long COVID at follow-up, but no 90-day symptoms at baseline; 3) Reported both 90-day symptoms at baseline and ever Long COVID at follow-up; 4) Reported neither 90-day symptoms at baseline nor ever Long COVID at follow-up. All procedures for this study were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines. This secondary analysis of de-identified survey data was deemed ‘not human subjects research’ by the Institutional Review Board at Advocate Health on October 19, 2023. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 15.1.

Results

The analytic sample was predominantly White (73.0 %), female (55.5 %), and high income (42.3 %; Table 1), with notable variation in prevalence across the four Long COVID definitions. At baseline, 17.0 % of respondents reported symptoms lasting at least 90 days after COVID-19 onset (“90-day symptoms”). At follow-up, nearly one quarter (24.5 %) reported that they ever had Long COVID (“ever Long COVID”), 16.9 % that they were currently experiencing Long COVID (“current Long COVID”), and 10.8 % that they had been diagnosed with Long COVID by a healthcare professional (“diagnosed Long COVID”).

Variations in prevalence across the Long COVID definitions had implications for racial and ethnic disparities (Fig. 1). For example, although AI/AN respondents had the highest prevalence of Long COVID as measured by “90-day symptoms” at baseline, Black respondents had the highest prevalence as measured by the “ever Long COVID” definition at follow-up. This is partly due to a significantly higher prevalence of Black respondents reporting “ever Long COVID” at follow-up (33.6 %) than “90 days symptoms” at baseline (22.9 %, p < 0.001). Additionally, Long COVID estimates were higher for Asian respondents based on the “ever Long COVID” definition at follow-up (27.1 %) compared to the “90-day symptoms” definition at baseline (11.6 %, p = 0.02). There were also differences in Long COVID prevalence estimates across definitions for the racial and ethnic groups often collapsed into a “non-Hispanic other” category in other studies due to sample size limitations, including AI/AN, Asian, Arab/Middle Eastern North African, or any race or ethnic group other than Hispanic, Black, or White. The prevalence of Long COVID based on the “90-day symptoms” definition was significantly higher for AI/AN respondents (24.5 %) than Asian respondents (11.6 %, p = 0.035). However, prevalence estimates were not statistically different when using the “ever Long COVID” (AI/AN: 30.2 % vs. Asian: 27.1 %, p = 0.673) and “diagnosed Long COVID” definitions (AI/AN: 14.0 % vs. Asian: 11.0 %, p = 0.567).

Additionally, although females consistently had a higher prevalence than males across all Long COVID definitions (Table 1), a more nuanced picture of sex differences emerged after additionally stratifying by race and ethnicity (Fig. 2). For example, when using “90-day symptoms” at baseline, the Long COVID prevalence for Black females (23.9 %) and Black males (21.4 %) was not statistically different (p = 0.607). However, when using self-report of “current Long COVID” at follow-up, Black females had a statistically higher prevalence (33.4 %) than Black males (14.7 %, p < 0.001).

Direct comparison of reporting discrepancies between “90-day symptoms” at baseline and “ever Long COVID” at follow-up revealed further insights. Among respondents reporting 90-day symptoms at baseline, 31.1 % subsequently reported never experiencing Long COVID at follow-up. Relatedly, among respondents reporting recovery within 90 days at baseline, 17.3 % subsequently reported ever experiencing Long COVID at follow-up.

Reporting patterns across these two definitions varied by sociodemographic characteristics and were not completely explained by reinfection (Table 2), although reinfection itself was associated with greater odds of reporting “ever Long COVID only” compared to respondents who reported “neither 90-day symptoms nor ever Long COVID”. Additionally, respondents who were Hispanic (vs. White) and low or mid income (vs. high income) had greater odds of reporting “90-day symptoms only” in adjusted models. Finally, compared to White respondents, Black respondents had greater odds of reporting “ever Long COVID only” and “90-day symptoms and ever Long COVID”, compared to respondents who reported “neither 90-day symptoms nor ever Long COVID.”

Discussion

Using data from a population-based sample of adults with PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 in Michigan, we found that Long COVID prevalence, and associated disparities, varied substantially when using different survey questions and survey timing relative to acute COVID-19 illness to define Long COVID. Overall, a higher proportion of respondents reported “ever Long COVID” at follow-up (24.5 %), one to two years after COVID-19 onset, than “90-day symptoms” at baseline (17.0 %). This discrepancy may reflect several factors, including the delayed onset and fluctuating nature of some Long COVID symptoms (Davis et al., 2021) or new onset of Long COVID due to reinfection (Bowe et al., 2022). Indeed, we found that reinfection since the baseline infection was associated with increased odds of reporting “ever Long COVID” at follow-up among adults who reported recovery within 90 days at baseline. Additionally, COVID-19 related stigma may be responsible. For example, the persistent stigma Asian individuals faced during the pandemic (Villa et al., 2020) may have led some Asian respondents to under-report ongoing symptoms due to COVID-19 (i.e., 90-day symptoms; 11.6 %) but accurately report that they experienced Long COVID in the past (i.e., ever Long COVID; 27.1 %).

Furthermore, Long COVID awareness may impact prevalence estimates. Given the diffuse and non-specific nature of Long COVID symptoms, some respondents may have attributed unrelated symptoms that were not present at baseline to Long COVID (Matta et al., 2022; Scherlinger et al., 2021). Conversely, 31.1 % of respondents who met the criteria for Long COVID at baseline reported that they had never had Long COVID at follow-up, suggesting that these individuals did not recognize their prolonged recovery as Long COVID. Recent national data demonstrate disparities in Long COVID awareness, reflecting lower awareness among individuals who were low (vs. high) income or Hispanic or Black (vs. White) (National Center for Health Statistics, 2024). This differential awareness could contribute to our finding that low income and Hispanic respondents were more likely to report 90-day symptoms as baseline, but that they never had Long COVID at follow-up. Low awareness among patients, and lack of Long COVID preparedness among providers (Kaufmann et al., 2025), may also partially explain the lower prevalence of diagnosed vs. self-reported Long COVID, in addition to barriers to healthcare (Au et al., 2022; Gardner et al., 2024).

Additionally, although females consistently had a higher prevalence of Long COVID than males across all four definitions, varying sex differences by race and ethnicity suggest that sex disparities in Long COVID may not solely be due to physiologic differences between males and females. For example, although “90-day symptom” prevalence was not statistically different for Black men and women, Black women had a higher prevalence of Long COVID than Black men using all other study definitions. While this may reflect sex differences in the body's response to COVID-19 (Hamlin et al., 2024), including variation in symptom duration (Shah et al., 2024), it likely also reflects social factors such as women being more likely to disclose symptoms (Barsky et al., 2001; Kroenke and Spitzer, 1998) and seek healthcare (National Center for Health Statistics. Health, United States, Annual Perspective, 2020–2021, 2022).

This study has several limitations. Given the use of PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 cases in Michigan, this study is likely not representative of individuals who did not obtain a PCR test in Michigan during the study period. Nevertheless, the study sample was diverse with respect to race, ethnicity, and income, facilitating the examination of disparities across various Long COVID definitions. Additionally, we did not have a control group without PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection with which to compare various definitions of Long COVID, including self-report of persistent symptoms. However, our findings illustrate variation in Long COVID prevalence estimates for individuals with known SARS-CoV-2 infection, a prerequisite for Long COVID.

In summary, we found that prevalence varied when using different survey questions and survey timing relative to acute COVID-19 illness to define Long COVID. Given the novel nature of this condition, future surveys should employ several distinct questions to define Long COVID and interpret findings within the context of interrelated factors that are likely contributing to prevalence estimates, including reinfection, stigma, awareness, and care-seeking behaviors.
