In-silico assessment of dynamic symbiotic microbial interactions in a reduced microbiota related to the autism spectrum disorder symptoms
Authors: Juan Manuel Olaguez-Gonzalez, Isaac Chairez, Luz Breton-Deval, Mariel Alfaro-Ponce
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in human health, with growing evidence linking its composition to the development of Autism Spectrum Disorder. However, inconsistencies in previous studies have hindered the identification of a definitive microbial signature associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder. Machine learning models have emerged as powerful tools for analyzing microbiome data, yet their interpretability remains limited. In this study, we integrate in silico simulations with machine learning predictions to explore microbial interactions under different dietary conditions and provide biological context to features of the intestinal microbiota that are linked to Autism Spectrum Disorder. This study employs constraint-based modeling to simulate metabolic exchanges among key bacterial taxa in order to assess their ecological relationships. Findings reveal that high-fiber diets foster mutualistic and balanced interactions, whereas Western-style diets promote competitive and parasitic dynamics, potentially contributing to gut dysbiosis in Autism Spectrum Disorder. In addition, the presence of oxygen (a factor associated with colonocyte permeability, a pathological condition of the colon) significantly alters microbial interactions, influencing metabolic dependencies and the overall structure of the community. This integrative approach enhances the interpretability of machine learning-based Autism Spectrum Disorder classifiers, bridging computational predictions with mechanistic insights. By identifying diet-dependent microbial interactions, our study highlights potential dietary interventions to modulate the composition of the gut microbiota in Autism Spectrum Disorder. These findings underscore the value of combining in silico modeling and machine learning for unraveling complex microbiome-host relationships and improving Autism Spectrum Disorder biomarker identification.

Introduction

The human gut microbiota composition (GMC) plays a crucial role in overall health. Disruptions in its balance, known as dysbiosis, have been linked to various diseases such as obesity, Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, type 2 diabetes, and Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) [1]. Although numerous studies worldwide have explored associations between GMC and ASD, the findings remain inconsistent, especially with respect to certain bacterial groups or relationships such as the Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratio and the role of specific genera [2], partly because of the difficulty in culturing and experimentally tracing complex metabolic exchanges between microbes [3] and their effects on the host organism.

Given the complexity of microbial communities and the diverse interactions in which microbes engage within the gut, ranging from mutualistic to competitive [4] or even parasitic [5], traditional statistical analyses [6], and even differences in the computational pipeline [7], often fall short of capturing the full scope of these relationships. More recent studies are turning to modern computational tools such as machine learning (ML) strategies [8] and graph neural networks [9] to improve the analysis and interpretability of gut dynamics and microbial interactions. These tools allow researchers to more deeply investigate the dynamics of microbiota, potentially clarifying conflicting findings in studies related to ASD and uncovering relationships that may be missed by classical methods alone. In particular, computational models, as described later, allow hypothesis generation and testing, helping researchers understand how the interactions and functions of specific microbes might influence symptoms and biomarkers of unhealthy conditions like ASD.

In a previous study [10], we identified that certain bacterial species and genera present in lower proportions within the gut might serve as potential markers of ASD in ML models, even when they do not show statistically significant differences (p<0.05) between ASD and neurotypical (NT) samples. These findings suggest new avenues for research, particularly on less-studied genera and species whose roles in microbial communities are not yet well understood. However, to fully understand the relationship between GMC and ASD, we must go beyond the individual bacterial presence and study the complex interactions between microbial groups, as GMC plays a critical role in host metabolism. Despite advances in computational tools, the complexity of the GMC remains challenging for direct experimental studies or complete simulation of all microbiota components. To address this limitation, researchers frequently use synthetic communities that define simplified models containing only a subset of GMC species to investigate interactions between selected microbes [11]. This approach enables a focused study of microbial relationships, building a better understanding of gut dynamics.

In silico experiments, such as the 2015 computational model by Heberling and Dhurjati [12], identified key enzymes and metabolites potentially related to ASD by mining bioinformatic data. Biological events and enzyme kinetics are incorporated into metabolic models and mathematical representations of the genome [13]. However, as with other studies [14], they focused on bacteria found in higher proportions, overlooking those present in lower amounts, whose roles could also be significant. These less abundant bacteria could offer insight into previously unrecognized GMC dysbiosis [15].

Based on the facts described and considering that the vast majority of intestinal microbes cannot be grown in vitro [16], this study introduces an in silico model that uses datasets and predictors from previous research. The model aims to provide more explainability to the classifiers developed previously and to investigate the interactions among predictor bacteria, examining how the presence of one genus may influence others under different dietary conditions and using Genome-scale metabolic models (GENREs) that provide a powerful method to explore microbial interactions and metabolism [17], generating hypotheses that can be experimentally validated to reveal previously unknown aspects of the microbiome's impact on health. Using this approach, we hypothesize that it is possible to improve the interpretability of ML models and explore some underlying mechanisms by which the GMC may influence the development or symptoms of ASD. Specifically, the bacterial genera selected for GENRE modeling in this study were those previously identified as predictive features by ML classifiers [10], thereby ensuring direct integration of computational predictions with metabolic simulations.

In autism research, computational approaches based on ML are indispensable for unraveling the role of intestinal microbiomes. The complexity of microbial interactions and the need to identify ASD-linked metabolic pathways make computational models crucial. Given the need for robust methods to analyze microbial interactions, the COBRA methodology (Constraint-Based Reconstruction and Analysis) [18] provides a valuable framework for GENRE refinement, complementing our approach by ensuring precision in the modeling of metabolic pathways for simplified microbiome compositions. Although they can be automatically generated, manual refinements are often necessary to address stoichiometric consistency, reaction directionality, gene annotations, and the accuracy of the biological functions of each individual strain and those associated with the broader microbiota [19].

Finally, pairwise growth simulations were conducted to evaluate how each bacterial species grew individually versus in co-culture, thereby providing deeper insights into the nature of their interactions. The initial relative bacterial populations used in the simulation were derived from the study by Ding et al. [22], since the ML-based predictors were originally developed from that dataset.

For the biological aspects configured to perform the simulations, the coupling factor (c) between different bacterial strains was set to 200, 400, and 600 to analyze whether significant differences arise from blocked reactions within the metabolic networks. These are reactions that cannot achieve a non-zero flux under the specific simulation conditions chosen in this study [23].

The growth threshold was defined by identifying the interaction between two paired species in the microbiota under analysis; each species' growth is optimized while suppressing the other and vice versa. Subsequently, a third optimization maximizes both growth rates simultaneously and evaluates whether the difference between the single and combined growths exceeds the established threshold. In this study, the threshold was fixed at 0.12 (12% difference). Interactions are classified based on the difference in growth rates between single-growing and co-growing conditions.

The types of interaction considered in the numerical assessment are as follows.•Competition: Both organisms experience slower growth in co-culture compared to when grown separately.•Parasitism: One organism grows faster in co-culture while the other grows more slowly. The faster-growing organism is the Parasite (Taker), and the slower-growing one is the Host (Giver).•Amensalism: One organism's growth remains unchanged in co-culture, while the other grows more slowly. PCA plots and Sankey diagrams were generated to provide clearer and more intuitive interpretations of the simulation results. The interaction clusters identified in the PCA plots reduce the dimensionality of the microbiome dynamics data, thereby simplifying the categorization of bacterial genera according to their behavioral patterns under different dietary conditions and offering insights into their ecological roles. Meanwhile, the Sankey diagrams provide a complementary perspective by visually mapping the strength, directionality, and type of interactions, along with the associated bacterial species. Together, these approaches enhance the understanding of microbial ecosystem dynamics, enabling a more intuitive interpretation of how dietary factors shape both the structure and function of the microbiota.

In this study, Sankey diagrams are specifically used to illustrate the complex relationships among key microbial components within the reduced microbiota. These diagrams enable direct comparison across experimental conditions, highlighting the effects of oxygen availability and dietary variations on microbial interactions. By mapping metabolic exchanges and interaction patterns among microbial strains, Sankey diagrams deliver an intuitive and structured representation of community shifts under diverse environmental factors, reinforcing the critical role of diet and oxygen in shaping microbiota composition and functionality.

Results

This section presents the simulation findings, focusing on the microbial strains involved in the selected microbiome interactions. It emphasizes bacterial growth variations under different dietary conditions and in the presence of oxygen, a factor commonly associated with colon-related diseases [44], [45], [46], [47].

The simulation results, shown in Fig. 2, depict the individual growth of the 14 genera analyzed in this study. The WDO exhibits the highest growth percentages among all diet configurations, suggesting that oxygen provides favorable conditions for aerobic bacterial proliferation, leading to imbalances in microbiota composition—an effect frequently observed in unhealthy colon conditions. Similarly, the HFO demonstrates that oxygen alters bacterial growth dynamics even under fiber-rich conditions. Several factors within the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, such as intra-luminal oxygen levels [48], colonocyte metabolism [49], inflammation [50], and the luminal mucus layer [51], directly influence microbiome composition and functionality. These previous studies reinforce oxygen's central role in shaping the gut microbiota during potential dysbiosis, justifying its inclusion as an environmental constraint in our simulations. In addition, oxygen-free diets produce lower growth percentages overall. Specifically, the WDNO shows the lowest growth rates, whereas the HFNO partially compensates for the absence of oxygen, resulting in intermediate growth compared to oxygen-rich scenarios. These findings are consistent with experimental evidence reported in studies on colonic diseases, which link microbial strain distribution in the human microbiome to disease progression [25], [47].Fig. 2Percentage of individual growth for the 14 genera under each dietary condition. Regarding individual genera, Bacteroides displayed variable growth across all dietary conditions, with peak proliferation observed under WDO and HFO, indicating its adaptability to diverse environments. Additionally, Parabacteroides exhibited strong growth in the same oxygen-rich conditions, further highlighting the influence of oxygen on this genus. In contrast, Bifidobacterium and Eubacterium recorded the lowest growth rates, suggesting higher sensitivity to environmental changes or dependence on nutrients not available in the tested diets. Interestingly, Roseburia was the only genus that maintained relatively stable growth across all dietary scenarios.

This decline reflects weaker positive interactions, consistent with the presence of oxygen that favors the growth of aerobic microorganisms. Additionally, a discrepancy arises between parasitism givers and takers: the number of takers is lower than that of givers, implying the release of excess secondary metabolites into the colon. The accumulation of these metabolites in the lumen, and their potential transport into the cardiovascular system, has been associated with several diseases, including conditions linked to ASD [27], [28].

The corresponding Sankey diagram for the Western diet with oxygen is presented in Fig. 9. This case demonstrates a clear reduction in the number of mutualistic interactions, driven by the increased intake of carbohydrates and lipids. However, the number of parasitic interactions between takers and givers is unbalanced, with takers' reactions being greater. This indicates that carbohydrates and lipids promote disproportionate growth of strains that consume these substrates [29].

The corresponding Sankey diagram for the most complex case (Western diet combined with oxygen) is provided in Fig. 10. The interaction results show a deficit in mutualistic behavior, leading to the smallest number of collaborative reactions among the cases considered in our study. This condition is accompanied by an increase in commensalism takers and givers that were not as prominent in the previous cases. This seems to emphasize a class of weak parasitic relationships between these microorganisms. This trend is further supported by the increase in parasitic reactions for both takers and givers. Collectively, these patterns suggest a diet-induced dysbiosis that intensifies competition among microbiota members and may produce an unfavorable metabolome, posing potential risks to the human host.

Discussion

The reduced microbiota simulation, based on predictors identified by our previous classifier using multilayer neural networks to detect ASD from microbiota composition, provides insights into the interactions underlying the predictive variables (agregar variables predichas). By simulating bacterial dynamics under various dietary conditions and environmental pressures (such as oxygen levels associated with dysbiosis), the results enhance the interpretability of the ML model by contextualizing how key microbiota features interact and contribute to classification outcomes.

The simulated dietary scenarios provide distinct ecological contexts, each shaping the interactions among bacterial genera in specific ways. These conditions help elucidate whether the genera used as predictors are dominant due to competitive interactions, mutualistic relationships, or their adaptability to specific environmental constraints.

It has been established that the gut microbiota produces and communicates through metabolite exchange [30]. This simulation was run in silico to forecast the impact of the interactions within the simplified microbiota model proposed in this study. The operation was simulated under various external nutritional conditions using COBRA [31].

High fiber without oxygen

A richer fiber diet is considered a protective dietary pattern associated with a reduced risk of metabolic and inflammatory diseases. Moreover, this condition, without oxygen in colon, emulates a healthy state. The results showed that mutualistic interactions play a central role in sustaining symbiosis, with most genera growing more than 12% faster when interacting with each other compared to their individual growth under the same conditions. In addition, parasitism emerged as the dominant interaction, with Clostridium and Roseburia serving as the main donors. Clostridium ferments carbohydrates and produces short-chain fatty acids, while Roseburia is well known for its production of butyrate, a metabolite beneficial for the intestinal mucosa. These metabolites primarily support the growth of Intestinibacter and Anaerobutyricum; however, the interaction is not reciprocal, as the donors do not receive benefits in return.

Competition was the least frequent interaction in this set, occurring twice between Bacteroides and Parabacteroides, and only once among the remaining genera in the synthetic microbiota.

Amensalism was observed between Blautia and Streptococcus, with Blautia being negatively affected while Streptococcus remained unaffected. This imbalance may contribute to gut barrier disruption, since Blautia plays a key role in preserving colonic mucus—a protective layer of the intestinal epithelium—through the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [32].

The classification of interaction types follows the internal processes of COBRA, which evaluate the growth ratio between monoculture and co-culture conditions:(1)r=Gmono/Gmulti

The simulated model with this dietary option indicates that, among the four tested environments, this one strongly promotes mutualism. Such interactions help sustain symbiosis within the gut microbiota and prevent dysbiosis, which could otherwise reduce health-promoting microorganisms—such as butyrate-producing bacteria—while increasing opportunistic pathogens and decreasing microbial diversity [33]. Mutualism fosters bacterial growth without negatively impacting the interacting species, particularly Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides, and Streptococcus. Previous studies have reported that high-fiber diets, rich in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, support the proliferation of beneficial bacteria [34].

Bifidobacterium achieves greater growth under this diet than under any other simulated condition. The positive effects of Bifidobacterium longum have been demonstrated in preclinical research involving children with autism [35]. In the same study, lower levels of this strain were found in ASD individuals when compared to controls.

In an autism rat model, administration of Bifidobacterium longum CCFM1077 strain influenced the kynurenine pathway, which is crucial for neurotransmitter regulation and neuro-immune responses, resulting in modified neurotransmitter levels and a reduction in autism-like behaviors [36]. Reports include not only treatments with this strain alone but also in combination with other genera that also show enhanced growth under the HFD, such as Streptococcus [37], which is not affected by parasitism, competition, or commensalism at this stage of the simulation.

High fiber with oxygen

The simulation findings highlight the role of oxygen levels in shaping gut microbiota interactions and their effect on intestinal health. Oxygen availability increased the number of bacterial interactions and enhanced pairwise growth when bacteria were adequately nourished under both WD and HFD conditions. Nevertheless, reduced levels of Bifidobacterium and Roseburia were observed following oxygen implementation, and these taxa acted as parasitism donors, implying that their growth was suppressed or nullified. The main parasitism recipients were Anaerobutyricum and Intestinibacter, but Subdoligranulum and Streptococcus were also involved. Previous studies have shown that lower levels of the Bifidobacterium genus are associated with diminished anti-inflammatory effects and impaired gut barrier function [38]. Moreover, De Angelis et al. reported that relatively high levels of Roseburia contribute to neuroprotective effects and may influence neurological development and functionality [38].

Western diet without oxygen

The simplified microbiota simulations with this diet showed that the overall growth of bacteria and the number of interactions are reduced compared to the HFD and even to the same diet with oxygen. SCFA production decreases, leading to limited growth and development [39]. Considering that propionate and butyrate production is limited to specific microorganisms within the phylum Bacillota or to the species Akkermansia muciniphila [40], this diet reduces the production of these SCFAs. Roseburia, related to immune and inflammatory properties within the gut, acts as a parasitism giver, its predominant interaction type, whereas other interactions occur trivially, as shown in Fig. 3.

Western diet with oxygen

This diet least promotes mutualism among the bacteria, and even among the five genera (Bifidobacterium, Clostridium, Faecalibacterium, Intestinibacter, and Roseburia) showing this interaction, it occurs only at low intensity. Parasitism is increased, being the most abundant interaction with strong effects on beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium, Roseburia, and Subdoligranulum. Although to a lesser extent, Ruminococcus's growth is also affected. Considering that the main producers of SCFAs in the gut are genera such as Akkermansia, Lachnospira, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Roseburia, Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, and Dorea [41], this diet impacts production by directly affecting the normal growth of several of these taxa. It has been reported that a WD consisting primarily of a high-fat, high-sucrose diet in wild-type mice reduces gut microbiota diversity and promotes the growth of opportunistic pathogens, leading to a reduced presence of bacteria that protect the gut barrier [42]. The overall tendency of the simplified microbiota model to maintain its composition with minimal or even harmful changes when exposed to the WD suggests that this dietary option may be the least favorable simulated environment in terms of metabolite production and its potential influence on ASD symptoms [43].

The distinct separation of clusters in Fig. 6 indicates how diet shapes interaction patterns, promoting specialized roles, balanced mutualism, or competitive dominance.

This is evident when evaluating the outcomes in HF diets, with the results highlighting mutualistic and balanced interactions. In contrast, WDs are more related to competitive or parasitic interactions, which could influence the predictive performance of ASD models. This supports arguments about the role of carbohydrates and lipids in driving variability in microbiota composition, including fewer collaborative interactions among strains that otherwise interact differently under alternative diets. Overall, these findings indicate that this simulation-based approach is a valuable tool for understanding how dietary modulation shapes bacterial behavior, clarifying the functional roles of each genus.

In addition, the presence of oxygen was evaluated in a complementary set of simulations, demonstrating that oxygen plays a significant role by reducing collaborative interactions among microbial strains in the microbiota.

This study presents several limitations. The most important aspect is that it relies on a reduced microbiota simulation, which does not consider the interactions that happen simultaneously with other genera not present in the initial conditions. Also, the outcomes of the simulation have not been validated with in vivo experiments, and finally, the analysis depends on the results of a previous ML model for predicting ASD based on gut microbiota composition, limiting its generalization to other cohorts that do not share the environmental conditions of those who provided the samples. For clinicians, this approach provides mechanistic evidence that supports dietary interventions (e.g., high-fiber diets) to promote mutualism and metabolic balance in the gut microbiota, thereby helping to reduce ASD-associated dysbiosis.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of diet and oxygen availability in shaping gut microbiota interactions, providing valuable insights into their influence on ASD-related microbial dynamics. Our findings demonstrate that high-fiber diets foster mutualistic and balanced interactions, supporting microbial diversity and metabolic stability, whereas Western diets promote competitive and parasitic interactions, potentially contributing to gut dysbiosis. Additionally, the presence of oxygen increases bacterial interactions, influencing gut microbiota composition. By integrating in silico modeling with machine learning-based predictions, we offer a novel approach to understanding microbial ecosystem behavior in ASD contexts. This method enhances the interpretability of ML models by linking predictive bacterial taxa to their functional roles, revealing mechanisms that may underlie ASD-associated microbial imbalances. Moreover, our results reinforce the potential of dietary interventions, particularly fiber-rich diets, in modulating gut microbiota toward a more symbiotic state. These findings could inform future strategies for microbiome-targeted ASD therapies, promoting beneficial bacterial interactions while mitigating harmful ones. To facilitate reproducibility and translational use, we have made available a GitHub repository containing all simulation scripts and detailed documentation (link in Supplementary Information). While a full web server is beyond the scope of this work, this resource enables direct reuse by the community. Despite these promising insights, our study has limitations, including the use of a simplified microbiota model that does not fully capture the complexity of gut microbial interactions in vivo. Additionally, experimental validation is required to confirm the observed metabolic dependencies and interaction patterns. Future research should focus on expanding microbial community simulations, incorporating host-microbiome interactions, and validating these findings through clinical and experimental studies. Ultimately, this study contributes to a growing body of research emphasizing the importance of microbiota composition and interactions in ASD. By bridging computational modeling with experimental microbiome research, we advance efforts to unravel the complex relationships between diet, microbiota, and neurodevelopmental conditions.
