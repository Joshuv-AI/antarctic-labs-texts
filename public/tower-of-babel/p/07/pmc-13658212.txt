Beyond estrobolome 1.0: unraveling endocrine-microbiome axis as a driver and therapeutic target in hormone-driven cancers
Authors: Exian Mou, Rui Guo, Yong Yi, Yang Li, Yutang Yao, Jia Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe endocrine-microbiome axis extends the estrobolome concept, recognizing gut microbes as active endocrine partners. Bacteria modulate estrogen recycling via diverse enzymes and produce SERM-like metabolites, while host hormones shape microbial communities. This bidirectional crosstalk influences hormone-driven carcinogenesis through inflammation, genomic instability, and epigenetic changes. Therapeutic opportunities include precision probiotics and selective enzymatic inhibition. Advancing the field requires causal models, multi-omics integration, and sex as a biological variable. Subject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Hormone-driven cancers, most prominently estrogen receptor positive breast cancer and many endometrial malignancies, remain clinical priorities because systemic and local estrogenic signaling sustain tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance1. Estrogens are synthesized both systemically and intratumorally through aromatase-catalyzed conversion of androgenic precursors, a process that generates biologically active 17β-estradiol and local concentration gradients that modulate tissue behavior2. Phase I cytochrome P450 enzymes then diversify estrogen chemistry by site-specific hydroxylation to yield 2-, 4- and 16-hydroxylated metabolites, changes that alter genotoxic potential and receptor interactions and have been linked experimentally to carcinogenic mechanisms3. Canonical estrogen signaling is mediated by nuclear estrogen receptors ERα and ERβ as well as membrane-associated receptors, with ERα generally driving proliferative transcriptional programs in breast and endometrial epithelia while ERβ and nonclassical receptors modulate or counterbalance those effects in context-dependent ways4,5.

The estrobolome concept, pioneered to explain gut microbial control over enterohepatic estrogen fate, posits that a subset of gut bacterial functions, principally microbial β-glucuronidase and sulfatase activities, deconjugate hepatic estrogen conjugates in the gut, thereby increasing enterohepatic recycling and systemic exposure to active estrogens6,7. This mechanism has been experimentally demonstrated in vitro for diverse human gut β-glucuronidase (GUS) enzymes and has been correlated with altered fecal enzymatic activity and hormone profiles in clinical cohorts, establishing the foundational biochemical link between microbial enzymes and host steroid homeostasis8,9. The estrobolome paradigm, therefore, provided the first mechanistic bridge between microbial communities and hormone-driven cancer risk and remains a useful, well-supported conceptual anchor10. Microbial endocrinology broadens this framing by treating host hormones as microbial cues and microbial products as endocrine signals, creating a bidirectional communication network rather than a one-way metabolite recycling loop. In this view, microbial sensing and hormone transformation can shape bacterial behavior, host endocrine tone, and tissue-level inflammatory responses that are directly relevant to cancer biology. Recent endocrinology-focused reviews emphasize that the gut microbiota participates in host hormonal regulation, while tumor-focused reviews highlight microbial metabolites as plausible contributors to endocrine tumorigenesis11–13.

Accumulating evidence, however, mandates a broader framing: the microbiome is not merely a passive modifier of enterohepatic estrogen conjugates but a dynamic endocrine partner that senses host hormones, produces hormonally active metabolites, and mediates host glandular and systemic endocrine function14,15. First, numerous bacterial two-component systems and quorum-sensing pathways detect mammalian catecholamines and related signals, with conserved sensors such as QseC and QseE transducing epinephrine and norepinephrine cues to alter bacterial physiology and interkingdom behavior16,17. Second, gut microbes enzymatically convert dietary and endogenous precursors into potent hormone-like compounds, exemplified by microbially derived equol from daidzein and by short-chain fatty acids and bile acid derivatives that engage host GPCRs and nuclear receptors to reshape metabolic and proliferative programs15,18,19. Third, microbial metabolites and signaling molecules modulate enteroendocrine output, hepatic metabolism, and even distant tissue microenvironments; for example, short-chain fatty acids bind FFAR2/FFAR3 on enteroendocrine L cells to stimulate GLP-1 and PYY release, thereby rewiring systemic metabolic-endocrine axes that indirectly influence hormone availability, insulin sensitivity, and tissue responsiveness19–22. Collectively these sensor, producer, and mediator functions position the microbiome as an active node in endocrine networks rather than a unidirectional modifier of estrogen clearance9,15.

In this narrative review, we therefore aim to move beyond an estrobolome 1.0 model toward an integrative endocrine-microbiome axis that recognizes bidirectional signaling, molecular mimicry, and multi-tissue mediation as central to hormone-driven oncogenesis and therapeutic responsiveness14,23. We emphasize original mechanistic studies and emerging multi-omics atlases that combine metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics to map causal pathways, and we critically appraise where experimental causality has been demonstrated versus where associative evidence still predominates1,24. By synthesizing recent mechanistic discoveries with the latest multi-omics frameworks, our goal is to define testable hypotheses and translational priorities that take the field beyond a singular enzyme-centric estrobolome and toward actionable, multi-level interventions for estrogen-driven cancers9,25. Mechanistically, metagenomics defines microbial functional potential, metabolomics captures the realized endocrine output, and host transcriptomics/proteomics reveal the downstream signaling programs that connect microbial state to tumor biology. In this framework, a community enriched in estrogen-reactivating or estrogen-inactivating genes should be interpreted together with estrogen conjugates, enterolignans, short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, and host readouts such as ER signaling, NF-κB/STAT3 activation, and DNA-damage responses23,26–29. In this review, human cohort findings are treated as associative or correlative unless supported by perturbation-based or mechanistic experimental evidence, whereas causal inference is reserved for gnotobiotic, humanized, and other controlled experimental models. The expanded estrobolome, beyond β-glucuronidase

Deconjugation is just the beginning

The original estrobolome concept focused attention on gut bacterial β-glucuronidases as gatekeepers of enterohepatic estrogen recycling, but comprehensive genomic and structural surveys demonstrate that GUS enzymes alone comprise a large, polyphyletic family with dozens of distinct structural classes and substrate preferences that cannot be reduced to a single activity metric30,31. High-resolution atlases of human gut GUS sequences reveal hundreds to thousands of nonredundant GUS orthologs that segregate into mechanistically distinct clades whose active site architectures predict differential processing of steroid versus xenobiotic glucuronides30,32. Structural and biochemical studies further show that modest sequence variation in substrate-binding loops produces large changes in catalytic efficiency and substrate scope, which undermines attempts to infer estrogen reactivation potential from crude fecal GUS activity assays alone31. Transcriptional control of GUS genes is likewise context dependent because many gut bacteria regulate hydrolase expression in response to substrate availability, nutrient status, and interspecies signals, producing temporally and diet-driven fluxes in deconjugation capacity33. Beyond GUS proteins, gut microbial communities encode complementary deconjugating enzyme classes such as β-glucosidases and carbohydrate and steroid sulfatases that act on glycoside and sulfate conjugates of plant and host steroids and thereby expand the biochemical routes through which estrogens and estrogenic precursors are liberated in the intestine34,35. Activity-based metaproteomics and targeted metagenomics in human and mouse cohorts show consistent co-occurrence of these enzyme families with GUS genes and indicate that their relative abundance shifts with diet, medication, and disease state, thereby motivating a gene set framing of the estrobolome rather than a single enzyme marker9,36. The two-way street: microbial metabolism can inactivate as well as reactivate estrogens

Microbial influence on estrogen homeostasis is bidirectional because multiple taxa express oxidative and reductive enzymes that chemically modify or degrade steroidal cores, thereby reducing estrogenic potency or routing molecules to nonabsorbed metabolites37,38. Isolates from environmental and gut origins, including members of the genera Microbacterium and Rhodococcus, have been shown to perform hydroxylation, dehydrogenation, and ring-cleavage reactions on 17β-estradiol and related steroids, and the responsible monooxygenases and dehydrogenases have been characterized biochemically in several recent studies37,38. Community-level screens and metatranscriptomic profiling identify candidate steroid reductases and ketosteroid dehydrogenases in intestinal consortia that correlate with reduced fecal and systemic aglycone levels, which implies that catabolic pathways can offset deconjugation and thereby determine net estrogenic load36,37. The relative rates of competing microbial reactions, host phase II conjugation efficiency, and intestinal transit collectively set a dynamic equilibrium in which the same microbiome may act as either an amplifier or a sink for biologically active estrogens, depending on ecological and host contexts39. Experimental animal models provide functional support for this view because colonization with estrogen-transforming bacteria alters circulating receptor activation and downstream transcriptional programs in target tissues, demonstrating that microbial inactivation can have protective effects in models of estrogen-driven proliferation37,38. Recognizing this two-way chemistry is essential for mechanistic models that seek to predict cancer risk or therapeutic interactions from microbial composition data9.

Novel microbial estrogen metabolites

Beyond modulating host-derived estrogens, gut bacteria synthesize and transform dietary precursors into nonhost metabolites that directly engage estrogen signaling with selective receptor profiles, and these molecules plausibly convert community structure into qualitative changes in endocrine signaling40. Classic examples include microbial conversion of soy isoflavones to S-equol, which is produced only by a subset of human microbiomes and displays preferential affinity for the estrogen receptor beta with selective agonist activity in cellular and in vivo models41,42. Similarly, microbial transformation of plant lignans yields enterolignans such as enterodiol and enterolactone that act as partial agonists or antagonists at estrogen receptors and modulate phase I and II metabolic enzymes relevant to estrogen bioactivity43,44. Recent metabolomic surveys and microbial genetics studies have expanded the catalog of candidate estrobolome metabolites to include dehydroequols, hydroxylated equol derivatives, and diverse phenolic conjugates whose ER subtype selectivity and non-genomic signaling differ from canonical estradiol, which raises the possibility that microbial communities shape tissue-specific estrogen signaling through both quantitative mass effects and qualitative ligand replacement45,46. Interindividual variation in the capacity to produce these metabolites, exemplified by equol-producer status, correlates with differences in biomarker profiles and disease associations in epidemiological cohorts, suggesting that identifying the enzymes and taxa responsible for SERM-like microbial metabolites is a tractable translational priority for stratifying hormone-driven cancer risk and for designing microbiome-based adjuvants to endocrine therapy47,48. The bidirectional dialogue, how hormones shape the microbiome

The hormonal milieu as a microenvironmental factor

Sex steroid hormones act as pervasive ecological signals that shape gut microbial composition, diversity, and gene expression across species, with convergent evidence from experimental animals and human cohorts showing sex differences in community structure that track circulating estradiol, progesterone and androgens49,50. Early mechanistic work in mice demonstrated that transfers of adult male microbiota raise testosterone in recipients and alter disease trajectories, directly linking microbial community state with host endocrine set points and thereby inaugurating the microgenderome concept that frames microbiome–sex hormone interactions as a driver of sexual dimorphism in immunity and disease susceptibility50,51. Human population studies and systematic reviews corroborate these findings by associating menopausal status, hormonal contraceptive use, and pregnancy with reproducible shifts in gut taxa and functional potential, indicating that life stage-dependent hormone milieus reorganize both taxonomic and metabolic features of the intestinal ecosystem52,53. Recent subtype-resolved breast-cancer microbiome data also suggest that these effects are functionally heterogeneous rather than uniform: a meta-analysis of TNBC tissue microbiomes found enrichment of Azospirillum and the butyrate-producing species Gemmiger formicilis and Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, supporting a model in which host hormonal context and tumor subtype jointly shape microbial metabolite outputs54. Notably, multiomic profiling reveals hormone-associated changes in microbial gene expression related to xenobiotic metabolism, bile acid transformation, and carbohydrate processing, which create a feed-forward relationship in which hormone-shaped communities further modulate host endocrine and metabolic networks49.

Mechanisms of hormonal influence on bacteria

Bacteria can sense and respond to eukaryotic hormones through diverse molecular mechanisms, including two-component systems and transcription factors that detect host-derived cues; canonical examples are bacterial adrenergic sensors such as QseC and QseE that transduce epinephrine and norepinephrine signals to alter growth and virulence programs17. Recent functional screens have identified bacterial transcription factors that directly bind steroid ligands and can be repurposed as biosensors, demonstrating that microbes possess eukaryotic-like sensing modalities for progesterone and related steroids55. In parallel, a growing set of environmental and gut taxa utilize sex steroids as metabolic substrates, upregulating monooxygenases, dehydrogenases, and transporters when exposed to estradiol or testosterone and thereby using hormones for carbon or electron flow in defined contexts56. Hormones also function as interkingdom signaling molecules because catecholamines modulate bacterial motility, biofilm formation, and toxin expression in multiple species, an effect mediated by conserved bacterial receptors and downstream regulatory cascades that reprogram community behavior in response to host stress states57. Finally, direct hormonal modulation of bacterial physiology alters ecological fitness and pathogenic potential: in vitro and ex vivo studies show that estradiol and synthetic hormonal drugs change growth kinetics, surface adhesins, and biofilm architecture in commensal and opportunistic species, which has plausible consequences for mucosal colonization and translocation58.

Implications across the lifespan

Hormonal transitions across the lifespan remodel the microbiome in temporally specific ways that plausibly alter long-term cancer risk trajectories by changing estrobolome capacity, immune set points, and metabolic mediators58. During puberty the activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis coincides with sex-specific maturation of gut communities and microbial metabolic functions, and experimental models demonstrate that absent or altered pubertal hormone signaling produces persistent changes in adult microbiome composition59. The menstrual cycle and exogenous hormonal contraceptives produce more modest but measurable cyclic and chronic shifts in mucosal and fecal communities, which can change local estrogenic microenvironments and immune tone on a month to month basis60. Pregnancy is a dramatic example in which rising estradiol and progesterone remodel gut and oral communities toward a metabolically permissive state that supports altered nutrient handling and immunoregulation, changes that may have downstream implications for postpartum breast and endometrial biology61. Menopause produces a reproducible decline in microbial diversity and a shift toward a composition more similar to that of men, accompanied by reduced estrobolome potential and altered bile acid and short chain fatty acid profiles, which align with increased cardiometabolic and potentially oncogenic vulnerabilities in postmenopausal women62. Taken together, these lifespan data argue that endocrine milestones are critical windows when microbiome modulation could recalibrate long-term hormone exposure and tissue susceptibility, and they justify longitudinal, mechanistic trials that target hormone–microbiome crosstalk for cancer prevention and therapy stratification49. The axis in action, carcinogenesis and progression

Systemic effects: inflammation and metabolism

Dysbiosis within the endocrine–microbiome axis is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and may contribute to increased translocation or systemic exposure to microbe-associated molecular patterns such as lipopolysaccharide and flagellin. Mechanistically, LPS activates TLR4-linked inflammatory signaling, while microbiota-triggered NLRP3 inflammasome activation can drive IL-1β release, together creating a pro-tumorigenic cytokine milieu that links microbial sensing to host inflammatory phenotype63–66. In human breast tissue, commensal microbiota-derived LPS has been linked to an S100A7/TLR4/RAGE signaling axis and enhanced cancer growth, suggesting translational plausibility, although causality in patients remains unproven64. Experimental models show that diet or microbiome shifts that raise circulating LPS can produce metabolic endotoxemia, insulin resistance, and adipose inflammation, phenotypes that augment insulin and IGF 1 axis signaling known to synergize with estrogen receptor-mediated mitogenic programs in breast and endometrial epithelia63,67. In this context, butyrate-producing taxa should be interpreted through a functional-duality lens: the TNBC tissue meta-analysis found enrichment of butyrate-producing species and pathway changes linked to inflammation, proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, reinforcing that microbial metabolites can be either anti- or pro-tumorigenic depending on concentration, tissue context, and host subtype54.

Gut microbial metabolites such as secondary bile acids and lipoteichoic acids further amplify NF kappa B and STAT3-driven inflammatory cascades, and some bacterial components directly modulate myeloid cell polarization toward tumor-promoting phenotypes, thereby linking systemic microbial signals to tumor associated inflammation68,69. Microbiome-dependent endocrine changes, particularly altered insulin and IGF 1 signaling, may further reinforce these inflammatory cues63,67.

Local effects: the role of tissue-resident microbiomes

Several culture-independent studies suggest that breast tissue may host a distinct microbial community that differs from skin and gut populations, and culture-independent surveys from multiple cohorts have identified reproducible taxa enriched in tumor versus adjacent normal tissue, which supports the biological plausibility of locally mediated microbial influences on mammary carcinogenesis70,71. Subtype-resolved analyses now extend this by showing that TNBC tissues are enriched not only for Azospirillum but also for butyrate-producing species such as Gemmiger formicilis and Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, implying that local breast microbiomes may influence carcinogenesis through metabolite-mediated effects and not merely through taxonomic shifts54. Mechanistically, breast-resident bacteria or their products may modulate local estrogen chemistry by expressing steroid-modifying enzymes including β-glucuronidases and sulfatases or by delivering inflammatory ligands such as LPS that activate TLR4-linked inflammatory signaling and upregulate local aromatase and paracrine estrogen production, thereby increasing local receptor activation independent of circulating hormone levels. In breast tissue, this LPS/TLR4 signaling axis has been associated with enhanced cancer growth, making a microbial product→receptor→tumor pathway biologically plausible, but not yet definitively causal in humans7,64. In addition, tissue-resident microbes may shape the local immune microenvironment by modulating macrophage polarization and γδ T-cell recruitment, thereby influencing whether local signaling is tumor-constraining or tumor-promoting. In breast and endometrial tissues, these local effects are best interpreted as microenvironmental rather than genotoxic mechanisms, because they act primarily through steroid chemistry, inflammatory signaling, and immune contexture72–74.

Beyond the breast, the uterine and endometrial microbiomes show altered community structure in endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma cohorts compared with controls, and several studies report taxa and functional signatures consistent with local inflammation and altered steroid metabolism that could plausibly shift the risk environment in the uterine lining75,76. Importantly, tissue resident microbiomes also shape local immune surveillance; microbial cues reprogram tumor-associated macrophage polarization and influence gamma delta T cell recruitment and activity, roles that can either constrain or facilitate tumor outgrowth depending on the microbial signals and immune contexture69,77. Taken together, these observations support a local tissue-level mechanism in which resident microbiota alter estrogen chemistry and immune surveillance, while the downstream consequences for mutation accumulation and chromatin remodeling are discussed separately in the next section.

Influence on genomic instability and epigenetics

Microbial products generated within the endocrine–microbiome axis can drive genomic instability through direct DNA damage and can also produce durable epigenetic reprogramming in host cells78,79. For genotoxicity, bacterial genotoxins such as colibactin and secondary bile acids such as deoxycholic acid provide mechanistically distinct routes to DNA damage, crosslinks, and mutational footprints that can promote carcinogenesis68,78. For epigenetic regulation, short-chain fatty acids act as histone deacetylase inhibitors and reshape chromatin accessibility, histone acetylation, DNA methylation, and non-coding RNA programs relevant to estrogen receptor signaling and cell-cycle control80,81. In humans, inulin-type fructans increased fecal total SCFA, acetate, and propionate in a randomized controlled trial, whereas butyrate-producing taxa such as Faecalibacterium and Coprococcus were associated with favorable host phenotypes in a large population cohort, giving this pathway direct translational relevance beyond rodent models82,83. Together, these findings support biological plausibility, but the cohort data remain associative and do not establish direct causality for cancer-related outcomes. In addition, fecal microbiota transplantation of depression-associated microbiota into germ-free mice reproduced host metabolic and behavioral changes, providing causal evidence in an experimental model that microbiome-derived metabolites can drive host phenotypes through metabolically mediated routes83,84. Taken together, these convergent pathways support a model in which the endocrine–microbiome axis promotes carcinogenesis through synergistic systemic inflammation and metabolic reprogramming, local microbial modulation of hormone chemistry and immune surveillance, and by generating both mutational and epigenetic perturbations that accelerate neoplastic transformation and progression; clarifying the relative contribution of each arm will require longitudinal human cohorts, mechanistic germ free and gnotobiotic models, and integrated multi-omics interrogation63,67,72.

Therapeutic targeting of the endocrine-microbiome axis

Modulating response to endocrine therapy

Emerging evidence indicates that the gut microbiome can meaningfully influence the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of orally delivered endocrine agents, with consequences for both efficacy and adverse effects. Most human findings in this area are biomarker- or association-based, whereas stronger causal evidence comes primarily from preclinical and gnotobiotic models85. For clarity, the therapeutic evidence below is separated into preclinical findings (in vitro, animal, and gnotobiotic models) and human translational findings (biomarker or feasibility studies), and the level of support is stated explicitly for each intervention. Studies in humans and model systems now show that variation in bacterial β-glucuronidase repertoires alters the enterohepatic cycling of drug metabolites and can prolong exposure to active or inactive drug species, a mechanism first demonstrated for chemotherapy agents and now reported for endocrine drugs such as tamoxifen86,87. Recent mBio and clinical cohort analyses link compositional differences, including enrichment of certain Bacteroides and Clostridium taxa, to altered tamoxifen metabolite levels and to measurable differences in systemic exposure to endoxifen, the principal active metabolite, supporting a microbial reactivation hypothesis for SERM biology85,88. Conversely, aromatase inhibitors appear to reshape gut communities in predictable ways, and preclinical letrozole models demonstrate time-dependent reductions in diversity and function that may feed back onto steroid precursor pools and local estrogen synthesis89,90. Taken together, these data argue that endocrine therapy outcomes may be modulated both by drug-driven microbiome perturbation and by preexisting microbial functions that alter drug and substrate availability, which implies that stratifying patients by microbial functional signatures could improve prediction of response and toxicity91,92. Precision probiotics, prebiotics, and postbiotics

Rational microbiome therapeutics for hormone-driven cancers fall into three complementary strategies: reduce deleterious microbial enzymatic activities, promote production of protective SERM-like metabolites, and sculpt community ecology through diet and prebiotic substrates87,93. However, the apparent benefit of psychobiotic/probiotic interventions is highly strain-, dose-, formulation-, duration-, and population-dependent; therefore, results from one probiotic product should not be generalized to other strains or indications, and pooled analyses are most defensible only when studies share a plausible common mechanism94,95.

Preclinical evidence shows that targeted inhibition of β-glucuronidases with selective small molecules reduces local reactivation of toxic or active metabolites in murine models while preserving global microbiota integrity86,96. In preclinical and proof-of-concept microbiome-engineering studies, synbiotic and probiotic approaches that expand indigenous equol-producing taxa or deliver engineered equol producers can increase S-equol production, a microbial metabolite with preferential estrogen receptor beta activity97,98. In small human interventional studies, combinations of Lactobacillus strains with prebiotic substrates such as lactulose or inulin increase equol production in low-producer hosts, but this remains biomarker-level evidence and does not yet establish cancer-prevention benefit97,99. Limited preclinical and translational studies suggest that probiotic formulations may reduce fecal β-glucuronidase activity, but the human evidence remains preliminary39,100. Finally, dietary patterns rich in fermentable fiber and phytoestrogens provide a low-risk lever to increase short-chain fatty acid production, enhance beneficial taxa, and favour SERM-like metabolite formation, although randomized trials with cancer endpoints are still lacking82,97,101. Human intervention data nonetheless support translational plausibility: randomized clinical trials in major depressive disorder have shown measurable benefits of microbiome-targeted probiotic or prebiotic approaches, and one trial reported that Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025 modulated gut microbiome composition and tryptophan metabolism in patients102,103. Pragmatic next steps include fermentable substrate trials in endocrine therapy cohorts and development of live biotherapeutic products engineered either to lack deleterious enzymes or to produce protective metabolites104,105. Overall, the current psychobiotic-like probiotic/prebiotic evidence base is strongest at the preclinical and biomarker level, whereas cancer-endpoint evidence remains absent. Fecal microbiota transplantation as a proof of concept

Preclinical and early translational evidence indicates that fecal microbiota transplantation can alter immune responsiveness and metabolic phenotypes in model systems, while early human studies mainly provide feasibility and biomarker signals rather than proof of disease modification106,107. However, extending FMT to prevent cancer or to modulate endocrine therapy faces substantial biological and operational hurdles. Donor selection should prioritize functional characteristics rather than taxonomy alone, because donor microbiome composition, recent antibiotic exposure, stool consistency, pathogen-screening breadth, and recipient factors can all affect engraftment and clinical outcomes. The safety bar is higher in oncology because investigational FMT has been associated with transmissible infections, and the FDA has required enhanced donor-testing safeguards, including NAAT screening for EPEC and STEC plus quarantine and repeat testing of donor lots, to reduce pathogen-transmission risk108,109. Real-world registry data are generally reassuring when screening is rigorous, but long-term follow-up remains uneven, and cancer-specific safety data are still limited108–111. Moreover, evidence that FMT can durably change estrobolome capacity or SERM-like metabolite production in humans remains limited to early-phase studies and animal models; randomized, mechanism-focused trials are required before clinical adoption107,110. Given these constraints, an intermediate translational pathway that prioritizes defined consortia or live biotherapeutic products with characterized enzymatic profiles may offer a safer and more controllable route to test causality and therapeutic benefit in endocrine-driven cancers112,113. Accordingly, for hormone-driven cancers, FMT should be framed as hypothesis-generating rather than established therapy.

Collectively, a precision approach to the endocrine-microbiome axis would combine baseline functional microbiome profiling, selective enzymatic blockade, targeted probiotic/synbiotic interventions and, where justified, rational consortia transfers to tilt the host–microbe equilibrium toward reduced estrogenic risk and improved response to endocrine therapies87,97,106.

Challenges and limitations

This section summarizes the major scientific, technical, and translational challenges that limit current progress on the endocrine–microbiome axis and suggests practical steps to address them. In particular, differences between 16S rRNA amplicon profiling and shotgun metagenomics matter because 16S captures only part of the community, whereas shotgun sequencing provides broader taxonomic and functional resolution but remains sensitive to sequencing depth, reference databases, and batch effects; these approaches should not be treated as interchangeable when inferring estrogen-transforming capacity23,114. Across cancer cohorts, heterogeneity in menopausal status, cancer subtype, sampling matrix, geography, diet, antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, and endocrine therapy can produce inconsistent signatures and limit cross-cohort comparability115–117. For that reason, most current human data remain associational, and stronger causal inference will require longitudinal sampling, prespecified confounder control, and perturbation-based designs such as randomized interventions or gnotobiotic experiments118,119.

Measurement, assay, and functional-resolution limits

Taxonomy ≠ function

Most clinical studies still rely on 16S rRNA or shallow shotgun sequencing to report taxonomic shifts, but functional interpretation requires gene-level and activity-level resolution; 16S detects only part of the community, and shotgun metagenomics still varies with depth and database choice, so genus-level abundance alone is insufficient to infer estrogen-transforming capacity23,114. Deep, function-focused assays (shotgun metagenomics plus activity-based metaproteomics and targeted enzyme assays) are required to bridge this gap. Operationally, net estrogenic load could be quantified in three linked layers: first, microbial functional signatures, such as the abundance and diversity of estrogen-reactivating and estrogen-inactivating genes (for example, GUS, arylsulfatase, and other steroid-transforming genes); second, microbial activity readouts, such as fecal β-glucuronidase/sulfatase activity or activity-based metaproteomics; and third, metabolite profiling, such as free-to-conjugated estrogen ratios and active-to-inactive estrogen metabolite ratios in stool and plasma. A recent human stool/plasma LC-MS/MS study combined with shotgun metagenomics showed that β-glucuronidase + arylsulfatase gene copy number, rather than β-glucuronidase alone, correlated with deconjugated stool estrogens and with combined plasma estrogens, supporting a multi-parameter readout rather than a single marker120. A 2025 review likewise identifies enzymes, taxa, precursors, metabolites, and phytoestrogens as the core targets for measuring the estrobolome121. Distinct GUS enzyme classes also differ in endobiotic reactivation and are modulated by drugs, reinforcing the need for composite rather than single-feature metrics9. See the β-glucuronidase atlases and structure–function work that illustrate large functional diversity within enzyme families30.

Biochemical assays lack standardization

Fecal GUS activity, steroid metabolite panels, and bile-acid/metabolite measurements are done with heterogeneous methods across labs; this reduces comparability and meta-analysis power. The field needs community standards for sample collection, normalization (e.g., accounting for transit time and bile-output), and validated targeted assays for estradiol/phase-I/II metabolites in stool and plasma. Evidence that different GUS orthologs show very different substrate preferences underlines the urgency of standardized functional assays6.

Causality, models and experimental design

Correlation dominates human cohorts

Observational associations between microbiome features and cancer risk or therapy response are informative, but they do not establish causality and are vulnerable to confounding by diet, BMI, medications, age, menopause, antibiotics, and other host factors; this is why causal inference frameworks and mechanistic perturbation studies are needed before translating associations into interventions117,118. Translationally useful causal claims demand randomized perturbations or mechanistic gnotobiotic experiments. The limitations of association-only inference are discussed in recent multi-omic integration critiques29.

Gnotobiotic models have external validity limits

While germ-free and humanized mice provide causal proof, interspecies differences in bile acid pools, estrogen metabolism, and intestinal physiology complicate human translation. Careful comparative validation (multiple donor communities, defined consortia, and rescue experiments) is necessary before extrapolating to patients. Structural enzyme diversity shown in microbial GUS work warns that single-strain results can be misleading31.

Longitudinal sampling, timing, and life-stage heterogeneity

Hormone dynamics are temporal

Estrogen exposure varies with menstrual cycle, pregnancy, menopause, and exogenous hormones; single time-point microbiome samples cannot capture fluxes that determine exposure. Longitudinal sampling synchronized with reproductive state, circadian timing, and medication dosing is essential to resolve true microbe–hormone relationships. Several cohort studies show life-stage shifts (pregnancy, menopause) that would be missed by cross-sectional designs9.

Confounding by diet, drugs and host physiology

Multiple, strong confounders

Diet (phytoestrogens, fiber), antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, and common oncology drugs reshape both microbiome and hormone metabolism. Separating a microbiome’s independent effect from these covariates requires either randomized dietary/drug controls or advanced causal inference methods (instrumental variables, negative controls). The extensive modulation of GUS activity by therapeutics is documented in recent functional studies9.

Multi-kingdom complexity and ecological dynamics

Beyond bacteria: virome and mycobiome

Bacteriophages, fungi, and protozoa influence bacterial gene flow, enzyme carriage, and metabolite production; ignoring them gives an incomplete picture. Recent multi-kingdom metagenomic analyses show that phages track functional shifts and fungi associate with metabolomic signatures relevant to inflammation and steroid metabolism. Incorporating viral and fungal layers increases complexity but is essential for mechanistic completeness30,107.

Computational and statistical challenges

High dimensionality, low sample sizes

Multi-omics (metagenome, metatranscriptome, metabolome, host-omics) yields huge feature spaces that risk overfitting. Reproducible module discovery, cross-cohort validation, and model interpretability are still evolving; recent methodological work highlights best-practice pipelines and pitfalls29.

Safety, regulatory, and translational hurdles

FMT and live therapeutics risks

Donor-derived transfers carry infection and unpredictable engraftment risks, especially in oncology patients who may be immunosuppressed. Reviews of FMT in cancer emphasize donor selection, pathogen screening, and regulatory oversight as major barriers. Defined consortia or engineered live biotherapeutics with characterized enzymatic profiles will likely be safer translational routes than crude FMT107. In practice, this means using protocolized donor selection, pre-specified pathogen testing, lot traceability, and adverse-event reporting with long-term follow-up. Recent standards-focused reviews emphasize that additional evidence-based technical and regulatory guidance is still needed to standardize FMT characterization, stability, safety, and efficacy108,109,122.

Sex as a core biological variable

Underpowered sex-disaggregated analysis

Many preclinical and clinical microbiome studies under-sample or fail to analyze sex-specific effects. Given the centrality of sex steroids in this axis, experimental designs and statistical plans must be powered to test sex interactions and report sex-disaggregated outcomes. See calls for improved inclusion and reporting123.

Standardization, reproducibility, and data-sharing

Harmonized protocols needed

To build cumulative evidence, we need community standards for sample collection (feces, tissue biopsies), storage, assay methods (targeted estrogen panels), and minimal metadata (medications, menstrual status, diet). Open data sharing of raw multi-omic matrices and harmonized pipelines will accelerate robust meta-analyses and replication. For FMT specifically, these standards should include donor eligibility criteria, pathogen-screening windows, product traceability, and pre-defined follow-up intervals so that safety and efficacy can be compared across centers and trials108,122.

Future Directions and Unanswered Questions

Translating associative microbiome–cancer findings into mechanistic insight and actionable interventions requires a decisive shift from correlative human cohorts to causal, perturbation-based models that recreate human endocrine–microbiome interactions. Microbial endocrinology provides the mechanistic vocabulary for this agenda: host hormones act as microbial signals, and microbial metabolites can feed back on endocrine pathways and tissue biology. This perspective is especially relevant in hormone-driven cancers because endocrine therapy, steroid metabolism, inflammation, and tissue-resident microbiomes intersect to influence disease progression and treatment response. Accordingly, future studies should pair hormone measurements with functional microbiome readouts rather than relying on taxonomic composition alone11–13. In human systems, validation should combine repeated stool metagenomics, targeted steroid metabolomics, and serial blood hormone measurements, ideally synchronized to menstrual phase, menopause transition, or endocrine therapy timing, with matched tissue sampling when feasible. This design would let investigators test whether microbial β-glucuronidase, sulfatase, or steroid-transforming gene abundance predicts higher deconjugated estrogen pools, altered ER signaling, or poorer treatment response. Causal inference in microbiome research is not established by cross-sectional association alone; it requires prospective cohort studies and interventional clinical trials124–126. Gnotobiotic and humanized mouse models remain the premier experimental platform for this purpose because they permit controlled colonization, host genetic manipulation, and precise hormonal modulation while measuring tumor phenotypes, and recent preclinical work linking microbiome composition to tamoxifen pharmacokinetics illustrates how these systems reveal causal microbe→host drug interactions that cannot be inferred from cross-sectional studies alone85,127.

To define mechanisms rather than correlations, future studies should pre-specify testable hypotheses in gnotobiotic and humanized models. For example, does enrichment of microbial estrogen-reactivating enzymes increase circulating estrogen recycling and tumor ER signaling, and does targeted suppression of those enzymes reduce the phenotype? Standardized experiments can then combine defined microbial consortia, targeted bacterial gene knockouts, and hormone-driven cancer models to test sufficiency and necessity for tumor promotion or suppression. Consensus work on microbiome causality emphasizes that germ-free animals, organoids, and ex vivo systems are useful, but that translational relevance depends on improved standardization and human-relevant isolates125. Comparative designs that transplant paired human donor communities into isogenic mouse hosts and then perturb single variables such as diet, host estrogen biosynthesis, or microbiome enzymatic capacity will be especially informative for mapping causal chains from microbial genes to systemic hormone exposure and tissue signaling. Recent methodological reviews and examples of structure-guided microbial gene discovery underscore that these approaches are now feasible at scale23,29.

High-resolution multi-omics integration is essential to resolve the endocrine–microbiome axis because taxonomic profiles alone cannot predict functional impacts on estrogen chemistry. This direction is reinforced by a recent review that integrates breast, gut, and oral microbiomes within a unified host–microbiome framework and highlights inflammatory signaling, endocrine regulation, microbial metabolites, therapeutic response, biomarker development, and translational challenges in breast cancer128. The strongest human design would be a prospective longitudinal cohort with repeated sampling before, during, and after hormonal transitions or endocrine therapy, coupled to targeted and untargeted metabolomics of estrogens and microbial co-metabolites, host transcriptomics from target tissues, and immune phenotyping. Such a design can test whether microbiome shifts precede changes in endocrine metabolites and tissue signaling rather than merely accompany them. Longitudinal breast-cancer studies already show that endocrine therapy can shift gut microbiota over time, but the current evidence remains underpowered and still requires validation in larger cohorts88,129. State-of-the-art integration frameworks and longitudinal multi-omic pipelines have begun to mature and should be adapted to hormone-driven cancer cohorts, with rigorous sampling schedules that capture menstrual cycle, menopausal transition, and therapeutic interventions23,29,130,131. Clinically, the most informative readout will not be taxonomy alone but a linked metagenomic-metabolomic-host-signaling signature, because microbial composition, metabolite abundance, and tissue or blood pathway activity together determine whether a community is functioning as an estrogen reactivator, an estrogen inactivator, or a neutral background state. The next causal step should be nested intervention studies, such as randomized dietary, synbiotic, or selective-enzyme-inhibition trials, with pre-specified biomarker endpoints rather than cancer endpoints alone. This layered design separates microbial capacity from actual host exposure and improves biomarker discovery for endocrine therapy response, toxicity, and recurrence risk23,26–29,124,125. In practice, a clinically useful workflow would pair stool metagenomics for β-glucuronidase, sulfatase, and steroid-transforming genes with targeted stool/plasma steroid metabolomics and key clinical covariates (menopausal status, BMI, antibiotics, diet, and endocrine therapy exposure) to identify patients most likely to benefit from selective enzyme inhibition, synbiotic/dietary intervention, or closer monitoring. This framework could then be converted into a prespecified ‘estrobolome activity index’ by standardizing each layer and combining them into a weighted composite score for prospective validation. Recent tamoxifen cohort data show treatment-associated microbiome remodeling, but correlations with endoxifen remain inconsistent, underscoring that biomarker-guided stratification is promising yet still requires prospective validation before routine use88,132,133.

Bacteria are necessary but not sufficient to explain the endocrine ecology of host tissues; the virome and mycobiome must be incorporated because phages modulate bacterial population dynamics and gene transfer, and fungi interact with both bacteria and the host immune system in ways that can alter metabolic outputs relevant to estrogen signaling. Multi-kingdom shotgun sequencing and viral particle enrichment approaches in recent cohort studies reveal that phage communities track functional shifts in bacterial hosts, and that fungal taxa correlate with metabolomic signatures linked to inflammation and steroid metabolism, which makes multi-kingdom profiling a priority for mechanistic work134,135.

Finally, sex must be treated as a fundamental biological variable in every experimental design and computational analysis. Basic microbiome research has historically under-sampled one sex in preclinical studies, which limits generalizability for sex hormone-dependent biology; contemporary guidance and evidence show that inclusion of males and females, explicit reporting of sex disaggregated results, and powered subgroup analyses are essential to uncover sex-specific microbiome mechanisms that modulate cancer risk and therapy response. Designing experiments that systematically vary sex chromosome complement, gonadal hormone exposure, and age will be critical to disentangle chromosomal versus hormonal drivers of microbe–host interactions123,136.

In sum, the next phase of endocrine–microbiome research must be integrative, causal, and multi-kingdom. Priority actions are standardized, longitudinal cohort studies with synchronized sampling and estrogen-focused metabolomics; gnotobiotic and humanized models with defined perturbations to test causality; explicit inclusion of virome and mycobiome layers; and rigorous incorporation of sex as a core variable. These investments will convert promising associations into mechanistic maps and testable interventions that can be evaluated in clinical trials addressing prevention and adjuvant strategies for hormone-driven cancers23,137. Conclusion

The endocrine-microbiome axis is a useful but still evolving framework for hormone-driven cancers. The strongest evidence supports microbial modulation of estrogen recycling through β-glucuronidase and related steroid-transforming enzymes, together with local inflammatory effects in breast and endometrial tissues; however, most human studies remain associative, and causal links to cancer initiation or progression are not yet established. The therapeutic implications are also promising but immature: probiotics, prebiotics, defined consortia, and fecal microbiota transplantation have generated encouraging preclinical and biomarker-level results, but cancer-endpoint evidence is still sparse. Accordingly, the field now needs causal gnotobiotic and humanized models, longitudinal multi-omics, multi-kingdom profiling, and sex-stratified analysis to distinguish robust mechanisms from context-dependent signals and to identify which microbial functions are genuinely actionable. Until that evidence matures, the endocrine-microbiome axis should be framed as a high-priority translational hypothesis rather than a validated clinical target.
