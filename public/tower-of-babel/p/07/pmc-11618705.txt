Exploring Oxygen Vacancy Effect in 1D Structural SnIP for CO2 Electro‐Reduction to Formate
Authors: Hyeon‐Seok Bang, Jiho Jeon, Jinsu Kang, Young‐Jin Ko, Cheoulwoo Oh, Hyunchul Kim, Xiaojie Zhang, Kyung Hwan Choi, Chaeheon Woo, Xue Dong, Hak Ki Yu, Woong Hee Lee, Jae‐Young Choi, Hyung‐Suk Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract1D nanostructures exhibit a large surface area and a short network distance, facilitating electron and ion transport. In this study, a 1D van der Waals material, tin iodide phosphide (SnIP), is synthesized and used as an electrocatalyst for the conversion of CO2 to formate. The electrochemical treatment of SnIP reconstructs it into a web‐like structure, dissolves the I and P components, and increases the number of oxygen vacancies. The resulting oxygen vacancies promote the activity of the CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), increasing the local pH of the electrode surface and maintaining the oxidative metal site of the catalyst despite the electrochemically reducing environment. This strategy, which stabilizes the oxidation state of the catalyst, also helps to improve the durability of CO2RR. In practice, 1D structured SnIP catalyst exhibits outstanding performance with >92% formate faradaic efficiency (FEformate) at 300 mA cm−2, a maximum partial current density for formate of 343 mA cm−2, and excellent long‐term stability (>100 h at 100 mA cm−2 with >86% FEformate). This study introduced a method to easily generate oxygen vacancies on the catalyst surface by utilizing 1D materials and a strategy to improve the durability of CO2RR by stabilizing the oxidation state of the catalyst.Keywords: 1D structure, CO2 reduction, electrocatalysts, formate, oxygen vacancy

Introduction

Global warming caused by massive CO2 emissions has resulted in serious climate change, and utilizing renewable electricity to transform CO2 into valuable chemicals is considered effective for alleviating the current crisis and reducing CO2 concentrations in the atmosphere.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] Carbon‐based liquid‐phase products (formate,[ 5 ] methanol,[ 6 , 7 ] ethanol[ 8 , 9 ]) and gaseous‐phase products (CO,[ 10 , 11 ] C2H4 [ 12 , 13 ]) can be obtained by synthesizing catalysts and optimizing reaction conditions. Particularly, formate produced via electrochemical CO2 reduction reactions (CO2RR) has gained interest owing to its potential for industrial applications such as fuel cells and hydrogen storage systems, offering feasible industrialization and economic benefits.[ 14 ]

Although numerous electrochemical catalysts capable of producing formate have been investigated, challenges related to their catalytic activity and stability have hindered their commercialization. Despite these challenges, research into CO2RR catalysts, particularly those based on metals, continues owing to their superior reactivity and durability.[ 15 ]

According to Trasatti's volcano plot,[ 16 ] metals including Pb,[ 17 ] Bi,[ 18 ] Hg,[ 19 ] In,[ 20 ] and Sn[ 21 ] exhibit high catalytic activity for formate production. Among these, Pb and Hg are unsuitable owing to their toxicity, while Bi is scarce in the earth's crust.[ 22 ] Therefore, Sn and In present a more viable option. Sn, particularly, has undergone extensive research, including morphological design,[ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 ] ion doping,[ 27 ] defect engineering,[ 28 ] and the formation of heterojunctions and bimetallic alloys,[ 26 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] to enhance its catalytic activity in the CO2RR to formate.

However, the long‐term stability of Sn‐based catalysts remains a barrier to their commercialization. Maintaining a high electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) in Sn while preserving its high oxidation state is critical for achieving high catalytic activity in the CO2RR to formate.[ 32 ] However, Sn‐based electrochemical catalysts with a high oxidation state can undergo self‐reduction at high reduction potentials, resulting in a loss of catalytic activity and a reduction of formate. This phenomenon can be mitigated by making the reaction environment alkaline. Maintaining an alkaline atmosphere through a relatively high local pH, facilitated via a high ECSA, can improve the stability of Sn‐based catalysts. However, the purging of CO2 during the CO2RR neutralizes alkaline electrolytes such as potassium hydroxide (KOH). Therefore, catalysts that remain active and stable at higher reduction potentials are required.

Low‐dimensional materials exhibit covalent bonds forming crystal structures, which are held together by van der Waals (vdW) forces.[ 33 , 34 ] External forces, such as ultrasonication, can disrupt the structures of these materials bound by vdW forces while leaving the bonding patterns originating from covalent bonds unchanged if external forces are suitable, and not too strong.[ 33 , 35 ] Building on these properties, exfoliation via external forces can result in structures with relatively large surface areas and increased catalytic activity.

Utilizing the delamination propensity of low‐dimensional materials can help in performance enhancement. Niobium selenide is an example of a low‐dimensional material that has been used for electrochemical catalysis.[ 36 ] Similarly, tin iodide phosphide (SnIP), a 1D material, can be delaminated via ultrasonication to increase its surface area. Furthermore, because SnIP exhibits relatively weak covalent bonding, subjecting it to a harsh reduction potential can reduce the Sn component, which leads to structural disintegration.

While low‐dimensional materials can improve the active surface, the introduction of surface defects is anticipated to improve performance by creating more active sites. With this in mind, this study was conducted to investigate these concepts and potentially achieve superior performance.

Herein, we investigate a Sn web‐like structure catalyst using SnIP as a precursor. It exhibits high catalytic activity across a wide potential range and remains stable over 100 h. To create the Sn‐web‐like structure, SnIP was electrochemically treated under harsh reduction conditions. Iodine and phosphorus were melted during the electrochemical treatment. This facilitated the formation of oxygen vacancies, increasing the active surface area of the web‐like Sn‐structure catalyst. Through in situ X‐ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis, we discovered that the oxidation state of the Sn web‐like structure increased even under reduction potentials. The increased active surface areas enable the catalyst to interact more frequently with OH− ions in the catalytic system, resulting in continuous oxidation at alkaline conditions via the Sn web‐like structure. This study explores how utilizing the surface porosity of low dimensional materials and we're making progress in understanding how such materials can be superiorly helpful as CO2RR catalysts in electrochemistry.

Results and Discussion

Structure and Physical Properties of SnIP

The crystal structure of SnIP is shown in Figure 1a. Phosphorus forms an internal helical structure via covalent bonding, whereas Sn and I form external helical structures through the same process. Each helix is part of a double‐helical structure composed of monowires. A scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of synthesized SnIP is shown in Figure 2b. The inset image confirms that the SnIP powder obtained is purple−brown in color. Bulk SnIP, like monowire SnIP, exhibits a rod‐shaped structure. Because the monowires were bundled using vdW forces, the rod‐like structure remained in its bulk state (Figure S1, Supporting Information). This structural characteristic is caused by the relatively strong covalent bonding between the helices within the monowire, whereas bundling between monowires is based on weaker vdW bonding. This allows the double helix structure in the monowires to be maintained while allowing bundle exfoliation via external forces such as ultrasonication, thereby preserving structural integrity. Morphological variations in ultrasonicated SnIP crystals are presented in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). A scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) image of an exfoliated bulk SnIP crystal is shown in Figure 1c. The exfoliation revealed particle size, which was smaller than the aggregates depicted in Figure 1b.

Fabrication of Sn Electrode with a Web‐Type Structure

The SnIP exhibits a 1D structure, which appears as a rod‐like structure in its bulk state. This distinct structure facilitated the formation of a web‐like Sn structure during electrochemical treatment. To form a Sn web‐like structure, SnIP must be uniformly deposited on a gas diffusion layer (GDL). This process is illustrated in Figure 2a. To fabricate the SnIP‐coated carbon paper, the precursor SnIP, which is essential to developing the Sn web‐like structure, was mixed with ethanol and nafion solution to uniformly disperse the SnIP, followed by ultrasonication. During ultrasonication, an external force of sufficient magnitude was applied to maintain the unique shape of the SnIP precursor. Figure S2 (Supporting Information) depicts how the ultrasonication amplitude affects the shape of the SnIP precursors. The SnIP ink was deposited using a spray gun. The SnIP‐coated GDL was used to assemble a cell, and electrochemical treatment was performed for 20 min at 50 mA cm2. Our previous study described the structure of the cell used in the reaction.[ 21 ] As the electrochemical treatment progressed, the distinct 1D double helix structure of SnIP was disrupted, and the I and P elements dissolved within the structure, leaving only the shape of the Sn rod‐like structures (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Therefore, the SnIP transformed into a Sn‐web‐like structure.

The SEM image of the deposited SnIP precursor on carbon paper is presented in Figure 2b. The SEM image in Figure 2e depicts the Sn web‐like structure after electrochemical treatment, revealing an irregular structure on the carbon paper's surface. This structure resulted from the collapse of the crystalline structure of the SnIP precursor, where the I and P components dissolve, resulting in numerous surface defects and Sn aggregation from various nano chains. This aggregation resulted in a larger surface area structure than in pristine SnIP. Because of these surface defects, the active surface area of the Sn web‐like structure increased, as shown in the following demonstration. Figure 2c exhibits the X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results for the SnIP precursor deposited on carbon paper. Only Sn4+ ions were observed, indicating non‐disruption of the SnIP precursor. XRD pattern of Sn web‐like structure after electrochemical treatment depicted in Figure 2d shows it contains Sn, SnO, and SnO2, respectively. It also presents the existence of the remaining SnIP precursor displayed at nearly 18 degrees. Figure 2f depicts the results of the SnIP precursor breakdown and the XPS analysis of the Sn web‐like structure. The oxidation states of Sn on the web‐like Sn surface were 0, +2, and +4. The EDS analysis of the Sn web‐like structure is presented in Figures 2g and S4 (Supporting Information). Despite the relatively high levels of Sn, I and P were rarely detected. Notably, oxygen was detected, most likely as a result of the electrochemical treatment. As previously stated, electrochemical treatment occurs within a cell supplied with electrolytes such as KOH and potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3). These electrolytes contain OH− ions, which appear to adsorb on the Sn web‐like structure's surface, resulting in oxidation. The atomic percentages of Sn, I, P, and O are presented in Table S1 (Supporting Information). Interestingly, the at% of Sn was significantly higher than that of O in the EDS analysis. Compared to the XPS analysis results, this suggests that the surface was oxidized while the interior remained metallic Sn. Therefore, the web‐like Sn structure derived from the SnIP precursor can be classified as a metallic Sn‐based structure with an oxidized surface.

CO2RR Performance of Sn‐Based Electrodes

To investigate the impact of surface defects, the CO2RR activities of the web‐like Sn structure, SnO2, and metallic Sn were measured. The SEM images of the commercial powder used in the experiments are presented in Figure S5 (Supporting Information). The electrocatalytic activity and selectivity of each catalyst were measured using a custom‐made flow cell. Our previous study described the structure of the homemade flow cell.[ 21 ] In Sn‐based electrochemical catalysts, higher oxidation states enhance formate production selectivity. However, under a reducing voltage, Sn‐based electrochemical catalysts undergo self‐reduction. To prevent self‐reduction, an anion exchange membrane (AEM) was used to deliver OH− to the cathode. To confirm the lack of CO2RR activity in SnIP contrasted to Sn web‐like structure, we examined the electrical conductivity before and after electrochemical treatment using LSV curves. It was observed that SnIP itself lacked sufficient electrical conductivity (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Following this, the CO2RR performance of the Sn web‐like structure was evaluated. In this study, alkaline 1 m KOH and neutral 0.5 m KHCO3 were utilized. In general, the proton concentration in 1 m KOH was significantly low, reducing the frequency of the hydrogen evolution reaction. Therefore, in the presence of 1 m KOH, each catalyst demonstrated its maximum electrocatalytic activity. The Sn web‐like structure achieved 95% formate faradaic efficiency (FEformate) in the 200 mA cm−2 region, surpassing metallic Sn (71.2%) and SnO2 (83.1%). Furthermore, the FEformate of the Sn web‐like structure maintained over 92% until 300 mA cm−2. At 400 mA cm−2, the maximum partial current density was 341.4 mA cm−2 (Figure 3a–c). The electrocatalytic activities of the catalysts under neutral conditions are shown in Figures S7–S9 (Supporting Information).

When CO2 is continuously added to the electrolyte for the CO2RR, KOH is converted into a neutral medium. Durability tests were performed under neutral 0.5 m KHCO3 to ensure the stability of the entire system. Despite neutral media, the surface of the Sn web‐like structure has a higher local pH than the entire electrolyte because of the feature of CO2RR so it could interact with OH− to maintain its oxidation state.[ 38 ] At 100 mA cm−2, metallic Sn exhibited a FEformate of 62%, while the Sn web‐like structure exhibited a FEformate of 90%. During the durability test at 100 mA cm−2, the FEformate of the Sn web‐like structure maintained over 85%, while that of metallic Sn decreased to 30% following a few hours (Figure 3d,e). Figure S10 (Supporting Information) depicts the results of the durability test for SnO2 catalysts. Figure 3f exhibits formate production rates in 1 m KOH. The Sn web‐like structure for formate production allowed for higher production rates over a longer period. Similarly, Figure 3g depicts the formate production rate in 0.5 m KHCO3. Similarly, the Sn web‐like structure could produce more formate over time. To assess the catalytic activity and stability of the Sn web‐like structure, a comparison of the Sn‐based catalyst performances in flow cells using neutral electrolytes is presented in Figure 3h. Compared to other Sn‐based catalysts, the Sn web‐like structure performed better in terms of catalytic activity and stability. Continuous surface oxidation, owing to the increased active surface area caused by surface degradation and aggregation, appears to promote long‐term electrochemical formate production.

In situ/Operando Analysis

The catalyst exhibits several major advantages, including high FEformate, high partial current density, and excellent stability. To better understand the underlying principles of the Sn web‐like structure, in situ/Operando X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES) analysis was performed in the Sn K‐edge region. To perform in situ/Operando XANES, a homemade in situ/Operando XAFS flow cell was used. In this study, XANES analysis was performed to identify the oxidation states of the Sn web‐like structures during the reaction. Metallic Sn, SnO, and SnO2 were measured to specify the energy and oxidation state of the Sn web‐like structure (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Metallic Sn exhibited no significant changes in the in situ/operando XANES measurements under conditions similar to those for the web‐like Sn structure (Figure 4a,b). The O 1s XPS spectrum of the Sn‐deposited electrode was deconvoluted into three peaks (Figure 4c): SnO lattice oxygen (≈530.6 eV), SnO2 lattice oxygen (≈531.2 eV), and C─O bond (≈532.3 eV).

The in situ/Operando XANES spectrum of the Sn‐web‐like structure is shown in Figure 4d. In ex situ pristine SnIP, before electrochemical treatment, the Sn K‐edge XANES spectrum indicated that the quadrivalent (+4) oxidation state is predominant (Figure 4e). When the Sn web‐like structure was formed using electrochemical treatment, the XANES linear combination fitting (LCF) results revealed a dominant zero‐valent (0) oxidation state along with the presence of divalent (2+) Sn ions. Under mild reduction conditions (−0.9 VRHE), zero‐valent and quadrivalent Sn ions coexisted in similar proportions. Although zero‐valent Sn ions dominated the harsh reducing environment of −1.3 VRHE, some divalent and quadrivalent Sn ions were also observed. After electrochemical treatment, oxygen vacancies of Sn web‐like structure act as surface‐active agents and react with humidity and OH− ions. This reaction leads to increasing oxidation states of the Sn web‐like structure.[ 38 ] The LCF analysis of the oxidation state following the reaction in the post‐open‐circuit voltage phase reveals similar behavior to that observed in the suitable reduction environment (−0.9 V), with a similar ratio of zero‐valent Sn and quadrivalent Sn. The results of the ex situ analysis obtained after removing the electrolyte from the cell demonstrated that the majority of the Sn was in the zero‐valent state. This observation is consistent with the SEM−EDS and XPS results (Figure 2). The difference in behavior between the reduced Sn web‐like structure and metallic Sn stems from their distinct ECSAs and the O 1s peak in the XPS spectra; these distinct behaviors were identifiable. The O 1s XPS spectrum of the SnIP‐deposited electrode is shown in Figure S12 (Supporting Information). It was deconvoluted into three peaks corresponding to the SnO2 lattice oxygen (≈531.2 eV), C═O bond (≈532.3 eV), and C─OH/C─O─C bond (≈533.3 eV). Figure 4f depicts the Sn web‐like structure, deconvoluted into five peaks: SnO lattice oxygen (≈530.6 eV), oxygen vacancy (≈531.6 eV), C═O bond (≈532.3 eV), C─OH/C─O─C bond (≈533.3 eV), and H2O/O2 bond (≈535.4 eV). The distinct peaks of the web‐like Sn structure corresponded to oxygen vacancies and H2O/O2 bonds. To elucidate the presence of oxygen vacancies in the Sn web‐like structure, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was used. The EPR spectra presented in Figure 4g reveal that the Sn web‐like structure contains a significant number of oxygen vacancies.[ 39 , 40 ] In contrast, metallic Sn shows almost no oxygen vacancies. The presence of oxygen vacancies in Sn web‐like structured electrodes is particularly crucial because it increases the ECSA of the electrochemical catalyst.[ 41 ]

The Cdl value of the Sn web‐like structure is superior to that of metallic Sn, as shown in Figure 4h. The Cdl values for each catalyst were determined from the cyclic voltammetry measurement results presented in Figures S13 and S14 (Supporting information). The ECSA of the Sn web‐like structure was greater than that of metallic Sn. This higher ECSA is generated by oxygen vacancies, which chemically react with OH− ions and it also leads oxidation of the catalyst. In an alkaline electrolyte, the high pH ensures a sufficient supply of OH− ions to react with the oxygen vacancies in the Sn web‐like structure. The high performance and durability of a neutral electrolyte can also be explained by the reaction of OH− ions with oxygen vacancies. When a CO2 reduction reaction occurs, OH− ions formed on the surface exist at high concentrations, creating a local electrolysis environment that becomes alkaline. This phenomenon can be confirmed by operando Raman spectroscopy (Figure S15, Supporting Information).[ 42 ] The oxidized surface created during the reaction not only increased the Sn web‐like structure's CO2RR‐related active surface, but it also served as a protective layer, preventing corrosion during the CO2RR. Based on these findings, it can be concluded that the exceptional activity and stability of this catalyst are owing to oxygen vacancies and the oxidation of the surface of the Sn web‐like structure during the reaction.

Conclusion

We prepared 1D double‐helical SnIP template materials as electrocatalysts for the CO2 reduction reaction using solid‐state synthesis. Following the electrochemical treatment, the SnIP collapsed and eventually formed a Sn‐web‐like structure. I and P were dissolved from the SnIP structure during the electrochemical treatment. This promotes the formation of oxygen vacancies in the catalyst, increasing the active surface area of the Sn‐web‐like structure. We discovered that the oxidation state of the Sn web‐like structure increases even at reduction potentials using in situ XAFS analysis. Oxygen vacancies increase the catalyst's active surface area, allowing for frequent interactions with OH− ions. This leads to continuous oxidation via the Sn web‐like structure under alkaline conditions. Our catalyst exhibits over 92% FEformate up to 300 mA cm−2, a 343 mA cm−2 partial current density for formate (FEformate = 85.8%), and 86% long‐term stability at 100 mA cm−2 over 100 h. This study highlights the research area of surface engineering by investigating surface porosity and the effect of vacancies in catalysts made from low‐dimensional materials.

Experimental Methods

Synthesis of SnIP

The 1D SnIP material was synthesized via solid‐state synthesis. To prepare the reaction precursor powder, a stoichiometric mixture of Sn (powder, Daejung), SnI4 (powder), and P (powder) was sealed in a 12 cm‐long evacuated quartz tube and heated to 400 °C for 72 h. Following the reaction, the mixture was cooled to room temperature to obtain SnIP powder.

The SnIP precursors included Sn, SnI4, and red phosphorus. Commercial Sn powder (Sigma–Aldrich) and red phosphorus (amorphous, Acros) were used. SnI4 was synthesized by combining Sn and iodine powders (Alfa Aesar). Sn and iodine were mixed for 2 h in 40 °C methanol, then cooled and washed using ethanol. Sn, SnI4, and red phosphorus were mixed stoichiometrically, pelletized, sealed in a 12 cm‐long quartz tube in a 10−3 torr vacuum, and heated to 400 °C for 72 h. Following the reaction, the SnIP pellets were naturally cooled and ground using a mortar and pestle.

Dispersing of SnIP

SnIP (120 mg) was ultrasonically dispersed in 8 mL of ethanol. Ultrasonication was performed by operating a probe sonicator (VC505, Sonics & Materials Inc.) at 80% amplitude for 1 h to break down the bulk of SnIP and improve the dispersion efficiency. Bath sonication (B2005S68 K, 68 kHz, 200 W, KODO technical) was used for 2 h to exfoliate crushed bulk SnIP into nanoscale chains.

Characterization

XRD (Bruker, D8 advance) was performed using Cu−Ka radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The SnIP nano chains were analyzed morphologically and compositionally using field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FE−SEM, Hitachi, Regulus8230) and an attached EDS. XPS (Ulvac Co., PHI 5000 Versaprobe) was performed using Cu−Ka radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) to investigate the oxidation state of SnIP and Sn web‐like structures. The morphological analysis of the SnIP nano chains was performed using HR−TEM (FEI Talos F200X).

Preparation of Metallic Sn and SnIP Catalyst Electrodes for CO2RR

The catalyst ink was prepared via ultrasonically mixing a 5wt% Nafion solution (5wt% target catalyst), carbon black (Ketjenblack EC‐600JD, 6 mg), and SnIP catalyst powder (120 mg) with ethanol (8 mL). The SnIP templated electrodes were fabricated by spraying the prepared catalyst ink onto a gas diffusion electrode (Sigracet 39 BB, SGL carbon) at 70 °C. The electrode area was 5 cm2, and the catalyst ink loading was set at 1 mg cm−2 amount of Sn element.

Preparation of IrO2/Pt‐coated Ti‐Foam Electrodes for Oxygen Evolution Reaction

A 5 wt% ionomer solution (5wt% target catalyst) and IrO2 powder (Alfa Aesar, 30 mg) were ultrasonically mixed in 1 mL DI and 7 mL IPA to make oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalyst ink. OER catalyst electrodes were fabricated by spraying the prepared OER ink onto a Pt‐coated Ti foam at 70 °C. The electrode area was 5 cm2, and IrO2 was applied until it was completely black.

Electrochemical CO2RR Flow Cell Tests and Product Analysis

The CO2RR performance of the SnIP was measured using a homemade flow cell, which was detailed in our previous research.[ 21 ] Our homemade flow cell consists of a serpentine Ti plate, an IrO2‐coated Ti mesh for the OER, a gasket for alignment and spacing, an AEM (dioxide materials, X37‐50 grade RT membrane), an electrolyte flow spacer, a GDL for the CO2RR, and a serpentine gas flow channel. The catholyte and anolyte were 1 m KOH on each side of the AEM (dioxide materials, X37‐50 grade RT membrane). Each electrolyte was circulated via a flow cell at a rate of 25 mL per min using a peristaltic pump. The catholyte was routed through the flow channel connecting the serpentine Ti plate and the AEM. The anolyte was passed through the GDL and AEM. Humidified CO2 at a flow rate of 100 ccm was introduced into the gas flow channel and reduced to CO and formate at the catalytic activity site. The formate concentration of the catholyte was measured to determine formate production. The total outflow composition of the gas‐phase product was measured using gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 7890A). The GC inlet, which included a water trap to prevent water from entering the GC, was connected to the cathode outline. The carrier gas was helium, and the carbon‐based gases CO, CH4, C2H4, and H2 were detected using a flame ionization detector and a thermal conductivity detector, respectively. In situ/Operando X‐ray Absorption Spectroscopy Analysis

The Sn K‐edge hard X‐ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) spectra of metallic Sn, SnIP, and Sn web‐like structures were recorded at the 10 C beamline of the Pohang acceleration laboratory. In situ and operando XAS measurements were performed using a homemade electrochemical flow cell with a small hole. To transmit and diffuse the X‐rays, a 1 cm2 hole was constructed. For electrical contact, a catalyst‐coated polyimide thin‐film electrode was attached to a thin carbon‐coated Kapton film. The counter and reference electrodes were Pt wire and Ag/AgCl, respectively. A 1 m KOH electrolyte saturated with CO2 flowed through the working, counter, and reference electrodes. The setup details are described in a previous study.[ 43 ]

Electrochemical Active Surface Areas Analysis

The ECSA was measured using a rotating disk electrode to assess CO2RR performance. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis was conducted in the voltage range from −0.30 V to −0.20 V with reference to the cell voltage. The ink of each catalyst was prepared without carbon black only for measuring the ECSA of each catalyst. The CV measurements were carried out in an environment where CO2 was purged in a 1 M KOH electrolyte. CV measurements were taken at scan rates with intervals of 50 between 100 and 300 mV s−1. A slope was generated from the current in the region where CV measurements were continuously repeated, and this was used to compare the ECSA of each sample.

Operando Raman Spectroscopy Analysis

The surface‐enhanced operando Raman (SERS) was measured using our homemade 3 electrode system cell. The light source was a Nd:YAG laser (532 nm). The catalysts loaded carbon paper used as the working electrode. A platinum wire was used as the counter electrode and Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) was used as the reference electrode. For the CO2RR, 100 sccm CO2 gas was supplied to the backward of the working electrode. Our homemade 3 electrode system cell was filled with 1 M KHCO3 electrolyte to the height of 0.5 cm. The electrochemical experiments were conducted using a VSP potentiostat.
