Comparative In vitro Analysis of Push-Out Bond Strength of Different Root Canal Sealers to Root Dentin
Authors: Joyeta Ghosh, Bhagyesh J. Cheta, Yogesh V. Rathod, Richa Pathak, Shruti A. Nayak, K Vishnu Prasad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:The study’s objective is to assess the bond strengths of four different endodontic sealers to root dentin using a push-out test technique.Material and Method:Sixty single-rooted teeth were selected and decoronated. Canal instrumentation was done using rotary files and divided into four groups (n = 15) based on the sealer used. Group 1: zinc oxide eugenol (ZOE)-based, Group 2: epoxy resin-based, Group 3: methacrylate-based, and Group 4: bioceramic. Following obturation with Gutta Percha, each tooth was prepared with root portions that were 2 mm thick at the apical and middle 1/3 so that universal testing equipment could measure the push-out bond strength. Tukey’s post hoc tests and one-way ANOVA were used to examine the data.Result:According to the results, group 4 had the strongest push-out relationship, followed by groups 3, 2, and 1, respectively. It can be concluded that the bioceramic sealers have superior bond strengths.Conclusion:Bioceramic sealers demonstrated the highest bond strengths to root dentin, outperforming methacrylate, epoxy resin, and zinc oxide eugenol sealers in the push-out test.

INTRODUCTION

The most efficient method in the field of endodontics is to promptly and completely obturate and sterilize the pulp chamber. For root fills, a firm core material and a sealer are necessary. Gutta Percha is the material most frequently employed and fills the maximum of canal area. To create a sufficient seal for the root canal system, solid or semisolid obturating materials that are physiologically acceptable are combined with root canal sealers. “Binding agents used to fill up the gap between root canal and obturating material” is how Dr. Vimal Sikri defines root canal sealers. Because of microleakage at the sealer–core material contact or sealer–dentin interface, sealers may be the cause of root canal failure. As a result, the push-out bond strength reduces the possibility of dislodgement under mechanical pressures, i.e., during restorative treatments or masticatory function by acting as a useful prognostic indicator for evaluating this connection.[12]

Therefore, the goal of the present investigation was to assess and contrast the push-out bond strength of four different endodontic sealers—ZOE-based, epoxy resin-based, methacrylate-based, and bioceramic—to the root dentin of teeth that had undergone endodontic treatment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Single-rooted 60 teeth that radiography verified of free of root caries, closed apices, and undamaged roots were selected. Multiple rooted teeth, open apices, fracture lines, root resorption, endodontic fillings, and prepared or calcified canals were omitted from the research. Enrolled freshly extracted teeth were washed under running water to remove any debris and blood clots, and then, they were disinfected for a whole day using a 0.1% thymol in distilled water solution. After removing calculus and stains with ultrasonic cleaning, tissue debris was eliminated by soaking the teeth in a NaOCl solution of 2.5% for a full day. They were then stored in distilled water until they were required once more. Decoronated of teeth were done to ensure a uniform root length for the study, ensuring consistency across all samples. Canal shaping and group allocation of the decoronated teeth were done using ProTaper Gold Rotary Files (Dentsply, Germany) to standardize the canal preparations using the irrigation guidelines. After shaping, the specimens were categorized into four groups each containing 15 teeth, distinguished by the type of sealer used for obturation:

Group 1: ZOE-based sealer (Zical Ultra–Root Canal Sealer, Prevest DenPro Limited, India)

Group 2: Epoxy resin-based sealer (Epoxy Seal, SafeEndo Dental India Pvt Ltd, India)

Group 3: Methacrylate-based sealer (EndoREZ, Ultradent Products, Inc.)

Group 4: Bioceramic sealer (EndoSequence BC, Brasseler, Savannah, USA).

Each root canal was then obturated using Gutta Percha cones with the respective sealer designated for each group, employing a standardized technique to ensure consistency in the obturation process across all samples. Bond strength testing of the post-obturated tooth was carefully sectioned to produce a 2-mm-thick segment from both apical and mid-thirds of the root using a precision saw. These slices were subsequently prepared for evaluating the push-out bond value by smoothing along with standardizing slice surfaces to ensure accurate measurements.

The push-out bond value was measured employing a universal force analyzing machine. Compressive weight was applied at a consistent pace; the test continued until the sealer—Gutta Percha—dentin interface failed. The requisite force to facilitate the dislocation of the root canal filling material from the dentin slices was documented. Data were collected and statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA to determine notable variations between the different sealer groupings. Tukey’s post hoc tests were subsequently employed to identify specific group differences, thereby providing detailed insights into the comparative bond strengths of the different sealers used in the study.

DISCUSSION

The two main elements that increase an endodontically treated tooth’s resistance to fracture are stable adherence to root canal dentin and an elastic modulus resembling dentin. The implications of this study are particularly relevant for enhancing the longevity and success of endodontic treatments. Sealers with higher bond strengths can reduce the chances of microleakage, thereby minimizing the risks of postoperative infections and treatment failures.[34]

The outcome of this investigation shed important light on the adhesive properties of contemporary endodontic sealers. Bioceramic sealer exhibited the maximum push-out bond value to root dentin, markedly outperforming the other groups. This superior performance is likely attributed to the unique properties of bioceramic materials, which include excellent biocompatibility, minimal shrinkage, and the ability to form hydroxyapatite upon setting. These features enhance the sealer’s interaction with the dentinal walls, creating a superior bond.[56]

The methacrylate-based sealer also showed commendable bond strength, possibly due to its ability to infiltrate and polymerize within the lateral dentinal tubules, thus forming a micromechanical interlock.[67] The epoxy resin-based sealer, while not as effective as the bioceramic or methacrylate sealers, still demonstrated a respectable bond. Epoxy resins are known for their long-term dimensional stability and adhesive properties, factors that likely contribute to their relatively strong performance in push-out tests.[8]

Conversely, the ZOE sealer displayed the lowest bond strength among the tested sealers. ZOE sealers, despite their long history of use in endodontics, are known for their brittleness and potential to microleak due to their solubility over time. This result underscores the limitations of ZOE-based sealers in achieving a robust bond with dentin when compared to newer sealers with advanced formulations. The study reinforces the significance of these differences, highlighting the need for clinicians to consider these variations in bond strength when selecting a sealer for root canal therapy.[9]

Furthermore, the findings advocate for the adoption of bioceramic sealers in clinical practice, leveraging their superior performance to improve treatment outcomes. However, it is also important to consider other factors such as handling properties, cost, and ease of removal during retreatment when choosing a sealer. Future research could delve into the long-term performance and clinical outcomes associated with these sealers to offer a more comprehensive evaluation.

CONCLUSION

The study concludes within its constraints that a better middle and apical third seal can be achieved by using bioceramic sealer instead of ZOE-based sealer.
