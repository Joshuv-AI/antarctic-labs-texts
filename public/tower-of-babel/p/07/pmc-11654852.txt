Agreement between resting heart rate measured by unattended automated office and office blood pressure measurement, ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, or electrocardiography
Authors: Piotr Sobieraj, Mateusz Leśniewski, Agnieszka Sawicka, Maciej Siński, Jacek Lewandowski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe application of unattended blood pressure measurement (uAOBPM) for resting heart rate (RHR) assessment is unknown. To assess the agreement between RHR measured during uAOBPM and other methods, the authors conducted a comparability study with office blood pressure measurement (OBPM), ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), and electrocardiogram (ECG) in a group of 110 participants referred for ABPM. RHR measured with uAOBPM (70.8 ± 12.5 bpm) was significantly lower than OBPM (72.8 ± 12.6 bpm) but higher than measured by 24 h ABPM (67.5 ± 10.2 bpm). There was no significant difference was found between RHR measured by uAOBPM and daytime ABPM (70.3 ± 11.2 bpm) or ECG (69.1 ± 11.6 bpm). Using Bland‐Altman statistics, the authors discovered a small difference in agreement between RHR measured by uAOBPM and daytime ABPM (bias: 0.4 with 95% confidence interval: ‐0.8 to 1.6 bpm), with a poorer agreement with OBPM (bias ‐2 with 95% confidence interval: ‐2.8 to ‐1.3 bpm) and ECG (bias 1.6 with 95% confidence interval: 0.5 to 2.7 bpm). The authors found significant agreement between uAOBPM and ECG in identifying subjects with RHR > 80 bpm OBPM, with Cohen's kappa coefficients of 0.783 and 0.671, respectively. Their findings indicate that RHR measured with uAOBPM remains in acceptable agreement with OBPM, ABPM, and ECG, the best agreement obtained with RHR from daytime ABPM.Keywords: ambulatory blood pressure/home blood pressure monitoring, hypertension—general, risk assessment, treatment and diagnosis/guidelines

INTRODUCTION

Resting heart rate (RHR) is a fundamental and frequently measured hemodynamic parameter in daily clinical practice. There is no single method for measuring RHR, though it can be obtained in multiple ways, the most common is auscultation of the heart or pulse measurement. However, even simple medical procedures, such as measuring heart rate during pulse palpation, can result in bias between different methods of RHR measurement. 1 It has been demonstrated that the accuracy of pulse measurement can depend on the counting method. For example, pulse measurement may differ more from electrocardiography (ECG) when the first pulse wave is counted as absent than when it is included. 2

ECG measures RHR and is used as a reference method in multiple trials. 3 , 4 However, using electrocardiography solely to determine heart rate appears disproportionate. Currently, new methods of RHR or even ECG measurement are being developed, including wearable devices 5 ; however, another simple method to record pulse rate as a surrogate of RHR remains BP (BP) measurement, as recommended by the European Society of Hypertension. 6 Recently, it was also demonstrated that the oscillometric technique provided accurate HR measurements during rest and mild exercise. 7

Clinical trials demonstrated that RHR can be used to predict adverse cardiovascular events. In these trials, RHR was measured during office (OBPM), home BP monitoring (HBPM), and ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM). 8 , 9 , 10 In hypertensive patients, proper assessment of the RHR can help identify those with an RHR greater than 80 bpm and may then suggest the use of RHR‐lowering drugs. 11 The detection of RHR > 70 bpm in patients with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction may also influence treatment decisions. 12

Aside from differences in BP measurements, the white coat effect may be associated with different results of RHR measurement during OBPM. 13 Unattended automated office BP measurement (uAOBPM) is becoming a popular method of BP measurement. 14 It may be considered a compromise between OBPM and ABPM methods because the results are available immediately and are less susceptible to the white coat effect. uAOBPM is based on an automated BP sphygmomanometer programmed to take three consecutive measurements, ensuring a rest period without the presence of medical personnel. The result of uAOBPM is the average of these measurements, which are calculated separately for systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) BP or RHR. However, uAOBPM has not yet been assessed as a tool for measuring RHR.

The study sought to determine the differences in heart rate measured with uAOBPM and OBPM. In addition, RHR measurements obtained via ABPM and ECG were compared.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study group description

The study was designed as a prospective observational study to compare various methods of RHR measurements. We enrolled subjects referred to conduct ABPM measurements in our department. Enrollment criteria included being between the ages of 18 and 100, as well as the ability to provide informed consent for participation in the study.

Subjects with permanent atrial fibrillation or flutter during study procedures, chronic kidney disease during dialysis therapy, or dialysis fistula, as well as carotid, lower extremity, or renal artery atherosclerosis disease, were excluded. The study's participants were enrolled in the Department of Internal Medicine, Hypertension and Vascular Disease, Medical University of Warsaw.

Heart rate measuring methods

Heart rate assessed by BP measurement

General rules

Medical staff took BP according to guidelines, ensuring proper cuff size and placement, a fasting period, a lack of physical activity, and the appropriate patient position. 15 All measurements were taken on the same day in the morning in the following order: ECG tracing, OBPM, uAOBPM, and then 24h‐ABPM began.

Ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM)

We measured ABPM using the Spacelab Ontrak 90227 device (Spacelabs Healthcare, Snoqualmie, USA). ABPM monitoring lasted approximately 24 h and was initiated in the morning. The daytime period was set from 6:00 a.m. to 10:00 p.m., and the night rest interval was defined from 10:00 p.m. to 6:00 a.m. Similarly, mean values of heart rate were measured. ABPM reports with more than a 70% error rate were excluded from the analysis. 16 In this paper, SBP/DBP ABPM 24 h refers to the average SBP/DBP recorded over the entire ABPM registration period. The terms dABPM and nABPM refer to the values obtained during daytime and nighttime rest intervals, respectively.

Office BP measurement (OBPM)

The subjects rested for 5 min before the BP measurement. The last two of three measurements, taken at 1‐min intervals with the WATCH BP Office ABI device (Microlife Corporation, Taipei, Taiwan), were used. The OBPM SBP/DBP was calculated as the average of these measurements. OBPM RHR was calculated using the average heart rate value displayed by the measuring device.

Unattended automated office BP measurement (uAOBPM)

WATCH BP office ABI (Microlife Corporation, Taipei, Taiwan) device was set to ensure 5 min of rest before measurements. More than three measurements were taken at 1‐min‐long intervals. From the start of the rest period until the end of the last measurement, the subjects remained alone in the room. The device calculated uAOBPM SBP, DBP, and HR using the mean of these three measurements.

Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Standard 12‐lead ECG tracing was recorded by an experienced technician using the EDAN SE‐1201 (Edan Instruments, Inc, Shenzhen, China) for 30 s on paper at 25 mm/s speed and the standard gain setting (10 mm). The ECG was performed while lying down. Standard 12‐lead ECG electrodes were positioned according to the American Heart Association guidelines. 17 The locations consisted of the right arm, left arm, right leg, left leg, and six precordial positions. RHR was calculated by a cardiologist as the average heart rate of 5 consecutive sinus beats using the distance between QRS complexes (using a magnifying lens with an accuracy of 40 msec) and rounded to the nearest whole number.

Statistical methods

The agreement between RHR measured with uAOBPM and OBPM was chosen as the study's primary endpoint. Comparisons with ABPM (including 24‐h, daytime, and night rest periods) and ECG were conducted. In fact, this is the first study to compare RHR uAOBPM measurement with other methods, so the agreement between SBP/DBP measured using different methods was also evaluated to ensure results comparability.

We calculated the required number of participants using power‐curve simulation for the Bland‐Altman analysis, assuming a range of 1−500 participants and a step of 1. We expected the difference between methods (uAOBPM and OBPM) to be 0 bpm with a standard deviation of 2 bpm, assuming a compliance level of 0.95 and a confidence interval of 0.95. Then, the point closest to the power of 0.8 was chosen on the power curve; this corresponded to N = 108. As a result, 110 participants were planned to be included in the study.

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations. Discrete variables were presented as numbers and percentages. To compare RHR values obtained using different methods, the difference between each pair of RHR measurements was calculated and the two‐way Student t‐test was performed. The Bonferroni correction was used to account for multiple comparisons. Results of statistical tests with p‐values less than .05 were considered significant. Pearson correlation coefficient was also used to assess the relationship between RHR measurements obtained using various methods.

The agreement between various methods of RHR measurement was assessed using Bland‐Altman statistics. The results are presented as a graph. Because there is no threshold for acceptable differences between RHR measurements obtained using different methods, the results were reported as a bias with lower (LLoA) and upper limit of agreement (ULoA). Bias, LLoA, and ULoA were reported with a 95% confidence interval. 18

We tested the accuracy of identifying subjects with RHR greater than 70 bpm and 80 bpm using uAOBPM versus OBPM with Cohen's kappa coefficient. 12 , 15

To better understand the factors that influence the difference in RHR measured by uAOBPM and OBPM, a multivariable linear regression model was developed. The model was chosen using an exhaustive method that included age, gender, body mass index, use of antihypertensive drugs (including beta‐blockers), history of hypertension, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, stroke, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and smoking as independent variables. Out of all the available models, we chose the model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion.

All computations and graphics were created using the statistical programming language R version 4.1.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The study was approved by the Bioethical Committee at the Medical University of Warsaw. All subjects gave informed written consent to participate in the study.

RESULTS

Most of the subjects received drugs (N = 98, 89.1% used any drug); the most common medications were beta‐blockers (51.8%), ACEI (41.8%), and calcium channel blockers (37.3%). Detailed characteristics of the study group are presented in Table 1.

Correlation analysis

RHR is measured using uAOBPM. HR was highly correlated with results obtained using other methods of BP measurement, as well as ECG. The highest correlation coefficient was found when uAOBPM was compared to OBPM. Regardless of the methods of RHR measurement used, generally high Pearson correlation coefficients were found (Figure 2). All correlations remained significant following adjustments made using the Bonferroni method.

Identifying subjects with RHR > 70 bpm or 80 bpm

Using OBPM as a reference, we calculated Cohen's kappa coefficients for identifying subjects with RHR > 70 bpm in uAOBPM and ECG. In both cases, we observed significant agreement with kappa values of 0.763 (p < .001) and 0.658 (p < .001), respectively. The agreement in identifying subjects with RHR > 80 bpm was slightly better with kappa coefficient values of 0.783 (p < .001) and 0.671 (p < .001), respectively.

DISCUSSION

Our study's main finding is that there is a small but significant difference in RHR measured using uAOBPM and OBPM, with the best agreement found between uAOBPM and dABPM. To date, no studies have compared RHR measured by uAOBPM in adults to other, more widely used BP measurement methods. Our data fills this gap, though we can only compare our findings to one study involving school‐aged children (mean age 13.7 years). In the trial, Seeman and colleagues found that RHR from uAOBPM was higher by 3.4 bpm with 95% CI from −2.4 to +9.4 bpm than dABPM, but the difference was nonsignificant (p = .63). 19 In our study, RHR values obtained using other methods of measurements (excluding nABPM) differed only slightly from those obtained during uAOBPM, which is unlikely to have an impact on clinical practice. However, there are at least several explanations for the disparity between RHR measured by uAOBPM and that assessed by other methods, particularly OBPM. It is thought that BP measurement with uAOBPM is less dependent on the white coat effect, like HBPM or ABPM. 20 The phenomenon of increased BP and HR in the presence of medical personnel, as well as the associated sympathetic activity, is well understood. 21 , 22 Furthermore, there is an explanation that includes sympathetic overactivity, demonstrating how the presence of an observer affects HR, SBP, and DBP. Grassi and colleagues demonstrated that during unattended AOBPM sessions, SBP, DBP, and RHR were lower than during attended sessions, indicating sympathetic overactivity. 22 These findings were confirmed in a previous study comparing RHR measured with ABPM to mercury sphygmomanometer (OBPM) in elderly subjects with sinus rhythm. 23 The authors found a bias of 2.8 ± 7.2 bpm According to the above explanations, we could expect a similar difference between RHR in uAOBPM (unattended) and ECG (attended), but no difference between the methods was found. In a previous validation study of an HBPM device, good RHR agreement was observed during deflation of the device cuff with simultaneous ECG recording in subjects with sinus rhythm (ECG 75 ± 20 vs. OBPM 75 ± 19 beats per min). 24 However, in our study, the lack of difference could be attributed to a decrease in RHR in the lying position (ECG) versus sitting (uAOBPM). 25

We discovered a satisfactory agreement between HR measured using uAOBPM and 24h‐ABPM or dABPM. However, RHR measured during 24h‐ABPM or dABPM is not truly resting, unlike nABPM. Interestingly HR recorded during nABPM is known to have a higher prognostic value than daytime RHR in ABPM. 8 Unfortunately, the largest observed difference was between RHR measured by uAOBPM and nABPM. According to our findings, RHR measured using uAOBPM is not interchangeable with RHR measured during nABPM. Similarly, there were differences in SBP/DBP measured by uAOBPM versus nABPM, which should be explained by normal diurnal variations in blood pressure and heart rate. 26 In a comparative study, RHR recorded during casual ECG showed poor agreement and correlation with Holter ECG recordings. 27 In another trial comparing heart rate measured by 24‐h ABPM with 24h‐Holter ECG monitoring, only small differences were found in mean HR (0.55 with 95% CI: 0.37 to 0.73, p < .001 for 24 h monitoring). The findings of these trials suggest that the difference between BP recordings taken at different times of day and resting ECG is due to changes in activities rather than differences in methods. Our findings on the agreement of SBP/DBP values measured by uAOBPM and ABPM are consistent with previous findings. 28 We found that SBP measured with uAOBPM is slightly lower than measured with dABPM. Similarly, a small difference was discovered in terms of DBP. There was less agreement between uAOBPM values and OBPM values for SBP but not DBP. Surprisingly, uAOBPM showed less bias when compared to 24 h‐ABPM SPB and DBP than dABPM values.

Despite numerous studies demonstrating the negative impact of increased RHR on cardiovascular risk, there is a lack of prospective studies assessing the impact of RHR measured using uAOBPM. 10 , 29 , 30 , 31

In a more recent subanalysis of the SBP Intervention Trial, we found that elevated RHR in hypertensive subjects at high cardiovascular risk is associated with an increase in risk. 32 In this post‐hoc analysis, RHR was measured using uAOBPM.

According to current hypertension treatment guidelines, people with hypertension and an elevated RHR are more likely to develop cardiovascular disease. However, there is no recommendation for how RHR should be measured. Most of the studies cited in the guidelines used ECG to calculate RHR. 3 , 4 , 33 Only in the International VErapamil‐SR/trandolapril study (INVEST) was RHR measured during the BP assessment. 30

In our study, we found that uAOBPM had only moderate agreement in identifying subjects with RHR > 70 or > 80 bpm when compared to ECG. This finding may limit the use of RHR measured in uAOBPM to identify high‐risk individuals. However, the evidence supporting the use of 80 bpm as a threshold value for increased risk is limited. For example, in the Framingham Heart study, the authors did not specify the threshold value associated with increased risk. 3 , 4 In contrast, the authors of the INVEST trial found an increased cardiovascular risk at RHR > 75 bpm 30 In contrast, data from a trial using RHR measured by HBPM revealed an increased risk at lower RHR values (70 bpm). 34 In our post‐hoc analysis, we discovered that RHR > 70 bpm measured by uAOBPM was associated with increased cardiovascular risk. 32 However, given the current study's findings, which show an error of less than 10 bpm between uAOBPM and other methods of measuring RHR, we speculate that previous studies assessing the effect of RHR on cardiovascular events have overlooked an increase in risk at values just above 70 bpm

Our study has a few limitations. First, we were unable to record uAOBPM and trace ECG data simultaneously. Because the effect of performing these procedures concurrently on cardiovascular indexes is unknown, we chose to perform them sequentially. It would be interesting to supplement the results with HBPM, but we were unable to provide identical devices to all study participants.

In conclusion, we discovered only minor differences between RHR measured using uAOBPM or other BP measurement methods and ECG. uAOBPM generally underestimated RHR compared to OBPM and overestimated it compared to ECG. However, in clinical settings, uAOBPM can be used interchangeably with OBPM and ECG to assess patients’ RHR.
