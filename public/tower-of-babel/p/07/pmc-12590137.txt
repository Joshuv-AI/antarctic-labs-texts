Bioinformatics and experimental validation of MAPK pathway activation in diabetic kidney disease induced by glucose variability
Authors: Sujuan Li, Huijuan Wang, Yuanhang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis article aims to evaluate the role of factors associated with oxidative stress induction in fluctuating hyperglycemia-induced diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of DKD.MethodsWe downloaded the gene expression datasets GSE30528 and GSE30529, which are associated with diabetic kidney disease (DKD), from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and selected the top four proteins from protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks using STRING and Cytoscape software. Concurrently, we conducted diabetic animal experiments to verify that these proteins contribute to the onset and progression of DKD through oxidative stress. Finally, we confirmed the correlation and roles of these genes in the animal model.ResultThe modulation of the MAPK cascade involves ITGB2 and TYROBP. The results showed that the areas under the ROC curve (AUC) for TYROBP were 0.889 and 0.966, respectively, while for ITGB2, they were 0.906 and 0.974. All other indicators had an AUC of 1.0; these indicators represent differentially expressed genes identified through data analysis. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), as well as the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), were measured in diabetic rats (P < 0.05). Apoptotic responses and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) activation were evident in the rat fluctuation group. Further research has demonstrated that oxidative stress regulates the MAPK pathway at both cellular and molecular levels. Gene and protein expression, particularly in the context of diabetic nephropathy, are influenced by multiple factors.ConclusionCollectively, fluctuating hyperglycemia directly and indirectly regulates MAPK-JNK signaling via ITGB2, TYROBP, and oxidative stress, ultimately influencing the onset and progression of diabetic nephropathy.

Introduction

The prognosis of diabetes mellitus (DM) is closely linked to the occurrence and progression of chronic complications.[1], [2] Additionally, greater fluctuations in blood glucose levels among diabetic patients are associated with a higher incidence of chronic complications and a poorer overall prognosis.

Glucose fluctuations represent a novel potential risk factor for diabetes and its associated complications.Compass has two key aspects: the degree of blood glucose variability and the time interval during which it is measured.Fluctuations in blood glucose levels have been identified as critical factors influencing diabetic complications.3 Evidence suggests that these fluctuations may be more detrimental than persistent hyperglycemia in increasing the risk of developing such complications.4 Moreover, studies indicate that oxidative stress induced by glucose variability is more severe than that caused by sustained hyperglycemic exposure.[5], [6] Blood glucose fluctuations have also been shown to accelerate kidney injury in diabetic rats.7 Although our previous research demonstrated that fluctuating hyperglycemia causes more severe oxidative damage than stable hyperglycemia in diabetic rats, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.[8], [9] In this study, we evaluated the effects of fluctuating hyperglycemia on renal tubular epithelial cells in rats to further elucidate the potential injury caused by glucose variability.5

Network analysis offers a systematic framework for elucidating disease interactions by delineating compound-protein/gene-disease networks. By examining these networks, researchers can discern the modulatory effects of compounds, proteins, or genes on diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and predict their impact on key targets and associated pathways. Additionally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, and the top four proteins from protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were selected using STRING and Cytoscape software for precise quantification. This analytical approach is further supported by in vivo studies using murine models, which validate the functional roles of these genes through various assays. The relationship between DEGs and the MAPK pathway was elucidated using WikiPathways software (https://www.wikipathways.org/search.html). Furthermore, the association between these genes and oxidative stress markers was analyzed, and the involvement of the apoptosis pathway was investigated through Western blotting and electron microscopy analyses of the MAPK pathway. This integrative methodology provides substantial empirical insights into the therapeutic potential of DEGs in DKD management, facilitating the development of multi-target therapeutic strategies.

Moreover, a fluctuating diabetic rat model was established to investigate the effects of hyperglycemia on renal health. Sujuan Li et al. reported that interleukin-8 (IL-8), a prototypical member of the chemokine CXC superfamily, induces chemotaxis of neutrophils and monocytes, promotes degranulation, increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and triggers inflammatory responses.[10], [11], [12] ROS stimulates smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation, resulting in enhanced coagulation capacity of endothelial cells.13 Abnormal hemodynamics stimulate the release of vascular endothelial active factors, ultimately leading to increased vascular permeability, leakage of large amounts of glomerular protein, and proteinuria.[14], [15] The presence of proteinuria promotes abnormal proliferation of mesangial cells, drives glomerular membrane hypertrophy, and leads to the deposition of collagen IV and fibronectin, contributing to glomerulosclerosis and exacerbating the progression of diabetic nephropathy.[16], [17] Furthermore, active factors released during oxidative stress are involved in the onset and development of diabetic kidney disease (DKD).18 We have previously reviewed extensive literature and found that oxidative stress also plays a significant role in other vascular diseases.19 Years of research have demonstrated that fluctuating hyperglycemia-induced ROS activates the MAPK-JNK signaling pathway, and this regulatory mechanism can be elucidated at both the gene and protein levels.[20], [21] First, using PCR amplification technology, it was found that the MAPK-JNK gene was abnormally overexpressed in the kidney tissues of animal models with fluctuating hyperglycemia compared to those with relatively stable hyperglycemia.[22], [23], [24] Concurrently, analysis of the related proteins regulated by these genes revealed increased expression of JNK and phosphorylated JNK, as well as a significant increase in p53 protein.[25], [26] In this study, we examined the roles of MAPK-JNK signaling and apoptosis in the pathogenesis of DKD, paving the way for innovative treatment modalities targeting these pathways in DKD.

Data collection

We obtained gene expression profiles from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

To identify differences between the diabetic kidney disease (DKD) group and the control group, we utilized R packages27,28 to standardize the datasets and detect differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GSE30528 and GSE30529. Genes meeting the screening criteria of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were selected as DEGs. Volcano plots generated using these packages illustrated the differential expression. Additionally, heatmaps were created to display the top 74 DEGs with the highest and lowest expression levels. Finally, significantly overlapping DEGs between GSE30528 and GSE30529 were identified through Venn diagram analysis.

Functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the potential functions of DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using the appropriate software package29. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered indicative of significant enrichment. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package and the curated gene set “c2.cp.v7.2.symbols.gmt,” with the reference gene set defined accordingly. Terms with an adjusted p-value below 0.05 and a false discovery rate (FDR) under 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Protein-protein interaction network generation and subnetwork analysis

The STRING database (version 12.0; https://string-db.org/) was utilized to integrate the DEG-encoded proteins and their interactions, enabling a comprehensive characterization of the query proteins. Multiple proteins were searched by name or identifier within the Homo sapiens organism. The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) for visualization. CytoHubba, a Cytoscape plugin, was employed to calculate the degree of each protein node within the PPI network. The top 10 hub nodes were analyzed using MCC, MNC, EPC, Degree, and Stress algorithms, and a protein interaction map was constructed. Genes with the top 17 node degrees were identified as hub genes. Among these, four genes—CD48, ITGB2, TYROBP, and IL10RA—were selected. The GeneCards Suite was used to retrieve genes associated with oxidative stress and apoptosis. The 9018 encoded proteins were compared with the proteins we validated, including the aforementioned four genes.

Exploration and validation of transcription factors in animal studies

Animals

Thirty-two adult male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, weighing between 180 and 220 g, were obtained from the Animal Laboratory of Wenzhou Medical University at 6 weeks of age. They were housed in polycarbonate cages with wood chip bedding. All procedures involving the animals complied with the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Wenzhou Medical University (approval number wydw2022-0738). The SD rats were trained to consume chow and drinking water within a single day. Food intake, water intake, and urine output were measured twice weekly, and relevant parameters were recorded. The food and water provided for one day were weighed before consumption, and the remaining food was weighed again the following morning. The difference between these two measurements was documented. Urine output was estimated by collecting the total volume of urine produced by each rat over the course of one day and calculating the output based on that value.

Model preparation and groupings

The rats were randomly assigned to two groups, each consisting of 32 rats (n = 32). Group 1, the normal control group, included eight nondiabetic rats (n = 8) that received only a vehicle and standard rat chow. Group 2, the diabetic control group, comprised 24 rats administered streptozotocin (n = 24). After successful induction of diabetes in Group 2, these rats were further divided into three subgroups.subgroups, each containing eight animals: the stable glucose (D) group, the fluctuating glucose (F) group, and the insulin (I) group. Diabetic Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were intraperitoneally injected with 65 mg/kg of streptozotocin (STZ), while rats in the normal control group received an equivalent volume of sodium citrate buffer. After 72 h, blood was collected from the tail to measure fasting glucose levels. Bladder was stimulated to assess urine glucose levels. A successful model was established if the blood glucose level exceeded 16.7 mmol/L and urine glucose tested positive (+++). Rats in the F group received subcutaneous abdominal injections of 3-8 units of regular insulin three times daily. The first injection was administered at 7:30 AM, the second at 11:30 AM, and the third at 3:30 PM. Additionally, the rats were intraperitoneally injected with glucose at a dose of 2.5 g/kg three times per day, with each injection volume being 1 ml. To establish the glucose fluctuation model, the first glucose injection occurred at 10:00 AM, the second at 2:00 PM, and the third at 6:00 PM. Rats in the F group received insulin daily, while rats in the I group were given the same dose of insulin, and the remaining SD rats received an equivalent volume of normal saline. When the F group rats were administered glucose, the other three groups received the same volume of normal saline. The daily blood glucose fluctuation pattern remained consistent over 12 weeks in the F group, and there was no significant difference in HbA1c levels between the D group and the F group, indicating successful modeling of fluctuating hyperglycemia. After 12 weeks, blood was collected from the abdominal aorta for biochemical analysis, and the protein and gene expression of JNK in renal tissue were examined.

Sample collection

At week 12, the rats in each group were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 3 % pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 40 mg/kg. Following successful anesthesia, the rats were positioned supine. Surgical instruments, including scissors and vascular forceps, were used to isolate the internal jugular vein. Blood was drawn from the internal jugular vein using a blood collection needle and a 5 ml vacuum blood collection tube. After allowing the collection tube to stand undisturbed for thirty minutes, the serum was centrifuged and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis of serum and other indicators. The anticoagulant tube was directly frozen at −80 °C for hemoglobin A1c measurement. The hair on the rats' abdomens was removed using a depilatory device, and a 3–4 cm incision was made in the mid-abdomen. The perirenal tissue was carefully separated to expose both kidneys. The renal pedicles were dissected from the kidneys, which were then removed and rinsed with normal saline; this procedure was performed as quickly as possible to preserve kidney tissue integrity. One kidney was immersed in 10 % paraformaldehyde for morphological observation, while half of the complete hippocampal tissue was placed in a cryotube, immediately stored in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently transferred to a −80 °C freezer for Western blotting (WB) and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR).

Measurement of blood glucose, urine glucose and HbA1c levels

Blood samples were routinely centrifuged at 2500 × g for 15 min. Urine glucose levels were measured using a Johnson Steady magnifying blood glucose meter and urine glucose test strips. Whole blood collected in EDTA tubes was used to assess glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, which were measured by affinity chromatography and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a D-10® automated analyzer (Bio-Rad).

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px)

MDA and SOD levels were measured to assess oxidative stress in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. MDA concentrations were determined using a thiobarbituric acid (TBA) assay with an MDA measurement kit (Jian Cheng Institute of Biotechnology, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. SOD activity was evaluated using an SOD activity assay kit (BioVision, San Francisco, CA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Additionally, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity was measured using the Sigma CGP1 GSH-Px cellular activity assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich).

Renal tubular epithelial cell apoptosis

The TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (Hoffmann-La Roche) was used to measure nuclear DNA fragmentation in cells during the early stages of apoptosis. The apoptosis index (AI) was determined by TUNEL staining. Five fields of view per section were examined at 400× magnification, with more than 200 positive cells counted in the glomeruli and renal tubule cells in each field. The number of positive cells in each field was used to calculate the AI, standardizing the total number of cells per field to 100. The data were then organized and subjected to statistical analysis.

Observation of renal morphology

Microscopic observation: Renal tissues were collected, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sectioned with conventional paraffin (thickness: 5 mm), stained with haematoxylin and eosin (HE), observed under a microscope and photographed.

Ultrastructural observation: kidneytissue blocks of 0.5 ∼ 1 mm3in size were prepared with a cold blade and quickly fixed with cold 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution. After the tissues were fixed with 1 % OsO4, the tissues were dehydrated with an acetone gradient, soaked with epoxy resin, embedded, sectioned, and stained with uranium acetate and lead citrate. Then, the sections were observed under a transmission electron microscope.

PCR and Western blotting

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used to isolate total RNA. After extraction. RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). The resulting cDNA was then used with the SYBR® Premix DimerEraser Kit (TaKaRa) for quantitative real-time PCR on an ABI PRISM 7500 RT-PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The specific primers used were as follows: JNK: 5′-AAC GAC CTT CTA CGA CGA TG-3′ and 5′-GCAGCG TAT TCT GGC TAT GC-3′; β-actin primers: 5′-GAG ACC TTC AAC ACC CCA GCC-3′ and 5′-TCG GGG GAT CGG AAC CGC TCA-3′. The levels of JNK, Bax, and Bcl-2 were assessed by Western blotting. All samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and then transferred onto membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5 % powdered milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05 % Tween 20 at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were probed with primary antibodies against JNK, Bax, Bcl-2, and β-actin (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 4 °C overnight. This was followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at room temperature for 2 h after washing three times. Enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) detection systems were then used to visualize the protein bands.

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Data were analyzed using SPSS software, version 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted.Variance (ANOVA) was used in conjunction with Tukey’s multiple comparison tests. This approach is primarily employed to compare single and multiple factors affecting the number of oxidative stress indicators and apoptosis in animal models. Additionally, p-JNK mRNA levels were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Spearman’s test was applied to perform correlation analysis, a method commonly used in bioinformatics to examine gene correlations. Differences with p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software.

Protein–protein interaction network generation and subnetwork analysis

We constructed the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using a combined score threshold greater than 0.4. As shown in Fig. 4A, the PPI network comprised 72 nodes and 377 edges. Using the MCODE plug-in algorithm, we identified five highly connected subnetworks containing 61 nodes and 337 edges. The PPI network was graphically visualized with Cytoscape software (Fig. 4A–F). Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis revealed that the genes within these subnetworks were primarily enriched in the MAPK-ERK1/2 signaling pathway, regulation of reactive oxygen species metabolic processes, regulation of apoptotic cell clearance, the external side of the plasma membrane, and cytokine receptor activity (Fig. 5A, B). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these genes were mainly enriched in cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, complement pathways, and cell adhesion molecules (Fig. 5C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that the significantly enriched pathways were associated with diabetic kidney disease (DKD), MAPK signaling, and apoptotic p53 pathways (Fig. 6A–C). Identification and analysis of hub genes

Based on the overlapping parameters of the top 17 genes identified by five algorithms, we pinpointed 17 hub genes: CD52, CD53, CD48, ITGB2, IRF8, TYROBP, LY86, HLA-DPA1, C1QA, IL10RA, CCL5, CD3D, CD2, C3, CD44, FCER1A, and CCL19. This identification was achieved through a combination of fold-change chord diagrams and grouped radiative bar diagrams (see Fig. 6D–H). We validated the co-expression of these 17 genes and examined their relationship with apoptosis-related genes in diabetic kidney disease (DKD), focusing specifically on CCL19, CD44, and CCL5. Discussion

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) has emerged as a critical and complex global health challenge. In this study, we demonstrated that elevated HbA1c levels and fluctuating high glucose concentrations significantly increase the risk of chronic complications24. Furthermore, we developed a rat model of fluctuating hyperglycemia to investigate the pathogenesis of DKD in greater detail.25

The fluctuation model was successfully established, demonstrating that blood glucose fluctuations modulate oxidative stress and damage associated with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and trigger a corresponding increase in biomarkers of diabetes mellitus (DM). Based on relevant literature[3], [25], [26], previous researchers' experience, observed daily physiological blood glucose fluctuation patterns in pre-experimental diabetic rats, and laboratory protocols, a regimen was developed involving intraperitoneal glucose injection (2.5 g/kg) three times daily combined with subcutaneous insulin injection (4 U). Blood glucose levels were measured 30 min after insulin administration. Intraperitoneal glucose injection rapidly elevated blood glucose levels in diabetic rats, followed by a rapid decrease due to subcutaneous insulin injection, resulting in brief but significant fluctuations in blood glucose. This protocol induced more than two substantial fluctuations in blood glucose levels within a single day in diabetic ratsThe primary issue encountered in our animal experiments is the high mortality rate among rats in the fluctuating hyperglycemia group, which can be attributed to several factors: 1. Diabetic nephropathy, a chronic kidney disease caused by diabetes, is the leading cause of end-stage renal failure and mortality in diabetic patients.27 A correlation between blood glucose metabolism disorders and immune cell activity in patients with diabetic nephropathy has been established. The results of this study demonstrated that increased blood glucose fluctuations were associated with a concomitant rise in biomarkers of diabetic kidney disease (DKD), including SOD, GSH-Px, MDA, and JNK. This finding aligns with patterns observed in both human DKD and rat models34. Notably, rats in the F group showed significant recovery from body weight loss and lethargy beginning at the twelfth week, with these improvements persisting until the end of the experiment (Table 1 and Fig. 1A, B). Levels of urinary microalbumin, urinary protein, urinary creatinine, and urea nitrogen were all elevated in the F group (Table 2). Fluctuating hyperglycemia was associated with a marked increase in urinary protein, indicating substantial damage to the glomeruli and renal tubules, impairing protein filtration and reabsorption. This damage corresponded to glomerular atrophy, podocyte injury, renal tubular apoptosis, lumen enlargement, congestion, and other morphological changes observed in this study, consistent with previous literature.35 Due to the metabolic disturbances inherent in diabetes, kidney damage leads to protein loss, pronounced emaciation, lethargy, and other symptoms, as demonstrated in this study.

Exposure to oxidative stress has been shown to induce apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells through the activation of MAPK, which subsequently leads to the upregulation of Bax and the downregulation of Bcl-2 (Fig. 4A, B). Previous studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress activates JNK, resulting in apoptosis[36], [37]. Building on these findings, we further investigated the relationships among MAPK, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. We found that JNK mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in the F group, and the protein levels of phosphorylated JNK in kidney tissue were elevated in the F group[38], [39], [40], [41] (Fig. 7A, B). Conclusions

In summary, the present study identified that the interaction between fluctuating hyperglycemia and oxidative stress influences apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells in diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Our results further revealed that oxidative stress regulates the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway at both the gene and protein levels. Through integrated bioinformatics analysis, we found that the onset of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is closely associated with the apoptosis signaling pathway mediated by mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and oxidative stress, offering a new avenue for exploring the mechanisms and treatment of DKD.
