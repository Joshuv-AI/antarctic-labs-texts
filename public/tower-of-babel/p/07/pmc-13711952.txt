Clinical spectrum and ascertainment pathways in pediatric female dystrophinopathy
Authors: Hui Jiao, Jinli Bai, Ping Li, Jing Sun, Dongqing Li, Jianzhao Zhang, Yujin Qu, Xiaoyin Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric female dystrophinopathy may be asymptomatic at evaluation or may show skeletal muscle and cardiac manifestations, but childhood presentations are often subtle. We aimed to characterize the clinical spectrum, ascertainment pathways, subclinical muscle involvement, and inheritance patterns in this population.MethodsThis single-center retrospective case series was conducted between January 2024 and December 2025 and included 35 girls with genetically confirmed DMD gene variants identified from existing clinical records. Primary ascertainment route was recorded separately from cross-sectional clinical status at evaluation and classified as symptom-driven presentation, incidental or unexplained hyperCKemia, family-based cascade screening, or incidental genomic discovery. Clinical status at evaluation was assigned after systematic clinical review with reference to published female dystrophinopathy classifications. Statistical analyses were exploratory.ResultsThe median age was 5 years (range, 1–10). Primary ascertainment routes were symptom-driven presentation in 8/35 (22.9%), incidental or unexplained hyperCKemia in 18/35 (51.4%), family-based cascade screening in 8/35 (22.9%), and incidental genomic discovery in 1/35 (2.9%). After systematic clinical review, clinical status at evaluation was classified as asymptomatic in 29 participants, BMD-like in 1, DMD-like in 3, and cardiac-symptom/CMR observations in 2. All 18 girls initially identified through incidental or unexplained hyperCKemia were classified as asymptomatic because no muscle weakness was documented on neurological examination, although some had exercise intolerance, easy fatigue, myalgia, or cramps. EMG abnormalities were identified in 14/34 participants, including definite myopathic findings in 5 and subtle short-duration motor-unit-potential changes in 9. Variants were apparently de novo in 15 cases, maternally inherited in 18, and paternally inherited in 2, including one case inherited from a father with low-level mosaicism in peripheral blood.ConclusionsPediatric female dystrophinopathy shows a broad childhood spectrum and may be recognized before objective muscle weakness is documented. Separating ascertainment route from clinical status highlights recurrent or persistent hyperCKemia and mild exercise-related complaints as practical entry points for targeted neuromuscular assessment and longitudinal follow-up. Earlier recognition in childhood may support timely neuromuscular and cardiac surveillance, parental evaluation when appropriate, family-based genetic counseling, and future reproductive planning.

Background

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) are X-linked dystrophinopathies caused by pathogenic variants in the DMD gene. Classic DMD and BMD predominantly affect males and are characterized by progressive skeletal muscle degeneration, proximal weakness, calf pseudohypertrophy, and variable cardiac involvement [1–3]. Females carrying heterozygous DMD variants have traditionally been described as carriers; however, this terminology is increasingly considered insufficient for females with clinical, biochemical, electrophysiological, imaging, or cardiac involvement [4]. Current expert discussions favor describing clinically affected individuals as females with dystrophinopathy or females with DMD gene variants, while reserving carrier terminology for genetic transmission when appropriate [4].

The clinical spectrum in females is broad. Earlier adult and mixed-age carrier cohorts showed that muscle weakness, dilated cardiomyopathy, or left ventricular dilation may occur in a substantial minority of carriers [5–9]. Pediatric data are more limited but clinically important. Mercier et al. described 26 symptomatic pediatric carriers with presentations ranging from Duchenne-like progression to very mild Becker-like phenotypes, including exercise intolerance, cognitive involvement, and cardiac dysfunction [10]. Skewed X-chromosome inactivation has also been implicated in symptomatic female carriers in prior studies [11, 12]. More recent longitudinal and cohort studies have further emphasized that females with early-onset dystrophinopathy may experience fatigue, myalgia, exercise intolerance, respiratory or cardiac involvement, and restrictions in participation over time [13].

Despite this growing recognition, practical diagnostic questions remain unresolved in childhood. Many girls with DMD gene variants are not referred because of classic progressive weakness; instead, they may be identified through incidental or recurrent hyperCKemia, cascade testing after an affected relative, or unrelated genomic testing. The relationship between the initial ascertainment route and clinical status at evaluation has rarely been analyzed explicitly. In routine pediatric practice, persistent or recurrent hyperCKemia may be the first clue to DMD-related variant status in girls and can prompt targeted history-taking that reveals otherwise under-recognized mild manifestations, such as exercise intolerance, myalgia, fatigue, or subtle gait abnormalities. Recognizing girls with DMD gene variants during childhood may help establish baseline neuromuscular and cardiac surveillance and inform family counseling, including parental testing, cascade testing of relatives, and future reproductive planning. This study therefore aimed to describe the clinical and genetic spectrum of pediatric females with DMD gene variants, distinguish primary ascertainment route from clinical status at evaluation, and identify practical clues for early recognition and genetic counseling.

Methods

Subjects

This single-center retrospective case series was conducted between January 2024 and December 2025 at the Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University. Eligible participants were pediatric females with genetically confirmed pathogenic or likely pathogenic DMD gene variants identified from existing clinical records. Inclusion criteria were: (1) female sex; (2) age < 18 years at evaluation; (3) a pathogenic or likely pathogenic DMD variant confirmed by MLPA and/or sequencing; and (4) available clinical data sufficient for assessment of ascertainment route and clinical status at evaluation. Cases with variants of uncertain significance, insufficient clinical records, or an alternative confirmed neuromuscular diagnosis explaining the presentation were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital Center for Children’s Health, Capital Medical University (approval no. SHERLL2024042). Cases were identified through multiple clinical pathways, including evaluation for neuromuscular symptoms, family screening after the diagnosis of DMD/BMD in relatives, repeated or incidental CK elevation during routine, infectious, or preoperative evaluations, and incidental genomic findings. Primary ascertainment route was recorded separately from clinical status at evaluation; detailed clinical-status classification criteria are described below.

Data collection and clinical assessments

Clinical data collection and assessments

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records, including age, age at onset or evaluation, initial reason for evaluation, clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, family history, cardiac findings, imaging, electrophysiological results, and molecular genetic data. The primary ascertainment route was defined as the initial reason for medical evaluation, referral, or genetic testing and was analyzed separately from the clinical status at evaluation.

Ascertainment routes were classified into four mutually exclusive categories: (1) symptom-driven presentation, including neuromuscular symptoms such as weakness, exercise intolerance, myalgia, toe walking, gait abnormality, or cardiac symptoms such as chest pain; (2) incidental or unexplained hyperCKemia detected during routine screening, infectious illness, preoperative assessment, or evaluation for unrelated medical problems; (3) family-based cascade screening after DMD/BMD or a DMD gene variant was identified in a relative; and (4) incidental genomic findings during genetic testing for non-neuromuscular indications. When medical records did not clearly distinguish whether subtle symptoms or CK elevation was the first trigger, cases were classified conservatively according to the documented CK-triggered evaluation pathway. Clinical status at evaluation was assigned after systematic history-taking, neurological examination, cardiac evaluation, and ancillary investigations. Therefore, the ascertainment route did not necessarily correspond to clinical status at evaluation.

Clinical status classification

Clinical status at evaluation was assigned with reference to published female dystrophinopathy classifications and pediatric DMD/BMD clinical principles [10, 14, 15]. We used the classification framework applied in a large Chinese female dystrophinopathy cohort as the primary reference because it provides clinically familiar categories of asymptomatic, BMD-like, and DMD-like status and incorporates pediatric age-at-weakness thresholds [14]. This framework was particularly suitable for the present pediatric cohort, in which most participants were younger than the age at which lifetime phenotype can be confidently established.

In the present cohort, asymptomatic status was used for participants without muscle weakness on neurological examination. BMD-like status was assigned to participants with muscle weakness beginning after 5 years of age who did not meet DMD-like criteria. DMD-like status was assigned to participants with muscle weakness before 5 years of age and early Duchenne-like signs, including Gowers sign, calf pseudohypertrophy, abnormal gait, delayed motor development, or major functional limitation. Exercise intolerance, easy fatigue, myalgia, cramps, toe walking, hyperCKemia, EMG changes, or MRI findings were recorded descriptively but did not by themselves upgrade skeletal-muscle clinical status in the absence of demonstrable muscle weakness. Cardiac-symptom/CMR observations were recorded separately and were not used to assign skeletal-muscle BMD-like or DMD-like status.

Molecular genetic testing

Genetic testing was performed as part of routine clinical evaluation or family testing. MLPA was used to detect exon-level deletions or duplications in the DMD gene, and sequencing-based methods, including targeted gene panels or whole-exome sequencing, were used to identify small variants when clinically indicated. Variant interpretation was based on the original clinical genetic reports and was reviewed according to ACMG/AMP criteria when sufficient information was available. Parental testing was performed when available to determine inheritance. Variant type and predicted reading-frame consequence were extracted from available reports and summarized descriptively; no genotype-severity or reading-frame-stratified analysis was performed. For cases described as mosaic, the tested tissue, detection method, and mosaic ratio were extracted from available clinical genetic testing records when available.

Muscle pathology

Muscle biopsy was performed in three participants as part of clinical evaluation before the genetic diagnosis was established, including one participant with prominent muscle symptoms and two participants with either markedly elevated CK levels or mild neuromuscular symptoms. Biopsy specimens were obtained from clinically selected skeletal muscle. The specimens were pre-cooled in isopentane, subsequently frozen and fixed in liquid nitrogen, and sectioned to a thickness of 6 μm. Comprehensive histological and enzyme histochemical analyses were performed, including hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, modified Gomori trichrome, periodic acid-Schiff reaction, oil red O fat staining, adenosine triphosphatase, reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) tetrazolium reductase, succinate dehydrogenase, cytochrome C oxidase, and non-specific esterase staining. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using antibodies targeting dystrophin (N-terminal, C-terminal, and rod domain), sarcoglycan (α, β, γ), desmin, dysferlin, major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), and complement component C5b-9.

Electromyography

EMG data were obtained from available clinical electrophysiological reports. Most EMG examinations were performed at our institution, whereas a small number were performed at outside hospitals before referral. Needle EMG generally included proximal and distal limb muscles, commonly the deltoid, biceps brachii, quadriceps femoris, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius. Motor unit potential morphology, recruitment pattern, insertional activity, and spontaneous activity at rest were reviewed when available. Nerve conduction studies showed no evidence of peripheral neuropathy in the examined participants. EMG findings were categorized as normal, subtle myopathic motor unit potential changes, or definite myopathic EMG findings. Subtle myopathic motor unit potential changes were defined as short-duration motor unit potentials in one or more examined muscles without a definite generalized myopathic pattern. Definite myopathic EMG findings were defined as abnormalities reported as myopathic damage or a myopathic pattern in the original EMG report.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Available skeletal muscle MRI studies, including bilateral thigh MRI performed at our institution or MRI images obtained from outside hospitals, were reviewed from clinical records. At our institution, routine thigh MRI included T1-weighted and fat-suppressed T2-weighted/STIR sequences. For outside imaging or older records, sequence information was extracted when available. MRI abnormalities were defined as skeletal muscle fatty infiltration, edema-like signal change, or atrophy reported on T1-weighted, T2-weighted, STIR, or fat-suppressed sequences. Isolated joint effusion or non-muscle findings were not counted as skeletal muscle involvement. When original images were available, findings were reviewed by experienced clinicians/radiologists in conjunction with the original radiology reports.

Cardiac assessment

All participants underwent electrocardiography and echocardiography as part of clinical evaluation. CMR was not performed systematically in this retrospective cohort. Available CMR data from two participants were extracted from the original clinical reports; the available detailed report included cine assessment, T2-weighted assessment, and delayed-enhancement imaging. A uniform CMR protocol was not available because CMR was not performed systematically.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) or range, and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Because this was a retrospective descriptive case series of all eligible patients during the study period, no formal sample-size calculation was performed. Exploratory comparisons between participants classified as symptomatic (BMD-like or DMD-like) and those classified as asymptomatic were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables; all P values were two-sided and unadjusted for multiple comparisons. CK values were summarized using estimated individual mean CK values where relevant; single CK values were used directly, CK ranges were represented by their midpoint, and the single recorded CK value of 185 U/L for Patient 28 was used directly. CK values were not used to define clinical-status categories or to derive a diagnostic threshold. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis for each summary.

Results

Ascertainment routes and clinical status at evaluation

Among the 35 pediatric females with DMD gene variants, primary ascertainment routes were symptom-driven neuromuscular or cardiac presentation in 8 (22.9%), incidental or unexplained hyperCKemia in 18 (51.4%), family-based cascade screening in 8 (22.9%), and incidental genomic discovery in 1 (2.9%) (Fig. 1). Clinical manifestations, CK levels, EMG and muscle MRI findings, cardiac status, family history, and DMD variants are summarized in Table 1. Within the symptom-driven group (n = 8), clinical status at evaluation was heterogeneous: 2 were classified as asymptomatic because no muscle weakness was documented, 1 as BMD-like, 3 as DMD-like, and 2 had cardiac-symptom/CMR observations. The two symptom-driven participants classified as asymptomatic (Patients 19 and 24) were initially evaluated in a cardiology setting for intermittent chest pain with elevated myocardial injury markers and/or markedly elevated CK; both had normal ECG and echocardiography in the available records, and CMR was not performed. A documented family history known at presentation or during initial evaluation, including DMD/BMD or definite muscle disease in relatives, was present in 8 participants (22.9%), but family history was analyzed separately because it was not always the initial reason for referral.

Clinical manifestations

After systematic clinical review, 29 participants (82.9%) were classified as asymptomatic, 1 (2.9%) as BMD-like, 3 (8.6%) as DMD-like, and 2 (5.7%) as having cardiac-symptom/CMR observations that were recorded separately from skeletal-muscle categories. The two participants with cardiac-symptom/CMR observations were recorded separately and were not included in this asymptomatic-versus-symptomatic comparison. Estimated individual mean CK values were used for CK summary. Exploratory P values were calculated only for this asymptomatic-versus-symptomatic comparison and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons; they should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of symptomatic participants. The DMD-like group comprised Patients 1, 16, and 17, who had muscle weakness before 5 years of age with early Duchenne-like signs. According to neurological examination, participants presenting with exercise intolerance, easy fatigability, myalgia, or cramps in the absence of muscle weakness were classified as asymptomatic. Toe walking or chest pain, when observed without documented muscle weakness or objective cardiac abnormalities meeting the criteria for cardiac-symptom/CMR assessment, were recorded descriptively and did not alter the skeletal-muscle clinical status. Among the 29 asymptomatic cases, 12 had such mild symptoms or observations recorded descriptively. The remaining asymptomatic participants had no recorded muscle-related symptoms in the available records. Regarding cognitive function, no participant had documented cognitive impairment or learning/developmental concern in the available records.

Laboratory tests

Blood biochemistry

Among 35 participants, 34 had elevated CK levels (> 220 U/L) at least once. Observed CK values showed substantial inter-patient and intra-patient variability (overall observed range: 185 − 16,388 U/L), and 31 participants (88.6%) had at least one CK value > 1000 U/L. For group summaries, CK was summarized as an estimated individual mean value, and the two cardiac-symptom/CMR observations were excluded from the skeletal-muscle comparison. Estimated individual mean CK values were numerically higher in symptomatic participants than in asymptomatic participants (median [IQR]: 7,187 [5,394-9,733] vs. 2,694 [1,346-4,263] U/L; P = 0.149; Table 2), although the exploratory comparison did not reach statistical significance in this small cohort. CK was not used to define clinical-status categories. These findings should be regarded as descriptive because CK values were obtained during routine care and may have been influenced by infection, exercise, timing of measurement, or the number of CK tests obtained for each participant.

Cardiac function evaluation

All 35 participants underwent electrocardiography and echocardiography. No participant showed reduced ejection fraction, and no clinically significant ECG abnormality was identified. One participant had a coronary-pulmonary artery fistula, and another had mild tricuspid regurgitation that was considered clinically insignificant. Two monozygotic twin sisters (IDs 21 and 22) reported post-exercise chest tightness or chest pain and had elevated myocardial injury markers in available records. Routine echocardiography showed preserved systolic function. The available detailed CMR report for one sister described patchy mid-wall/subepicardial delayed enhancement in the basal-to-mid left ventricular lateral wall and T2 hyperintensity consistent with myocardial edema, with preserved left ventricular systolic function. Coronary CTA and stress/rest myocardial perfusion imaging did not support coronary artery disease or ischemia. Because myocarditis was also considered clinically and CMR data were limited in this retrospective cohort, these cases were described as cardiac-symptom/CMR observations rather than as an established dystrophinopathy-associated cardiac phenotype. Patients 19 and 24 reported intermittent chest pain but had normal ECG and echocardiography in the available records; CMR was not performed in either patient. Because no muscle weakness was documented on neurological examination, they were classified as asymptomatic rather than as cardiac-symptom/CMR observations.

Electromyography

Thirty-four participants underwent EMG. Overall EMG abnormalities were identified in 14 (41.2%), including definite myopathic EMG findings in 5 and subtle short-duration MUP changes in 9. EMG abnormalities were present in 4/4 (100.0%) symptomatic participants and 10/28 (35.7%) asymptomatic participants with available EMG data (exploratory Fisher’s exact P = 0.028; Table 2). Definite myopathic EMG findings were frequent in symptomatic participants but were also observed in one asymptomatic participant, and subtle short-duration MUP changes were observed in several asymptomatic participants. Therefore, EMG findings were interpreted as supportive evidence of muscle involvement but were not used alone to upgrade skeletal-muscle clinical status.

Imaging studies

MRI abnormalities were defined, for descriptive group summaries, as skeletal muscle signal changes compatible with fatty infiltration, edema-like signal change, or atrophy on T1-weighted, T2-weighted, STIR, or fat-suppressed sequences. Isolated joint effusion and findings outside the routine bilateral thigh skeletal-muscle field used for group comparison were not counted as skeletal muscle MRI abnormalities. Twenty-seven participants underwent bilateral thigh muscle MRI. Nineteen (70.4%) showed no definite skeletal muscle abnormality. Two (7.4%) showed DMD-like changes with marked fatty infiltration (Fig. 2). Five additional participants had signal abnormalities described in the original reports, including patchy STIR or fat-suppressed T2-weighted hyperintensity; three of these were clearly asymmetric. For group summaries, MRI abnormalities were observed in 2/3 (66.7%) symptomatic participants and 4/22 (18.2%) asymptomatic participants with available skeletal muscle MRI data (exploratory Fisher’s exact P = 0.133; Table 2). Because MRI was not available for all participants and may have been selectively performed, MRI findings were interpreted as supportive evidence and were not used alone to define BMD-like or DMD-like status.

Muscle pathology

In Patient 1, muscle pathology showed marked variation in muscle fiber size on HE staining, with both hypertrophic and atrophic fibers. Muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration were also observed (Fig. 3). Dystrophin staining revealed markedly reduced but not completely absent expression, consistent with DMD-like status despite residual dystrophin positivity. In Patient 9, muscle pathology showed mild variation in muscle fiber size, a few regenerating fibers, and occasional necrotic fibers. Dystrophin staining showed mosaic partial loss of sarcolemmal expression alongside normally expressed fibers. In Patient 19, the original pathology report described mild dystrophic changes, including focal basophilic fibers and internalized or clustered nuclei, with partially reduced dystrophin staining. Overall, the available pathological findings from the three biopsied participants were compatible with dystrophinopathy. Because biopsies were performed in only three clinically selected participants and were not assessed using a standardized quantitative grading system, pathological findings were considered supportive and were not used for clinical-status classification.

Molecular genetic findings and inheritance

A documented family history of DMD/BMD or definite muscle disease known at presentation or during initial evaluation was present in 8 participants (22.9%). Among the 18 maternally inherited cases, Patient 13, her mother, and her maternal grandmother were reported to have exercise intolerance; however, the mother and maternal grandmother had not undergone further detailed neuromuscular evaluation, so their clinical status remained unclear. Patient 13 was classified as asymptomatic under the weakness-based framework because no muscle weakness was documented on neurological examination. No mother of a BMD-like or DMD-like participant had documented skeletal-muscle weakness in the available records; the remaining mothers had no recorded neuromuscular symptoms in available clinical records. Among the two paternal-origin cases, one was inherited from a father with low-level mosaicism in peripheral blood, and the other from a father diagnosed with BMD after his daughter’s diagnosis. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating both parents when determining inheritance and providing genetic counseling.

Discussion

Female dystrophinopathy is increasingly recognized as a broad and clinically heterogeneous condition, but how affected or at-risk girls are identified and classified in pediatric practice has received limited attention. This pediatric case series highlights four clinically relevant observations. First, girls with DMD gene variants may be identified through several real-world routes, including symptoms, persistent or recurrent hyperCKemia, family-based screening, and incidental genomic findings. Second, the initial reason for evaluation does not necessarily correspond to clinical status at evaluation. Third, mild exercise-related complaints such as exercise intolerance, easy fatigue, myalgia, and cramps were common in the records but did not necessarily indicate objective muscle weakness. Fourth, cardiac symptoms and selected CMR observations require separate description because they should not be folded into skeletal-muscle BMD-like or DMD-like categories.

No universally accepted clinical-status standard exists for pediatric female dystrophinopathy. Two recent approaches are particularly relevant. The large Chinese cohort classified females as symptomatic when muscle weakness in at least one muscle group or dilated cardiomyopathy was present, and further categorized symptomatic individuals as BMD-like or DMD-like [14]. In that framework, DMD-like status included loss of ambulation before 15 years of age or, for girls younger than 15 years, muscle weakness before 5 years of age; symptomatic individuals who did not meet DMD-like criteria were classified as BMD-like [14]. A more recent severity framework for females with heterozygous DMD variants also separates hyperCKemia, myalgia, cramps, or calf hypertrophy without weakness from weakness-based symptomatic grades [15]. These two frameworks support a conservative distinction between biochemical or subjective manifestations and clinically documented muscle weakness.

In applying these published principles to a pediatric cohort, three children (Patients 1, 16, and 17) had muscle weakness before 5 years of age together with early Duchenne-like signs and were classified as DMD-like. One child (Patient 5) had muscle weakness after 5 years of age without meeting DMD-like criteria and was classified as BMD-like. Most children had no objectively documented muscle weakness on neurological examination and were therefore classified as asymptomatic at evaluation. Importantly, asymptomatic in this framework indicates absence of documented muscle weakness, not absence of all clinical observations. Some children in this group had exercise intolerance, easy fatigue, myalgia, cramps, occasional toe walking, hyperCKemia, EMG changes, or MRI findings documented in clinical records. Although these findings did not meet current weakness-based criteria for BMD-like status, they should not be dismissed as clinically insignificant, as they reflect parental history, patient complaints, or ancillary-test abnormalities encountered in routine pediatric practice. Given the young age of the children and the limited follow-up, these findings may represent an early disease stage rather than a stable mild phenotype, and they may therefore be subject to misclassification. It is possible that some of these individuals may develop clinically detectable muscle weakness during long-term follow-up and eventually be reclassified as having a BMD-like phenotype. This highlights the inherent difficulty in distinguishing between early-stage disease and truly mild phenotype in a cross-sectional pediatric cohort. Longitudinal reassessment with standardized strength testing and functional assessments will be critical to determine whether these initially asymptomatic children progress to meet weakness-based criteria, and to guide timely intervention and surveillance. Therefore, longitudinal reassessment during growth is essential in pediatric female dystrophinopathy.

This distinction is central to the pediatric interpretation of female dystrophinopathy. Current categories borrow heavily from DMD/BMD clinical logic, in which muscle weakness and age at weakness onset are key anchors. However, pediatric females may present before measurable weakness is apparent, while still showing mild exercise-related symptoms or supportive biochemical, electrophysiological, or imaging abnormalities. For this reason, the present cohort supports separating ascertainment route, mild manifestations without weakness, cardiac-symptom/CMR observations, and weakness-defined skeletal-muscle status. Such separation reduces overclassification while preserving clinically relevant information that may guide targeted neuromuscular assessment, cardiac surveillance, parental evaluation, family-based genetic counseling, and future reproductive planning. Future prospective studies with standardized strength testing and functional scales are needed to determine whether children with mild symptoms or ancillary abnormalities but no documented weakness require a dedicated pediatric descriptor or risk category, and which of them later develop clearer BMD-like or DMD-like phenotypes.

CK values should be interpreted with caution. In this cohort, estimated individual mean CK values tended to be higher in symptomatic participants, although the exploratory comparison did not reach statistical significance and CK sampling was not standardized. Some children had only a few tests, whereas others underwent repeated testing because CK remained elevated. We therefore treated CK as descriptive biochemical information, not as a diagnostic threshold or a severity-defining criterion. In routine pediatric practice, pediatric hyperCKemia has heterogeneous causes but can lead to the recognition of underlying genetic or neuromuscular disorders even when weakness is absent or subtle [16–18]. Therefore, persistent or recurrent hyperCKemia may be more useful as a trigger for targeted neuromuscular history-taking, earlier recognition, and longitudinal follow-up than as a standalone measure of clinical severity. Future prospective studies should analyze first available CK, clinically stable CK, maximum CK, and longitudinal CK trajectory separately.

Cardiac assessment is an important consideration in pediatric females with DMD gene variants. Earlier cohorts and cardiac-focused studies showed that females with DMD variants can develop dilated cardiomyopathy or subclinical cardiac abnormalities [8, 19], and current recommendations advise baseline cardiac assessment and ongoing surveillance for females carrying DMD gene variants [20]. In this cohort, two monozygotic twin sisters had exertional chest symptoms, elevated myocardial injury markers in available records, and selected CMR abnormalities, while routine echocardiography showed preserved systolic function. The available detailed CMR report for one sister showed a non-ischemic lateral-wall pattern with mid-wall/subepicardial delayed enhancement and T2 hyperintensity; however, coronary CTA and stress/rest myocardial perfusion imaging did not support ischemia, and myocarditis was also considered clinically. Because attribution to dystrophinopathy-associated cardiomyopathy cannot be considered definitive and CMR data were limited in this retrospective cohort, these cases were described separately as cardiac-symptom/CMR observations rather than as a defined cardiac phenotype. Two additional participants initially evaluated for intermittent chest pain had normal ECG and echocardiography in the available records, and CMR was not performed; therefore, they were not classified as cardiac-symptom/CMR observations. However, the absence of CMR data should not be interpreted as absence of subclinical cardiac involvement. Together, these observations support careful evaluation of cardiac complaints and longitudinal cardiac surveillance, but they cannot determine the prevalence or screening yield of CMR abnormalities in this cohort. Dilated cardiomyopathy represents one of the most prominent manifestations of dystrophinopathy in adult females. Although the two cases with cardiac-symptom/CMR observations in the present study did not show classic features of dilated cardiomyopathy, and definite myocardial fibrosis was not established, the available findings suggest that myocardial edema-like changes with preserved systolic function may still warrant attention in young pediatric females with dystrophinopathy. The subsequent clinical significance of such early cardiac observations requires longitudinal follow-up.

EMG, skeletal muscle MRI, and muscle pathology provided complementary descriptive evidence of skeletal-muscle involvement, but none was used alone to define clinical status. Definite myopathic EMG findings were concentrated in participants with BMD-like or DMD-like status, whereas focal short-duration MUP changes were also observed in some children classified as asymptomatic at evaluation. Skeletal muscle MRI showed selective muscle signal abnormalities, including patterns compatible with dystrophinopathy-related changes and prior MRI literature [21–24]. Muscle pathology, available in three clinically selected participants, supported dystrophinopathy but was not used to define clinical status because biopsies were limited and not systematically graded. Although EMG abnormalities were more frequent in symptomatic participants in the exploratory comparison, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the symptomatic group was very small and electrophysiological sampling was not standardized.

X-chromosome inactivation is an important biological context for interpreting phenotypic heterogeneity in females with DMD gene variants. Skewed inactivation of the X chromosome carrying the normal DMD allele has been associated with symptomatic female dystrophinopathy, and prior studies have suggested that skewed XCI can contribute to muscle symptoms in females with DMD variants [11, 12]. Skewed XCI may partly account for variable skeletal-muscle or cardiac manifestations among females carrying similar DMD variants, but it is unlikely to explain phenotype in a simple deterministic manner. In pediatric cohorts, the relationship between XCI and clinical status may be especially difficult to interpret. Some girls may be evaluated before weakness becomes objectively detectable, while others may have mild exercise-related complaints, hyperCKemia, EMG changes, or MRI abnormalities without meeting weakness-based BMD-like or DMD-like criteria. Thus, XCI should be considered as one potential biological modifier rather than as a standalone explanation for cross-sectional clinical status. In the present retrospective study, XCI analysis was not performed as part of further evaluation. Therefore, we could not assess whether skewed XCI contributed to the observed phenotypic variability, and XCI was not used to infer clinical severity or assign clinical-status categories. Future longitudinal studies integrating XCI analysis with standardized strength testing, functional assessment, variant interpretation, and phenotype follow-up may help clarify how molecular modifiers contribute to phenotype evolution in pediatric female dystrophinopathy.

Genetic findings also have practical counseling implications. The observed mixture of large deletions, duplications, and point or small variants was consistent with the known DMD variant spectrum [25]. In males with dystrophinopathy, the reading-frame rule is a major genetic principle underlying the distinction between Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy, although exceptions are recognized [25]. In females with heterozygous DMD variants, however, clinical severity may be modified by XCI, mosaicism, and other biological factors; therefore, variant type, reading-frame status, variant severity, or the functional domain affected by the variant cannot be interpreted in the same deterministic manner as in affected males. Although maternal inheritance and apparently de novo variants accounted for most cases, two participants had paternal-origin variants, including one inherited from a father with low-level mosaicism in peripheral blood and one from a father diagnosed with BMD after his daughter’s diagnosis. These findings support evaluation of both parents when determining inheritance, especially for genetic counseling and future reproductive planning, rather than assuming maternal transmission or de novo occurrence. Variants described as apparently de novo should still be interpreted with caution because low-level parental mosaicism, including germline mosaicism, cannot be fully excluded by routine testing. Because XCI and other modifiers were not systematically evaluated, we did not infer clinical severity from reading-frame status or variant location in this pediatric female cohort.

Several sources of bias should be considered when interpreting the clinical-status distribution. This was a clinically ascertained retrospective cohort from a tertiary pediatric center, so girls with persistent hyperCKemia, family history, or parental concern may have been more likely to be evaluated than girls without laboratory testing or referral. Ascertainment routes were reconstructed from clinical records and may not fully capture the earliest symptoms noticed by families. Recall bias may have affected the accuracy and completeness of historical symptom reporting, particularly for subjective symptoms such as fatigue, exercise intolerance, myalgia, and cramps, which may be underreported or recalled differently by parents and caregivers. Observer bias may have been introduced because the treating neurologists were not blinded to the genetic diagnosis or the reason for referral at the time of clinical evaluation. In addition, because the cohort was young and symptoms were generally mild, cross-sectional classification may misclassify children whose weakness or functional limitations become clearer only with growth, increasing activity demands, or longer follow-up.

The study also has limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective case series with a modest sample size, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Second, clinical, EMG, skeletal muscle MRI, cardiac imaging, pathological, and CK data were collected during routine care rather than according to a uniform protocol. Retrospective clinical records were also subject to uneven documentation depth across participants, which may have led to under-ascertainment of subtle or historical symptoms in some cases. Therefore, clinical-status categories should be interpreted as cross-sectional categories at evaluation, not validated lifetime prognostic stages; standardized prospective strength and functional assessments, including MRC grading and validated motor performance scales where feasible, are needed to validate pediatric clinical-status classification. Moreover, because some children may not yet have reached the age at which symptoms fully manifest, longitudinal reassessment is needed to confirm current classifications. Third, although cognitive changes have been reported in dystrophinopathies [26], this study did not include formal neuropsychological testing or systematic brain MRI. Fourth, CMR data were available only for selected participants in this retrospective cohort, and cardiac-symptom/CMR observations should not be interpreted as cohort-level cardiac prevalence or screening yield. Fifth, the retrospective and clinically referred design may have amplified the bias sources discussed above. Finally, XCI analysis was not performed as part of further evaluation; therefore, the biological mechanisms underlying phenotypic variability could not be determined.

Conclusions

In conclusion, pediatric female dystrophinopathy encompasses a broad clinical spectrum and may be recognized before objective muscle weakness is documented. Separating the initial ascertainment route from clinical status at evaluation provides a practical framework for understanding how girls with DMD gene variants enter clinical care through symptoms, persistent or recurrent hyperCKemia, family-based screening, or incidental genomic findings. Mild exercise-related complaints, hyperCKemia, EMG or MRI abnormalities, and cardiac symptoms should be recorded carefully and followed longitudinally, even when they do not meet weakness-based BMD-like or DMD-like status at initial evaluation. Collectively, these findings highlight an early pediatric observation window in which targeted neuromuscular assessment, cardiac surveillance, parental evaluation, family-based genetic counseling, and future reproductive planning may be particularly valuable. Longitudinal follow-up through adolescence is essential, as cross-sectional classification in early childhood may not reflect the eventual lifetime phenotype. These observations should be interpreted with caution given the retrospective design and modest sample size, and prospective studies are needed to validate the proposed clinical-status framework.
