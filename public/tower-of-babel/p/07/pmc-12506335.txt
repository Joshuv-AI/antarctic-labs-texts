Identification of candidate genes and proteins for tasseling stage drought tolerance through integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis approach in maize
Authors: Songtao Liu, Hanbo Shi, Linan Yan, Chao Jiang, Haichao Zhao, Haibo Lu, Haoyang Li, Shuo Wang, Zhihong Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought stress, particularly at the tasseling stage, is the most devastating abiotic factor and major contributor to yield reduction in maize (Zea mays L.). Despite recent scientific advances in deciphering maize drought stress responses, the overall picture of key genes and proteins regulating maize tasseling drought tolerance remains less understood. In this study, we conducted comparative physiological, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to monitor the changes in the leaf tissues of two contrasting maize hybrid cultivars exposed to drought stress at the tasseling stage. We identified 1701 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in RNA-sequence runs and 424 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) from an iTRAQ-based analysis. Mapman analysis revealed several regulatory processes influenced by drought conditions, including signal transduction, cell-wall remodeling, cellular redox homeostasis and hormone metabolism that were observed at both mRNA and protein levels. However, transcription factor regulation and secondary metabolism were specifically identified at the transcript level, whereas photosynthesis was uniquely identified to be affected by drought stress at the protein level. Meanwhile, a weak correlation between DEGs and DEPs was observed, indicating the drought response of maize at tasseling stage is largely regulated post-transcriptionally. Furthermore, comparative physiological analysis and qRT-PCR results substantiated the trancriptomic and proteomic findings. Additionally, we screened ZmPOD, ZmRAV1, ZmTPP and performed phenotypical and physiological characterizations of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana (Arabidopsis) lines and wild-type. Resultantly, the transgenic Arabidopsis lines exhibited stronger tolerance to drought than the WT. This functional verification reinforces the reliability of our omics-based candidate gene selection. Overall, our research provides insights on the drought-responsive genes and pathways mediating maize drought tolerance at the tasseling stage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07264-5.Keywords: Transcriptome, Proteome, Physiological responses, Drought stress, Zea may L., Tasseling stage

Introduction

Drought stress is one of the major abiotic stresses affecting global crop productivity and threatening food security [1]. Physiologically, drought stress causes decreased leaf water potential, loss of cell turgor, disrupted plant water relations, reactive oxygen species (ROS) over-accumulation, impaired photosynthesis, inhibited cell growth, and impaired metabolism, etc., which ultimately reduces plant growth and crop productivity [2, 3]. Therefore, the increasing frequency and intensity of drought events, exacerbated by climate change, have intensified the need for developing resilient crops that can survive and provide sustainable yields under drought stress conditions [1]. Developing drought stress tolerant crops depends on first gaining a mechanistic understanding of the intricate molecular mechanisms governing plant drought stress responses [4].

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the globally significant cereal crops, ranking third after rice (Oryza sativa L.) and wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) [5]. It serves as a primary source of food, livestock feed, biofuel, and industrial raw materials [6]. However, maize is highly susceptible to drought stress, posing a serious food insecurity threat across the regions prone to water scarcity, including the arid and semi-arid regions of northern China [7]. The maize crop is more vulnerable to drought stress from pre-flowering to grain-filling stage, with the tasseling period being the most water-demanding and critical for grain yield formation [8]. Consequently, drought stress during this stage can significantly reduce yield, impacting overall productivity. Therefore, improving maize tolerance to drought stress at the tasseling stage is critical for ensuring sustainably better yields and stable crop production under climate change conditions.

To adapt to drought stress, plants employ a range of cellular, anatomical, physiological, biochemical, and molecular mechanisms. These include cell wall modifications, root architectural and biochemical adjustments, osmotic adjustment, phytohormonal elicitation, transcription factor (TF) regulation, stress-responsive genes expression, etc. [2, 9, 10]. Physiologically, drought-tolerant maize genotypes typically maintain higher leaf relative water content (RWC) under drought stress conditions as compared to drought-sensitive genotypes [11]. Additionally, plants mitigate ROS-induced oxidative stress by activating antioxidant defense systems, involving key enzymes such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and peroxidases [12, 13]. At the molecular level, various TFs, protein kinases, and phytohormones orchestrate drought tolerance by modulating the expression of stress-responsive genes [14]. Despite extensive research on molecular mechanisms underlying crop drought resistance, the regulatory networks governing drought stress response in maize remain less understood [15]. Therefore, exploring the molecular mechanisms of maize drought stress tolerance remains key to developing climate resilient cultivars and ensuring sustainable yields.

Meanwhile, RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), has been widely used in studies examining the dynamic gene expression patterns and key mechanisms governing drought stress response in maize [16–18]. For instance, an RNA-seq transcriptomic study revealed that a comparatively drought-tolerant maize inbred line YE8112 maintained higher leaf RWC and proline content, alongside increased peroxidase activity, and upregulated expression of MYB, NAC, WRKY, and PLATZ transcriptional factors under drought stress conditions, which contributed to its better drought tolerance [18]. Equally, the isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ) proteomic approach, known for its high reproducibility and robust quantitative accuracy, has been widely applied to investigate maize protein responses to drought stress [19–23]. For example, a comparative iTRAQ-proteomics study revealed that the comparably drought-tolerant hybrid cultivar Nongdan 476 (ND476) tolerated drought stress at the grain filling stage better than the sensitive hybrid cultivar Zhongxin 978 (ZX978), due to its enhanced antioxidant enzymes (peroxidase, oxidoreductase, etc.) activities and redox homeostasis maintenance, as well as increased expression of stress defense proteins under drought stress conditions [22]. Despite proving instrumental in elucidating drought stress resistance mechanisms in plants, RNA-Seq transcriptomic and iTRAQ proteomic approaches have mostly been used individually and rarely in integration. However, their integration may provide vital insights that may not be decoded from individual analysis of mRNA transcript or protein expressions [24–26]. Thus, it is important to perform conjoint RNA-Seq transcriptomics and iTRAQ proteomics analysis of drought stress responses in maize.

In light of the above, in this study, RNA-seq and iTRAQ were jointly utilized to perform comparative analysis of mRNA transcript and protein changes between two contrasting maize hybrids (drought-tolerant ND476 and drought-sensitive ZX978) under drought stress and well-watered (control) conditions at the tasseling stage. Subsequently, an integrated RNA-seq and iTRAQ analysis revealed unique and commonly-shared drought stress regulatory mechanisms at the transcript and post-transcriptional levels. Physiology parameters were also measured to corroborate the identified drought stress response-related transcriptomic and proteomic changes. Further, we used quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) to validate RNA-seq results, and we assessed the functions of ZmPOD, ZmRAV1 and ZmTPP genes in drought response. Our data provide resources for future genetic analyses of drought stress response and tools for molecular breeding of drought-tolerant maize cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and drought stress treatment

The two maize hybrid cultivars with contrasting drought tolerance, ND476 (drought-tolerant) and ZX978 (drought-sensitive), used in this study were provided by the North China Key Laboratory for Crop Germplasm Resources of Education Ministry (Hebei Agricultural University, Baoding, China). ND476 is a comparably drought-tolerant hybrid developed by the Dryland Research Institute of Hebei Academy of Agricultural and Forestry Sciences (Hengshui, China), whilst ZX978 is a comparably drought-sensitive hybrid developed by the Hebei Zhongxin Seed Technology Company Limited (Handan, China). Both hybrid cultivars were identified as drought tolerant and susceptible, respectively, through laboratory and field screening of several maize lines for drought tolerance by the North China Key Laboratory for Crop Germplasm Resources of the Education Ministry, Hebei Agricultural University, Baoding, China, patent ID: 201910634005.2) [22].

The field experiment was conducted in May 2018 at the Qing Yuan Experiment Station of Hebei Agricultural University, Baoding, Hebei Province, China (115.5602790°E, 38.7950930°N, 118 m), under a fully automated rain-proof shelter. A randomized complete block design was employed, with both control and drought stress treatments replicated three times. Each experimental plot covered 25 m2 (5 m × 5 m), with a planting arrangement of 60 cm row spacing and 30 cm plant spacing, resulting in 128 plants per plot. Seeds were sown at a depth of 6 cm, two per station, in plots fertilized with 512 kg ha−2 of compound fertilizer. The nutrient management in the field experiment followed standard agronomic practices suitable for the region, including topdressing with urea at the V6 stage (approximately 60 kg N ha−1) under both control and drought conditions to ensure consistent nitrogen availability. Following Hsiao [27], the well-watered control plots were maintained at 70–80% soil water content, whereas the drought-stress plots were maintained at 15–20%. Soil moisture was monitored using a TZS-1 soil moisture measurement instrument (Zhejiang Tuopu Technology Co., Ltd.), with a one-meter-deep waterproof membrane installed between the control and treatment plots to prevent lateral water infiltration. During the tasseling stage, the average relative humidity in the experimental area was 58–65%, and mean daytime temperature ranged between 29–34°C, as recorded by an on-site weather station. These controlled environmental parameters ensured consistent and reproducible stress conditions. Drought treatment began when the twelfth leaf was fully expanded and continued until tassel emergence. At tasseling, leaf tissues were collected from the flag leaves of maize plants under both control and drought stress treatment conditions, with three biological replicates each sample/treatment condition/parameter. For RNA and protein extraction, a 5-cm segment from the middle portion of the flag leaf (excluding the midrib) was harvested from each of three individual plants and pooled together to constitute one biological replicate. All collected samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at –80 °C for further analysis.

Leaf samples for physiological parameters assays were collected from both control and drought-treated plants at the onset of the treatment, followed by subsequent collections every three days. Leaf RWC was assessed following the method described by Galmés et al. [28]. POD activity was determined using Han’s guaiacol method [29], while proline content was quantified using the ninhydrin-based assay according to Bates et al. [30]. Lipid peroxidation levels were evaluated by measuring MDA accumulation via the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction, following the protocol established by Dhindsa et al. [31]. For each physiological parameter assay, three biological replicates were used per each sample, with three technical replications each analysis.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA library construction and transcriptome analysis

Leaf samples were sent to Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for RNA isolation, cDNA library construction, and RNA sequencing. In brief, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and its concentration and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) and electrophoresis on 1% agarose gels, respectively. cDNA libraries were then constructed following the manufacturer’s protocol using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform using paired-end sequencing technology.

The raw sequencing data (FASTQ format) generated from the Illumina HiSeq 4000 system underwent initial processing using in-house Perl scripts. Low-quality reads were filtered out, and the remaining clean reads were aligned to the maize reference genome (B73 RefGen_v4) using TopHat 2.0.12, a splice-aware aligner that allows intron-spanning gaps of up to 50 kb [32]. Quality control metrics, including the Q30 base percentage (an indicator of the overall reproducibility and quality of the assay) and GC content were assessed. Only reads with a perfect match or a single mismatch were retained for further analysis and genome annotation. Gene expression was quantified by counting mapped read numbers using HTSeq v0.6.1 and normalized as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Gene differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq R package (v1.10.1) [33], whereby the ratios of FPKM values between the control and treatments were analyzed, and the resulting P-values corrected for multiplicity using the Benjamini and Hochberg method [34]. Genes with a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 and a q-value < 0.05 were classified as differentially expressed.

Gene ontology (GO) functional annotation and classification of drought-responsive DEGs

To reveal the underlying biological functions of the identified DEGs, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (p-value < 0.05) was conducted using agriGO web-based program (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2/#). The expression abundances of each gene appearing in the two libraries (control versus drought treatment) were used to determine the expression changes of the genes in response to drought stress. The total DEGs identified to respond to drought stress (those meeting the selection criteria specified above) were analyzed by way of Venn diagram. After filtering, the important drought-responsive genes were specified as those DEGs that specifically expressed in the tolerant genotype ND476 after drought treatment (NDD_NDC), DEGs that specifically expressed in the sensitive genotype ZX978 after drought treatment (ZXD_ZXC), and DEGs shared between the two hybrids under drought stress treatment [35]. This was supported by their GO functional annotation, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, and literature review [35]. The same criteria applied to drought-responsive DEPs.

Expression validation of DEGs with qRT-PCR

To generate the cDNA template, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed in a 20 µL reaction volume using the HiFiscript cDNA Synthesis Kit (CWBIO, Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

To validate the sequencing results, 12 genes were selected based on their functional relevance, and specific primers for qRT-PCR analysis were designed using Premier 5 Designer software (Supplementary Table S1). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using 2 × Fast Super EvaGreen® qPCR Mastermix (US Everbright Inc., Suzhou, China) in a C1000 Thermal Cycler (CFX96 Real-Time System, Bio-Rad). Each 20 µL qRT-PCR reaction mixture contained 1 µL of cDNA template, 1 µL each of forward and reverse primers (50 pmol), 10 µL qPCR master mix, and 7 µL ddH₂O. The maize GAPDH gene (accession No. X07156), known for its stable and constitutive expression, was used as an internal reference gene. The amplification protocol followed the method described by Zenda et al. [20]. Each sample was analyzed in three biological replicates, and gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method [36].

Protein extraction and isobaric labeling

Proteins were extracted from the same leaf samples used for RNA-Seq analysis using the cold acetone precipitation method, as detailed in our previous study [21]. The extracted proteins were resuspended in 8 M urea, and their concentrations determined using the Pierce Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China). Protein integrity was assessed by SDS-PAGE (tricine-sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) [37]. A total of 100 µg of protein was enzymatically digested with trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at a protein-to-trypsin ratio of 50:1 at 37 °C overnight (16 h), followed by a second 4-h digestion at a ratio of 100:1. Post-digestion, peptides were desalted using a Strata X C18 SPE column (Phenomenex) and vacuum-dried. The dried peptides were then reconstituted in 0.5 M TEAB and labeled using the iTRAQ reagent kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each unit of iTRAQ reagent, defined as the amount required to label 100 µg of protein, was dissolved in 70 µL acetonitrile. The labeled peptide mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 2 h, then pooled, desalted, and vacuum-dried to differentiate isobaric tags. For control samples, drought-sensitive ZX978 was labeled with iTRAQ tag 127, while drought-tolerant ND476 was labeled with tag 129. For drought-treated samples, ZX978 and ND476 were labeled with tags 126 and 128, respectively.

Strong cation exchange (SCX) and LC–MS/MS analysis

Each labeled peptide sample was fractionated using high-pH reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on an Agilent 300Extend C18 column (5 µm particle size, 4.6 mm inner diameter, 250 mm length). The resulting peptide fractions were then resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and loaded onto a strong cation exchange (SCX) reversed-phase analytical column. Peptide separation was achieved using a gradient elution, starting with an increase from 6 to 23% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 98% acetonitrile) over 26 min, followed by an increase from 23 to 35% over 8 min, then rising to 80% within 3 min, and maintaining 80% for the final 3 min, all at a constant flow rate of 400 nL/min on an EASY-nLC 1000 ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system.

Mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis was conducted on a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Triple TOF 5600 Plus) equipped with an AB SCIEX analytical column. The m/z scan range for full-scan acquisition was set between 350 and 1800, with a resolving power of 120 K. Intact peptides were detected in the Orbitrap mass analyzer at a resolution of 70,000 at 200 m/z, with automatic gain control (AGC) set at 1 × 10⁶ and the fixed first mass set to 100 m/z. For tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), peptides were selected based on a normalized collision energy (NCE) setting of 28, and fragment ions were analyzed in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 17,500. A data-dependent acquisition (DDA) method was employed, alternating between one MS scan followed by 20 MS/MS scans, with a dynamic exclusion time of 15.0 s [38].

Protein identification and quantification

The original MS/MS data files were converted into mgf format using Proteome Discoverer 1.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The converted data were then analyzed using Mascot software version 2.2 (Matrix Science, London, UK) (v.1.5.2.8) for database searching. The MGF files were queried against the Uniprot Zea mays L. database, which comprises 132,339 sequences, using the Mascot search engine. The Mascot search was performed with the following parameters: trypsin as the specified enzyme, allowing a maximum of two missed cleavages; fragment mass tolerance set at ± 0.02 Da; peptide mass tolerance at ± 20 ppm; and monoisotopic mass values. Fixed modifications included carbamidomethylation of cysteine (Cys) and oxidation of methionine (Met), as well as iTRAQ 8-plex labeling (Y), iTRAQ 8-plex (N-terminal), and iTRAQ 8-plex (K). To ensure high data reliability, a confidence threshold of 95% was applied, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was adjusted to < 1%. Peptide identification was considered significant only if the Mascot score exceeded 40. Differentially abundant proteins (DEPs) were identified based on protein abundance levels. Student t test was used to analyze DEPs, with proteins exhibiting fold change > 1.3 (up-regulated) or < 0.77 (down-regulated), p-value < 0.05, and unique peptides > 1, considered statistically significant DEPs [20].

DEGs and DEPs functional enrichment, and protein–protein interaction analyses

To interpret the biological and functional properties of DEGs and DEPs, GO analysis was performed to categorize the biological processes involved in response to drought stress. The GO terms and categories with a p-value ≤ 0.05 were considered significant according to Fisher's exact test. Moreover, MapMan was used to classify maize genes into hierarchical categories. A protein interaction network was constructed using the String program (version 10.5) (http://www.string-db.org/).

Correlation of proteomics and transcriptomics

All identified proteins were compared against all genes using BLAST reciprocal best hits (RBH) analysis [39]; they were considered the same if they qualified as"best BLAST hits". Based on this criterion, the shared DEGs and DEPs from RNA-Seq and iTRAQ quantitative analysis were observed.

Function validation of drought candidate genes in transgenic arabidopsis lines

To validate the function of drought-responsive candidate genes, coding sequences or gene fragments were amplified and cloned into the binary expression vector pGreen-35S-6HA, which carries the Cauliflower Mosaic Virus (CaMV) 35S promoter for constitutive expression and a C-terminal 6 × HA tag for protein detection. The vector also contains the bar gene, conferring resistance to glufosinate ammonium (Basta), as a plant-selectable marker. The recombinant plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and used to transform Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) via the floral dip method, following standard protocols [40]. Mature Arabidopsis plants were dipped following standard procedures. Seeds harvested from the transformed plants were collected and dried, representing the T0 generation. T0 seeds were stratified at 4 ℃ for 2–4 days, then sown densely into a soil mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite (1:1, v/v). When seedlings developed two well-formed true leaves, they were sprayed with Basta herbicide (25 mg/L glufosinate ammonium, working dilution 1:2000) every other day for a total of three treatments to select for transgenic-positive seedlings. Positive seedlings were then grown under normal conditions, and leaf tissues were collected for DNA and RNA extraction using the CTAB method and standard RNA isolation protocols, respectively. PCR and qRT-PCR were performed to confirm the presence and expression of the transgenes. Homozygous T3 generation plants were obtained through subsequent propagation and used for further phenotypic and physiological analyses.

To evaluate drought tolerance during germination, seeds from both wild-type (WT) and transgenic lines were surface-sterilized with 15% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, rinsed 2–3 times with sterile water, and then sown on 1/2 MS solid medium. After 4 days, uniform seedlings were transferred to 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 0 mmol/L, 200 mmol/L, or 300 mmol/L mannitol and vertically grown for 9 days under controlled conditions. Root length was measured, and root growth status observed.

A separate set of seedlings was transplanted into plastic trays (28 cm × 21 cm × 6 cm) filled with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite and grown under controlled conditions (22 ± 1℃, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, and 60% relative humidity) for an additional 25 days. At this stage, WT and transgenic plants were then divided into two groups. For the control group, plants were regularly watered to maintain soil moisture at approximately 70% field capacity. For the drought treatment group, watering was completely withheld for 7 consecutive days, during which soil moisture declined to below 20% field capacity, inducing visible drought stress symptoms. After treatment, phenotypic differences were visually documented, and leaf samples collected for physiological measurements.

Statistical analysis of physiological data

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (version 22.0; SPSS Institute Ltd., Armonk, NY, USA), with data presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. To evaluate differences in physiological parameters across treatments and genotypes, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the least significant difference (LSD) test was applied. Meanwhile, qRT-PCR data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Duncan's multiple range test. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

Results

Physiological characterization of the two maize hybrid cultivars under drought stress

Drought stress responses of the two maize hybrid cultivars, ND476 and ZX978, were evaluated at the tasseling stage, after the maize plants had been exposed to gradual natural drought treatment under field conditions. Compared to the control groups, the leaf RWC of two hybrid cultivars significantly (p < 0.01) decreased as the duration of drought stress prolonged, with ZX978 exhibiting a much steeper decline than ND476 (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, both Pro content and guaiacol POD activity showed significant increases (p < 0.01) in both cultivars from day 3 to day 12 post drought treatment, with ND476 consistently maintaining higher values at all the time points under stress conditions (Fig. 1B-C). Furthermore, the analysis of MDA content indicated that the drought-sensitive genotype ZX978 exhibited higher MDA levels than the tolerant ND476, with a sharp decline observed in ND476 from day 9 to day 12 of drought treatment (Fig. 1D). In summary, under drought conditions, ND476 exhibited higher RWC, Pro content, and POD activity, whereas ZX978 showed relatively higher MDA levels, suggesting that ND476 could better adapt to drought stress than ZX978 which showed greater sensitivity.

Transcriptome analysis of DEGs responsive to drought stress

Transcriptome profiling of two divergent maize hybrid cultivars under drought stress

After removing low-quality reads, a total of 66.75 GB of clean data was generated from the twelve samples. About 66.8 million clean reads, each 150 bp in length, were obtained from the twelve samples, averaging 5.56 million reads per sample (Table S2). Among these high-quality reads, 84.7% to 92.9% were successfully mapped to the maize reference genome B73. Quality assessment metrics, including the Q30 base percentage and GC content, surpassed 96.01% and 55.64%, respectively, demonstrating the reliability and reproducibility of the sequencing data (Table S2). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the relationships among the twelve samples. The results showed that biological replicates within each group clustered closely, while a clear distinction was observed between drought-tolerant and drought-sensitive cultivars (Fig. S1). These findings confirm that the experiment was highly reproducible and suitable for further analysis.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

To detect the transcriptional variations that occur in response to drought stress, genes of two contrasting cultivars (in terms of drought tolerance) were tested for differential expression between the well-watered and water-deficit conditions using Cuffdiff software package. Within the tolerant genotype ND476, a total of 658 DEGs displayed differential expression before and after drought treatment, with 399 DEGs being up-regulated and 259 down-regulated (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, in the sensitive genotype ZX978, 1088 DEGs including 923 up-regulated and 175 down-regulated were identified before and after drought treatment (Fig. 2A). The identified DEGs were further screened by Venn diagram analysis, whereupon 603 DEGs (365 up-regulated and 238 down-regulated) were observed to be specifically expressed in drought-tolerant genotype ND476. A total of 1043 DEGs were unique to drought-sensitive genotype ZX978, with 878 being up-regulated and 165 down-regulated (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, 55 DEGs commonly shared between the two cultivars under drought treatment were hierarchical clustering analyzed to observe their differential expression patterns (Fig. 2D).

Taken together, these results signpost the important functional metabolic processes and pathways altered in response to drought stress in maize at the transcriptional level, that can be targeted for further dissection of drought tolerance mechanisms.

Validation of DEGs by qRT-PCR

To validate RNA-Seq results, qRT-PCR analysis of 12 randomly selected genes related to drought stress response was performed. These genes were selected based on their expression patterns being changed remarkably in response to drought treatment as revealed by gene differential expression data, and their functions as depicted by the GO and MapMan enrichment analyses. Our results showed that all the tested genes presented similar expression patterns as the differential analysis results from RNA-seq (Fig. S3A). Interestingly, a high consistence (correlation coefficient, R2, of 92.11) between the qRT-PCR and RNA-seq was observed (Fig. S3B). In a nutshell, the qRT-PCR analysis results confirmed our transcriptomics analysis-based findings.

Proteomic analysis of DEPs responsible for drought stress

Analysis of drought-responsive differentially abundant proteins (DEPs)

With the help of Mascot software (version 2.2), 17 5356 spectra were matched with known spectra (Uniprot Zea mays L. database: 132339–2018.01.12), 68 234 peptides, 56 163 unique peptides, and 5451 proteins were identified from twelve samples (Fig. S4). The PCA results showed a clear separation between the drought-sensitive cultivar ZX978 and the drought-tolerant cultivar ND476 (Fig. S5). Interestingly, the replicates of each treatment clustered together and were consistent with the PCA results of RNA-Seq. Based on the criteria |FC|> 1.3 or < 0.77 (p-value < 0.05), 187 DEPs (90 up-regulated and 97 down-regulated) were observed in the tolerant cultivar ND476 before and after drought treatment. In the sensitive cultivar ZX978, 271 DEPs, including 178 up-regulated and 93 down-regulated, were identified before and after drought treatment (Fig. 2A). The identified DEPs were further screened by Venn diagram analysis. Resultantly, 153 DEPs were observed to be specific to ND476 under drought stress conditions, among which 75 were up-regulated and 78 down-regulated. Of the 237 DEPs unique to ZX978, 154 were up-regulated and 83 down-regulated (Fig. 2C). Meanwhile, 34 DEPs that were commonly identified in the two cultivars under drought treatment were analyzed by hierarchical clustering to observe their differential expression patterns (Fig. 2E).

Analysis of DEPs enrichment into GO terms, and classification of drought-responsive DEGs by MapMan

To gain a comprehensive understanding of drought-induced proteomic changes, GO enrichment analysis was performed using agriGO. In the drought-tolerant genotype ND476, key biological processes such as translation (GO:0006412), peptide biosynthetic process (GO:0043043), and cellular component assembly (GO:0022607) were significantly enriched (Fig. S6A). The GO terms metal ion binding (GO:0046872), antioxidant activity (GO:0016209), and cation binding (GO:0043169) were apparent under the MF category, whereas chloroplast (GO:0009507), plastid (GO:0009536), and cytoplasmic part (GO:0044444) were the apparent CC locations for the DEPs. Additionally, the GO terms response to stress(GO:0006950) and response to stimulus (GO:0050896) were enriched, highlighting ND476’s adaptive mechanisms under drought stress (Table 1).

In contrast, the drought-sensitive genotype ZX978 exhibited significant enrichment of organ nitrogen compound metabolism (GO:1,901,564), small molecule metabolism (GO:0044281), and photosynthesis (GO:0015979) within the BP category (Fig. S6B). In the MF category, binding (GO:0005488) and ion binding (GO:0043167) were most apparent. For the CC functions, cytoplasm (GO:0005737), chloroplast (GO:0009507), and plastid (GO:0009536) were the most significantly enriched terms. Meanwhile, response to stress(GO:0006950) and response to stimulus (GO:0050896) were also enriched, indicating shared and distinct stress adaptation pathways in ZX978 (Table 1).

Further, functional enrichment analysis using MapMan identified key DEPs in ND476 associated with transport, photosynthesis, the TCA cycle, peroxidases, and ribosomal protein synthesis (Fig. 3C, Table 2). In contrast, ZX978 showed significant enrichment of DEPs in amino acid metabolism, glycolysis, transport, abiotic stress response, light reaction in photosynthesis, and hormone metabolism of abscisic acid (Fig. 3D, Table 2).

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis of DEPs

To predict how drought stress signals are transmitted within maize leaf cells to regulate specific cellular functions, we conducted a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using the web-based tool STRING 10.5 (http://www.string-db.org/; accessed 1 May 2020). By selecting DEPs with confidence scores above 0.7, we identified two major hub networks and four interacting protein pairs in the drought-tolerant cultivar ND476 (Fig. 4A).The primary network consisted of eleven proteins, primarily associated with ribosomal translation and RNA processing (Supplementary Table S3). The second cluster comprised of eight proteins involved in photosynthesis (Table S3). Besides, four protein pairs were predicted to participate in the drought stress response (Fig. 4A). In the drought-sensitive cultivar ZX978, PPI analysis revealed one large and one small cluster, along with seven interacting protein pairs (Fig. 4B).

Correlation of transcriptome and proteome data

To evaluate the congruence between RNA-Seq and iTRAQ, we performed a global correlation analysis based on the mRNA and protein data. Of the quantified proteins from iTRAQ, 85.31% and 85.67% were detected in the transcriptomic profiles of ND476 and ZX978, respectively (Fig. 5A). A scatter plot analysis based on the log 2-transformed mRNA and protein ratios was generated to show the distribution of the differentially expressed genes and differentially expressed proteins. The results revealed poor correlations between the expression levels of the quantified proteins and their corresponding mRNAs (ND476 rPearson correlation = 0.0441, and ZX978 rPearson correlation = 0.1679) (Fig. 5B,C), suggesting inconsistency between mRNA transcription and protein expression. Among the 658 DEGs and 187 DEPs identified in ND476, only 6 were commonly regulated both transcriptionally and translationally in response to drought (Fig. 5D), of which four genes exhibited similar trends whilst two genes showed the opposite trends at the mRNA and protein levels (Table 3). Extracellular ribonuclease LE (Zm00001d022630), involved in secondary metabolites of phenylpropanoid, and ribonuclease-3 (Zm00001d032186), related to RNA phosphodiester bond hydrolysis, showed increased abundance at transcription level, but decreased abundance at translation level. Natterin-4 (Zm00001d004344) was up-regulated at both transcript and protein levels, whilst guaiacol peroxidase-1 (Zm00001d040702), putative uncharacterized protein (Zm00001d011461) and CP12-1 (Zm00001d044925) were down-regulated at both transcript and protein levels. In the sensitive line ZX978, 7 DEPs could match to DEGs which showed up-regulated trend at both transcript and protein levels (Fig. 5E). In response to drought, protein P21 (Zm00001d033455), stress-induced protein-1 (Zm00001d021901) related to defense response, pathogenesis-related protein-1 (Zm00001d018734) involved in plant hormone signal transduction, and 23.6 kDa heat shock protein mitochondrial (Zm00001d052194) involved in response to heat all showed increased abundance at both transcript and protein levels (Table 3).

Candidate drought-responsive genes function validation in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana

Bioinformatics analysis revealed ZmPOD DEG as commonly regulated at both transcriptional and translational levels. Additionally, we observed ZmRAV1 and ZmTPP were specifically expressed in the drought-tolerant line ND476 under drought condition. Through GO annotation and KEGG enrichment analysis, we also found that peroxidases, starch and sucrose metabolism, and RNA regulation of transcription were significantly enriched after drought treatments, at both transcript and protein levels. By juxtaposing our analysis results with information from the literature, we hypothesized ZmPOD, ZmRAV1 and ZmTPP to potentially contribute to maize drought tolerance, and then functionally verified them in model plant Arabidopsis.

At the germination stage, under normal conditions (0 mmol/L mannitol), the root lengths of transgenic Arabidopsis lines were comparable to those of the wild type (WT). However, under osmotic stress induced by 200 mmol/L and 300 mmol/L mannitol on 1/2 MS medium, transgenic Arabidopsis plants exhibited significantly enhanced root growth (Fig. 6A). Specifically, at 300 mmol/L mannitol, transgenic lines had approximately 30–50% greater root lengths compared to WT, indicating improved osmotic stress tolerance during early development (Fig. 6B).

Sensitivity of ZmPOD, ZmRAV1, and ZmTPP over-expressing seedlings to mannitol stress. A Growth of wild-type and over-expressing seedlings at different concentrations of mannitol; (B) Statistical graphs of root length of wild-type and over-expressing seedlings under different mannitol concentrations, p < 0.05

At the seedling stage (25-day-old plants), after a 7-day drought treatment, transgenic plants maintained more robust growth, with less wilting and dehydration symptoms compared to WT controls. Quantitative real-time PCR analysis showed that the expression levels of the three candidate genes under drought stress treatment were significantly higher in all transgenic lines compared to the WT. Genomic DNA was extracted from transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana plants to verify the integration of a drought-resistance gene using PCR amplification. All Arabidopsis plants tested positive for the transgene, demonstrating stable transformation and presence of the drought-resistance gene marker (Figure S7). After 7 days of drought stress, the leaf relative water content (RWC) and survival rates of all transgenic lines were significantly higher than those of WT (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7).

The physiological characterization of transgenic Arabidopsis lines overexpressing three different candidate genes under both control and drought conditions revealed distinct drought tolerance mechanisms. All the transgenic lines exhibited improved performance compared to the WT. Under drought stress, transgenic lines maintained significantly higher leaf RWC and chlorophyll content, accumulated more SOD, POD and proline, and exhibited the lowest MDA levels (Figure S8), indicating enhanced photosynthetic stability, osmotic adjustment, and membrane integrity, respectively. Overall, these results demonstrate that the validated maize candidate genes contribute to enhanced drought tolerance in Arabidopsis by promoting root growth under osmotic stress, improving antioxidant defense mechanisms and maintaining membrane integrity under drought conditions.

Discussion

Drought stress at the tasseling stage significantly affects maize plant growth, reproduction, and productivity [41]. Therefore, understanding how maize plants respond to drought stress is crucial for reproductive-stage drought tolerance improvement and optimization of yield. Here, we have performed comparative transcriptome and proteomic, and integrated, analyses, of two contrasting maize hybrid cultivars responding to drought stress at the tasseling stage. We report key DEGs, DEPs, and regulatory mechanisms involved in maize drought stress tolerance. Besides, comparative physiological analysis of the two maize cultivars under drought stress buttresses the “omics” analysis results.

Maize hybrid cultivars` contrasting physiological responses to drought stress

Different genotypes exhibit varying responses to drought and other abiotic stresses, at multiple levels of biological organization, and across different growth stages [42, 43]. In this study, our findings demonstrated a significant decline in leaf RWC in both hybrid cultivars under drought stress conditions. However, the drought-tolerant cultivar ND476 consistently maintained a higher leaf RWC than the sensitive genotype ZX978 throughout the stress exposure period (Fig. 1A). The higher leaf RWC in ND476 possibly helped to minimize cell turgor loss and structural damage. Previously, Zenda et al. observed significantly higher leaf RWC in the drought-tolerant maize inbred line YE8112 than in the sensitive inbred line MO17, which helped the drought-tolerant line to better adapt to drought stress at the seedling stage [20].

Plants preserve membrane and protein stability, as well as cell integrity under stress conditions by maintaining turgor and osmotic adjustment, which helps to mitigate ROS-induced damage [44]. Peroxidases (PODs) primarily scavenge hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) to prevent ROS-induced cell damage [45]. In this study, ND476 exhibited higher POD activity than ZX978 under drought stress conditions (Fig. 1B), suggesting a stronger ROS detoxification capacity in ND476. Additionally, the greater accumulation of proline in ND476 under drought stress conditions (Fig. 1C) may have helped ND476 to sustain turgor pressure, which contributed to its enhanced drought tolerance compared to ZX978 [46]. Meanwhile, MDA is a byproduct of lipid peroxidation often used as an indicator of cell membrane damage [19]. Here, both maize hybrid cultivars showed increased MDA levels under drought stress, suggesting that drought stress induced membrane lipid peroxidation and ROS-triggered cell injury. However, the drought-tolerant genotype ND476 exhibited significantly lower MDA content compared to the sensitive genotype ZX978, indicating a better ROS detoxification system in ND476, which likely contributed to improved cell membrane stability (Fig. 1D). Taken together, the two maize hybrids exhibited contrasting physiological responses to drought stress, with ND476 showing better drought tolerance than ZX978.

Cell wall and signaling related genes are vital for maize survival under drought

In this study, numerous cell wall metabolism-associated genes were up-regulated in ND476 under drought stress (Fig. 8). Notably, β-expansins, O-glycosyl hydrolase, and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) proteins were expressed at both the transcript and protein levels (Fig. 8).

Drought stress-related genes and proteins altered in response to 12-day drought treatment. Genes (up-red, down-green) and proteins (up-yellow, down-blue) that were differentially expressed between well-watered and drought conditions at the transcript and protein levels in drought tolerant hybrid cultivar ND476

β-expansins, along with α-expansins, crucially regulate cell wall remodeling, including during tissue elongation [47]. O-glycosyl hydrolases has been implicated in abiotic stress response, phytohormone activation, cell wall remodeling, and lignification in rice [48]. LRR proteins, thought to be cell wall-associated, contribute to cell wall composition and extensibility modifications [49]. Glycine-rich and protein-rich proteins crucially underpin cell wall remodeling-mediated drought stress tolerance in maize at the flowering stage [50]. Previously, in a study involving two maize genotypes with contrasting lodging resistance, drought stress induced significant changes in the expression of cell wall-related genes in the tolerant inbred line, revealing genotype-specific cell wall strategies for drought tolerance [51].

Meanwhile, stress sensing starts at the cell wall and the signal is transduced into the cell interior via the plasma membrane (PM), aided by a series of PM-localized receptors, and signaling proteins and secondary messengers such as Ca2+, sugars and ROS [52, 53]. In this study, the expression of signaling related genes or proteins (such as G-proteins and protein kinases) was also altered in the tolerant genotype ND476 under drought stress (Fig. 8). GTP-binding proteins (small G-proteins) are a group of regulatory GTP hydrolyses related to cell signaling [50]. Protein kinases underpin stress signaling transduction by initiating phosporylation cascades and modulating transcription factors which activate the downstream drought stress-responsive genes [54, 55]. Taken together, the expression of cell wall-related proteins and protein kinases was upregulated to coordinate effective drought stress sensing, signaling and response in the drought-tolerant maize cultivar ND476.

Transcription factor related genes are essential in regulating drought stress response at the transcript level

Transcription factors (TFs) critically control multiple downstream stress-responsive genes (Wang et al., 2016). Several TF families, including ZmbZIP, bHLH, WRKY, NAC, and MYB play critical roles in drought stress response [50, 54]. Herein, more than 26 drought-responsive TF families were differentially expressed in the tolerant genotype ND476 under drought stress conditions, including five MYB (2 up- and 3 down-), five bHLH (3 up- and 2 down-), four AP2/EREBP (all down-), four NAC (2 up- and 2 down-), three C3H zinc finger family (1 up- and 2 down-), three HB (all up-), two WRKY (1 up- and 1 down-) and two C2H2 zinc finger family (all down-regulated) (Fig. 8).

The role of TFs in regulating abiotic stress tolerance in various crops, including maize, has been extensively studied [55–57]. A transcriptome analysis investigating drought-responsive genes in two contrasting maize inbred lines identified five bZIP, three MYB, and one AP2/EREBP TFs associated with drought stress adaptation [58]. Similarly, several TFs, including C2H2, NAC, bHLH and MYB, exhibited expression patterns closely linked to plant water potential, highlighting their involvement in maize drought response during flowering [56]. Further, multiple NAC, MYB, and WRKY genes have been identified, cloned, and characterized for their roles abiotic stress response in maize and model Arabidopsis. For instance, overexpression of ZmSNAC1 in Arabidopsis significantly improved drought tolerance at the germination stage, indicating that ZmSNAC1 plays a positive role in drought resistance [59]. ZmMYB3R over-expression enhanced maize resilience to drought and salt stress [60]. Taken together, the differential expression of TF genes likely underpins the drought tolerance of the maize hybrid cultivar ND476, by orchestrating complex regulatory networks that enhance stress adaptation.

Plant hormones play vital roles in drought stress response regulation

Phytohormones essentially regulate plant growth, development, and response to stress [61]. In this study, the expression of several ABA, brassinosteroids (BRs), jasmonic acid (JA), and auxin-related genes and proteins was altered in response to drought stress (Fig. 8). The role of ABA as an important abiotic stress signaling molecule in plants is well-known [62]. Under drought conditions, plants accumulate ABA, which subsequently activate downstream stress responses [63]. ABA serves as a key regulator of gene expression, signaling cascades, and synthesis of essential protective compounds that mitigate water loss [64]. BRs contribute to stress tolerance by inducing the expression of stress-responsive genes, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, maintaining photosynthetic efficiency, and promoting osmoprotectant accumulation under drought stress conditions [61, 65, 66].

Auxin regulates the expression of three major family genes (commonly known as primary auxin response genes), viz, Aux/IAA, GH3, and SAUR [67]. Auxin also participate in stress and defense responses [68, 69]. Thus, in this study, auxin-related genes were altered in response to drought stress. However, their exact role remains to be elucidated. Meanwhile, the up-regulated expression of JA and salicylic acid (SA)-related genes is consistent with previous findings where JA and SA-related genes were up-regulated in common buckwheat seedlings under drought stress conditions [60]. Overall, crosstalk between different plant hormones create a complex network of synergistic and antagonistic relationships, which collectively regulate maize responses to drought stress.

Response to stress- and response to stimuli- related genes under drought conditions

Our GO enrichment analysis of genes expressed in the drought-tolerant genotype ND476 identified 46 DEGs and 5 DEPs significantly associated with the GO term “response to stress” (GO:0006950). Further examination revealed that these genes were also enriched in the GO term “response to stimuli” (GO:0050896). This group comprised several heat shock proteins (HSPs), dehydrins (DHNs), peroxidases, glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), among others (Supplementary Table S4). Additionally, we observed that small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) and HSP70 were up-regulated under drought conditions. HSPs are well-known in plant stress protection, by ensuring proper protein folding, preventing aggregation, and maintaining protein homeostasis under abiotic stress conditions [70]. DHNs, a specialized subgroup of late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins [71], also show increased abundance under drought stress, aiding in whet drought tolerance [72]. DHNs are widely present in plants, and are responsive to ABA, with their expression induced by ABA, suggesting their involvement in ABA-mediated stress response [73].

Meanwhile, eight peroxidases were down-regulated while one was up-regulated under drought stress (Table S5). In addition, one MAPK was up-regulated, while another was down-regulated (Table S5). MAPK signaling cascades essentially orchestrate enzyme activation/deactivation via phosphorylation/dephosphorylation, respectively, enabling rapid and specific signal transduction in response to external stimuli [74, 75]. However, down-regulation of these key-stress responsive enzymes and kinases here may suggest a complex regulatory network involved in redox homeostasis, signaling pathways, and abiotic stress response [76].

Secondary metabolism related pathways play vital roles in response to drought

As previously observed, secondary metabolites biosynthesis crucially regulate plant drought stress response [20]. In this study, numerous secondary metabolites biosynthesis-associated enzymes were activated in the drought-tolerant cultivar ND476 at both transcript and protein levels under drought stress conditions (Fig. 8). Whereas primary metabolites are essential for plant growth and development, secondary metabolites primarily contribute to ecological functions, including plant defense and environmental stress response [77]. Secondary metabolism has been demonstrated to enhance grapevine tolerance to prolonged drought stress [78]. Similarly, several secondary metabolites biosynthesis-related pathways, including “flavone and flavonol biosynthesis” and “isoflavonoid and flavonoid biosynthesis”, were altered in response to abiotic stress in Rehmannia glutinosa [79]. Further, a conjoint omics analysis study also revealed the significant role of secondary metabolites biosynthesis-related genes in maize roots adaptation to drought stress [80]. Taken together, the observed gene expression changes related to secondary metabolites biosynthesis following water deprivation may indicate a broad metabolic shift in maize as an adaptive response to drought stress.

The weak correlation between differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs)

Our analysis of the mRNA transcript and protein expression data revealed a weak correlation (ND476 rPearson correlation = 0.0441, and ZX978 rPearson correlation = 0.1679) between the DEGs and DEPs (Fig. 5B, C). Whilst systems biology literature has subtly assumed the existence of a proportional correlation between transcript and protein expressions analyzed for the same tissue, analysis of transcript and protein expression data from similar tissues under similar conditions has, however, disapproved this assumption in several studies [81–83]. This discrepancy might be due to several reasons, including post-transcriptional regulation, translational efficiency, time-dependent sampling differences between transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, and technical limitations in protein detection sensitivity.

Firstly, post-transcriptional factors such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and protein localization may create varied half-lives of proteins, causing variation in protein expression and mRNA transcript [84, 85]. Secondly, several factors that influence translational efficiency affect mRNA transcript-protein correlation. The physical properties of the transcript, including the whole structure of the mRNA and codon-bias, influence translation efficiency. Temperature change may alter mRNA conformation, thereby influencing translational efficiency [86]. Thirdly, ribosome density (number of ribosome in a transcriptional unit) and ribosome-associated mRNA (mRNA that enters ribosome for translation) also influence translational efficiency, with ribosome-associated mRNA exhibiting better correlation with proteins than typical mRNA expression [87]. Additionally, those mRNAs` occupancy time in ribosome has an effect on translational efficiency [81].

Moreover, time-dependent sampling differences between transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, technical limitations in protein detection sensitivity, experimental errors in sample collection, and data extraction approach in mRNA and protein expression might have introduced extrinsic noise that might have significantly affected the correlation between mRNA and protein expression [81, 88–90]. Nonetheless, an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis can still offer vital insights that may not be decoded from individual approaches [81].

Function Analysis of ZmPOD, ZmRAV1, and ZmTPP

In this study, we functionally validated three maize drought-responsive genes, ZmPOD, ZmRAV1, and ZmTPP in Arabidopsis. Our findings revealed that transgenic Arabidopsis lines over-expressing these genes had enhanced drought tolerance at both germination and seedling stages. Notably, expressions of these genes were consistent with their transcriptional and translational regulation patterns observed in the drought-tolerant maize line ND476, suggesting that these genes possibly exhibit consistent expression patterns under drought, and may be involved in maize drought tolerance. ZmPOD encodes a peroxidase enzyme, involved in scavenging ROS generated under drought-induced oxidative stress. The significantly higher POD and SOD activities observed in transgenic lines suggest that ZmPOD contributes to the ROS detoxification, maintaining cellular redox homeostasis, and preserving membrane integrity [91]. As previously observed, enhanced POD activity correlates with increased drought stress tolerance in species such as rice and wheat [92–94].

ZmRAV1, a member of the RAV transcription factor family, was specifically expressed in ND476 genotype under drought stress, implying a regulatory role. RAV family TFs have been reported to integrate stress signals and modulate down-stream gene expressions related to growth inhibition and stress adaptation [95]. Here, our GO enrichment analysis results revealed the"regulation of transcription"pathway to be activated at both transcription and translational levels, supporting the hypothesis that ZmRAV1 may act as a central regulator coordinating drought-responsive gene expression. ZmTPP encodes a trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatase, an enzyme involved in the trehalose biosynthesis pathway, which has been associated with stress tolerance through its role in carbohydrate metabolism and osmoprotection [96]. Our KEGG enrichment analysis revealed"starch and sucrose metabolism"to be significantly enriched under drought, indicating that ZmTPP orchestrates trehalose accumulation or possibly modulate sugar signaling and energy homeostasis under drought stress [97]. Collectively, our data provide evidence that ZmPOD, ZmRAV1, and ZmTPP enhance drought tolerance, via distinct but complementary mechanisms, including ROS scavenging, transcriptional regulation, and osmotic adjustment. Based on these findings, considered together with other physiological, and conjoint transcriptome and proteome analyses results described and discussed above, we have proposed a model summarizing the possible key mechanisms regulating drought tolerance in maize at the tasseling stage (Fig. 9).

Proposed model of drought-stress tolerance in maize tasseling stage, based on findings of this study. The model illustrates a multilevel regulatory network activated in response to drought stress

Limitations of the study

Notwithstanding the vital insights offered by our research on the drought-responsive genes and pathways mediating maize drought tolerance at the tasseling stage, there are two main limitations to this study that we need to point out. First, the correlation between transcript and protein expression was generally low, which is a known phenomenon due to post-transcriptional and post-translational regulations. However, this discrepancy suggests that transcriptomic data alone may not fully predict protein-level functional outcomes, emphasizing the need for more refined time-course or tissue-specific proteomic analyses. Secondly, although three candidate genes were functionally validated in Arabidopsis, cross-species validation may not fully reflect gene function in maize due to differences in regulatory context and physiology. Future studies involving gene knockouts or overexpression directly in maize would better elucidate their native functional roles. Nonetheless, our findings provide a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underpinning the response of maize to drought stress.

Conclusion

Here, we have employed an integrated transcriptomic, proteomic and physiological analysis approach to decipher differential responses of tolerant ND476 and sensitive ZX978 maize hybrids to field drought stress at the tasseling stage, unraveling the molecular mechanisms underpinning maize drought tolerance. At the physiological level, the drought-tolerant genotype ND476 demonstrated better adaptation to drought stress, primarily due to its enhanced osmotic regulation and ROS scavenging capabilities. RNA-seq transcriptome and iTRAQ proteome analyses identified 1701 and 424 drought-responsive DEGs and DEPs, respectively. Chief among them were those related to stress signal transduction, cell-wall remodeling, cellular redox homeostasis, and phytohormonal regulation. MapMan analysis revealed transcription factor regulation and secondary metabolites biosynthesis to critically mediate maize drought stress response at mRNA-level, whilst photosynthesis is important at the protein-level. However, the observed weak correlation between DEGs and DEPs may suggest maize drought stress response at the tasseling stage to be largely regulated post-transcriptionally. Overall, this study enhances our understanding of the molecular networks mediating maize drought tolerance at the tasseling stage and highlights the utility of integrating multi-omics with reverse genetics approaches to uncover key drought stress-responsive genes and pathways.
