Physicochemical characterization and sensory enhancement of cold plasma treated black whole wheat flour
Authors: Teng Zhang, Ling He, Meng Zhang, Hao Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study examined the effects of cold plasma (CP) treatment on the physicochemical properties and sensory quality of black whole wheat flour (BWWF). Various factors including nutrient composition, color, amino acids, aroma, particle size, microstructure, antioxidant activity, and water migration were analyzed before and after CP treatment. The findings revealed that CP treatment had a minimal impact on the baseline nutrient composition of BWWF, but significantly improved its free amino acid profile, enhancing its nutritional value. The treated BWWF had an improved surface color, appeared brighter and yellower, and gave off a pleasant mellow aroma, while removing unpleasant flavor. The total phenolic content of BWWF increased while the flavonoid and anthocyanin content decreased after the treatment. CP-treated BWWF underwent aggregation cross-linking in the microstructure, and the content of bound water decreased, but the stability increased. In conclusion, CP treatment had great potential to improve the physicochemical properties and sensory quality of BWWF.

Introduction

Wheat, the second largest grain crop globally, was a crucial resource for human survival. Its derivatives could be used to produce a wide variety of edible pasta products, which were enjoyed by people worldwide. In recent years, as people's quality of life improved, there had been a growing demand for wheat products that offered enhanced nutrition, functionality, and health benefits. This had led to a rising market for the development and promotion of colored grains. Black wheat, favored by the food industry as an important resource for black food, not only retained the processing characteristics of regular wheat but also boasted a higher nutritional content1. Research had shown that black wheat was abundant in wheat protein, dietary fiber, starch, and minerals, among other essential components. Additionally, it contained higher levels of antioxidants such as phenolic acids, flavonoids, and anthocyanins compared to ordinary white wheat, which contributed to its black coloration. These natural antioxidants could effectively interact with free radicals in the human body. Consistently incorporating them into one's diet helped maintain the body's normal antioxidant function, thereby potentially slowing down the aging process and preventing various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases2. Furthermore, recent studies by Liu et al.3 indicated that black wheat might benefit individuals with diabetes. Not only could it provide the required energy for diabetic patients, but it could also enhance the sense of satiety. This finding opened up new possibilities for developing weight loss foods using black wheat. However, it was worth noting that the antioxidants in wheat were primarily concentrated in the bran layer, whereas the endosperm contained lower amounts. To minimize antioxidant loss and achieve superior milling results, it was advisable to process black wheat into whole wheat flour4. This processing method offered several advantages, including fine powder texture, high yield, low energy consumption, and high-quality flour production. Therefore, the primary form of consumption for black wheat was typically in the form of Black whole wheat flour (BWWF). However, there was still a lack of research on further enhancing the quality and processing characteristics of BWWF, which posed a crucial challenge to be addressed.

Various techniques were explored to enhance the stability and quality of whole-wheat flour in cereal production. These techniques included the use of flour improvers, high-pressure processing, microwaves, and ionizing radiation. However, it was important to note that some chemical additives might have had toxic side effects5. Similarly, high-pressure processing at levels above 50 MPa could lead to increased hardness and undesirable changes in protein structure6. Microwaves, on the other hand, were found to negatively impact the physical properties of whole-wheat flour. Additionally, the high instrumentation costs associated with ionizing radiation treatment had to be taken into consideration7. Given these factors, it became apparent that alternative technologies were required for improving the quality of whole-wheat flour.

Cold plasma (CP) treatment was one such promising technology. It was a non-thermal and environmentally friendly pretreatment method widely utilized in various applications such as fruit and vegetable treatment8, microbial inactivation9, film treatment10, and grain processing11. The dielectric barrier discharge in air generated by CP produced a large amount of plasma (including photons, ions, as well as free electrons.) and ozone. This combination of active species enhanced the treatment effects on materials. Several studies reported notable advancements in the CP treatment of grain flours. For instance, Park et al.12 demonstrated the significant reduction of Escherichia coli in wheat flour through atmospheric cold plasma (ACP) treatment. Furthermore, this treatment led to improvements in the thermal, pasting, and gelling properties of the wheat flour. Similarly, CP treatment showed positive effects on the physicochemical and functional properties of steamed grain rice flour7. Another study conducted by Guiyun et al.13 highlighted how ACP treatment increased the γ-aminobutyric acid content in buckwheat, thereby improving its anti-hypertensive functional components. Despite the potential of CP treatment to enhance cereal flour quality, there was a scarcity of research on the surface CP treatment and its impact on the quality and physicochemical properties of BWWF.

The primary objective of this study was to examine the impact of CP treatment utilizing a dielectric-blocked discharge (DBD) type device on enhancing the physicochemical characteristics and sensory attributes of BWWF. Specifically, the study focused on evaluating changes in nutrient composition, color, amino acid fraction, aroma composition, particle size, microstructure, antioxidant activity, and water migration status of BWWF before and after CP treatment. The aim was to obtain improved traits in BWWF and offer insights into the processing approach for this particular type of black cereal resource.

Materials and methods

Materials

The raw material, BWWF, originated from the "Xinong Black Spike" variety developed by the College of Agriculture at Northwest Agriculture and Forestry University. Raw material BWWF from the Northwest Agriculture and Forestry University, College of Agriculture cultivated “Xinong Black Spike” varieties (XH-88), XH-88 is a black large grain 86306 line through the mixed genealogy method of continuous selection of varieties, because of the large spike tentatively named “Xinong Black Spike”. “Xinong Black Spike” is characterized by high yield, strong disease resistance, excellent quality, etc. It is a kind of high-protein content, high-quality nutritional function of bio-enhanced health food raw materials. The suitable sowing period is mid-October in Shaanxi, Henan, and Anhui Provinces. To conduct further experiments, these raw materials were stored in a well-ventilated and dry location. All the required chemicals and reagents were procured from reputable suppliers such as Beijing Solexpro Technology Co. and Aladdin Reagents (Shanghai) Co.

CP treatment of BWWF

The plasma instrument utilized in this study consisted of a CTP-2000 K-type CP experimental power supply, a DBD-50 type reactor, and a TGDC2-1 type contact regulator. The device was designed with upper and lower circular plate electrodes, both having a diameter of 550 mm. The distance between these plates could be adjusted using the fixing knob. During operation, the upper layer of media (consisting of a 100 mm diameter quartz glass sheet with a thickness of 1 mm) was secured with a media card. The sample is spread on the lower medium (90 mm diameter, 2 mm thickness quartz glass sheet14.

The sample, weighing 1.5 g of BWWF, was evenly pressed into a 450 mm diameter circle using a mold, ensuring consistency and stability during processing. For the experiment, the samples were subjected to CP treatment at a voltage of 40 V, a current of 0.9 ± 0.1 A, and a spacing of 1 mm between the polar plates. The treatment duration varied, with samples exposed for 2 min, 4 min, and 6 min. Repeat several times to get a sufficient amount of flour. As a comparison, untreated samples were also included and kept as controls for subsequent analysis. Following treatment, both the treated and control samples were stored at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C) until further analysis could be conducted.

Physicochemical properties of BWWF

Color

The color of BWWF was referred to the method reported by Argaw et al.15 with slight modifications. A portable colorimeter was used for the determination of flour color, and the CGV (Dry) mode was selected and calibrated with standard white tiles before testing. An appropriate amount of BWWF was weighed and pressed into a dough block using mechanical pressure, then spread evenly and flatly on the bottom of the observer and completely covered for colorimetric determination. Moisture, protein, ash and lipid content

The moisture, protein, ash contents and lipid content in BWWF were determined using the method outlined by AOAC (1995). The protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl method. In this method, the sample was digested with concentrated sulfuric acid and then distilled with alkali. The nitrogen content obtained from the distillate was used to calculate the protein content. The ash content was determined by placing a well-mixed flour sample in a muffle furnace and heating it to 550 °C. The sample was burned until a constant weight was achieved. After cooling, the sample was weighed, and the ash content was calculated using the dry method. Lipids are determined by Soxhlet extraction, where the sample is placed in an extractor in contact with the solvent and then heated and cooled in repeated cycles to extract the solvent, and the lipids are extracted from the sample by reflux. The amount of lipid extracted is then determined by evaporating the solvent and weighing the residue.

Trace element content

In order to determine the concentrations of Ca, Fe, Cu, Zn, Mn, K and Na in flour, the method of Gu et al.16 was used in this experiment with appropriate modifications. First, 0.4 g of BWWF sample was accurately weighed in a small beaker, to which 5 mL of concentrated nitric acid-perchloric acid (4:1) mixture was added and heated to abate at a low temperature until the solution was transparent and evaporated until a thick white smoke was emitted, and then a little bit of secondary distilled water and 2.0 mL of OP solution were added, heated to near-boiling and cooled before transferring it to a 50 mL volumetric flask and fixed the volume. The blank solution was prepared without adding the sample and the rest of the process was consistent. For the preparation of standard solution, the appropriate amount of standard reserve solution was accurately pipetted into five 50 mL volumetric flasks, 2.5 mL of concentrated nitric acid-perchloric acid mixed acid blank solution and 2.0 mL of OP solution were added, and the volume was fixed with secondary distilled water. For the determination of the sample solution, after setting the working parameters of the instrument, the absorbance values of the standard solutions of the elements Ca, Fe, Cu, Zn, Mn, K and Na were firstly determined, and the instrument automatically plotted the standard solution curve, then the sample solution was determined, and the measured absorbance values were used to calculate the contents of trace elements in wheat flour through the standard curve. Phosphorus (P) was determined using a colorimetric method17. The analysis was repeated for each sample and the results are reported in dry matter (g/kg).

Essential and non-essential amino acid analysis (amino acid analyzer, AAA)

The original sample was dried to eliminate moisture effects, then accurately weighed to 50 mg and transferred to the hydrolysis tube. Consequently, the final amino acid content was reported in mg/g dry weight (DW). Add 10 ml of analytical grade hydrochloric acid in a 1:1 ratio, and then introduce nitrogen into the tube for 30 s before sealing it. Subsequently, hydrolyze the mixture in an oil bath at 110 °C for 24 h. Once the hydrolysis is complete, allow the solution to cool to room temperature and then filter it into a 50 ml volumetric flask using a 0.45 um membrane filter. Following this, withdraw 2 ml of the sample for further processing, deacidify it in a rotary evaporator at 45 °C, and continue the drying process until a small amount of solid residue or stain is visible at the bottom of the container. Next, dissolve the residue by adding 2 ml of sample buffer, and filter the solution through a 0.45 um filter before proceeding with the analysis using the British Biochrom 30+ Amino Acid Analyzer. The instrument is set up with the following conditions: the buffer flow rate is 20 ml/h, while the reaction flow rate is 10 ml/h. The separation column used is a Na-type cationic resin chromatography column, with dimensions of 200 mm in length and 4.6 mm in diameter. UV detection is carried out at wavelengths of 570 nm and 440 nm. The column temperature is programmed to be within the range of 55–65–77 °C, and the reaction tank is maintained at a temperature of 138 °C. A sample size of 50 µl is used for analysis. The detection method involves the separation of amino acids in the separation column, followed by their reaction with ninhydrin. The product was detected by spectrophotometer and the amino acid concentration was determined by post column derivatization. AA content is reported in mg/g (DW) of flour. Essential amino acids are denoted as EAA and non-essential amino acids are denoted as NEAA.

Aroma composition analysis

The methods of T. Zhang et al.14 and Bi et al.18 were referred to and adapted using a Shimadzu GCMS-TQ8050 NX type gas chromatograph-mass spectrometer. Head space solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) was used to extract the aroma compounds contained in BWWF. About 4.0 g of sample was put into a 20 mL screw cap glass vial, and a SPME fiber (0.05/0.03 cm DVB/CAR/ PDMS, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA) was equilibrated at 45 °C for 30 min. The desorbed flavor substances were separated with a capillary column (DB-17MS, 30 m × 0.25 mm). Splitless mode injections were used in this study, and the temperature of the GC column oven was set at 40 °C for 3 min and increased to 120 °C at 4 °C min-1, then increased to 240 °C at 6 °C min-1 for 9 min. The mass spectrometry was set in scan range (mz-1) from 35 to 500 in the positive ion mode, interface temperature was 230 °C, and solvent cut time was 2 min. Particle size analysis (laser particle sizer, LPZ) and microstructure (scanning electron microscope, SEM)

Particle size was determined using a Beckman Coulter LS13320 (USA). A 3 g sample of BWWF was weighed into a test tube and the tube was placed into a Cyclone Dry Powder System (DPS) to determine the particle size of the BWWF and the measurement was repeated three times. The microstructure of BWWF was photographed by SEM (Nano SEM-450, USA). The samples were glued with conductive adhesive, sprayed with gold and fixed on the instrument for observation. The conditions were as follows: accelerating voltage of 3 kV, magnification of 500×, and particle size of 300 μm.

Quantification of total phenols, flavonoids and anthocyanins

Preparation of BWWF extract

Phenolic extracts of BWWF were extracted using 60 % ethanol as the extraction solvent, and the active ingredients were extracted by ultrasonic assistance. The BWWF was accurately weighed 1.0 g in a 50 mL centrifuge tube, added 25 mL of 60 % ethanol solution, was mixed well, and then controlled the ultrasonicator temperature to 40 °C, set the ultrasonic power to 300 W, and treated for 1.0 h. Then the extract was centrifuged on a centrifuge at 5000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant after centrifugation was the BWWF extract.

Total phenolic content

Total phenol content was determined by referring to N. Sharma et al.19 and modified. The Folin-Ciocalteu method was used to determine the total phenolic content by setting the wavelength at 765 nm and measuring the absorbance, using a blank reagent as a reference. Standard curves were made with different concentrations of gallic acid standards (μg/mL). The polyphenol content of BWWF was calculated from the standard curve and the absorbance of the BWWF and the results were expressed in (mg GAE/g), where GAE stands for “Gallic Acid Equivalent”.

Flavonoid content

The flavonoid content was determined regarding A. Sharma et al.20 and modified. NaNO2-Al (NO3)3 colorimetric method was used to determine the flavonoid content, and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm with a blank reagent as reference. Standard curves were made with different concentrations of rutin standards (ug/mL). Based on the standard curve and the absorbance of BWWF, the flavonoid content of BWWF was calculated and the results were expressed in (mg RE/g).

Moisture migration

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) was used to determine the moisture migration of BWWF before and after CP treatment22. The transverse relaxation time (T2) was determined by Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequence. The test parameters were: spectrometer frequency (SF) of 21 MHz, number of sampling points (TD) of 1000088, repeat sampling waiting time (TW) of 3000 ms, number of slices (NS) of 32, echo time (TE) of 1 ms, and number of echoes (NECH) of 5000. inversion of the obtained CPMG relaxation decay curves was performed to obtain T2 and sample relaxation profiles.

Statistical analysis

All parameters were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and differences were tested by Duncan's multiple range test. Figures were plotted using Origin 2023. The aroma compounds contained in BWWF varieties were studied with reference to NIST chemistry web book.

Results and discussion

Changes of color

Changes in colorimetry of BWWF before and after CP treatment are shown in Table 1. The parameters L*, a*, and b* represent the lightness and darkness spectra, the red and green spectra, and the yellow and blue spectra, respectively. The disparity between the CP-treated group and the control group can be explained by ΔE. It was observed that longer treatment times resulted in significant changes in the color quality of the BWWF. This change was attributed to the reaction between plasma actives (mainly ROS and RNS) and the pigmented substances, enzymes, and starch granules present in the BWWF23. The overall trend was that CP treatment had a positive effect on L* and b* values as L* values increased from 87.64 ± 0.01 in the control group to 88.73 ± 0.01 in the CP-6 min group, and b* values increased from 9.77 ± 0.04 in the control group to 10.29 ± 0.02 in the CP-6 min group. These findings were consistent with the visual observations made during the experiment. Similar increases in L* values of grains following CP treatment have been reported by other researchers24–26. On the contrary, a* values exhibited a decreasing trend, resulting in a greenish hue of the samples post-treatment. The changes in L* values can be attributed to the moisture content of the BWWF and the reduction in the activities of amylase, protease, and lipase during CP treatment27. The increase in b* values was associated with the natural pigments in BWWF, leading to an augmentation of the brown pigment after CP treatment. Changes of basic nutrient composition

The basal nutrient indexes of BWWF were analyzed before and after CP treatment, and the results are presented in Fig. 1. In the control group (raw material), the Moisture content was 11.16 ± 0.01 %, protein content was 16.54 ± 0.01 g/100 g, and ash content was 2.34 ± 0.05 %. Significant changes (p < 0.05) in moisture content were observed in the CP-treated group under 40 V CP treatment at all ionization times (2, 4, and 6 min). The moisture content of BWWF decreased from 11.16 to 7.65% with strong voltage (40V) and long time (6 minutes) treatments. This finding is consistent with the studies conducted on rice flour by Thirumdas et al.28 and parboiled rice flour by Chaitanya Sarangapani et al.29. The reduction in moisture content can be attributed to the utilization of moisture for the generation of active substances and surface moisture transfer caused by the etching phenomenon in CP treatment, as discussed by Joy et al.30. However, the decrease in moisture content is beneficial for extending the shelf life of the grains. The protein content of BWWF (16.54%) was significantly higher than that of common wheat varieties (10–15 %). The impact of CP treatment on the protein content of the samples was minimal, with 16.54 g/100 g in the control group and 16.09 g/100 g in the CP-6 min group. This suggests that CP treatment preserve the nutritive value of the protein fractions. Similarly, the ash content was also minimally affected by CP treatment. The CP-treated group showed a slight increase from 2.34 to 2.62 % compared to the control group. This phenomenon can be attributed to the aggregation and cross-linking of substances at the microscopic level during CP treatment, as explained by Y. Huang et al.31. However, these changes did not have a dramatic effect on the overall ash content. In conclusion, CP treatment had a significant effect on the moisture content of BWWF, while protein content was not greatly disturbed. These findings suggest that CP treatment helps to maintain the nutritional quality of BWWF and has the potential to extend its shelf life.Fig. 1Changes in moisture, protein, ash and lipid content of BWWF before and after CP treatment (2, 4 and 6 min). Changes of trace element content

The presence of various minerals, including Ca, Fe, K, Na, Mn, Cu, and Zn, was investigated in BWWF samples at different CP treatment times (Fig. 2). Compared to common wheat, BWWF has a higher content of trace elements, particularly Fe, Cu, and Zn32. The Fe content exhibited a decreasing trend following CP treatment, decreasing from 0.72 g/kg in the control group to 0.11 g/kg in the CP-6 min group. Exposure to plasma current resulted in an 84.72 % reduction in Fe content. This reduction can be attributed to the chemical reaction between the strong oxidizing active substances (ROS and RNS) produced by CP and the metal ions33. However, most of the other elemental contents (Ca, Na, Mn, Cu) remained stable before and after CP treatment. A slight increase in K and Zn levels could be attributed to the redox reaction between the CP and the metal cations in the laboratory environment during ionization, thereby slightly increasing the metal elemental contents34.Fig. 2Changes in mineral content of BWWF before and after CP treatment (2, 4 and 6 min), (A) content of Ca, Fe, K, Na; (B) content of Mn, Cu and Zn.

Changes of amino acid components

The amino acid (AA) composition of BWWF was analyzed before and after CP treatment, and the changes are summarized in Table 2. The findings reveal that BWWF contains a total of 17 amino acids, including 6 essential amino acids (EAA) and 11 non-essential amino acids (NEAA). Comparing the control group with the CP-treated groups, it is evident from Table 2 that the total amino acid content of BWWF was higher in the CP-treated groups, although the individual free amino acids exhibited varying changes. These variations can be attributed to the interaction between CP's active substances and proteins. The strong oxidizing property of reactive oxygen species damaged the protease structure, leading to reduced protease activity and affecting protein hydrolysis. Consequently, this resulted in diverse trends in the content of different amino acids35. Furthermore, CP treatment induced the generation of ionic substances, altering the microenvironment polarity and influencing the conformation of amino acids to some extent36. Reactive oxygen species generated by CP treatment also selectively oxidized certain amino acids, such as Met37. Overall, CP treatment had a positive impact on the free amino acid composition of BWWF. The CP-treated BWWF samples exhibited higher levels of EAA compared to the control group, with significant increases observed in Thr, Val, Leu, and Phe. Additionally, NEAA like Ser, Cys, and Tyr were found in greater abundance in the CP-treated BWWF samples compared to the control group. Notably, Cys showed the most significant increase in the CP-2 min group, with a 25% higher abundance compared to the control group. The ratio of essential amino acids (EAA) to total amino acids (TAA) remained constant at 0.29% across all groups, and the ratio of EAA to non-essential amino acids (NEAA) was consistently 1:1.5. This falls within the recommended range for healthy adults, which is between 1:1 to 1:2. Changes of aroma composition

Aromatic compounds play a vital role in determining the flavor of food and are essential for evaluating food products. The analysis of BWWF was conducted using GC-MS. This technique was chosen because it allows for the detection of pleasant aromas produced during sample processing under controlled processing CP conditions. Fig. 3a and Table 3. present an analysis of the overall characteristics of retention time and signal peak intensity for the compounds detected. In Fig. 3b, a heatmap was utilized to cluster and classify the normalized aroma components of BWWF treated with CP. This heatmap effectively illustrates the differences between the aroma profiles of BWWF before and after CP treatment. The aromatic components of BWWF that were identified and screened can be categorized into six major groups: aldehydes, esters, alcohols, acids, and alkanes. It is worth noting that aldehydes accounted for more than 90 % of the relative content in both the control and CP treatment groups. Conversely, esters and alkanes decreased following CP treatment, while alcohols and acids were newly formed. In the control group, unpleasant flavor such as undecane, dodecane, tetradecane, pentadecane, hexadecane, and phenol were detected. Surprisingly, these substances were eliminated after the CP treatment. Additionally, the CP-treated BWWF exhibited a strong and pleasant aroma, primarily attributed to the presence of nonanal (C9H18O). Nonanal is well-known for its rose and citrus fragrance, as well as its strong, oily scent often found in red and green tea. Its relative content increased from 8.83 % in the control group to 36.91 % in the CP-2 min group, 40.92 % in the CP-4 min group, and 32.40 % in the CP-6 min group. This significant increase corresponded with a decrease in the percentage of hexanal, which gives off an aroma reminiscent of fresh cut grass, from 85.26 % in the control group to 42.18 % in the CP-2 min group. Moreover, the presence of 2-nonenal, which imparts a cucumber and melon aroma, and decanal, known for its citrus and floral fragrance, further enriched and enhanced the positive flavor characteristics of BWWF following CP treatment. According to a study by Coutinho et al.42, CP treatment resulted in the loss of certain volatile compounds and the formation of other compounds. Specifically, carboxylic acids such as heptanoic, butanoic, and n-decanoic were absent, while five major aldehydes (nonanal, hexanal, heptanal, octanal, and decanal) were present.

The microstructure of BWWF before and after CP treatment was observed under SEM, as shown in the SEM image (×500) in Fig. 4b. Compared to the control group, the CP-treated group exhibited polymer production and larger, coarser, and more pronounced particulate material, which is consistent with the findings of Huang et al.31. Changes of total phenols, flavonoids and anthocyanins

The total phenolic content (TPC), flavonoid content (FC) and anthocyanin content (AC) of BWWF were richer than that of common white wheat. The high levels of phenolic compounds contributed to the distinctive yellowish-black color of BWWF. As shown in Fig. 5, the TPC levels increased from 4.48 mg GAE/g to 4.92 mg GAE/g with an increase in CP treatment time, in contrast to the control samples. Conversely, FC and AC levels decreased noticeably, with the CP-6min group showing an 18.76 % reduction in FC and a 9.3 % reduction in AC compared to the control group. According to C. Sarangapani et al.7, CP treatment caused the release of larger glycosidic components, which then degraded into smaller phenolic compounds, thereby increasing the TPC. Additionally, the decrease in FC and AC in all CP-treated samples was attributed to the dual effect of plasma ionization-generated free radicals. On one hand, these radicals triggered a defensive stress response in the samples, leading to increased synthesis of antioxidants45. On the other hand, the antioxidants reacted with the free radicals, forming intermediate compounds7. In conclusion, while the CP treatment weakened some antioxidants in BWWF, it also stimulated an increase in TPC, which is not necessarily a negative phenomenon.Fig. 5Changes in the content of three antioxidant components of BWWF before and after CP treatment, (A) total phenols; (B) anthocyanins; (C) flavonoids.

Water migration by LF-NMR

Fig. 6 illustrated the LF-NMR relaxation time (T2) inversion plots of BWWF before and after CP treatment. The plots exhibited three distinct peaks representing water molecules with different mobilities within the sample, labeled as T21, T22, and T23. Previous research by Zhang et al.46 had established that the T2 value reflected the mobility and binding state of water molecules. Based on the findings of Li et al.47, water molecules with T2 values ranging from 0.01-1 ms and 1-30 ms were classified as bound water and weakly bound water, respectively. These water molecules were bound or tightly associated with starch, proteins, and other polymeric substances. Conversely, water molecules with T2 values exceeding 100 ms were considered weakly bound to various substances, displaying high degrees of freedom and mobility, and referred to as free water48. The CP treatment resulted in the shift of the areas of the three BWWF peaks (T21, T22, and T23) towards lower values, indicating a reduction in the content of bound, weakly bound, or free water. This reduction was attributed to the decomposition of water molecules into reactive hydroxyls and ions by the plasma-reaction products49. Notably, the T21 peak consistently shifted to the left as the duration of CP treatment increased. This phenomenon correlated with the cross-linking of starch molecules and their interaction with other substances, resulting in the formation of complex polymers with ordered structures. Additionally, the plasma etching of starch granule surfaces during treatment played a role in altering the binding properties of water, leading to water being more tightly packed and exhibiting lower mobility within the system50. This observation aligned with SEM and LPZ measurements, which revealed the aggregation of starch granules with macromolecules and the formation of furrowed textures on the clump surfaces after CP treatment.Fig. 6Relaxation time spectrum of LF-NMR of BWWF before and after CP treatment

Conclusions

This study investigated the effect of different durations of CP treatment on BWWF. The analysis encompassed a wide range of physicochemical properties, including moisture content, protein content, ash content, trace elements, color characteristics, amino acids, flavor components, particle size distribution, microstructure, antioxidant activity, and water migration. The results revealed an improvement in the nutrient composition of CP-treated BWWF. Specifically, there was a decrease in moisture and protein content, accompanied by an increase in ash content. Most trace elements remained stable following treatment. Notably, essential and non-essential amino acids showed a significant increase after CP treatment, resulting in an elevation of the EAA/NEAA values. Color analysis indicated that the CP treatment imparted a brighter and yellower appearance to the BWWF. This change could be attributed to modifications in moisture levels, pigments, and enzymes. The CP-treated BWWF flavors became pleasantly aromatic due to changes in specific flavor compounds, and importantly, unpleasant flavor compounds disappeared. Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy observations demonstrated that CP treatment induced particle aggregation and cross-linking, leading to an enlargement of particle size and surface etching. Additionally, CP treatment had a positive impact on the water content of BWWF. It decreased the water content in all states and enhanced the stability of bound water. In conclusion, our study validated the significant potential of CP treatment in enhancing the physicochemical properties and sensory attributes of BWWF, as well as a novel and effective non-thermal pretreatment method.
