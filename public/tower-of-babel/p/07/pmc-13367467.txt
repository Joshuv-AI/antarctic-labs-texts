Diagnostic Performance of the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS Classification Systems for Thyroid Micronodules: A Multicenter Prospective Study
Authors: Jun-Chao Zhang, Hong-Lan Feng, Qi-Yuan Li, Hong-Jian Li, Wei-Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate and contrast how effectively the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RAD identify thyroid micronodules.MethodsIn this prospective study, thyroid micronodules (maximum diameter ≤10 mm) confirmed by postoperative histopathology were enrolled. These nodules were assessed on ultrasound and categorized using ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS. Postoperative pathological results served as reference standard. Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) were performed to define optimal cutoff for each TI-RADS classifications in identifying papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, and the subsequent diagnostic efficiencies of the systems were compared.ResultsThe areas under the curve (AUCs) corresponding to ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS were 0.685, 0.691, 0.673, and 0.608, respectively. ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, and K-TIRADS did not differ significantly from one another, and all three exceeded the performance of EU TI-RADS. When the Youden index reached maximum, the optimal cut-off points were >Category 4, >Category 4B, >Category 4, and >Category 4, respectively. Under these optimal thresholds, the ACR TI-RADS demonstrated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy values of 90.12%, 46.62%, 90.95%, 44.23%, and 83.86%; those of the C-TIRADS were 85.58%, 48.31%, 90.78%, 36.02%, and 80.21%; those of the K-TIRADS were 88.19%, 45.95%, 90.66%, 39.53%, and 82.11%; and those of the EU TI-RADS were 98.69%, 22.97%, 88.40%, 74.73%, and 87.80%. Overall, all four TI-RADS exhibited relatively high sensitivity, positive predictive value, and accuracy. However, the specificity and negative predictive value remained low.ConclusionThe ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS demonstrated relatively limited diagnostic performance for thyroid micronodules. However, all thyroid micronodules from a surgically selected cohort with a very high malignancy rate, which is not representative of real-world thyroid nodule populations. The diagnostic metrics and optimal cutoff values cannot be directly extrapolated to the general population of patients with incidentally detected subcentimeter thyroid nodules.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer constitutes one of the most prevalent endocrine system malignancies, and its global incidence increased by more than 200% between 1992 and 2018. Currently, on a global scale, thyroid cancer holds the seventh position for incidence and the twenty-fourth position for mortality among malignancies.1,2 Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is recognized as the principal variant of thyroid cancer, accounting for close to 90% of cases.3 The prognosis of PTC is closely related to tumor size, and studies have shown that most thyroid cancers grow slowly, particularly papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (≤10 mm), which generally has a favorable prognosis and does not significantly affect patient mortality.4 According to contemporary evidence-based guideline recommendations, large or suspicious nodules necessitate cytological evaluation following fine-needle aspiration biopsy for accurate classification. Surgery is no longer the only treatment for symptomatic or malignant nodules, active surveillance or local ablative treatments might be beneficial for appropriately selected patients, especially for subcentimeter thyroid nodules without high-risk clinical features, such as gross extrathyroidal extension or clinically evident cervical lymphadenopathy, this strategy largely avoids the incidence of overdiagnosis and overtreatment of papillary thyroid microcarcinoma.5 However, some patients are more inclined towards surgical treatment due to the influence of anxiety emotions. Meanwhile and a subset of thyroid microcarcinomas exhibit aggressive pathological and clinical characteristics including regional and distant metastasis and postoperative structural recurrence, and rare cases may even progress to high-grade malignancy with fatal outcomes, such as poorly differentiated, ana plastic thyroid carcinoma and medullary thyroid cancer.6,7 Consequently, in clinical practice, some clinicians continue to recommend surgery for suspicious thyroid micronodules, while some patients, influenced by anxiety, insist on surgical intervention. For patients whose postoperative pathology confirms malignancy, surgery is acceptable However, in routine practice, most subcentimeter nodules are not surgically removed and most incidental thyroid nodules are benign, but for benign nodules with suspicious features, especially for those whose nodules are confirmed benign by both fine-needle aspiration (FNA) and postoperative pathology, surgery not only increases the risk of postoperative complications but also imposes psychological stress. Multivariable analysis and the second online survey for patients with heightened regret confirm that post operative lower thyroid cancer-specific quality of life is the most common factor for heightened regret of thyroidectomy, followed by preoperative understanding of disease, presentation of complications and other factors.8 Therefore, accurately evaluating whether thyroid micronodule is benign or malignant holds significant value for selecting optimal clinical management, particularly for patients with anxiety.

As an imaging modality, ultrasound provides superior sensitivity in the detection of thyroid nodules. The Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (TI-RADS) further allows stratification of malignancy risk, thereby providing imaging-based guidance for clinical decision-making. In May 2016, the Korean Society of Radiology and the Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology jointly introduced K-TIRADS, the first TI-RADS issued by a professional society worldwide.9 In August 2017, the European Thyroid Association released the EU TI-RADS.10 In the same year, as the pioneer of the BI-RADS system, the American College of Radiology (ACR) officially published the ACR-TI-RADS classification system,11 which garnered widespread international attention. However, due to the relatively late introduction of the ACR-TI-RADS and its partial inconsistency with China’s clinical practice, Chinese scholars subsequently developed the China-TIRADS (C-TIRADS), a system tailored to domestic medical conditions.12 These guidelines aim to assess the malignant potential of thyroid nodules based on sonographic features and thereby guide clinical management. Although they emphasize similar suspicious ultrasound characteristics, such as solidity, hypoechogenicity, marked hypoechogenicity, irregular margins, and microcalcifications,9–12 studies have demonstrated that benign and malignant thyroid nodules ≤10 mm share substantial overlap in their sonographic appearances. Except for microcalcifications, other suspicious features occur more frequently in ≤10 mm nodules compared with those >10 mm, which increases the likelihood of misdiagnosing benign thyroid micronodules.13 This is also a limitation of conventional ultrasound. Research indicates that molecular diagnostics, radiomics, artificial intelligence, and computational methods contribute to the diagnosis of thyroid nodules, significantly improving the accuracy of thyroid cancer detection. As a complementary strategy to traditional ultrasound, the application of these new technologies can greatly enhance the risk stratification of thyroid micro-nodules in conventional ultrasound.14 Moreover, studies based on ACR-TI-RADS have reported only modest diagnostic performance for nodules ≤10 mm.15 Therefore, the diagnostic value of other TI-RADS systems in evaluating thyroid micronodules warrants further investigation.

The present study prospectively enrolled patients with thyroid micronodules who elected to undergo surgical treatment. Each nodule was assessed based on the criteria outlined in the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS classification guidelines, with the aim of assessing and comparing the diagnostic performance of these systems in identifying papillary thyroid microcarcinoma.

Materials and Methods

General Information

From January 2022 to October 2025, a total of 1732 patients with thyroid nodules confirmed by postoperative histopathology were prospectively recruited from Jiangnan University Affiliated Hospital, Zhong Da Hospital, Medical School, Southeast University, Huai’an Cancer Hospital, and General Hospital of Zhongshan, Liupanshui Municipality, yielding 2,057 nodules. Among the included patients, 737 were male and 995 were female, aged 16–79 years, with a mean age of 49.26 ± 12.42 years. Nodule maximal diameters were between 2–10 mm, with a mean size of 5.74 ± 2.10 mm. There were 769 nodules situated in the left lobe, 1,256 in the right lobe, and 32 in the isthmus. The primary surgical indications were as follows: (1) nodules confirmed or suspected to be malignant by FNA; (2) absence of definitive FNA pathology or benign FNA results but with highly suspicious sonographic features; and (3) significant patient anxiety prompting request for surgery. Inclusion criteria were: (1) first-detected thyroid nodule; (2) maximum nodule diameter ≤10 mm; (3) definitive postoperative pathological diagnosis; (4) complete pre-FNA ultrasound data. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior ablation or other treatments potentially altering ultrasound features; (2) indeterminate FNA results without postoperative pathology; (3) diffuse thyroid disease without a distinct nodule on ultrasound. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University Affiliated Hospital (approval No. LS2021092), and all patients provided written informed consent prior to surgery.

Instruments and Methods

Color Doppler ultrasound systems, including the Mindray Resona 7 EXP, GE Logiq E20, ACUSON S3000, and SonoScape S60, were used. A linear-array transducer operating at 7.5–15 MHz was utilized. During scanning, patients were placed in the supine position with the neck fully exposed. The thyroid gland was examined in both transverse and longitudinal orientations, and each nodule was assessed from multiple planes and viewing angles. Nodule characterization was conducted concurrently according to the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS systems by two sonographers at or above the attending level, each with more than 10 years of experience, and the sonographers were blinded to cytology, clinical information, and final pathology. When discrepancies occurred between the two evaluators, the images were further reviewed by a senior chief physician.

ACR TI-RADS Classification

Based on the ACR TI-RADS system, ultrasound assessment and scoring criteria were as follows: (1) Composition: cystic or predominantly cystic, 0 points; spongiform appearance, 0 points; mixed solid-cystic pattern, 1 point; solid or nearly solid, 2 points. (2) Echogenicity: anechoic, 0 points; hyperechoic or isoechoic, 1 point; hypoechoic, 2 points; markedly hypoechoic, 3 points. (3) Shape: wider-than-tall, 0 points; taller-than-wide, 3 points. (4) Margin: smooth or poorly defined, 0 points; lobulated or irregular border, 2 points; extrathyroidal invasion, 3 points. (5) Echogenic foci: none or comet-tail artifact, 0 points; macrocalcifications, 1 point; peripheral (rim) calcifications, 2 points; punctate echogenic foci, 3 points. The total score determined the final category: 0 points, TR1; 2 points, TR2; 3 points, TR3; 4–6 points, TR4; ≥7 points, TR5.11

C-TIRADS Classification

In accordance with the C-TIRADS scoring system, vertical orientation, solid composition, marked hypoechoic, microcalcifications, Ill-defined/irregular margins or extrathyroidal extension are defined as positive ultrasound features, each assigned 1 point. During nodule assessment, scores are calculated by summing all suspicious features. Punctate echogenic foci with comet-tail artifacts are considered a negative feature and scored as −1 point. When both suspicious microcalcifications and comet-tail artifacts are present within the same nodule, only microcalcifications are recorded. Final classification is determined based on the total score: a score of −1 corresponds to Category 2, 0 to Category 3, 1 to Category 4A, 2 to Category 4B, 3–4 to Category 4C, and 5 to Category 5.12

K-TIRADS Classification

According to the K-TIRADS system, classification is determined based on nodule echogenicity, shape/contour, and three specific suspicious sonographic features (microcalcifications, non-parallel orientation, and irregular/microlobulated margins). The categories are defined as follows: K-TIRADS 1, no nodule; K-TIRADS 2, benign nodules, including typically benign patterns such as spongiform, partially cystic nodule with comet tail artifact, purely cystic; K-TIRADS 3, partially cystic or isohyperechoic nodules without any of 3 suspicious ultrosound

tail artifact, purely cystic; K-TIRADS 3, partially cystic or isohyperechoic nodules without any of 3 suspicious ultrosound features; K-TIRADS 4, 1) Solid hypoechoic nodule without any of 3 suspicious US features* or 2) Partially cystic or isohyperechoic nodule with any of 3 suspicious US features; and K-TIRADS 5, Solid hypoechoic nodule with any of 3 suspicious US features.9

EU TI-RADS Classification

In accordance with the EU TI-RADS criteria, nodules are classified as follows: (1) Category 1 (normal): no thyroid nodule detected; (2) Category 2 (benign): purely cystic nodules or entirely spongiform nodules; (3) Category 3 (low-risk catergory): oval shape, smooth margins, isoechoic or hyperechoic, without any features of high risk; (4) Category 4 (intermediate-risk category): oval shape, smooth margins, mildly hypoechoic, without any features of high risk; (5) Category 5 (high-risk category): at least one highly suspicious feature, including non-oval shape, irregular margins, microcalcifications, and marked hypoechogenicity.10

Statistical Methods

All statistical processing was carried out using SPSS version 27.0 and MedCalc version 19.3.1. For variables that exhibited a normal distribution, data were reported as mean ± standard deviation, and differences between two independent groups were examined with the independent-samples t-test. When the distribution was non-normal, continuous data were described as M (P25, P75), and the Mann–Whitney U-test was applied for between-group comparisons. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages (%); comparisons were performed using the χ2-test, with Fisher’s exact test adopted when expected frequencies were insufficient. Interobserver agreement was assessed using the Kappa coefficient test, with a Kappa value of 0–0.20 indicating minimal agreement, 0.21–0.40 indicating weak agreement, 0.41–0.60 indicating moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 indicating high consistency, and 0.81–1.00 indicating extremely high consistency. Postoperative pathological diagnosis was regarded as the gold standard. To evaluate diagnostic capability, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for each system, and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values were obtained. An AUC of 0.85–0.95 was interpreted as good diagnostic performance, values between 0.70 and 0.85 indicated moderate performance, and values within 0.50–0.70 suggested poor discrimination. AUC differences across systems were analyzed using the DeLong method. Medcalc was used to calculate sample-size, the minimum sample size is 532 cases. When the Youden index was maximized, comparisons of sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy corresponding to the optimal cutoff values were performed using the χ2-test or Z-test. A P-value <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

ROC Curves and Diagnostic Performance of the ACR-TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS Systems

ROC curves were generated based on nodule pathology and the corresponding TI-RADS scores and classifications (Figure 3). The AUCs for the ACR-TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS systems were 0.685 (0.664–0.705), 0.691 (0.670–0.710), 0.673 (0.652–0.693), and 0.608 (0.587–0.630), respectively. The AUCs for ACR-TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, and K-TIRADS were all significantly higher than that for EU TI-RADS (Z = 5.425, 6.367, and 4.617, respectively; all P < 0.0001). When the Youden index reached its maximum, the optimal cutoff values for ACR-TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS were >Category 4, >Category 4B, >Category 4, and >Category 4, respectively. The remaining comparisons of AUC values did not show significant differences, with corresponding Z values of 5.425, 6.367, and 4.617 (all P < 0.0001). Discussion

The thyroid imaging reporting and management framework served as the foundation for the development of the TI-RADS thyroid classification system. By establishing a standardized lexicon and stratifying the estimated malignancy risk for thyroid nodules, TI-RADS reduces the subjectivity of ultrasound interpretation to a certain extent and provides a foundation for clinicians to select appropriate management strategies. Among the various TI-RADS systems currently used worldwide, ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS are the most influential. In this study, postoperative histopathology served as the reference standard to evaluate the diagnostic value of these four TI-RADS systems for thyroid micronodules. The results demonstrated that all systems exhibited high sensitivity for the detection of papillary thyroid microcarcinoma while their specificity was uniformly low, resulting in relatively limited overall diagnostic performance for thyroid micronodules. However, these findings apply to a highly selected surgical cohort rather than to the broader population of patients with incidentally detected subcentimeter thyroid nodules. Several factors may account for these findings.

First, from a pathological perspective, this study included a total of 2,057 thyroid micronodules, among which malignant nodules were predominantly PTC, whereas benign nodules comprised nodular goiter, chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, follicular adenoma, and granulomatous thyroiditis. Classic ultrasound features of PTC include solid composition, hypoechogenicity or marked hypoechogenicity, microcalcifications, taller-than-wide shape, and ill-defined margins.18–21 However, benign nodules in this study also exhibited suspicious sonographic characteristics. Prior studies have shown that typical sonographic features of nodular goiter include mixed cystic–solid composition, well-defined margins, and a wider-than-tall shape.22 In this cohort, some nodular goiters were small in size and newly detected, exhibiting features similar to PTC. Such nodules often arise due to hemorrhage, degeneration, infarction, or fibrosis. During hematoma absorption, as the lesion gradually shrinks and undergoes degeneration, the altered thyroid tissue may become irregular in shape and display malignant-appearing ultrasound characteristics.23 Moreover, when nodular goiter is accompanied by stromal collagenization, calcification, or crystal deposition, punctate echogenic foci may develop over time. In small solid thyroid nodules, ultrasound assessment often cannot accurately differentiate such echogenic foci from true microcalcifications.24 In addition, because thyroid micronodules are small in size, they are less likely to undergo cystic degeneration, which further increases the difficulty in distinguishing them from thyroid carcinoma. For benign nodules such as chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis and granulomatous thyroiditis, their sonographic features also exhibit substantial overlap with those of PTC.25–27 Studies have shown that both chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis and granulomatous thyroiditis present characteristic ultrasound manifestations: chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis may appear as the classic “white knight” nodule, characterized by a well-defined, regularly shaped hyperechoic lesion, whereas granulomatous thyroiditis typically presents as an ill-defined, irregular hypoechoic area.28,29 Under such typical circumstances, ultrasound imaging can more reliably differentiate these conditions from PTC; however, when the lesion is very small, these characteristic features may not be evident, thereby complicating differentiation from PTC. Moreover, thyroid follicular neoplasms—including follicular adenoma and follicular carcinoma—share overlapping clinical manifestations, ultrasound characteristics, and molecular features, making it difficult to distinguish follicular adenoma from follicular carcinoma through ultrasound, FNA, or immunohistochemistry prior to surgery.30 These factors help explain the relatively high proportion of benign nodules in this study that were classified as Category ≥4, which in turn contributed to misdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsy or surgical intervention.

The ROC curves in this study showed that the AUCs for the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS systems were 0.685, 0.691, 0.673, and 0.608, respectively, all falling within the range of 0.50–0.70. This indicates that for papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, the diagnostic effectiveness of these systems remained suboptimal. These results are clearly inferior to findings from previous studies, which have reported high diagnostic efficacy for the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS systems in differentiating thyroid malignancies, with AUC values exceeding 0.85.31–34 The discrepancy may be explained by the fact that this study exclusively included thyroid nodules smaller than 10 mm. As nodule size increases, the diagnostic ultrasound traits exhibited by benign and malignant thyroid nodules become more distinct, leading to improved diagnostic performance. Although some researchers have reported that TI-RADS diagnostic accuracy does not significantly differ between nodules >10 mm and ≤10 mm, the sample size for ≤10 mm nodules in those studies was limited to only 71 cases, which may have introduced unavoidable bias. Additionally, the substantial overlap in ultrasound characteristics distinguishing benign from malignant thyroid micronodules contributes to lower diagnostic accuracy in this subgroup, whereas nodules >10 mm tend to exhibit more distinct benign or malignant imaging characteristics, thereby reducing overlap and improving diagnostic efficiency.35

Furthermore, in this study, the optimal cutoff values for the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS systems were >Category 4, >Category 4B, >Category 4, and >Category 4, respectively, indicating relatively high thresholds. Previous studies have suggested that nodules >Category 3 typically warrant consideration of FNA based on their size; however, this criterion clearly does not apply to thyroid micronodules. Therefore, current TI-RADS recommendations advise against FNA for thyroid nodules ≤10 mm, which can effectively reduce overtreatment. At these optimal thresholds, all systems exhibited high sensitivity, positive predictive value, and accuracy, whereas their specificity and negative predictive value were relatively low. Notably, EU TI-RADS demonstrated higher sensitivity, positive predictive value, and accuracy than ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, and K-TIRADS, but had lower specificity and negative predictive value. This may be attributed to differences in classification criteria among the systems, particularly the definition of EU TI-RADS Category 4 nodules, which are characterized as oval-shaped, smooth-margined, and mildly hypoechoic without high-risk features. Similar nodules may be categorized differently in other systems; for instance, when such nodules are solid, they may be classified as Category 4 in other systems, whereas if not solid, they may fall under Category 3. Consistent with the high sensitivity and positive predictive value observed, the low specificity and negative predictive value across systems contributed to an increased misdiagnosis rate for benign micronodules. Under the current clinical context in which thyroid microcarcinoma is frequently overdiagnosed and overtreated, unnecessary interventions for benign micronodules further aggravate this issue. In the case of cytology yielding indeterminate results, additional tools, such as molecular testing, can assist in guiding the management plan.36 Genomic testing of thyroid nodules to improve preoperative diagnosis has become an important component supporting decision-making in clinical care, reducing the need for diagnostic surgeries and improving accuracy of cancer risk assessment.The progress of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly its core algorithms-machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL)-has been significant in the medical field, impacting both scientific research and clinical practice.37 The future trend points to these AI systems becoming the preferred choice for thyroid diagnostics. Due to the development of AI, Machine Learning (ML), and the improvement of medical imaging equipment, radiomics hasgradually evolved in recent years. Radiomics can obtain various quantitative features from medical images, highlighting the invisible image traits and significantly enhancing the ability of medical imaging identification and prediction.38 Computer-interpretable guideline a derivative of conventional clinical practice guidelines, which harnesses the power of computational logic, workflow engines, and artificial intelligence to deliver patient-specific recommendations at the point of care.39 Thus, considering the modest ability of TI-RADS systems to accurately diagnose thyroid micronodules and the favorable prognosis of thyroid micronodules ≤10 mm, it may be reasonable to avoid classifying such nodules and instead recommend routine follow-up without intervention. This strategy may help alleviate patient anxiety, reduce unnecessary procedures, and more effectively prevent overtreatment of thyroid microcarcinoma.

Limitations: (1) Firstly, our study population consists only of surgically resected nodules with postoperative histopathological confirmation, resulting in an extremely high malignancy rate that is not representative of real-world thyroid nodule populations. The diagnostic metrics and optimal cutoff values cannot be directly extrapolated to the general population of patients with incidentally detected subcentimeter thyroid nodules. (2) TI-RADS assignment depends on subjective interpretation of subtle sonographic features. Although the evaluation of thyroid micro-nodules in this study relied on two experienced ultrasound physicians, with discrepancies in interpretations reviewed by one attending physician, the assessment of thyroid micro-nodules inevitably carries a certain degree of subjectivity. (3) Although this study was designed as a multicenter prospective investigation, differences in investigators, ultrasound equipment, and technical expertise across centers may have introduced variability in evaluating thyroid nodules. It potentially leads to bias in the results. (4) This study exclusively included thyroid nodules ≤10 mm. Given the ongoing debate regarding overtreatment of thyroid microcarcinoma, the inclusion of 2,057 thyroid micronodules may raise concerns regarding non-standardized management and potential overtreatment.

In conclusion, the diagnostic performance of the ACR TI-RADS, C-TIRADS, K-TIRADS, and EU TI-RADS systems for thyroid micronodules was relatively limited. Although these systems demonstrated high sensitivity, positive predictive value, and accuracy, their specificity and negative predictive value were low, which could result in benign micronodules being incorrectly identified as malignant. In light of the good prognosis associated with thyroid microcarcinoma, together with the inadequate diagnostic capability of current TI-RADS systems for such lesions, it may be reasonable to recommend regular surveillance forsuspicious nodules with no clear signs of invasiveness. Such an approach could help mitigate overdiagnosis and overtreatment of thyroid microcarcinoma while reducing patient anxiety and unnecessary clinical interventions. However, all thyroid micronodules from a surgically selected cohort with a very high malignancy rate, the results are applicable mainly to selected patients undergoing surgery and cannot be directly generalized to routine screening or incidental thyroid nodule populations. And the ultrasound-based systems alone may be insufficient for reliable risk stratification of subcentimeter nodules and that further validation in unselected, prospectively followed cohorts is needed.
