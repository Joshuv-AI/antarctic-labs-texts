Functional connectivity alterations of the somatomotor network in euthymic bipolar disorder
Authors: Anna Luisa Klahn, William Hedley Thompson, Christoph Abé, Benny Liberg, Carl M Sellgren, Philipp Klahn, Mikael Landén
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile individuals with bipolar disorder are assumed to recover between mood episodes, some nevertheless experience lingering subsyndromal symptoms and suffer from cognitive and functional impairments. Here, we propose that these enduring impairments may be linked to aberrations in brain networks. To test this, we conducted a resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study and used network-based statistic to compare functional connectivity between euthymic individuals with bipolar disorder (N = 96) and healthy control individuals (N = 61) both within and between resting-state networks. We also investigated the association of functional connectivity with lingering psychomotor symptoms and illness severity in bipolar disorder. We found stronger functional connectivity between the somatomotor network and the frontoparietal network in individuals with bipolar disorder compared with healthy controls, but weaker functional connectivity within the somatomotor network as well as between the somatomotor and the visual network. Results remained after adjusting for antipsychotic medication. No significant association with psychomotor performance and illness severity was found. We conclude that stronger functional connectivity between the somatomotor and frontoparietal network might be associated with lingering symptoms in bipolar euthymia. Dysconnectivity within the somatomotor network might relate to psychomotor symptoms beyond the impact of medication. Our findings contribute to the sparse field of somatomotor network aberrancies in bipolar disorder and may present a potential target for brain stimulation treatment.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Resting-state fMRI, Brain networks, Functional connectivity, Somatomotor network

Introduction

Euthymia in bipolar disorder is defined by affective stability and the absence of clinically relevant manic, hypomanic, or depressive symptoms (World Health Organization, 2019; McIntyre et al., 2020). Nevertheless, clinically remitted individuals with bipolar disorder may experience lingering subsyndromal affective and cognitive symptoms and functional impairments (Drakopoulos et al., 2020; Sparding et al., 2017). While some illness manifestations, like impaired reward processing and affective decision-making, are strongly related to depressive or manic episodes (Miskowiak et al., 2019), other impairments may persist during remission, such as deficits in emotion regulation (Dodd et al., 2020; Townsend and Altshuler, 2012), executive function, attention, and psychomotor symptoms (Sparding et al., 2017; Mann-Wrobel et al., 2011; Sole et al., 2012; Bourne et al., 2013). The term “psychomotor” encompasses how cognitive and emotional processes influence the primary motor function, while also referring to bodily movements resulting from mental activity necessary for interaction with the environment (Northoff et al., 2021). Interestingly, although mania and depression feature distinct psychomotor symptoms, these have received less attention than affective and cognitive symptoms. In depression, patients mostly experience psychomotor inhibition, though a subgroup shows restlessness and agitation, in combination with reduced or inhibited affect. Conversely, mania is characterized by an intense psychomotor excitation such as hyperactivity, impulsivity, and logorrhoea (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Faurholt-Jepsen et al., 2016). Notably, milder but persisting alterations of psychomotor activity and altered sensory perception have also been reported during euthymic periods (Mann-Wrobel et al., 2011; Volkert et al., 2016; Parker et al., 2017; Scott et al., 2017). The functional neurobiological correlates of such lingering psychomotor symptoms in bipolar euthymia have yet to be elucidated.

Graph-theory based network approaches have recently entered the field of functional brain imaging research and applied on resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data (Fornito et al., 2016). Viewing the brain as a network of nodes (i.e., brain regions) and edges (i.e., correlated functional activity) provides a framework for investigating brain function at various levels, including nodal, global, or community level. The community level corresponds to connectivity within and between resting-state networks, such as the default mode network or the frontoparietal network (Thomas Yeo et al., 2011). These networks are highly sensitive to disease (Niazy et al., 2015). Recent studies have revealed aberrancies in resting-state brain networks in psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia (Baker et al., 2014; Nelson et al., 2017), but fewer studies have investigated functional connectivity alterations of these networks in euthymic bipolar disorder (Chase and Phillips, 2016; Lois et al., 2014; Wessa et al., 2014). In general, bipolar disorder has been associated with aberrant network connectivity within and between the default mode network, the frontoparietal network, the salience network, and the somatomotor network (Seeley et al., 2007; Syan et al., 2018; Zovetti et al., 2020). However, brain network functional connectivity varies significantly depending on mood state in bipolar disorder. For instance, higher functional connectivity between the somatomotor and the default mode network, as well as within the somatomotor network, were observed in remitted compared to depressed patients with bipolar disorder. By contrast, remitted bipolar individuals showed lower functional connectivity between the somatomotor and the ventral attention network, as well as between the default mode, limbic, and frontoparietal network compared to depressed bipolar patients (Liu et al., 2021). Another study comparing euthymic bipolar disorder with healthy controls found lower connectivity within visual, temporal, and motor resting-state networks, but higher connectivity between motor areas and parts of the default mode and frontoparietal network, in bipolar disorder (Bellani et al., 2020). In accordance with these findings, one study revealed functional connectivity alterations between the somatomotor and default mode network in bipolar disorder (Doucet et al., 2017).

These findings are interesting given that connectivity changes in the default mode network have been associated with depressive state and ruminative thinking while normalisation of resting-state functional connectivity between the default mode network, the frontoparietal and the salience network has been proposed as biomarker of clinical remission in bipolar disorder (Syan et al., 2018; Vargas et al., 2013; Yoon et al., 2021). For a further review on general and mood state dependent functional connectivity alterations see (Syan et al., 2018; Vargas et al., 2013; Claeys et al., 2022).

We propose that bipolar disorder can be characterised as a brain network disorder based on the previously observed trait and state alterations in functional connectivity. To investigate this, we studied resting-state functional connectivity in clinically remitted individuals with bipolar disorder and healthy control individuals. Based on previous findings (Liu et al., 2021; Doucet et al., 2017), we expected to find that functional connectivity would differ between euthymic patients with bipolar disorder and healthy controls in the somatomotor network, but not in the default mode network, the frontoparietal, or the salience network. We then further explored the association of lingering clinical and psychomotor symptoms with alterations in functional connectivity in euthymic bipolar disorder.

Material and methods

Participants

Study participants were individuals with bipolar disorder and healthy control individuals enrolled in the St. Göran bipolar project, which is a longitudinal study at the bipolar disorder outpatient clinic of the Northern Stockholm psychiatric clinic. For the present study, we included healthy control individuals and individuals diagnosed with either bipolar disorder type 1 or 2. The diagnoses were made by board certified specialists in psychiatry or residents in psychiatry according to the DSM-IV TR criteria using the SCID-I included in the Swedish version of the Affective Disorder Evaluation (Sachs et al., 2003). As the St. Göran bipolar project started in 2005, we have continuously applied the DSM-IV criteria to use the same assessments during the whole study. The M.I.N.I. Neuropsychiatric interview (Sheehan et al., 1998) was administered to screen for co-occurring psychiatric disorders. The final diagnoses were made by a consensus panel of experienced psychiatrists specialised in bipolar disorder. Further details regarding recruitment, exclusion criteria, and diagnostic tools have been described in (Ekman et al., 2010). Age- and sex-matched healthy control individuals were selected randomly by Statistics Sweden, the Swedish government agency for official statistics. Control individuals were examined by a psychiatrist using the M.I.N.I. interview and parts of the Affective Disorder Evaluation. Exclusion criteria were any current psychiatric disorder, a family history of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder in first-degree relatives, drug or alcohol abuse, and neurological conditions (for details see Rolstad et al., 2015).

All participants consented in writing to participate in the study that was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the regional ethics committee in Stockholm, Sweden.

Clinical and cognitive measures

We used the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) to assess manic symptoms and the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) to assess depressive symptoms. To assess illness severity, we used the illness severity scale and the improvement scale of the Clinical Global Impressions Scales (CGI). Additionally, we assessed functional (daily life functioning) and symptom (severity of psychiatric symptoms) domains of the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF). The Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS) was administered to assess functional impairment in work life.

Psychomotor functioning was assessed with three different tests: 1) The motor speed variable from Trail Making Test (TMT), which is a subtest from the Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (D-KEFS) measuring cognitive flexibility, problem solving, and the ability to inhibit automatic, impulsive responses. 2) The time needed to copy a complex geometric figure in the Rey Complex Figure Test (RCFT), which is a test assessing visual-spatial and constructional abilities. 3) The reaction time variable from the Conner’s Continuous Performance Test (CPT-II), which is used to assess attention and impulsivity.

MRI acquisition

MRI scans were acquired at the MR Research Centre at the Karolinska University Hospital in Stockholm on a General Electric 1.5T Signa Excite MRI medical scanner with an 8-channel head coil. For resting-state fMRI, we acquired 180 vol with 39 BOLD sensitive T2-weighted axial echo-planar images each (resolution of 3.79 mm x 3.79 mm, slice thickness 4 mm covering the whole brain, repetition time = 2.5s, echo time = 40ms, field of view = 24.3 cm, flip angle = 85°). The total duration of the resting-state fMRI scan was 7.5 min. The first 4 vol were discarded due to T1 equilibration. Imaging data was collected between September 2012 and March 2017 at the 7-year follow-up assessment of the longitudinal study and analysed in 2022. The baseline MRI scan did not include resting-state fMRI scans. Additionally, we acquired T2-weighted images that were examined by radiologists to exclude any clinical pathologies. We included only individuals in the bipolar group that were in a euthymic state (as defined by MADRS <8 and YMRS <4) at the time of MRI.

Resting-state fMRI preprocessing

Preprocessing of the resting-state fMRI data was performed using fMRIPrep 22.0.0 (Esteban et al., 2019; RRID:SCR_016216) based on Nipype 1.8.3 (Gorgolewski et al., 2011; RRID:SCR_002502). See supplementary methods for full details of preprocessing methods.

Denoising

We used 36 nuisance regressors (Ciric et al., 2017) to denoise the data. Simultaneously to the denoising step, the data was band-passed between 0.008 and 0.1 Hz and 400 regions were extracted applying the Schaefer atlas (Schaefer et al., 2018) using nilearn (Abraham et al., 2014). To ‘scrub’ the data for micromovement, we removed all values with a framewise displacement greater than 0.5. This data was replaced with estimations using a cubic spline from the remaining data.

Connectivity estimation

We calculated a 400 x 400 adjacency matrix per individual using Pearson correlation for the statistical relationship between the time series for each pair of nodes. We then assigned the 400 nodes to 7 resting-state networks according to the node network template of Yeo et al. (Thomas Yeo et al., 2011).

Network-based statistic

We used network-based statistic (NBS) as implemented in the NBS toolbox of Matlab (2019b) to identify group differences in whole-brain functional connectivity (Zalesky et al., 2010). We entered 400 x 400 connectivity matrices for each individual into the NBS general linear model, representing the pairwise correlations between the 400 nodes from the Schaefer parcellation (Schaefer et al., 2018). T-tests for the group contrasts bipolar < controls and bipolar > controls were computed for each pairwise association adding age and sex as covariates. Any association with a test statistic above tprimary = 4 was submitted to 5000 permutations to control the family-wise error rate for topological clusters. We chose the primary threshold tprimary = 4 to achieve a meaningful effect size above Cohen’s d = 0.30. As medication has been reported to influence resting-state fMRI (Claeys et al., 2022), we entered antipsychotic, anticonvulsive, antidepressant, and lithium medication as covariates to the statistical model (one at a time).

Furthermore, we conducted separate NBS analyses with age and sex as covariate testing for the interaction effect group (bipolar, controls) x clinical (GAF, CGI, SDS, MADRS, YMRS) or psychomotor data (TMT, RCFT, CPT-II), respectively.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical analyses of the clinical and cognitive data, as well as for post-hoc analysis of the resting-state fMRI data, we used SPSS version 28 (IBM). We tested group differences using two-sided t-tests or Fischer exact χ2 tests as appropriate.

Functional connectivity: Network-based statistic

Significant clusters identified through NBS analysis allow for rejecting the null hypothesis that there is no difference between the groups being compared. Therefore, the resulting clusters should not be overinterpreted as the extent and size of the clusters depends on factors such as signal-to-noise ratio and the chosen threshold parameter. We will nonetheless describe the composition of each statistically significant cluster to provide a foundation for our subsequent interpretations of the findings.

The NBS-analysis examining the contrast bipolar > controls (i.e., higher functional connectivity in bipolar compared to controls) showed two significant clusters: Cluster 1 (see Fig. S1A in the supplementary) comprised 50 edges across 32 nodes from the default mode network, frontoparietal, visual, dorsal attention and limbic network (pFWE < .001, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = .36). Cluster 2 comprised 82 edges across 62 nodes from the somatomotor, default mode network, salience/ventral attention network, and the frontoparietal network (pFWE < .001, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = .36; see Fig. 1A).

The NBS-analysis examining the contrast bipolar < controls (i.e., lower functional connectivity in bipolar than controls) yielded one significant cluster, as depicted in Fig. 2A. This finding supports to reject the null hypothesis that there is no difference between the two groups. Again, we describe the cluster to provide a foundation for our subsequent interpretations. This cluster consisted of 242 edges across 133 nodes from somatomotor, visual, frontoparietal, and salience/ventral attention network (pFWE < 0.001, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = 0.36).

In a second step, we entered antipsychotic medication as a covariate into the NBS general linear model. The analysis revealed similar but topologically smaller clusters. For the contrast bipolar > controls corrected for antipsychotic medication, cluster 1 (see Supplementary Fig. S1B) comprised 8 edges across 8 nodes from orbitofrontal nodes of the limbic network with the dorsal attention network, default mode network, and frontoparietal network (pFWE = .007, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = .36). Cluster 2 comprised 12 edges across 12 nodes from the somatomotor and the frontoparietal network (pFWE = .003, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = .36; see Fig. 1C). The NBS analysis examining the contrast bipolar < controls corrected for antipsychotic medication yielded evidence to reject the null hypothesis that there is no difference between the groups. This analysis yielded two significant clusters: Cluster 1 comprised 52 edges across 45 nodes from the somatomotor and visual network (pFWE < .001, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = .35; see Fig. 2C). Cluster 2 comprised 15 edges and 15 nodes from the salience/ventral attention network, frontoparietal, and default mode network (pFWE < .001, tprimary = 4, Cohen’s d = .35; see Supplementary Fig. 2).

To test for confounding medication effects, we entered anticonvulsive, antidepressant, and lithium medication as covariates (one at a time) but found no significant impact. Furthermore, we found no significant clusters when we tested connectivity differences between type 1 and 2 bipolar disorder.

Association with clinical and cognitive data

To explore the associations between psychomotor performance, symptom severity, and level of functioning with resting-state brain network connectivity, we conducted separate NBS analyses within the bipolar disorder group. In each analysis, we included measures from TMT, RCFT, CPT-II, CGI, GAF, and SDS as covariates. Additionally, we corrected for age, sex, and antipsychotic medication. These analyses revealed no significant clusters. Similarly, when testing for interaction effects between group (bipolar, controls) and psychomotor performance (TMT, RCFT, CPT-II), or clinical data (CGI, GAF, SDS, YMRS, MADRS), while correcting for age, sex, and antipsychotic medication, no significant clusters were found.

Discussion

This study investigated brain network connectivity from resting-state fMRI in euthymic bipolar disorder. The two main findings were: 1) we found higher functional connectivity in bipolar disorder compared to healthy controls in a cluster of connections between the somatomotor and the frontoparietal network. 2) we found lower functional connectivity in bipolar disorder than healthy controls within the somatomotor network, as well as between the somatomotor and the visual network. Furthermore, we explored the association of psychomotor performance, symptom severity and level of functioning with functional connectivity but did not find any significant clusters. We found no differences in functional connectivity between the bipolar subtypes 1 and 2.

Based on the previous literature (Syan et al., 2018), we did not expect to find differences regarding the activity and connectivity of the default mode network between euthymic bipolar disorder and healthy controls. Yet, our analyses revealed higher functional connectivity in bipolar patients in a cluster involving orbitofrontal nodes of the limbic network, default mode network, and frontoparietal network with connections to the visual and dorsal attention network. Interestingly, increased functional connectivity between frontal regions and the paralimbic network has previously been reported in bipolar disorder and interpreted as abnormal integration of information along the ventral emotion network (Lois et al., 2014; Phillips et al., 2008). Furthermore, altered functional connectivity of the orbitofrontal cortex and temporal regions has been reported in individuals with brain lesions and associated manias as well as in individuals with bipolar mania (Lee et al., 2019). We hence interpret our finding as an orbitofrontal compensation of visual and attentional perception deficits, which previously have been reported in both bipolar disorder and schizophrenia (Jimenez et al., 2019).

Furthermore, we observed higher functional connectivity in individuals with bipolar disorder between temporal nodes of the somatomotor network with nodes of the frontoparietal network. This finding in is line with one study that reported increased functional connectivity of the thalamus - an integrative hub region of the frontoparietal and salience network (Hwang et al., 2017) - with the dorsolateral part of the somatomotor network in bipolar disorder (Skåtun et al., 2018). Moreover, increased connectivity between the somatomotor network and the left precentral gyrus as part of the frontoparietal network has been reported for bipolar disorder and interpreted as a marker for disturbed attention regulation (Thomas et al., 2019). Although we did not find any significant association with symptom severity and level of functioning, our finding of abnormally increased functional connectivity between the somatomotor network and the frontoparietal network may still hint at a pathological mechanism that contributes to persisting symptoms and impairments even in clinical remission.

This finding of stronger connectivity between somatomotor and frontoparietal network needs to be discussed in context with our finding of abnormally weaker within-network connectivity of the somatomotor network and weaker between-network connectivity of the somatomotor and the visual network. These somatomotor network aberrancies could relate to psychomotor activity dysregulation, which has been described in a meta-analysis on motor behavior in bipolar disorder (Scott et al., 2017). There, the authors reported reduced motor activity for both euthymic and depressed bipolar individuals compared to healthy controls and these motor alterations were clearly distinct from mood symptoms. In line with our findings, some studies have described similar abnormalities of somatomotor network connectivity in bipolar disorder. One study found reduced within-network connectivity of the somatomotor network for bilateral somatosensory areas as well as an association of mania scores with connectivity of premotor areas (Ishida et al., 2017). Applying graph theoretical measures, reduced cohesiveness of the somatomotor network was found in bipolar disorder patients and their unaffected siblings. While the connectivity alterations in the bipolar disorder group were further related to decreased integration of core nodes of the default mode network, unaffected siblings showed increased default mode network integration which was interpreted a potential mechanism of resilience (Doucet et al., 2017). Two subsequent studies revealed a shift from decreased somatomotor network activity in depression to increased somatomotor network activity in manic episodes thereby highlighting somatomotor network connectivity as a neurophysiological substrate of psychomotor excitation and inhibition in mania and depression (Martino et al., 2016a, 2020). The somatomotor network has been described as cohesively self-contained in healthy individuals, which means it communicates more within itself than with other networks (Thomas et al., 2019). However, in our study, individuals with bipolar disorder showed abnormally low within-network connectivity of the somatomotor network while at the same time revealing more intense communication of the somatomotor with the frontoparietal network. We therefore conclude that euthymic bipolar patients exhibit distinct somatomotor network connectivity aberrancies that persist beyond the impact of antipsychotic medication and might even contribute to lingering symptoms in clinical remission.

Additionally, we found a cluster of lower functional connectivity in bipolar disorder involving nodes of the salience/ventral attention network, the frontoparietal and the default mode network. This is interesting because previous studies have reported decreased connectivity between the default mode network and frontoparietal regions (Chai et al., 2011; Gong et al., 2019). Moreover, an independent component analysis related dysconnectivity in a subnetwork including the salience network, frontoparietal and the default mode network to disrupted self-referential processing (Wang et al., 2020). Another study revealed decreased between-network functional connectivity between default mode network and the salience network to be related to more illness severity and cognitive deficits (Magioncalda et al., 2015). However, we did not find any association of the functional connectivity around these networks with symptom severity.

We used network-based statistic to identify functional connectivity alterations in euthymic bipolar disorder. So far, only two other studies have applied a similar approach on resting-state fMRI. One study reported increased functional connectivity in a cluster comprising bilateral temporal regions including insulae and hippocampal nodes in bipolar disorder compared to healthy controls (Dvorak et al., 2019). Another study investigated the inferior frontal gyrus as hub node involved in affective and cognitive control and found reduced functional connectivity in bipolar disorder within a cluster comprising frontotemporal nodes including bilateral insulae, ventrolateral prefrontal gyri, superior temporal gyri and putamen (Roberts et al., 2017). In sum, the present studies including our own findings, suggest aberrant functional connectivity even during episodes of clinical remission in bipolar disorder within several brain networks, most prominently including the somatomotor and the frontoparietal network.

Limitations and outlook

Antipsychotic medication has been described as a potential confounder in specifically functional brain imaging data (Birner et al., 2020; Kraguljac et al., 2016; Lui et al., 2010). Our overall findings persisted after correcting for the intake of antipsychotic medication. However, as medication intake and illness severity are confounders to one another, we might have overcorrected for bipolar disorder severity by strictly correcting our resting-state fMRI data for antipsychotic medication.

We found no notable impact of any other psychopharmacological treatment. Nevertheless, more studies are needed to disentangle medication side effects from effects of psychopathology. In this context, we emphasize to control for effects of lithium, as first choice treatment for bipolar disorder, with common side effects such as fine tremors and impaired motor coordination as well as for antidepressant medication which can lead to dizziness and thereby further impact psychomotor function.

As bipolar disorder is heterogeneous and clinical remission does not imply the absence of any impairments, more research is needed investigate potential subgroups of bipolar euthymia regarding occupational functioning, psychomotor alterations, or cognitive impairments. Furthermore, most studies on bipolar disorder are limited to euthymic or depressed episodes of the disorder. Functional MRI studies in manic patients with bipolar disorder can be challenging due to their heightened agitation and restlessness, which can lead to excessive motion artifacts which compromise data quality. Therefore, only little is known about functional connectivity alterations during manic episodes (Lee et al., 2019; Martino et al., 2016b; Strakowski et al., 2011).

Aiming at exploring a potential association of the derived brain network connectivity patterns with clinical condition and behavioural data, we entered the variables of interest stepwise into the general linear model and tested for an interaction effect. Unfortunately, correlation analyses cannot be run in the initial model and post-hoc correlations of extracted connectivity values with clinical or behavioural data are restricted due to analysis circularity (Kriegeskorte et al., 2009; Button, 2019).

We chose network-based statistic to define increased and decreased functional connectivity patterns. However, this static approach is restricted to the mean connectivity across the whole fMRI scan. Adding the temporal component to graph theoretic analyses, dynamic, or time-varying, functional connectivity measures would expand our knowledge about brain network dynamics by revealing how connectivity patterns fluctuate over time.

Conclusions

This study revealed aberrant functional connectivity of brain networks in clinically remitted individuals with bipolar disorder. Our findings hint at two potential functional connectivity markers of euthymic bipolar disorder: First, an abnormal increase of connectivity between temporal regions of the somatomotor network with the frontoparietal network might display a pathological mechanism contributing to persisting symptoms despite clinical remission. Second, reduced connectivity within the somatomotor network and between the somatomotor and visual network might exhibit a potential neurobiological correlate of psychomotor symptoms in euthymic bipolar disorder. Overall, our findings on aberrant somatomotor within- and between-network functional connectivity shed light on a brain network circuit distinct from previously reported fronto-limbic alterations. However, further research is needed to fully understand these neural mechanisms to enable the development of more precise treatments such as providing target regions for brain stimulation techniques.
