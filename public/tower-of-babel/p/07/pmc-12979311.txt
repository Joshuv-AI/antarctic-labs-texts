CAR-T therapy for autoimmune rheumatic diseases: navigating clinical frontiers between breakthroughs and uncertainties
Authors: Juntao Cheng, Xiaohui Zhang, Yong Fan, Zhuoli Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, a transformative breakthrough originally pioneered in oncology, is being successfully repurposed for the treatment of refractory autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs). By enabling profound depletion of pathogenic B cells—a central driver in conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic sclerosis (SSc), and idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM)—CAR-T offers a promising therapeutic strategy for patients who have failed conventional therapies. Preliminary clinical evidence from early-phase trials indicates encouraging short-term efficacy across these ARDs. In SLE, treatment with CD19-directed CAR-T has been associated with high rates of lupus low disease activity state and a drug-free remission in a majority of patients. In SSc, CAR-T effectively suppresses inflammatory activity, although established fibrosis often requires continued adjunctive therapy. Promising clinical responses have also been observed in refractory IIM. The short-term safety profile appears favorable, with cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome being predominantly low-grade and manageable. Nevertheless, significant challenges remain. Key knowledge gaps include the long-term durability of responses, the patterns and functional competence of B-cell reconstitution, potential risks of secondary malignancies, and the substantial economic burden of current autologous platforms. Future translation efforts should focus on optimizing patient selection, developing next-generation CAR constructs (including allogeneic “off-the-shelf” products), exploring rational combination strategies, and establishing standardized outcome measures through larger prospective studies with extended follow-up. If validated in rigorous clinical development, CAR-T cell therapy may offer a novel and potent treatment option for carefully selected patients with severe, refractory autoimmune rheumatic diseases.Keywords: Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, Autoimmune rheumatic diseases, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Systemic sclerosis, Idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, B cell depletion

Introduction

Autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) are a heterogeneous group of immune-mediated disorders characterized by systemic inflammation, pathogenic autoantibody production, and multi-organ involvement [1, 2]. ARDs encompass a spectrum of systemic disorders including but not limited to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), systemic sclerosis (SSc), and idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM), all unified by core mechanisms of B cell dysregulation, aberrant interferon activation, and compromised immune tolerance [3–6]. Despite therapeutic advancements with conventional approaches, including glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and biologics, a substantial proportion of patients experience refractory disease or relapse [7–10]. A critical limitation underpinning these therapeutic failures is the disability to achieve complete and sustained depletion of pathogenic B cell subsets and reservoirs. This failure allows the persistence of autoantibody-producing cells and autoreactive immune niches, fueling the self-perpetuating cycle of autoimmunity [11–14]. This persistent therapeutic gap underscores the critical need for interventions capable of enforcing complete and sustained depletion of pathogenic B cell reservoirs, a prerequisite for disrupting the self-perpetuating cycle of autoimmunity.

The quest to overcome therapeutic limitations in ARDs has drawn inspiration from an unlikely source: cancer immunotherapy [15–17]. In the past decade, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, a paradigm in which genetically engineered T cells target specific surface antigens to eliminate malignant cells, achieved landmark success against refractory B cell malignancies [18–23]. CAR-T cells are generated by genetically engineering patient-derived T cells to express a synthetic chimeric antigen receptor. This receptor typically combines an extracellular antigen-binding domain with intracellular T cell activation domains, enabling potent, MHC-unrestricted recognition and elimination of cells expressing the target antigen [24–26]. Crucially, the application of CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy in patients suffering from both B-cell malignancies and ARDs revealed an unexpected therapeutic benefit beyond oncology [27–29]. A pivotal observation emerged from real-world analyses of patients with concurrent ARDs and B cell malignancies receiving CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy: not only the tumors were eradicated, but also profound remissions of the co-existing ARDs occurred. This unexpected "dual efficacy” was evidenced by serological normalization and significant reductions in disease activity scores, providing compelling clinical evidence that deep B cell depletion could serve as a central therapeutic strategy across these seemingly disparate conditions [30]. Building on this therapeutic principle, CAR-T therapy is now being explored in a distinct patient population: those with refractory ARDs who have experienced inadequate response to multiple prior lines of therapy, including conventional immunosuppression (glucocorticoids, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide) and/or biologic agents (rituximab, belimumab, tocilizumab). This represents a population with a genuine unmet therapeutic need. Preclinical validation in murine lupus models demonstrated CD19-targeted CAR-T’s capacity to achieve durable B cell depletion and disease suppression, with effective resolution of nephritis and anti-dsDNA antibody clearance [31]. These proofs-of-concept highlighted CAR-T’s unique capacity to penetrate immunological sanctuary sites, a feat unattainable by monoclonal antibodies. Building directly upon these clinical observations in comorbid patients and robust preclinical data, Georg Schett and his colleagues achieved a landmark milestone with the first reported application of CD19 CAR-T therapy specifically for a 20-year-old woman suffering from refractory SLE [32]. This seminal study transformed the therapeutic landscape, igniting widespread enthusiasm for CAR-T as a potentially disease-modifying intervention in autoimmunity and cementing its translational journey from bench to bedside. Notwithstanding the transformative potential of CAR-T therapy in ARDs, vigilance against its toxicities remains imperative. In this narrative review, we aim to synthesize the rapidly evolving clinical landscape of CAR-T therapy in autoimmunity, critically examining emerging efficacy signals, redefining safety considerations, and exploring future frontiers.

This narrative review synthesizes the rapidly evolving clinical landscape of CAR-T therapy across key ARDs, focusing on pioneering trials in SLE, SSc, and IIM. To capture the nascent evidence base, we conducted a targeted literature search using PubMed with combinations of key terms [(chimeric antigen receptor OR CAR-T) AND (systemic lupus erythematosus OR systemic sclerosis OR scleroderma OR idiopathic inflammatory myopathy OR dermatomyositis OR polymyositis OR antisynthetase syndrome OR immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy) AND (clinical trial OR case report OR case series)], supplemented by manual screening of reference lists and abstracts presented at the 2025 European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) Annual Congress and American College of Rheumatology (ACR) Convergence. Given the heterogeneity of study designs, small cohort sizes, and limited follow-up in this emerging field, evidence is narratively synthesized with explicit attention to sample characteristics, follow-up duration, and potential cohort overlap. We critically appraise efficacy outcomes (emphasizing clinically meaningful responses per established or study-defined composite endpoints) and treatment-emergent adverse events (AEs), particularly cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). We focus exclusively on reports in patients with isolated ARDs, excluding studies involving concomitant malignancies. This review aims to consolidate preliminary yet promising clinical findings, critically examine the emerging safety profile, and highlight key knowledge gaps requiring dedicated investigation.

In this narrative review, we synthesize and critically appraise the rapidly evolving clinical and translational evidence on CAR T-cell therapy in ARDs. It aims to provide a clinically oriented framework that highlights emerging efficacy signals, mechanistic hypotheses, safety considerations, and key uncertainties.

Mechanistic basis and therapeutic workflow of CAR-T therapy

CAR-T therapy involves a multi-step process beginning with the collection of patients’ own (autologous) or donor-derived (allogeneic) T lymphocytes via leukapheresis. These T cells are then genetically engineered ex vivo to express a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) targeting specific B-cell antigens such as CD19 or BCMA. Following expansion to therapeutic doses, the CAR-T cells are infused back into the patient after a lymphodepleting chemotherapy regimen (e.g., fludarabine/cyclophosphamide), which enhances engraftment and activity. The infused CAR-T cells then recognize and eliminate target antigen-expressing B cells, leading to profound B-cell depletion. A schematic representation of this workflow is provided in Fig. 1.

CAR-T therapy in SLE

SLE is characterized by dysregulated B-cell activity driving pathogenic autoantibody production and multi-organ damage [33–35]. CD19-directed CAR-T therapy achieves deep depletion of autoreactive B-cell lineages, including CD19+ plasma blasts residing in lymphoid sanctuaries, thereby resetting autoimmune pathophysiology. This approach circumvents limitations of conventional B-cell depletion, offering potential disease modification in refractory cases [10, 36]. Preliminary data from 25 refractory SLE patients across 10 studies (with known overlapping patient cohorts, as noted in Table 1) point to short-term efficacy signals with CD19/BCMA-CD19 CAR-T therapy. This population was characterized by a predominance of young females (median age 23 years, range 12–46; 80% female), a long disease duration (median 5 years, range 1–21), and failure to respond to at least three prior immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory agents, underscoring their high unmet medical need. In these small cohorts with limited follow-up (3–6 months), lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS) appeared to occur in 68% (17/25) at 1 month, escalating to 88% (22/25) at 3 months, and stabilizing at 86.3% (19/22) by 6 months. Approximately 96% (23/24 evaluable patients) appeared to attain a drug-free state at 3 months post-infusion. These preliminary observations in small, heterogeneous early-phase cohorts are consistent with potential therapeutic benefit, though they require validation in larger prospective trials with longer follow-up.

A single case report highlights CAR-T therapy’s impact on neuropsychiatric SLE. Post-treatment evaluation appeared to demonstrate resolution of arthritis and cutaneous vasculitis, followed by apparent clinical improvement in neurological manifestations, temporally associated with B-cell depletion, autoantibody clearance, and apparent regression of inflammatory lesions on MRI. While this case suggests potential therapeutic relevance of CAR-T in neuropsychiatric lupus, the single-patient nature and uncontrolled design preclude definitive conclusions regarding CAR-T’s efficacy for this challenging manifestation, and further investigation in larger cohorts is warranted [38]. Moreover, a seminal case report has unveiled CAR-T therapy’s efficacy in SLE with predominant refractory immune thrombocytopenia (ITP). The patient initially manifested thrombocytopenia (platelet < 20 × 10⁹/L) and proteinuria. While renal remission was achieved with glucocorticoid, mycophenolate and rituximab, profound ITP persisted. Subsequent CAR-T cell infusion induced sustained platelet recovery (platelet > 100 × 10⁹/L at 6 months), unveiling novel therapeutic applicability for this recalcitrant phenotype [44]. Furthermore, SLE predominantly affects women of childbearing age, making unstable disease and treatment risks a critical determinant in fertility decisions [33]. A landmark case demonstrates CAR-T therapy’s dual benefits in this context. A refractory SLE patient achieving sustained LLDAS after BCMA/CD19-targeted CAR-T infusion successfully conceived 6 months post-treatment and delivered a healthy term infant, highlighting its potential to reconcile disease control with reproductive safety [41]. Emerging data from 3 juvenile-onset SLE cases reveal nuanced efficacy patterns. While CAR-T therapy induced clinical responses in all of them, the magnitude of improvement appeared attenuated compared to adult cohorts [39, 43]. This disparity may stem from age-related immunological differences, evidenced by prolonged CAR-T cell persistence (> 180 days) in one adolescent, or confounders like lower cell doses and limited sample size. Notably, one of them with pre-treatment end-stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis regained partial renal function (CKD stage 3) post-CAR-T, enabling dialysis discontinuation. This milestone underscored CAR-T’s transformative potential even in advanced pediatric lupus nephritis [39].

CAR-T therapy in SSc

SSc arises from a pathogenic triad of immune dysregulation, vasculopathy, and fibrosis, with maladaptive B-cell activation driving autoantibody production and profibrotic cytokine release [4, 8]. Clinically, SSc is classified into diffuse cutaneous (dcSSc) and limited cutaneous (lcSSc) subtypes based on skin involvement. Emerging CD19-targeted CAR-T therapies hold dual promise in these complex cases, potentially resetting shared autoimmune drivers while mitigating fibrosis. The heterogeneity across subtypes underscores the need for precision strategies addressing both B-cell hyperactivity and fibroblast-endothelial crosstalk. Preliminary data from 6 small studies involving 14 distinct SSc patients (with documented cohort overlap as noted in Table 2) are summarized [40, 45–49]. The 14 distinct patients evaluated had a median age of 46 years (range 32–60), with an equal sex distribution (50% female). Despite a relatively shorter median disease duration of 2 years (range 4 months–11 years), all had rapidly progressive or severe disease refractory to at least two lines of immunosuppressive therapy, defining a cohort with early but aggressive dcSSc.

All evaluated CD19-targeted constructs; disease subtyping confirmed 9/10 characterized patients as diffuse cutaneous (dcSSc) and 1 as overlap with rheumatoid arthritis. Using composite endpoints (ACR-CRISS score ≥ 0.6 or EUSTAR activity index ≥ 2.5) [50–52], these small cohorts appeared to demonstrate composite response rates of 58.3% (7/12) at 3 months, with apparent escalation to 100% (13/13) at 6 months among evaluable patients, although these tiny sample sizes and limited follow-up preclude definitive conclusions. These early responses likely reflect the successful suppression of ongoing inflammation. In contrast, drug-free state (discontinuation of immunosuppressive, antifibrotic, and vasoactive agents) was observed in only 12.5% (1/8 evaluable patients) at 3 months. This stark contrast underscores a key pathophysiological insight: while CAR-T can effectively target the immunologic component of SSc, the established fibrosis and vascular dysfunction—hallmarks of the disease—represent relatively autonomous pathological processes. Consequently, most patients continued to require adjunctive antifibrotic or vasoactive therapies, supporting a potential future paradigm where CAR-T serves as an immune resetting induction therapy, to be combined with organ-targeted strategies. Notably, the absence of grade ≥ 3 CRS across all studies (n = 14) reinforces CAR-T’s tolerability in SSc, though longer follow-up is required to assess durability and late-onset sequelae.

A pivotal study by Xu and his colleagues challenges the autologous CAR-T paradigm in SSc, demonstrating comparable or superior efficacy with allogeneic CD19-targeted CAR-T cells in 3 dcSSc patients exhibiting rapid skin progression (ΔmRSS > 10 at 6 months) [47]. This proof-of-concept trial achieved 100% response (ACR-CRISS score ≥ 0.6) by Week 12, aligning with autologous cohort outcomes despite HLA-mismatched donors. However, transient B-cell reconstitution (5 months shorter than post-autologous therapy) and theoretical graft rejection risks necessitate extended follow-up [22, 23]. These early data position allogeneic CAR-T as a viable alternative for dcSSc patients with manufacturing failures, pending validation in larger cohorts.

CAR-T therapy in IIM

IIM, a clinically and immunologically heterogeneous spectrum encompassing polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), antisynthetase syndrome (ASS), and inclusion body myositis (IBM), are systemic autoimmune disorders unified by immune attack on skeletal muscle [6, 7]. Despite the considerable heterogeneity among IIM subtypes and the fact that their precise pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, emerging evidence underscores a pivotal role for pathogenic autoantibodies derived from dysregulated B cell activity in driving disease progression across multiple subtypes [6, 53]. Notably, conventional therapies (e.g., glucocorticoids, methotrexate) and even biologics fail to induce sustained remission in a substantial proportion of IIM patients, with a pooled response rate of merely 26% observed across all IMM subtypes, as evidenced by recent meta-analyses [54]. Among evaluable patients with sufficient longitudinal data (n = 4 at 3 months; n = 3 at 6 months representing a very small and heterogeneous subset), all apparent achieved MCR by TIS criteria. For the remaining 4 patients lacking sufficient data to determine MCR status, preliminary functional improvements were documented, including apparent muscle strength improvement, CK level changes, MMT-8 score progression, PGA reduction, and imaging findings—consistent with potential CAR-T-mediated therapeutic activity in IIM, though these preliminary observations in very small cohorts require validation in larger, more rigorously characterized trials. Notably, 75% (6/8) attained drug-free state at 3 months, with 2 exceptions: 1 patient in Lengerke’s cohort required adjunctive mycophenolate mofetil for persistent CD8+ T cell expansion [56], and 1 in Xu’s study remained on low-dose glucocorticoids due to protracted serological abnormalities [47]. Notably, 2 ASS patients in this cohort exhibited transient symptom exacerbation, including myalgia and elevated CK, within days post-CAR-T infusion. Schett et al. attributed this phenomenon to pseudomyalgia linked to CRS [55], whereas Lengerke et al. concurrently observed a surge in circulating CD8+ terminally differentiated effector memory Temra cells and empirically initiated adjunctive mycophenolate mofetil, hypothesizing cytokine-driven myocyte hyperexcitability as the underlying mechanism [56]. Notably, 2/8 patients received allogeneic CAR-T therapy and also achieved favorable clinical responses, demonstrating comparable efficacy to autologous CAR-T in this cohort [47, 57].

Safety profile: from acute toxicity to long-term considerations

Despite the remarkable clinical efficacy demonstrated by CAR-T therapy as an emerging treatment for ARDs, vigilant attention to its AE remains imperative. Here, we report the incidence and severity of short-term AEs following CAR-T cell infusion, focusing on CRS and ICANS [62–65]. Management strategies, primarily corticosteroids and tocilizumab, are also summarized. Notably, cytopenia and anemia were frequently observed across studies; however, given their common association with underlying disease activity and the standard pre-infusion lymphodepletion regimen (cyclophosphamide/fludarabine), these events were excluded from this specific AE analysis as they cannot be solely attributed to CAR-T therapy.

To mitigate potential bias from overlapping patient cohorts, only studies with subsequent long-term follow-up data were included for analysis. Detailed results are presented in Table 4. Among 47 distinct patients treated with CAR-T for ARDs, CRS occurred in 36 cases (31 Grade 1, 5 Grade 2). ICANS was reported in 3 cases (all Grade 1). Critically, no AEs with Grade ≥ 3 were documented. Management involved corticosteroids in 4 patients and tocilizumab in 8 patients [66]. All events were rapidly and effectively controlled, aligning with findings in the oncology settings.

Beyond these acute toxicities, monitoring of longer-term immunological sequelae is essential. Profound B-cell aplasia, the intended therapeutic effect, carries associated risks. Among the same cohort of 47 patients, hypogammaglobulinemia was reported in 23 cases (48.9%). This condition likely results from sustained B-cell depletion, although its attribution can be confounded by prior therapies such as rituximab [67]. Intravenous immunoglobulin replacement was administered in at least 4 cases. Infections, predominantly upper respiratory tract infections, were observed in a subset of patients and led to hospitalization in a few instances; no ICU admissions or fatal infections have been reported to date. Preliminary data on humoral immunity suggest that while vaccine antibody titers (e.g., against tetanus or pneumococcus) may decline post-treatment, they often remain above protective thresholds. The optimal strategy and timing for re-vaccination during immune reconstitution remain an area for future investigation.

However, recent reports highlight the risk of secondary malignancies following CAR-T therapy [68–70], which has prompted an FDA boxed warning, and underscored the necessity for ongoing assessment of its long-term safety [71]. This theoretical risk warrants particular vigilance in the context of ARDs, as the patient population—often young adults—faces a potentially decades-long lifespan post-treatment. Although no such cases have been reported in ARDs cohorts to date, the current follow-up duration remains insufficient to definitively quantify this risk. Long-term, prospective registry studies are imperative to monitor this potential late effect. Other long-term uncertainties include the impact on fertility. While a case of successful pregnancy after CAR-T therapy has been reported, comprehensive data are awaited to fully understand the reproductive safety profile. Moreover, our investigation highlights the occurrence of local immune effector cell-associated toxicity syndrome (LICATS), a distinct and frequent toxicity observed after CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy in patients with autoimmune diseases [72]. LICATS is defined as a self-limited, organ-specific inflammatory reaction that occurs exclusively during the B-cell aplasia phase and only in organs previously affected by the patient’s underlying autoimmune disease (e.g., skin, kidneys, joints, lungs). Its clinical spectrum ranges from mild, transient rash or creatinine elevation (Grade 1–2) to rare events requiring brief hospitalization (Grade 3), but it consistently resolves without sequelae. Crucially, LICATS should be distinguished from both cytokine release syndrome (CRS, which is systemic) and a true disease flare; its recognition is important to avoid unnecessary reinstatement of immunosuppression, as it may reflect a local “cleansing” process associated with deep B-cell depletion.

Emerging insights from EULAR convergence 2025

The 2025 EULAR Congress showcased preliminary yet influential developments in CAR-T therapy for autoimmune diseases, offering a timely snapshot of the field’s trajectory despite the non-peer-reviewed nature of abstract data. Consistent with published trials, research focus remained on SLE, SSc and IIM, predominantly targeting CD19. Notably, dose-escalation strategies emerged as a key theme. Among 10 SLE patients receiving variable CAR-T doses (1 × 105/kg, 2 × 105/kg, 3 × 10⁶/kg), preliminary data appeared to show clinical responses without obvious dose-dependent efficacy differentiation, though sample sizes preclude definitive dose–response conclusions [73]. However, higher level CRS rates occurred in the 3 × 10⁶/kg group. 5 SSc patients undergoing similar dose exploration lacked detailed safety/efficacy stratification, though this represents progress toward standardized regimens [73]. Alternative preconditioning approaches exploring cyclophosphamide or bendamustine as fludarabine substitutes demonstrated preserved clinical efficacy [74, 75], while NxM CAR-T platforms showed feasibility in bypassing conventional lymphodepletion regimens [76]. Nevertheless, preconditioning chemotherapy remained associated with AE including infections and cytopenias, necessitating vigilant monitoring even with modified regimens. The uniform application of RESE-CEL (allogeneic CD19 CAR-T) across SLE, SSc, and IIM cohorts revealed differential therapeutic responses [77–79]. SLE and IIM patients exhibited faster and more pronounced clinical improvement compared to SSc cases, aligning with the inflammatory-dominant pathology in SLE/IIM versus fibrotic mechanisms in SSc. Former analyses showed superior efficacy in non-renal SLE versus lupus nephritis, and faster major response attainment in dermatomyositis (29 days) compared to IMNM (24 weeks). Notably, a patient of LN developed grade 4 ICANS potentially linked to occult infection [78]. The event resolved completely with standard management, though its temporal association with CAR-T infusion highlights the imperative for rigorous antimicrobial prophylaxis protocols in this population. Recent methodological discussions in SSc research highlight the value of employing both EUSTAR Activity Index and ACR CRISS to comprehensively assess CAR-T therapeutic outcomes, enabling simultaneous evaluation of inflammatory activity and organ-specific damage [80]. Of particular relevance to rheumatologic practice is the observed incidence of secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) following CD19-targeted CAR-T infusion, with 3.6% of patients developing this complication within 90 days (median 11 days) [81]. Clinical management with interleukin-1 blockade and glucocorticoids achieved 80.9% survival at the 90-day mark in reported cases [82, 83]. These findings, though primarily documented in hematologic malignancies, underscore the critical need for proactive HLH monitoring in ARDs cohorts receiving CAR-T therapy, particularly given their baseline susceptibility to immune dysregulation.

Emerging insights from ACR convergence 2025

The 2025 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) Convergence further advanced the translational landscape of CAR-T therapy in autoimmunity, with several abstracts providing critical mechanistic depth and highlighting next-generation innovations; these presentations, while preliminary, are essential for understanding the current research frontier. A notable emerging trend is the exploration of CD20 as a complementary or alternative target to CD19, with dual CD20-containing strategies aiming to enhance B-cell lineage coverage and achieve deeper tissue-resident B-cell depletion. Two pivotal studies exemplify this shift: Ding and colleagues reported longitudinal B-cell receptor (BCR) repertoire tracking in lupus nephritis patients treated with CD20/BCMA dual-targeting CAR-T cells (n = 11 total SLE cohort), demonstrating absence of pathogenic BCR clonal expansion post-therapy—a molecular correlate potentially underpinning durable remission and addressing a critical knowledge gap regarding immune reconstitution dynamics [84]. In parallel, Kim et al. presented proof-of-concept data on a novel CD19/CD20 bispecific CAR-T construct entering phase I trials, further validating the dual-targeting paradigm [85].

Mechanistic abstracts by Freitag and Dehdashtian revealed unexpected immunological breadth: post-CAR-T reductions in circulating immune complex (cIC) activity and neutrophil reconstitution patterns, respectively, suggesting therapeutic effects extend beyond direct B-cell depletion to encompass broader innate and adaptive immune remodeling [86, 87]. While these observations are preliminary and derived from small samples, they raise the intriguing hypothesis that deep B-cell depletion via CAR-T may indirectly ameliorate dysregulated T-cell help, monocyte activation, and neutrophil effector functions—key pillars of disease pathogenesis in SLE, SSc, and IIM. Validating these systemic immunological effects in larger studies is a crucial future direction.

From a translational perspective, a systematic review of CAR-T therapy in rheumatoid arthritis (though outside the primary scope of this review) underscored a critical barrier to widespread adoption: the substantial economic burden, with current autologous CAR-T manufacturing costs averaging approximately USD 750,000 per patient [88]. This echoes early challenges faced by biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs), which required decades of biosimilar development, health technology assessments, and reimbursement negotiations to achieve broad accessibility. Analogous strategies—including allogeneic "off-the-shelf" CAR-T platforms, streamlined manufacturing workflows, and value-based pricing models—will be essential to democratize CAR-T therapy beyond academic centers and high-resource settings.

Therapeutic paradigms: reset, depletion, and suppression

The investigation of CAR-T cell therapy necessitates a paradigm-based understanding of its place among advanced therapies for refractory ARDs. Direct comparisons of efficacy rates between these modalities are not currently meaningful, as they originate from vastly different stages of clinical development (e.g., early-phase cohorts versus large randomized trials) and involve fundamentally distinct patient populations (multi-refractory versus earlier treatment lines). Therefore, contrasting their core therapeutic intent and biological rationale is more instructive. Current approaches can be categorized conceptually: 1) targeted lymphodepletion aiming for immune reset (e.g., CAR-T); 2) antibody-mediated cell depletion (e.g., anti-CD20 therapy); and 3) pathway inhibition or broad immunomodulation (e.g., belimumab). This framework provides a conceptual lens to distinguish CAR-T from other advanced therapies. It positions CAR-T not as a direct competitor to established continuous depletion or suppression strategies, but as a distinct, investigational approach predicated on the goal of a single-intervention immune reset. Whether this mechanistic promise translates into durable clinical benefit, and for which patients, hinges on resolving the key uncertainties outlined below.

Current knowledge gaps and methodological constraints

The promising outcomes of CAR-T therapy in ARDs must be interpreted within the context of significant limitations inherent to the current evidence base: (1) limited cohort scale and heterogeneity: findings are predominantly derived from small, uncontrolled phase I/II trials and case series. These studies provide unstable estimates of treatment effects, lack statistical power for subgroup analyses, and preclude any definitive efficacy conclusions or valid comparisons against standard-of-care therapies. The heterogeneity in treatment protocols—including CAR construct design, lymphodepletion intensity, and endpoint definitions—further complicates cross-study synthesis; (2) susceptibility to publication bias: this nascent field is particularly vulnerable to publication bias. Early-phase reports and case series naturally tend to highlight breakthrough responses and promising safety profiles, while experiences with limited efficacy, treatment failure, or significant toxicity may remain unpublished. This creates a distorted evidence ecosystem where the published literature likely presents an optimistically skewed representation of the true risk–benefit profile; (3) unaddressed long-term safety concerns: while short-term toxicities (CRS/ICANS) appear manageable, the long-term risks—particularly secondary malignancies as highlighted in recent FDA warnings—remain unquantified due to the limited follow-up duration in all reported cohorts. These constraints underscore an urgent need for harmonized trial designs, standardized ARDs-specific CAR-T protocols, and international registries to consolidate longitudinal safety/efficacy data.

Beyond the promising short-term efficacy and manageable acute toxicity profile, the long-term durability of CAR-T-induced remission remains the paramount unanswered question in its application for ARDs. The ultimate clinical value will be determined not only by the depth of initial B-cell depletion but also by the kinetics and quality of immune reconstitution. Critical unknowns include: (1) the heterogeneity in B-cell reconstitution patterns among patients and across different ARDs, and how these patterns correlate with relapse risk; (2) the fate of long-lived plasma cells, which are CD19-negative and may persist as reservoirs of pathogenic autoantibodies, potentially influencing serological remission and late relapse; and (3) the functional competence of the reconstituted immune system, including responses to neoantigens and vaccines, which has implications for long-term infection risk and the feasibility of re-vaccination. Addressing these questions is essential to define the potential need for, and timing of, retreatment with CAR-T or other therapies, and to understand whether a single intervention can truly achieve a sustained “immune reset.”

Future directions and ongoing clinical development

The promising yet preliminary outcomes reviewed herein have catalyzed the expansion of prospective clinical development. To translate early enthusiasm into evidence-based practice, several critical pathways must be pursued.

First, precision in patient selection must advance beyond the broad label of “refractoriness”. Prospective studies are needed to define the clinical features (e.g., specific organ involvement, activity thresholds) and biomarkers (e.g., autoantibody profiles, B-cell signatures) that predict response. Equally crucial is identifying the optimal therapeutic window, ideally prior to irreversible organ damage, and recognizing subsets less likely to benefit.

Second, therapeutic platforms and constructs are evolving. To date, experience centers on CD19, BCMA, and CD20 (Fig. 2). Next-generation targets (e.g., CD70, BAFF receptor) and bispecific constructs aim to deepen depletion or disrupt autoimmune circuitry more precisely. Parallel platform innovations include exploring metabolically armed CAR-T cells to overcome T-cell dysfunction in heavily pretreated patients, and allogeneic “off-the-shelf” products to circumvent manufacturing challenges. Concurrently, optimizing lymphodepletion regimens and exploring disease-specific combinations are active areas of investigation.

B-cell antigen expression across lineage

To date, clinical experience has centered on CD19, BCMA, and CD20 as primary B-cell depletion targets. The selection among these is guided by their complementary expression patterns across the B-cell lineage (Fig. 2). Registered trials now under way are exploring mechanistically distinct antigens, including CD70 (expressed on activated B and T cells, mediating co-stimulatory signals critical for germinal center T-B collaboration) and BAFF receptor (sustaining autoreactive B-cell survival via BAFF/APRIL signaling, and clinically validated as a therapeutic target through belimumab in SLE) [89–95]. These next-wave targets aim to disrupt autoimmune circuitry beyond conventional B-cell depletion. Looking further ahead, the field may draw additional translational insights from B-cell malignancy CAR-T development, where CD22 (co-expressed with CD19 on mature B cells, addressing potential CD19-low or -escaped clones) [96], CD79b (the B-cell receptor signaling complex β-chain, validated as a therapeutic target via antibody–drug conjugates such as polatuzumab vedotin) [97], and CD37 (a mature B-cell tetraspanin with broader expression than CD20) have demonstrated feasibility and, in some cases, clinical efficacy [98]. Translating these antigens to autoimmune contexts will require validation of tissue penetration, safety in non-malignant settings, and comparative efficacy against established targets.

In addition to antigen diversification, platform-level innovations and manufacturing considerations are entering clinical evaluation. For example, a registered study is assessing metabolically armed CD19 CAR-T cells in moderate-to-severe active SLE, underscoring efforts to optimize CAR-T fitness beyond receptor targeting alone—a pertinent approach given the potentially compromised T-cell function in heavily pre-treated ARD patients [99, 100]. Concurrently, the exploration of allogeneic “off-the-shelf” products aims to overcome challenges related to autologous manufacturing (time, cost, variable T-cell quality). However, their development necessitates careful gene editing to mitigate graft-versus-host and host rejection risks. Translating these advanced therapies to clinic also hinges on addressing manufacturing scalability and cost, critical factors for ensuring equitable access. The fundamentally different risk–benefit profile in ARDs, compared to oncology, further mandates that manufacturing and quality control paradigms prioritize long-term safety, particularly regarding genotoxicity. Ongoing studies are also investigating lymphodepletion regimen variations (cyclophosphamide/fludarabine dosing, alternative agents such as bendamustine) and, in select protocols, reduced-intensity or non-lymphodepleting conditioning—though the latter remains exploratory with insufficient data to draw conclusions regarding efficacy or toxicity implications [101]. Disease-specific adaptations (e.g., combination with antifibrotic therapies in SSc, fertility preservation protocols in pediatric-onset ARDs) and harmonization of trial design, composite endpoints, and international registry infrastructure will be critical to translate early enthusiasm into evidence-based practice.

Conclusion

CAR-T cell therapy has rapidly evolved from an oncological breakthrough to a promising investigational strategy for refractory ARDs. Preliminary clinical evidence across SLE, SSc and IIM from small, early-phase cohorts suggests potential capacity to induce remissions through deep B-cell depletion, with apparent efficacy signals that may exceed those of conventional B-cell depleting biologics, though definitive conclusions await longer-term prospective validation. Pioneering trials describe apparent favorable efficacy signals, with over 95% drug-free state rate within 3 months in SLE and 100% composite response rate by 6 months in SSc despite delayed fibrotic remodeling. Similarly, universal major clinical responses in IIM highlight B-cell targeting as a unifying therapeutic principle. Critically, short-term safety profiles are favorable, with predominantly low-grade CRS/ICANS.

The emergence of allogeneic "off-the-shelf" products further expands the therapeutic arsenal, however significant challenges persist. Long-term durability beyond 12 months, B-cell reconstitution dynamics, and potential secondary malignancy risks require vigilant monitoring. Disease-specific barriers, such as residual vascular dysfunction in SSc, transient symptom flares in IIM, and attenuated responses in pediatric lupus, demand tailored strategies. Future translation efforts should prioritize precision patient selection, mechanistically informed combination approaches (e.g., CAR-T plus antifibrotic strategies in SSc), standardized endpoints, and next-generation CAR constructs with refined safety profiles. If current early-phase signals are validated in larger prospective trials with extended follow-up, CAR-T cell therapy has potential to offer a novel therapeutic option for carefully selected patients with truly refractory ARDs, though its ultimate clinical role and place in treatment algorithms remain to be defined through rigorous clinical development.
