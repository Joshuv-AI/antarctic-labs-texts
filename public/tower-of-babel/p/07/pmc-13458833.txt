Carbene-catalyzed site-selective sulfonylation and modular edition of monosaccharides for hydroxyl group stereoinversion and swapping
Authors: Hongyan Long, Gangyu Wang, Chunyan Jian, Luhang Yang, Xingxing Wu, Yonggui Robin Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonosaccharides are ubiquitous motifs in natural and synthetic molecules, yet methods for their selective structure editing remain limited due to the multiple hydroxy groups for differentiation and their structural complexity. Among possible strategies for stereoinversion and functional group swapping of saccharides, the classical SN2 substitution of alcohols via sulfonate esters offers a potentially robust approach. A central challenge, however, is the selective functionalization of sulfonyl groups on specific hydroxyl moiety. Here we report an organocatalytic approach to enable site-selective sulfonylation of minimally protected saccharides by exploiting an underexplored N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC)-derived sulfonyl-azolium intermediate. Distinct NHC scaffolds govern regioselectivity for the sulfonyl group transfer, overcoming the challenge of differentiating multiple hydroxyl units in complex sugar substrates. The installed sulfonates serve as versatile leaving groups for subsequent nucleophilic substitution, constituting a “sulfonylation–swapping” strategy that transforms abundant sugars into diverse high-value rare sugars, including pharmaceutically relevant structures such as dapagliflozin analogues. Furthermore, the readily accessed rare sugars exhibit intriguing antimicrobial activity against various plant pathogens, demonstrating significant potential for the development of potent fungicide agents.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Stereochemistry

Introduction

Monosaccharides are fundamental structural motifs in a wide range of biomacromolecules, including polysaccharides, nucleic acids, and glycoproteins1–3. They are also widely present in natural and synthetic molecules with important applications in areas such as medicine discovery4–8. It is well established that both the stereochemical configuration and the specific functional groups of monosaccharide units play critical roles in determining their biological activity and other properties9–16. For example, the inversion of the OH group in position 4′ of the glucose unit of doxorubicin resulted in another drug agent, epirubicin, which exhibits less toxicity, particularly in regard to cardiotoxicity (Fig. 1A)17,18. In another instance, the arylation of the anomeric carbon of glucose lead to the discovery of the medicine empagliflozin for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)19. Given their proven applications, synthetic methods for altering either the stereochemistry or functional group of monosaccharides and their analogs have received significant attention20–33. One emerging research direction focuses on stereochemical inversion of hydroxyl units on saccharides (Fig. 1B). Notable examples include Wendlandt’s photochemical radical reactions and network-control strategy for a site-selective alcohol epimerization method to transform abundant glycans into their rare isomers34–36. Meanwhile, the Tang group demonstrated a general synthesis of rare sugars from commercially available common monosaccharides via a one-step Ru(II)-catalyzed and boron-mediated selective epimerization of 1,2-trans-diols to 1,2-cis-diols37,38. Another important direction involves transforming hydroxyl groups into other functional groups, such as hydrogen atoms via hydroxyl deletion (Fig. 1B)39. For instance, Miller et al. demonstrated selective deoxygenated derivatization of sugar-containing complex bioactive molecules using peptide-based organocatalysts40,41.

Among the possible strategies for stereoinversion and functional group swapping of saccharides, the classic bimolecular nucleophilic substitution (SN2) of alcohols via their corresponding sulfonate esters can potentially be developed as a robust and straightforward approach for saccharide transformation (Fig. 1C)42–46. A key challenge toward this goal relies on the selective installation of a sulfonyl unit as an activating group on the hydroxyl moiety. Conventional synthetic routes to these compounds typically rely on a protection and deprotection strategy to achieve selective sulfonylation of specific hydroxyl groups within the sugar scaffold47–49. Direct site-selective sulfonylation on the sugars has been developed by Dong, Arai and Wong groups, wherein iron(III), organotin or organoboron catalysts were employed to amplify the intrinsic substrate selectivity differences between equatorial and axial OH groups (Fig. 1C, strategy 1)50–53. This diol activation strategy usually requires a saccharide bearing the structure of cis−1,2-diols or cis−1,3-diols. In contrast, the direct chemo- or stereocontrol through catalyst-bound at the sulfur(VI) center for selective O-sulfonylation reactions has been rarely explored. Liu achieved a selective sulfonylation of diols by Cu-catalyzed enantioselective radical heteroatomic S–O cross-coupling54,55, while Miller developed excellent enantioselective sulfonylation reactions on the meso-diols mediated by a tetrapeptide catalyst bound-sulfonyl species (Fig. 1C, strategy 2)56. Despite advances in these elegant catalytic desymmetrization strategies, they both focused on the desymmetrization strategies, the selective sulfonylation reactions of minimally protected-monosaccharides with multiple hydroxy groups and structural complexity have not been achieved.

We’re interested in developing modular methods for selective transformation of unprotected or minimally protected saccharides and related complex molecules57,58 by means of N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) organocatalysis. We envision that the strong nucleophilicity of the carbene catalyst would provide a general sulfonyl activation strategy by attacking the sulfur (VI) center and eliminating the fluoride to form a distinct sulfonyl-bound-NHC azolium intermediate (Fig. 1D). The resulting carbene-bound S(VI)-azolium would thus behave as an efficient sulfonyl group transfer species with a specific OH group on the sugars. Noteworthy is that despite a diverse range of NHC-derived reactive intermediates having been broadly explored for (chiral) functional molecules synthesis59–71, their heteroatom-centered azolium species have been scarcely studied72–75.

In this work, we report an NHC catalytic approach for the selective sulfonylation of saccharides containing multiple free hydroxyl groups. Efficient activation of sulfonyl fluoride was enabled by the carbene organocatalyst to form the sulfonyl-bound-NHC azolium intermediate. Notably, the NHC catalyst bearing different scaffolds was found to be crucial to achieve the challenging site-selective functionalization of the polyols in monosaccharides. The installed sulfonate groups by catalyst control at the first stage serve as good leaving units that provide a second SN2 operation with a broad range of nucleophilic components, offering a “sulfonylation-swapping” approach that provides a general and modular synthetic route for selective conversion of abundant common sugars to a diverse set of rare sugars, including various significant drug molecules such as dapagliflozin. Furthermore, the readily accessed rare sugars exhibit intriguing antimicrobial activity against various plant pathogens, demonstrating significant potential for the development of potent fungicide agents.

Results

To validate the feasibility of the reaction, our investigation into site-selective sulfonylation commenced with methyl 6-O-trityl-β-D-glucopyranoside (1a) and 4-nitrobenzenesulfonyl fluoride (2a) as model substrates (Fig. 2). An initial catalyst screening was conducted using Cs2CO3 as the base and CHCl3 as the solvent. The use of achiral catalysts NHC A and NHC B resulted in the formation of benzenesulfonate products with low regioselectivity and yield, along with substantial amounts of di- and trisubstituted byproducts (entries 1–2). Switching to NHC C led to a significant improvement in both regioselectivity and yield (entry 3). Notably, no selectivity was observed in the absence of a catalyst (entry 4). Subsequent optimization using NHC C as the catalyst focused on evaluating different bases (entries 5–7). We were delighted to find that K2CO3 was effective to improve both the regioselectivity and yield of the C3-O-sulfonylation product 3a. Further solvent screening revealed that the use of CH2Cl2 substantially enhanced regioselectivity (entries 8–10), with the sulfonate product 3a obtained with 57% yield and 8:1:1 regioselectivity (entry 10). We next studied the effect of different NHC catalysts D–F for their capability to control the site selectivity of the sulfonyl transfer reaction between 2a and saccharide 1a (entries 11–13, see Supplementary Information for more details). Satisfyingly, we found that the use of catalyst F bearing an N-mesityl moiety on the indanol scaffold afforded the product 3a with 72% yield and 23:2:1 site selectivity (entry 13). Employing its enantiomer resulted in diminished performance (entry 14). Furthermore, replacing sulfonyl fluoride 2a with sulfonyl chloride 2b led to a substantial decrease in both yield and selectivity, underscoring the importance of the balanced reactivity of sulfonyl fluoride for the highly site-selective sulfonylation transformation (entry 15).

Upon establishing the optimal reaction conditions (Fig. 2, entry 13), we evaluated the generality of the NHC-catalyzed sulfonylation of various monosaccharide analogs using 4-nitrobenzenesulfonyl fluoride (2a) (Fig. 3). An array of β-D-glucopyranoside derivatives with diverse glycosides was first explored. The catalytic system demonstrated the broad applicability of the site-selective sulfonylation across various carbohydrate substrates bearing either O-alkyl or O-aryl substitution at the anomeric carbon. For example, aryl substitution at the 1-OH site maintained comparable regioselectivity to provide products 3b–3d and 3h in 52%–73% yield and 11:4:1 to 22:1:2 site selectivity. Notably, an extended reaction time of 36 h was required to ensure complete consumption of the starting material 1. Similarly, alkyl substitution at the 1-O-position afforded sulfonate products 3a and 3e–3g with regioselectivities ranging from 11:2:1 to 23:2:1 and yields of 64%–74%. As a technique note, when substrates 2e, 2f, 2g, 2h and 2r were used, the trityl group of the resulting C3-O-sulfonate products was readily cleaved over the column chromatography, yielding the naked sugars 3e, 3f, 3g, 3h and 3r. Moreover, the antidiabetic drugs dapagliflozin and empagliflozin (modified monosaccharide analogs) were also compatible, delivering 3i and 3j with excellent selectivity and good yields. Replacing D-glucose with other types of monosaccharides could successfully afford the 3-OH sulfonylation products under this catalytic system. For instance, when D-galactose derivatives bearing alkyl or aryl groups at the 1-OH position were used as substrates, their corresponding products 3k–3n were obtained with 51%–87% yields and high regioselectivity ranging from 6:1:1 to 36:2:1. The mannose and L-rhamnose derivatives with 1-OMe units proceeded smoothly as well to afford the 3-OH sulfonylation products 3o and 3q. Intriguingly, when compound 1p was used as the starting material under the standard conditions, the reaction yielded the sulfonylation product 3p at the 2-OH position with 68% yield and over 20:1 site selectivity. In addition to pentose, tetrose was compatible with the catalytic system, as demonstrated by the sulfonylation of β-D-ribofuranose derivative 1r, wherein the corresponding product 3r was obtained in 63% yield and 5:1 regioselectivity.

The nosylate group is an exceptionally good leaving group and thus widely utilized in various synthetic transformations76. Therefore, the obtained nosylated monosaccharide products offer a vast range of opportunities for further straightforward swapping on the carbohydrate scaffold through diverse C–O, C–X (X = Cl, Br, I), C–N, and C–S bond transformations employing nucleophilic substitution reactions (Fig. 4A). First, reactions with carboxylic acid nucleophiles (including aryl, heteroaryl, and alkyl derivatives) afforded the corresponding formal acylation products 7a–7f in 54%–72% yields. In contrast to the recent emerging studies on catalyst-controlled site-selective acylation of the polyols in saccharides56–58, the configuration of the hydroxyl group in our transformation is stereospecifically inverted from an equatorial to an axial configuration, enabling efficient modular construction of a broad range of rare monosaccharides. Furthermore, this protocol proved highly effective for saccharide modification of commercial pharmaceuticals such as probenecid, naproxen, and oxaprozin, which provided 7g–7j in 64%–75% yields under NaH-mediated conditions. Amino acid derivatives were also readily incorporated with the saccharide under simple basic conditions to provide an array of amino-acid functionalized rare sugars 7k–7n with 78%–89% yields, demonstrating the synthetic utility of our editing method to construct structurally diverse unnatural carbohydrate functional molecules.

As key functional building blocks and bioactive molecules, halogenated monosaccharides have found broad applications across various fields such as chemical biology, drug development, and materials science77. Conventional synthetic routes to these compounds typically rely on multi-step protection and deprotection strategies to achieve selective halogenation within the sugar scaffold78,79. By leveraging the nosylate group in compound 3b, an array of medicinally relevant deoxy halogenated sugars 7o–7q was rapidly prepared by employing LiX as a nucleophilic halogen source. This protocol offers a promising strategy for the synthesis of versatile biological probes and key synthetic intermediates with substantial potential. Furthermore, modification of the glucose through versatile C–N and C–S bond formations was achieved by reactions with NaN3 and 4-fluorothiophenol under simple conditions, yielding 7r and 7s with 71% and 77% yields, respectively.

Recent progress has witnessed various strategies to achieve direct stereochemical inversion of hydroxyl groups on natural carbohydrates as a general strategy to access rare sugars, as reported by Wendlandt and Tang34–37. In this context, the prepared sulfonate products 3 readily facilitate the streamlined stereoinversion of the natural sugars. Products 8a–8b with a C3-hydroxyl group at the uncommon axial position were prepared in 50%–78% yields by treatment of the C3-O-sulfonylated product 3b under NaNO2/DMSO conditions (Fig. 4B). Notably, this approach also enabled the inversion of the 3–OH in the commercial drug dapagliflozin, yielding product 8c with 70% yield. Given the significance of these unnatural sugars in the study of structure-activity relationships, our OH stereoinversion approach underscores notable potential in the modular modification of sugar-containing bioactive molecules for drug discovery.

Triazole derivatives represent a significant class of compounds in medicinal chemistry due to their broad-spectrum biological activity, including notable antimicrobial and antitumor properties80. The catalytically obtained azido sugar product 7r provided a versatile clickable handle that enables efficient construction of a series of triazole derivatives via ruthenium-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (RuAAC) reactions. We thereby successfully prepared a range of triazole compounds 10a–10d with 68%–83% yields through reactions between the azide-glucose 7r and various alkynes (Fig. 5A). Notably, the optically enriched natural product (R)-(–)-2-phenylpropionic acid could also be easily assembled with 7r to furnish the product 10e in 65% yield, providing a modification strategy of natural product through incorporation of glucose scaffold. Furthermore, we investigated the substrate scope with respect to various substituted benzenesulfonyl fluorides 2 (Fig. 5B). The study revealed that both electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups at the para-position of the benzene ring were well tolerated under this catalytic system, affording the corresponding 3-O-sulfonylated products 3s–3u with 59%–70% yields and high regioselectivity (>20:1:1).

Motivated by the highly selective C3-sulfonylation, we then turned to investigate the catalyst-controlled sulfonylation of β-D-glucopyranoside at 6-OH and 2-OH positions. Simple modification of the conditions with catalyst G, the reaction between substrate 11 and sulfonyl fluoride 2a afforded the C6-O-sulfonate derivative 12 as the sole mono-sulfonylation product, albeit in a modest yield 42% yield, owing to competing multi-substitution byproducts. Subsequent displacement transformation with sodium azide gave the azide-functionalization product 13 in 65% yield (Fig. 5C). Regarding functionalization at the more challenging 2-OH position, after extensive studies (see SI, Table S2), we found that the merge of carbene catalyst A and boronic acid 14 as an additive could deliver the sulfonate product 4b with >20:1:1 regioselectivity. Boric acid 14 likely controls chemoselectivity by reversible formation of cyclic boronate esters with the vicinal diol moiety of saccharide 1a. In combination with NHC catalysts, this cooperative catalytic system further amplifies site selectivity. Notably, the product 4b was obtained in only modest yield (31%), presumably due to incomplete conversion of the sulfonyl fluoride 2a, with 41% of the starting material remaining unconsumed even after 4 days of reaction. Furthermore, product 4a’ was obtained in high selectivity through a two-step sequence involving initial sulfonylation to give 4a followed by trityl deprotection using H2SO4/EtOH (Fig. 5D).

Rare sugars and their derivatives exhibit diverse biological activities with excellent biocompatibility, rendering them promising candidates for green agrochemical development81,82. Consequently, we conducted preliminary studies to evaluate the antimicrobial properties of the synthesized compounds, aiming to identify potential leads for agrochemical development (Fig. 5E). For this purpose, we assessed the inhibitory activity of products 3−13 at a concentration of 100 μg/mL against various common plant pathogens83,84. Carboxylate ester 7m and halogenated derivative 7o displayed broad-spectrum fungicidal activity. Notably, 7o showed superior inhibition (76.2%) against Phomopsis sp. (Ps) relative to the commercial agent azoxystrobin (65.4%). Furthermore, 7m outperformed the commercial reference against Sclerotinia sclerotiorum (Ss), Fusarium oxysporum (Fo), and Botrytis cinerea (Bc). In particular, 7m exhibits a notably high inhibition rate of 97.0% against Ss, which is significantly superior to that of the positive control (60.6%). Additionally, the triazolyl sugar 10e also effectively suppressed the growth of Ss at the tested concentration, demonstrating a high inhibition rate of 94.5%. Compounds 3e and 3k exhibited notable inhibition rates against C. capsici (Cc), which is slightly lower than that of the commercial fungicide. Notably, the EC50 values for selected compounds in Fig. 5E that exhibited significant antimicrobial activity against the plant pathogens Ps, Bc, and Ss, including compounds 7 m and 7o. In particular, compounds 7o (1.3 μg/mL) and 7r (1.8 μg/mL) displayed substantially enhanced potency against Ps compared to the commercial control (25.3 μg/mL). These findings highlight the potential of these rare sugars as promising lead structures for developing potent antifungal agrochemicals.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed an organocatalytic method for the site-selective sulfonylation and modular edition of monosaccharides, addressing the long-standing challenge of rapid access to rare sugars by differentiating multiple hydroxyl groups within complex sugar scaffolds. Sulfonyl fluorides are readily activated through an underexplored heteroatom-centered NHC-derived sulfonyl-azolium intermediate to enable highly site-selective sulfonylation of minimally protected monosaccharides. The carbene catalyst scaffold proved crucial in governing regioselectivity, providing a controllable sulfonyl group transfer agent for selective control over hydroxyl differentiation. Importantly, the installed sulfonates act as versatile leaving groups, allowing subsequent nucleophilic substitutions and establishing a modular “sulfonylation-swapping” strategy for the efficient conversion of abundant common sugars into a broad range of rare monosaccharides with potent antimicrobial activity. In the long round, we expect that the concept of NHC-catalyzed sulfonyl-centered activation shall offer a broadly applicable strategy in stereoselective catalysis and open avenues for the streamlined synthesis of glycoconjugates, rare sugars, and other complex biomolecules.

Methods

General procedure for C3-O-benzenesulfonate (3) formation

To an oven-dried anaerobic tube (10 mL) equipped with a magnetic stir bar was added NHC pre-catalyst F (0.02 mmol, 20 mol%), K2CO3 (0.3 mmol, 3.0 equiv.) in 1.7 mL CH2Cl2. The reaction was allowed to stir at room temperature for 1 h. Then monosaccharide 1 (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) and sulfonyl fluoride 2 (0.11 mmol, 1.1 equiv.) were successively added. The reaction was allowed to stir at 0 °C for 36 h. After that, the reaction tube was opened, and the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. Purification by flash column chromatography afforded the title compound 3.
