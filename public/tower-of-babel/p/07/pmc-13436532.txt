Writing Big in Plant Genomes: Advances, Challenges and Strategies for Targeted Large‐Fragment DNA Insertion
Authors: Fengfeng Zhang, Daqi Yan, Jiayi Hou, Dan Yang, Yan Xiong, Mingzhang Wen, Xiaoyue Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrecise genome editing has transformed plant biology and crop improvement by enabling targeted modification of endogenous loci. Beyond gene knockout and base editing, the site‐specific insertion of exogenous DNA, particularly large DNA fragments, has become a central goal for engineering complex traits, reconstructing metabolic pathways and constructing plant artificial chromosomes. A diverse toolkit is now available for targeted DNA integration, including nuclease‐dependent strategies, serine and tyrosine recombinases, transposon‐derived systems, and CRISPR/Cas‐coupled insertion platforms. Here, we review the mechanistic principles and recent advances of these four major tools, highlighting their capacities, insertion precision and compatibility with plant systems. We compare their strengths and limitations in terms of insertion‐size capacity, integration efficiency, target site flexibility and technical complexity. Emerging innovations such as AI‐guided nuclease and recombinase design, fusion of Cas with recombinases or viral replication proteins and RNA‐guided transposition offer promising solutions to overcome these constraints. Together, these advances are rapidly expanding the landscape of targeted DNA insertion in plants and will reinforce future applications in molecular breeding, metabolic pathway engineering and the construction of synthetic genomic architectures.Keywords: CRISPR/Cas, genome editing, nucleases, prime editing, recombinases

Introduction

Climate change, increasing pest and disease pressure and rapid population growth create urgent demands for crop varieties with improved yield (Jin et al. 2024), resilience and quality. Modern crop improvement increasingly relies on the ability to introduce or modify genes and pathways in elite germplasm, thereby expanding genetic diversity and accelerating breeding (Gao 2021). For decades, plant genetic engineering has depended on Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation and particle bombardment (Kohli et al. 2003). Agrobacterium exploits the T‐DNA transfer system to deliver foreign DNA into the plant cells, but integration is random and can result in position effects, gene silencing or disruption of endogenous genes. The transformation process itself is also technically demanding, and identifying lines with ideal integration events often requires lab‐intensive screening. Particle bombardment can be applied broadly across species and tissues, but often yields low transformation efficiency, multi‐copy insertions and requires expensive equipment, limiting its scalability and routine use.

Recent advances in genome editing technologies have reshaped plant genetic engineering by enabling more precise, efficient and flexible manipulation of endogenous loci. Gene editing encompasses gene knockout, base editing, and the insertion, deletion and inversion of DNA fragments of diverse lengths (Rönspies et al. 2021). Among these, the precise integration of exogenous DNA into defined genomic sites has attracted growing attention, as it is essential for targeted gene stacking, fine‐tuning regulatory elements, reconstructing complex metabolic pathways and ultimately supporting the design of synthetic genomic structures such as plant artificial chromosomes (Yang et al. 2025). To date, multiple strategies have been developed to achieve site‐specific gene insertion in plants. This review summarises the fundamental principles and recent advances of four major platforms for targeted DNA insertion: nuclease‐based systems, recombinases, transposons and CRISPR/Cas‐based tools (Figure 1). We further discuss key limitations and outline future directions for the development of next‐generation targeted DNA insertion technologies in plant genomics.

Techniques for Large DNA Fragment Editing in Plant

Nuclease‐mediated strategies induce double‐strand breaks (DSBs) at targeted loci and rely on endogenous repair for fragment insertion. However, this process is prone to causing mutations, and due to the limitations of repair efficiency and tool design, precise editing of large fragments still faces challenges. Methods based on DSBs generally have low efficiency, insufficient accuracy and a relatively high off‐target rate. In contrast, recombinases and transposons can achieve large fragment integration without DSBs, showing significant application potential. However, recombinant enzymes are limited by sequence specificity, and the transposon insertion is random, making it difficult to precisely target. In addition, both have few available species in plants and lack programmability, which limits their application. In recent years, novel non‐breaking tools such as guided editing, programmable recombinases and transposases have developed rapidly, providing new paths for precise and efficient large fragment genetic modification (Table 1).

Nuclease‐Mediated DNA Integration

Three major classes of engineered nucleases, including zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs), TALENs and CRISPR‐associated (Cas) nucleases, have been widely used to induce targeted DNA DSBs and thereby enable site‐specific DNA insertion in plants.

ZFNs‐Mediated DNA Integration

ZFNs are chimeric proteins engineered by fusing a customisable DNA‐binding domain with a nonspecific DNA cleavage domain derived primarily from the FokI endonuclease (Carroll 2011). The DNA‐binding domain typically consists of 3–6 tandemly arranged zinc finger motifs, each recognising a 3‐base pair (bp) DNA sequence, thereby enabling precise targeting of genomic regions spanning 9–18 bp. By assembling zinc finger arrays to match specific DNA sequences, ZFNs can be designed to bind virtually any desired genomic locus. Because the FokI nuclease functions as an obligate dimer, a pair of ZFNs must be designed to bind opposite DNA strands at adjacent target sites separated by a 5–7 bp spacer (Porteus and Carroll 2005), to facilitate FokI dimerisation and subsequent cleavage (Figure 2A).

The first plant application of ZFNs was reported in tobacco, where ZFNs targeting the SuRA gene stimulated homologous directed repair (HDR) using a co‐delivered donor to correct a defective allele and insert a 600 bp fragment with ~10% efficiency (Wright et al. 2005). This demonstrated that ZFN‐triggered DSBs can elevate HR frequencies in plants, which are otherwise extremely low. Although individual ZFN‐mediated events typically insert only relatively small DNA fragments, iterative application of ZFNs enables gene stacking at defined genomic loci (M. Wang et al. 2025). Ainley et al. (2013) use ZFNs in combination with HR to replace a pre‐existing herbicide‐resistant transgene (pat) with a second herbicide‐resistant transgene, aad1, at the same genomic site of maize, achieving precise stacking of multi‐kilobase DNA fragments. Later, Bonawitz et al. (2019) successfully integrated a 16.2 kb donor DNA fragment containing four transgenes into the soybean genome through iterative use of ZFNs mediated by non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ).

Despite these successes, ZFNs have several limitations. Target recognition requires extensive protein engineering, as specific DNA sequences are addressed by modifying amino acid residues within the α‐helix of individual zinc fingers (Beerli and Barbas 2002). This process is technically demanding and time‐consuming, and context‐dependent interactions between neighbouring zinc fingers can reduce binding specificity. Moreover, achieving efficient large‐fragment insertion traditionally relies on HR, which is inherently inefficient in plants and often results in insertion frequencies below 1%. Meanwhile, DNA recognition by zinc finger modules is strongly influenced by adjacent modules, making the assembly of arrays with high target specificity and binding efficiency particularly challenging. Consequently, although zinc‐finger proteins are theoretically programmable, their design, screening and optimisation are often cumbersome, and their application is further complicated by a relatively high risk of off‐target activity.

Recent advances have started to overcome these obstacles. One strategy to enhance HR efficiency in plants involves modulating DNA repair pathways. Qi et al. (2013) achieved a targeting efficiency exceeding 1% in Arabidopsis by simultaneously downregulating NHEJ‐related genes KU70 and upregulating HR‐promoting factors RAD54, thereby synergistically shifting DSB repair from NHEL to HR. Although the application of ZFN technology in plants remains limited by challenges such as complex protein design and off‐target effects, recent advances in mammalian systems provide useful conceptual and technical directions for its future optimisation in plants. First, ZFDesign, a machine‐learning platform trained on billions of zinc finger‐DNA interactions, has been successfully applied in the human genome and can generate engineered zinc‐finger arrays targeting nearly arbitrary sequences. This approach provides a promising design framework for overcoming the design difficulties of ZFN in plants and achieving efficient targeting (Ichikawa et al. 2023). Second, the FokI nuclease domain has been further optimised in animal systems through structure‐guided engineering to reduce off‐target cleavage. For instance, Miller et al. (2019) demonstrated that single‐amino acid substitution in the FokI catalytic domain could reduce cleavage kinetics, thereby significantly reducing off‐target activity. Using this optimised architecture, ZFNs targeting the TRAC sites in human T cells achieved editing efficiency exceeding 98%, with no detectable off‐target effects (Miller et al. 2019). Although these advances have not yet been tested in plants, they highlight potentially valuable strategies for future optimisation of ZFN design, specificity and safety in plant genome engineering.

TALENs‐Mediated DNA Integration

Conceptually similar to ZFNs, TALENs use transcription activator‐like effectors (TALE) proteins for DNA recognition (Christian et al. 2010). TALENs are generated by fusing the FokI nuclease domain to a TALE‐derived DNA‐binding domain composed of multiple tandem repeats. Each repeat comprises approximately 34 amino acids and recognises a single nucleotide, with specificity dictated by the repeat variable diresidues at positions 12 and 13 (Boch et al. 2009). By arranging multiple TALE repeats in a defined sequence, precise binding to target DNA sites can be achieved. As for ZFNs, two TALEN monomers bind opposite strands at appropriately spaced target sites to promote FokI dimerisation and create site‐specific DSBs (Miller et al. 2011) (Figure 2B).

Compared to ZFNs, TALENs are more modular and predictable, as each repeat functions largely independently, reducing context effects between neighbouring modules. Golden Gate cloning technology enables rapid and standardised assembly of TALE arrays, streamlining the construction of custom TALENs (Miller et al. 2011). In Čermák et al. (2015), a 35S promoter (approximately 500 bp) was used upstream of a key regulatory gene in the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway using TALEN‐triggered HR, achieving gene targeting efficiency exceeding 66.6% and strong anthocyanin accumulation. Compared with ZFN technology, TALENs in plants are less affected by adjacent modules and therefore provide greater flexibility and programmability in target‐site selection. The design and assembly of long TALE arrays is still labour‐intensive and technically complex, particularly when multiple loci or large numbers of constructs are required.

Recombinases‐Mediated DNA Integration

Site‐Specific Recombinase (SSR)

SSRs catalyse precise DNA integration, inversion, or excision at defined target sequences without requiring external cofactors or DNA synthesis. They form covalent enzyme‐DNA intermediates and carry out DNA strand synthesis via a series of phosphotransfer reactions (Smith 2013). Based on sequence homology and catalytic mechanisms, SSRs are classified into serine recombinases and tyrosine recombinases.

Serine Recombinase

Serine recombinases are particularly attractive for the targeted integration of large DNA cargo. They catalyse DNA strand exchange through a conserved transesterification reaction that avoids the formation of DSBs, thereby proceeding independently of the host DNA repair pathways. By recognising cognate attP and attB sites, these enzymes can mediate efficient and precise integration of exogenous DNA segments up to tens of kilobases in size (Grindley et al. 2006). BXB1 and PHIC31, derived from bacteriophages, have been widely used. For example, the STRAIGHT‐IN platform uses Bxb1 to integrate > 50 kb cargo into human pluripotent stem cells and then removes auxiliary sequences with Cre to produce clean and stable lines (Blanch‐Asensio et al. 2026).

In plants, Kpo3 represents a breakthrough large serine recombinase (LSR) (Yan et al. 2025). In rice (Oryza sativa) and Arabidopsis thaliana protoplasts, Kp03 integrate donors up to 27.3 kb, with efficiencies approaching 99% for 3.4 kb donors. Kp03 also outperformed commonly used recombinases such as FLP. To date, Kp03 is the only reported recombinase capable of integrating plant DNA fragments exceeding 27 kb, greatly expanding the serine recombinase toolbox for plant genome engineering (Figure 3).

Stepwise strategies can further extend effective cargo size. BXB1 has been used in a two‐stage scheme in which attB landing pads are first introduced by Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation, followed by repeated rounds of BXB1‐mediated integration of additional genes at the same locus (Li, Han, et al. 2022). The GAANTRY system assembles up to 10 DNA fragments (total 28.6 kb) in Agrobacterium using multiple recombinases (A118, TP901‐1, ParA), and then delivers the entire cassette as a single T‐DNA, achieving high‐efficiency transformation of multi‐gene traits in maize (Collier et al. 2018).

A key limitation of current SSR applications is their reliance on pre‐installed landing pads, which restricts targeting flexibility. Recent work from Hsu and colleagues (Fanton et al. 2025) addressed this by identifying LSRs that recognise endogenous ‘pseudosites’ in the human genome, enabling one‐step targeted integration without engineered landing pads. By combining high‐throughput screening, machine‐learning‐assisted design, dCas9 fusions and donor optimisation, they achieved 41%–53% integration efficiency at the attH1 locus with ~97% genome‐wide specificity and successful insertion of 12 kb cargos. These advances provide a conceptual framework for adapting landing pad–free LSR systems to plants.

Previous efforts for developing SSR tools have largely focused on strategies such as directed evolution and domain swapping (Sclimenti 2001), but such strategies have been applied to only a limited number of recombinases and are experimentally demanding. Systematic discovery and AI‐assisted evolution are beginning to overcome these limitations. Bioinformatics mining of microbial genomes has increased the known diversity of LSRs by ~100‐fold and identified variants that outperform Bxb1 in human cells, enabling integration of > 7 kb fragments without iterative steps (Durrant et al. 2023). AI‐based tools such as EVOLVEpro perform in silico directed evolution to enhance recombinase activity; evolved Bxb1 variants show up to fourfold higher integration efficiency (Jiang et al. 2025). These approaches provide powerful routes to develop high‐activity plant‐adapted SSRs.

Tyrosine Recombinase

Tyrosine recombinases such as Cre and FLP mediate recombination between loxP and FRT sites, respectively. Integration efficiency can be enhanced by optimising both recombinase expression and transformation conditions. For example, Anand et al. (2019) used a pollen‐specific promoter to drive FLP expression together with Wus2 driven by a maize egg cell‐specific promoter to enhance in planta transformation competence. This strategy increased targeted insertion efficiency of exogenous DNA to approximately 7% (Anand et al. 2019).

Similar to LSR, machine‐learning guided engineering has also been applied to tyrosine recombinases. Frank et al. (Schmitt et al. 2022). developed ReGen, a generative model based on a conditional variational autoencoder trained on over one million Cre‐like recombinase sequences from 89 distinct target sequences. ReGen can predict recombinase variants tailored to new DNA motifs and was experimentally validated for its catalytic activity. This innovative strategy significantly accelerates the development of customised recombinases for diverse genomic loci, demonstrating strong potential to overcome sequence‐specificity constraints.

Another strategy to expand targeting range is to fuse recombinases with programmable DNA‐binding domains, such as zinc fingers, TALEs, or dCas9. However, in many designs, the catalytic activity of the recombinase domain is only partially coupled to the external DNA‐binding module, leading to off‐target activity at native recognition sites. Recently, an optimised zinc finger–Cre fusion protein was developed by systematically tuning the spacing between zinc finger motifs and recombinase recognition sites, as well as the linker length between the domains, achieving up to 10‐fold enhancement of on‐target recombination with reduced off‐target activity (Mukhametzyanova et al. 2024).

Transposons‐Mediated DNA Integration Technology

Transposons (TEs) are mobile genetic elements,]historically termed ‘jumping genes’ that can move within genomes and mediate the targeted integration of DNA into host chromosomes via transposase activity, independently of HR (Tansirichaiya et al. 2019). Based on their transposition mechanisms, TEs are classified into Class I (retrotransposons) and Class II (DNA transposons) (Lisch 2013). Both classes are being used as tools for precise insertion of large DNA fragments, particularly when coupled with genome editing platforms.

DNA Transposons Mediated Large Fragment DNA Integration

DNA transposons move via a ‘cut‐and‐paste’ mechanism. Transposase catalyses excision of the elements from their original genomic location, followed by integration at a new site (Lisch 2013). Diverse transposon systems facilitate the precise directional insertion of large DNA fragments in eukaryotes. These systems can currently be classified into two broad categories. The first comprises CAST (CRISPR‐associated transposase) systems, which originate from prokaryotes and in which CRISPR‐based target recognition is naturally coupled to the transposition module, enabling RNA‐guided DNA integration.

In 2019, independent studies by the laboratories of Feng Zhang and Samuel H. Sternberg showed that ShCAST from cyanobacteria and Tn7‐like systems utilising Cascade complexes derived from Vibrio cholerae can mediate programmable gene insertion without generating DSBs (Strecker et al. 2019). In the same year, Sternberg and colleagues (Klompe et al. 2019) further engineered Tn7‐like CAST systems to achieve efficient, full‐length integration of DNA cargos up to 10 kb in bacteria. In addition, engineered CAST systems, including eCAST‐3 and evoCAST, have successfully achieved insertions of up to 15 kb in human cells (Lampe et al. 2024; Witte et al. 2025). These studies demonstrated that bacterial RNA‐guided transposition systems can be adapted across species barriers and may offer a promising framework for future development of DSB‐free site‐specific integration of large DNA fragments in plants.

The second category comprises artificially engineered systems, which primarily focus on engineering and modifying classical eukaryotic transposases to impart site‐specific recognition capabilities. This approach is then coupled with the targeted module of CRISPR. Currently, this strategy has been applied in both animal and plant cells. Kovač et al. (2020) reported the first successful fusion of the Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposase with dCas9 in human cells. Engineered synthetic single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) guided the SB–dCas9 complex to selected target regions, enabling biased transposition and improved specificity relative to random SB insertion. P. Liu, Panda, et al. (2024) developed Transposase‐Assisted Target‐Site Integration (TATSI), a targeted transposition system that combines the Pong DNA transposon from rice with programmable nucleases Cas9 or Cas12a, together with co‐expression of the helper protein ORF1. TATSI exploits CRISPR/Cas for precise genomic targeting while leveraging the high‐efficiency integration capacity of the Pong transposase. In Arabidopsis thaliana, TATSI enabled precise insertion of an 8.6 kb DNA fragment, and in soybean, it achieved a targeted integration efficiency of up to 18.2%. As an emerging genome editing tool, CAST‐ and TATSI‐based systems provide powerful platforms for large‐fragment insertion in plants and show strong potential for crop engineering (Figure 4A). Although the TATSI system can achieve targeted insertion, the intrinsic DNA‐binding preferences of the transposases are difficult to eliminate completely, resulting in substantial off‐target activity even after retargeting. Currently, a key strategy for optimising this system is to engineer transposases with reduced affinity for their native preferred DNA sequences, thereby enabling them to primarily depend on dCas9 guidance for targeted recognition.

The transposase‐Cas9 fusion system is also fundamentally constrained by the catalytic requirements of the transposase, which must recognise the reverse repeat sequences located at both ends of the donor DNA. As a result, transposon‐derived terminal sequences remain permanently flanking the integration fragment, precluding true traceless editing.

CRISPR/Cas‐Mediated DNA Integration

CRISPR/Cas systems have become the dominant platform for plant genome editing due to their simplicity, versatility, high efficiency and editable loci. In this section, we summarise CRISPR/Cas‐based methods for targeted DNA insertion, focusing on Cas9, Cas12a and prime editors.

CRISPR/Cas9‐Mediated DNA Integration Technology

The CRISPR‐Cas9 system relies on an sgRNA that base‐pairs with the target DNA and forms a ribonucleoprotein complex (RNP) with Cas9. Target recognition requires a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) in the target DNA (Anders et al. 2014). Upon binding, Cas9 uses its HNH domain to cleave the DNA strand complementary to the sgRNA and its RuvC domain to cleave the non‐complementary strand, resulting in blunt‐ended DSB. This blunt‐ended lesion is subsequently subjected to nucleolytic processing, during which 5′ strands are resected to generate long 3′ overhangs when the break is channelled toward the HDR pathway. The resulting DNA end structures are thus distinct for different repair outcomes: relatively unprocessed ends favour NHEJ, whereas resected ends with 3′ overhangs are essential for (Nishimasu et al. 2014). The choice between these pathways is tightly coupled to the cell cycle (Figure 2C).

NHEJ is a DNA DSB repair mechanism active throughout the cell cycle, with peak activity during the G1 phase. It directly ligates broken DNA ends without requiring a homologous template, making it the predominant DNA repair pathway in higher eukaryotes (Chaplin et al. 2021). However, in plant systems, NHEJ‐mediated repair often leads to random small insertions or deletions at the break site, complicating its application for achieving predictable and targeted DNA insertion. To address this limitation, P. Xu et al. (2025) developed a CRISPR/Cas9‐based strategy for targeted T‐DNA integration in Arabidopsis. The efficient targeted insertion requires two concurrent events: (i) a DSB at a defined genomic site and (ii) transient availability of unintegrated T‐DNA in the same cell. To coordinate these events, a two‐step transformation strategy was established. First, plants stably expressing Cas9 under an egg cell‐specific promoter were generated. Second, a T‐DNA vector encoding both the target‐specific sgRNA and the donor cassette was introduced into these lines. In egg cells, newly transcribed sgRNA rapidly associates with pre‐accumulated Cas9 to form RNPs that induce DSBs at the target locus, while the T‐DNA from the same vector remains transient and integration‐competent. This spatiotemporal coupling greatly increases the probability that NHEJ captures the donor T‐DNA at the Cas9‐induced break. Compared with conventional one‐step transformation, this sequential approach increased targeted integration efficiencies to ~30% in T1 plants and enabled insertion of multi‐kilobase fragments. This study markedly enhanced the targeted integration efficiency of T‐DNA in Arabidopsis thaliana through a CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated approach. However, free T‐DNA may still give rise to non‐targeted insertion events through random integration. In addition, because T‐DNA border sequences and selectable marker elements are co‐integrated with the target cargo, this strategy does not enable fully traceless editing.

HDR enables precise knock‐in of defined sequences (Li et al. 2020). In HDR‐mediated insertion, exogenously supplied DNA repair templates that carry homology arms flanking the desired target genomic region are used to repair Cas9‐induced DSBs (Li, Yang, et al. 2025). At the molecular level, HDR primarily relies on the canonical HR pathway (J. Li et al. 2019; J. F. Li et al. 2013). However, the low intrinsic HDR activity in somatic plant cells remains a major barrier. Current efforts to improve HDR focus on engineering Cas9. One major approach is to modify the DSB ends generated by Cas9. Schreiber et al. (2024) showed that efficient HDR in plants requires long 3′ ssDNA overhangs. Because wild‐type Cas9 typically produces blunt or short‐overhang DSBs, they fused Cas9 to 5′→3′ exonucleases from herpes simplex virus type 1 or bacteriophage T7, which changed DSB ends to extended 3′ overhangs. In transient tobacco assays, these Cas9–exonuclease fusions increased HDR efficiency by up to 38‐fold and enabled precise insertion of multi‐kb fragments.

A second strategy is to tether donor DNA to Cas9. Xie and colleagues (Zhou et al. 2025) developed a Rep‐Based Knock‐In (RBKI) system by fusing Cas9 to the replication protein (Rep) from wheat dwarf virus (WDV). Under natural conditions, WDV Rep covalently attaches to viral DNA and drives rolling‐circle replication. In RBKI, Cas9–Rep functions as a molecular bridge that simultaneously binds the donor DNA and the genomic DSB site, enriching donor molecules in the vicinity of the break and substantially boosting HDR‐mediated insertion in rice.

Optimisation of donor design can also significantly improve NHEJ‐mediated targeted integration. Lu et al. (2020) used thiomodified donor DNA to enhance donor stability and prevent exonuclease‐mediated degradation, achieving ~25% efficiency for 2 kb insertions. Kumar et al. (2023) developed DOTI, which exploits NHEJ by utilising 1‐nt 5′overhangs for directional integration.

CRISPR/Cas12a‐Mediated DNA Integration

Cas12a (Cpf1) and Cas9 are both Class 2 CRISPR effectors, but Cas12a exhibits several features that are particularly advantageous for precise DNA insertion. Following DNA cleavage, Cas12a is reported to exhibit asymmetric binding behaviour, remaining associated with the proximal PAM fragment while releasing the distal PAM fragment. This asymmetry may create distinct DNA end states at the two sides of the break and may influence the selection of repair pathways. The distal end of the PAM can rapidly engage in the NHEJ repair machinery, whereas the proximal end, occupied by Cas12a, must first be processed to allow access to the NHEJ machinery. Blocked (Cas12a‐bound) DNA ends are substrates for the MRN complex, nicking one of the strands leaving DNA‐ends with 3′ overhangs of intermediate length (the sequence occupied by Cas12a) a suitable terminal structure for participation in HDR. This regulated end‐of‐pipe process may create a temporal window that prioritises the activation of the HDR pathway. Moreover, Cas12a autonomously processes precursor crRNA into mature 42–44 nt guide RNAs, whereas Cas9 requires a separate tracrRNA, making Cas12a well‐suited for multiplex editing and base editing (Safari et al. 2019). Cas12a often shows higher knock‐in efficiencies and reduced off‐target activity compared with Cas9 in plants (Lee et al. 2019).

Kim and colleagues (Vu et al. 2020) exploited these properties by combining LbCpf1 (LbCas12a) with a geminiviral replicon to create a high‐efficiency HR platform in tomato. They constructed a Bean yellow dwarf virus–based replicon (LIR–SIR–Rep/RepA–LIR) to amplify donor DNA copy number and paired it with LbCpf1‐induced sticky‐end DSBs. Using this system, a 1146 bp mutated HKT1;2 allele carrying the A43T salt‐tolerance mutation was inserted into the tomato genome with an HR efficiency of 10.6%. This work demonstrates that coupling geminiviral replicons with Cas12a can markedly enhance donor availability and HDR‐mediated insertion, providing an effective strategy for precise crop improvement.

Prime Editor‐Based DNA Integration

Prime editing (PE) is a DSB‐free genome editing strategy that combines a programmable nickase with a reverse transcriptase and an extended guide RNA. It offers high versatility for precise base substitutions and small insertions/deletions, and is now being adapted for larger DNA insertions.

Small‐Fragment DNA Insertion Mediated by Prime Editors

Prime editing was first reported by Liu and colleagues in 2019 (Anzalone et al. 2019). The system comprises an SpCas9 nickase fused to a reverse transcriptase (RT) and a prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA). Unlike a conventional sgRNA, the pegRNA carries a 3′ extension containing a primer binding site (PBS) and a reverse transcription template (RTT) encoding the desired edit. The PE mechanism proceeds in three main steps (Y. Liu et al. 2020). First, the nCas9–RT–pegRNA complex binds the target site and forms an R‐loop. nCas9 introduces a nick on the non‐complementary DNA strand three nucleotides upstream of the PAM, exposing a 3′ hydroxyl. Then, the exposed 3′ end hybridises with the PBS, initiating reverse transcription from the RTT and generating an extended 3′ flap containing the edited sequence. The edited 3′ flap and the unedited 5′ flap exist in a dynamic equilibrium. Endogenous, structurally specific endonucleases, such as FEN1, preferentially cleave the 5′ flap, and DNA ligases subsequently seal the resulting incisions, thereby incorporating the editing sequence into a single strand and generating heterologous double‐stranded DNA. At this stage, the permanent incorporation of the final edit is contingent upon the repair mechanism of the complementary strand. In the PE2 system, this process depends on endogenous mismatch repair within the cell; however, mismatch repair occasionally utilises wild‐type strands as templates to rectify the edited strand, leading to a reduction in editing efficiency. To further enhance editing efficiency, the PE3 strategy employs a second sgRNA to induce additional incisions on the unedited complementary strand, directing cells to utilise the edited strand as the repair template, thereby facilitating efficient and homozygous gene editing.

In principle, PE can perform all 12 possible base transitions and transversions, as well as precise small insertions and deletions. However, early implementations in plants achieved only modest efficiencies and were limited to short insert sizes. To overcome this, multiple strategies have been developed (Table 2). For example, Twin prime editing (TwinPE) uses two pegRNAs targeting opposite strands to generate complementary 3′ ends that recombine to install replacements or insertions. TwinPE can, in principle, install larger edits but is limited to ~100 bp insertions in mammalian cells (Anzalone et al. 2022). In plants, GRAND editing has achieved genomic insertions up to 135 bp (R. Xu et al. 2024). F. Li, Hou, et al. (2025) optimised Template‐Jumping Prime Editing (TJ‐PE) in rice by combining a Csy4‐based pre‐pegRNA processing system with restoration of the RNase H domain in the plant prime editor ePPEplus. This study provided the first experimental demonstration of TJ‐PE in plants, enabling targeted insertions up to 710 bp and precise edits of 202–403 bp, substantially extending the upper limit of PE‐mediated insertion in crops (Figure 4B). Nevertheless, its broader applicability across diverse plant species and genomic contexts remains to be established.

Optimisation strategies of the PE system in plants.

A second major focus has been to improve pegRNA stability and activity. Incorporating structured RNA motifs such as evopreQ. 1 at the 3′ end of pegRNAs (epegRNAs) protects them from exonuclease degradation, increasing PE efficiency (Nelson et al. 2022). Long pegRNAs can also be generated by ligating two shorter RNA fragments using splint DNA and T4 RNA ligase. Systematic optimisation of ligation conditions improved 200 bp insertion efficiencies by two‐fold in HEK293T cells (Lei et al. 2025). Wolfe's group (Ponnienselvan et al. 2023) further showed that self‐complementarity between the PBS and spacer regions within pegRNA can lead to auto‐inhibition; reducing this complementarity significantly enhances editing across many targets. Machine‐learning tools such as DeepPE, Easy‐Prime, PRIDICT and OPED now assist in pegRNA design and efficiency prediction (F. Liu et al. 2023).

In parallel, protein‐level engineering has yielded more active prime editors. In the enhanced prime editor (ePPE), deletion of the RNase H domain of Moloney murine leukaemia virus RT and fusion of a viral nucleocapsid protein improved activity (Y. Zong et al. 2022). Plant prime editors (PPEs) have been built using RTs from cauliflower mosaic virus or E. coli BL21 retroelements (Lin et al. 2020). Split‐PE architectures divide the PE fusion into two inactive halves, for example, split nSpCas9 and split RT, that can be reconstituted in a spatiotemporally controlled manner (Grünewald et al. 2023). Finally, phage‐assisted continuous evolution (PACE) has generated RT variants (including Tf147 and others) with improved catalytic properties, further boosting prime editing efficiency (Doman et al. 2023).

Notably, the recently developed PE6c variant has now been functionally validated in both monocot and dicot plants. PE6c incorporates an engineered RT derived from the Tf1 retrotransposon, which exhibits enhanced stability and increased tolerance to structured RNA templates. Compared with earlier prime editing architectures such as PEmax, PE6c achieves substantially higher editing efficiency, particularly for targeted small‐fragment DNA insertions and precise genome modifications (Van Vu et al. 2026). This performance advantage has been further confirmed in recent plant studies.

Collectively, these advances are transforming PE into a robust tool for precise small‐to‐medium DNA insertions in plants, although kb‐scale integration still requires additional strategies.

Prime Editor‐Mediated Large DNA Fragment Insertion

Prime editors alone are currently limited to insertions of several hundred bps and cannot directly mediate kb‐scale integration. In contrast, SSRs readily integrate large DNA segments but require pre‐installed recognition sites, which restricts genomic flexibility. A logical solution is to combine prime editors with recombinases, using PEs to install recombinase sites and recombinases to integrate large DNA cargos.

In 2022, Liu's group introduced Prime Editing‐Assisted Site‐Specific Integration of Genes (PASSIGE), which uses PE to insert a Bxb1 attB site into the genome and then employs Bxb1 recombinase to integrate donor DNA containing the matching attP site (Anzalone et al. 2022). PASSIGE enabled insertion of nearly 5 kb fragments without DSBs, with efficiencies up to ~6.8%. In 2024, the same group further evolved Bxb1 using PACE, generating recombinase variants that support integration of 10 kb fragments at efficiencies up to 35% (Pandey et al. 2025). Concurrently, Gootenberg and colleagues developed PASTE (Programmable Addition via Site‐specific Targeting Elements), which fuses nuclease‐dead Cas9 (nCas9) with RT and Bxb1 recombinase (Yarnall et al. 2023). In PASTE, nCas9–RT first uses a pegRNA to write an attB site at the target locus; Bxb1 then catalyses integration of an attP‐flanked donor cassette. PASTE can insert DNA cargos up to 36 kb in mammalian cells without generating DSBs. Analogous concepts are also beginning to be implemented in plants. PrimeRoot (Sun et al. 2024), a two‐step platform was established which integrates prime editors with SSRs for large‐fragment insertion up to 11 kb in rice(Figure 4C). However, following PrimeRoot‐mediated integration, recombinase recognition sites remain on both sides of the inserted fragment, preventing truly traceless editing. To overcome this limitation, Gao's group developed the RePCE strategy (Sun et al. 2025). After the insertion of large fragments mediated by Cre recombinase, a specially designed Re‐pegRNA guided editor is employed to precisely restore the residual lox sites to the original genomic sequence, thereby enabling near‐complete traceless editing.

Large‐Fragment DNA Editing Technologies in Non‐Plant Systems

Beyond classical SSRs, several emerging ‘recombinase‐inspired’ systems offer promising routes to DSB‐free large‐fragment integration. A cleavage‐free dCas9–single‐strand annealing protein (SSAP) platform fuses nicking dCas9 with SSAPs to promote recombination between donor DNA and the genome at nicked sites, enabling precise multi‐kb knock‐in in human cells without DSBs (C. Wang et al. 2022). In 2024, Patrick Hsu et al. developed a novel bridging RNA tool (IS621) that is guided by RNA and enables sequence‐specific recombination between two DNA molecules (Durrant et al. 2024). This bridging RNA contains two customisable loop structures: one designed to bind the donor DNA and the other to target the genomic locus. Both loops are readily programmable, enabling precise manipulation of arbitrary DNA sequences. Utilising this system, the researchers achieved high‐efficiency integration of a 4.85 kb plasmid into the Escherichia coli genome, with 94% specificity and 60% integration efficiency. The ISCro4 bridging recombinase system, newly developed by the team, facilitates the separation and expression of functional modules by dividing the bridging RNA into two independent RNA molecules, which carry the target sequence and the donor sequence, respectively. At the AAVS1 safe harbour site in mammalian cells, ISCro4 successfully inserted an exogenous fragment of 3.5 kb, achieving an editing efficiency of approximately 6%. This development signifies the official transition of RNA‐guided bridging recombinase systems into the practical application phase for mammalian genomes. This strategy circumvents the error‐prone DNA terminal connection repair pathway, enabling precise and scarless integration (Pelea et al. 2026). Recent advancements in recombinant enzyme systems have introduced novel approaches for editing large genomic fragments in plants without inducing breaks. While there are currently no reports detailing the application of these systems in plant biology, their modular RNA design and site‐specific integration capabilities suggest significant potential for applications in multi‐gene stacking and site‐specific transgenics. Future efforts focused on optimising components and enhancing delivery systems may establish these tools as pivotal resources in the field of plant synthetic biology.

Different from DNA transposons, retrotransposons transpose through a ‘copy‐and‐paste’ mechanism involving an RNA intermediate and reverse transcription. The R2 retrotransposon is a well‐characterised example that specifically targets the 28S rDNA locus and integrates through target‐primed reverse transcription. These properties render R2 an attractive scaffold for programmable insertion tools. In 2023, Wilkinson et al. used Cas9 and sgRNAs to retarget the R2 retrotransposon in vitro toward synthetic non‐28S DNA sequences, providing proof‐of‐concept that R2‐based elements can be reprogrammed for site‐specific gene insertion (Wilkinson et al. 2023). Subsequent work has extended this concept to mammalian genomes. In 2025, Zhang et al. developed PRINT (Programmable Retrotransposon Insertion Technology) based on an R2 element from the sparrow genome (Zhang et al. 2025). PRINT enables precise, site‐specific insertion of large DNA fragments into the human 28S rRNA locus without compromising ribosomal function. Chen et al. engineered R2Tg, a tool derived from the zebra finch (Taeniopygia guttata) R2 element (Chen et al. 2024). Through rational design and optimisation, R2Tg achieved efficient and stable integration of large DNA constructs into the 28S rDNA region across multiple mammalian cell types, with integration efficiencies exceeding 60%. The 28S rDNA site targeted by the R2 transposon represents a naturally occurring genomic safe‐harbour locus that is highly conserved across species, thereby enabling cross‐species application through adaptation to homologous sites in other organisms. Although gene integration mediated by the R2 transposon exhibits a high degree of site specificity, the resulting products frequently show 5′ truncation, formation of 3′ polyA tail and the presence of micro‐homologous sequences. Consequently, this process cannot be considered traceless editing at the molecular level.

These R2‐derived platforms demonstrate that RT–mediated mechanisms can support high‐efficiency, large‐fragment DNA integration in complex eukaryotic genomes. Adapting such retrotransposon‐based approaches to plants could provide a complementary strategy for targeted, high‐efficiency insertion of large DNA constructs, particularly at repetitive or ‘safe harbour’ loci, and would further expand the toolkit for plant genome engineering.

With the development of technology, novel recombinant enzyme systems and reverse transcription transposons have successfully achieved a species leap from prokaryotes to eukaryotes. There is reason to believe that these emerging technologies are expected to be further extended to the plant field, opening up broad prospects for the application of large DNA fragment insertion.

Prospects

A rapidly expanding toolbox now supports targeted DNA insertion in plants, ranging from nuclease‐triggered NHEJ and HDR to recombinase‐, transposon‐, retrotransposon‐ and prime editor–based systems. These advances have greatly broadened the options for crop improvement, gene stacking and synthetic pathway construction. However, major bottlenecks remain: nuclease‐based approaches still suffer from off‐target effects and low HDR efficiency in somatic tissues; recombinase and transposase systems often depend on engineered landing pads or rare endogenous pseudosites; and integration efficiency declines sharply as cargo size increases, in part because very large DNA molecules are difficult to deliver across the cell wall and into the nucleus. In many crops, transformation and regeneration remain genotype dependent, further limiting deployment in elite lines.

Emerging technologies point to practical solutions. AI‐guided protein design and in silico evolution are accelerating the discovery and optimisation of recombinases, transposases and Cas variants with higher activity, broader target scope and improved specificity, while natural diversity mining has yielded thousands of LSRs with distinct sequence preferences that can be adapted to plants. Fusion strategies that couple Cas proteins with recombinases, RTs, viral replication proteins or DNA‐tethering domains are enabling more efficient, programmable and often DSB‐sparing insertion of large cargos. For plant systems, it will be particularly important to further optimise plant‐adapted LSRs such as Kp03, to combine them with CRISPR for one‐step, landing‐pad‐free insertion of very large fragments, and to refine hybrid prime editor–recombinase platforms such as PrimeRoot for precise multi‐kb integration. Continued advances in delivery technologies, including geminiviral replicons, RNA or RNP delivery, nanocarriers and genotype‐flexible transformation methods, will be essential to make these tools routine across diverse crops.

Targeted DNA insertion is central not only to molecular breeding but also to building plant artificial chromosomes and other synthetic genomic scaffolds for complex trait engineering. Recent work by Rönspies et al. used CRISPR–Cas9 and Cas12a to induce DSBs at subcentromeric and subtelomeric sites in Arabidopsis, fusing entire chromosome arms and reducing the karyotype from 10 to 8 chromosomes (Rönspies et al. 2025). The resulting fusion lines were viable, phenotypically indistinguishable from wild type, and showed only minimal changes in global gene expression, underscoring the remarkable robustness of plant genomes to large‐scale karyotype restructuring. However, when crossed with wild‐type plants, hybrids displayed markedly reduced fertility and strongly altered crossover landscapes along the fused arms, effectively redefining linkage groups and creating partial reproductive barriers. This study illustrates how CRISPR‐based chromosome engineering can be used not only to probe genome evolution and speciation, but also to design breeding strategies that reshape recombination and support genetic containment of engineered traits in crops. As computational design, high‐throughput screening, synthetic genomics and improved delivery converge, targeted large‐fragment insertion is likely to become a standard operation in crop biotechnology, enabling climate‐resilient, resource‐efficient crops and designer plant genomes tailored to future agricultural and industrial needs.
