High-grade spindle cell metaplastic carcinoma of the breast in a 15-year-old girl
Authors: Gabi Barajas, Cathy Chen, Michelle Basha, David Haider, Hua Guo, Amanda Treece, Stefanie Woodard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetaplastic breast cancer in pediatric patients is exceedingly rare with only occasional case reports to present imaging and guide management. Typically diagnosed in patients over 50 years old, metaplastic carcinoma is an aggressive type of breast cancer representing less than 1% of all breast cancers in the United States. To our knowledge, we describe the youngest person to be diagnosed with metaplastic breast cancer in the US. This case underscores the need for clinical vigilance in evaluating rapidly growing breast masses in adolescents. It also emphasizes the value of histopathologic confirmation, even when imaging findings suggest a benign process, to ensure timely diagnosis and management.Keywords: Metaplastic, Spindle cell, Pediatric, Breast

Introduction

Metaplastic breast carcinoma (MPBC) is a heterogeneous group of invasive breast cancers characterized by squamous differentiation and/or mesenchymal-looking elements, including but not restricted to spindle, chondroid, and osseous cells, with different outcomes. MPBCs have been reported to represent 0.2%-1% of all breast cancers [1]. MPBCs are rare and the majority of them are aggressive and have a triple negative phenotype in most cases [[2], [3], [4]]. Rarely, HER2 or hormone positive cases can be seen [5]. Metaplastic changes, such as spindle cell or squamous, occur rapidly, and patients often present with fast-growing, palpable masses [6,7]. At presentation, MPBCs are usually painless and larger than breast carcinomas without metaplastic changes [7,8].

Black women are disproportionately affected by MPBC and often present at advanced stage, leading to poorer outcomes. Genetic links have been found, such as the BRCA, the PIK3, the AKT, the MTor and the WNT pathways. While the exact etiology of the epithelial to mesenchymal transformation is not completely understood, it is also thought that factors like chronic inflammation and hormone exposure may also contribute to the development of MPBC [4].

MPBCs of the breast are not typically seen in the pediatric population [2,[7], [8], [9]]. Only isolated case reports are available with 1 case describing a 15-year-old patient from Pakistan [10] and a case of a 27-year-old female [9] in the UK. In this report, we describe a case of MPBC presenting as a rapidly growing mass in a 15-year-old female. We will discuss imaging findings and treatment, as well as diagnostic pitfalls and best practices.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old patient with no elevated risk factors for breast cancer presented to her pediatrician with a painless lump in her right breast. This lump was initially palpated by the patient 3 months prior to presentation. The patient denied skin changes, nipple discharge, and did not recall trauma to the breast. On physical exam, there was no warmth, redness, drainage or palpable axillary lymphadenopathy.

Targeted ultrasound initially performed at an outside institution 1 month prior demonstrated an oval circumscribed hypoechoic mass with internal vascularity at 9 o’clock 5 centimeters from nipple measuring 23 mm. Repeat ultrasound at our institution found interval growth with the mass measuring 49×32×45 mm (Fig. 1).

Prior to surgical excision and treatment, metastatic work-up was performed with computed tomography (CT), which demonstrated a subcentimeter left upper lobe noncalcified nodule. Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (FDG PET-CT) demonstrated a hypermetabolic breast mass consistent with known malignancy did not demonstrate distant metastatic disease (Fig. 3).

Axial slice from whole-body PET-CT scan obtained 90 minutes after radiotracer injection (9.03 mCi FDG f-18 20 mCi to 300 mCi/ml intravenous solution) revealed high FDG uptake in the right breast mass with a standardized uptake value of 8.4. Central absence of radiotracer uptake can be noted, indicative of necrosis.

Ultrasound-guided core needle biopsy was performed. Pathology demonstrated a malignant spindle cell neoplasm, favoring metaplastic carcinoma. There was increased cellularity, marked cytology atypia, increased mitotic activity (>20/10HPF), and with stromal myxoid change (Fig. 4). Immunohistochemical stains were performed and showed scattered positive findings for TRPS1, patchy diffuse strong positive findings for multiple cytokeratins (pancytokeratin, HMWCK, CK CAM5.2, CK5/6) and p63, with patchy Desmin and myoDA1 positivity; and negative for SMA, CD34, S100, SOX10 and Myogenin (Fig. 5). The tumor was also positive for ERG (scattered) and negative for CD31. INI1 nuclear staining was retained. Subsequent biopsy of a right axillary lymph node did not demonstrate metastatic disease. Lumpectomy was performed, and the gross specimen showed a well-circumscribed tan-white edematous mass, measuring 7.5×6.8×4.8 cm (Fig. 6). The tumor was extensively sampled, and only focal entrapped small breast ductules were found without leaflike structures. Histology confirmed a high-grade spindle cell tumor, most consistent with metaplastic carcinoma with a similar immunoprofile to the biopsy. The anterior and superior margins were focally positive. The patient is currently receiving adjuvant chemotherapy and planning for definitive surgical management with sentinel lymph node biopsy upon completion of systemic therapy.

Discussion

Spindle cell MPBC typically presents in women over 50 years old. Diagnosis in younger patients remains difficult given the common similar-appearing benign mimickers, such as fibroadenomas, phyllodes tumors and pseudoangiomatous stromal hyperplasia (PASH). Imaging features of MPBC on ultrasound and MRI can significantly overlap with both benign and malignant lesions. Pathologic distinction between MPBC and malignant phyllodes tumor or primary or secondary sarcoma is challenging in biopsy sample [6,8]. For this reason, the diagnosis cannot be made without extensive sampling by pathology with immunohistochemical stains.

Ultrasound of MPBC typically shows an oval or irregular hypoechoic mass with heterogeneous internal echotexture. The mass can also be complex cystic and solid due to hemorrhage [6,11]. On MRI, MPBC has been described as a solid mass with irregular margins and spiculations, heterogenous T1 iso- or hypointense signal, and T2 hyper-or iso-hyper-intense signal also indicative of necrosis [8,12]. PET-CT is important to show the extent of disease [13]. MPBC has high FDG uptake, showing greater uptake than IDC in some studies [14].

While imaging is important to establish a differential diagnosis, features of MPBC are typically not distinctive enough for definitive diagnosis. An image-guided biopsy is critical, and histopathological examination is needed to differentiate spindle cell carcinoma from similar lesions, such as fibroadenoma or phyllodes tumor [11]. A diagnosis of metaplastic spindle cell carcinoma can be rendered based on the presence of any evidence of epithelial differentiation by histopathological and/or immunohistochemical evaluation. The majority express p63 and keratins, including AE1/AE3, 34bE12, CAM5.2, CK5/6 and CK14. The vast majority (>90%) lack expression of ER, PR, and HER2/neu [11] (WHO Classification of Tumors Editorial Board Editor, WHO Classification of Tumours, 5th ed., International Agency for Research on Cancer, Lyon, 2019, pp. 134-138). Gross inspection of MPBC typically shows a solid white-gray, nodular mass [15] similar to our case.

Differentiating between MPBC, phyllodes tumor and conventional IDC is clinically important because surgical approaches, chemotherapy regimens and metastatic evaluation differ between breast pathologies [2,6]. Radiation is also common in the treatment of MPBC of the breast but minimizing exposure in pediatric patients is important. In cases like our patient with high grade spindle cell triple negative MPBC, hormonal and HER2 targeted therapies are not effective. The limited interventions available further highlight the need for a swift diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rare and challenging diagnosis of MPBC in a 15-year-old patient, an age group where this pathology is a rarity. Early recognition and intervention are crucial. The treatment plan involves a multidisciplinary approach with surgery and chemotherapy, taking into consideration the potential long-term implications of radiation.
