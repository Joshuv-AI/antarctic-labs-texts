Single‐Cell RNA‐Seq Reveals the Effect of Commensal Bacteria on the Multistep Development of Esophageal Cancer
Authors: Xuan Xie, Shuyue Zhang, Zihang Mai, Jing Zhan, Sheng Lei, Zhiqiang Ouyang, Zelin Weng, Xiuying Xie, Jingjing Han, Jing Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicrobiomes within the tumor microenvironment (TME) are intricately linked to the modulation of cancer cell properties and the prognoses of various types of cancer, including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). However, their contribution to the onset and advancement of ESCC is still not fully elucidated. We adopted single‐cell RNA sequencing to investigate the effects of esophageal commensal bacteria during the development of mice's spontaneous ESCCs. By analyzing the single‐cell transcriptomes of 54,562 cells across different ESCC pathological stages, commensal bacteria were observed to display a cellular heterogeneous distribution in the ESCC TME. These bacteria were associated with the malignant transformation of epithelial cells and the formation of inflammatory fibroblasts, contributing to a chronic inflammatory microenvironment. However, after depriving commensal bacteria with an antibiotic cocktail (ABX), infiltrations of inflammatory fibroblasts and anti‐bacteria macrophages were lower than those in the control H2O group, while the infiltration of regulatory Cd4 + T (Treg) cells was higher. Interactions between inflammatory fibroblasts and Treg cells were intensified in the ABX group, which exacerbated the immunosuppressive state in the TME and developed more invasive carcinomas. This study demonstrates the significant role of commensal bacteria in ESCC tumorigenesis and sheds light on the potential clinical applications of adjusting commensal bacteria for the prevention and treatment of ESCC.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer ranks seventh among the most frequently diagnosed cancers and sixth in terms of cancer‐related mortality globally. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) constitutes roughly 90% of all cases [1]. The pathogenesis of ESCC is a multi‐step process, which includes inflammation (INF), dysplasia (DYS), carcinoma in situ (CIS), and invasive carcinoma (ICA) [2]. Understanding ESCC progression from precancerous lesions to invasive cancer is critical for early intervention to improve survival, given its staging‐dependent prognosis.

While DNA mutations and copy number variations (CNVs) emerge early in precancerous ESCC lesions and persist during ESCC development [2, 3], the drivers of malignant transformation remain unclear. Increasing evidence suggests that the tumor microenvironment (TME), including the microbiome, plays a critical role in tumor progression [4, 5, 6]. Our earlier work identified Lactobacillus enrichment correlated with increased microbial diversity and an immunosuppressive TME in ESCC [7]. Other microbes like Fusobacterium nucleatum , Streptococcus, and Porphyromonas gingivalis have also been implicated in ESCC formation, therapeutic efficacy, and prognosis [8, 9, 10]. However, these studies relied on bulk tissue analysis, lacking cell‐type‐specific resolution.

While single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) has advanced our understanding of tumorigenesis and the implications of host–microbiome interactions [11, 12, 13, 14, 15], the application of single‐cell analysis of host–microbiome interactions (SAHMI) [15] in ESCC remains unexplored. The 4‐nitroquinoline 1‐oxide (4‐NQO)–induced mouse model, which recapitulates human ESCC progression [13, 16, 17], provides a valuable system for such investigations.

Here, by integrating scRNA‐seq with SAHMI analysis in the 4‐NQO‐induced mouse ESCC model, we demonstrated that the influence of commensal bacteria on epithelial transformation and TME remodeling offers novel insights into ESCC pathogenesis and therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Animal Model Construction and Samples Preparation

Four‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center and were maintained in a specific pathogen‐free (SPF) environment.

To study the effects of esophageal commensal bacteria on ESCC development, mice in the antibiotic cocktail (ABX) group were pre‐treated with vancomycin (EDDING, 0.5 g/L), neomycin (Sigma‐Aldrich, 0.5 g/L), and primaxin (MSD, 0.5 g/L) added to the drinking water for 3 weeks to deplete esophageal commensal bacteria, while mice in the H2O group were just fed with water (35 mice in each group). After pretreatment, 5 mice in each group were euthanized, and the esophageal mucosae were obtained for 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing. Subsequently, the remaining 30 mice in both groups were treated with 100 μg/mL 4‐NQO (Sigma‐Aldrich) in drinking water starting from week 0 for 12 weeks to induce multi‐staged ESCC carcinogenesis. Drinking water containing the carcinogen was replaced once a week with freshly prepared water, and mice were allowed to access drinking water ad libitum during treatment. After carcinogen treatment, 4‐NQO drinking water was replaced with sterile water until the mice were euthanized at 28 weeks, with the time of death for each mouse recorded throughout this period. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from treatment with 4‐NQO to death. At 28 weeks, the esophageal mucosae of 5 mice were subjected to 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing, and full‐thickness esophageal tissues of 12 mice were paraffin‐embedded for pathological evaluation.

In order to study the effects of esophageal commensal bacteria on TME evolvement during ESCC progression, scRNA‐seq was performed on esophageal mucosal samples of 4‐NQO‐induced mice after the ABX or H2O pretreatment at four different time points: Week 0, 12, 22, and 28, as described previously [13]. Esophageal mucosae were gained from another 20 mice at each time point of each group for scRNA‐seq. The full‐thickness esophageal tissues of 5 mice at each time point of each group were paraffin‐embedded for pathological evaluation and multiplex fluorescent immunohistochemistry (mfIHC).

16S rDNA Amplicon Sequencing

At 0 and 28 weeks, DNA extraction and 16S V3–V4 region amplification were performed in the mice esophageal mucosae. Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

scRNA‐Seq and Single Cell T Cell Receptor Sequencing (scTCR‐Seq)

The esophageal mucosae from mice in each group were harvested to perform scRNA‐seq and scTCR‐seq immediately after euthanasia at weeks 0, 12, 22, and 28. The tissues were dissected into small pieces, enzymatically digested, and dissociated into single cells. Following single‐cell partitioning, reverse transcription, and amplification, sequencing libraries were constructed and subsequently processed for sequencing and downstream analyses. Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

Bioinformatics Analyses

We performed Seurat v4.4.0 [18] to analyze data of scRNA‐seq. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was used for data visualization, and Harmony (v0.1.0) was used for eliminating batch effects. We employed SAHMI to evaluate host‐microbiome interactions at the single‐cell level [15]. Additionally, we applied the InferCNV package (v1.14.2) (https://github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV) to identify tumor cells and scVelo (v0.2.5) [19] to explore the potential inter‐relationship of cell lineages. Pathway activity for the cell cluster was estimated using the GSVA package (v1.50.0) with standard settings [20]. CellChat package (v1.6.1) [21] was used for cell–cell interaction analysis. Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

Multiplex Fluorescent Immunohistochemistry

Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) slides were subjected to mfIHC using the PANO Multiplex IHC kit (Panovue Biotechnology, BJ, China) to examine specific cell markers. Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2). The overall survival probability of mice was evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were compared using the log‐rank test. Continuous or categorical variables were compared using Student's t‐test, Wilcoxon test, or Chi‐square test, respectively. Spearman's correlation was calculated using the R function cor.test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Variations of Pathological Stages and Bacteria During ESCC Tumorigenesis

We used the 4‐NQO‐spontaneous ESCC mice model [13] to investigate the effects of bacteria on ESCC tumorigenesis (Figure 1A). The 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing confirmed that the α‐diversity of commensal bacteria in the esophageal mucosa was significantly reduced in the ABX group compared to the H2O group, and the β‐diversity also showed significant differences between the two groups at both 0 and 28 weeks (Figure 1B,C). When dissecting the esophagus of mice at 28 weeks, we found a higher percentage of mice with invasive esophageal carcinomas in the ABX group compared to the H2O group (Figure 1D and Figure S1). Survival analyses also revealed that the mice in the ABX group had significantly worse survival (Figure 1E). Therefore, we speculated that the esophageal dysbacteriosis could influence ESCC initiation and development.

Identification of Main Cell Types and Distribution of Commensal Bacteria During ESCC Tumorigenesis

To delineate commensal bacterial impacts on ESCC pathogenesis, we conducted paired scRNA‐seq and scTCR‐seq profiling of 4‐NQO‐treated murine esophageal mucosa at four different time points using the 10× Genomics platform (Figure 2A). Pathological examination confirmed the different stages: normal (NOR) at week 0, INF at week 12, DYS/CIS at week 22, and ICA at week 28, consistent with previous findings by Yao et al. [13]. After quality controls, 54,562 cells were retained: 27,675 cells from the H2O group and 26,887 cells from the ABX group (Figure S2A). Using UMAP, we identified 10 main cell populations, including epithelial cells (n = 1126), fibroblasts (n = 27,963), endothelial cells (n = 2712), pericytes (n = 861), lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC, n = 252), vascular associated smooth muscle cells (VASMC, n = 163), T cells (n = 10,708), B cells (n = 212), natural killer cells (NK, n = 713), and myeloid cells (n = 9852) (Figure 2B and Figure S2B,C).

At the NOR stage, major cell type proportions were similar between ABX and H2O groups, suggesting that deprivation of esophageal commensal bacteria did not affect the cellular composition of normal esophageal mucosa. Following 4‐NQO treatment, fibroblast proportions dropped at the INF stage but gradually recovered, while T cells increased early and then declined (Figure 2C), consistent with reported esophageal fibrosis progression and T cell reduction during human ESCC tumorigenesis [22]. Notably, these changes were less pronounced in the ABX group. Given the role of bacteria in cancer‐related inflammation [17, 23], we assessed inflammatory pathway activities. Results showed that the activities of inflammatory response, IL6‐JAK‐STAT3 signaling, and TNFA signaling via NFKB pathways increased progressively in the H2O group; however, these pathways peaked at the DYS stage but declined at the final ICA stage in the ABX group (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that in the H2O group, commensal bacteria triggered sustained pro‐inflammatory signaling upon carcinogen exposure, fostering a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that may promote tumor initiation [24]. Meanwhile, TGF‐β signaling pathway activity progressively increased in the ABX group during ESCC progression, suggesting microbiota depletion shifted the TME from chronic inflammation to TGF‐β‐dominated immunosuppression [25, 26].

We then applied SAHMI [15] to map commensal bacteria across cell types in the esophageal mucosa with approximately 30%–45% cells colonized by bacteria at different stages, similar to the bacterial colonization ratios observed in different types of cancers [15, 27, 28, 29] (Figure 2E,F). ABX treatment significantly reduced bacterial colonization at baseline. After 12‐week 4‐NQO exposure, the ratio of bacteria‐colonized cells in the ABX group rebounded to comparable levels in the H2O group, but the composition of commensal microbiota changed (Figure S2D). It peaked at the DYS stage but declined at the ICA stage in both groups, with a significantly lower level in the ABX group compared to the H2O group (Figure 2F). mfIHC staining for lipopolysaccharide (LPS), an indicator of bacterial infection, confirmed fewer bacteria‐colonized cells in the ABX group at the ICA stage (Figure 2G). Cell‐type‐specific analysis identified epithelial cells with the highest bacterial colonization frequency in both H2O and ABX groups, though ABX treatment significantly reduced epithelial colonization levels compared to H2O control. Moreover, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, endothelial cells, pericytes, and VASMCs had higher bacterial colonization frequencies in the H2O group, while T cell colonization showed the inverse pattern (Figure S2E). These findings suggest antibiotics selectively reshape microbial‐host cell interactions during ESCC tumorigenesis.

Association Between Commensal Bacteria and Malignant Transformation of Epithelial Cells

Using InferCNV analysis, we identified recurrent chromosomal alterations in epithelial cells emerging as early as the INF stage, with chromosome 7 showing the most frequent CNVs (Figure S3A). CNV scores increased significantly from NOR to DYS and plateaued thereafter in both ABX and H2O groups (Figure S3B). Epithelial cells were further stratified into tumor or normal epithelial cells based on the median CNV score (0.00175, Figure S3C). Tumor cells exhibited elevated expression of mitochondrial genes (e.g., mt‐Nd1, mt‐Atp6, mt‐Co2), the oncogenic long non‐coding RNA Malat1 [30], along with enriched mitotic/proliferation pathways, indicative of higher energy demand and proliferation potential of tumor cells. In contrast, normal epithelial cells predominantly expressed keratinization‐related genes (e.g., Krt4, Krt13), consistent with physiological self‐renewal of the esophageal epithelium (Figure S3D,E). The proportion of tumor cells among all epithelial cells progressively increased from NOR to DYS stages (p < 0.05) but remained stable during ICA transition in both groups, with no significant difference between ABX and H2O groups at any stage during ESCC development (Figure S3F). These results suggested that early CNVs accumulation during IFN initiates malignant transformation; however, commensal bacteria could not modulate 4‐NQO‐induced genomic instability. The divergent pathological outcomes at the ICA stage between the ABX and H2O groups likely reflect microbiome‐dependent remodeling of the mucosal microenvironment, particularly the immune and stromal compartments.

Additionally, intracellular bacterial colonization was significantly higher in tumor cells compared to normal epithelial cells in both ABX and H2O groups. However, bacteria‐colonized normal epithelial cells were more abundant in the H2O group (Figure S3G). Pseudotime and PCA analyses revealed that both tumor cells and bacteria‐colonized epithelial cells were enriched at later pseudotime stages (Figure S3H). Moreover, bacterial UMI counts were positively correlated with CNV scores (R = 0.49, p < 0.001; Figure S3I), suggesting a potential link between bacterial colonization and epithelial malignant transformation.

Association Between Commensal Bacteria and Formation of Inflammatory Fibroblasts

Transcriptomic profiling of mucosal fibroblasts revealed four distinct subpopulations (Figure 3A–C and Figure S4A). FibC1 displayed a normal fibroblast phenotype with high expression of collagen genes (Col1a1, Col1a2, Col3a1), dominated at the NOR stage but declined during tumorigenesis. FibC2 exhibited progenitor‐like features (high Sfrp2, enriched P53, Wnt/β‐catenin, KRAS signaling) [31], and FibC3 was proliferative (Ptn, Cilp, Epyc, enriched cell cycle pathways). The proportions of FibC2‐3 remained stable throughout tumorigenesis. FibC4 displayed upregulation of chemokine and cytokine genes (e.g., C3, C4b, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Ccl7, Ccl2, Ptx3, and Il6) and enhanced pathway activities of inflammatory response, complement, TNFA signaling via NFKB, and IL6‐JAK‐STAT3 signaling, establishing this cluster as the primary inflammatory fibroblast subset in ESCC [32]. It decreased at the IFN stage but expanded after 4‐NQO induction, especially in the H2O group, suggesting a bacteria‐dependent inflammatory response. Correspondingly, fibroblasts from the H2O group also showed significantly higher expression of inflammatory and chemokine‐related genes (e.g., Il6, Ptx3, Cxcl1) and enrichment of inflammatory pathways (IL6‐JAK‐STAT3, TNFA via NFKB) compared to the ABX group (Figure S4B,C), suggesting commensal bacteria promoted inflammatory fibroblast activation.

We next explored the commensal bacteria colonization in fibroblasts during ESCC tumorigenesis. The ratio of bacteria‐colonized fibroblasts was significantly higher in the H2O group than that in the ABX group, except for progenitor FibC2. Inflammatory FibC4 in the H2O group had the highest proportion of bacteria‐colonized cells (Figure 3D,E). The scVelo analysis identified two distinct differentiation trajectories from FibC2 progenitor fibroblasts toward either normal FibC1 or inflammatory FibC4 in both groups (Figure 3F), demonstrating plasticity in fibroblast fate determination. However, transitions to FibC4 were markedly reduced in the ABX group. Furthermore, fibroblasts with bacterial colonization were more likely to differentiate into FibC4 (Figure S4D). mfIHC analysis of ICA‐stage tissues revealed significantly increased inflammatory fibroblasts (Dcn+C3+) and bacteria‐colonized fibroblasts (Dcn+LPS+) in the H2O group compared to the ABX group, with their frequencies showing a strong positive correlation (Figure 3G,H). These findings suggested commensal bacteria promoted the differentiation of inflammatory fibroblasts, fostering an inflammatory TME in 4‐NQO‐induced ESCC.

Association Between Commensal Bacteria and T Cell Amplification

We identified three T cell clusters: Cd8 +, Cd4 +, and double‐negative T cells (DNTs) (Figure 4A). Cd4 + T cells were subdivided into regulatory Cd4 + T cells (Tregs), helper type 1 Cd4 + T cells (Th1), helper type 17 Cd4 + T cells (Th17), and central memory‐like Cd4 + T cells (Cd4 + Tcm) based on marker expression and functional scores (Figure 4B and Figure S5A,B) [33, 34]. Tregs peaked at the IFN stage, then declined but remained higher in the ABX group at DYS and ICA stages, suggesting a more immunosuppressive environment after bacterial depletion (Figure 4B). TCR sequencing revealed that Tregs were the main Cd4 + subtype with clonal expansion, and more medium‐level expanded Tregs were seen in the ABX group (Figure S5C,D). Lineage analysis suggested Tregs mainly originated from Cd4 + Tcm, and this transition was enhanced in the ABX group (Figure 4C). By mfIHC staining of the Treg markers Cd4 and Foxp3, we confirmed a significant increase in Treg infiltration in the ABX group at the ICA stage (Figure 4D). SAHMI analysis showed Tregs were the only Cd4 + subset with significantly increased bacterial colonization in ABX mice (Figure 4E,F and Figure S5E), suggesting that residual bacteria preferentially colonized Tregs, promoting their expansion and thereby reinforcing the immunosuppressive TME.

scRNA‐seq revealed 3 distinct Cd8 + T cell subtypes, including naïve Cd8 + T (Cd8 + Tna) cells enriched for lymphoid homing markers (Ccr7, Sell, and Lef1), precursor exhausted Cd8 + T (Cd8 + Tpex) cells co‐expressing immune checkpoints (Pdcd1, Ctla4, Lag3, Tigit, Hacvr2, and Entpd) and cytotoxic effector (Gzmk and Gzmb), and tissue‐resident memory‐like exhausted Cd8 + T (Cd8 + Trm‐like Tex) cells co‐expressing immune checkpoints and tumor‐reactive markers (Itgae and Icos) [35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40] (Figure 4G and Figure S5F). Accordingly, Cd8 + Tna cells had the highest quiescence scores, Cd8 + Trm cells displayed the highest proliferation scores, and Cd8 + Tpex cells showed the highest cytotoxicity and exhaustion scores (Figure S5G). Cd8 + Trm constituted the predominant Cd8 + subset in the ABX group at both DYS and ICA stages, exhibiting expanded clonal size particularly at the ICA stage (Figure 4G and Figure S5H,I), consistent with previous studies that Cd8 + Trm was highly clonally expanded within TME [40]. Clonal tracking further revealed enhanced transitions from Cd8 + Tna or Tpex to Cd8 + Trm in the ABX group (Figure 4H). By mfIHC staining with Cd8α and Itgae, we confirmed elevated Cd8 + Trm (Itgae+Cd8α+ cells) infiltration in ABX mice (Figure 4I). However, the proportion of bacteria‐colonized Cd8 + T cells did not differ significantly between the two groups (Figure S5E). Therefore, we proposed that Cd8 + Trm‐like Tex cells in the ABX group had more of the amplified clones in response to tumor antigen stimulation. However, these cells exhibited exhaustion characteristics and lower cytotoxicity, preventing them from effectively executing anti‐tumor cytotoxic functions.

Association Between Commensal Bacteria and Monocyte/Macrophage (Mo/Mφ) Transformation

Myeloid cells were clustered into multiple subtypes of dendritic cells (DCs), Mo/Mφ, neutrophils, and mast cells, with their specific gene expression [13, 41, 42] (Figure 5A and Figure S6A). Subtypes of DCs include type 1 conventional DCs (cDC1s, Cst3 + Itgae + Clec9a +), type 2 conventional DCs (cDC2s, Cd209a + Napsa +), and tolerogenic DCs (tDCs, Fscn1 + Tnfrsf9 + Cd274 +). Proportions of DCs remained relatively stable across tumorigenesis stages in both ABX and H2O groups (Figure 5B).

Mo/Mφ were identified among myeloid cells according to high expression of the gene Adgre1. Mo/MφC0 were tissue resident macrophages with the highest expression of Timd4, Lyve1, Folr2, and Cd163 [43]. Mo/MφC1 exhibited high expression of anti‐bacterial genes (Retnla and Cd163) and lysozyme 1 (Lyz1), demonstrating their specialized antimicrobial functions [44, 45, 46, 47]. Mo/MφC2 highly expressed perivascular tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs)‐associated genes Mrc1, Vcam1, and Ccl8, which probably facilitated immune cell intravasation [48]. Mo/MφC3 had the highest expression of the Cd72 gene associated with pro‐inflammation [49]. And Mo/MφC4, with high Ly6c2 and Thbs1 expression, was regarded as a monocyte‐like macrophage (Figure S6A) [50, 51].

As shown in Figure 5B, Mo/MφC0 was the main cell type in the NOR stage and disappeared immediately in the IFN stage in both ABX and H2O groups. The pro‐inflammatory Mo/MφC3 increased at the IFN stage and then decreased gradually at the DYS and ICA stages, while the anti‐bacterial Mo/MφC1 decreased sharply after 4‐NQO treatment but slowly increased later. The Mo/MφC1 increase and Mo/MφC3 decrease at the DYS and ICA stages were less obvious in the ABX group than those in the H2O group (Figure 5B). SAHMI analysis identified preferential bacterial colonization of DCs and Mo/Mφ cells compared to other myeloid cell subsets (Figure 5C and Figure S6B), consistent with their specialized roles in mucosal immune surveillance [52]. However, the application of ABX decreased bacterial colonization in Mo/MφC0 and Mo/MφC1 (Figure 5D). As shown by scVelo analysis, anti‐bacterial Mo/MφC1 could have originated from tissue resident Mo/MφC0 and monocyte‐like Mo/MφC4. However, these transitions occurred less frequently in the ABX group (Figure 5E), which could partially explain the less increase of Mo/MφC1 in the ABX group during ESCC development. These results suggested that the presence or absence of esophageal commensal bacteria might influence macrophage transformation into distinct subclusters after 4‐NQO exposure during ESCC development.

Changes in Cell–Cell Interaction Patterns Along With Commensal Bacteria Alteration

Cell–cell interaction analysis revealed fibroblasts as the dominant signal‐sending populations in both ABX and H2O groups across all stages (Figure 6A–D). However, signal‐receiving cell types showed stage‐ and treatment‐dependent patterns. At the INF stage, Tregs were the major signal recipients in both groups (Figure 6B). In later stages, anti‐bacterial Mo/MφC1 cells became the main recipients in the H2O group, whereas Tregs remained predominant in the ABX group, suggesting commensal bacteria‐dependent rewriting of stromal‐immune crosstalk. Notably, in the DYS and ICA stages, interactions between inflammatory FibC4 and anti‐bacterial Mo/MφC1 were markedly intensified in the H2O group, facilitated by chemotaxis, immune regulation, and adhesion mechanisms, such as Ccl7‐Ccr1, C3‐C3ar1, C3‐(Itgam + Itgb2), App‐Cd74, Col1a1‐Cd44, and Fn1‐Cd44 interactions (Figure 6E,F). Conversely, inflammatory FibC4 exerted the strongest regulatory effect on Tregs in the ABX group with significantly enhanced interactions via chemotaxis (Ccl2‐Ccr2 and Ccl7‐Ccr2) and adhesion (Col1a1‐Cd44 and Col1a2‐Cd44) mechanisms (Figure 6G,H). These interactions might attract more Tregs to infiltrate and expand, exacerbating immunosuppression in the ABX group. Together, these findings suggest that the distinct cell–cell interaction patterns may drive divergent pathological outcomes between the ABX and H2O groups. In the ABX group, inflammatory FibC4's communication with Tregs appeared to be a pivotal mechanism establishing the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Discussion

Microbial organisms contribute to not only infections but also tumorigenesis, as demonstrated by activating Toll‐like receptors, triggering inflammation and DNA damage in HPV‐, EBV‐, and Helicobacter pylori ‐related cancer [53]. The esophagus hosts diverse commensal microbes, yet their roles in ESCC development remain incompletely understood. In this study, using the spontaneous ESCC mouse model, we found that the inflammatory response triggered by 4‐NQO relied on commensal bacteria, while commensal bacteria deprivation by ABX treatment would attenuate antibacterial immunity, promote immunosuppression, and eventually exacerbate tumor progression. A similar effect on cancer promotion by microbiome dysbiosis was also observed in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [54].

While murine and human esophageal microbiomes differ compositionally, the use of antibiotics to deplete microbiota in the 4‐NQO‐induced ESCC mice model enables us to capture sequential lesions with histological concordance to human ESCC progression and investigate underlying mechanisms that are ethically unfeasible in humans. Growing evidence has supported the involvement of commensal microbiota in human ESCC progression. For example, compared with NOR and DYS tissues, microbial genera such as Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, and Treponema were found to be more prevalent in ICA tissues of ESCC patients [55]. However, the details of commensal microbiota in mice and humans are not completely different. It has been reported that the relative abundance of Streptococcus was significantly higher in advanced‐stage ESCC tissues compared to early‐stage ones and was negatively correlated with the survival of ESCC patients [56]. This bacterium was also identified to be enriched in ICA compared to NOR in our 4‐NQO‐induced ESCC mice model, strongly supporting the translational relevance of our murine model.

In this current study, we found that CNVs emerged early during the IFN stage and progressively increased at later DYS and ICA stages, accompanied by elevated inflammatory pathway activities, which were consistent with established DNA damage‐inflammation interplay during ESCC development [3, 17]. However, tumor cells showed significantly higher bacterial colonization than normal epithelia, suggesting microbial involvement in malignant transformation. Despite ABX‐induced microbial depletion altering inflammation, it neither affected CNV burden nor tumor cell ratio. Together, these results indicated that worse outcomes in the ABX group likely stemmed from commensal bacteria‐dependent microenvironmental changes rather than epithelial genomic instability.

Diverse cell populations, signaling factors, and structural molecules in the TME can interact with tumor cells and support all stages of tumorigenesis. Recently, mounting evidence has suggested that the composition of the human microbiome comprises one of the major integral parts and modifiers of a microenvironment [57]. Pathobionts from the commensal microbiota played a role in inflammatory processes that affected tumor growth [23]. Dysbiosis has been linked to ESCC development and treatment response [58, 59]. In this study, we found that the proportions of some subtypes of fibroblasts, Cd4 + T, Cd8 + T, and Mo/Mφ were imbalanced between the commensal bacteria‐deprived ABX and control H2O groups, suggesting the functions of these cells might be affected by commensal bacteria during ESCC development.

Fibroblasts, as key components of tumors, are central to extracellular matrix remodeling and can promote immune evasion [60]. In human ESCCs, inflammatory fibroblasts have been shown to secrete chemokines that recruit immune cells [61]. In our study, inflammatory fibroblasts (FibC4) interacted mainly with Mo/MφC1 in the H2O group but with Treg in the ABX group, suggesting bacterial status influences fibroblast‐immune cell crosstalk. We found that Mo/MφC1 was greater in number in the H2O group during the DYS and ICA stages, while Tregs were abundant in the ABX group, which might be attributed to different interactions between inflammatory fibroblasts and these cells in each group, respectively. While ABX treatment reduced FibC4 infiltration and inflammatory activity, it increased Treg proportions and enhanced FibC4‐Treg interactions through chemotaxis and adhesion pathways. This suggests residual FibC4 in ABX‐treated mice shifted from pro‐inflammatory to immunoregulatory phenotypes that promoted Treg recruitment. Recruitment of Tregs would attenuate antitumor immunity via mechanisms such as the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, interference with tumor‐associated antigen presentation, and inhibition of cytotoxic cell function and granule release [62], which finally led to worse pathological results and survivals of mice in the ABX group. Collectively, these results underlined the importance of stromal cells and proximal immune cells' interactions in tumor development, and the bacteria‐affected fibroblast functional state might play crucial roles in the process.

Furthermore, we found that inflammatory fibroblasts, Mo/MφC1, and Tregs exhibited higher bacterial colonization ratios than other subclusters, suggesting that bacterial colonization might guide their developmental trajectories. It has been demonstrated that bacteria were unevenly distributed in human tumors and altered the biological properties of different cellular compartments [29]. For instance, Helicobacter pylori and Staphylococcus aureus have been shown to promote fibroblasts' transformation into inflammatory fibroblasts [63, 64]. Similarly, macrophages colonized by bacteria showed significantly increased expression of inflammation‐related genes and chemokines [29]. Moreover, bacteria have been shown to promote the differentiation of CD4 + T cells into Tregs through multiple mechanisms, including the production of short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), activation of the LPS‐TLR signaling pathway, and metabolic reprogramming [65]. However, the specific mechanisms by which esophageal commensal bacteria influence the differentiation of inflammatory fibroblasts, Mo/MφC1, and Tregs, as well as their crosstalk, remain to be further investigated in our 4‐NQO–induced ESCC mouse model.

In summary, scRNA‐seq of carcinogen‐induced mouse ESCC revealed stage‐specific changes in the esophageal mucosa, highlighting the impact of commensal bacteria on tumor progression. The results revealed that commensal bacteria in the esophagus might be related to the inflammatory TME during ESCC progression. Deprivation of commensal bacteria would exacerbate the immunosuppressive state in the TME, further promoting the formation of invasive cancer. A better understanding of the role of microbiota in inflammation‐related ESCCs and future verification of our findings in human ESCCs would potentially lead to the development of microbial prevention and treatment methods for ESCCs.
