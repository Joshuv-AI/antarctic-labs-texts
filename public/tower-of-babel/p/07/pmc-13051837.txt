Therapeutic Decisions in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: ChatGPT‐4.0 Compared to Medical Expertise
Authors: Helena Loncour, Jennifer Aoun, Alice Hoyois, Vinciane Muls
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionWith the increasing prevalence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and its complex management, evaluating innovative approaches became essential. Large language models (LLM), such as ChatGPT‐4.0, have remarkably advanced, yet their application in IBD management remains unexplored. This study compares therapeutic recommendations generated by ChatGPT‐4.0 with those made by IBD specialists, evaluating its potential and limitations in clinical practice.Materials and MethodsThis prospective observational study, conducted between September 2024 and March 2025, included 47 cases of IBD patients requiring initiation or modification of biologic therapy. Standardized clinical vignettes were presented to both IBD specialists and ChatGPT‐4.0. For each case, three sequential questions were addressed: the choice of the therapeutic class of the treatment, the specific molecule in this class, and if the proposition is a monotherapy or combination therapy. Agreement was measured using the percentage concordance and Cohen's kappa coefficient (κ).ResultsThe agreement between the IBD specialists and ChatGPT‐4.0 was 76.6% (κ = 0.677) for the therapeutic class and 44.7% (κ = 0.352) for specific molecule selection. Therapeutic class discordance occurred in 11 cases (23.4%); all were reviewed and deemed acceptable by the gastroenterologists. Notably, seven of ChatGPT‐4.0's second‐line suggestions matched with the IBD specialists' treatment decisions.ConclusionThis study highlights a promising area of ChatGPT‐4.0 application in the therapeutic management of IBD. However, it is not suitable as a standalone or fully reliable resource for decision‐making in healthcare. Future development of tailored healthcare GPT integrating European guidelines and experts' feedback may further improve LLM performance.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), including Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are chronic immune‐mediated disorders characterized by inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract with alternating periods of remission and recurrence [1, 2]. The global prevalence approaches seven million people and is projected to increase in both developing and industrialized countries [3]. The chronic nature and unpredictable progression of IBD impact various aspects of patients' health, including biological, social, and psychological dimensions. Over the past decade, advances in understanding IBD pathophysiology and the emergence of multiple therapeutic options, particularly biologic therapies, have contributed to improving patients' outcomes [4, 5].

Nevertheless, IBD management remains challenging for both patients and clinicians due to frequent therapeutic adjustments, diverse treatment strategies, and the need for personalized care [3, 5]. Multidisciplinary meetings, well‐established in oncology, represent a key strategy for improving personalized therapeutic management of chronic diseases [6]. Nonetheless, innovative approaches are still needed to address the complexity of clinical decision‐making related to inflammatory disorders such as IBD.

Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) has experienced remarkable growth in recent years, in everyday life and the healthcare system. This technology can generate high‐quality, personalized texts or images based on a pre‐trained database. With increasing accessibility and popularity, these systems can now address complex questions with human‐level performance [7, 8].

Chat Generative Pre‐Trained Transformer (ChatGPT), developed by OpenAI and released in November 2022, represents one of the most widely used AI applications. It is based on a large language model (LLM), capable of generating human‐like responses. Its performance stems from extensive pre‐training and continuous reinforcement learning. Version 4.0, launched in March 2023, was the most up‐to‐date model at the beginning of the study and provided access to an extensive database [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

LLMs are evolving rapidly, with the potential to improve clinical outcomes and support personalized care, leading to an expanding body of medical literature [7, 9]. In gastroenterology, promising results have already been observed in patient education, screening, and diagnosis [13]. ChatGPT has also shown potential in guiding therapeutic decisions for conditions such as gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and pancreatic cysts, although its reliability remains debatable [14, 15]. Recent oncology studies have demonstrated encouraging concordance between AI‐based and physicians' responses across different cancer types, including colorectal, breast, and cervicofacial cancer [16, 17, 18].

The increasing prevalence of IBD and its complex therapeutic management represent a growing challenge that AI‐based solutions could potentially address [19]. In a recent study on severe UC management in the emergency department, Levartovsky et al. reported an 80% concordance between AI‐generated attitudes and physicians' approaches [20]. However, no studies to date have specifically explored ChatGPT's utility in guiding biologic therapy decisions for IBD patients. Evaluating the impact and limitations of such technologies on clinical decision‐making is essential for safe and effective integration into medical practice [21].

Based on this background, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the performance of ChatGPT‐4.0 in providing therapeutic decisions for patients with IBD. For this purpose, we compared biologic therapy recommendations provided by ChatGPT‐4.0 with those made by gastroenterologists specialized in the field of IBD.

Materials and Methods

This observational prospective monocentric study was conducted over a period of 7 months, from September 2024 to March 2025 at a University Hospital.

Inclusion criteria encompassed IBD patients requiring initiation or the modification of biologic therapy. Exclusion criteria comprised patients responding to ongoing treatment (with or without optimization) and those not requiring medical therapy, such as surgical intervention for disease flare. Medical data were collected in the electronic health records and compiled in Excel to create a standardized clinical vignette (Figure S1).

Clinical IBD Meeting (CIM)

Three gastroenterologists specialized in IBD convened to determine the optimal biologic therapy for included patients. For each case, decisions were made regarding the choice of the therapeutic class of the treatment, the specific molecule in this class, and monotherapy versus combination therapy. All decisions were documented and uploaded to electronic medical records.

ChatGPT version 4.0, the most recent version at the study initiation, was consistently employed. Data upload was carefully synchronized with the medical team's decisions to ensure temporal comparability. To avoid potential bias from previous interactions, each clinical vignette was uploaded in a separate discussion. Questions followed a consistent sequence, with instructions to provide brief answers.

ChatGPT‐4.0 first generated a biologic therapy suggestion, then was asked whether the provided answers would change when considering both the European Crohn's and Colitis Organization (ECCO) recommendations [22] and the Belgian National Institute for Health and Disability Insurance (INAMI) reimbursement criteria, and to provide verifiable references for its statements. A second‐choice therapeutic approach was then requested. To assess internal variability, identical clinical vignettes were re‐uploaded 48 h later in new ChatGPT‐4.0 conversations.

After data collection, the medical team reviewed all discordant cases to assess clinical safety of ChatGPT‐4.0's recommendations.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.2.0. Descriptive statistics were applied to characterize the study population, including means with standard deviations (SD) and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables, as well as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Agreement was evaluated using percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa coefficient (κ), interpreted according to the Landis and Koch scale [23]. To identify predictive factors of discordance, we compared agreement and disagreement groups using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p value < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

This study complies with the “General Data Protection Regulation” and was approved by the local University Hospital ethical committee (reference: CE‐24‐09‐11). All patients provided written informed consent for the use of their anonymized clinical data.

Results

We included a total of 41 IBD patients between September 2024 and March 2025. Two patients underwent three therapeutic changes and two patients underwent two therapeutic changes, resulting in 47 clinical vignettes.

The general characteristics of the study population are described in Table S1. The 47 clinical vignettes included 24 males (51.1%) and 23 females (48.9%), with 24 CD (51.1%) and 23 UC (48.9%) patients. The mean age was 37 ± 13 years. The mean of Harvey‐Bradshaw Index for CD was 4 ± 2, and the mean of partial Mayo Score for UC was 5 ± 2. Twenty patients (42.6%) presented extra‐intestinal manifestations (EIM): 6 peripheral polyarthritis (12.8%), 5 ankylosing spondylitis (10.6%), 3 uveitis (6.4%), 2 psoriasis (4.3%), 2 erythema nodosum (4.3%), and 2 juvenile idiopathic arthritis (4.3%). Seven patients (14.9%) had a history of intestinal surgery in their course of disease. Thirteen patients (27.7%) initiated biologic therapy, while 34 (72.3%) had a change in their existing treatment. Indications included disease flare (91.5%), medication allergies (4.3%), fertility concerns (2.1%), and recurrent respiratory infection (2.1%).

Agreement between the CIM and ChatGPT‐4.0's first decision was significant (p < 0.05), with 76.6% concordance (36/47) and κ = 0.677 for the therapeutic class. The concordance for the specific molecule was lower, at 44.7% (21/47), with κ = 0.352 (Table 1). Discordance in the therapeutic class occurred in 11 cases (24.4%), all reviewed by the gastroenterologists and considered acceptable. Among these, ChatGPT‐4.0's second‐choice suggestions aligned with CIM decision in 63.6% (7/11), as reported in Table S3.

ChatGPT‐4.0 reported consistency with the latest ECCO guidelines in 40 patients (85.1%) and with Belgian INAMI reimbursement criteria in 41 patients (87.2%). Upon review, all answers aligned with the ECCO guidelines, while 46 out of 47 responses aligned with the Belgian INAMI reimbursement criteria. The only inconsistent case involved the proposition of a Janus Kinase (JAK) inhibitor as a first‐line biologic treatment.

Regarding ECCO recommendations and Belgian INAMI references, ChatGPT‐4.0 did not provide any references for 30 patients (63.8%), and among the 17 generated, only eleven were correct.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the performance of LLM, particularly ChatGPT‐4.0, in therapeutic decision‐making concerning patients with inflammatory bowel disease. Indeed, previous studies have explored the potential of ChatGPT in the medical field and reported promising results across different specialties [7, 8, 9, 13]. In this observational prospective study, we evaluated ChatGPT‐4.0's role in supporting IBD management by comparing its treatment recommendations with those provided by the CIM.

In our cohort of 47 clinical vignettes, the primary endpoint was the substantial agreement between the CIM and ChatGPT‐4.0 regarding the selection of therapeutic class, with a concordance rate of 76.6% and a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.677. These results are consistent with similarly high levels of agreement reported by Gorelik et al. and by Henson et al. (87% and 91.3%, respectively) in their studies on the treatment of GERD and pancreatic cyst lesions using LLMs [14, 15]. Additionally, regarding the management of severe UC in the emergency department, a concordance rate of 80% was observed in disease severity classification and the need for hospitalization [20]. In contrast, lower agreement rates (51%) were reported in a study on AI‐based antibiotic recommendations in the field of infectious diseases [24]. These encouraging results support the potential of LLMs to provide clinical guidance in the management of IBD. Nevertheless, the existing literature on this topic is limited, and interpretations should be made with caution [23].

On the other hand, a lower agreement rate (44.7%) was observed for the choice of the specific molecule in the therapeutic class, reflecting individual clinical preferences rather than a relevant discordance, especially since therapeutic class selection is mostly considered the cornerstone in IBD management. Interestingly, ChatGPT‐4.0 did not suggest a previously administered biologic molecule to any of the 47 clinical vignettes, highlighting its ability to adhere to standard treatment algorithms and limit redundancy.

Regarding the disagreement group, ChatGPT‐4.0's first‐choice therapeutic class differed from that selected by the CIM in 24.4% of cases (11/47). All discordant decisions were reviewed and deemed clinically acceptable by the gastroenterologists. This outcome aligns with a recent rheumatology study, in which all ChatGPT‐generated strategies were validated as safe options, despite the absence of concordance with medical experts [21, 25]. Among the 11 disagreement cases, ChatGPT‐4.0 predominantly recommended anti‐TNF agents (72.7%), indicating a preference for well‐established broad‐acting therapies. This contrasts with the up‐to‐date and targeted approach of the CIM for treatment selection.

Notably, while the CIM suggested a Sphingosine‐1‐Phosphate (S1P) modulator for a biological‐naive UC patient, ChatGPT‐4.0 recommended a JAK inhibitor, not eligible for first‐line treatment in Belgium. This case highlights a limitation of ChatGPT, particularly when the integration of medical guidelines and national criteria is lacking in the current model. Interestingly, seven of ChatGPT‐4.0's second‐choice suggestions matched the decisions of the gastroenterologists, likely providing an alternative targeted treatment strategy, consistent with the CIM's approach as a secondary opinion.

Non‐parametric analysis identified a single predictive factor associated with the disagreement between the CIM's decision and ChatGPT‐4.0. The agreement rate was significantly higher in patients with EIM, most likely due to the limited therapeutic options available for these involvements, which guided the LLM. As for the disease type, a non‐significant trend towards better agreement was observed in patients with CD compared to UC. This trend towards better agreement was also seen in biologic‐exposed patients, who underwent biological treatment changes. A potential explanation may lie in the variations in decision‐making algorithms and training‐related biases inherent to LLMs [8, 9, 10].

Several limitations of ChatGPT‐4.0 were identified during our research. Mirikizumab, a new‐generation monoclonal antibody inhibiting selectively interleukin 23, was never suggested as a first‐choice treatment option and only once as a second‐choice option [5]. This highlights one of the limitations of LLMs in providing up‐to‐date knowledge compared to the clinician's approach, adapted to the results of the latest publications [7, 8, 10]. Additionally, ChatGPT‐4.0 failed to propose combination therapies, suggesting its inability to recommend complex strategies for the management of IBD flares. We also observed minor internal variability in ChatGPT‐4.0's responses when identical clinical vignettes were re‐entered 48 h later, providing different first‐choice suggestions in 6 cases (12.76%). This inconsistency, previously reported in the study on GERD management, remains a known shortcoming of LLMs [10, 14].

Moreover, the ChatGPT‐4.0 model failed to cite the ECCO recommendations and Belgian INAMI reimbursement criteria in 30 cases. Among the 17 references generated, six (12.6%) were found to be inaccurate upon verification. This challenge, termed artificial hallucination, is characterized by outputs that appear evidence‐based but, upon closer examination, contain fabricated information. These issues underscore the need for cautious interpretation of AI‐generated references [9, 12, 26].

Previous studies have pointed out the potential of alternative LLMs and tailored GPT models in generating therapeutic decisions for clinicians. More broadly, Griewing et al. and Rider et al. compared the capabilities of several LLM applications, including Llama 2 and Bard, with ChatGPT‐4.0 emerging as the most accurate [13, 17, 27]. In the context of customized GPT, Gorelik et al. and Lammert et al. demonstrated that incorporating guideline‐based management and expert feedback can significantly enhance the performance of LLMs [15, 21, 28]. In this context, enriching ChatGPT with European medical guidelines and national criteria, as well as real‐world clinical decisions, could be a promising direction for improving IBD therapeutic management.

The main limitations of this study included its monocentric design, a small sample size (47 vignettes of 41 patients), and a limited data collection period of 7 months due to academic constraints. Additionally, methodological instructions hindered ChatGPT‐4.0 from requesting additional case‐specific information. Therapeutic decisions made by the CIM were considered the gold standard but may have been influenced by ongoing pharmaceutical clinical trials, patient preferences, or individual prescribing habits.

Nonetheless, the study's major strength lies in its use of real‐world clinical data and standardized clinical vignettes within a prospective design. To reduce biases, gastroenterologists were blinded to ChatGPT‐4.0's responses before the selection of biologic therapy, and three pilot cases were used to refine the vignette template. Finally, a safety verification of ChatGPT‐4.0's recommendations was conducted, providing an additional perspective to this investigation.

An important implication of this study relates to how LLM‐based tools such as ChatGPT should be positioned within clinical practice. Based on our findings, LLMs should be considered clinical decision support tools rather than an autonomous decision‐making system. Its potential value lies in assisting clinicians with structured therapeutic reasoning, rapid access to guidelines, and exploration of alternative treatment strategies, particularly in complex diseases such as IBD [8, 9, 11]. However, therapeutic decision‐making in real‐world practice requires integration of multiple patient‐specific factors, including patient profiles, patient preferences, and national reimbursement policies. These elements cannot be fully captured by current LLMs, reinforcing that final clinical responsibility must remain with the treating physician.

With the increasing accessibility of LLMs platforms to the public, patients may independently consult these platforms and identify discrepancies with clinicians' approaches and proposed treatments [29]. Since LLM‐generated outputs do not constitute regulated medical advice, may be outdated, and are not subject to validation and certification processes, clear communication with patients regarding the supportive, non‐prescriptive role of AI tools is essential. In addition, appropriate documentation of clinical reasoning and guideline‐based decisions may help reduce potential medico‐legal risks and preserve shared decision‐making between physicians and patients [7, 11].

Another concern relates to whether reliance on LLMs could reduce clinicians' engagement in continuous medical education. When used appropriately, AI tools may instead support ongoing learning by synthesizing medical information and encouraging verification against updated guidelines and recent evidence [7]. In our study, ChatGPT‐4.0 demonstrated limitations in identifying newly available therapies, integrating national reimbursement criteria, and proposing complex treatment strategies such as combination therapy. These shortcomings highlight that expert clinical judgment and continuous professional updating remain indispensable for safe clinical use.

This observational prospective study illustrates the rapid evolution of ChatGPT‐4.0 and its promising potential in supporting therapeutic decision‐making within the increasingly complex management of IBD. The model demonstrated substantial agreement with expert decisions at the therapeutic class selection and an excellent safety profile of its recommendations, validated by the IBD specialists. However, ChatGPT‐4.0's role remains constrained by significant limitations, including a preference for broad‐acting agents rather than newer‐generation or gut‐selective therapies, an inability to provide complex management strategies, and the generation of outdated or fabricated references.

In conclusion, although ChatGPT‐4.0 demonstrates significant potential as a clinical support tool in IBD management, it is not suitable as a standalone or fully reliable resource for decision‐making in healthcare. Future efforts should focus on developing tailored healthcare GPT models that incorporate European medical guidelines as well as national criteria, with appropriate real‐world clinical reasoning, to ensure safe, context‐aware, and evidence‐based applications in the medical field.
