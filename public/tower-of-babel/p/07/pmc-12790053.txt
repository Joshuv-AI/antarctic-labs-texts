A Scoping Review of Large Language Models in Dental Education: Applications, Challenges, and Prospects
Authors: Mengjie Kong, Elise Hoi Wan Fok, Cynthia Kar Yung Yiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsWith the rapid development of large language models (LLMs), their potential in dental clinical healthcare and education has become increasingly evident. However, the application of this emerging technology within dental education has not yet been systematically reviewed. This scoping review aims to summarize current applications, identify challenges encountered in the implementation process, and outline future prospects.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in the PubMed, Web of Science, and Embase databases to identify studies related to LLMs and dental education. Relevant studies were screened, selected, and subjected to data extraction to collate information pertinent to the research questions.ResultsA total of 37 studies were included. LLMs have been applied across seven distinct domains in dental education, notably in dental examinations (n = 24), educational information resources (n = 5), and assessment of students’ assignments (n = 3). These applications have introduced several challenges, including technical issues (ie, inaccurate information, inability in image analysis, inaccessibility, and high cost) and application aspects (ie, ethical issues, absence of usage guidelines, potential harm to students’ knowledge and skills, and cautious attitudes towards LLMs). Addressing these challenges requires coordinated efforts from students, educators, curriculum administrators, and technical developers.ConclusionsThe application of LLMs in dental education remains in its early stage. Future research should focus on expanding the range of applications and establishing standardized, ethically satisfying, and technically sound guidelines for integrating LLMs into dental education.Key words: Artificial intelligence, Curriculum, Dental education, Dental examination, Large language model

Introduction

With the rapid development of digital technologies, dental education is transforming from traditional to digital education model.1,2 Digital technologies such as virtual reality and artificial intelligence (AI) have been increasingly integrated into dental education.3,4 With more dental schools and rising student enrolment, the shortage of qualified dental educators has become more pronounced.5 In response, digital dental education has emerged as a vital solution to address the imbalance between students and faculty, supporting more effective instruction, and improving students’ clinical competencies and professional qualities,6 making it a key direction for the future development of dental education. However, the implementation of digitalization is associated with technological updates and innovations, and current digital technologies still face various challenges when applied in educational settings.

Defined as systems that simulate human cognition and behaviour via computational methods, AI holds great potential for digital dental education. Since the introduction of the transformer architecture in 2017,7 AI development has accelerated, leading to the emergence of transformer-based large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT (OpenAI) and DeepSeek (DeepSeek). LLMs have significantly more parameters than conventional models, which are trained on extensive datasets and have demonstrated advanced language reasoning and understanding capabilities.8 Leveraging the robust language processing and comprehension abilities, LLMs have been used in dental clinical healthcare,9 exhibiting superior performance over traditional AI models (eg, machine learning, convolutional neural network) in areas such as diagnosis,10 treatment planning, and patient communication.11 These successes highlight the considerable potential of LLMs in clinical training as well, further emphasizing its role in shaping the future of dental education.

However, the distinctive nature of dental education presents additional challenges for the application of LLMs in this field.12,13 Firstly, dental training is extensive and multifaceted, encompassing didactic instruction, clinical skill development, preclinical simulation, and specialty rotations, while also serving a diverse range of learners, including undergraduate students, graduates, residents, and specialists. Effectively integrating LLMs into such a complex educational environment remains an unresolved issue. Moreover, the rigorous standards of dental education require content that is systematically structured and scientifically validated. As an emerging technology, the educational impact of LLMs in dental settings remains largely uncertain. In addition, the foundational architecture of LLMs differs markedly from that of conventional AI models, and best practices for their appropriate and effective use in dental education have yet to be established.

As these technologies continue to evolve, dental educators must stay informed and adapt their pedagogical approaches to ensure that LLMs are implemented effectively and responsibly. Nonetheless, a comprehensive analysis of the role of LLMs in dental education is still lacking, highlighting the urgent need for a systematic analysis to inform dental students, educators, curriculum administrators, and technical developers in the responsible and strategic adoption of LLMs.

In summary, this scoping review explored the current applications, limitations, and future prospects of LLMs in dental education. By synthesizing existing literature and identifying key research gaps, this review seeks to provide strategic guidance for the effective integration of LLMs into dental education. Ultimately, the goal is to support the advancement of a more innovative, structured, and practical dental education framework.

Search strategy

The following databases were used for the search: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science. The search for publications was conducted from their inception to 10 March 2025. We utilized a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) relevant to LLMs and dental education. Details of search queries were listed in Table 1.

Study selection and screening process

The literature search resulted from PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Web of Science were imported into EndNote. Two authors (M.K. and E.F.) independently screened the articles for eligibility (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.844), and discrepancies were resolved through independent review by a third author (C.Y.). A final consensus was reached through author meetings.

The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: (1) focused on the use of LLMs (such as ChatGPT) in the context of dental education, (2) written in English, and (3) published up to 10 March 2025. The exclusion criteria were: (1) non-English publications, (2) articles focusing on nondental education, such as general medical education or nursing education, (3) articles that discussed LLMs unrelated to dental education (eg, general AI technology or use in other industries), (4) articles from nonacademic sources (eg, newspapers, internet websites, magazines, etc.). Data extraction

The following data was extracted by one author (M.K.) from each included article: (1) Article type (research article, commentary, etc.), (2) potential applications of LLMs in dental education (eg, clinical training, patient communication, anatomy education, etc.), (3) potential limitations of LLMs in dental education (eg, accuracy concerns, ethical issues, etc.), and (4) suggestions and recommendations for incorporating LLMs into dental curricula (eg, instructional methods, faculty training, ethical guidelines).

Data synthesis and analysis

This study conducted a bibliometric analysis of the included literature, utilizing VOSviewer software (Leiden University, The Netherlands) for visualization. The extracted data were organized, statistically analysed, and summarized. The numbers of applications, and LLM brands, and models were determined based on their respective frequencies. The data was visualized using Excel software (Microsoft Corp.) and GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, Inc.).

Result

Characteristics of the included studies

A total of 748 records were initially identified, after removing 162 duplicates, 586 studies were retained for title and abstract screening. Of these 586 studies, 537 were excluded, leaving 49 for full-text review. After full-text screening, 12 of the 49 studies were excluded (the reasons for exclusion are shown in Figure 1), and 37 studies were finally included for synthesis after meeting the inclusion criteria (Figure 1; Table 2).

Other applications of LLMs in dental education

Beyond dental examination, LLMs demonstrated versatile applicational potential in assisting dental education. Educational information resources refer to the use of LLMs to summarize textbooks and internet resources, providing easily accessible reference for didactic lectures and assignments.19,20 Several studies also focused on investigating knowledge, attitude and expectation of both dental students and educators regarding the use of LLMs.21,22 LLMs have also been shown to be effective in assessing students’ assignments and providing feedback on overall quality, accuracy in terminology and grading of the work.16,23 From a preclinical support perspective, research has shown that LLMs can provide diagnosis support, facilitate patient communication and generate personalized informed consent for dental interns.24, 25, 26 Moreover, some researchers have reported that they have adapted LLMs to automatically generate test questions based on existing textbooks, aligning the difficulty level with preset parameters.23,27 It should also be reported that LLMs could serve as virtual patients to simulate diverse clinical scenarios, thereby enhancing students’ history-taking and clinical reasoning skills.28

Technical challenges

Integrating LLMs into dental education involves technical challenges related to their architecture, data, and computational demands. These factors can affect the accuracy, consistency, and relevance of their outputs in clinical and educational settings. A primary concern of using LLMs in dental education is generating inaccurate information. Due to the ‘hallucination’ phenomenon, a notable proportion of responses generated by LLMs are factually incorrect, regardless of model types or versions.29 Some studies have also reported the inconsistencies between answers and the accompanying explanations.30

Another limitation is the inability in analysing images. LLMs, particularly their earlier versions, are text-based models and thus incapable of interpreting image-embedded information, eg, X-rays, clinical photographs. Even for the latest version that can process images, they also underperformed on image-based questions compared to text-based ones.30, 31, 32 Additionally, restricted access and costly subscription pose practical barriers. Some advanced LLMs are not free-for-use, along with the online traffic problems impacting the users’ experiences.33,34

Application-related issues

Beyond technical issues, using LLMs in dental education raises concerns about ethics, misuse, and overreliance on the technology. Ethical issues include risks of academic dishonesty, such as plagiarism, as well as legal misuse involving copyright infringement. There are also concerns regarding fairness in assessment and learning.19,26,35 Additionally, the absence of clear usage guidelines, such as standardized prompting technologies and institutional policies, making the use of LLMs prone to inaccuracy and unreliability.36 Overreliance on LLMs-generated answers may hinder the development of students’ independent thinking, clinical reasoning and knowledge in dentistry.27,35 As a result, some dental students, educators and stakeholders remain sceptical about the reliability and accuracy of LLMs-generated content, leading to lower willingness in integrating LLMs in their daily use.22

Future prospects of LLMs in dental education

This review outlines seven potential future directions for the development and application of LLMs in dental education (Figure 4), including: (1) utilizing LLMs as supportive teaching tutor/tools: LLMs can serve as 24/7 available study partner, providing supplementary learning materials and explanations to support formal dental courses37; (2) providing technical support and guidance for dental students and educators: this includes offer training on how to integrate LLMs with authoritative reference and appropriate technologies to enhance academic effectiveness23; (3) facilitating interactive and customized learning: leveraging the large-data-processing capacity of LLMs, personalized teaching and feedback can be created to meet individual needs20; (4) integrating LLMs into the dental education for curriculum reform: LLMs can assist in developing a novel curriculum, generating abundant didactic resources, and refining existing teaching methods (eg, flipped classroom)36; (5) offering emotional support for dental students: the balanced use of LLMs and human-based teaching methods contribute to students’ emotional well-being24; (6) supporting evidence-based preclinical training: there should be more focus on the LLMs’ performance in clinical settings38; (7) enhancing technological development: there is a need for ongoing technological improvements in mitigating hallucinations, improving response accuracy, developing multimodal models for more effective image interpretation.15,39

Discussion

LLMs demonstrate significant potential across multiple applications in dental education

This review systematically identified and evaluated current research on the application of LLMs in dental education. The findings indicate that, although the integration of LLMs in this field is still early, these technologies have already demonstrated notable potential across various educational functions. Specifically, the reviewed studies outline seven primary areas of application. However, certain specialties such as orthodontics remain underexplored, likely due to the highly case-dependent and complex nature of orthodontic knowledge, as well as the technical limitations of current LLMs in handling such specialized domains as reported in recent studies.32 Besides, some instructional tasks, including virtual patient simulation and exam question generation, remain underinvestigated.

Most of the studies included in this review have been published within the past 3 years, indicating the recent focus on this research domain. Nonetheless, the distribution of publications across regions and journals is uneven, and interinstitutional and interdisciplinary collaborations remain relatively scarce. These findings underscore that the use of LLMs in dental education is still in a preliminary and exploratory phase. This slow progress can be partially attributed to the rapid pace of technological advancement, particularly the continuous updates of existing models such as ChatGPT and the introduction of new platforms like DeepSeek. These developments further expand the potential scope of LLMs applications in dental education and underscore the importance of continuous research to keep pace with evolving capabilities.

Dental education typically follows a structured progression, starting with preclinical training, advancing to clinical practice, and subsequently to specialty training. LLMs demonstrate the potential to provide comprehensive support across this continuum. In the preclinical stage, students must acquire foundational knowledge in both general and specialized areas of dentistry. LLMs have shown promising results in supporting knowledge acquisition, particularly in the context of dental examinations. For example, Xiong et al15 evaluated the performance of several LLMs on various item types from the Chinese Dental Licensing Examination, including MCQs, case analysis, integrated case scenarios, and select-and-fill items, and reported favourable outcomes in terms of accuracy, consistency, and instructional value. However, the limited accuracy of LLMs in answering dental examination questions highlights gaps in their reliable integration into the sophisticated educational contexts.

During the clinical practice phase, LLMs can function as accessible repositories of dental knowledge, offering educational resources that support clinical learning.17 Notably, LLMs can simulate virtual patient interactions. Or et al developed a chatbot based on ChatGPT-3.5 to emulate virtual patients across different clinical scenarios. Through both text- and voice-based interactions, the chatbot was found to enhance students’ history-taking skills.28 In the specialty training stage, where students are expected to perform clinical tasks with greater autonomy, LLMs can offer clinical assistance in several key areas, including diagnosis and decision-making,24 treatment planning, patient communication and education,26 and informed consent generation.25 These applications demonstrate that LLMs are well-positioned to offer comprehensive educational support across the entire continuum of dental education. While the current paucity of evidence regarding the clinical use of LLMs calls for more in-depth investigations into their effectiveness in assisting clinical training.

Given the broad scope of dental knowledge, the emphasis on advanced clinical skills, and the high technical threshold of clinical procedures, dental educators are frequently required to possess substantial professional expertise while managing significant workloads and prolonged teaching hours. Indeed, training qualified educators can often be more demanding than training students. Although virtual reality and conventional AI tools have facilitated the optimization of teaching strategies, the development and delivery of instructional content and assessment processes remain largely dependent on educators’ manual input.

With their advanced cognitive and language-processing capabilities, LLMs offer potential advantages in addressing dental educators’ multifaceted challenges. For instance, Quah et al employed ChatGPT-4 to automatically assess oral and maxillofacial surgery essays written by 69 final-year undergraduate dental students. The AI-generated scores demonstrated moderate to strong correlations with evaluations provided by expert assessors.16 Similarly, Ahmed et al utilized ChatGPT and Gemini to generate MCQs based on content from a dental caries textbook. The generated questions showed no significant differences in quality or cognitive level compared to those created by human experts.27 Automated essay grading and question generation reduce educators’ workload and enhance the availability of diverse, high-quality teaching materials. Thus, LLMs offer substantial educational support for both instructors and students, expanding the utility of intelligent dental education. However, there is still room to promote the proper use of LLMs among dental students and educators to prevent misuse and unintended consequences.

Technical and application challenges of LLMs in dental education

The continuous advancement of technology has kept LLMs in a state of rapid iteration and evolution. However, their implementation in educational settings is accompanied by various technical and application-related challenges. Among these, the most frequently reported technical issue is the phenomenon of ‘hallucination’, which LLMs generate unverified, inaccurate, or illogical content. This issue is generally attributed to limitations in training data and the inherently probabilistic nature of language generation.40 Such inaccuracies pose significant risks in dental education,9 For instance, Danesh et al38 found that ChatGPT-3.5 exhibited a high error rate in periodontal assessments, with longer responses correlating positively with the number of inaccuracies. Similarly, Kavadella et al20 reported that LLMs occasionally produced fabricated citations or nonexistent references, which may mislead students and undermine academic integrity. Brozovic et al26 further observed that the sources cited by LLMs were often drawn from commercial websites or advertisements rather than peer-reviewed scholarly literature. In addition, Jaworski et al41 demonstrated that LLMs performed poorly when confronted with complex clinical cases requiring deep reasoning and contextual understanding, thereby compromising their reliability in high-demanding educational scenarios. Therefore, future research should focus on how LLMs can generate accurate and high-quality information, as well as on developing strategies to assist users to avoid misinformation caused by hallucination.

Another notable technical limitation is that many LLMs are currently restricted to text-based inputs and cannot process visual data.20,31 Although some multimodal models have recently emerged, empirical studies indicate that their performance on image-based tasks remains inferior to that on text-based questions.30,32 This limitation is particularly consequential in dental education, where radiographs and clinical visual imagery such as the identification of caries on X-rays are essential components of diagnostic training. The lack of robust multimodal analytical capabilities thus constrains the broader utility of LLMs in this field. Moreover, the commercialization of LLMs introduces further barriers to their integration into dental education. Other common challenges include restricted access, server latency, and high subscription costs.33,38 These issues significantly increase the cost associated with refining or training LLMs for domain-specific applications, thus limiting the full utilization of the benefits offered by open-source and cloud-based deployment.

In addition to technical challenges, the implementation of LLMs in educational practice raises numerous ethical, legal, and pedagogical concerns. Prominent among these are the risks associated with the dissemination of inaccurate information, which may mislead learners,22 instances of academic misconduct such as plagiarism,36 and the potential for biased or unjust grading practices that could adversely affect students’ long-term development and psychological well-being.16,35 In high-stakes assessments, such as national licensing exams designed to ensure that candidates possess adequate clinical knowledge and skills for safe clinical practice, the unauthorized uses of LLMs during the exams could compromise academic integrity, threaten the fairness of credentialing processes, and erode public trust in professional qualifications. Furthermore, the issue of copyright infringement concerning AI-generated content remains unresolved.22 Given the rigorous standards inherent in medical and dental education, such concerns have fostered a cautious and sceptical attitude towards the adoption of LLMs among educators and stakeholders.22 While there is still a lack of studies addressing the ethical and legal issues related to the use of LLMs in dental education, this limitation hampers the development of guidelines for proper implementation. The educational institutions and examination boards must develop robust policies and technological safeguards to prevent the ethical misuse of LLMs.

The central aim of dental education is to cultivate clinicians capable of independently managing complex cases. An overdependence on LLMs may hinder the development of critical thinking and autonomous decision-making skills, potentially giving rise to an illusion of competence.24,41 Moreover, in the absence of clear, context-specific guidelines for appropriate use, LLMs may be misapplied by students, educators, or administrators.33 To address these challenges, a collaborative approach is essential. Stakeholders, including dental students, educators, curriculum administrators, and AI engineers, must work collectively to establish comprehensive, evidence-based frameworks for the ethical and effective integration of LLMs into dental education.

Future perspectives on LLMs in dental education

Based on current evidence, it is clear that dental education is entering an era shaped by the integration of LLMs. By synthesizing current evidence, this review outlines future directions and offers recommendations for the application of LLMs across key stakeholder groups, including dental students, educators, curriculum administrators, and technology developers.

For dental students, LLMs function as virtual learning companions and overcome spatial and temporal constraints by offering continuous, location-independent support and real-time feedback.35,37 These tools can assist students throughout the entire learning cycle, from preparation, active engagement, to review. In alignment with the clinical training progression from student, intern to specialist trainee, LLMs may also simulate authentic clinical scenarios, enabling students to engage in interactive, experiential learning.42 Such applications can potentially strengthen evidence-based practice, critical thinking, and clinical problem-solving skills, provided that students cross-check AI-generated information and refrain from over-reliance.19

For dental educators, LLMs provide opportunities to deliver personalized instruction. Traditional pedagogical models often adopt standardized approaches aimed at the average learner, whereas individualized guidance tailored to students’ specific needs can be resource-intensive. By leveraging their data-processing and adaptive capabilities, LLMs can help generate tailored learning plans, develop assessments with appropriate difficulty levels, and deliver individualized feedback.35,36,43 However, preserving a learner-centred approach that incorporates emotional and interpersonal support is crucial to ensure that teaching does not devolve into impersonal information delivery.37

For curriculum administrators, integrating LLMs into course design offers opportunities to enhance both efficiency and pedagogical innovation.20 LLMs can support flipped classroom models, produce diverse instructional materials, and provide timely, objective in-class assessments. In addition, curriculum developers should explore novel instructional strategies such as LLM-supported case-based learning. A hybrid model that balances conventional and LLM-enhanced methodologies is essential to capitalize on the strengths of both paradigms.19 The integration of LLMs into dental education aligns with educational frameworks like constructivist learning and self-directed learning.44,45 For example, by engaging in interactive dialogue with the LLMs, dental students can set personal learning goals, simulate clinical reasoning processes, and thus promote deeper understanding rather than rote memorization. Moreover, the strategic deployment of LLMs can help address disparities in educational resources across institutions,35 and support the training of future dental educators through the provision of high-quality AI-generated teaching content.27

For technology developers, it is imperative to design tiered, user-friendly interfaces and provide practical guidance tailored to the diverse needs of different user groups.24 User feedback should be systematically collected to inform the development of an ethically responsible and legally compliant usage framework. Future technological advancements, such as improved prompting techniques,25,33 enhanced interpretability,15 deeper clinical reasoning capabilities, and domain-specific fine-tuning, must be incorporated to maximize the pedagogical impact of LLMs in dental education.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the considerable heterogeneity among the included studies precluded conducting a meta-analysis. Future quantitative studies should focus on more homogeneous tasks, such as LLM performance in dental examinations. Second, constraints in the search strategy may have led to the exclusion of recently developed models, such as DeepSeek, which could provide new insights into the integration of intelligent technologies in dental education. It is expected that future reviews should update relevant information about the latest developments in LLMs. Third, this scoping review did not include grey literature or publications from technical databases (eg, IEEE Xplore), as its focus was more aligned with dental and educational research. However, commercial applications of LLMs in educational area may involve more comprehensive and up-to-date information. Therefore, it is possible that some relevant technical or early-stage studies were not included due to these limitations. These limitations warrant consideration in future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, LLMs have demonstrated promising potential across various domains of dental education, including examination support and clinical training. Despite these encouraging findings, persistent technical and practical challenges underscore the necessity for collaborative efforts involving dental students, educators, curriculum administers, and technology experts. Moving forward, it is imperative to establish a comprehensive and clearly defined framework to ensure the responsible, effective, and ethical integration of LLMs into dental education, thereby laying the foundation for a transformative educational paradigm.
