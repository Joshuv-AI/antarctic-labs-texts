HAR-Net: Automatic delineation of clinical target volumes for cervical cancer guided by hierarchical anatomical refinement
Authors: 婷婷 程, 俍 邓, 瞻 梁, 杜 唐, 子健 张
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIn radiotherapy for cervical cancer, accurate delineation of the clinical target volume (CTV) is essential for ensuring prescription dose coverage while minimizing radiation exposure to organs at risk (OARs). In clinical practice, however, CTV boundaries are often poorly defined on standard medical images and exhibit substantial morphological variability. Consequently, automated methods based solely on image appearance or a single segmentation framework may fail to consistently capture the fine-grained anatomical structures and spatial coherence of the CTV. Existing methods are therefore prone to boundary displacement, morphological discontinuity, and inadequate generalizability across patients, which may compromise the reproducibility and clinical applicability of radiotherapy planning. This study aims to develop a hierarchical anatomical refinement network (HAR-Net) for automatic CTV delineation in cervical cancer. By explicitly integrating anatomical priors and spatial relationships in addition to image appearance information, HAR-Net was designed to improve segmentation accuracy and consistency.MethodsComputed tomography images and manual delineations of the CTV and 5 OARs, the bladder, rectum, bilateral femoral heads, and small bowel, were retrospectively collected from 132 patients with cervical cancer who received radiotherapy at Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, between May 2022 and May 2025. All patients had stage IB2-IVA disease according to the 2018 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system. The delineations were completed by experienced radiation oncologists. Patients were randomly divided into a training set (n=92), validation set (n=15), and test set (n=25). HAR-Net used nnU-Net as its backbone and incorporated 2 collaborative modules. First, multi-scale OAR-CTV cross-attention (MOCCA) dynamically integrated the anatomical context of OARs into the CTV segmentation pathway through window-based three-dimensional cross-attention. Second, tri-axial coordinate attention (TCA) encoded global positional information along the depth, height, and width axes and spatially refined the features through adaptive reweighting. Segmentation performance was evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), and average symmetric surface distance (ASSD). HAR-Net was compared with several mainstream segmentation methods, including MultiResUNet, AMTA-Net, Swin UNETR, ResUNet-a, and nnU-Net.ResultsHAR-Net outperformed multiple state-of-the-art segmentation methods. It achieved a mean DSC of 82.420%, an HD95 of 8.390 mm, and an ASSD of 1.920 mm. Compared to the nnU-Net baseline, HAR-Net improved the DSC by 3.260 percentage points and reduced the HD95 by 4.080 mm. Ablation experiments further demonstrated that both the MOCCA and TCA modules contributed significantly to the improvement in model performance.ConclusionBy hierarchically integrating multi-scale anatomical context from OARs with tri-axial spatial priors, HAR-Net effectively improves the accuracy and anatomical consistency of automatic CTV delineation for cervical cancer. This framework may reduce the need for repeated manual corrections during clinical delineation, decrease the risk of unnecessary radiation exposure to surrounding OARs, and enhance the overall quality and safety of radiotherapy planning. HAR-Net therefore represents a promising auxiliary tool for automated radiotherapy planning.

Accurate delineation of the clinical target volume (CTV) is a cornerstone of effective radiotherapy planning for cervical cancer, directly influencing treatment efficacy and patient outcomes[1-2]. The CTV is conceptualized not merely as an expansion of the visible gross tumor volume but as a clinically defined volume encompassing regions presumed to contain microscopic disease. Unlike organs with visually distinct borders, CTV boundaries are often indistinct on standard imaging modalities like computed tomography (CT). Their delineation relies critically on the interpretation of complex anatomical relationships and strict adherence to consensus guidelines rather than direct grayscale variations in the image[3]. This fundamental characteristic—that CTV is a “virtual” volume defined by anatomical understanding and clinical protocols—poses a significant challenge for manual contouring, leading to inter-observer variability and consuming considerable clinical resources[4-5]. Consequently, developing robust and accurate automated CTV delineation methods is paramount for improving treatment consistency, efficiency, and potentially, patient prognosis by minimizing radiation toxicity to surrounding organs at risk (OARs) while ensuring adequate target coverage[6].

Deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has revolutionized computer vision since seminal works like Krizhevsky, et al[7] demonstrated their power on large-scale image classification, and studies such as Simonyan, et al[8] highlighted the importance of network depth. This success rapidly translated to medical image analysis. The application of CNNs to dense prediction tasks such as segmentation was significantly advanced by architectures like fully convolutional networks (FCNs)[9], paving the way for models like the U-Net[10], its 3D extension[11], and their subsequent variants, which have demonstrated considerable success in various medical image segmentation tasks[12-16]. However, the unique, guideline-driven nature of CTV delineation presents specific challenges that generic segmentation architectures, which primarily learn from visual patterns, often fail to adequately address. Because CTV boundaries are not always visually apparent and are defined by intricate anatomical context and clinical rules, standard models struggle to internalize and apply this crucial prior knowledge. While some approaches have attempted to incorporate anatomical priors, such as multi-task learning for OAR segmentation[17] or attention mechanisms focusing on specific regions[18], they often lack the mechanisms for fine-grained, multi-scale spatial reasoning or explicit enforcement of boundary adherence to clinical guidelines. The robust integration of complex anatomical knowledge—such as precise margin definitions relative to specific OARs or ensuring consistent delineation along key structural landmarks (e.g., the psoas muscles) as dictated by protocols—into end-to-end trainable networks remains an open research area. There is a pressing need for models that not only segment the CTV but also inherently understand and respect these critical anatomical constraints to produce clinically valid and reliable contours.

Deep learning approaches, particularly architectures based on U-Net and its variants, have demonstrated significant potential in medical image segmentation tasks. Key early architectures such as V-Net also paved the way for volumetric medical image segmentation[19]. However, CTV delineation for cervical cancer presents unique challenges compared to traditional organ segmentation. CTVs represent virtual volumes encompassing potential microscopic disease extensions, often with inherently ambiguous boundaries on standard imaging like CT, making them difficult to segment accurately[3]. Various research efforts have attempted to address these complexities through innovative architectural designs and methodological improvements.

Several studies have explored sophisticated network architectures specifically tailored for cervical cancer CTV segmentation. For instance, Ma, et al[20] developed a three-channel adaptive auto-segmentation algorithm that leverages enhanced CT information to improve boundary delineation. To address the common challenge of limited annotated data, Peng, et al[21] introduced an innovative multi-decoder semi-supervised learning approach. The applicability of deep learning has also been demonstrated for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based CTV segmentation, highlighting the potential for multi-modal imaging integration[3].

Advanced architectural innovations continue to emerge. The field has seen a progression toward transformer-based architectures and, more recently, state-space models. For example, Mamba-based frameworks have been investigated for rapid and precise high-risk CTV delineation in cervical cancer, showcasing the potential of these newer models[22]. These developments aim to better capture the complex spatial relationships inherent in CTVs.

The integration of anatomical prior knowledge is increasingly recognized as crucial for overcoming the limitations of purely visual pattern-based approaches. Khor, et al[23] developed anatomically constrained attention-guided networks that explicitly incorporate spatial relationships during training. Similarly, approaches like the prior-knowledge-guided U-Net (PK-UNet) aim to systematically integrate anatomical understanding into network architectures to guide the segmentation process, although its initial validation was in breast cancer[24].

Despite these promising developments, a fundamental challenge persists: CTVs are conceptually defined volumes whose boundaries depend heavily on anatomical interpretation and adherence to clinical guidelines rather than solely on direct visual cues in medical images. Current automated approaches can struggle with maintaining consistency and accuracy in regions where visual information is insufficient, potentially leading to clinically suboptimal contours. This underscores the continuing need for more sophisticated integration of anatomical knowledge and clinical reasoning into automated segmentation frameworks to ensure the generation of clinically valid and reliable CTV delineations.

Recognizing the limitations of purely image-driven segmentation, there has been a growing research emphasis on integrating anatomical prior knowledge into deep learning frameworks. This integration aims to address the inherent challenges of delineating structures that are guided more by anatomical understanding than by explicit image features[25]. Such anatomical intelligence can be incorporated at various stages, including data preprocessing, model architecture design, and post-processing refinement.

In the preprocessing stage, anatomical priors can inform the selection of regions of interest or enhance features critical for segmentation. For example, Zhang, et al[26] demonstrated a prior knowledge-guided deep learning approach where anatomical insights were used to construct improved input representations for segmenting complex abdominal structures, leading to better consistency.

During model design, several strategies allow for the direct embedding of anatomical constraints. Dalca, et al[27] utilized variational autoencoders to model expected anatomical configurations, guiding networks to maintain structural plausibility, particularly in ambiguous image regions. Multi-task learning presents another effective method; Boutillon, et al[28] showed that concurrently learning related anatomical segmentation tasks can enhance the primary segmentation by leveraging shared anatomical understanding. Similarly, Xu, et al[29] developed asymmetric multi-task attention network (AMTA-Net), an asymmetrical multi-task framework where a U-Net first segments foundational OARs (bladder and rectum). A cascaded attention mechanism then integrates multi-level OAR features and segmentation masks to guide prostate bed delineation, explicitly modeling the clinical reliance on OAR context for this challenging target volume. Furthermore, modifying loss functions to include anatomical constraints, such as those encouraging shape preservation, has shown considerable promise in maintaining the structural integrity of segmentations[30].

Post-processing techniques offer a final opportunity to enforce anatomical consistency. Mlynarski, et al[31] developed a method for CNN-based segmentation that applies topological constraints and shape regularization to ensure the final contours conform to expected anatomical properties. Hybrid approaches, which combine initial deep learning predictions with anatomically informed active contour models, have also been effective in refining boundary precision[32].

Despite these varied and advancing strategies for incorporating anatomical priors, comprehensively modeling the complex, multi-scale anatomical relationships crucial for accurate CTV delineation remains a significant challenge. Effectively capturing the intricate spatial dependencies between the CTV and surrounding OARs, while simultaneously ensuring adherence to clinical margin definitions and consistency along key anatomical axes, is an ongoing pursuit[23]. These persisting limitations highlight the need for novel frameworks that can more deeply embed anatomical understanding directly within the network architecture to produce clinically robust and reliable CTV segmentations.

In summary, while deep learning, particularly U-Net architectures and their derivatives, has advanced automated CTV segmentation for cervical cancer, a critical gap remains. The inherent nature of CTV—defined by anatomical relationships and clinical guidelines rather than solely by image appearance—necessitates a more profound integration of anatomical intelligence than current standard approaches offer. Existing studies have highlighted various strategies for incorporating prior knowledge. However, challenges persist in comprehensively modeling multi-scale contextual interactions with OARs, ensuring consistency along key anatomical axes (such as psoas muscles), and explicitly enforcing adherence to clinical margin requirements and boundary sharpness.

To address these limitations, the hierarchical anatomical refinement network (HAR-Net) is proposed, a novel multi-stage framework designed for precise and clinically informed CTV delineation in cervical cancer. HAR-Net progressively refines the CTV segmentation by explicitly modeling and leveraging hierarchical anatomical relationships. It begins with a robust nnU-Net-based backbone for multi-scale 3D feature extraction. The core of this approach lies in 2 novel components: 1) Multi-scale OAR-CTV cross-attention (MOCCA) module, which dynamically incorporates OAR-specific contextual information at multiple spatial scales into the primary CTV segmentation pathway using windowed 3D cross-attention enhanced with learned relative positional biases. 2) Tri-axial coordinate attention (TCA) module further refines the segmentation by adaptively reweighting features based on learned tri-axial spatial priors, particularly relevant for consistent delineation along anatomical structures such as the psoas muscles.

Overall HAR-net framework: Integrating hierarchical anatomical knowledge

This study introduced the HAR-Net (Figure 1), a novel deep learning framework designed to learn a mapping H from an input 3D CT volume I∈RD× H× W to a corresponding CTV segmentation mask SCTV∈{0, 1}D× H× W , where D, H, W are the depth, height, and width of the volume. HAR-Net achieves this by progressively integrating hierarchical anatomical knowledge through a multi-stage refinement process. The core philosophy involves an initial stage of OAR-contextualization, followed by a refinement stage incorporating global structural priors. This hierarchical transformation is formulated as SCTV= ℋI= frefinefcontextualizefextractI , where fextract represents initial feature extraction, fcontextualize embodies OAR-CTV interaction modeling, and frefine applies axis-aware refinements.

Proposed architecture

Framework processes a 3D input CT volume through a shared nnU-Net encoder φbackbone to extract multi-level pyramidal features. These features diverge into 2 parallel decoding pathways: An auxiliary OAR decoder φOAR , which provides anatomical context, and the primary CTV decoder. The MOCCA module bridges these pathways at 3 hierarchical resolutions (e.g., 1/8, 1/4, 1/2), explicitly modeling the spatial relationship between OAR and the CTV via windowed 3D cross-attention. Finally, the aggregated features undergo global structural refinement via the TCA module φTCA to enforce axis-aware anatomical consistency before the final segmentation head generates the CTV probability map. HAR-Net implements this hierarchical strategy via a set of pivotal architectural modules. It begins with a robust nnU-Net-based backbone (responsible for fextract ) for rich multi-scale 3D feature extraction from I . This backbone employs an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections, forming the basis of fBB and the encoder part of fOAR , following the principles of the nnU-Net framework[12], to effectively capture hierarchical features. Building upon this, the novel MOCCA module dynamically integrates OAR-specific contextual information from the fOAR decoder into the fBB decoder (a key part of fcontextualize ), producing OAR-aware features FMOCCA (representing the output of the MOCCA-enhanced decoder). Following this, the TCA module (contributing to frefine ) further refines these features to yield FTCA , by adaptively reweighting them based on learned tri-axial spatial priors, promoting consistency along critical anatomical axes. The final CTV segmentation SCTV is derived from the refined features FTCA after TCA processing.

The synergistic operation of these components facilitates the learning and application of anatomical intelligence at different levels of abstraction, from local OAR interactions to global structural rules.

MOCCA module: MOCCA with learned positional biases

CTV delineation is intrinsically linked to the localization and morphology of adjacent OARs. To model these dependencies, the MOCCA module is introduced, a component of HAR-Net (Figure 2). MOCCA dynamically integrates OAR-specific contextual information into the CTV segmentation pathway at multiple spatial scales within HAR-Net’s decoder arm. This ensures learned relationships are sensitive to varying levels of feature abstraction.

Detailed architecture of the MOCCA module

Input CTV features ( FCTVs ) and OAR features ( FOARs ) undergo 3D windowing. Queries (Q) are derived from FCTVs via linear projection. Keys (K) and Values (V) are similarly derived from FOARs . Windowed 3D cross-attention is performed, incorporating learned 3D relative positional biases ( Bij ). The attention output is added to the original FCTVs features via a residual connection, followed by 2 3D convolutional layers to produce enhanced CTV features; conv: Convolutional layer.

Let FCTVs∈RCs× Ds× Hs× Ws denote the feature maps from the CTV decoding pathway at a given scale s , where Cs, Ds, Hs, Ws are the channels, depth, height, and width, respectively. Similarly, FOARs represents corresponding feature map from a parallel OAR-focused decoding stream, providing salient information regarding the OARs at the same scale. The MOCCA module takes these 2 sets of feature maps as input at each relevant decoder stage. The OAR features FOARs are obtained from a dedicated OAR decoder that shares the same encoder architecture as the main CTV pathway but is specifically trained to segment relevant OARs.

The core mechanism of the MOCCA module is a windowed 3D cross-attention operation. Input feature maps FCTVs and FOARs , which are projected to the same channel dimension Cs' , are partitioned into non-overlapping 3D windows of size 8×8×8 voxels. Within each window, feature vectors are extracted. Specifically, queries ( Q∈ RN× dq ) are derived via a linear projection from the windowed FCTVs tokens, while keys ( K∈RM× dk ) and values ( V∈RM× dv ) are derived via separate linear projections from the windowed FOARs tokens. Here, N= M= 83= 512 represents the number of tokens (voxels) per window. The query, key, and value dimensions ( dq, dk, dv ) are set to Cs' . A single attention head is employed to balance model complexity and efficacy for these localized interactions. This localized attention strategy significantly reduces the quadratic complexity associated with global self-attention on large 3D volumes, making it computationally tractable while concentrating the learning on inter-structural relationships to spatially pertinent sub-volumes where direct OAR-CTV influence is most anatomically relevant. The window size of 8×8×8 voxels was chosen based on anatomical scale matching and computational efficiency: Given a typical voxel spacing of approximately 0.98 mm× 0.98 mm× 3.00 mm , this window corresponds to a physical extent of roughly 7.8 mm×7.8 mm× 24.0 mm , covering the critical OAR-CTV interface range (typically 10-30 mm). Smaller windows (e.g., 4×4×4 , n=64) provide insufficient receptive fields, while larger windows (e.g., 16×16×16 , n=4 096) incur approximately 64× the computational cost with diminishing returns due to attention dilution. The output of this attention mechanism—a set of OAR-contextualized value vectors—is then integrated with the original CTV features FCTVs (from which Q was derived). This fusion is realized through a residual connection, which adds the attention output to FCTVs . This fused representation subsequently passes through a residual block comprising 2 sequential units of [3D Convolution (kernel 3×3×3 , stride 1, same padding) → Instance Normalization → ReLU activation]. These subsequent convolutions further process and refine the integrated features, producing an enriched representation F'CTVs that robustly combines the primary image features with the learned OAR-CTV spatial priors. The MOCCA module is implemented at 3 specific decoder stages, corresponding to resolution scales of 1/8, 1/4, and 1/2 of the input volume, namely decoder Stage 3 (1/8 resolution, 256 channels), decoder Stage 4 (1/4 resolution, 128 channels), and decoder Stage 5 (1/2 resolution, 64 channels), establishing a hierarchical refinement pattern that progressively integrates anatomical context from coarse to fine resolutions. Stage 3, with the largest receptive field, captures global spatial relationships between CTV and distant OARs (e.g., femoral heads). Stage 4 models mid-scale organ distance and orientation constraints (e.g., CTV relative to bladder and rectum). Stage 5, at near-original resolution, refines fine-grained boundary interactions. The earlier decoder stages (Stage 0 - 2) operate at 1/32 to 1/16 resolution, where excessive spatial compression renders precise spatial relationship modeling impractical. This multi-scale implementation facilitates the development of a resolution-dependent understanding of OAR-CTV spatial interdependencies, addressing anatomical constraints that define CTV boundaries. Encoding these structural relationships across multiple scales is designed to improve boundary precision and anatomical fidelity in the final CTV delineation.

Regarding how MOCCA learns clinically relevant spatial relationships, the cross-attention weights softmaxQKT/dk+ B encode the degree to which each CTV voxel attends to OAR voxels. During training, these weights are optimized via backpropagation and converge toward high attention at OAR-CTV interfaces and low attention in distant regions. The 3D relative positional bias table εpos further encodes directional attention preferences—for instance, different biases along the anterior-posterior axis (bladder-CTV interface) versus the superior-inferior axis (small bowel-CTV interface), reflecting the model’s learned directional anatomical constraints.

TCA module: TCA for global structural refinement

While the MOCCA module effectively captures local OAR-CTV contextual information, precise CTV delineation often necessitates adherence to global anatomical rules and consistent orientations along the principal body axes (e.g., superior-inferior, anterior-posterior, left-right). For instance, CTV boundaries may need to align with elongated structures like the psoas muscles or follow specific cranio-caudal extents. Standard convolutional layers, due to their inherently local receptive fields, often do not effectively capture these long-range, axis-aligned dependencies.

To address this, the TCA module is introduced, a 3D adaptation of the Coordinate Attention mechanism[35]. As illustrated in Figure 3, the TCA module is designed to explicitly model long-range dependencies and encode precise positional information along each spatial axis. It processes the feature maps that have been contextualized by OAR interactions through the preceding MOCCA modules within the decoder pathway. This allows the network to selectively emphasize informative features based on global, axis-aware anatomical context. The TCA module operates in 3 main steps: Axis-wise feature encoding, tri-axial attention generation, and feature re-weighting.

Training strategy

The HAR-Net framework, including the nnU-Net-based backbone, the MOCCA modules, the TCA module, and the final segmentation head, is trained in an end-to-end fashion. The training objective is to learn the complete mapping from an input 3D CT volume I to the final CTV segmentation mask SCTV , by optimizing all network parameters simultaneously.

During the forward pass, an input CT volume I is processed through the following stages.

1) Feature extraction: I is fed into the nnU-Net-based backbone ( fBB ), which extracts multi-scale hierarchical features through its encoder-decoder structure.

2) OAR context integration via MOCCA: At specific decoder stages (resolutions 1/8, 1/4, and 1/2 of the input volume, as detailed in Section 1.3), the CTV pathway features from fBB are combined with corresponding OAR-specific features (derived from fOAR ). The MOCCA module at each of these scales takes the CTV and OAR features as input, performing windowed 3D cross-attention with learned relative positional biases. This yields OAR-contextualized CTV features F'CTVs at each scale s . The output of a MOCCA module at a coarser scale is upsampled and concatenated with skip-connection features from the encoder before potentially being processed by a MOCCA module at a finer scale.

3) Global axial refinement via TCA: The feature map resulting from the deepest (or final) MOCCA-enhanced decoder stage, denoted as FMOCCA_out , is then passed to the TCA module. As described in Section 1.4, the TCA module applies axis-wise feature encoding, generates tri-axial attention weights, and reweights FMOCCA_out to produce globally refined features FTCA . This step allows the network to emphasize features consistent with learned long-range, axis-aligned anatomical priors.

4) Final segmentation output: The refined features FTCA from the TCA module are channeled into a final segmentation head, which comprises a 1×1×1 convolution followed by a sigmoid activation function for binary CTV segmentation. This produces a probability map, subsequently thresholded at 0.5 during inference to yield the final binary CTV segmentation mask SCTV .

Participants and experimental setup

Participants and datasets

The primary dataset used in this study was retrospectively collected from 132 patients diagnosed with cervical cancer undergoing radiotherapy at Xiangya Hospital of Central South University between May 2022 and May 2025.

Inclusion criteria: 1) Histologically confirmed cervical cancer (squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, or adenosquamous carcinoma); 2) International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) 2018 stage IB2-IVA[36]; 3) planned for definitive or adjuvant external beam radiotherapy (EBRT); 4) available planning CT with standard acquisition protocol; 5) complete CTV and OAR delineations by senior radiation oncologists. Exclusion criteria: 1) Prior pelvic radiation therapy; 2) significant motion or metallic artifacts on CT; 3) incomplete or missing contour data; 4) concurrent non-cervical pelvic malignancy.

CT images were acquired using a SOMATOM Definition AS scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany) with a tube voltage of 120 kV and auto-modulated tube current (120 mAs). Slice thickness was 3 mm, and images were reconstructed with a voxel spacing of approximately 0.98 mm×0.98 mm×3.00 mm . Scans covered the region from the upper border of L2 vertebra to the inferior border of the ischial tuberosity.

CTVs and 5 relevant OARs (bladder, rectum, left femoral head, right femoral head, and small bowel) were manually delineated following RTOG consensus guideline[33] on planning CT scans. All contours were independently delineated by 2 radiation oncologists with more than 5 years of pelvic radiotherapy experience; discrepancies were resolved by a senior physician with over 10 years of experience.

The dataset was randomly split into training (92 cases), validation (15 cases), and test (25 cases) sets with no patient overlap, stratified by FIGO stage to ensure consistent stage distribution across subsets.

Implementation details

The HAR-Net framework was implemented using PyTorch (Version 2.6.0). Training was conducted on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 Ti GPUs, each with 24 GB of VRAM. Network parameters were optimized using the AdamW optimizer, initialized with a learning rate of 2×10-4 and a weight decay of 5×10-4 . A multi-step learning rate schedule was employed to adjust the learning rate during training.

To enhance model robustness and mitigate overfitting, extensive online data augmentation was applied, encompassing random horizontal and vertical flipping, random rotation, random contrast adjustments, and random translations and scaling. Prior to network input, CT volumes underwent pre-processing, which involved clipping Hounsfield unit (HU) values to the relevant range for soft-tissue visualization, followed by normalization. The network was trained using randomly extracted 3D patches, with patch dimensions ( 64×128×128 ) determined by nnU-Net heuristics based on dataset characteristics, and a batch size of 2 was used. The training typically extended for 1 000 epochs for the initial stage. The optimization process was guided by the composite loss function Ltotal [Equation (11)], which combines the primary CTV segmentation loss LCTV [Equation (10)] with an auxiliary loss LOAR_aux derived from OAR segmentations. The loss weights were set to λ1=1.0 and λ2=0.5 . The learning rate followed a multi-step schedule with decay at epochs 500 and 800 ( γ=0.1 ). HU values were clipped to [-175, 250] for soft tissue visualization.

Compared methods (baselines)

To contextualize the performance of HAR-Net, it was benchmarked against several state-of-the-art deep learning architectures prevalent in medical image segmentation. These included:

1) MultiResUNet[13]: A multi-resolution U-Net architecture for biomedical image segmentation (adapted to the present 3D CTV task).

2) NnU-Net[12]: A self-configuring framework that automatically adapts U-Net-based architectures to various medical segmentation tasks, often serving as a strong baseline. This forms the backbone of our HAR-Net.

3) AMTA-Net[29]: An asymmetrical multi-task framework leveraging OAR context for challenging target volume segmentation.

4) Swin UNETR[15]: A Transformer-based model that uses a Transformer encoder with a U-Net-like decoder structure for 3D medical image segmentation.

5) ResUNet-a[16]: A 3D CNN architecture for enhanced feature learning.

For all baseline methods, we utilized their publicly available implementations where possible and trained them on our dataset using similar data pre-processing and augmentation strategies as HAR-Net to ensure a fair comparison. Hyper-parameters were tuned according to their respective papers or default configurations if specific tuning guidelines were unavailable for our dataset characteristics. The nnU-Net baseline corresponds to the output of the standard nnU-Net framework without our proposed MOCCA and TCA modules.

Ablation experiment

To assess the individual contributions of the MOCCA and TCA modules, ablation experiments were performed. The configurations evaluated were: 1) Baseline (nnU-Net backbone), 2) Baseline+MOCCA, 3) Baseline+TCA, and 4) HAR-Net (Baseline+MOCCA+TCA).

The results (Table 2 and Figure 6) illustrated the impact of each component. The baseline nnU-Net established the reference performance. Integrating the MOCCA module improved the DSC and ASSD, though HD95 slightly increased, highlighting the general benefit of OAR-CTV contextualization. The addition of the TCA module alone to the baseline yielded improved surface distance metrics. The complete HAR-Net model, incorporating both MOCCA and TCA modules, achieved the best overall performance. This demonstrated a synergistic effect, where the combination of MOCCA and tri-axial coordinate attention led to substantial improvements in segmentation accuracy. We further examined the window size of the MOCCA module (Table 3) and the activation function in TCA (Table 4). The intermediate window size configuration yielded the highest DSC and the lowest surface distances, and was therefore adopted. Among the tested activation functions, Hard-Swish consistently outperformed ReLU and GELU across all metrics (Table 4), justifying its selection in the final model.

Discussion

This study introduced HAR-Net, a deep learning framework designed for precise CTV delineation in cervical cancer. HAR-Net addresses the inherent challenge of segmenting CTVs, which are often defined by complex anatomical relationships and consensus guidelines rather than distinct image features, by hierarchically integrating anatomical knowledge. This is primarily achieved through its novel MOCCA module and the TCA module, built upon a robust nnU-Net backbone.

Experimental results indicated that HAR-Net substantially outperformed several state-of-the-art methods in DSC, HD95, and ASSD. HAR-Net achieved a DSC of (82.420±5.420)%, an HD95 of (8.390±6.120) mm, and an ASSD of (1.920±0.980) mm. This represents a notable improvement over the strong nnU-Net baseline, with an increase in DSC by 3.26 percentage points and significant reductions in HD95 by 4.080 mm and ASSD by 0.650 mm. When compared to AMTA-Net, another method that leverages OAR context, HAR-Net demonstrated superior performance across all metrics. This suggests that HAR-Net’s explicit multi-scale OAR feature integration via MOCCA, combined with the global refinement from TCA, provides a more effective approach to anatomical prior incorporation than the cascaded attention mechanism used in AMTA-Net. The consistent improvements by HAR-Net are particularly noteworthy given the inherent variability observed in surface distance metrics (HD95, ASSD) across all benchmarked methods. This underlying variability, reflected in the standard deviations (e.g., nnU-Net HD95 standard deviation: 7.380 mm; HAR-Net HD95 standard deviation: 6.120 mm), underscores the complexity of achieving uniformly precise CTV boundary delineation, likely stemming from factors such as subtle inter-patient anatomical differences, indistinct tumor margins on CT, and the guideline-driven nature of CTVs. While HAR-Net achieved the most favorable mean HD95 (8.390 mm), indicating superior average boundary accuracy, its associated standard deviation (6.120 mm) warrants consideration. This suggests that while HAR-Net is, on average, highly accurate, a subset of cases might still exhibit larger boundary deviations. This is a common challenge in automated segmentation, highlighting the ongoing need for refinement in worst-case scenario robustness, even with advanced architectures. Nevertheless, the average improvements in DSC, HD95, and ASSD are promising for clinical translation, potentially leading to more reliable target coverage and OAR sparing.

The ablation experiments provide critical insights into the interplay of HAR-Net’s components and their individual contributions. The baseline nnU-Net achieved a DSC of (77.920±6.280)%, HD95 of (13.290±6.920) mm, and ASSD of (2.470±1.030) mm. The MOCCA module, when integrated alone (Baseline+MOCCA), yielded an improved DSC and a slightly higher ASSD over this baseline. This underscores the value of explicitly modeling OAR-CTV spatial relationships at multiple scales for better volumetric overlap. However, a notable observation was the degradation in HD95 [(14.140±7.920) mm vs (13.290±6.920) mm for baseline] with a concurrent increase in its standard deviation when MOCCA was used in isolation. This “DSC improves but HD95 degrades” phenomenon arises from the fundamentally different measurement dimensions of these metrics. DSC quantifies global volumetric overlap and is sensitive to overall segmentation accuracy; HD95 captures the 95th percentile worst-case surface distance and is highly sensitive to localized boundary errors. MOCCA effectively increases correctly predicted voxels (improving DSC) by incorporating OAR contextual features. However, in anatomically complex OAR-CTV interface regions (e.g., areas with variable bladder filling), uncertainties in OAR feature representations can propagate through cross-attention weights to CTV boundary predictions, inducing localized over- or under-segmentation. These sporadic large deviations, while negligible for global DSC, are acutely captured by HD95. When TCA is subsequently added (full HAR-Net), its global axis-wise structural priors effectively regularize these local boundary perturbations, reducing HD95 dramatically to 8.390 mm. This “local enhancement followed by global constraint” hierarchy produces the observed synergy: MOCCA enhances volumetric accuracy, while TCA ensures boundary consistency.

This suggests that while local OAR context is powerful, it might, without subsequent global regularization, occasionally lead to larger localized boundary errors. This could occur if OAR features become overly influential in regions of ambiguity or if upstream OAR segmentations themselves possessed inaccuracies. Conversely, the TCA module alone (Baseline+TCA) demonstrated its specific strength in improving surface distance metrics relative to the baseline+MOCCA, consistent with its design to enforce long-range, axis-aligned structural priors relevant to anatomical features like the psoas muscles. The DSC with TCA alone (78.470±6.070) was slightly better than baseline, but did not reach the level of MOCCA alone, possibly reflecting a trade-off where global smoothing for anatomical consistency could slightly impact fine voxel-level agreement if not complemented by strong local context.

Crucially, the complete HAR-Net model (Baseline+MOCCA+TCA) not only achieved the best overall performance but also demonstrated a strong synergistic effect. The inclusion of TCA alongside MOCCA rectified the HD95 degradation seen with MOCCA alone, reducing it significantly to 8.390 mm, and simultaneously uplifted the DSC beyond what either module achieved individually. This robustly supports the hypothesis that a hierarchical approach—integrating local OAR-derived context (MOCCA) followed by global, axis-aware structural refinement (TCA)—is essential for achieving both high volumetric accuracy and precise, anatomically consistent CTV boundaries. This explicit, two-stage refinement strategy distinguishes HAR-Net from approaches that rely on more implicit learning of anatomical context or employ general attention mechanisms without specific adaptations for OAR-CTV interplay and tri-axial volumetric priors.

The primary strengths of HAR-Net lie in its novel architectural components designed to intelligently embed anatomical intelligence and its hierarchical refinement strategy. Leveraging the robust nnU-Net framework as a backbone further ensures effective multi-scale feature extraction. The enhanced accuracy and consistency demonstrated by HAR-Net could potentially translate to more reliable CTV delineations in clinical practice, possibly reducing inter-observer variability and streamlining the radiotherapy planning workflow.

The OAR branch achieved a mean DSC of 90.410% across 5 pelvic organs, with a moderate positive correlation (r=0.470, P=0.018) between per-case OAR accuracy and CTV segmentation quality. This supports the design premise that high-quality OAR features serve as reliable anatomical anchors for MOCCA, while also suggesting that further improvement in OAR segmentation—particularly for challenging organs like rectum (DSC of 87.230%) and small bowel (DSC of 81.360%)—could yield additional CTV accuracy gains. The stage-wise analysis revealed expected performance differences: early-stage cases (IB2-IIB, DSC of 84.870%) exhibited more favorable results than locally advanced cases (IIIA-IVA, DSC 79.300%), reflecting the increased CTV morphological complexity and boundary ambiguity in advanced disease. The multi-task comparison demonstrated that while joint OAR-CTV learning contributes meaningfully (a 1.340 percentage point improvement in DSC), the explicit spatial reasoning provided by MOCCA and TCA accounts for the majority of the performance gain (a 2.550 percentage point improvement in DSC), confirming that implicit feature sharing alone is insufficient for optimal CTV delineation. The interpretability analysis further demonstrates that MOCCA learns attention patterns consistent with clinical knowledge, with a progressive coarse-to-fine focusing behavior across decoder stages.

Despite promising results, this study has limitations. The findings are based on a single-institution dataset; multi-center validation is crucial for assessing generalizability. The observed variability in HD95 for HAR-Net, even with its superior mean, indicates that ensuring robustness across all patient cases, particularly those with highly challenging anatomical presentations or atypical OAR configurations, remains an area for improvement. Furthermore, the specific observation that the MOCCA module, in isolation, can sometimes adversely affect HD95 suggests that the interplay between OAR features and CTV prediction needs careful management, and its performance is likely dependent on the accuracy of the OAR features it receives. The impact of OAR segmentation accuracy on MOCCA’s effectiveness, and the optimal selection of OARs for contextualization, therefore warrant further dedicated study. Additionally, HAR-Net’s computational cost is inherently higher than the baseline nnU-Net due to the additional modules.

Future research should prioritize several avenues: 1) External validation on diverse, multi-center datasets to confirm robustness and generalizability. 2) Targeted research to understand and mitigate the variance in HAR-Net’s HD95 performance, potentially by exploring adaptive mechanisms that modulate the influence of MOCCA and TCA based on image-derived uncertainty or anatomical complexity. 3) In-depth investigation into optimizing OAR feature integration within MOCCA, including its sensitivity to upstream OAR segmentation quality, the impact of different OAR combinations, and methods to make it more robust to OAR segmentation errors. 4) Exploration of methods to enhance clinical guideline integration more directly, potentially through advanced loss functions or rule-based post-processing guided by the network’s outputs. 5) Efforts to reduce computational demands, perhaps through model pruning or knowledge distillation, without sacrificing performance. 6) Qualitative clinical evaluation by radiation oncologists to assess the clinical acceptability and utility of HAR-Net’s segmentations, particularly in ambiguous or challenging cases.

In conclusion, HAR-Net shows significant promise for advancing automated CTV delineation in cervical cancer. By explicitly modeling hierarchical anatomical relationships through its MOCCA and TCA modules, it addresses key limitations of existing approaches. The framework has the potential to improve the efficiency, accuracy, and consistency of radiotherapy planning, which may ultimately contribute to better patient outcomes.
