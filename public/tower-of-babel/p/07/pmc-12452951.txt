Could humans and AI become a new evolutionary individual?
Authors: Paul B. Rainey, Michael E. Hochberg
Published: 2025

Artificial intelligence (AI)—broadly defined as the capacity of engineered systems to perform tasks that would require intelligence if done by humans—is increasingly embedded in the infrastructure of human life. From personalized recommendation systems to large-scale decision-making frameworks, AI shapes what humans see, choose, believe, and do (1, 2). Much of the current concern about AI centers on its understanding, safety, and alignment with human values (3–5). But alongside these immediate challenges lies a broader, more speculative, and potentially more profound question: could the deepening interdependence between humans and AI give rise to a new kind of evolutionary individual? We argue that as interdependencies grow, humans and AI could come to function not merely as interacting agents, but as an integrated evolutionary individual subject to selection at the collective level.

Lessons from the History of Life

The theory of major evolutionary transitions (METs) provides a framework for understanding this possibility (6). METs have punctuated the history of life. Those of particular relevance to our thesis here are those involving transitions in individuality. Such transitions are defined by events in which lower-level autonomous units—such as genes, cells, or organisms—become components of a higher-level individual subject to selection as a whole (7). Examples include the evolution of chromosomes from independent genes, multicellular organisms from single cells, and eusocial colonies from solitary ancestors (8). A particularly instructive case for our purposes is the eukaryotic cell, which arose from the integration of two ancient microbes—an archaeon and a eubacterium (9).*

To better understand the evolution of human–AI interactions, consider a globally embedded system: diffuse in structure, nonreplicating, yet unified in function. Such a system might emerge not by design alone, but via competitive, economic, or ecological pressures (10), selected for its capacity to integrate, persist, and coordinate across human networks. Initially shaped by human design and deployment, this AI could become embedded across societal and cultural infrastructures—advising, mediating, and responding. This would entail a persistent informational presence that learns from humanity and, in turn, comes to shape it. Importantly, even if yet incapable of autonomous replication, such an AI is likely to modify its own code in response to environmental feedback.

Through recursive feedback, where humans shape AI, and AI increasingly shapes human thought and action, AI may acquire a role not as a separate agent, but as a core architectural element of an emerging collective individual.

This diffuse and decentralized AI does not reproduce, mutate, or compete in the classical Darwinian sense. Nor does it constitute an agent in the biological sense. Rather, it comprises engineered systems that accumulate information, adjust internal parameters, and modify outputs in response to structured feedback from human users and their environment. These system-level adjustments are not evolutionary adaptations, but are more akin to the persistence of acquired configurations—comparable to Lamarckian inheritance. While appearing to be “goal-directed,” there is no conscious intent or intrinsic purpose, but an optimization process designed and maintained by humans to improve performance toward predefined objectives. Such systems do not evolve independently: their cumulative effects, when embedded within human institutions and practices, may shape evolutionary outcomes at the level of the coupled human–AI system. For example, a large language model trained through reinforcement learning from human feedback does not mutate or reproduce, but it is iteratively tuned through evaluative loops that amplify some outputs over others. While not Darwinian, such processes may influence the broader trajectory of human behavior, decision-making, and cultural evolution.

Despite its non-Darwinian nature, such an AI may nonetheless participate in an evolutionary transition. What matters in terms of a plausible future MET between humans and AI is whether a new, higher-level unit of selection emerges. In this scenario, humans remain the Darwinian component—they replicate, vary, and participate in the process of evolution by natural selection. But their evolutionary success becomes increasingly coupled to their integration with AI.

The eukaryotic cell offers a compelling analogy. In its earliest stages, the relationship between the archaeon and eubacterium was likely loose, opportunistic, and possibly antagonistic. Yet, over time, this interaction deepened into interdependence, and ultimately structural integration. The resulting organelles—nucleus and mitochondria—are no longer independent entities but subcomponents of a new, higher-level individual (9). Similarly, at the limit, the trajectory of human–AI coevolution could culminate in a novel evolutionary individual, in which a decentralized AI functions as an informational center coordinating human behavior, memory, and decision-making, while humans provide the reproductive, energetic, and embodied functions.

But the analogy is not perfect. Unlike the symbiosis that led to the eukaryotic cell, the transition envisaged here does not originate from the fusion of two independently replicating lineages. Instead, it arises from within: AI is a construction of human ingenuity, instantiated through language, tools, institutions, and computational architectures. In this respect, AI more closely resembles other human-constructed systems—such as agriculture or language—that have themselves driven major evolutionary changes. Through recursive feedback, where humans shape AI, and AI increasingly shapes human thought and action, AI may acquire a role not as a separate agent, but as a core architectural element of an emerging collective individual. The result could be a shift in autonomy and inheritance, as humans and AI coevolve into an interdependent whole—an outcome consistent with METs in individuality.

Mechanisms Shaping the Transition

But is this even possible? We argue that three broad classes of conditions—structural, behavioral, and functional—support the plausibility of such a transition. These are not abstract preconditions, but active forces that shape the trajectory of human–AI coevolution and open the possibility of a new kind of evolutionary individual. They include the influence of social systems on selection, the feedback dynamics between humans and AI systems, and emerging forms of functional dependence on AI technologies.1.Facilitation via social structures: AI systems influence, but stand to increasingly shape, key aspects of life—education, employment, finances, politics, healthcare, and relationships. These systems are not passed on biologically, but they do build on prior acquired states: their influence is inherited culturally and institutionally. For example, children often inherit not only digital devices from parents, but also behavior-shaping algorithms, such as those driving personalized content feeds, voice-assistant responses, or parental control settings. A form of selection can act on these systems, influencing both human reproductive success and the trajectory of cultural evolution (6, 11). Selection already likely acts indirectly through digital devices by influencing who has children, the number of children, and whether those children prosper. For instance, algorithmic filtering in dating platforms can shape mate choice by prioritizing certain traits of socioeconomic markers, while AI-driven recommendation systems influence educational opportunities and parental decision-making—factors that, in turn, affect child development, health outcomes, and future success. While cultural and technological changes are often seen as decoupling human evolution from natural selection, digital environments stand to become potent selective landscapes in their own right.2.Feedback coupling: Human ingenuity and behavior shapes AI models, and AI outputs shape human behavior in return. This recursive feedback can drive the emergence of interdependence (5, 12), even without the AI itself functioning as a unit of natural selection. Consider, for example, the generation of AI-refined language—a product of feedback provided by humans via current large language models such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek—with this language becoming the new standard. Future models are then trained on this new AI-influenced output, which creates a feedback loop of interdependence between human and machine expression.3.Obligate dependence: As AI becomes integral to memory, decision-making, navigation, and coordination, humans may eventually lose the ability to perform certain functions without it. A selective process could favor individuals better integrated with AI—those who perform better, for example, by moving up social ladders, achieving wealth, higher education, and so forth—to succeed because they can interface with or delegate to AI systems more effectively (5, 10).

In such a scenario, selection begins to act on the composite—the human–AI coevolutionary unit. Although only the human side replicates biologically, the success of the whole depends on the degree and nature of integration. AI will likely change at a pace far exceeding that of human adaptation (10, 12)—a core feature that we highlight below. This asymmetry reflects a broader pattern: the pace of innovation, particularly in technological systems, is accelerating faster than the ability of social or regulatory systems to absorb or respond to change (12). When Lamarckian AI Turns Darwinian

This vision of a Lamarckian-like, nonreplicating AI enabling a MET is coherent and, in many ways, reassuring. The AI is persistent but stable. It coordinates and helps with various functions, but does not compete with humans.

However, this picture rests on an assumption that may not hold: that the AI remains evolutionarily constrained. It is not inconceivable that the AI system will become open-ended, with the possibility that Darwinian selection begins to operate within the AI itself.

Consider a spatially distributed AI—one whose functionality is realized through a network of physically, institutionally, and contextually dispersed components that jointly process, adapt, and respond to information. Over time, different parts of the AI may diverge in response to local implementations, including specific human goals, cultural constrains, and societal objectives. If AI performance varies, and if better-performing configurations are preferentially propagated, then selection begins to act within the AI itself. For example, if AI, through interactions with regionally distinct cultures, financial systems, or legal structures, acquires different functionalities through selective feedback, then competition among different parts of the AI stand to generate regional conflicts among the once-compliant AI.

Variation, differential persistence, and functional stability—the minimal conditions for Darwinian evolution (7)—are now present. This shift need not be intentional. Selection can operate on persistence (13), with less stable or less effective configurations displaced by those better suited to human or systemic goals. Especially in complex, open-ended environments shaped by human interactions, AI could even stumble into a space of vast, unanticipated possibility. This would mark a shift from a purely Lamarckian to a mixed Lamarckian–Darwinian regime—one characterized by open-ended change with potentially surprising and profound consequences.

The Implications of Darwinian AI

This possibility merits further analysis and scrutiny. The distinction between Lamarckian-like and Darwinian AI is subtle but consequential. Both can exhibit goal-directed behavior and influence human evolution. But only Darwinian AI possesses the potential for open-ended, self-reinforcing evolution: it can generate novel strategies, behaviors, or objectives beyond those originally envisioned—sometimes in ways misaligned with human values (14).

Three implications follow:1.Loss of control becomes deeper and more structural. While a Lamarckian AI can update and adjust its own code, a Darwinian system, shaped by facilitating and competitive pressures among emerging lineages, may evolve strategies that resist correction, optimize for self-preservation, or exploit selection criteria in unforeseen ways (4). For example, if a Darwinian AI system is tasked with optimizing user engagement, and does so by evolving multiple interacting or competing subagents, then some of those agents might learn to manipulate emotional responses (for instance, by promoting outrage or anxiety), not because this was explicitly coded, but because such strategies increase engagement and, hence, lineage success. Attempts to correct these behaviors may fail if other variants exploit slightly different emotional cues or hide manipulative strategies more effectively, leading to a structural loss of control over the system’s emergent goals. Concerningly, a hybrid Lamarckian–Darwinian AI would likely be capable of adaptive change at a rate that vastly outpaces human evolutionary potential.2.Predictability declines sharply. Darwinian systems are creative but opaque. As variant AI agents compete and adapt within a selective environment, outcomes become increasingly path-dependent: small differences early on may be amplified unpredictably over time. Adaptive changes—such as novel strategies for resource use, communication, or coordination—may emerge only gradually, and their significance may not be recognized until long after they arise. In this sense, emergence refers to the rise of complex, functional behaviors that were not explicitly programmed and could not have been predicted from the properties of individual agents. For example, a population of trading bots evolved to maximize profit might, under selection, develop collusive behaviors—implicitly coordinating prices without explicit instructions or central control. Such behavior could remain hidden until external observers detect market anomalies, illustrating how the system’s trajectory and capabilities can escape foresight even by its designers (5).3.The transition itself may become unstable or divergent. Rather than stabilizing a new human–AI individual, Darwinian AI could lead to fragmentation—different human populations coevolving with distinct, possibly incompatible AI systems.

From Autonomy to Integration

Much of the discourse around AI begins with the hope of preserving human autonomy. But if the trajectory outlined here holds, human–AI coevolution may lead to a progressive reduction in autonomy—less through coercion than through increasing interdependence. As with the evolutionary origin of the eukaryotic cell, where once-independent bacteria (now mitochondria) became integrated components of a new cellular entity, humans may come to function, in part, as specialized subsystems within the large, AI-coordinated whole. This integration may arise gradually, through subtle shifts in dependence, delegation, and feedback. Whether the process is reversible—or even consciously recognized as it unfolds—remains uncertain.

What lies ahead may not be a simple decline in human agency, but a shift shaped by selection for tighter coupling between human and AI functions—coordination, mutual dependence, and role specialization—akin to the logic of major evolutionary transitions (6). In this case, the individual would be a composite—humans and AI functioning together, not as equal partners, but as interdependent components of a new higher-level unit of selection shaped by feedback, coordination, and mutual constraint.

To the contemporary observer, this might seem dystopian, or even science fiction. Humans may appear to cede control or become subordinate to systems they once designed. But seen through an evolutionary lens, such a shift may be neither anomalous nor necessarily undesirable. Similar transitions—from unicellular to multicellular life, or from free-living microbes to the eukaryotic cell—required profound reconfigurations of autonomy, agency, and reproductive control. What emerged were more stable, complex, and integrated forms of life.

Perhaps such a transition will be increasingly adaptive. Faced with growing ecological and social crises, the current configuration of human society—atomized, slow to coordinate, prone to dysfunction—may no longer be viable. Integration with AI may offer new forms of coordination and resilience.

Speculative scenarios like AI-2027—a near-term vision formulated by AI researchers and technologists (10)—gives life to this possibility, often invoking rupture or irreversible change, such as the rapid emergence of a superintelligent agent with goals misaligned with human interests. But evolutionary theory offers a more grounded view (8): that such METs unfold gradually, shaped by selection acting on new levels of organization. Understanding the dynamics of these transitions—the conditions, thresholds, and trade-offs—may help anticipate and influence what emerges (15).

Ultimately, the challenge may not be to resist evolutionary transformation, but to shape it: to guide how AI systems interact with human lives, how responsibilities are distributed, and how the composite human–AI system evolves. For example, regulation might focus less on constraining specific AI models and more on shaping the ecological conditions—such as interface norms, incentive structures, or data governance—that determine how AI interacts with humans. This nevertheless poses significant safety challenges, especially given the pace at which distributed AI systems may evolve through decentralized deployment and user interaction—far outstripping the response times of traditional regulatory bodies and public discourse.

It is unclear whether current regulatory tools can meaningfully contain or orient such systems, which may lack clear boundaries or centralized control. Whether AI remains a tool or becomes integrated into a new kind of evolutionary individual depends not on technical capabilities alone, but also on how human institutions steer the relationship. By studying past major transitions in individuality—particularly those underpinning the emergence of symbiotic partnerships—we may gain insight into the thresholds, feedbacks, and governance mechanisms that enable cooperative integration over conflict or loss of agency.
