WEF nexus trends, opportunities, barriers, foresight, and future direction beyond 2030
Authors: Luxon Nhamo, Sylvester Mpandeli, Cuthbert Taguta, Stanley Liphadzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe water-energy-food (WEF) nexus is transitioning into a vital transformative framework for promoting sustainable development across spatial scales and sectors in an era of mounting resource insecurity, environmental degradation, and climate change. It is envisioned to enhance resource-use efficiency, climate change adaptation, policy coherence, and sustainable development. Nevertheless, challenges remain, particularly in its implementation, including knowledge gaps, misunderstandings, and misconceptions in the absence of a practical operationalization mechanism and harmonized, coherent governance structures. This systematic review assesses the trends, opportunities, barriers, foresight, and future direction of the WEF nexus beyond the 2030 global sustainable development agenda, highlighting the factors limiting its adoption and operationalization and offering policy recommendations. WEF nexus technological innovations are key to countering operationalization barriers. Its future use depends on adopting integrated, cross-sectoral frameworks and analytical tools, improving data accessibility, gathering empirical evidence, building capacity, and encouraging multi-stakeholder engagement now.Keywords: Policy coherence, transformational change, sustainable development, resource security, nexus options

Introduction

The water-energy-food (WEF) nexus has become a fundamental, transformative, and integrated framework for resource management that catalyzes sustainable development and promotes resource security.1 The WEF nexus acknowledges the intricate interdependencies among the water, energy, and food systems, and its adoption balances resource demand, minimizes trade-offs, augments synergies, and safeguards human and environmental health.2 If implemented, it enhances climate change adaptation, facilitates the transition to a circular economy, and promotes resource use efficiency.3,4 This is because WEF resources are intricately interconnected: changes in water management affect energy and food production; water scarcity impacts energy generation and food supply; and increased energy demand influences water use and food distribution.5,6 This interconnectedness means that disruptions in one sector have cascading effects on others, often leading to breaches of planetary boundaries and large-scale shifts in processes that regulate the stability and resilience of life on Earth.7,8 These effects require integrated, systematic, and holistic intervention to drive sustainable resource management.9 Any changes or impacts in one of the three sectors, whether management, demand, or supply, will affect the other two, thereby impacting the entire socio-ecological system.

The perfect storm of the linear or sector-based management of WEF resources and their interactions with the environment and climate manifests through catastrophic global climate tipping points and the breaching of planetary boundaries.10 The main challenges of the traditional linear economy are its dependence on depleting finite, irreplaceable resources, massive waste and pollution, and environmental degradation, habitat destruction, and climate change.11 It is an unsustainable economic model because of its “take-make-use-dispose” practice, which leads to the rapid depletion of resources and the generation of enormous amounts of waste in the environment, which could be reused.12 This is unlike the circular “take, make, use, recycle/reuse” economy that is restorative and regenerative by design, aiming to eliminate waste and pollution by keeping resources and products in use for as long as possible.13 Therefore, the absence of a transformative, systematic, and holistic approach to natural resource management is a major cause of the serious global environmental degradation and social inequity.14 The WEF nexus is considered transformative as it restructures and revolutionizes the sector-based linear resource management process while enhancing the knowledge base, promoting paradigm shifts, creating technological cascades, improving existing technologies, and offering a deeper understanding of the intricate interlinkages and interactions among the water, energy, and food sectors.15,16 This comprehensive understanding of the WEF nexus leads to new hypotheses that support sustainability. The conflicting demands on WEF resources transcend political boundaries, as most natural resources are transboundary and shared, while trade-offs and risks manifest at the local level, affecting mostly the vulnerable.17,18

As a transformative and circular approach, the WEF nexus simplifies human understanding of the three-way interactions between WEF resources, facilitates timely identification of synergies and trade-offs, promotes positive and beneficial socio-ecological change, and demonstrates the interdependencies across the sectors.4,19 These are key to resource security amidst the climate crisis, resource depletion and degradation, and the increasing demand for WEF resources. In addition, the WEF nexus has evolved to become a valuable decision support tool for interpreting and understanding complex cross-relations, integrated planning and management, and informing coherent policy decisions.20 Therefore, the WEF nexus promotes socio-ecological stability from a multicentric perspective and steers coupled water, energy, and food systems through integrated, system-wide decision-making.21 In particular, policy coherence is essential for transitioning the WEF nexus from theory to practice, as it promotes policy harmonization, thereby bridging the gap among the interconnected WEF sectors.22 Recent WEF nexus tools have shown the framework as an essential decision support system capable of detecting disruptive trade-offs and constructive synergies that encompass multiple spatial and temporal scales while bridging diverse knowledge systems and multidisciplinary viewpoints.4,23,24 These transformative attributes provide pathways that lead to integrative governance, circular economy, climate change adaptation and resilience, and sustainable development, to guide the establishment of norms for intersectoral cooperation.25,26

However, besides these promising attributes, adopting the WEF nexus is constrained by various factors, including, among others, a lack of actionable strategies to address complex realities, the absence of a harmonized and coherent governance framework, stakeholder uncertainty related to cross-sectoral cooperation, poor financial support for research, and the complexities of climate change and rapid urbanization.1,3 Other limiting factors include data unavailability, entrenched silo structures, a lack of empirical evidence through case studies, institutional barriers such as poor funding and lack of capacity, and the applicability of the approach at different spatial scales.21,27 These constraints are further compounded by failure to embrace change and transition from the norm, geopolitical protectionism, particularly in exploiting unsustainable resources for fear of the unknown, prioritization of short-term national interests over long-term and global sustainability, protection of national economies and interests at the expense of planetary crises, and the increasing frequency and intensity of natural disasters.26,28

Besides the constraints to operationalizing the WEF nexus, which is still expected to play a pivotal role in achieving sustainability and ensuring resource security, it needs to move from theory to practice and influence policy decisions on integrated and circular resource management. The essence of the WEF nexus evolves around its analytical capabilities, which are crucial for identifying intervention points and guiding policy decisions in integrated resource management.4,20 By catalyzing the transition of the WEF nexus from a conceptual framework to a quantitative and qualitative assessment tool for resource interdependencies and problem framing, and promoting cross-sectoral collaboration, the approach is envisaged to drive the sustainability transformational agenda.29 This involves shifting the WEF nexus from a discourse and conceptual framework to a data-driven analytical tool that evaluates resource interdependencies, from focusing on single sectors to integrated systems, and informing evidence-based policy and decision-making, fostering transdisciplinary collaboration between science, policy, and implementation, and developing implementation strategies that account for the complex interlinkages between the interlinked WEF sectors and dimensions of sustainable development.30,31

Research on the WEF nexus has evolved from an abstract theory into a practical, analytical decision-support tool, aiming at addressing the current grand challenges of rapid environmental degradation, climate change, and the breaching of planetary boundaries. However, the transition is constrained by the lack of a coherent policy framework to drive the WEF nexus and inform strategic, harmonized policy decisions.21 This requires overcoming barriers through institutional and governance changes, leveraging innovation platforms, and building political will and financial commitment, processes that facilitate the transition from sectoral silos to collaborative, harmonized, cross-cutting strategies.26 This review assesses the WEF nexus, examining current trends, identifying future opportunities, and outlining the barriers hindering its operationalization. The barriers are addressed through a comprehensive understanding of the nexus, aiming to create a more integrated approach to resource management and providing insights into these barriers.

The review approach

The review employed a transdisciplinary, mixed-methods approach to systematically interrogate the existing literature on trends, opportunities, barriers, foresight, and future directions of the WEF nexus. The study is transdisciplinary as it involves diverse expertise, disciplines, and stakeholders to achieve an integrated, diverse knowledge base and create an entirely new, unified framework and practical solution.32 The review addresses the following questions: (1) What are the current trends in WEF nexus research guiding its adoption and shifting its approach from theory to practice? (2) What opportunities does the WEF nexus offer in tackling current grand challenges? (3) What barriers hinder the adoption and operationalization of the WEF nexus beyond 2030? (4) What are the anticipated risks and trade-offs threatening the expected increase in resource demand from a WEF nexus perspective? and (5) What prospects and future directions is the WEF nexus showing? These questions guided the selection and screening of the existing literature (Figure 1) and informed the formulation of rational and coherent recommendations. These recommendations aim to inform policy on catalyzing the adoption and operationalization of the WEF nexus and formulating coherent policies.

A stepwise process followed during the literature search

The review questions and the population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes (PICOs) framework33 guided the selection and screening of the literature related to the WEF nexus themes under consideration. The selected publications focused on WEF nexus trends, opportunities, barriers, and foresight and future direction. The literature search used the major literature search engines, namely Google Scholar, Scopus, Mendeley, and Web of Science to extract the relevant literature, using search statements like “WEF nexus trends,” “WEF nexus barriers,” “WEF nexus foresight,” “future direction of WEF nexus,” “risks to WEF nexus operationalization’s,” “WEF nexus adoption and operationalization,” “catalyzing the WEF nexus,” “WEF nexus and planetary boundaries,” “WEF nexus and climate tipping points,” “WEF nexus and resources use efficiency,” “transitioning the WEF nexus from theory to practice,” “misunderstandings and misconceptions of the WEF nexus,” and “use and mis-use of the WEF nexus.” The search yielded 856 peer-reviewed and gray publications, which were assessed and subsequently screened for eligibility. A total of three hundred thirty-one (n = 331) articles related to the study theme were identified through examining the titles, abstracts, and keywords. After screening, 92 studies were deemed relevant based on the full article content, which were then retrieved for analysis and synthesis. Figure 1 is a stepwise methodological framework used during the literature search, selection, and screening. The search process yielded n = 856 documents. After screening, a total of 525 and 329 articles were excluded, leaving 92 deemed suitable for synthesis. The 92 articles deemed suitable for the study are from throughout the world and are representative at all spatial scales.

Literature screening and grounds for inclusion and exclusion of articles

The literature search was conducted using the title, abstract, keywords, and all relevant fields from the specified search engines. WEF nexus review papers, as well as research reports, articles not focused on all three WEF components, without a defined methodology or case study, and those that consider nexus as a buzzword were excluded, leaving those that were linked to modeling, with a clear methodology and showing a clear trend, and other key variants. Only articles written in English and published between 2015 and 2026 were considered, as they were deemed sufficient to address issues related to resource security and sustainable development. The 2015–2026 period coincides with the formulation of sustainable development goals (SDGs). Only downloadable articles were considered. The screening was conducted manually in two stages: initially at the title, abstract, and keywords level, and later by reading the body of the article. This was conducted independently by two authors, with a third author serving as an arbiter in cases of disagreement. The screening was conducted by scoring an article’s relevance on a five-point Likert scale (1 – extremely irrelevant; 5 – very relevant), using the strength-of-evidence ranking criteria.34 Articles that were scored 3 or above by both authors were automatically included, and those scored 3 or above by only one author were resolved through discussions between the two authors until consensus was reached, or by involving the arbiter if necessary. On the other hand, articles that were scored 2 or below by both authors were excluded.

Foresight literature selection

A WEF nexus foresight analysis is an interdisciplinary, structured, systematic process to explore, anticipate, and shape plausible WEF nexus futures.35 Foresight was chosen as it reduces the risk of focusing on a single, fixed outcome, and enables the identification of emerging trends and drivers of change, the development of multiple scenarios that allow decision-makers to mitigate risks and trade-offs, and the capitalization on opportunities and synergies across different WEF nexus applications.36 The selected foresight literature focused on addressing trade-offs, enhancing resource-use efficiency (synergy), building resilience to climate change, fostering policy coherence, and promoting socio-economic benefits. This also included literature assessing different scenarios to anticipate long-term resource stresses, breaking siloed decision-making, providing pathways for navigating risk and vulnerability, and designing resilient and sustainable systems before a crisis.37 Strategic foresight added a proactive element to the study, a sustainability future indicator from a WEF nexus perspective.

Limitations of the review process

Restricting search and inclusion criteria in systematic reviews can lead to the exclusion of some relevant literature. However, systematic reviews are highly regarded for their transparency and replicability. This study followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines in line with the research questions and objectives. Although using major search engines such as Google Scholar, Scopus, Mendeley, and Web of Science may omit some pertinent literature, these databases are among the most used in comprehensive reviews.38 Although the exclusion of non-English articles may have shown bias, most peer-reviewed scientific manuscripts are published in English.39 While considering only papers published between 2015 and 2026 may exclude some relevant literature outside this period, this was deemed necessary given the proliferation of nexus-related literature post-2011 and sufficient to address the objectives of the study. The subjectivity bias related to the screening process was minimized by involving multiple authors who are experts on the WEF nexus, have developed and published several systematic reviews, and made every effort to be comprehensive and objective.

Results

Trends in WEF nexus research

The WEF nexus is gradually evolving from a conceptual framework to an operational decision-support tool, emphasizing the need to balance resource security, climate change impacts, and equity.40,41 The transition involves implementing strategies to formulate coherent policies that improve resource-use efficiency and promote sustainable development, using integrated, data-driven methods to understand and manage the complex interdependencies between the sectors.19,21 The shift is driven by cross-sectoral coordination and spurred by technological innovation to manage resources sustainably, reduce poverty, and achieve SDGs.42 This transformational shift from a linear economy to nexus thinking and doing is facilitating quantitative understanding of the interconnectedness of WEF resources and guiding responses and interventions to reduce human and environmental risks at multiple spatial scales.4 The trend is spearheading regional and international cooperation and collaboration, inclusive decision-making, and formulation of coherent policies that prioritize system resilience43 (Figure 2). Without this transformational change to guide coherent decision-making, the world will continue facing the risk of increased climate extremes and environmental crises of greater magnitude, with a high risk of breaching planetary boundaries.31,44

Trends in WEF nexus research from framing to operationalization

Thus, operationalizing the WEF nexus requires transitional pathways to move beyond sector-specific policies by developing analytical tools, harmonizing policy frameworks, promoting stakeholder engagement, and integrating diverse models with scenario planning.45 In addition, the WEF nexus subject is expanding beyond the initial WEF model to include other dimensions, including planetary and human issues, such as ecosystems and health as entry points, with a growing emphasis on applying the framework to solve real-world challenges and development agendas by creating better models, policies, and participatory solutions to resource insecurity.46 The pursuit of operationalizing the WEF nexus and its eventual application beyond theory has led to the emergence of new nexus dimensions, including health, ecosystems, land, and climate change. These dimensions are assessed in practical contexts, including urban areas and local levels, across various spatial scales, and through real-world empirical examination of trade-offs and synergies.47 This has led to the emergence of WEF nexus dimensions that extend the original concept from WEF to WEFE (for ecosystems or environment), WEFH (for health), WEFL (for land), and WEFC (for climate), among others.26 This has seen a rise in new terms such as broadening the WEF nexus, which involves moving beyond a purely resource-centric view to a people and planet-centric approach, proposing the inclusion of environmental factors, climate change, and human/societal well-being, often referred to as the WEF+ nexus.30,48

The broadening of the WEF nexus is driven by the need for a more holistic framework to achieve SDGs, creating integrated policies and operational models that account for synergies and trade-offs across all interconnected dimensions, delivering human and planetary outcomes.49 However, these dimensions have led to significant WEF nexus misconceptions, as some have strayed from the fundamental foundational triad of nexus thinking, failing to recognize that the WEF nexus is the basis, and that the other elements manifest at other layers through human and planetary outcomes within the WEF nexus.23,26 The misconceptions, complexities, and conflations arise when other factors (such as land use, health, ecosystems, climate, or economic variables), which are either nexus inputs, outputs, or outcomes, are elevated to the same level and layer as the core WEF triad, or when the triad is overlooked entirely.50 The core idea of the WEF nexus is sometimes overlooked in complex and overly detailed models.51 The main WEF nexus remains supreme in resource management, policy, climate, and development contexts because of its simplicity, highly quantifiable indicators, and direct link to national and global developmental goals.52,53 While the other derivatives (WEFE, WEFH, and WEFL) are valuable for localized research and application, they are fundamentally extensions and not replacements of the WEF methodology, as they merely adapt the core concept to address specific local realities and research interests.54

It should always be noted that water, energy, and food are the key resources whose unsustainable exploitation and linear management have caused the grand challenges faced by humankind today, including climate change and environmental degradation.26,40 The three resources are both victims and culprits of these grand challenges. As culprits, their consumption, production, and management contribute to environmental problems, such as water pollution from agriculture or agriculture- and energy-related greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.55 Conversely, they are also victims, as climate change and other environmental stresses negatively impact their availability and quality, threatening socio-ecological health and sustainable development.56,57 The other challenges manifest as trade-offs or synergies, meaning health challenges, environmental degradation, and the decline in ecosystem services are embedded in the unsustainable linear exploitation and management of water, energy, and food.6 However, these proposed dimensions, such as climate, can serve as entry points for a study or developmental project. While the WEF nexus is the node, it is not isolated, as it is the lens through which broader, complex environmental and socio-economic systems should be managed.

Opportunities presented by the WEF nexus

The essence of the WEF nexus lies in providing a holistic understanding of the complex interrelations and interconnectedness among the three sectors.4 It offers opportunities to explore synergies and trade-offs among competing uses of water, energy, and food resources, moving beyond traditional, isolated “silo” or linear management to identify and manage these interactions across sectors.58 Furthermore, it has evolved into a platform that connects stakeholders across various sectors to improve resource utilization, ensure their equitable distribution, and promote sustainable development.1 Thus, the WEF nexus presents numerous benefits, including enhanced resource security and improved socio-economic development, such as job creation and economic growth. It promotes better health outcomes and greater sustainability by effectively balancing trade-offs. Additionally, the nexus encourages holistic governance through cross-sector collaboration and fosters regional stability by managing shared resources and minimizing conflicts.58,59 By addressing these interconnected sectors holistically, the WEF nexus fosters sustainable solutions, supports the achievement of SDGs, and creates a virtuous cycle of positive impacts for communities and the environment.26 This holistic overview of the WEF nexus facilitates the formulation of solutions that address multiple challenges simultaneously, creating and unlocking synergies, multiplying gains, and avoiding unintended negative outcomes that arise from sector-based planning. These attributes have made the WEF nexus a fundamental tool in resource management, climate action, sustainable development, and socio-ecological integration, making the framework key in addressing global challenges beyond 2030.21,40

The WEF nexus, therefore, offers significant opportunities (Figure 3) to address resource insecurity and catalyzes the SDGs by promoting integrated and efficient management of interconnected systems. The WEF resources have dedicated SDGs: SDG 2 (Zero Hunger), SDG 6 (Clean Water and Sanitation), and SDG 7 (Affordable and Clean Energy), the three goals that address the core resource interdependencies that the nexus framework focuses on for integrated management and achieving broader sustainability.26 These core SDGs (2, 6, and 7) are deeply interconnected and essential for achieving the other 14 SDGs.29 This is because water, energy, and food are fundamental to human well-being, economic activity, and ecosystem stability.60 Managing them holistically is crucial, as actions in one sector significantly affect the others, creating synergies or trade-offs that influence overall sustainable development. Therefore, any mismanagement of the three resources has cascading effects on the entire socio-economic and ecological system.61 The grand challenges facing humanity today stem from the linear management of the three resources, which is generally sector-based.26 Managing resources across sectors through the WEF nexus optimizes resource use and improves the productivity of water, energy, and food, while reducing waste and maximizing output.28 These WEF nexus attributes are at the heart of climate change adaptation and resilience, as they promote water conservation, energy efficiency, and sustainable food production practices.1 By promoting sustainable resource management and creating new economic opportunities, the WEF nexus contributes to poverty reduction and improved livelihoods, especially in vulnerable communities, even after the 2030 global sustainable development agenda.

The interconnected benefits derived from operationalizing the WEF nexus

Success stories have shown that operationalizing the WEF nexus eliminates sector-based planning and ensures human and environmental health without exceeding ecological boundaries.1,21 Practical success stories of WEF nexus implementation have shown four main pillars: (1) integrated governance that binds coordinated management practices to co-create solutions, (2) development of synergy-driven infrastructure supported by technological innovation that results in multi-sector benefits, (3) promoting quantified trade-off management to reduce risk, and (4) informed decentralized and localized implementation for timely intervention.3,15 Practical successful implementation of the WEF nexus includes.

Foresight and future direction for the WEF nexus: beyond 2030

The essence of the WEF nexus foresight is to provide future-oriented scenario planning strategies to effectively manage the interconnected WEF systems across generations and beyond the 2030 global sustainable development agenda.30,37 It applies forward-looking models, scenario-planning frameworks, and strategic tools to forecast interactions among WEF systems under future pressures and shocks such as climate change and population growth. Rather than viewing these sectors in isolation, foresight maps out future trade-offs and synergies to prevent resource failure and enhance resilience. Foresight acknowledges the deep interdependencies, dynamic spatiotemporal evolutions, drivers of change, and challenges among these systems, including climate change, resource insecurity, and environmental degradation and depletion.74 Foresight’s forward analysis is beneficial for building resilience, promoting sustainable resource management, achieving desired outcomes, detecting trade-offs (e.g., hydropower versus irrigation), identifying opportunities for synergy (nexus gains), and developing policies that prevent unintended negative outcomes across these critical sectors, leading to improved governance and innovation.21 It is also being applied in future-oriented analysis to understand and manage the intricate interconnections of WEF systems37 and identify synergies and trade-offs that inform better policies and innovations, even post the 2030 global agenda on sustainable development.

These are being achieved by engaging stakeholders to support decision-making on anticipated challenges and promote transformative, innovation-driven solutions across various sectors. The use of WEF nexus foresight is enabling decision-makers to manage vulnerabilities and build more resilient systems to meet the demands of a growing population and a changing climate.75 Therefore, WEF nexus foresight is preparing for the future, emphasizing the formulation of coherent policies that promote integrated resource management. A WEF nexus foresight enhances synergy optimization, builds climate resilience, and aligns with SDGs.37 The assessment identifies mutual benefits within the WEF sectors, e.g., it highlights how precision agriculture can simultaneously reduce water and energy inefficiencies, rather than managing these interconnected resources in isolation.83 Furthermore, it enhances climate resilience by facilitating the anticipation of systemic shocks from extreme weather.7 This proactive planning enables communities to develop adaptive infrastructure before disruptive events, as it is a forward-thinking process critical for mapping out pathways to achieve the SDGs. By mapping the interconnected WEF sectors alongside climate, socio-economic, and political variables, the WEF nexus foresight enables decision-makers to build resilience against global disruptions.84

Barriers to implementing the WEF nexus

The global grand challenges, including climate change, population growth, environmental degradation, pandemic risk, rapid urbanization, and resource depletion, necessitate a departure from traditional linear methods toward transformative approaches that promote a circular economy and sustainable development.85 Instead, the rate of population growth, expected to reach 9–10 billion by 2050, and the increasing pressure on global life-supporting natural systems require proactive global action before the situation reaches a boiling point. To date, we have surpassed seven of the nine planetary boundaries, risking large-scale, abrupt, and irreversible environmental damage.86 However, the transition from the current linear model to the circular economy is constrained by various challenges. Sectoral silos and institutional inertia, incoherent policies and fragmented governance structures, lack of uniform, accessible, interlinked data, financial constraints, and challenges in aligning diverse stakeholder interests and political will are the core barriers to implementing these approaches.21 Breaking down traditional sector-specific thinking and data systems for integrated planning has been identified as the core hurdle.4 These constraints risk the WEF nexus from going beyond 2030, the cut-off date for the SDGs, as the two frames are strongly linked.3,87 Failure to overcome these barriers risks the WEF nexus becoming another rhetorical framework.

The lack of awareness and human capacity limits understanding of the WEF nexus, including among communities and policymakers.88 This has hindered effective implementation and collaboration, resulting in insufficient proactive measures to shift from the current linear model. The lack of cooperation has led to geopolitical tensions, territorial protectionism, conflicts, denial of reality, anxiety among decision-makers, and unattended trade-offs and synergies.26 This has also led to low uptake of WEF nexus technological innovations and implementation of transformative models at a wider scale, as they have remained at the project level.89 However, studies have shown that a major obstacle to implementing the WEF nexus is the sector-based policies designed to function independently. These often hinder coordination and lead to conflicting goals and unintended negative outcomes.26 Addressing these barriers requires stronger political commitment, better stakeholder engagement, innovative financing, harmonized institutions, and coherent policy frameworks, if the WEF nexus is to remain relevant beyond 2030 (Table 1).

Risks threatening the success of the WEF nexus

Besides the inherent benefits of WEF nexus implementation, it faces significant risks and trade-offs that threaten its intended positive outcomes, primarily driven by climate change, governance failures, and competing sectoral demands.21,58,90 The core risk is that ignoring interlinkages leads to unintended consequences, but managing them holistically is overwhelming. Without high-level political commitment to break down silos and robust data to manage trade-offs, the WEF nexus risks remaining a theoretical concept rather than a practical solution for sustainable development. The anticipated risks and trade-offs threatening the success of the WEF nexus include (1) the inherent resource and environmental trade-offs, (2) linear-focused or sector-based governance and institutional barriers, (3) climate change as a threat multiplier, and (4) financial gaps (Table 2). In terms of resource and environmental risks, increasing the number of hydropower or bioenergy stations enhances energy security, but also increases water consumption, reducing water availability for food production.5 Seawater desalination and wastewater recycling are essential for water security, but are energy-intensive, leading to high energy demand.91 On the other hand, increasing land and water use for bioenergy can directly conflict with food security goals.92

From a governance perspective, the absence of harmonized policies is undermining water and food security, as the current sector-based policies focus on a single sector, achieving ultra-efficiencies in those selected sectors at the expense of other equally important sectors.93 For example, promoting mining for economic growth at the expense of other sectors increases the risk of acid mine drainage on productive lands, affecting water and food security.94 In transboundary contexts, national interests often override regional integrated planning, reducing the effectiveness of shared resource management.26 From a climate change perspective, extreme weather events such as droughts and floods reduce the available water for irrigation and hydropower, resulting in water, energy, and food insecurity.95 Furthermore, the high cost of implementing integrated, sustainable technologies (e.g., wastewater reuse, seawater desalination, and precision agriculture) can hinder the WEF nexus.96 These challenges increase vulnerabilities, particularly among smallholder farmers, making it difficult to adopt new, more efficient nexus-oriented technologies. To mitigate these risks, the WEF nexus should transition from theory to practice and promote integrated management that explicitly identifies these trade-offs, ensuring that solving a problem in one sector does not create a crisis in another. For the WEF nexus to succeed, it must transition from a conceptual framework to an operational analytical tool that incorporates context-specific and quantitative analyses of trade-offs, backed by strong, collaborative governance capable of managing conflicting sectoral interests. If these issues are not addressed, the WEF nexus faces significant risks of unintended, unsustainable, and often contradictory outcomes.

Although the WEF nexus has emerged as an important transformative and circular approach, it has faced criticism for failing to translate its concepts into a practical, everyday operational framework.97 Critics argue that the approach often serves as a theoretical buzzword without offering the adaptable metrics, practical tools, and cross-departmental governance necessary to manage complex resource trade-offs in real-world situations,98,99 and others have even branded it as a repackaging of the integrated water resources management (IWRM).100 Recent criticisms have been supported by a significant body of literature that emphasizes the importance of the approach in sustainable development, but lacks practical solutions and empirical evidence.15,101 However, recent advances in WEF nexus research and case studies have shifted the approach from merely a discourse framework that promotes stakeholder engagement or a conceptual model for policy coherence to a practical analytical tool for quantifying cross-sectoral synergies and trade-offs.102,103,104 The three-dimensional attributes have strengthened the approach to be an important transformative and revolutionary resource for security, policy coherence, integrated management, cross-sectoral synergy optimization, and a catalyst for sustainable development.24,105,106 Operational WEF nexus analytical tools include (1) the iWEF tool (https://www.iwef.app/),4,20 (2) WEF-Tools developed by UNESCO-IHE (https://wef-tools.un-ihe.org/en/project), (3) the climate, land, energy, and water (CLEWs) model (https://un-modelling.github.io/modelling-tools/), and (4),107 WEF Nexus Index (https://wefnexusindex.org/).105

Discussion

WEF nexus maladaptation outcomes and the strategic sustainability pathways

Harmonized WEF nexus strategies enhance sustainability, including human and environmental health, by shifting from fragmented, siloed management to an integrated, systems-based approach,26 by strategically mitigating trade-offs and optimizing synergies. Adopting and implementing the WEF nexus is essential for climate change adaptation, as it ensures resource security and fosters the circular economy, thereby directly improving public health, ecosystem services, and economic stability.46,58 However, failure to operationalize the WEF nexus risks breaching planetary boundaries, with catastrophic consequences (Figure 4).21 The current, disjointed management of WEF resources has contributed to the transgression of seven of the nine planetary boundaries, with catastrophic consequences including ecosystem collapse, food and water insecurity, and runaway climate change.108 The current challenges require transitioning to a circular economy through integrated WEF nexus interventions, which facilitate holistic management and catalyzes returning to a safe operating space.

Risks of WEF nexus maladaptation and the resultant consequences

Breaching planetary boundaries increases the risk of large-scale, abrupt, or irreversible environmental damage.

WEF nexus maladaptation occurs when strategies designed to improve security in one sector, often in response to environmental degradation and climate change, unintentionally exacerbate vulnerabilities, deplete resources, or increase emissions in another, leading to a net loss of resilience.6,21 Anthropogenic GHG emissions, mainly from burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and industrial agriculture, are the primary drivers of current environmental degradation and climate change.109 As of 2024–2025, global GHG emissions reached new records, but the deployment of smart, data-driven technologies is accelerating mitigation strategies to achieve net-zero goals, informed by circular and transformative approaches such as the circular economy and the WEF nexus.108 Failure to adequately curb GHG emissions, primarily carbon dioxide and methane, is behind the accelerated environmental degradation and the worsening of climatic, economic, and social challenges faced today.110 This breach has resulted in dire consequences, including resource degradation, loss of livelihoods, and the surpassing of planetary boundaries. When GHGs exceed certain atmospheric thresholds and exacerbate the breaching of planetary boundaries, mitigation may become impossible, leading to irreparable and irreversible environmental damage that endangers human health.44,108 Surpassing this boundary is highly risky, as it is a point of no return once planetary boundaries are breached. This is a stage where humankind can only adapt or become resilient to severe climatic, health, and environmental risks (Figure 4). However, the WEF nexus is envisioned to guide the formulation of coherent strategic policy decisions beyond 2030 and continue supporting the next phase of the global sustainability goals.

However, maladaptation in the nexus transforms the WEF system from a “virtuous cycle” of resource sharing into a “vicious circle” of depletion. It results in compound crises, including resource insecurity. It will become difficult to manage resources, exacerbating sector shocks and potentially transforming the Earth System into one hostile to humankind.44,108 Once planetary boundaries are crossed, the only path forward is to adapt or build resilience against adverse climatic extremes and environmental risks, including pandemics and loss of livelihoods, among other irreversible threats.108 Adopting circular and transformative approaches, in which the output of one system (e.g., wastewater) becomes the input to another (e.g., irrigation), catalyzes sustainability by transforming resource management into a tool for environmental restoration and human well-being.

WEF nexus strategies to achieve sustainability include transitioning to low-carbon, renewable energy (solar, wind, hydropower), which reduces GHG emissions and directly improves air quality, thereby reducing climate-related health risks and extremes, and advancing SDGs 7 and 13.111 Using energy-efficient technologies in water treatment and agricultural processing reduces overall resource consumption and makes water resources available to other sectors.112 In sustainable agriculture and food systems that aim to address SDGs 2 and 12, the use of AI and data-driven tools optimizes resource use, reduces soil degradation and water pollution, enhances soil health, and reduces food losses in the supply chain, thereby reducing methane emissions from landfills.113 From a water security and efficiency perspective, achieving SDG 6, recycling wastewater for agricultural irrigation and industrial cooling, particularly in water-scarce regions, conserves freshwater and protects aquatic ecosystems.114 All these initiatives, coupled with coherent governance structures and holistic and integrated planning, contribute to improved health, environmental sustainability, and climate resilience beyond 2030.

Recommendations

Transforming the WEF nexus requires a paradigm shift from the current linear, sector-based management to an integrated, circular, and cross-sectoral approach backed by coherent policies, technological innovations, and stakeholder collaboration.3,21 Moving the WEF nexus forward and beyond 2030 requires shifting from theoretical, siloed management to practical, integrated approaches that incorporate climate change, involve stakeholders, and prioritize sustainable investments.21,115 This transition, often described as moving from “nexus thinking” to “nexus doing,” or from theory to practice, is necessary to address resource insecurity amidst climate change, resource degradation, and depletion. The following recommendations (Table 3) are, therefore, proposed to transition the nexus from a “vicious cycle” of competition to a “virtuous cycle” of resilience and cooperation. The transition catalyzes sustainability, enhances the circular economy, resource-use efficiency, and builds resilience to climate change.

Conclusion

The WEF nexus has undergone a steady transition from a theoretical conceptualization to an operational, data-driven framework capable of driving progress toward achieving the SDGs and beyond the 2030 global sustainability agenda. This review has outlined key trends, including rising empirical research on the framework, increased focus on climate change adaptation, catalyzing progress toward achieving the SDGs, and the integration of circular economy principles. However, multiple challenges remain in implementing the model, due to data scarcity, sectoral silos and policies, and the need for region-specific adaptation strategies. Operationalizing the WEF nexus involves actively implementing the framework by managing interlinked sectors not as separate entities, but as interconnected systems. This is vital if the WEF nexus is to remain a key strategic policy decision tool beyond 2030. Available options to advance the WEF nexus include integrated resource management, technological advancements, institutional harmonization, and policy coherence and convergence. These options lead to opportunities that enhance resource efficiency, reduce trade-offs, promote synergies, and improve livelihoods. However, future research should prioritize addressing barriers to implementing the WEF nexus at all spatial scales, including data scarcity, institutional fragmentation, policy coherence, lack of political will, and awareness. The rapidly growing global population and the subsequent intensifying pressure on life-supporting natural systems, driven by increased resource use and consumption and exacerbated by extreme climate events, require the development of innovative technologies and comprehensive, coherent policy frameworks to address the looming crisis of resource insecurity. The growing interest in WEF nexus research highlights its importance as a critical intersectoral framework that supports the transition from traditional, siloed resource management to a sustainable, resilient future. As demand for water, energy, and food increases amidst a rapidly growing population and reduced productivity due to worsening climate change, the nexus serves as a framework for navigating trade-offs and enhancing resilience. The demand for these essential resources is projected to increase by over 50% by 2050, further stressing an already degraded and depleted resource base. This review shows that the WEF nexus has evolved from an academic concept into a fundamental and practical framework for ensuring resource security, economic growth, and environmental sustainability. Its success, however, is dependent on transitioning from the norm to a more collaborative, data-driven, circular, and innovative system that breaks down institutional barriers and actively engages stakeholders in the decision-making process.
