Inorganic sulfate is critical for Mycobacterium tuberculosis lung tissue colonization and redox balance
Authors: Wendy Le Mouëllic, Florence Levillain, Ting-Di Wu, Maxime Caouaille, Philippe Bousso, Yannick Poquet, Olivier Neyrolles
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTuberculosis, caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis, remains a leading cause of death worldwide. Understanding how M. tuberculosis survives in the nutrient-limited environments of infected host cells and tissues is key to developing new treatments. This study reveals that M. tuberculosis critically depends on inorganic sulfate, imported by the SubI-CysTWA transporter, as a sulfur source during infection. Sulfur fuels essential processes, including redox balance, enabling M. tuberculosis to thrive in host tissues. Unlike many pathogens that prefer organic sulfur, M. tuberculosis employs an energetically demanding strategy to assimilate sulfate. These findings highlight a previously overlooked vital role for sulfate uptake and sulfur metabolism in M. tuberculosis virulence, identifying these processes as promising targets for innovative tuberculosis therapies.

With 10.8 million new cases and 1.25 million deaths reported in 2023, tuberculosis remains the deadliest infectious disease caused by a single pathogen: the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis (1). There is an urgent need to develop new therapeutics or improve existing ones since drug-resistant cases keep arising, with 400,000 new cases in 2023. In this context, a better understanding of how M. tuberculosis survives and acquires essential nutrients to sustain its metabolic activity during infection of host cells and tissues could identify critical pathogen vulnerabilities to target for the treatment of tuberculosis.

While several studies have elucidated how M. tuberculosis acquires key elements such as carbon (2–6) and nitrogen (7–10) within the host cell environment (11–13), much less is known about how the bacterium acquires sulfur during infection. Sulfur acquisition is vital for bacterial survival and growth, as sulfur is involved in numerous metabolic pathways. These include the synthesis of the amino acids cysteine and methionine, essential for protein synthesis, the production of various coenzymes, the assembly of iron-sulfur clusters, the synthesis of low-molecular-weight thiols that act as redox buffers, and the generation of hydrogen sulfide, which plays a role in various regulatory processes in bacteria. Therefore, identifying the sulfur sources used by M. tuberculosis during infection could shed light on key metabolic pathways and reveal potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

The sulfur metabolism of bacterial pathogens has garnered significant attention in recent years (see ref. 14 for a comprehensive review). However, our understanding often remains limited to biochemical analyses or studies in in vitro cultures. In the case of M. tuberculosis, it has long been established that the bacillus can assimilate inorganic sulfate through a complete pathway that reduces and assimilates oxidized sulfate into hydrogen sulfide (15–19) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Downstream of sulfate assimilation, three cysteine synthases, namely, CysK1, CysK2, and CysM, have been identified in M. tuberculosis (20–24). These enzymes enable the synthesis of cysteine from inorganic sulfur, which subsequently fuels all sulfur metabolism in the bacterium. The conservation of a complete sulfate assimilation pathway and multiple cysteine synthases suggests that inorganic sulfur sources may play a central role for M. tuberculosis during infection. However, sulfur sources and their role in M. tuberculosis remain unclear.

The SubI-CysTWA transporter was previously identified as the sole sulfate transporter in the vaccinal strain Mycobacterium bovis BCG (25). This ABC transporter is composed of two transmembrane subunits (CysT and CysW), a dimeric intracytoplasmic nucleotide-binding subunit (CysA), and an extracellular sulfate-binding subunit, SubI (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). However, the role of this transporter under infectious conditions appears limited. Specifically, M. bovis BCG mutants lacking CysA or SubI (also known as SbpA1) exhibit similar survival rates to the wild-type strain in the lungs of infected mice (25, 26). This finding led to the conclusion that inorganic sulfate may not serve as the primary sulfur source for M. bovis BCG during infection. By extension, it was hypothesized that the virulent M. tuberculosis species similarly relies on reduced sulfur sources scavenged from the host during infection to sustain its sulfur metabolism. Further supporting this hypothesis, it was demonstrated that M. tuberculosis can utilize methionine to produce cysteine via a functional reverse transsulfuration pathway (27). Consequently, it was postulated that M. tuberculosis depends on host-derived methionine to meet its sulfur requirements during infection. However, this idea was challenged when a ∆metB mutant of M. tuberculosis, which lacks the bifunctional cystathionine γ-lyase (CGL)/cystathionine γ-synthase (CGS) MetB enzyme required for the reverse transsulfuration pathway, exhibited no survival defect in vivo (28). This finding demonstrated that MetB activity, and the underlying production of cysteine from methionine, does not play a critical role in M. tuberculosis survival during infection. More recently, it was shown that a methionine auxotrophic mutant (∆metA) was completely cleared from lungs of infected mice within a few weeks (29). These findings suggest that M. tuberculosis cannot acquire methionine or its intermediates in sufficient quantities to support its metabolism during infection, leaving the source of sulfur sustaining this pathogen unresolved.

In addition to methionine utilization, it has been suggested that alternative sulfate transporters may contribute to sulfate acquisition under infectious conditions. Redundancy between sulfate acquisition systems would explain how cysA and subI (sbpA) transposon mutants of M. bovis BCG survive during infection (25, 26). Interestingly, the genome of M. tuberculosis contains three potential sulfate transporter-encoding genes from the SLC26 family (SulP superfamily): rv1707, rv1739c, and rv3273. Among these, the putative permeases Rv1707 and Rv1739c contain a sulfate transporter and anti-sigma factor antagonist (STAS) domain. This domain is involved in the interaction of the Arabidopsis thaliana SULTR1;2 sulfate transporter with a cysteine synthase, forming a putative complex for sulfate assimilation (30). Supporting its role in sulfate transport, heterologous expression of rv1739c in Escherichia coli has been shown to enhance sulfate acquisition (31). Furthermore, rv1707 and rv1739c expression levels increase during infection of murine IFNγ-activated macrophages, hinting that these putative transporters may function in the bacillus during infection (32). These genes are also expressed during infection of human macrophages and dendritic cells (33) and in the presence of physiological stimuli such as nitric oxide or severe hypoxia (34, 35). However, the specific roles of Rv1707 and Rv1739c in sulfate import in M. tuberculosis have yet to be experimentally confirmed.

Thus, the utilization of inorganic and organic sulfur sources by M. tuberculosis during infection remains poorly understood. To address this, we investigated whether inorganic sulfate supports M. tuberculosis sulfur metabolism under infectious conditions. Using nanoSIMS analyses on infected murine macrophages, we demonstrated that M. tuberculosis can exploit sulfate to acquire sulfur in the host cell environment. In addition, we generated a ∆subI deletion mutant of M. tuberculosis, demonstrated the critical role of SubI in sulfate acquisition in the pathogenic species, and uncovered the central role of this transporter in intracellular sulfate uptake by M. tuberculosis. Interestingly, our data also suggest that the ∆subI mutant compensates for the loss of sulfate uptake by acquiring cysteine from the host cell. Furthermore, we demonstrated that growth of the ∆subI mutant is markedly attenuated during infection of murine macrophages and in vivo in the lungs of mice. Finally, we found that impaired sulfate acquisition disrupted M. tuberculosis redox balance in vitro and in vivo, resulting in marked oxidative stress, consistent with the role of sulfate in fueling cysteine and redox buffer biosynthesis. Collectively, our results reveal that M. tuberculosis largely relies on inorganic sulfate imported via the SubI-CysTWA transporter to sustain sulfur metabolism in a nutrient-restricted host environment. This finding expands our understanding of M. tuberculosis metabolism in infected cells and tissues and identifies sulfate as a critical sulfur source for this pathogen.

Results

Sulfate-Derived Sulfur Accumulates in M. tuberculosis Within Infected Macrophages.

Since M. tuberculosis harbors a complete machinery for sulfate assimilation (15–19) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and is classically cultured in 7H9-ADC medium, in which sulfate is the primary sulfur source, we hypothesized that inorganic sulfate might serve as a sulfur source for this pathogen within infected host cells. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated sulfate-derived sulfur acquisition by M. tuberculosis in murine bone-marrow derived macrophages using nanoSIMS analysis. Macrophages were infected with 13C-labeled bacteria, followed by the addition of 33S-labeled sulfate to the extracellular medium (Fig. 1A). 33S isotopic enrichment was quantified across the entire sample (Fig. 1 B, Middle) and specifically in the 13C-enriched regions corresponding to intracellular M. tuberculosis (Fig. 1 B, Right).

We observed a moderate 33S enrichment of approximately 10% above natural abundance in macrophage cytoplasmic regions (Fig. 1C). This is consistent with the known capacity of animal cells to import inorganic sulfate from the extracellular environment and the limited number of metabolic processes that incorporate sulfate into cellular components (36). Strikingly, intracellular M. tuberculosis exhibited a dramatic accumulation of 33S, with an average enrichment of 500% compared to natural abundance (Fig. 1C). This finding argues that inorganic sulfate is a significant source of sulfur for bacilli infecting macrophages.

Interestingly, alongside the striking accumulation of 33S in intracellular M. tuberculosis, we observed significant heterogeneity in 33S labeling among individual bacteria, with enrichment levels ranging from less than 1% to over 1,000% excess 33S (Fig. 1C). This variability was evident even among bacteria residing within the same macrophage (Fig. 1B), suggesting that M. tuberculosis may encounter heterogeneous microenvironments within distinct intracellular compartments. To explore this further, we performed SIMS analysis to monitor both 33S-labeled sulfate trafficking in infected macrophages, where intracellular bacteria were identified by 13C labeling, and the late endosomal/lysosomal compartment using a gold-conjugated anti-v-ATPase antibody. The data show 33S accumulation in both v-ATPase-positive and -negative bacteria (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), indicating that sulfate can reach intracellular bacilli regardless of their localization, whether in early/late phagosomes, phagolysosomes, or the cytosol. Moreover, a negative correlation was observed between 33S enrichment and 13C level within the bacterial population, where bacteria with higher 33S enrichment displayed lower 13C levels (Fig. 1D). Assuming that the starting M. tuberculosis population was uniformly 13C-labeled, a reduction in 13C level likely reflects the active uptake of 12C-compounds from the host cell and bacterial replication. This observation suggests that higher metabolic activity, associated with bacterial proliferation, correlates with an increased sulfur demand, which could be met by sulfate utilization within the intracellular environment.

SubI-CysTWA Is the Sole Sulfate Transporter in the Virulent Strain M. tuberculosis.

We next sought to identify the sulfate transporter responsible for inorganic sulfate acquisition by M. tuberculosis during infection. Specifically, we investigated the roles of the ABC transporter SubI-CysTWA, previously identified as the major sulfate transporter in the closely related vaccine species M. bovis BCG, and the putative sulfate permeases Rv1707 and Rv1739c, whose function remains uncharacterized. To this end, we generated single deletion mutants lacking the putative sulfate-binding subunit SubI, Rv1707 or Rv1739c, as previously described (37) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A−D).

In contrast to the wild-type, ∆rv1707, and ∆rv1739c strains, the ∆subI mutant was unable to grow in the classical rich medium 7H9-ADC, where sulfate is the predominant sulfur source (Fig. 2A). This finding aligns with the initial annotation of subI, cysT, cysW, and cysA, encoding components of the SubI-CysTWA transporter, as essential genes, as such screens are typically conducted in 7H9 medium (38). We further confirmed that the ∆subI mutant cannot grow in minimal medium supplemented with sulfate as the sole sulfur source (Fig. 2B), supporting the pivotal role of the SubI-CysTWA transporter in sulfate acquisition by M. tuberculosis. Growth of the ∆subI mutant was successfully restored when cysteine was provided as the sulfur source in the medium (Fig. 2B), demonstrating that M. tuberculosis can utilize reduced sulfur compounds to sustain its sulfur metabolism. Additionally, genetic complementation of the ∆subI mutant with the complete subI-cysTWA operon (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C) fully restored bacterial growth on sulfate (Fig. 2C). Finally, we assessed the survival of the ΔsubI mutant in sulfate-free 7H9 medium supplemented with either 2 mM or 20 mM MgSO4. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4A, the mutant not only failed to grow but also exhibited a progressive loss of viability over time, indicating a loss of viability rather than a simple growth arrest. Collectively, these findings suggest that the absence of the SubI-CysTWA transporter cannot be compensated by increasing extracellular sulfate concentrations, underscoring the essential role of this transporter system in sulfate uptake and survival under sulfate-limited conditions.

We hypothesized that the putative permeases Rv1707 and Rv1739c might contribute to sulfate acquisition to a lesser extent. To test this, we generated a ∆rv1707 ∆rv1739c double mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D) to uncover any potential subtle phenotype. However, the double mutant was still able to grow in minimal medium supplemented with sulfate as the sole sulfur source (Fig. 2B), further reinforcing the central role of SubI-CysTWA transporter in sulfate acquisition in vitro.

Last, we demonstrated that the subI homologue msmeg_4533 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E) is also essential for the growth of the nonpathogenic, fast-growing species Mycobacterium smegmatis in the presence of sulfate (Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that msmeg_4533 is part of the sole sulfate transporter in M. smegmatis. This result is somewhat unexpected, given that M. smegmatis is known to encode a broader repertoire of metabolite transporters than M. tuberculosis (39). Moreover, heterologous expression of the M. tuberculosis subI-cysTWA operon in a ∆msmeg_4533 mutant fully restored its ability to grow in the presence of sulfate (Fig. 2D), providing functional evidence that this transporter alone is sufficient to mediate sulfate uptake in mycobacteria.

Altogether, these results establish SubI-CysTWA as the sole sulfate transporter in the virulent species M. tuberculosis and that this role may be conserved in other Mycobacteria. Additionally, the ∆subI mutant serves as a valuable tool to further investigate the role of sulfate in M. tuberculosis survival during infection.

SubI-CysTWA Mediates Sulfate Acquisition by M. tuberculosis Within Infected Macrophages.

We used the ∆subI mutant, deficient in sulfate acquisition, to determine whether direct sulfate import was responsible for the 33S incorporation observed in intracellular M. tuberculosis (Fig. 1). NanoSIMS analysis was performed on macrophages infected with 13C-labeled wild-type or ∆subI strains of M. tuberculosis in presence of 33S-sulfate. Consistent with our observations above, 33S labeling in the wild-type strain was heterogenous, with some bacteria highly enriched and others lacking significant 33S accumulation (Fig. 3A). In sharp contrast, no significant 33S enrichment was detected in any ∆subI bacteria. Quantitative analysis revealed that the 33S level fell below the detection threshold in a majority of ∆subI bacteria (58 values < 0, Fig. 3B) due to uncertainty in nanoSIMS measurement for individual bacterium, with an average 33S enrichment of only 2%, compared to 300% in the wild-type strain. The inability of the ∆subI mutant to accumulate 33S demonstrates that the 33S labeling of intracellular M. tuberculosis relies on direct uptake of inorganic sulfate. Furthermore, these results identify SubI-CysTWA as the primary transporter mediating sulfate acquisition in M. tuberculosis within the host cell environment, effectively ruling out a significant role for alternative sulfate transporters under these conditions.

Having demonstrated that M. tuberculosis can be grown with cysteine as the sole sulfur source and that growth of the ∆subI mutant can be restored by the addition of cysteine in vitro (Fig. 2 B and C), we investigated whether cysteine could serve as an alternative sulfur source for M. tuberculosis in the intracellular environment. A nanoSIMS experiment was conducted with 15N-labeled cystine added to the extracellular medium (Fig. 3C). Cystine is indeed acquired by macrophages via the xc- transporter and subsequently reduced to cysteine intracellularly (40). Quantification of 15N abundance in intracellular M. tuberculosis revealed a significant enrichment of 75% in wild-type bacteria compared to the natural 15N isotopic abundance (Fig. 3D), suggesting that 15N-cysteine was imported by the bacilli.

Interestingly, the 15N enrichment increased further to an average of 110% in the ∆subI mutant population. This observation suggests that bacteria deficient in sulfate acquisition may rely on reduced sulfur sources, such as cysteine, available within macrophages, to sustain their sulfur metabolism during infection. Other sulfur sources, including methionine and the very abundant tripeptide glutathione, could also serve as potential nutrients for M. tuberculosis. Notably, M. tuberculosis harbors a functional reverse transsulfuration pathway (27), enabling the conversion of methionine to cysteine. Accordingly, the growth of a ∆cysH mutant, which is deficient in sulfate assimilation, was successfully restored by the addition of methionine (41). Additionally, despite the initially reported bacteriostatic effect of glutathione on M. tuberculosis (42, 43), we demonstrated that M. tuberculosis can grow in minimal medium when reduced glutathione is provided as the sole sulfur source (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B).

To explore how the mutant compensates for its inability to acquire sulfate, we performed RNA-seq analysis of wild-type and mutant strains grown in the presence of sulfate and cysteine (Fig. 4, SI Appendix, Table S1). Although the cysteine/cystine transporters in M. tuberculosis are not known, our data show induction of several candidate genes: Rv1979c (an amino acid permease of unknown specificity), Rv2686c (part of an ABC transporter), and the OppA-D peptide transport system. These findings suggest that one or more of these transporters may mediate increased cysteine uptake in the mutant. Furthermore, genes involved in methionine biosynthesis from cysteine or sulfide (metA, metC, metZ) and all the genes involved in ergothioneine biosynthesis were up-regulated in the mutant, suggesting that increased cysteine uptake could result from upregulation of these pathways. In addition, redox-responsive genes, including nearly all genes of the DevR regulon (44), and genes regulated by the copper-responsive regulators CsoR (45) and RicR (46), by the zinc-responsive regulator Zur (47), and by MosR (48, 49) and WhiB5 (50) were modulated in the mutant compared to the wild-type, suggesting that disruption of the subI gene alters the intracellular redox homeostasis of M. tuberculosis, leading to broad transcriptional reprogramming involving multiple stress-responsive regulatory networks. Yet, untargeted metabolomic analysis of wild-type and mutant strains revealed no major differences in amino acid, lactate, and TCA cycle intermediate levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), suggesting that the metabolic adaptations observed by RNA-seq are sufficient to sustain central metabolism.

In the context of infection where M. tuberculosis is likely exposed to multiple sulfur sources, it remains unclear whether one of these putative nutrients is preferentially utilized to fuel its sulfur metabolism. Next, we used our sulfate uptake-deficient mutant to investigate the importance of this pathway in vivo.

SubI-CysTWA-Dependent Sulfate Acquisition Is Crucial for M. tuberculosis Lung Tissue Colonization.

To assess the role of SubI-CysTWA in the multiplication of M. tuberculosis in host cells, we infected murine macrophages with wild-type, ΔsubI and ΔsubI::subI-cysTWA strains. As shown in Fig. 5A, a significant reduction in the intracellular bacterial load of the ∆subI mutant was observed 5- and 10-d postinfection compared to the wild-type and complemented strains.

To extend our observations to an in vivo model, we first generated a reporter strain constitutively expressing GFP and expressing mCherry under the control of the subI-cysTWA operon promoter to monitor expression of the sulfate transporter in situ in the lung tissue. This reporter strain was used to infect M. tuberculosis-sensitive C3HeB/FeJ mice. Imaging of infected lung regions at 60 d postinfection revealed mCherry fluorescence in a subset of bacteria (Fig. 5B), indicating that the in vivo infectious environment promotes SubI-CysTWA expression, likely driving sulfate import. Whether SubI-CysTWA induction occurs in intracellular bacteria, extracellular bacteria, or both remains to be further explored.

To directly assess the importance of SubI-CysTWA during in vivo infection, wild-type, ∆subI, and ∆subI::subI-cysTWA strains were used to infect C3HeB/FeJ mice, and lung bacterial burden was measured at 7, 21, and 60 d postinfection. As shown in Fig. 5C, the survival of the ∆subI mutant was severely impaired, exhibiting a 1.5- to 2-log reduction in lung bacterial load compared to the wild-type and complemented strains at both 21 and 60 d postinfection. Lung histopathology revealed that mice infected with the mutant strain developed fewer lung infiltrates than those infected with wild-type or complemented strains (Fig. 5D). This observation correlates with the reduced bacterial burden in the lungs of mutant-infected animals. Moreover, immune profiling of lung tissue showed reduced accumulation of immune cells, including T and B cells, in mutant-infected mice compared to wild-type, further supporting the attenuated phenotype of the mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SubI-CysTWA-mediated sulfate acquisition is critical for M. tuberculosis survival during infection, highlighting sulfate as a key sulfur source utilized by the bacillus during in vivo infection. This contrasts with previous studies on M. bovis BCG vaccine strains, where cysA::Tn and subI::Tn mutants exhibited wild-type levels of survival in infected mice (25, 26). Notably, our observation that metabolically active or dividing bacilli increase sulfate utilization suggests that this discrepancy may stem from differences in infection dynamics between the two species. Unlike M. bovis BCG, which exhibits limited replication in lung tissue, M. tuberculosis is capable of active replication, potentially driving its reliance on sulfate uptake for survival in vivo.

SubI-CysTWA-Dependent Sulfate Acquisition Maintains Redox Balance in M. tuberculosis During Infection.

After being imported into M. tuberculosis, sulfate is assimilated by two distinct pathways: the nonreducing branch, which is involved in sulfolipid biosynthesis, and the reducing branch, which leads to the production of hydrogen sulfide (HS-), a precursor of cysteine biosynthesis (15–19) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Subsequently, cysteine plays a key role in the sulfur metabolism of the bacillus, distributing sulfur to a wide range of molecules. In particular, beyond its own function as a reducing molecule, cysteine is essential for the biosynthesis of molecules involved in maintaining redox balance such as mycothiol, ergothioneine, and gamma-glutamylcysteine (51).

Thus, the acquisition of sulfate via the SubI-CysTWA transporter may enable M. tuberculosis to synthesize these sulfur-containing redox buffers during infection. To test this hypothesis, we generated redox-reporting strains of wild-type, ∆subI, and ∆subI::subI-cysTWA M. tuberculosis expressing the Mrx1-roGFP system. This redox-sensitive GFP detects variations in the bacterial redox status and reports these changes through alterations in 405/488 nm excitation properties (52).

To assess the impact of the subI deletion on the ability of M. tuberculosis to maintain its redox balance during infection, we infected C3HeB/FeJ mice with strains expressing the Mrx1-roGFP protein. Confocal microscopy was used to measure the fluorescence of the reduced and oxidized forms of the reporter system in infected lung tissue regions (Fig. 5E). Quantification of the oxidized/reduced ratio in intrapulmonary bacteria revealed a significantly higher ratio in the ∆subI mutant compared to wild-type and complemented strains (Fig. 5F), indicating increased oxidative stress in the sulfate acquisition-deficient mutant.

We further explored the sensitivity of the mutant to oxidative stress in vitro and in vivo. First, we observed a decrease in reduced free thiols in the mutant, compared to the wild-type and complemented strains (Fig. 6A). Consistent with this observation, the mutant exhibited increased sensitivity to nitric oxide and a trend toward greater sensitivity to hydrogen peroxide compared to wild-type and complemented strains (Fig. 6B). To directly assess the contribution of nitric oxide to the mutant’s attenuation in vivo, we infected C57BL/6 wild-type and Nos2-deficient mice. In wild-type mice, the mutant showed a 1.5-log reduction in bacterial burden (Fig. 6C), whereas in Nos2-KO mice, attenuation was still observed but less pronounced, with only a 0.5-log decrease (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that nitric oxide contributes, at least in part, to the in vivo attenuation of the mutant strain.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that SubI-CysTWA-mediated sulfate acquisition is essential for maintaining redox balance in M. tuberculosis during in vivo infection, which is in line with our RNA-seq data showing perturbation of redox-responsive genes in the ΔsubI mutant. Our data further confirm the role of sulfate in supplying the sulfur required for the biosynthesis of redox buffers, reinforcing its importance in M. tuberculosis survival under host conditions.

Discussion and Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that M. tuberculosis sustains sulfur metabolism by exploiting inorganic sulfate in infected macrophages and lung tissue. Our data show that the SubI-CysTWA transporter is the sole sulfate transporter in M. tuberculosis and essential for sulfate acquisition both in vitro and during infection. Using redox-sensitive reporter strains, we further showed that SubI-CysTWA-mediated sulfate import supports redox balance in M. tuberculosis, most likely by supplying sulfur for the synthesis of critical redox buffers, such as cysteine, mycothiol, and ergothioneine. Altogether, these findings highlight sulfate as a vital nutrient for bacterial survival and pathogenesis within the host environment.

The metabolic pathways involved in sulfur metabolism in M. tuberculosis have been progressively elucidated over time (15, 19, 20, 22–25, 27, 41), revealing that this pathogen utilizes diverse sulfur sources and metabolic pathways while complicating our understanding of sulfur acquisition during infection. Previous studies have implicated inorganic sulfur sources in M. tuberculosis survival during in vivo infection. For instance, a ∆cysH strain, deficient in sulfate assimilation, exhibited reduced survival but only during the adaptive phase of infection (41). More recently, mutants lacking the cysteine synthases CysK2 or CysM were also reported to exhibit impaired survival in vivo (53). Our findings build on this body of work, extending it by directly demonstrating that inorganic sulfate is a primary sulfur source for M. tuberculosis during infection. Thus, our findings, along with those of others, challenge the widespread assumption that organic sulfur sources, in particular methionine, are the primary sulfur sources for M. tuberculosis during infection. This is consistent with studies showing that MetB, a key enzyme in the reverse transsulfuration pathway, is not essential for M. tuberculosis survival in mouse models (28), while methionine biosynthesis remains crucial (29).

Despite its ability to use reduced sulfur compounds, our study reveals that M. tuberculosis mainly relies on the uptake and assimilation of inorganic sulfate during in vivo infection, setting it apart metabolically from other bacteria that occupy similar host niches. Many intracellular pathogens, such as Francisella tularensis, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, and Legionella pneumophila, have indeed lost the capacity to assimilate sulfate (54–57). Instead, these organisms exploit host-derived organic sulfur compounds, a strategy that fits within the concept referred to as “nutritional virulence” (58). For example, F. tularensis can utilize glutathione (54), while L. pneumophila (56) and N. meningitidis (59) can scavenge cysteine. These organic sulfur sources are in a reduced state and thus energetically favorable for incorporation into bacterial metabolism. Even among bacteria retaining some sulfur assimilation capacity, this pathway appears dispensable during infection. For example, although L. monocytogenes encodes the cysteine synthase CysK, which enables synthesis from thiosulfate or sulfide, studies indicate that this pathway does not significantly contribute to in vivo survival (55), further supporting the idea that inorganic sulfur sources are largely irrelevant for most intracellular pathogens in the host environment.

In this context, the strategy of M. tuberculosis, which imports sulfate through the SubI-CysTWA ABC transporter and reduces it to sulfide for incorporation into cysteine, is metabolically unusual and costly, particularly given the nutrient-limited and hypoxic conditions of the host lung (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Why would this pathogen rely on such an energy-intensive pathway?

First, cysteine and glutathione are readily degraded or oxidized in inflammatory settings (60–62), which may limit their availability. In addition, when present in excess, these thiols can generate reactive oxygen species via Fenton-like reactions, exacerbating oxidative stress. M. tuberculosis appears to tightly regulate its thiol pool as intracellular cysteine levels are notoriously difficult to detect, likely to avoid redox toxicity. In this vein, controlled acquisition of sulfate and subsequent cysteine biosynthesis might allow M. tuberculosis to avoid importing excess cysteine or glutathione that could be toxic.

Additionally, in contrast to relying on the availability of host-derived reduced thiols, this autonomy in sulfur metabolism is likely part of a broader “metabolic autonomy” strategy (63), allowing M. tuberculosis to synthesize all amino acids and many cofactors de novo. Such independence may help the bacterium resist host-imposed nutrient limitations and maintain redox homeostasis during persistent infection. Besides, uptake or breakdown of host-cell thiols might interfere with host metabolic pathways and redox signaling, thus eliciting immune recognition. Sulfate assimilation, by contrast, may represent a metabolically “silent” strategy, i.e. less likely to trigger immune detection.

Finally, we can hypothesize that reductive sulfate assimilation could serve, itself, as a redox-balancing mechanism. By consuming reducing equivalents, such as reduced ferredoxins and thioredoxins that accumulate under hypoxic conditions, this pathway may help M. tuberculosis maintain redox equilibrium, regardless of its final role in redox buffer synthesis.

Our findings, and those of others (41, 53), identify inorganic sulfate import and assimilation, along with subsequent de novo cysteine biosynthesis, as central pathways supporting M. tuberculosis during infection. Since animal cells do not assimilate inorganic sulfur, these pathogen-specific metabolic pathways present promising targets for the development of new antibacterial compounds. Interestingly, regarding sulfate utilization, the CysA subunit of the SubI-CysTWA transporter has already been identified as a promising target for the development of inhibitory molecules through in silico analysis (64). Additionally, a screening also identified CysH inhibitors with bactericidal activity against nonreplicating M. tuberculosis (65). Downstream of sulfate assimilation, inhibitors targeting the three cysteine synthases have also been developed, with some demonstrating bactericidal activity against M. tuberculosis in vitro (66–68). The identification of inorganic sulfate as a key sulfur source for M. tuberculosis under infectious conditions provides a strong impetus for testing these inhibitors in relevant infection models.

Ultimately, consistent with the close link between sulfur metabolism and redox homeostasis, we demonstrate that impairing sulfate import by deleting subI results in increased sensitivity to nitrosative stress in vitro and in vivo during infection. Supporting this finding, CysH inhibitors have been shown to reduce levels of sulfur-containing metabolites in M. smegmatis and lead to oxidative imbalance in M. tuberculosis (65). Taken together, these results suggest that targeting sulfur acquisition could be an effective strategy to disrupt redox homeostasis and sensitize the bacteria to compounds that induce oxidative stress, particularly antibiotics (69). Given that inhibitors of M. tuberculosis cysteine synthases have already been shown to enhance the efficacy of the first-line anti-tubercular drug isoniazid (39), future studies could explore whether disrupting sulfur metabolism more broadly, in particular through targeting sulfate acquisition, might potentiate additional antimicrobial strategies, opening new avenues for combination therapies against tuberculosis.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions.

M. tuberculosis H37Rv, M. smegmatis mc2155, and mutants or recombinants strains were grown at 37 °C in liquid 7H9 medium (Difco) supplemented with 10% albumin-dextrose-catalase (ADC, Difco) and 0.05% Tyloxapol (Sigma) or on solid 7H11 medium (Difco) supplemented with 10% oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase (OADC, Difco). When required hygromycin (50 µg/mL), streptomycin (25 µg/mL), kanamycin (20 µg/mL), or zeocin (25 µg/mL) were added to the culture medium. 2 mM cysteine was added to cultivate ∆subI strains.

Mycobacterial Growth in Minimal Medium.

M. tuberculosis bacteria cultivated in classical rich 7H9-ADC medium were transferred in minimal M9 medium (Sigma) supplemented with 10 g/L glycerol, 2 g/L citric acid, 2 mM MgSO4, and 0.05% Tyloxapol until exponential phase was reached. After five washes in sulfur-free M9 medium (2 mM MgCl2 replacing MgSO4), bacteria were inoculated at OD600nm 0.025 in triplicates in sulfur-free M9 medium supplemented with different sulfur sources: Cultures were incubated without agitation at 37 °C and growth was monitored by measuring turbidity (McFarland’s units) using a Densimat apparatus (BioMerieux).

M. smegmatis bacteria cultivated in classical rich 7H9 medium were washed twice in DPBS before a 4 h starvation in DPBS. Cultures were inoculated at OD600nm 0.05 in triplicates in homemade minimal medium composed of 0.5 g/L KH2PO4, 10 g/L glycerol, 2 g/L citric acid, 2 mM aspartate solubilized in tap water, buffered to pH 6.5-7.5 and supplemented with 100 µM MgSO4.

Construction of M. tuberculosis and M. smegmatis Mutants and Complemented Strains.

Mutant strains of M. tuberculosis H37Rv were constructed by allelic exchange using the recombineering technology (70) as previously described (37). Briefly, 500 bp upstream and downstream of the target gene were amplified by PCR using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC Buffer (Thermo Scientific) and specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S2). The allelic exchange substrate (AES) was assembled by a three fragments PCR using the upstream fragment, a zeocin-resistance cassette flanked by two identical and parallelly oriented dif sites, and the downstream fragment. The recipient strain carrying pjv53H plasmid was cultivated in 7H9 medium supplemented with 10% ADC, 0.05% Tyloxapol, and hygromycin until exponential phase was reached. 0.2% acetamide (Sigma) was added to induce the expression of recombineering enzymes from the pjv53H plasmid (70). Competent bacteria were electroporated with 100 ng of AES before 48 h recovery in 7H9 medium without antibiotics. Bacteria were then plated onto 7H11 agar plates supplemented with zeocin (25 µg/mL) and hygromycin (20 µg/mL). Zeocin-resistant colonies were picked and cultivated in 7H9 medium until spontaneous recombination between identical dif sites and loss of zeocin-resistance cassette that was phenotypically verified. Multiple mutants were obtained by electroporation of a new AES harboring a different dif site.

To construct mutant strains of M. smegmatis mc2155, two DNA fragments of approximately 0.5 kb, flanking the target gene, were amplified by PCR (DNA polymerase Phusion™, Thermo Scientific) with primers allowing the insertion of SapI restriction sites at the upstream and downstream ends. The zeocin resistance cassette flanked by the dif site variants was amplified by PCR with a Phusion™ DNA polymerase and primers allowing the introduction of a SapI restriction site. The three fragments were assembled with The NEBridge® Golden Gate Assembly method (New England Biolabs®) within plasmid pLJR965 also possessing a SapI restriction site. After enzymatic digestion with SapI (Thermo Scientific), the upstream and downstream fragments of the gene of interest were assembled on either side of the zeocin resistance gene using a T4 polymerase (Thermo Scientific) within this plasmid.

The subI-cysTWA operon (rv2400c-rv2397c) preceded by ≈700 bp upstream including its native promoter were amplified from M. tuberculosis genomic DNA by PCR using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC Buffer (Thermo Scientific) and specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S3). pGMCS-subI-cysTWA plasmid was constructed using Infusion cloning (Takara) and pDE43-MCS as destination vector. Complementation plasmid was electroporated into recipient M. tuberculosis H37Rv ∆subI::dif5 pvj53H strain or into M. smegmatis mc2155 ∆msmeg_4533 and integrated at the attL5 mycobacteriophage insertion site in the glyV tRNA gene.

Deletion of the targeted gene and complementation were verified by PCR on genomic DNA (SI Appendix, Table S4) or RT-qPCR on reverse transcribed total RNA using specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S5).

Construction of M. tuberculosis Reporter Strains.

To construct the plasmid reporting the activity of the subI-cysTWA promoter, a fragment of ≈700 bp upstream of the subI-cysTWA operon was amplified by PCR using specific primers (SI Appendix, Table S3) and cloned by Infusion cloning (Takara) upstream of the mCherry-encoding sequence into a recipient pGMCS plasmid harboring GFP gene controlled by the constitutive P1 promoter.

The Mrx1-roGFP redox reporter system was constructed as described in (52). The Mrx1 (Rv3198A) coding sequence was amplified from M. tuberculosis H37Rv genomic DNA. The open reading frame (ORF) of linker-roGFP2 was amplified from the vector pLPCX cyto Grx1-roGFP2. PCR fragments were assembled and cloned into linearized pGEM-5Z plasmid by Gibson Assembly. The Mrx1-roGFP2 linear fragment has been cloned in pvv16 vector.

Reporter plasmids were electroporated into recipient M. tuberculosis strains and fluorescence of recombinant strains was verified by flow cytometry.

Sensitivity to Oxidative and Nitrosative Stresses.

Bacterial cultures were grown to an OD600 of approximately 1.0 in 7H9 medium supplemented with 0.05% tyloxapol, 2% glycerol, 10% ADC, and 2 mM methionine for the ΔsubI strain. The cells were then washed three times, dissociated, and resuspended in a modified 7H9 medium lacking a sulfate source and supplemented with 0.05% Tyloxapol and 2% glycerol. A total of 107 bacteria per sample were treated with the following stress-inducing agents: 5 mM of a freshly prepared hydrogen peroxide solution (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.5 mM of DETA-NONOate (Thermo Scientific). Bacterial survival was assessed by plating serial dilutions from each condition onto 7H10 solid medium supplemented with 10% oleic acid–albumin–dextrose–catalase (OADC) and 2 mM methionine for the ΔsubI strain at day 0 and subsequent time points.

RNA Extraction and Sequencing.

Total RNA was extracted from 10 mL of bacterial cultures grown to logarithmic phase (OD600 0.8) using RNeasy kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications aspreviously described (71). RNA samples were treated with DNAse I (Turbo DNA free kit, Ambion). Total bulk RNA sequencing was performed at Single Cell Discoveries, a (single cell) sequencing provider located in the Netherlands, using an adapted version of the VASA-seq protocol (72). Each extracted total RNA sample was barcoded with CEL-seq primers (73) during the enzymatic lysis. It was then followed by fragmentation, end repair, poly(A)-tailing, reverse transcription, and second-strand synthesis. After second-strand synthesis, the barcoded samples were pooled into one library and amplified using in vitro transcription. Further, ribosomal aRNA was depleted. After depletion, adapter ligation, reverse transcription, and indexing PCR were performed to prepare a cDNA library. The cDNA library was paired end sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeqXPlus™ platform (read 1: 26 cycles, index read: 6 cycles, read 2: 60 cycles).

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cutadapt (v4.9) to remove adapter sequences and homopolymers, followed by Ribodetector (v0.3.1) to filter out ribosomal reads. Processed reads were then aligned to the M. tuberculosis H37Rv reference genome (assembly ASM19595v2) using STARsolo (v2.7.11b), with the parameter−soloFeatures set to GeneFull_Ex50pA.

The filtration of low-abundance reads, normalization, DEG analysis, and z-score calculation were carried out using edgeR (74). DEGs were defined based on an absolute log2(fold change) > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Macrophages Infection.

Bone marrow from femurs and tibias of C57BL/6 was flushed and extracted cells were cultivated for 7 d at 37 °C in presence of 5% CO2 in RPMI GlutaMAX HEPES medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 10u/mL penicillin, 10 µg/mL streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL M-CSF (Preprotech) to obtain differentiated macrophages. At 7 d, macrophages were transferred to 24-well plates (3.105 cells/well) on cover glasses in antibiotic-free medium. Infections were performed in nine wells at a multiplicity of infection of 1 for 4 h at 37 °C. Cells were then washed three times with DPBS before addition of fresh medium. Five days postinfection, cells were lysed in 200 µL H2O 0.01% Triton X-100 (Sigma). Three wells per condition were pooled and, after centrifugation (3 min, 9000×g), the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of RPMI medium and used to infect new macrophages (1 well) for five additional days. At 0, 5, and 10 d of infection, cells were lysed using Triton X-100 and cell lysates were plated onto agar medium for CFU scoring.

Mouse Infection for CFUs Counting.

All procedures including animal studies were conducted in our facilities which fulfill all legal requirements (F31555005 agreement) and by qualified personnel to minimize discomfort for the animals. All procedures were conducted in strict accordance with French laws and regulations and all protocols were approved by the French Ministry (APAFIS #40827- 2023020711364609 v8). 6- to 8-wk-old female C3HeB/FeJ or C57BL/6 (Charles River) mice, or Nos2-KO mice (gift from Philippe Bousso, Institut Pasteur, Paris, France), were infected through aerosols inhalation using a DSI Buxco Inhalation Tower where mice were exposed for 17 min to aerosols produced from a 5 mL suspension containing 2.106 bacteria. Then, 7, 21, 42, or 60 d after infection, five mice per tested strain were killed and lung homogenates were plated on 7H11 agar plates supplemented with OADC and 2 mM cysteine for CFUs counting.

Histology.

6- to 8-wk-old female C3HeB/FeJ mice were anesthetized with a cocktail of ketamine (60 mg/kg, Merial) and xylasine (10 mg/kg, Bayer) and infected intranasally with ≈100 CFUs of fluorescent mycobacteria in 25 µL PBS 0.01% Tween 80. At 60 d postinfection, mice were killed through intraperitoneal injection of 50 µL euthasol (400 mg/mL sodic pentobarbital). Lungs were intratracheally perfused with 1 mL agarose 2% (UltraPure LMP Agarose Invitrogen) and fixed for 24 h at 4 °C in periodate-lysine-paraformaldehyde. After being transferred in 10, 20, and 30% sucrose in 0.1 M Phosphate Buffer, lungs were frozen in Optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT). 10 µm-thick sections were cut using a cryostat before staining with TOPRO-3 (Molecular Probes) and acquisition by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Zeiss LSM710). For histopathological analysis, lung samples were embedded in Tissue Freezing Medium (TFM) were sectioned at 20 µm and stained with hematoxylin (BioGnost) and eosin (Sigma-Aldrich). The area of infiltrated lung tissue was measured using Fiji software to perform histopathological scoring of TB lesions in mice infected with the H37Rv, ΔsubI, or complemented strains.

Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry.

For 13C labeling, bacteria were grown in 7H9 medium supplemented with ADC, 0.05% Tyloxapol, and 10 g/L 13C3-glycerol (Sigma, Purity: 99 atom %13C). Bone-marrow derived macrophages plated on glass slices covered with a thin transparent conductive layer of Indium-Tin oxide (ITO) (PGO, Praezisions Glas & Optik, GmbH, Germany) were infected with 13C-labeled bacteria at a multiplicity of infection of 1. Using glass slices allowed the control of sample preparations and the repair of infected cells using confocal microscopy before nanoSIMS analysis. After 4 h at 37 °C in RPMI GlutaMAX HEPES (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, infected cells were washed three time with DPBS and medium was replaced by modified glutamine-, methionine-, cystine-free RPMI medium (Sigma R7513) supplemented with 300 mg/L glutamine, 15 mg/L methionine, 200 µM 15N-cystine (Sigma, 600105), and 2 mM 33S-sulfate (5X excess, Sigma 719374). After three days, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 2 h and washed three times with distilled water.

After drying in air at room temperature, the ITO glass slices were introduced into a NanoSIMS-50 Ion microprobe (CAMECA, Gennevilliers, France) operated in scanning mode (75, 76). The detailed analytical conditions for SIMS imaging were described previously (8). For this study, a tightly focused Cs+ primary ion beam steps over the surface of the sample and four secondary ion species (12C14N-, 12C15N- or 13C14N- alternatively, and 32S-, 33S-) were monitored in parallel to create images of these selected ion species. The location of bacteria inside the host cell was highlighted by high 13C composition.

For the quantification of 15N/14N and 33S/32S ratios, together with the measurement of 13C composition, one would imagine a parallel detection of 6 isotopic ion species, 12C-, 13C-, 12C14N-, 12C15N-, 32S-, and 33S-. To achieve the detection of the 6 species using a 5-detectors nanoSIMS, a pair of 2 different combinations of 4 ion species, first with 12C14N-, 12C15N-, 32S-, and 33S-, then with 12C14N-, 13C14N-, 32S-, and 33S-, were applied. In practice, the 12C15N- and 13C14N- can be easily selected, by using the electrical beam deviator in front of the electron multiplier assembly of the same detector. The image acquisition was therefore carried out using multiframe mode by interlacing the above 2 combinations. In such a way, up to 80 frames were acquired for each combination of ions species. During the acquisition, the primary beam intensity was 1 pA with a typical probe size of 100 nm and the raster size was about 30 µm. The image definition was 512x512 pixels, with a dwell time of 0.5 ms per pixel. The overall counting time for 12C15N- or 13C14N- was 40 ms, and the one for 12C14N-, 32S-, and 33S- was 80 ms so as to ensure better counting statistics for 33S/32S ratio measurement. The total analysis time was about 6 h.

Image treatment was performed using ImageJ software (77) by means of OpenMIMS plug-in (78). After raw data extraction, multiframe images were properly aligned using 12C14N- images as reference before a summed image was obtained for each ion species. A map of 13C atomic fraction was then derived from 12C14N- and 13C14N- images as 13C14N/(12C14N + 13C14N). The map of 15N/14N ratio was obtained by 12C14N- and 12C15N- images, while the one of 33S/32S ratio by 32S- and 33S- images. All the isotopic maps were displayed using Fire mode LUT (lookup table) in ImageJ. Further, regions of interest (ROI) were manually defined based on the 13C composition map so as to outline individual bacterium for 15N/14N ratio and 33S/32S ratio extraction.

Metabolite Extraction.

The bacteria were cultivated to an OD600 of 1 in a medium containing 7H9-0.05% Tyloxapol, 10% ADC. The bacteria were then centrifuged and resuspended in DPBS at a concentration three times higher than the original. One milliliter of each sample was then centrifuged and resuspended in 1 mL of quenching solution (ACN/MeOH/H2O at a ratio of 40:40:20, v/v/v). The bacteria were lysed using glass beads in a bead beater (FastPrep-24™ 5G, MP Biomedicals; three cycles of 45 s). After centrifugation for 5 min, the resulting supernatant was collected and filtered twice through Spin-X columns (0.2 µm) at 14,000 rpm for 15 min. The bacterial extracts were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo FisherScientific).

Free Thiol Quantification.

The Fluorometric Thiol Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) was used to quantify free thiols in bacterial extracts according to the supplier’s instructions.

Statistical Analyses.

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad software). The statistical test used is mentioned in the respective figure legends. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; ns, not significant.
