Harnessing NLRX1: a new frontier in mitigating inflammation in pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Yingchou Xiao, Zhigui Cai, Mengcai Bai, Xingan Wu, Wenlan Wang, Rongrong Liu, Ying Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH) serves as a crucial link in the pathogenesis of chronic high-altitude diseases and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and ultimately may lead to right heart failure. Hypoxia triggers immune responses and inflammatory processes, which contribute to vascular remodeling and elevated pulmonary artery pressure. NLRX1, a member of the Nod-like receptor family, plays a significant regulatory role in immunity, antioxidation, and apoptosis. However, its role in the occurrence and development of HPH remains unknown.MethodsWild type (WT) C57BL/6 mice and NLRX1 knockout (NLRX1−/−) Mice were subjected to intermittent chronic hypoxia for 6 weeks to establish a hypoxic pulmonary hypertension model that is similar to the moderate pulmonary hypertension (PH) induced by hypoxia in humans. Subsequently, data on hemodynamics and pulmonary pathomorphology were collected. Additionally, bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) were cultured to determine the effects of up-regulating and down-regulating NLRX1 on cell function under hypoxia exposure. Western blotting or reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was utilized to detect changes in inflammation factors and oxidative stress-related indicators in rat lung tissue and cultured BMDMs.ResultsHypoxia downregulates the expression of NLRX1 in the lung tissues of mice and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM). Chronic hypoxia significantly increases right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP), the ratio of right ventricle weight to left ventricle plus septum weight (RV/LV + S), and the medial width of pulmonary arterioles in NLRX1-knockout (NLRX1−/−) mice. NLRX1 mediates the hemodynamic response and right ventricular hypertrophy in mice with hypoxic pulmonary hypertension. The deficiency of NLRX1 upregulates inflammatory mediators by activating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and simultaneously promotes oxidative stress and the activation of nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2). The activator NX-13 of NLRX1 can reduce the production of inflammatory cytokines in BMDMs stimulated by hypoxia and mitigate oxidative stress.ConclusionsNLRX1 may suppress hypoxia-induced lung inflammation and alleviate hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Therefore, targeted up-regulation of NLRX1 may offer a new strategy for the treatment of HPH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-025-03364-w.

Background

Hypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH) is a condition characterized by elevated pulmonary artery pressure due to prolonged exposure to a hypoxic environment. It is prevalent among individuals residing at high altitudes and those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [1]. Pulmonary vascular remodeling is generally considered as a primary cause of increased pulmonary artery pressure, while hypoxia is agreed to be the pivotal trigger of HPH, stimulating inflammatory response within the immune system. Persistent hypoxia induces the proliferation, migration and phenotypic alteration of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), leading to the thickening of vascular wall and narrowing of lumen [2]. Emerging evidence indicates that hypoxia-induced inflammation and metabolic dysregulation in vascular cells contribute to the development of HPH. Perivascular inflammation is closely associated with intimal and medial thickening of the vascular wall [3]. Histopathological examinations reveal inflammatory infiltrates macrophages, mast cells and neutrophils surrounding pulmonary vascular lesions in HPH patients [4]. Immune cells not only directly interact with pulmonary vascular cells but also release inflammatory mediators that promote the proliferation of PASMCs or induce endothelial cell dysfunction. These processes together drive vascular remodeling, leading to elevated pulmonary artery pressure.

The Nod-like receptor (NLR) family is a vital group of pattern recognition receptors in human body, primarily orchestrating the innate immune response and upholding internal homeostasis [5]. Among these receptors, NLRX1 holds a prominent position, predominantly localized within the cytoplasm and mitochondria, where it plays a central role in regulating immunity, antioxidation, and apoptosis [6]. Researches have reported that NLRX1 negatively regulate the lipopolysaccharide-induced NF-κB activation downstream of Toll-like receptors 4 (TLR4) by interacting with TNF receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) in fibroblasts or macrophages, forming a complex [7]. In T cell immune response, NLRX1 modulates T cell activation and differentiation by influencing NF-κB signaling [8, 9]. Notably, NLRX1 exhibits a dual functionality in both inflammatory response and immune regulation [10]. It promotes autophagy and apoptosis to combat pathogenic microorganisms and tumor cells, while simultaneously hindering inflammatory pathways to mitigate inflammation. The pivotal roles played by NLRX1 are evident in the pathogenesis and progression of various diseases, encompassing viral infections, tumors and autoimmune disorders [11–15]. Although the specific involvement of NLRX1 in HPH remains unexplored, existing studies indicate that NLRX1 supplementation can markedly reduces lung inflammation, displaying an inverse relationship with the expression level of inflammatory cytokines [16, 17]. These observations point to a potential role of NLRX1 in ameliorating hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension by suppressing inflammatory response in lung tissues.

In summary, NLRX1 acts as a suppressor of NF-κB, a pivotal pathway in hypoxia-induce inflammation and oxidative stress. NLRX1 was reported to inhibit NF-κB signaling across diverse cell types, suggesting that its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties may be influenced by hypoxic conditions. Consequently, further investigation are warranted to elucidate the precise contribution of NLRX1 in the context of HPH.

Methods

Experimental animals

Wild type (WT) C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University (China). NLRX1 knockout (NLRX1−/−) C57BL/6 mice were generated in collaboration with the Department of Microbiology at the Fourth Military Medical University [18]. Both Mice were male, aged 6–8 weeks. The protocols in this research were sanctioned by the Institutional Animal Ethics and Use Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University.

Modeling and grouping of PH mice

WT and NLRX1−/− Mice were randomized into 4 groups, i.e., Control, NLRX1−/−, Hypoxia, Hypoxia + NLRX1−/− groups, with 20 mice in each group. Mice were placed in a hypobaric chamber (Hypoxia, Hypoxia + NLRX1−/−, 10% O2) or room air (Control, NLRX1−/−, 21% O2) for 6 weeks. Throughout the study, all mice were given unrestricted access to food and water under both hypoxia and normoxia conditions.

Hemodynamics and hypertrophy measurement

Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) of Mice was measured after pentobarbital sodium anesthesia. The ribs were exposed by surgical operation, and the pressure transducer was cannulated into the right ventricle after connected with the intravenous needle, and the right ventricular pressure was continuously detected for at least 30 s [19]. All data were recorded and analyzed using Powerlab data-acquisition system (AD Instrument). Following the data collection, the mice were euthanized by immediate exsanguination with their pulmonary and hearts tissues were harvested. The right ventricle (RV) was separated from the left ventricle (LV) plus septal wall (S), and both of them were weighed to calculate the ratio of right ventricular free wall to left ventricular plus septal weight (RV/LV + S) [20].

Histopathological examination of right ventricular and lung

The pulmonary and cardiac tissues extracted from the Mice were soaked in paraformaldehyde and fixed for 24 h. Subsequently, these tissues were embedded within wax blocks, from which thin sections of 5 μm were cut. Tissue sections were examined with a microscope after hematoxylin-eosin staining. The vascular wall thickness (WT), vascular external diameter (ED), vascular wall area (WA), total vascular area (TA) were measured to calculate WT% (WT/ED) and WA% (WA/TA) [21]. Right ventricular thickness was measured by Olympus system.

Immunofluorescence

The lung sections were initially rinsed three times with PBS, followed by permeabilization through soaking in Triton X-100. The sections were first blocked with an immunofluorescence blocking solution (P0102, Beyotime), then incubated with anti-CD4 (ab133616, 1:500, Abcam), anti-F4/80 (29414-1-AP, 1:200, Proteintech), anti-α-SMA (A17910, 1:200, Abclonal), anti-Nrf2 (A11159, 1:100, Abclonal) at 4 ℃ throughout the night. The sections were then incubated with secondary fluorescent antibodies (ab150077, 1:500, ab150115, 1:200, Abcam) for 1 h and DAPI (C1005, Beyotime) for 30 min, respectively. The fluorescence was imaged through fluorescent microscope and data were processed with Caseviewer.

Isolation and culture of bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Mice of the WT and NLRX1−/−, aged between 4 and 6 weeks, were selected for the isolation of bone marrow cells [22]. Following erythrocyte lysis, the extracted bone marrow cells were resuspended in 2 mL of PBS. Next, cells were placed in RPMI1640 with 10% FBS (10099-141, Gibco) and a concentration of 10 ng/ml m-CSF (HZ-1192, Proteintech). Subsequently, the suspension was placed into 6-well plates following a 24-hour incubation. The proliferation and differentiation of BMDMs extended over a week, necessitating the renewal of the medium on days 2, 4, and 6.

Cell treatment and grouping of BMDMs

WT and NLRX1−/− BMDMs were randomized into 5 groups, i.e., Control, NLRX1−/−, Hypoxia, Hypoxia + NLRX1−/−, Hypoxia + NX-13 groups. NX-13 (HY-141521, MedChemExpress) was prepared in DMSO with the final concentration adjusted to 0.5 µM [8, 23]. The hypoxia groups were hypoxically cultured in an incubator with 5% O2, and normoxia groups were cultured in a incubator with 21% O2 for 24–48 h. Cell samples were then collected for subsequent experiments.

RNA extraction and detection

RNA was isolated from the lung tissues and BMDMs with RNA extraction kit (R0026, Beyotime). The concentration of RNA was measured and diluted, followed by the synthesis of cDNA through the SweScript RT cDNA synthesis kit (G3333, Servicebio). The TB green PCR kit (G3320, Servicebio) was mixed with the samples. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on Biosystems PCR machine. The levels of gene expression in relation to controls were evaluated with 2-ΔΔCt formula. Expression was normalized to the mean expression of all control individuals.

The following primer were used: β-actin, 5’-CACGATGGAGGGGCCGGACTCATC-3’ (forward) and 5’ -TAAAGACCTCTATGCCAACACAGT-3’ (reverse); NLRX1, 5’-CAGATTGGTAACAAAGGAGCCA-3’ (forward) and 5’-CGTTCGGTTTATCTTCAGAGCA-3’ (reverse).

Western blot

Total proteins were isolated from lung tissues and BMDMs, and the concentration was quantified with BCA assay kit (P0012, Beyotime). Samples containing 50 µg of protein were applied to 10% SDS-PAGE and subjected to one-hour gel electrophoresis. Following this, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes and treated with western blot blocking solution (P30500, NCM Biotech). Membranes were incubated with anti-NLRX1 (17215-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), anti-IL-1β (A16288, 1:1000, Abclonal), anti-IL-18 (A1115, 1:1000, Abclonal), anti-pNF-κB p65 (82335-1-RR, 1:1000, Proteintech), anti-NF-κB p65 (10745-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech), anti-Nrf2 (A0674, 1:1000, Abclonal), anti-Keap1 (10503-2-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech) and anti-β-actin (ab8227, 1:5000, Abcam) at 4 ℃ throughout the night, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody (RGAR001, 1:5000, Proteintech) for 1 h. Detection was performed using the chemiluminescence system (CLINX ChemiScope).

Cytokines measurement

After aspiration and centrifugation of the supernatant from the BMDMs’ culture, the concentration of IL-1β and IL-6 was measured by enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D). Instructions for use were provided by manufacturer.

Measurement of superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

The protein levels in the supernatants of lung tissue and BMDMs homogenates were measured after centrifugation. Subsequently, the total SOD activity in the supernatant was measured using the total SOD assay kit (S0101S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of total antioxidant capacity (TAC)

The protein levels in the supernatants of lung tissue and BMDMs homogenates were measured after centrifugation. Subsequently, the TAC in the supernatant was measured using the total antioxidant capacity assay kit (S0121, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analyses

The results were described as mean ± SEM, and analysis was carried out with the GraphPad prism 9.0. Significant differences among the two groups were confirmed using Student’s t test. Determination of differences among multiple groups was conducted through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc test as appropriate. P < 0.05 was taken to denote statistical significant.

Hypoxia down-regulated the expression of NLRX1 in lung tissues of mice

NLRX1 is recognized for negatively regulating inflammation and oxidation. However, the impact of hypoxia on NLRX1 function has not been investigated. Therefore, we assessed the expression of NLRX1 in the lung tissues of mice with HPH. Our findings, depicted in Fig. 1A and B, reveal a reduction in both mRNA and protein levels of NLRX1 in lung tissues of HPH mice compared to the control group.

NLRX1 deficiency aggravated pulmonary vascular remodeling in HPH mice

In the prior investigation, we observed a down-regulation of NLRX1 in lung tissues of HPH mice. We thereby postulated that the absence of NLRX1 could exacerbate the vascular remodeling linked to HPH. Then, HPH in NLRX1−/− mice were exposed to hypoxia to testify this postulation. To evaluate the effect of pulmonary vascular remodeling, we measured the media thickness of pulmonary arteries. HE staining revealed a notable increase in the WT% and WA% in the lungs of HPH mice compared to the normoxia group. Moreover, the WT% and WA% of NLRX1−/− mice were significantly higher than those in WT mice in the hypoxia group. Additionally, HE staining demonstrated that the thickness of the pulmonary smooth muscle layer in NLRX1−/− mice in the hypoxia group exceeded that in WT mice (Fig. 2A and B). Subsequently, we conducted immunofluorescence staining to label α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in lung sections. The findings showed an increasing number of α-SMA positive cells surrounding pulmonary vessels in NLRX1−/− mice with HPH compared to WT mice, further indicating the exacerbation of vascular remodeling (Fig. 2C).

NLRX1 mediated hemodynamic response and right ventricular hypertrophy in HPH mice

Hemodynamic changes, primarily resulting from vascular remodeling, are primary indicator of pulmonary hypertension. Vascular dynamics are assessed through the measurement of RVSP. As illustrated in Fig. 2D, Exposure to hypoxia led to a significant increase in RVSP in mice with HPH. In particular, RVSP was higher in NLRX1−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2D). These hemodynamic changes can further strain the right ventricle, ultimately causing right ventricular hypertrophy. Histological analysis revealed thickening of the right ventricular in HPH mice compared to the normoxia group, with more pronounced hypertrophy observed in NLRX1−/− mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 2E). RV/LV + S is a commonly used indicator of right ventricular hypertrophy. Results indicated an increase in RV/LV + S in HPH mice compared to the normoxia group, with even higher values observed in NLRX1−/− mice under hypoxic condition compared to WT mice (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these findings suggest that the deletion of NLRX1 exacerbates HPH in mice.

NLRX1 deficiency up-regulated inflammatory mediators though activating NF-κB in lung tissues of HPH mice

Hypoxia primarily stimulates inflammatory responses in pulmonary hypertension, by inducing the production of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18. To investigate the role of NLRX1 in hypoxia-induced inflammation, we assessed the levels of inflammatory cytokines in the lung tissues of mice in each experimental group. The levels of IL-1β and IL-18 were elevated in the lung tissues of mice with HPH compared to the normoxia group. Moreover, the levels of these cytokines in NLRX1−/− mice with HPH were significantly higher than those in WT mice (Fig. 3A).

NF-κB is a key transcription factor that manipulates the generation of inflammatory mediators during the development of HPH [24]. Given its mediating role, our study investigated the impact of NLRX1 on NF-κB activation in the lung tissues of HPH mice. Our results revealed that the phosphorylation of p65 in lung tissues of HPH mice was elevated compared to the normoxia group. Importantly, the absence of NLRX1 significantly augmented p65 phosphorylation in the hypoxia group (Fig. 3B).

NLRX1 deficiency promoted oxidative stress and Nrf2 activation in lung tissues of HPH mice

In HPH, mitochondrial dysfunction due to hypoxia disrupts the respiratory chain and membrane potential, leading to the production and release of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Elevated ROS levels can trigger various signaling pathways, contributing to inflammation or enhancing the proliferation and migration of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells [25]. While existing studies have identified that the mitochondrial localization of NLRX1 and its role in regulating ROS production, the specific function of NLRX1 in a hypoxic environment remains elucidated. In this study, we evaluated the activities of TAC and SOD in the lung tissues of mice across different experimental groups. Our results demonstrated increased TAC and SOD activities in the lung tissues of HPH mice compared to the normoxia group, with a more pronounced elevation observed in NLRX1−/− mice under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 4A and B).

Under oxidative stress, generated oxidants can interfere with the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction, allowing Nrf2 to translocate into the nucleus and activates the transcription of antioxidant genes [26]. To investigate the effect of NLRX1 on Nrf2 activation in HPH, we examined the protein expression levels of Keap1 and Nrf2 in mice lung tissues. The results showed decreased Keap1 levels and increased Nrf2 expression in the lungs of HPH mice following hypoxia exposure. The deficiency of NLRX1 in HPH mice might have exacerbated this shift in expression patterns (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining of Nrf2 in lung sections revealed a significantly higher number of Nrf2-postive cells in NLRX1−/− mice compared to WT mice, indicating that NLRX1 deficiency promotes the generation of oxidative stress-inducing molecules in HPH, leading to excessive Nrf2 activation (Fig. 4D).

Hypoxia affected the recruitment of immune cells through NLRX1

Previous studies have shown that NLRX1 can inhibit the activation of macrophages and T cells. Immunofluorescence staining was performed on CD4+T cells and macrophages in the lungs of mice to investigate how hypoxia affects immune cell recruitment through NLRX1. The results in Fig. 5 demonstrated that upon hypoxia stimulation, the presence of CD4-postive cells in the lungs of HPH mice decreased significantly, while the number of F4/80-postive cells increased markedly. Additionally, there were more F4/80-postive cells in NLRX1−/− mice than WT mice in the hypoxia group (Fig. 5B), indicating its potential role in macrophage recruitment during HPH rather than CD4+T cells.

Hypoxia down-regulated the expression of NLRX1 in BMDMs

Our prior investigation revealed suppressed NLRX1 expression in the lung tissue with HPH, accompanied by a notable accumulation of macrophages. Given the intimate relationship between macrophages, inflammation and oxidative stress in HPH, it is crucial to identify the in vivo anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of NLRX1. We assessed the NLRX1 expression level in BMDMs. Upon hypoxia stimulation, both the mRNA and protein levels of NLRX1 in BMDMs decreased at 24 and 48 h compared to the control group (Fig. 6A and B).

NLRX1 deficiency up-regulated inflammatory cytokines production by promoting NF-κB activation in hypoxia-stimulated BMDMs

The concentration of inflammatory cytokines in the culture medium of BMDMs from different groups was measured. After 24 and 48 h of hypoxia stimulation, both WT and NLRX1−/− BMDMs showed an increase in IL-1β and IL-6 levels in the culture medium. Notably, NLRX1−/− BMDMs in the hypoxia group exhibited higher cytokines concentrations compared to WT BMDMs at each time point (Fig. 6C and D).

Previously, we have found that NF-κB overactivated in the lung tissues of NLRX1−/− mice with HPH. In addition to this discovery, we investigated NF-κB activation in BMDMs. And found that post hypoxia stimulation, phosphorylation of p65 in WT BMDMs increased at 24 h compared to the normoxia group. Moreover, phosphorylation of p65 in NLRX1−/− BMDMs in the hypoxia group was significantly elevated compared to both WT BMDMs and the normoxia group at each time point (Fig. 6E).

NLRX1 deficiency promoted oxidative stress and Nrf2 activation in hypoxia-stimulated BMDMs

The absence of NLRX1 was found to contribute to the activation of Nrf2 and a heightened antioxidant capacity in the lung tissues of HPH mice. To explore the potential role of NLRX1 as an antioxidant in macrophages, we assessed the TAC and SOD activities in BMDMs across different experimental groups. Following hypoxia stimulation, TAC and SOD activities in BMDMs increased at 24 h but showed no significant difference at 48 h compared to the normoxia group. Notably, NLRX1−/− BMDMs exhibited significantly higher TAC and SOD activities than WT BMDMs after 24 h of hypoxia stimulation (Fig. 7A and B). Subsequent analysis of Keap1 and Nrf2 expression in BMDMs revealed that, Keap1 expression decreased in WT BMDMs following 24 h of hypoxia, while Nrf2 increased compared to the normoxia group, with no significant differences at 48 h. Importantly, the deletion of NLRX1 induced more pronounced alterations in Keap1 and Nrf2 expression in hypoxia-induced BMDMs at each time point compared to WT BMDMs and the normoxia group (Fig. 7C).

NX-13 reduced inflammatory cytokines production by attenuating NF-κB activation in hypoxia-stimulated BMDMs

NX-13, a symmetrical triphenyl compound, is specifically engineered to activate NLRX1, thereby exhibiting anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Recent research has demonstrated that NX-13 can modulated the differentiation and metabolism of T cells through NF-κB [8]. To identify how NX-13 influences macrophage function under hypoxic condition, we assessed the levels of inflammatory cytokines in the medium of BMDMs following a pretreatment with NX-13. Our findings indicated that NX-13 pretreatment effectively reduces the concentration of IL-1β and IL-6 in the BMDMs medium at 24 and 48 h post-hypoxia induction (Fig. 8A and B). Furthermore, we evaluated the activation of NF-κB in BMDMs and observed a decrease in the phosphorylation of p65 in hypoxia-induced BMDMs at each time point following NX-13 pretreatment (Fig. 8C).

NX-13 alleviated oxidant stress and attenuated Nrf2 activation in hypoxia-stimulated BMDMs

NX-13 was found to enhance the expression of antioxidant enzymes and suppress ROS generation in T cells. Thus, we investigated the TAC and SOD activities in BMDMs. It is worth noting that, pretreatment with NX-13 led to a reduction in TAC and SOD activities at 24 h compared to the hypoxia group, with no significant difference observed at 48 h (Fig. 9A and B). Previous research done by our group indicated that the absence of NLRX1 enhanced Nrf2 activation in lung tissues and BMDMs from HPH mice. Subsequently, we assessed the levels of Keap1 and Nrf2 in BMDMs. Interestingly, NX-13 pretreatment restored the Keap1 expression at 24 h of hypoxia exposure but reduced Nrf2 expression at each time point (Fig. 9C). Taken together the findings from animal and cellular studies, we postulate that NX-13 may mitigate oxidant stress and NF-κB activation by stimulating NLRX1 in macrophage, thereby attenuating cell aggregation and diminishing the production of inflammatory cytokines.

Discussion

Our study reveals that hypoxia down-regulates NLRX1 expression in lung tissues and macrophages in mice. We provide the first time evidence that NLRX1 deficiency results in significant pulmonary artery remodeling and right ventricular hypertrophy in HPH mice, along with a notable increase in lung macrophages and inflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest that NLRX1’s protective role in HPH may involve the suppression of macrophage activity and infiltration, potentially through negative regulation of the oxidative stress and NF-κB activation.

Pathologically, HPH features the elevated pulmonary artery pressure leading to right ventricular hypertrophy. Pulmonary artery vascular remodeling is a key initiating factor in this process [27]. Hypoxia triggers abnormal proliferation and migration of PASMCs, resulting in progressive luminal narrowing, reduces vascular compliance, increased vascular resistance and the development of pulmonary hypertension [28]. Concurrently, the right ventricle adapts to the elevated pulmonary pressure by increasing myocardial contractility, leading to cardiomyocyte hypertrophy [29]. NLRX1, a member of the NLR family, acts as a pattern recognition receptor. Emerging evidence suggests that NLRX1 plays a negative regulatory role in various infectious and autoimmune diseases. Dysregulation of NLRX1 expression or activity often exacerbates the disease progression [30, 31].

In this study, we utilized a low-pressure oxygen chamber to simulate the hypoxic conditions experienced at an altitude of 5500 m, thereby inducing pulmonary hypertension in mice. Following the hypoxia-induced pulmonary hypertension, a decrease in NLRX1 expression was observed in the lung tissues of WT mice. Histopathological assessment revealed that the lack of NLRX1 resulted in pronounced pathological alterations in the lungs and right ventricles of mice with HPH. Comparative analysis between WT and NLRX1−/− mice, following HPH induction demonstrated an elevated WT% to WA% ratio in NLRX1−/− mice, indicating abnormal constriction of the pulmonary vascular lumen. Additionally, a notably increase in the population of α-SMA-positive cells suggested abnormal smooth muscle cell proliferation. Moreover, NLRX1−/− mice exhibited higher RVSP and RV/(LV + S) ratios compared to WT mice. Taken together, our results indicate a potential protective function of NLRX1 in HPH. Pathologically, the absence of NLRX1 exacerbates pulmonary artery remodeling and right ventricular hypertrophy, leading to increased pulmonary artery pressure.

Prolonged hypoxia exposure induces oxidative stress and inflammation, key factors in the development of pulmonary hypertension, by enhancing the proliferation and migration of PASMCs and subsequent vascular remodeling. Hypoxia disrupts mitochondrial respiratory chain function, leading to ROS generation [32]. ROS can directly oxidize IKKα or activate NF-κB signaling via TLR4 to initiating a sustained inflammatory responses [33, 34]. Inflammation is modulated by NLRs, which assemble multi-protein complexes and activate signaling pathways that culminate in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-18 [35]. Studies propose that NLRX1 indirectly modulates cellular processes by controlling ROS levels and suppressing TLR4 mediated NF-κB activation through interaction with TRAF6 in activated immune cells [36]. Nonetheless, the specific regulatory mechanism of NLRX1 on NF-κB under hypoxic conditions remains unclear.

We evaluated the oxidative stress in the lungs of WT and NLRX1−/− mice by analyzing NF-κB activation and inflammatory cytokine levels. The analysis showed that post-HPH, NLRX1−/− mice exhibited significantly increased TAC and SOD activities in lung tissues compared to WT mice, along with heightened Nrf2 activation, indicating elevated production of oxidative stress substances, including ROS. Furthermore, NF-κB signaling activation, IL-1β and IL-6 cytokine production were notably higher in NLRX1−/− mice lung tissues than in that of WT mice. These findings suggest a pivotal role of excessive oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in pathogenesis of severe HPH with NLRX1 deletion. Although Nrf2 and NF-κB signaling were concurrently activated in NLRX1−/− mice lung tissues during HPH, with no apparent correlation observed between them.Previously, we have reported a significant disparity in macrophage quantities within the lung tissues of HPH mice between the WT and NLRX1−/− groups. However, it remains ambiguous whether NLRX1 facilitates macrophage hyperactivation through a similar mechanism. To address this question,, we performed co-staining of NLRX1 and F4/80 in mouse lung tissues and found that hypoxia downregulates the expression of NLRX1 in pulmonary macrophages (Figure S1). Subsequently, we used primary BMDMs from NLRX1−/− mice and our in vitro investigations demonstrated that NLRX1 was down-regulated in hypoxia-induced BMDMs. Collectively, our findings indicate that hypoxia triggers oxidant stress and activates NF-κB/Nrf2 in lung tissue and macrophages and the reaction is notably potentiated by NLRX1 deficiency. This augmentation aligns with an escalated production of inflammatory cytokines both in vivo and vitro. The findings suggest that the activation or upregulation of NLRX1 could ameliorate excessive immune activation in macrophages by modulating oxidative stress. To further investigate the protective capacity of NLRX1 in hypoxic conditions, we pre-treated BMDMs with NX-13, an NLRX1 agonist. The pretreatment with NX-13 not only suppressed hypoxia-induced oxidative stress and NF-κB activation in BMDMs but also diminished the generation of inflammatory cytokines and Nrf2 activation.

Notably, NLRX1 also modulates other key PH pathways. Its deficiency enhances NF-κB activation and elevates proinflammatory cytokines in HPH mice, which is consistent with its roles in inhibiting NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Preclinical evidence has demonstrated that NLRX1-KO mice exhibit enhanced cardiac inflammation and NLRP3 activation under stress [37], which is consistent with the observation that NLRX1 deficiency exacerbates pulmonary inflammation in HPH. NLRX1 also controls ROS production and FUNDC1-mediated mitophagy [38]. HPH is characterized by excessive mitochondrial fusion, impaired mitophagy, and oxidative stress. Our study indicates that NLRX1 deficiency increases ROS, TAC, SOD activity, and Nrf2 activation, while its loss exacerbates mitochondrial defects in cardiomyocytes, suggesting that NLRX1 can prevent HPH through mitochondrial regulation. Indirectly, pressure overload-induced cardiac hypertrophy shows downregulated NLRX1, and its overexpression rescues mitochondrial function, which parallels the findings in HPH [39]. Moreover, the Human Protein Atlas shows NLRX1 is present in human lung macrophages, aligning with our observations in mice, highlighting relevance to human disease [40].In summary, our study demonstrates that hypoxia reduces NLRX1 expression, leading to increased macrophage infiltration, heightened oxidative stress and hyperactivation of NF-κB. Previous research indicates that NLRX1 can stimulate ROS production in acute infection, thereby bolstering the innate immune response [41–43]. However, prolonged exposure to hypoxia results in sustained oxidative stress and elevated pro-inflammatory mediators, ultimately leading to the progressions of chronic inflammation and pulmonary hypertension (Fig. 10). Hence, NLRX1 is likely to serve as a negative regulator of ROS generation during chronic inflammation. As such, to identify the role of NLRX1 in HPH, further research is necessary to uncover its underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

HPH remains a life-threatening disease, characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling due to hypoxia-induced inflammation. Our research demonstrates that hypoxia triggers a notable increase in macrophages in lung tissue, coupled with a marked reduction in NLRX1 levels. Moreover, the absence of NLRX1 exacerbates pulmonary vascular remodeling, pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricular hypertrophy in mice. In vitro, the lack of NLRX1 leads to enhanced oxidative stress and overactivation of NF-κB signaling in macrophages under hypoxia conditions, leading to elevated release of inflammatory cytokines. However, pretreatment of NX-13 effectively mitigates oxidative stress, inhibits NF-κB activation, and reduces the subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines. These findings suggest that NLRX1 plays a negative regulatory role in hypoxia-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory response in macrophages, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for HPH.
