Avidity-based biosensors for ubiquitylated PCNA reveal choreography of DNA damage bypass
Authors: George Yakoub, Yun-Seok Choi, Ronald P. Wong, Tina Strauch, Kezia J. Ann, Robert E. Cohen, Helle D. Ulrich
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In eukaryotes, the posttranslational modifier ubiquitin is used to regulate the amounts, interactions, or activities of proteins in diverse pathways and signaling networks. Its effects are mediated by monoubiquitin or polyubiquitin chains of varying geometries. We describe the design, validation, and application of a series of avidity-based probes against the ubiquitylated forms of the DNA replication clamp, proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), in budding yeast. Directed against total ubiquitylated PCNA or specifically K63-polyubiquitylated PCNA, the probes are tunable in their activities and can be used either as biosensors or as inhibitors of the PCNA-dependent DNA damage bypass pathway. Used in live cells, the probes revealed the timing of PCNA ubiquitylation during damage bypass and a particular susceptibility of the ribosomal DNA locus to the activation of the pathway. Our approach is applicable to a wide range of ubiquitin-conjugated proteins, thus representing a generalizable strategy for the design of biosensors for specific (poly)ubiquitylated forms of individual substrates.

INTRODUCTION

Similar to many other posttranslational protein modifications, ubiquitylation regulates a variety of biological pathways by modulating the interaction properties of its substrates (1–3). Modification with the small protein ubiquitin generally involves the engagement of ubiquitin’s C terminus in an isopeptide bond to the ɛ-amino group of an internal lysine (K) or, less commonly, a peptide bond to the α-amino group of the substrate’s N terminus. Ubiquitin signaling is particularly complex because of the multitude of polyubiquitin chain structures that are generated when one of ubiquitin’s seven lysine residues or its N terminus is used as the acceptor in ubiquitylation (2, 3). Effector proteins are thought to decode polyubiquitin structures in a linkage-selective manner via ubiquitin-binding domains (UBDs), thereby determining the fate or function of the modified protein (4, 5).

Investigating the mechanisms of ubiquitin-mediated signaling ideally would use real-time, substrate-specific monitoring of polyubiquitin topology in cells. Although selective antibodies or affinity probes are available for most polyubiquitin chain linkages (6–8) and some are even suitable for use in live cells, reagents for detecting specific ubiquitylated proteins generally have been lacking. Rare examples are monoclonal antibodies specific for histone H2B ubiquitylated at K120 (9) or the replication clamp, proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), ubiquitylated at K164 (Cell Signaling Technology). Both were raised against branched peptides representing the modification sites and therefore may recognize the epitopes optimally in the unfolded ubiquitin-protein conjugate. Thus, this strategy is unlikely to yield biosensors for the in vivo detection of native ubiquitylated proteins, especially where the modification site resides within a folded domain. Moreover, such probes are unable to differentiate between mono- and polyubiquitylated forms of the substrate, and because of their disulfide bond–stabilized structures, conventional antibodies typically cannot be used as probes for intracellular live-cell imaging.

To overcome these problems, we have exploited a principle by which natural readers of ubiquitylated proteins recognize their targets. Unlike small modifications such as phosphate groups that usually are recognized by single binding sites that encompass features localized to the modifier and its surroundings (10), ubiquitylation of a protein is often recognized by effectors exhibiting a region with low basal affinity for the substrate and a separate dedicated UBD (5). The combination of two cooperative low-affinity interactions can result in high affinity and selectivity by avidity effects. This principle has guided the successful design of linkage-selective polyubiquitin binders (11–14) and probes directed specifically to ubiquitylated nucleosomes (15) and unanchored mono- or polyubiquitin (16). In these examples, two or more independent protein-binding domains were fused in a suitable spatial arrangement to promote avid and, in some cases, linkage-selective interaction with (poly)ubiquitin moieties.

Here, we describe the rational design, validation, and application of a series of avidity-based probes against the ubiquitylated forms of a physiological ubiquitylation substrate, the eukaryotic replication clamp PCNA. Under conditions that stall the progression of replicative DNA polymerases, PCNA is mono- or polyubiquitylated at K164 by two pairs of ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2s) and ubiquitin protein ligases (E3s); in budding yeast, these are Rad6/Rad18 and Ubc13·Mms2/Rad5, respectively (17), and K63-linked polyubiquitin chains of up to four ubiquitin moieties are readily detectable (18). Whereas monoubiquitylation of PCNA activates a set of damage-tolerant DNA polymerases for mutagenic translesion synthesis (TLS), polyubiquitylation is required for an error-free recombination-like process of template switching (TS) to complete replication in the presence of DNA lesions (17, 19–22). Insight into the spatiotemporal regulation of PCNA mono- versus polyubiquitylation is therefore essential for our understanding of pathway choice and the overall fidelity of DNA damage bypass. Moreover, we previously showed in budding yeast that damage bypass is mostly uncoupled from the replication fork (23, 24). Instead, it predominantly acts in so-called postreplicative repair territories (PORTs) arising from daughter-strand gaps that emerge via repriming of the replisome downstream of lesions. The gradual expansion of these gaps via exonuclease and helicase activities causes an accumulation of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) coated by the replication protein A (RPA) complex; these are visible as microscopic foci that are finally resolved by gap filling mediated via ubiquitin-dependent TLS or TS (24, 25). Where and when PCNA is ubiquitylated in relation to the movement of the replisome and the emergence and expansion of daughter-strand gaps and their subsequent filling are unknown.

Our design of probes for ubiquitylated PCNA (PUPs) has now enabled us to dissect the sequence of events during DNA damage bypass in live cells, pinpointing the time and location of the PCNA ubiquitylation step within the pathway and identifying the ribosomal DNA (rDNA) as a genomic region particularly susceptible to the accumulation of ubiquitylated PCNA. We show that the probes, directed alternatively against total ubiquitylated PCNA or specifically against K63-polyubiquitylated PCNA, are tunable in their activities, thus acting either as biosensors or as inhibitors of damage bypass. We expect that our approach will be applicable to a wide range of ubiquitylation substrates, thus representing a generalizable design principle.

RESULTS

Design and in vitro validation of an avidity probe directed against ubiquitylated PCNA (mPUP)

Our strategy for sensor design was to promote avid binding to monoubiquitylated PCNA by engineering fusions of a PCNA-binding domain to a UBD. The PCNA-interacting peptide (PIP) from budding yeast Srs2 (amino acids 1107 to 1167), for which a PIP·PCNA structure and affinity measurements [dissociation constant (Kd) = 0.17 to 18 μM, depending on the conditions] have been reported (26, 27), was chosen for the PCNA-binding component. Prior knowledge from extensive mutagenesis studies allowed us to modulate the affinity of PIPSrs2 for PCNA to tune sensor specificity. Naturally occurring UBDs typically exhibit low affinities for ubiquitin, i.e., Kd of 0.1 to 1 mM. Therefore, to balance better the ubiquitin- and PCNA-binding contributions to the overall sensor affinity, we used two UBDs in tandem to tighten binding to the monoubiquitin moiety. For this purpose, the ubiquitin-interacting motif of S5a (UIM-2; amino acids 282 to 295, Kd = 73 μM) (28), which recognizes the hydrophobic patch on ubiquitin around I44, and the Rabex-5 ubiquitin binding zinc finger (RUZ) domain (amino acids 15 to 42, Kd = 23 μM) (29, 30), which binds ubiquitin at a distinct surface around residue D58, were fused by a flexible linker (Fig. 1, A and B); we refer to this bipartite ubiquitin binder as “mUBD.” The mUBD bound to monoubiquitin with Kd = 7.1 μM (Table 1 and Fig. 1C), which is tighter than either of the component UBDs and is on par with the PIPSrs2·PCNA interaction. We then fused PIPSrs2 via a glycine-serine (–GS–) linker to mUBD (Fig. 1, A and B, and fig. S1A) and measured binding of the resulting mPUP to ubiquitin and monoubiquitylated PCNA. For these assays, cysteine (C) 80 of the RUZ domain was labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (Alexa488); the fluorescence intensity of Alexa488RUZ increases in response to occupancy by ubiquitin (31). Binding assays showed that mPUP has a high affinity (Kd = 53 nM) for monoubiquitylated PCNA and thus exhibits 130-fold enhanced binding compared to free ubiquitin (Table 1 and Fig. 1D). Competition assays showed a 250-fold selectivity against unmodified PCNA (Fig. 1E), indicating that the combination of the two weak interactions resulted in substantial avidity in accordance with the design strategy. Considering the arrangement of mUBD, mPUP would be expected to interact with mono- and polyubiquitylated PCNA. Accordingly, we found that mUBD has little or no preference for polyubiquitin over monoubiquitin and, among four different types of polyubiquitin, shows negligible linkage selectivity (fig. S1, B and C).

Design and in vitro validation of avidity probes directed against polyubiquitylated PCNA (pPUP)

Sensors for K63-polyubiquitylated PCNA were constructed by linking PIPSrs2 to Vx3(A7), a high-affinity K63 chain–binding protein assembled from three UIMs joined by 7-alanine (A7) linkers (12, 14). This arrangement is expected to adopt the structure of an uninterrupted α helix that interacts with the hydrophobic patches of three ubiquitin moieties in a ruler-like fashion, thus generating a high selectivity for the K63 linkage (Fig. 1, A and B). The PIPSrs2-Vx3(A7) fusion was further modified to increase selectivity for K63-polyubiquitylated over monoubiquitylated PCNA. The first UIM (directly connected to PIPSrs2) could, in principle, bind to the ubiquitin moiety in monoubiquitylated PCNA, thereby decreasing the selectivity for polyubiquitylated versus monoubiquitylated PCNA. We therefore mutated this first UIM at multiple residues to disfavor such an interaction (fig. S1A). We reasoned that the rigidity of the helical structure of the resulting polyubiquitin-specific binding domain (pUBD) would prevent the second and third UIMs from binding to the ubiquitin unit attached directly to PCNA. Thus, pUBD in the context of the resulting construct, pPUP1 (fig. S1A), should bind tightly to only the second and third ubiquitin moiety extending from the polyubiquitin chain attached to PCNA (Fig. 1, A and B). In vitro binding assays showed that pPUP1 has high affinity for K63-polyubiquitylated PCNA (Kd = 6.9 nM) and that its binding to monoubiquitylated PCNA was 800-fold weaker (Table 1 and Fig. 1, F and G). Notably, comparing the affinity of pPUP1 for monoubiquitylated PCNA with that of mPUP for unmodified PCNA validates our design strategy because it shows that the interaction of the mutated first UIM with the proximal ubiquitin affords only a roughly twofold enhancement of affinity. Compared to the 800-fold enhancement provided by the second and third UIM, this is a minor effect.

Two variants of this probe were designed to reduce the affinities for both PCNA and K63-polyubiquitin chains to minimize potential perturbations of physiological functions dependent on PCNA and its K63-polyubiquitylated forms (Table 1; Fig. 1, F and G; and fig. S1A). In pPUP2, the affinity for PCNA was decreased by means of an F1153A substitution in PIPSrs2. This change lowered the affinity for Ub3-PCNA threefold relative to pPUP1. In a third version, pPUP3, we further aimed to decrease the binding to K63-polyubiquitin. The A7 linkers between the UIMs increase affinity for K63-polyubiquitin by promoting the α-helical secondary structure (12, 14). We therefore mutated pUBD by replacing the A7 linker between the second and third UIMs with a sequence derived from human RAP80 (REVNAQE, amino acids 97 to 103). In combination with the F1153A mutation, this change reduced the affinity for Ub3-PCNA 10-fold relative to pPUP1 (Table 1; Fig. 1, F and G; and fig. S1A).

We further characterized the binding properties of mPUP and pPUP1 in vitro by competitive pull-down assays using the probes as baits for a mixture of unmodified, monoubiquitylated, and K63-polyubiquitylated PCNA. To avoid complications arising from PCNA trimers harboring both modified and unmodified subunits, we partially polyubiquitylated a linear ubiquitin-PCNA fusion (Ub-PCNA) and combined this with native, unmodified PCNA (32, 33). Unexpectedly, mPUP was found to discriminate against Ub-PCNA in this assay, but preferentially bound to higher modified forms of PCNA and, to some extent, to unmodified PCNA (fig. S1D). We therefore repeated the assay with a mixture of native PCNA and split Ub-PCNA, a more accurate mimic of K164-monoubiquitylated PCNA (34). Here, we found a strong preference of mPUP for the monoubiquitylated form, although residual binding to unmodified PCNA was again observed (fig. S1E). In contrast, pPUP1 predominantly enriched high–molecular weight conjugates of PCNA carrying K63-linked chains, demonstrating its strong preference for polyubiquitylated PCNA (fig. S1F). In a direct competition with ubiquitylated PCNA, unanchored ubiquitin chains were strongly disfavored by both probes (fig. S1, G and H). In summary, these assays confirm the binding preferences of the probes, although their degree of selectivity was somewhat reduced in the pull-down format involving immobilization on beads, particularly for mPUP. On the basis of the in vitro validation, we next explored the suitability of the probes for use as inhibitors or biosensors.

Inhibitory effects of the probes on the DNA damage response in cells

High-level expression of the probes should perturb the function of ubiquitylated PCNA in vivo, for example, by shielding it from downstream effectors such as TLS polymerases. Thus, a selective interference could serve as evidence for correct targeting of the probes in the cell. We therefore expressed the probes with an epitope tag derived from vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) and a nuclear localization signal (NLS) in budding yeast under control of the strong constitutive ADH1 promoter (Fig. 2, A and B). Isolated PCNA-binding domains and UBDs (PIPSrs2, mUBD, and pUBD) served as specificity controls. Western blots showed an additional band above mUBDVSV and mPUPVSV that matched the sizes of monoubiquitylated forms of these constructs (Fig. 2B). The phenomenon of coupled monoubiquitylation via recruitment of charged E2s is well documented (35–37), and the UIM of S5a is known to be ubiquitylated by several E3s (38). Treatment of a lysate from mPUPVSV-expressing cells with a promiscuous ubiquitin-specific isopeptidase, Usp2cc (39), abolished the larger species, indicating that mPUPVSV (and, most likely, mUBDVSV) is monoubiquitylated in vivo (fig. S2A). Mutation of the four lysine residues within mUBDVSV did not prevent the modification; thus, ubiquitylation occurred elsewhere in the protein (fig. S2B). The ubiquitylated form of mPUP failed to interact with recombinant Ub-PCNA in pull-down assays (fig. S2C). Thus, the modification reduces the effective concentration of active probe in cells.

All probes conferred notable sensitivity toward a DNA-damaging agent, methyl methanesulfonate (MMS; Fig. 2A). The degree of sensitization observed for the pPUPs corresponded well to their respective affinities for polyubiquitylated PCNA. In contrast, the isolated PIP, mUBD, and pUBD domains had no influence on the damage sensitivity of the host strain despite similar expression levels, indicating that the effects of the probes resulted from their combined affinities for ubiquitin and PCNA.

Targeting of the intended pathways by the probes was examined by expressing mPUPVSV and pPUP1VSV in selective mutant backgrounds: In ubc13Δ and mms2Δ mutants, deficient in PCNA polyubiquitylation, pPUP1VSV largely lost its sensitizing effect, whereas a TLS-deficient strain carrying a combined deletion of the genes encoding the TLS polymerases, RAD30, REV1, and REV3 (i.e., tlsΔ) was still sensitized, thus suggesting a TS-specific action (Fig. 2C). In contrast, mPUPVSV sensitized ubc13Δ, mms2Δ, and tlsΔ mutants. Unexpectedly, mutation of the major ubiquitylation site on PCNA (pol30K164R) did not abolish the negative action of the two probes (Fig. 2C), suggesting that they interfere not only with DNA damage bypass but also possibly with DNA repair pathways unrelated to PCNA ubiquitylation. Deletion of MMS2 or UBC13 in the pol30K164R background abolished the effect of pPUP1VSV, indicating that this probe requires K63-linked polyubiquitin chains, although not necessarily on K164 of PCNA (Fig. 2C and fig. S2D). Thus, it is possible that the probes bind to ubiquitin conjugates on other targets nearby. As budding yeast PCNA was reported to be ubiquitylated at an additional lysine, K107, under conditions where unligated Okazaki fragments accumulate (40), the probes could potentially also recognize such alternatively modified forms of PCNA.

Another in vivo effect of the probes indicating their correct targeting would be a stabilization of the ubiquitylated forms of PCNA by shielding them from the action of isopeptidases. We therefore coexpressed mPUPVSV or pPUP1VSV with a hexahistidine (His6)–tagged allele of PCNA (19), exposed the cells to MMS, and isolated HisPCNA under completely denaturing conditions to visualize its modifications. Anti-ubiquitin blots clearly showed a stabilization of mostly the monoubiquitylated form of PCNA by mPUPVSV, whereas pPUP1VSV selectively stabilized polyubiquitylated but not monoubiquitylated PCNA (Fig. 2D). Sumoylation of PCNA, a damage-independent modification, was not affected (fig. S2E). Moreover, no additional conjugates were stabilized in a K164R mutant of PCNA, arguing against a relevance of PCNA ubiquitylated at alternative lysines (Fig. 2D).

To directly assess possible inhibitory effects of the probes on the kinetics of DNA damage bypass, we followed the appearance and resolution of ssDNA marked by the RPA complex in synchronized cultures after MMS treatment in the G1 phase (24). High-level expression of mPUPVSV strongly interfered with the resolution of RPA foci in a strain carrying a green fluorescent protein (GFP)–tagged RFA1 allele, caused enhanced and prolonged PCNA ubiquitylation, and substantially delayed progression through S phase (fig. S2, F to H). Compared to mPUPVSV, the effects of pPUP1VSV were milder, but the resolution of RPA foci was also delayed (fig. S2F).

From these assays, we conclude that the probes recognize their intended targets in vivo and sensitize cells toward DNA damage at high expression levels. We found that the action of pPUP1 depends on Ubc13·Mms2-mediated assembly of K63-linked polyubiquitin chains. However, our genetic assays also suggest an interference of the probes with pathways unrelated to PCNA ubiquitylation. For example, they might inhibit DNA repair or replication stress tolerance by binding to a monoubiquitin or K63-polyubiquitin conjugate on one of the many PCNA-interacting proteins.

Validation of the probes as biosensors for fluorescence microscopy

Use of the probes as biosensors for ubiquitylated PCNA would require conditions where interference with the endogenous DNA damage response is tolerable. We therefore generated a series of GFP-tagged constructs allowing regulated expression by a doxycycline-inducible tetracycline (TET) promoter (41) and enhanced nuclear localization by including three NLS. DNA damage sensitivity assays (fig. S3A) showed that both the concentration of doxycycline and the number of NLS contributed to the toxicity of the constructs and that conditions of low interference could be identified at moderate doxycycline concentrations for constructs carrying a single NLS. Fluorescence microscopy of the same series of constructs after MMS treatment revealed varying numbers of distinct foci upon a diffuse background (fig. S3B). The number of detectable foci correlated with the concentration of the inducer, the toxicity of the constructs, and the affinity of the respective pUBD, suggesting that the probes’ sensitivities as biosensors and their in vivo effects are tunable via their affinity, expression levels, and nuclear targeting.

On the basis of these results, we used the constructs mPUPGFP and pPUP2GFP carrying a single NLS and with doxycycline (2 μg·ml−1) in the growth medium for further characterization and validation unless otherwise noted (fig. S3C). To rule out interference with DNA damage bypass under these conditions, we monitored the formation and resolution of RPA foci (via Rfa1mRuby) in synchronized cultures over the course of a cell cycle after applying MMS damage in G1. As shown in fig. S4A, Rfa1mRuby foci emerged with similar kinetics in the presence or absence of the probes. Whereas pPUP2GFP had very minor effects on the total number of foci and their resolution, mPUPGFP caused enhanced accumulation of RPA foci and delayed their resolution; however, in comparison with high-level expression (fig. S2E), the effects were mild. Likewise, the constructs caused only a minor accumulation of ubiquitylated PCNA or checkpoint activation, and there was no detectable delay in cell cycle progression (fig. S4B).

To verify that the fluorescently tagged probes recognized their intended targets in vivo, we first confirmed that their foci formation was DNA damage–dependent and localized to the nucleus (Fig. 3A and fig. S4C). Additional evidence came from colocalization of mRuby-tagged versions of the probes with GFPPCNA; we found that 3xNLS constructs were needed for efficient detection of foci with this fluorophore (fig. S4, D to F). As expected, 3xNLSmPUPmRuby and 3xNLSpPUP1mRuby largely colocalized with GFPPCNA upon MMS treatment (Fig. 3B). Foci were also enhanced under other replication-stalling conditions known to induce PCNA ubiquitylation such as ultraviolet (UV) irradiation or treatment with 4-nitroquinoline 1-oxide (NQO) or hydroxyurea (HU) but not in response to camptothecin (CPT) or Zeocin (Zeo) (Fig. 3C). The latter two agents cause DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) and activate checkpoint signaling but do not induce PCNA ubiquitylation (fig. S4, G and H) (42). Next, it was confirmed that the individual PIP, mUBD, and pUBD domains did not form foci (Fig. 3D and fig. S4, I and J). Last, we examined the appearance of foci in strains deficient in PCNA ubiquitylation (Fig. 3E and fig. S4, K and L). As expected, deletion of UBC13 or MMS2 abolished the formation of foci by pPUP2GFP but not by mPUPGFP. Moreover, neither of the probes formed foci in a pol30K164R mutant, indicating that, despite their toxicity at high expression levels in this background, their localization at moderate expression levels tracks with the intended targets.

We also examined the consequences of coexpressing 3xNLSmPUPmRuby and 3xNLSpPUP2GFP (Fig. 3F and fig. S4F). While around 70% of cells expressing either of the two probes exhibited at least one focus, coexpression of the probes reduced the percentage of foci-containing cells to 40 to 50%, indicating mutual interference. Together, these observations validate the fluorescently labeled probes as biosensors selective for total (i.e., mono- and poly-) ubiquitylated or specifically polyubiquitylated PCNA, provided that their expression levels are appropriately tuned to avoid excessive interference with DNA damage processing by the cell.

Enrichment of ubiquitylated PCNA in the nucleolus

Analysis of the localization of fluorescently tagged mPUP and pPUP2 relative to various nuclear features should give insight into the sites of action of ubiquitylated PCNA. Whereas DSBs and collapsed replication forks tend to localize to nuclear pores and, if persistent, the nuclear periphery (43–45), we found that the probes were not enriched at those sites (Fig. 4, A and B). Their distribution instead bore resemblance to MMS-induced RPA foci representing daughter-strand gaps; these are slightly depleted at the nuclear periphery and randomly distributed in relation to nuclear pores (24).

When we assessed the probes’ localization relative to a nucleolar marker, Nop1 (46), we found that ubiquitylated PCNA was strongly overrepresented in the nucleolus. We used two distinct methods, a quantification of fluorescent nucleolar versus nuclear markers and a quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)–based assay, to estimate the expected volume fraction that the rDNA should occupy (Fig. 4C and fig. S5A). Nucleolar localization of ubiquitylated PCNA by a similar amount would thus be expected if it was randomly distributed. Notably, MMS-induced mPUPYFP foci were two- to threefold enriched in the nucleoli. Nucleolar accumulation of spontaneous foci in the absence of external DNA-damaging agents was even stronger, exceeding 40% for mPUPYFP and 30% for pPUP2YFP. Consistent with the association of the probes with regions of ssDNA, spontaneous and MMS-induced Rfa1YFP foci were similarly overrepresented in the nucleolus (Fig. 4D). In contrast, colocalization of the homologous recombination (HR) factor Rad52YFP with Nop1mCherry matched the values expected for a random distribution (Fig. 4E).

The enrichment of ubiquitylated PCNA in the nucleolus, particularly under basal conditions, is consistent with the notion that the repetitive and highly transcribed rDNA is in itself a source of spontaneous replication stress (47). We now find this to be associated with stretches of ssDNA that are subject to processing by the ubiquitin-dependent damage bypass pathway. The lack of overrepresentation of Rad52 in the nucleolus suggests that processing via TS or HR takes place at least in part outside the nucleolus. This observation is reminiscent of previous reports that HR-dependent repair of DSBs in the rDNA is preceded by a transient relocalization of the affected sequences out of the nucleolus (48).

Timing of PCNA ubiquitylation and daughter-strand gap expansion

When cells damaged in G1 are released into the cell cycle, PCNA ubiquitylation appears early in S phase and peaks in G2-M, consistent with a predominantly postreplicative mode of DNA damage bypass (24). However, this distribution provides little information about the timing of the modification relative to replication fork movement, gap expansion, and initiation of TS or HR. To dissect these events at high resolution, we performed time course experiments monitoring the colocalization of the PUP avidity probes with relevant markers of DNA replication and replication stress. We had previously shown that damage-induced RPA foci emerge in early S phase as multiple small speckles that develop over time into a few larger, brighter foci via resection-mediated gap expansion (24). We now found that both mPUPGFP and pPUP2GFP exhibited a profile resembling but only partially overlapping with that of RPA (Fig. 5, A and B, and fig. S5, B to D). Notably, ubiquitylated PCNA tended to form foci slightly earlier than RPA in most experiments. The initial overlap between RPA and the probes was minimal and rose gradually during S phase, never exceeding 50%. These properties suggest that PCNA ubiquitylation precedes the formation of expanded stretches of ssDNA but persists at least for some time during gap expansion. Foci induced by UV irradiation and spontaneous foci in undamaged cells, presumably reflecting endogenous replication problems, exhibited a similar pattern (fig. S5, E and F).

As it was important to rule out unwanted effects of the probes themselves in eliciting the early appearance of PCNA-containing foci, we examined the colocalization of PCNA with RPA in strains not expressing any probe (fig. S6A). In undamaged cells, GFPPCNA formed foci with a timing similar to RPA; however, upon MMS treatment, GFPPCNA foci, like mPUPGFP and pPUP2GFP, emerged before the bulk of RPA foci. These results suggest that the earlier appearance of ubiquitylated PCNA compared to RPA foci is not induced by the probes but is inherent in the replication stress response.

Colocalization of mPUPGFP with mRubyPCNA was comparable to the overlap of the sensor with RPA (Fig. 5C and fig. S6, B and C), indicating that (i) not all PCNA associated with PORTs is detectably ubiquitylated and (ii) there are regions with a similarly high degree of PCNA ubiquitylation that do not appear as PCNA foci, possibly because the overall abundance of PCNA at these sites is low compared to other sites. The early emergence of ubiquitylated PCNA could indicate an association not with PORTs but with stressed replication forks. We therefore examined colocalization of mPUPGFP with an mRuby-tagged subunit of the replicative helicase, Mcm4. In budding yeast, the minichromosome maintenance (MCM) complex is loaded onto chromatin during G1 phase and leaves the chromatin during S phase (49, 50); thus, colocalization with MCM subunits indicates association with either replication forks or unreplicated chromatin. During early S phase, we observed between 40 and 75% overlap of mPUPGFP with Mcm4mRuby, depending on the experiment (Fig. 5C and fig. S6, B and D). This degree of colocalization was considerably higher than that of RPA with Mcm4, which is less than 5% at any time (24). We therefore conclude that PCNA ubiquitylation is initiated at a stage where PCNA is still at or near stressed replication forks and before the emergence of large stretches of ssDNA.

Last, to assess the relationship between PCNA ubiquitylation and the initiation of TS or HR, we monitored colocalization of mPUPGFP with an mRuby-tagged Rad52, which forms foci largely coinciding with RPA but appearing later in time (24). Overlap between mPUPGFP and Rad52mRuby reflected the overall rise in the number of Rad52mRuby foci (Fig. 5C and fig. S6, B and E), suggesting that ubiquitylation of PCNA precedes TS/HR but persists well into the later stages of damage processing (Fig. 5D).

DISCUSSION

Our development of biosensors specific for ubiquitylated PCNA represents a demonstration of the successful application of the avidity principle for the detection of individual ubiquitylated proteins in cells. Compared to the currently available reagents directed against polyubiquitin chains (7), the substrate selectivity of our probes is a major advance. Moreover, by the choice of UBDs and appropriate design of their spatial arrangement, we were able to not only incorporate linkage-specific detection of the polyubiquitin chain but also distinguish between total ubiquitylated and polyubiquitylated forms of the substrate. Such distinction, achieved by selectively disfavoring binding to the monoubiquitylated substrate, is impossible to achieve by alternative design principles based on the direct recognition of the ubiquitin attachment site (9, 51).

The 130- to 800-fold selectivity of our probes (Table 1) compares favorably to other cases of ubiquitin recognition via avidity effects. The RAP80 protein as a natural example of an avid polyubiquitin binder uses two UIM domains to contact the hydrophobic patches on neighboring ubiquitin moieties within a K63-polyubiquitin chain, thereby achieving ca. 24-fold enhanced affinity over monoubiquitin (12). A tandem UBD construct, t-UBD, designed as a combination of a ZnF-UBP domain selective for ubiquitin’s free C terminus and a K48-specific UBA domain, exhibited an 11-fold enhancement of binding to a K48-linked ubiquitin dimer over monoubiquitin (16).

Avidity-based recognition of conjugated ubiquitin is facilitated by the naturally low affinity of most UBDs, which disfavors monoubiquitin binding in isolation (5). As we have shown for the set of pPUP probes, the overall affinity of the combined construct can be tuned over an order of magnitude by mutations that weaken the substrate- and/or UBD-ubiquitin interaction even further. In our case, the latter is achieved by varying the helicity of the linker between the UIM domains, as we demonstrated previously (14). In other cases, structural information about the interaction surface of the UBD can provide clues to inform the design. We therefore anticipate that the approach that we describe here is generalizable to construct biosensors for other polyubiquitylated proteins, provided that a low-affinity peptide or binding domain directed against the substrate itself can be identified. In our recent development of engineered “readers” for specifically monoubiquitylated nucleosomes, naturally occurring binders of the nucleosome acidic patch were used as “anchors” to direct the UBD to the ubiquitin-protein target (15). In the cases of mPUP and pPUP, a PIP domain from Srs2 served as the anchor. More generally and for other targets, nanobodies and other selectable probes such as DARPins or affimers (52, 53) may provide suitable anchors compatible with live-cell applications, provided that low-affinity binders can be obtained.

Application of our biosensors to live-cell imaging has revealed important information about the temporal and spatial regulation of the DNA damage bypass pathway (Fig. 5D). First, we found that PCNA ubiquitylation initiates near DNA replication forks and, therefore, before the expansion of daughter-strand gaps. However, the modification persists in PORTs until gap filling via TLS or TS is observable. Given the predominantly postreplicative action of damage bypass, such early ubiquitylation of PCNA is unusual if the modification is indeed the signal for gap filling. It is therefore conceivable that ubiquitylated PCNA already contributes to the process of gap expansion. The observations are also compatible with a certain amount of fork-associated damage bypass, for example, by a transient association of TLS polymerases with the replisome (54).

A second important outcome of our study is the finding that an unexpectedly high fraction of S phase–associated PCNA ubiquitylation in the absence of DNA-damaging agents is associated with nucleoli and therefore appears to be attributable to spontaneous replication problems in the rDNA repeats. Whether this local overrepresentation is due to the programmed unidirectional replication fork barriers in the rDNA repeats, an accumulation of transcription-associated R-loops in this part of the genome, or other aspects of replication stress caused by the repetitive nature of the rDNA (55, 56) remains to be determined. The concomitant enrichment of RPA foci indicates that the modification is associated with a large concentration of ssDNA. At the same time, the lack of overrepresentation of Rad52 suggests that the resolution of the replication problems by means of TS or other HR-dependent processes occurs at least in part outside the nucleolus. A transient relocation of DSBs in the rDNA out of the nucleolus following the initiation of break resection has been reported previously in the context of HR-mediated DSB repair (48). Our findings now suggest that postreplicative regions of ssDNA arising in the nucleoli from replication problems in the rDNA may follow a similar path.

Last, high-level expression of our constructs in cells has revealed a potential limitation of avidity-based probes. In line with their design principle, it should be feasible to use them as pathway-specific inhibitors, as they would block the physiological interactions of the ubiquitylated substrate at high concentrations. This effect should be stronger than for probes raised against the attachment sites because the probe-associated UBDs are expected to compete with those of the natural effectors for a common interaction site, e.g., the ubiquitin hydrophobic patch. Similarly, the PIP motif might block access of natural PCNA interaction partners. As expected, high-level expression of mPUP and pPUP1 blocks DNA damage bypass. At the same time, however, the probes interfere with DNA damage resistance even under conditions where PCNA ubiquitylation is prevented. Isolated PIP or UBD constructs do not show this effect, and for pPUP1, the activity depends on K63-polyubiquitylation by Ubc13·Mms2. These results suggest that the probes might act via bridging interactions between PCNA and ubiquitin conjugates on unrelated proteins nearby. Several interaction partners of PCNA, including DNA polymerases and RPA, are known to be subject to ubiquitylation (22, 57–59). Thus, off-target effects caused by such bridging interactions need to be considered when using avidity probes as inhibitors. Reducing the distance between substrate-binding domains and UBDs or reducing flexibility of the interdomain linker could possibly enhance specificity and provide a way around this problem.

We envision that the avidity-based probes will be useful for further exploration of ubiquitin-dependent DNA damage bypass in the future. In particular, the pPUP constructs can be used to elucidate the mechanism of the TS pathway not only in budding yeast but also in mammalian cells, where polyubiquitylated PCNA is still an understudied phenomenon. Considering the diversity of ubiquitin-dependent signaling events on chromatin (60), generic polyubiquitin-specific probes will not be sufficient to tease apart the details of DNA damage bypass and its consequences for DNA replication and mutagenesis. Our substrate-specific approach promises to overcome this problem, thus acting as a model case for the real-time analysis of protein ubiquitylation in live cells. The availability of binding domains selective for other polyubiquitin linkages (6–8) opens up a wide range of opportunities for the design of probes directed toward other ubiquitin-mediated signaling pathways.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Construction of plasmids

Relevant oligonucleotides are listed in table S1, and plasmids are in table S2. Avidity probes were designed using UCSF Chimera (61) to assemble fusion proteins from protein domains of known structures (Fig. 1). The genes encoding mPUP and pPUPs were constructed using the Infusion cloning method (Takara Bio) and then inserted into the pET28a vector; all gene sequences were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. The plasmid harboring the PIPSrs sequence was a gift from C. Lima (Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center). For overexpression in yeast, the probe constructs were cloned into episomal vectors under control of the strong, constitutive ADH1 promoter. A C-terminal VSV-tag was introduced by PCR. Fusions with fluorescent proteins [GFP, yellow fluorescent protein (YFP), or mRuby] were created by overlap extension PCR. Their regulable expression was achieved by a doxycycline-inducible TET-ON promoter (62). FLAG tags, GFP tags, and 1x or 3xNLS fragments were added to the N termini of probe constructs either by PCR or using annealed double-stranded DNA oligos via cut-and-paste cloning.

Preparation of avidity probes

For quantitative interaction assays (Fig. 1 and Table 1), the pET28a plasmids harboring mPUP and pPUPs were used to transform BL21-CodonPlus (DE3) Escherichia coli for protein expression. All probes were expressed upon induction with 0.4 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and growth at 25°C for 8 hours. Cells were centrifuged, resuspended with His-binding buffer [25 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4), 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol], and lysed by sonication. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,199g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was applied to a HisTrap FF column (Cytiva) for immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC). After washing with 5 column volumes of the His-binding buffer, bound His-tagged proteins were eluted by a gradient of 10 to 250 mM imidazole in elution buffer [25 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.4), 500 mM NaCl, and 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol]. Eluted proteins were further purified by gel filtration in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) with 1 mM TCEP, using a Superdex 75 size exclusion column (Cytiva). Purified proteins were stored in the same buffer at −80°C.

For in vitro pull-down assays (fig. S1, D to H), His6-FLAG– and His6-GFP–tagged probes were produced in BL21-CodonPlus (DE3)-RIL E. coli cells (Stratagene) and prepared as above with minor modifications using pET28b-based vectors (table S1). Before induction, cells were shifted to 4°C for 30 to 60 min. After addition of 0.2 mM IPTG (and 50 μM ZnCl2 for mPUP expression), cultures were incubated at 18°C for 16 hours. IMAC buffers were tris-HCl–based, and bound His-tagged probes were eluted with 500 mM imidazole. Dithiothreitol (DTT; 1 mM) served as a reducing agent. Relevant fractions were pooled, buffer-exchanged into storage buffer [50 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol] using a PD-10 column (GE HealthCare), and concentrated using Vivaspin concentrators (Thermo Fisher Scientific). This was followed by a gel filtration step in storage buffer using a Superdex 75 Increase column (Cytiva). Relevant fractions were pooled, and purified proteins were flash-frozen and stored at −80°C.

Preparation of mono- and polyubiquitylated PCNA and free polyubiquitin chains

For quantitative binding assays, Ub-PCNA was prepared by using protein expressed from the split Ub-PCNA plasmid (a gift from T. Washington, University of Iowa) as described (34). To generate Ub3-PCNA, we adopted a method by Carlile et al. (63) for cross-linking of Ub(G76C) to PCNA(K164C). We first prepared K63-linked Ub3 with Ub(K63R) as the distal ubiquitin and Ub(G76C) in the proximal position [i.e., Ub(K63R)-Ub-Ub(G76C)]. To generate this, we prepared K63-linked Ub2(D77) by enzymatic conjugation of Ub(K63R) and Ub(D77) using ubiquitin-activating enzyme (UBA1), Ubc13, and Mms2 (64). The K63-linked Ub2(D77) product was digested by YUH1 to remove D77 from the proximal ubiquitin and purified by cation exchange chromatography (Mono S column; Cytiva) in pH 4.5 ammonium acetate buffer. After confirmation by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization–time-of-flight mass spectrometry, the purified K63-linked Ub2 was further conjugated to His-tagged Ub(G76C) using UBA1, Ubc13, and Mms2. The resulting K63-linked Ub3(G76C) was purified by size exclusion chromatography (Superdex 75; Cytiva) in 50 mM Hepes, 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP. The K63-linked Ub3(G76C) then was cross-linked to PCNA(K164C) using 1,3-dichloroacetone (63). and the resulting Ub3-PCNA was captured with a HisTrap FF column. Unreacted Ub3 and cross-linked Ub3 dimers were removed by size exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200; Cytiva), and the column fractions containing Ub3-PCNA were pooled. SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and staining with Coomassie blue followed by quantitation using Image Studio showed that 50% of the PCNA polypeptides were conjugated with Ub3; therefore, each PCNA trimer carried at least 1 Ub3 chain and contained, on average, 1.5 Ub3 chains.

For in vitro pull-down assays (fig. S1, D and F to H), PCNA and a linear fusion of ubiquitin to the N terminus of PCNA (Ub-PCNA) were produced and purified as described previously (33). The latter was used to set up polyubiquitylation reactions [0.05 μM UBA1, 2 μM Ubc13 and Mms2 each, 1 μM PIP-E3 (63), 5 μM ubiquitin, and 2 μM Ub-PCNA for 45 min at 30°C] as described (33), which were used without further purification. A His6-tagged version of Ub-PCNA, used for pull-down of mPUP from cells (fig. S2C), was purified as described previously (32). For pull-down assays comparing the binding of the probes to ubiquitylated PCNA versus unanchored polyubiquitin chains (fig. S1, G and H), polyubiquitylation reactions [0.05 μM UBA1, 2 μM Ubc13 and Mms2 each, 1 μM Pib1, 5 μM ubiquitin, and 2 μM mycUb(K63R) for 2 hours at 30°C] were set up as described (65). Reactions were terminated by addition of apyrase.

Labeling of sensor proteins

mPUP (50 μM), pPUPs (50 to 60 μM), or Ub(S20C) (50 μM) in 50 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP were incubated with Alexa488-maleimide (75 μM; Thermo Scientific) or Atto532-maleimide (75 to 90 μM; ATTO-TEC GmbH) at room temperature for 2 hours to label C80 of the RUZ domain in mPUP with Alexa488, C39 of pUBD in pPUPs with Atto532, or Ub(S20C) with Atto532. Subsequently, 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol was added to consume unreacted maleimide dyes, and the proteins were purified by IMAC to remove the unbound dye. The degree of labeling (DOL) and concentrations of the labeled sensors were calculated using the equations belowDOL=Am×εprot(A280−Am×CF280)×εmCF280=ε280εmProtein concentration(M)=[A280−(Am×CF280)]εprot

Here, Am represents the absorbance at the dye absorption maximum, A280 is absorbance at 280 nm of the labeled protein, ɛprot is the unlabeled protein extinction coefficient at 280 nm, ɛ280 is the extinction coefficient at 280 nm of the dye alone, ɛm is the extinction coefficient at the absorption maximum of the dye, and CF280 is the correction factor at 280 nm. The DOL obtained for the Alexa488-labeled mPUP and Atto532-labeled pPUPs typically were 55 ± 10%.

Quantitative binding assays

All binding assays to determine affinities were performed using a FluoroMax 4 spectrofluorometer (HORIBA Scientific) equipped with L-format polarizers or with a SpectraMax M2 (Molecular Devices). The binding assay buffer was PBS (pH 7.4) with 0.05% Brij35, ovalbumin (0.3 mg·ml−1), and 2 mM DTT. Measurements were made using 60-μl samples kept at 25°C. All Ub-PCNA concentrations and Kd and inhibition constant (Ki) values refer to subunit concentrations. The affinities of Alexa488-labeled mPUP for Ub-PCNA and of Alexa488-labeled mUBD for ubiquitin were determined by monitoring the fluorescence intensity change of 3 to 10 nM Alexa488-labeled sensor titrated directly with increasing amounts of Ub-PCNA or ubiquitin, respectively. The affinity of Alexa488-labeled mPUP for unmodified PCNA was determined using a similar assay format but with Ub-PCNA fixed at 80 nM and titrating with PCNA as a competitive ligand. The affinities of the pPUPs for Ub3-PCNA were obtained from direct titrations of the Atto532-labeled pPUPs (5 nM) with Ub3-PCNA (6 to 1000 nM, corrected for 50% ubiquitylation of the PCNA subunits) by monitoring the decrease in fluorescence anisotropy of the Atto532-labeled sensor protein upon complex formation. The binding affinities between pPUPs and Ub-PCNA were obtained from competition assays by titrating Alexa488-labeled mPUP (30 nM) in the presence of Ub-PCNA (80 nM) with pPUPs (0.2 to 200 μM). Kd and Ki values and statistical errors were calculated from fits to binding data using GraphPad Prism.

In vitro pull-down assays

Pull-down assays to qualitatively compare binding of the probes to unmodified and ubiquitylated PCNA were carried out with purified His6-FLAG– or His6-GFP–tagged probes in PD buffer [for mPUP, 1× PBS supplemented with 1 mM DTT; for pPUP, 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.5 mM EDTA]. Probes (100 μl of a 0.2 μM solution in PD buffer) were coupled to M2-FLAG or GFP-Trap magnetic beads [15-μl slurry, preblocked for 30 min with bovine serum albumin (1 mg·ml−1)] by a 90-min incubation at 4°C. For comparison of binding to unmodified, monoubiquitylated, and polyubiquitylated PCNA, 10 μl of a polyubiquitylation reaction containing 2 μM PCNA and 5 μM ubiquitin were diluted 10-fold with the respective PD buffer, and native, unmodified PCNA was added to 0.05 μM. This mixture was used for pull-down assays with immobilized His-FLAGmPUP or His-FLAGpPUP. For comparison of binding to unmodified versus monoubiquitylated PCNA, 100 μl of an equimolar mixture of unmodified HisPCNA and split Ub-PCNA (0.2 μM each) were used for pull-down assays with His-GFPmPUP immobilized on GFP-Trap magnetic beads (use of His-FLAGmPUP was precluded by the presence of a FLAG-tag on split Ub-PCNA). Here, beads bound to free GFP (produced in-house) served as negative control. Binding was allowed during a 1-hour incubation at 4°C on a rotating wheel. After washing five times with PD buffer (supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 for mPUP or 0.05% IGEPAL for pPUP), bound protein was eluted by adding 20 μl of 1× NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and heating for 5 min at 95°C for analysis by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting. Samples of input and unbound material (4%) were analyzed in parallel.

Pull-down assays to compare binding of the probes to ubiquitylated PCNA versus unanchored polyubiquitin chains were performed analogously with His6-FLAG–tagged probes. The input was generated by adding 10 μl each of a PCNA polyubiquitylation reaction and a free chain assembly reaction (see above) to 80 μl of the respective PD buffer, resulting in final total concentrations of 0.2 μM PCNA, 1 μM ubiquitin, and 0.2 μM mycUb(K63R). Binding, washing, and elution were performed as described above for His-FLAGmPUP and His-FLAGpPUP.

Western blots were imaged with an Odyssey CLx system (LI-COR) using fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies and quantified using Image Studio version 3.1. The percentage of bound material was calculated separately for each species after subtraction of the background signal from input and bound material. In addition, signals of unbound material [flowthrough (FT)] relative to signals from samples passed through control beads without probe were plotted to judge the extent of depletion.

Yeast strain construction and culture conditions

Unless otherwise mentioned, all Saccharomyces cerevisiae strains used in this study are haploid (MATa) and are derived from DF5 (table S3). All strains were cultured at 30°C in yeast extract, peptone, and dextrose or in synthetic complete (SC) media supplemented with appropriate amino acids. Probe constructs were introduced into relevant strains via integration into the LEU2, URA3, or TRP1 locus. Strains with gene deletions or tags were created following standard PCR-based methods (66, 67) or by combining relevant modifications through mating and tetrad dissection. To induce replication stress and detect fluorescent probes, asynchronous exponential cultures of relevant strains were incubated for 90 min with doxycycline (2 μg·ml−1) and treated with genotoxic agents as indicated [MMS (0.02%), 4-NQO (0.2 μg·ml−1), HU (100 mM), CPT (20 μg·ml−1), Zeocin (75 μM), or UV (100 J·m−2)–254-nm exposure on plates]. Cells were imaged either directly or, for UV-treated samples, after 40 min. For cell cycle analyses and time course experiments, cells were incubated with doxycycline (2 μg·ml−1) for 1 hour and then synchronized in G1 by adding alpha-factor (5 μg·ml−1) for 90 min. Subsequently, cells were treated as indicated with MMS (0.02%) for 30 min or with UV (20 J·m−2) before release into S phase in the absence of the damaging agent.

Analysis of DNA damage sensitivity by spot assay

For comparative spot assays, ca. 1.6 × 106 cells from exponential cultures were spotted in fivefold serial dilutions onto freshly prepared SC or SC-selective agar plates containing the indicated concentrations of damaging agents. For UV treatment, cells were exposed to the indicated doses of UV radiation after spotting. For inducible expression of the probes, specified doses of doxycycline were added to the agar plates. Plates were imaged after 3 days of incubation at 30°C.

Analysis of proteins and PCNA modifications in yeast extracts

Denatured whole-cell extracts were prepared by trichloroacetic acid precipitation as described previously (68). His-tagged PCNA and its modified forms were isolated by IMAC under denaturing conditions as described (42) and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting using antibodies listed in table S4. For interaction assays with monoubiquitylated mPUP (fig. S2C), mPUPGFP expression was induced overnight with doxycycline (2 μg·ml−1), and cell lysates were prepared by mechanical disruption (Precellys) in a buffer containing 50 mM tris (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT, supplemented with SIGMAFAST protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by the addition of 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min on ice. After centrifugation (16,000g for 20 min), the protein concentration of the supernatant was adjusted to 5 mg·ml−1. Ni–nitrilotriacetic acid beads were equilibrated in IMAC A buffer [50 mM tris (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, and 1 mM DTT]. Aliquots of 25 μl were loaded with 100 μl of 2 μM purified recombinant HisUb-PCNA for 2 hours at 4°C where indicated; beads were washed three times with IMAC A buffer and incubated with 250 μl of cell lysate per aliquot for 90 min at 4°C. After washing three times with IMAC A buffer, bound material was eluted in 2× NuPAGE LDS buffer at 95°C for 10 min and analyzed, together with samples of input and flow-through, by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

Deubiquitylation assay

Deubiquitylation was performed by adding purified recombinant Usp2cc (39) and 1 mM DTT to whole-cell lysates in the absence of protease inhibitors. Ub-GFP from cleared E. coli lysates was used as a positive control for enzymatic activity of Usp2cc. Samples were incubated at 15°C for 150 min, heated for 10 min at 95°C in NuPAGE LDS Sample Buffer supplemented with 100 mM DTT, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting.

Cell cycle analysis

Samples (1 ml) of yeast cultures were collected at the indicated times and inactivated with 1% of sodium azide. Cells were pelleted and fixed in 70% ethanol for 15 min at room temperature. After washing twice with 50 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 7.0), cells were resuspended in 1 ml of the same buffer containing ribonuclease A (80 μg·ml−1) and incubated at 50°C for 1 hour. Proteinase K was added to a concentration of 80 μg·ml−1, and incubation was continued for 1 hour at 50°C. Cells were then stained with propidium iodide (32 μg·ml−1) and sonicated on a Branson sonifier for 3 s at 10% power. Last, DNA content was analyzed by flow cytometry on a FACSVerse cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were visualized with FlowJo v10 software (FlowJo LLC).

Fluorescence microscopy and live-cell imaging

Live-cell imaging was performed as described (24). Briefly, aliquots of cell cultures (0.7 to 1.0 optical density at 600 nm) were immediately plated on concanavalin A–coated chambered coverslips with glass bottoms (Ibidi), washed twice with medium, and released into fresh prewarmed SC medium with doxycycline (2 μg·ml−1) in an environmentally controlled chamber at 30°C. Images were taken at the indicated time points. Corresponding fluorescence-activated cell sorting samples at each time point were collected from a liquid culture incubated in the same environmentally controlled chamber. Imaging was performed using a wide-field DeltaVision Elite system (Cytiva) equipped with a 60× oil immersion objective (numerical aperture 1.42), InsightSSITM solid-state illumination, and a Scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera. Z stacks with 20 to 30 planes (step size, 0.2 μm) were acquired for each image. Fluorescent proteins cyan fluorescent protein (CFP), GFP, YFP, and mCherry were imaged with their optimized filters; mRuby2 was imaged with a tetramethyl rhodamine isothiocyanate filter, and differential interference contrast was used for whole-cell images. The built-in deconvolution algorithm from SoftWoRx was used to reconstruct images.

Image processing and analysis

For foci counting in deconvolved live-cell images, two different image analyses were used: two-dimensional (2D) manual foci counting and 3D automated colocalization and foci counting. For 2D analysis, 20 Z planes were projected with maximum intensity using ImageJ Fiji software. The Hta2mCherry signal was used to create nuclear masks, and nuclei with at least one focus were manually counted using the Cell Counter plugin of ImageJ. Automated 3D foci counting was carried out using unbiased and customized scripts written in ImageJ macro and Jython languages with ImageJ Fiji software (24). Nuclear masks were created from Hta2mCherry or the background signal of Rfa1, Rad52, or PIP-UBD sensors using filtering and autothresholding. The number of foci per cell was determined from each nuclear mask using an absolute threshold for foci segmentation in 3D. For colocalization analysis, nuclear masks were generated from the background signal of fluorescent protein–tagged nuclear proteins (Rfa1, Rad52, or PUP sensors). An absolute threshold was applied to deconvolved live-cell images to segment objects from each channel. Colocalization was quantified from the volume percentage of each object covered by the other object in 3D. The size of objects was calculated from the volumes of object masks.

The zoning assay (Fig. 4A) was performed as described (24) by creating 3D nuclear masks from the nuclear Hta2mCherry signal using a Laplacian or Gaussian filter and autothresholding. The circularity of the resulting 3D objects was tested, and objects that did not pass a threshold value were excluded. Ellipsoids were then fitted onto the objects and eroded into three 3D zones of equal volume. Last, an absolute threshold was used to segment the foci, and their localization in the nuclear zones was quantified as previously described (24).

Estimation of relative nucleolar volumes

Nucleolar volumes relative to nuclear volumes were estimated by two independent methods. In one approach, copy numbers of the rDNA were determined by qPCR for each analyzed strain by comparing relative signals corresponding to the 18S ribosomal RNA gene and to ACT1 (69). Copy numbers were multiplied by the length of the rDNA repeat (9.1 kilo–base pair) and divided by the total size of the yeast genome (11.8 mega–base pairs). Alternatively, the Nop1mCherry-marked volume corresponding to the nucleolus was divided by the Hta2CFP-marked volume of the nucleus on a cell-by-cell basis and averaged across all analyzed cells of the respective strain.

Statistical analysis

All damage sensitivity assays and Western blot experiments were performed at least twice, with identical or near-identical results. Microscopy-based localization assays were also performed in at least two biological replicates, except for the doxycycline titration (fig. S3B), which was performed only once. All statistical tests and relevant numbers of data points or replicates are indicated in the respective figure legends. To test whether foci are enriched or depleted in the nucleolus, we calculated the logit transformation of the nucleolar volume fraction (over nuclear volume) and the logit transformation of the fraction of foci overlapping with the nucleolar signal per biological replicate. We confirmed the normal distribution assumption visually and with Shapiro-Wilk tests. Significance values for enrichment or depletion of foci in the nucleolus (Fig. 4, C to E) were then calculated with two-sided paired t tests, comparing the nucleolar volume logits to the overlapping foci logits.
