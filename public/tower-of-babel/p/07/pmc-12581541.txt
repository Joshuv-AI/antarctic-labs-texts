Stage-specific transcriptomic alterations in the spinal cord reveal key regulatory genes and potential therapeutic targets in a rat model of neuropathic pain
Authors: Liqiong He, Kailu Zou, Zhifeng Huang, Caiyun Xi, Qulian Guo, Changsheng Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropathic pain is a prevalent chronic pain condition with complex pathogenesis, posing significant challenges due to its diverse etiologies and limited treatment options. We aim to analyze different stage transcriptomic changes to identify potential molecular targets for guiding future research and treatment of neuropathic pain.MethodsSpinal cords were collected from rats subjected to chronic constriction injury surgery (CCI) at 0.5, 1, 3, 7, and 14 days post-injury. RNA sequencing was performed (n = 3 per group), followed by differential gene expression analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, and protein-protein interaction analysis. The differentially expressed genes identified were validated using quantitative real-time PCR. TAK-242 were intrathecally administered to pharmacological inhibit Ly86, mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia were evaluated in chronic constriction injury rats.ResultsSignificant changes in differentially expressed genes enriched in immune, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways were observed at various time points, particularly during the stabilization phase (7 and 14 days post-chronic constriction injury), with 529 and 352 differentially expressed genes identified at these respective time points. Notably, genes related to the complement pathway, neurotrophic factors, and lysosomal functions were consistently upregulated, suggesting their potential roles in neuropathic pain pathogenesis. Protein-protein interaction network analysis identified key regulatory genes, including Clec12a, Ly86, Ctsz, and Fcgr3a, which could be potential therapeutic targets for neuropathic pain. Inhibition of Ly86 alleviated pain in CCI mice, confirming its role as a critical modulator of neuropathic pain. These findings may contribute to the development of targeted therapies for human neuropathic pain.ConclusionsOur findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying neuropathic pain and may contribute to the development of more effective treatment strategies.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07221-8.

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a prevalent and challenging chronic pain condition resulting from lesions or diseases affecting the somatosensory nervous system. This condition encompasses various types of pain, including peripheral nerve injury pain, post-herpetic neuralgia, trigeminal neuralgia, and central post-stroke pain, among others [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the prevalence of NP in the general population ranges from 6.9% to 10%, representing approximately 20% of all chronic pain cases [2, 3]. Clinically, NP is often characterized by persistent or intermittent spontaneous pain, typically described as burning, pricking, or squeezing, and may be accompanied by evoked pain phenomena such as hyperalgesia and allodynia [4, 5]. The high incidence of NP is partly attributable to the limited efficacy of current treatments. While opioids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are fundamental in managing nociceptive pain, their effectiveness in treating NP is significantly lower, benefiting only a minority of patients [6]. Consequently, further investigation into the pathogenesis of NP and the identification of novel therapeutic targets are critical for advancing treatment options for this debilitating condition.

The pathogenesis of NP is intricate and multifaceted, involving various cell-cell interactions and alterations in molecular signaling pathways. Key mechanisms include: (1) peripheral sensitization driven by peripheral mechanisms; (2) central sensitization and disinhibition mediated by spinal cord and supraspinal central mechanisms; (3) activation of neuroglial cells and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines; and (4) changes in the expression of pain receptors and ion channels [7–11]. Under NP conditions, nociceptive signals generated by peripheral sensory afferents are synaptically transmitted to secondary sensory neurons in the spinal dorsal horn. These signals trigger a cascade of inflammatory and neurological processes, leading to altered synaptic plasticity and central sensitization within the spinal cord. Subsequently, these sensitized signals are relayed to the brain via ascending pathways mediated by secondary sensory neurons in the spinal cord [12]. Therefore, the spinal cord serves as a crucial relay site for the transmission of peripheral pain signals to the brain [13].

In recent years, the rapid advancement of high-throughput transcriptome sequencing technology has significantly enhanced our understanding of the critical regulatory genes and molecules involved in disease. Deciphering the transcriptional changes induced by nerve injury is crucial for gaining fundamental insights into the complex pathogenesis of NP. Wang et al. conducted single-cell RNA sequencing of the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) in mice at various time points (6 h, 1d, 2d, 7d, and 14d) following spared nerve injury (SNI). This study explored the dynamics of primary sensory neurons during NP development and identified several potential algogenic cytokines, including Atf3, Egr1, and Clcf1 [8]. Our group previously conducted a transcriptomic analysis at different time points (12 h, 1d, 3d, 7d, and 14d) in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of peripheral nerve injury model rats, revealing that chemokines and their target genes in the ACC may play distinct roles in the initiation and maintenance of NP-associated emotional states [14]. Despite these advancements, the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying spinal cord alterations during NP pathogenesis remain largely unexplored.

Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying NP will enable the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. In this study, we characterized the temporal transcriptome changes in the spinal dorsal horn of chronic constriction injury (CCI) model rats by RNA sequencing at established time points (12 h, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d) after NP [8, 14]. Through gene ontology (GO) analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, we identified critical genes and pathways involved in the development and maintenance of NP. While transcriptomic studies in the DRG post-NP primarily focus on neuron-related reactive changes, our findings elucidate the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying NP pathogenesis in the spinal cord and highlight key pain regulators and potential therapeutic targets, such as Clec12a, Ly86, Ctsz, and Fcgr3a may offer promising avenues for NP treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. The rats were maintained in a controlled environment with regulated temperature and humidity, provided with ad libitum access to food and water, and subjected to a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All experimental procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Rat model of CCI

The adult male Sprague-Dawley rats (8–10 weeks old, 220–250 g) were randomly assigned to 6 experimental groups: Sham-control and CCI-induced NP at 12 h, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d post-surgery. This study implemented sample blinding and employed independent biological replicates per time point to account for individual variability. In line with our previous research, the chronic constriction injury (CCI) model was established following the method described by Bennett and Xie [9, 15]. Briefly, after anesthetizing the rats with sodium pentobarbital, the sciatic nerve of the left hind limb was exposed. Four square knots were then tied around the main trunk of the sciatic nerve using 4–0 chromic gut, spaced 1 mm apart. The knots were tightened just enough to induce slight tremors in the surrounding muscles while ensuring that the nerve’s blood supply remained intact. Afterward, the nerve and muscle were repositioned, and the skin incision was sutured. In the sham-operated group, the sciatic nerve was exposed but not ligated.

Behavioral test

Paw Withdrawal Mechanical Threshold (PWMT): The ipsilateral paw withdrawal mechanical threshold was assessed in rats using Von Frey filaments (North Coast Medical). Rats were placed on a metal stand with a glass grid separator (22 cm × 12 cm × 22 cm). Once the rats were acclimated, the plantar surface of the left hind paw was stimulated vertically with Von Frey filaments, starting at 0.4 g and increasing to a maximum of 15 g. The PWMT was determined using the up-and-down method [9].

Paw Withdrawal Thermal Latency (PWTL): The ipsilateral paw withdrawal thermal latency was evaluated using a thermal pain test apparatus (Ugo Basile). Rats were placed on a 2 mm thick glass plate for 30 minutes to acclimate. The thermal pain test’s radiation source was focused on the center of the left plantar surface. Each measurement was repeated three times with a 5-minute interval between trials, and the mean latency was recorded as PWTL. In accordance with previous studies, the cut-off time for the thermal test was set at 30 seconds to prevent tissue damage [9]. Both mechanical and thermal pain assessments were performed in a double-blind manner.

Total RNA extraction and quality control

Total RNA was extracted from the spinal cord tissues of rats euthanized at various time points under deep anesthesia. The tissues were homogenized, and total RNA was isolated using TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies). The RNA precipitates were purified with isopropanol and ethanol, and the resulting total RNA was stored at −80 °C. The quality and quantity of the extracted RNA were assessed using UV absorption (NanoDrop®ND-1000) and denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. Only samples that met the quality criteria were subjected to subsequent RNA sequencing. Quality control (QC) metrics were systematically evaluated, with analysis of the raw sequencing data confirming high-quality results as evidenced by a Q30 rate exceeding 80% for each individual sample.

RNA sequence and data analysis

RNA from spinal cord samples of CCI and sham group rats was purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced by Shanghai Aksomics Biotechnology Company. Raw sequencing reads were preprocessed to remove rRNA, sequencing adapters, short fragments, and other low-quality reads. Clean reads were then mapped to the rn6 reference genome using HISAT2 (version 2.0.4). Gene expression was normalized by converting clean reads into fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on criteria including a fold change of at least 1.5, a p-value ≤0.05, and a within-group FPKM mean ≥0.5. Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses [16, 17]. The spinal cord sequencing results were submitted to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository and assigned GEO accession numbers: GSE175760. All other spinal cord transcriptomic datasets related to NP in this study were obtained from the GEO database (accession numbers: GSE18803, GSE111216, GSE180627). Detailed information is provided in Table S2.

PPI network analysis

We utilized the STRING database to predict potential protein interactions for the differentially expressed genes identified by RNA-seq. For PPI analysis, the minimum interaction confidence score was set at 0.4. The PPI network was visualized using Cytoscape software. In the Cytoscape visualization, each node represents a protein or gene, while the connecting edges between nodes indicate molecular interactions. A greater number of edges connected to a node signifies a higher number of interactions involving that protein [18].

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Spinal cord tissues from sham and CCI group rats were collected for RT-qPCR analysis. Total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Transgen) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA. RT-qPCR amplification was conducted using SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix (Transgen) on an ABI QuantStudio 7 instrument. Following the PCR reaction, the Ct values of the target genes were analyzed and compared between groups using the 2-ΔΔCt method, consistent with our previous methodology. The primer sequences used in this study are listed as follows. Actin forward primer: CATCCTGCGTCTGGAACCTGG, reverse primer: TAATGTCACGCACGATTTCC, Clec12a forward primer: GGCTTCAGATTGGTACAAGGACAG, reverse primer: TCCGAGCAGAGCAGAACTCAAC, Ly86 forward primer: TCCGAGCAGAGCAGAACTCAAC, reverse primer: GCATTAGCACAAGCCACAGTAGC, Ctsz forward primer: GCATTAGCACAAGCCACAGTAGC, reverse primer: ACATCCTCTCGGTTGCCATTATCC, Fcgr3a forward primer: ACATCCTCTCGGTTGCCATTATCC, reverse primer: GCGTCCTGGTGCGAGATGAG, Clec4a forward primer: GCGTCCTGGTGCGAGATGAG, reverse primer: AGCAGCAGGAGAAGTACCATAAGG, Csf2rb forward primer: AGCAGCAGGAGAAGTACCATAAGG, reverse primer: AGGAGGAAGGTGAGGATGAGGAAG, Fcer1g forward primer: AGGAGGAAGGTGAGGATGAGGAAG, reverse primer: CGACAGTAGAGCAGGGTAAGGAC.

ScRNA-seq data analysis

This study employed the Seurat v4.0 analysis pipeline on the R 4.4.0 platform to systematically analyze single-cell transcriptomic data. The experimental workflow was conducted as follows: First, quality control was performed by filtering cells expressing genes detected in at least 3 cells, retaining cells with more than 200 detected genes, and excluding low-quality cells with mitochondrial gene expression exceeding 20%. To mitigate batch effects, the Harmony algorithm was applied to integrate cell data across different experimental conditions. For dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis, the top 20 principal components were selected for cell clustering, with the resolution parameter set to 0.5. Cell subpopulation distributions were visualized using UMAP, incorporating all significant principal components in the analysis. Finally, the FindConservedMarkers function was utilized to identify conserved cell-type-specific marker genes across conditions. The single-cell dataset relevant to this study was obtained from the GEO database (GSE189070).

Intrathecal delivery of Ly86 inhibitor

The intrathecal catheter implantation procedure and TAK-242 dosage (a selective Ly86 inhibitor) were implemented according to established protocols [19, 20]. Briefly, under general anesthesia, the L4–L5 lumbar intervertebral space was surgically exposed in rats. A PE-10 catheter was aseptically inserted into the subarachnoid space, with correct placement confirmed by spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid outflow. On postoperative day 3, catheter patency was validated through a lidocaine challenge test (10 μL, 2%); only animals exhibiting immediate ( < 1 min) bilateral hindlimb motor blockade were included in subsequent experiments. For pharmacological intervention, CCI model rats received intrathecal administration of TAK-242 (100 μg in 1% DMSO-saline, MCE), while vehicle controls received an equivalent volume of 1% DMSO-saline solution.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 7 software. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess data distribution. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used, while one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Validation of key DEGs in external datasets and functional role of Ly86 in NP

To further investigate the expression dynamics and cellular distribution of potential NP-associated DEGs (Clec12a, Ly86, Ctsz, and Fcgr3a) in the spinal cord, we analyzed additional NP-related sequencing datasets from the GEO database. First, we examined scRNA-seq data from the spinal cord of mice with spinal cord injury (SCI)-induced NP (GSE189070). After rigorous quality control and filtering, we identified and annotated 10 distinct spinal cell clusters. Notably, Clec12a, Ly86, and Ctsz exhibited significant alterations post-SCI. Specifically, Ctsz and Ly86 were upregulated primarily in spinal macrophages, microglia, and oligodendrocytes, while Clec12a expression increased predominantly in astrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) (Fig. 7A–D). Next, we analyzed bulk mRNA transcriptome datasets from rodent NP models (GSE18803, GSE111216, GSE180627). In spared nerve injury (SNI) rats, spinal Clec12a, Ly86, and Ctsz were significantly elevated, aligning with our sequencing results (Fig. 7E). However, in CCI and SNI mice, only Ly86 showed consistent upregulation following NP induction (Fig. 7F–G). To functionally validate this, we assessed the therapeutic potential of Ly86 inhibition in CCI rats. We found that intrathecal administration of the Ly86 inhibitor TAK-242 significantly increased mechanical (PWMT) and thermal (PWTL) withdrawal thresholds compared to vehicle-treated CCI rats, ameliorating NP behaviors (Fig. 7H–I). These results indicate that spinal Ly86 is a promising therapeutic target, and its modulation may offer novel clinical strategies for NP management.

Conclusion

Our study delineates dynamic transcriptomic alterations in the spinal cord during NP progression, revealing stage-specific DEGs and pathway enrichments. Key genes (Clec12a, Ly86, Ctsz, and Fcgr3a) were consistently upregulated post-injury, with functional validation implicating Ly86 as a critical modulator of NP. These findings uncover novel molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets for NP management.

Discussion

NP is a form of chronic pain that often persists and causes significant physical and psychological suffering for patients. However, current clinical treatments for NP are often inadequate due to its complex pathogenesis [1, 7, 33]. Effective treatments are essential to alleviate symptoms and improve the quality of life for affected patients. In this study, we investigated transcriptomic changes in the spinal cords of rats with NP induced by CCI surgery to the sciatic nerve at various time points (0.5 d, 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d) using high-throughput RNA sequencing. We found that a substantial number of DEGs were altered at 3 d, 7 d, and 14 d post-CCI. Enrichment analysis (GO and KEGG) of these DEGs revealed that, in addition to the continuous activation of immune, metabolic, and signal transduction pathways, specific pathways were activated at different stages, reflecting distinct transcriptomic changes in the rat spinal cord during the progression of NP. By overlapping DEGs across different stages, we also identified genes consistently upregulated during NP development. Subsequent PPI analysis predicted novel key genes, including Clec12a, Ly86, Ctsz, and Fcgr3a, which may play crucial roles in NP regulation. Furthermore, analysis of additional NP-related sequencing datasets from the GEO database identified Ly86 as a key molecule, whose pathological role in NP was experimentally validated. In conclusion, our findings offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying NP and may serve as a theoretical foundation for developing potential therapeutic targets.

Given the pivotal role of the spinal cord in pain signaling and processing, this study aims to analyze the spinal cord transcriptome at various time points following peripheral nerve injury in rats. Specifically, we characterize changes in cellular, molecular, and signaling pathway expressions during both the early (CCI-D0.5, CCI-D1, and CCI-D3) and stable (CCI-D7 and CCI-D14) stages of the NP pathogenetic process. Our experimental results revealed that during the early stage of peripheral nerve injury, the number of rat spinal cord DEGs was relatively low. These DEGs were predominantly enriched in metabolic and immune regulatory pathways, with significant activation observed in cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways. Additionally, there was a marked increase in protein binding activity within the cytoplasm and nucleus. These findings are consistent with those of Fiore and Miller, suggesting that the organism responds rapidly and efficiently to noxious stimuli from sciatic nerve compression [34, 35].

Further sequencing results revealed a significant increase in the number of DEGs in the spinal cord during the stabilization phase of the NP disease course in rats, although most were consistent with those identified in the CCI-D3 group. Notably, genes related to the complement pathway, neurotrophic factors, immune response, and lysosomal functions were significantly upregulated in the CCI-D7 and CCI-D14 groups compared to the early phase. Xu’s study demonstrated that activation of complement C3 facilitates NP induced by the chemotherapeutic drug paclitaxel via its receptor C3aR1, and that targeting C3aR1 significantly alleviates pain symptoms [36]. The critical regulatory role of neurotrophic factors, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), in NP pathogenesis have been extensively documented in the literature [37, 38]. Additionally, the role of neuroinflammation induced by microglia and astrocyte activation, along with programmed cell death mechanisms such as apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, has been a longstanding focus of NP research [39–42]. In line with these findings, our sequencing results demonstrated that during the stabilization phase of NP, there was marked activation of glial cell proliferation, inflammatory pathways, intercellular adhesion, and programmed cell death-related pathways, with a significant increase in intercellular signaling compared to the initial phase of NP. These results suggest that at this stage, various cells in the spinal cord are intricately interconnected, collectively promoting the development of spinal cord synaptic plasticity and central sensitization.

While investigating the changes in signaling pathways at different stages of NP, our study also focused on DEGs that co-vary across these stages. These genes were consistently upregulated from the initial to the maintenance stages of NP, suggesting their potential critical regulatory roles in NP pathogenesis. In NP, the neuroprotective versus pathological roles of these DEGs are context-dependent, dynamically regulated by microenvironmental signaling networks. We identified a total of 216 genes that were consistently upregulated at different stages following CCI, with the top 20 most significantly altered DEGs showing substantial interactions among each other. Several of these genes, including Ccl2, Atf3, C1q, and Rac2, have been previously demonstrated to play crucial roles in the regulation of NP [28, 29, 32, 43]. Notably, genes such as Clec12a, Ly86, Ctsz, and Fcgr3a have not been previously reported in NP-related studies. Clec12a is a C-type lectin receptor involved in immune recognition and regulation of inflammatory responses. It recognizes specific molecular markers of damaged cells and modulates immune cell activity, which suggests that Clec12a may influence NP development and maintenance by regulating immune responses and inflammatory pathways [44, 45]. Ctsz is a lysosomal protease involved in protein degradation and antigen presentation. It is expressed in various immune cells and plays a role in intracellular protein degradation and recycling [46]. The potential role of Ctsz in NP may be linked to its function in microglial cells [47]. Ly86 was demonstrated to induce neutrophil extracellular traps via the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, which in turn stimulates macrophages to produce more Ly86 [48]. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed upregulated Ly86 expression in spinal cord macrophages and microglia under NP contexts. Consistent with this, existing studies confirm that macrophages are one source of soluble Ly86 in serum, and soluble MD-1 serves as a valuable biomarker for inflammatory diseases [49]. Also, the role of spinal microglia in regulating neuroinflammation and immune responses in NP has been extensively documented [50]. Collectively, these findings suggest that peripheral nerve injury-induced elevation of Ly86 in spinal macrophages and microglia may exacerbate neuroinflammation and amplify pain perception through immunomodulatory mechanisms [34]. Currently, Clec12a is a prominent target in acute myelocytic leukemia immunotherapy and the functional small-molecule inhibitors effectively suppressing Ly86 activity have been identified [19, 20, 51].

Although we analyzed multiple transcriptomic datasets from rodent NP models in the GEO database, including single cell sequencing data, no sequencing data from human spinal cord samples of pain patients were identified.Future studies should thus characterize pain-induced transcriptomic alterations in human specimens and validate the expression of key genes and proteins in clinical samples. Additionally, integrated multi-omics approaches combining proteomics and epigenomics are warranted to verify transcriptomic findings across multiple levels. Critically, future work must define the cell-type-specific molecular mechanisms by which these molecules regulate immune-driven nociceptive signaling in NP, thereby accelerating therapeutic validation of targeted agents or inhibitors.

Research on NP is critically important due to its significant impact on a large patient population and the limitations and suboptimal efficacy of current treatment options [52]. The complexity of NP arises from its diverse etiologies, intricate pathophysiological mechanisms, and substantial inter-individual variability. In-depth exploration of the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic targets of NP is essential not only for developing more effective treatment strategies and alleviating patient suffering but also for reducing the socioeconomic burden and improving public health outcomes [53, 54]. Our study demonstrates the complex transcriptomic alterations in the spinal cord at different time points following NP, highlighting changes in immune, metabolic, and inflammatory signaling pathways at various stages of the disease. Additionally, we identified core regulatory genes including Ly86 that may govern the onset and persistence of NP, offering potential therapeutic targets. We hope that our findings will enhance researchers’ understanding of the complex pathological changes in the spinal cord during different stages of NP, thereby providing valuable insights and support for the development of more effective treatments.
