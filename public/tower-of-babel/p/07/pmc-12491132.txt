Comprehensive bioinformatics evaluation uncovers the possible oncogenic contribution of acrylamide to endometrial cancer
Authors: Yiting Zou, Shan Lu, Shiqiang Han, Renfeng Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndometrial cancer (EC) is a prevalent gynecological malignancy with increasing incidence worldwide. Despite advancements in treatment, challenges such as tumor recurrence and chemoresistance persist. Acrylamide, a probable carcinogen formed in high-temperature cooked foods, has been associated with EC risk, but its oncogenic mechanisms remain underexplored.MethodsTranscriptomic data from GEO datasets (GSE17025, GSE63678, GSE106191, GSE115810) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (PRJNA786266) were integrated to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the limma package. Acrylamide molecular targets were predicted via SwissTargetPrediction and ChEMBL databases. Machine learning approaches, including LASSO regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest, were employed to identify key genes. SHAP analysis evaluated gene importance, while ssGSEA assessed immune cell infiltration. Molecular docking experiments investigated acrylamide’s binding affinity with key proteins.ResultsDifferential expression analysis identified 2274 downregulated and 2545 upregulated genes in EC. Nine consensus genes were identified across GEO DEGs, scRNA-seq, WGCNA modules, and acrylamide targets, enriched in Notch, Wnt, and microRNA pathways. Machine learning pinpointed four key genes: ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN. SHAP analysis highlighted ROCK1 as the top predictor (AUC = 1.00). ssGSEA revealed significant correlations between these genes and immune cell infiltration, particularly with NK and T cells. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinities of acrylamide with CLK1 (− 6.3 kcal/mol), PSENEN (− 6.1 kcal/mol), ROCK1 (− 6.0 kcal/mol), and SIRT1 (− 5.8 kcal/mol).ConclusionThis study elucidates acrylamide’s potential oncogenic role in EC, identifying ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN as key mediators. These findings underscore the interplay between dietary exposures and EC pathogenesis, suggesting novel therapeutic targets. Further validation is needed to translate these insights into preventive and therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03612-x.

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) is one of the most prevalent gynecological malignancies globally, with a rising incidence particularly in high-income countries [1, 2]. According to recent global cancer statistics, EC ranks among the top cancers affecting women, with over 417,000 new cases diagnosed in 2020, and its incidence has increased by approximately 132% over the past three decades [2, 3]. Despite advancements in early detection and treatment modalities, many patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to poorer prognosis and increased mortality [4, 5]. Current treatment strategies for EC include surgery, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, and immunotherapy [6–8]. However, these approaches face challenges such as tumor recurrence, chemoresistance, and limited efficacy in advanced cases [9, 10]. For instance, while surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, patients with metastatic disease often cannot undergo curative surgery [7]. Furthermore, resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapies significantly hampers long-term survival [11]. Therefore, understanding the molecular and environmental factors contributing to EC pathogenesis and identifying novel therapeutic targets are critical for improving patient outcomes.

Acrylamide is primarily formed during high-temperature cooking processes from carbohydrate-rich foods, commonly occurring in frying, baking, or roasting. Typical dietarysources include French fries, potato chips, bread, biscuits, and coffee [12–14]. Beyond dietary exposure, acrylamide is also present in cigarette smoke and industrial settings, contributing to its widespread human exposure [15, 16]. However, many consumers—particularly those in low-incomecountries and regions—remain largely unaware of the presence ofacrylamide and its potential health risks [17]. Recent studies have explored acrylamide’s potential role in carcinogenesis, with evidence suggesting associations with various cancers, including breast, ovarian, and endometrial cancers [18, 19]. In vitro studiesindicate that acrylamide may promote carcinogenesis by inducing DNAadduct formation and oxidative stress [20, 21]. In endometrial cancer, acrylamide exposure has been linked to increased risk [22]. Additionally, acrylamide may exert endocrine-disrupting effects, potentially influencing hormone-related pathways critical to EC development [23]. Despite these findings, the precise mechanisms by which acrylamide contributes to EC pathogenesis, including its impact on tumor microenvironment and cancer stem cell characteristics, remain underexplored.

Given the rising incidence of EC and acrylamide’s widespread presence in the human diet, investigating its oncogenic role in EC is of paramount importance. Such research could elucidate how environmental and dietary exposures contribute to EC development, offering insights into the interplay between external factors and molecular mechanisms. Moreover, understanding acrylamide’s specific effects on EC could lead to novel preventive strategies, such as dietary interventions to reduce exposure, or targeted therapies to mitigate its carcinogenic effects. This study aims to bridge existing knowledge gaps by exploring acrylamide’s role in EC pathogenesis, potentially informing public health policies and clinical strategies for EC prevention and treatment.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of transcriptomic data and acrylamide molecular targets

In this study, we collected data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets GSE17025, GSE63678, GSE106191 and GSE115810. By merging these datasets, we obtained a new combined dataset, including 53 normal samples and 186 endometrial cancer patients. These data were used to analyze endometrial cancer-related gene expression patterns and differences. We obtained the SMILES number of Acrylamide from the PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database and predicted its molecular targets using the SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) databases. These targets are proteins or genes that Acrylamide may interact with. Finally, we removed duplicates and merged the Acrylamide molecular targets to obtain a more accurate set of targets. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of endometrial cancer were obtained from the GEO database under accession number PRJNA786266. The dataset comprises transcriptomic profiles from 5 normal endometrial tissue samples and 5 tumor tissue samples derived from patients with endometrial carcinoma.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset was processed using Seurat v4 following established computational pipelines. Initial quality control measures retained cells expressing 200-7,500 genes while excluding those with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 15%. Following quality filtering, raw counts were normalized using the LogNormalize method (scale factor = 10,000) and the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected through variance stabilization transformation to mitigate technical variability. To address potential confounding factors, batch effects were corrected using Harmony integration and cell cycle effects were regressed out based on phase-specific scoring. Dimensional reduction was performed using principal component analysis (40 PCs), followed by graph-based clustering at a resolution of 0.06, which identified 9 transcriptionally distinct cell populations. These clusters were annotated based on established marker genes: NK cell (PTPRC, CCL5), epithelial cells (KRT18, KRT19), fibroblasts (LUM, DCN), macrophages (C1QB, C1QA), mast cells (IGLC2, IGKC), endothelial cells (VWF, A2M), pericytes cells (MYL9, RGS5), plasma cell (TPSB2, TPSAB1), Lymphatic endothelial cell (CCL21, PROX1), and ciliated cell (CAPS, RSPH1). Subsequent analysis employed AddModuleScore to evaluate enrichment patterns of Acrylamide-associated gene signatures across cell types, revealing significant differences between tumor and normal samples (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p < 0.05). Single-cell populations were stratified into high- and low-scoring groups according to median expression values of the target signature. Subsequently, differential gene expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function with stringent thresholds (absolute log2 fold-change > 0.1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05). This analytical approach enabled identification of transcriptionally distinct features between scoring-defined subpopulations while controlling for multiple testing effects through Bonferroni correction. Cell-cell communication networks were reconstructed using CellChat, demonstrating distinct interaction patterns among identified populations. Finally, differential expression analysis of network hub genes highlighted patient-specific variations in intercellular signaling pathways. All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.2) with visualization implemented using ggplot2 and patchwork packages.

Differential gene analysis in GEO and target intersection analysis

The current investigation employed a comprehensive bioinformatics pipeline to characterize molecular alterations in endometrial carcinoma. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R, incorporating surrogate variable analysis (SVA) to account for inter-dataset variability. Linear modeling was implemented through the lmFit function, followed by empirical Bayes moderation using eBayes to enhance statistical power. Stringent filtering criteria (p-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold-change| > 0.25) were applied to identify significant transcriptional changes between malignant and normal urothelial tissues. These differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were subsequently visualized through pheatmap and volcano plot analysis. To evaluate pathway-level alterations, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) enrichment scores for the Acrylamide gene set were computed and compared between clinical groups. For co-expression network analysis, sample clustering was performed using hierarchical clustering (hclust) with average linkage, followed by outlier removal. An appropriate soft-thresholding power was selected to ensure scale-free topology, and module identification was accomplished through dynamic tree cutting. Four clinically relevant modules (brown, green, and yellow) demonstrating strong associations with disease phenotype were selected for downstream functional characterization. All statistical computations were performed in R with visualization implemented using ggplot2 and WGCNA packages.

GO and KEGG analysis

To systematically identify core molecular signatures, we performed integrative bioinformatics analyses on four distinct gene sets: WGCNA module genes, GEO-derived DEGs, single-cell-based median-split differential genes, and Acrylamide targets. Intersection analysis using the VennDiagram package revealed consensus genes across these datasets, with graphical representation through four-way Venn diagrams. Subsequent functional annotation was conducted through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses implemented in clusterProfiler. The GO analysis systematically evaluated three ontological domains: biological processes (e.g., cell proliferation, immune response), cellular components (e.g., membrane compartments, organelle structures), and molecular functions (e.g., catalytic activity, binding capacity). Concurrently, KEGG pathway analysis identified significantly enriched signaling cascades (adjusted p < 0.05, q < 0.05) potentially implicated in endometrial carcinogenesis. Results were visualized via ggplot2-generated bubble plots, where the x-axis represented functional terms, the y-axis displayed gene counts, and bubble attributes (size/color intensity) encoded statistical significance and effect magnitude. Finally, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were employed to assess differential expression patterns of consensus genes between endometrial cancer patients and controls, with multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Machine learning for key gene screening

First, we performed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis using the `glmnet` package, determining the optimal regularization parameter λ through cross-validation to screen for key genes. We then used the `e1071` package for Support Vector Machine (SVM) analysis, selecting the model with 7 genes, which achieved an accuracy rate as high as 0.833. Additionally, we used the `randomForest` package for Random Forest analysis, screening for genes with importance greater than 10. Finally, we obtained key genes by taking the intersection of the results from the three methods using the `VENN` tool.

SHAP model construction

To further understand the mechanisms by which key genes act in colorectal cancer, we constructed a SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) model using the kernelshap, ggplot2, ranger, and shapviz R packages in this study. The SHAP model calculates the SHAP values for each gene’s contribution to model predictions, helping to interpret the model’s output results. First, we randomly divided the GEO dataset into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. Based on the training set, we built a Random Forest model using the ranger package and calculated the SHAP values using the kernelshap package. Kernelshap efficiently implements the Kernel SHAP algorithm, assigning SHAP values to each feature in the model to quantify its importance to model predictions. During the calculation, we set the background dataset size to 200 and used exact calculation mode to ensure the accuracy of the results. We obtained ROC curves, beeswarm plots, feature dependence plots, and feature attribution waterfall plots.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using limbal tissue samples and the ClusterProfiler software package to investigate biological functions associated with hub genes under differential expression conditions. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction). The top three significantly enriched gene sets (ranked by normalized enrichment score, NES) were selected for visualization.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

To systematically characterize immune cell infiltration patterns in endometrial cancer, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was implemented using the GSVA package in R. Furthermore, we conducted an analysis of immune cell infiltration using the CIBERSORT method. This algorithm enables quantification of immune cell abundance in individual tumor samples by calculating enrichment scores for predefined immune gene signatures. Transcriptomic data from GEO datasets were processed through the limma pipeline for normalization and quality control. Subsequently, correlation analysis between key genes and immune infiltration scores was performed, with results visualized using ggplot2 to generate comprehensive association heatmaps. All statistical analyses were conducted with FDR correction (q-value < 0.05) to account for multiple hypothesis testing. Furthermore, an analysis of the correlation between the expression of hub genes and common immune checkpoint-related genes was conducted.

Consensus clustering analysis

Consensus clustering was performed using the R package “ConsensusClusterPlus” to identify robust molecular subtypes based on hub gene expression patterns in the GEO cohort. This unsupervised machine learning approach was iterated 100 times to ensure classification stability, with optimal cluster number (k) determination through systematic evaluation of consensus matrices and cumulative distribution function (CDF) analysis. Following subtype identification, principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to visualize inter-subtype transcriptional differences. Subsequent comparative analyses included: (1) evaluation of immune cell infiltration patterns using ssGSEA, and (2) functional annotation of subtype-specific biological pathways through Gene Ontology enrichment. All computational procedures were implemented in R (v4.2.2) with statistical significance threshold set at FDR < 0.05. Finally, the differences in TMB, MSI classification and common chemotherapy drug treatments of endometrial cancer in the TCGA database were compared between the two subtypes.

Molecular docking

To investigate the binding capacity of Acrylamide with key genes, we obtained the corresponding protein molecular structures of these genes from the PDB database in this study. When selecting protein structures, we prioritized X-ray crystallography structures with higher resolution, as they provide more accurate atomic position information. After obtaining the protein structures, we visualized the protein molecular structures using PyMOL software. Subsequently, we performed molecular docking experiments using AutoDock software. During the docking process, we set the binding energy threshold to -5.0 kcal/mol, a commonly used standard in molecular docking experiments, to screen for ligand-receptor complexes with strong binding capabilities. Finally, we obtained the binding energies of the key genes with Acrylamide, identifying key proteins that can bind Acrylamide effectively.

Cell culture

Endometrial cancer cells (HEC1-A, HEC1-B, and Ishikawa) and Normal endometrial cells (hEEC) were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma - Aldrich; Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (FBS; Gibco; Expression Vector; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 100 IU/mL penicillin and 10 µg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2. The aforementioned cells were donated by the Reproductive Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region. All cell lines were obtained from authenticated sources and cultured under standard conditions.

qRT-PCR analysis

These RNAs were extracted by TRIzol® (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc) and reverse transcribed by HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme Biotech). Gene primer sequences are shown in Table S1. PCR cycling parameters were: 95 °C for 5 min, 40 cycles of 30 s, and 60 °C for 1 min. each sample was performed in 3 separate reactions, and the average of each point was calculated. mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH levels.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis in this study was primarily conducted using the R language and various bioinformatics tools. First, we used the `limma` package to perform differential analysis on gene expression data to screen for significantly different genes. Next, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analysis using the `clusterProfiler` package to reveal the functional and pathway associations of genes. Additionally, we combined the `randomForest`, `xgboost`, and `caret` packages for machine learning analysis to screen for key genes and assess their contributions to the model using the SHAP model. Finally, we analyzed immune cell infiltration using the `ssGSEA` package and verified the binding capacity of key genes with Acrylamide through molecular docking experiments using AutoDock software. The expression levels of the four genes were validated using the GEPIA 2 online database (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) and UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) .

Machine learning-based evaluation of diagnostic biomarkers in endometrial cancer

Four candidate genes (ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN) were subjected to comprehensive SHAP analysis for diagnostic model development. The GEO dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and validation (30%) cohorts, with ten distinct machine learning algorithms evaluated for predictive performance. Among these, the random forest (RF) model demonstrated optimal diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 1.00), prompting its selection for subsequent analyses. Feature importance assessment revealed ROCK1 as the most influential predictor, as evidenced by both bar plot ranking (Fig. 5A) and beeswarm plot distribution (Fig. 5B). Partial dependence analysis indicated a positive correlation between ROCK1 expression levels and SHAP values (Fig. 5C), a finding further corroborated by waterfall plot visualization of individual feature contributions (Fig. 5D). Model performance metrics, including ROC curves, recall (sensitivity), precision, and F1 scores, were systematically compared across all algorithms in both training and validation sets (Fig. 5E, F), confirming the superior robustness of the RF approach.

Immune cell infiltration and significant correlations with key genes

In this study, immune cell infiltration in endometrial carcinoma patients and healthy controls from the GEO database was evaluated using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Notably, significant disparities in immune cell composition were observed between the two cohorts (P < 0.05). Specifically, tumor tissues exhibited elevated infiltration levels of activated CD4 + T cells, central memory CD8 + T cells, type 2 T helper (Th2) cells, and natural killer (NK) cells (Fig. 7A). Subsequently, correlation analyses were performed to assess associations between four pivotal genes (ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN) and immune cell subsets. Intriguingly, CLK1 demonstrated robust correlations with central memory CD4 + T cells, immature B cells, NK cells, Th17 cells, Th1 cells, and central memory CD8 + T cells (Fig. 7B, P < 0.001). Similarly, PSENEN was significantly linked to Th17 cells, and Th1 cells (Fig. 7C, P < 0.001). Furthermore, ROCK1, and SIRT1 displayed pronounced associations with activated CD4 + T cells, and γδ T cells (Fig. 7D–G, P < 0.001). These results were verified by the CIBERSORT method (Figure S1). Comprehensive correlation analysis revealed distinct associations between the identified hub genes and key immune checkpoint regulators: ROCK1 expression showed a significant positive correlation with PD-L1 (CD274) but a negative correlation with PD-1 (PDCD1). Similarly, CLK1 exhibited a strong positive association with CTLA-4, while SIRT1 demonstrated an inverse relationship with PD-1. In contrast, PSENEN displayed no significant correlations with major immune checkpoints (CTLA-4, PD-1, or PD-L1; all p > 0.05). These findings suggest that ROCK1, CLK1, and SIRT1 may differentially modulate immune evasion pathways, whereas PSENEN likely operates through alternative mechanisms (Fig. S2).

Two subtypes classified based on hub genes for endometrial cancer

To further elucidate molecular heterogeneity, consensus clustering analysis was performed on the GEO training cohort of endometrial carcinoma patients based on four candidate genes (ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN). Notably, two distinct molecular subtypes (C1 and C2) were identified (Fig. 8A, B). Subsequent analysis revealed differential expression patterns of these genes between subtypes, with CLK1 and SIRT1 exhibiting significantly higher expression in the C2 subgroup (Fig. 8C). Moreover, comparative immune profiling demonstrated marked disparities in immune cell infiltration between the two subtypes, with the majority of immune subsets showing statistically significant variations (Fig. 8D). To investigate functional implications, GSVA was conducted, revealing pronounced enrichment of upregulated genes in oncogenic pathways (Fig. 8E). Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed clear stratification of endometrial carcinoma patients into the two predefined subtypes (Fig. 8F), underscoring the robustness of the classification. Comprehensive characterization of the C1 and C2 molecular subtypes revealed significant differences in genomic profiles and drug sensitivity. The C1 subtype demonstrated a markedly higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) compared to C2 (p < 0.001). Microsatellite instability (MSI) analysis showed a predominant MSI-high phenotype in C1 tumors (35% vs. 26% in C2). Notably, drug sensitivity profiling identified enhanced cisplatin response in C1, with significantly lower IC50 values (p = 0.0011), suggesting distinct therapeutic vulnerabilities between subtypes (Fig. S3).

We investigated the binding capacity of Acrylamide with the four key gene proteins (CLK1, PSENEN, ROCK1, and SIRT1) through molecular docking experiments in this study. The results showed that the binding energies of Acrylamide with CLK1, PSENEN, ROCK1, and SIRT1 were − 6.3, -6.1, -6.0 and − 5.8 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating that Acrylamide can bind well with these proteins (Fig. 9A–D).

Further analysis of the GEO database revealed that only CLK1 and PSENEN exhibited statistically significant differential expression, with CLK1 being downregulated and PSENEN upregulated in EC. Consistent with the clinical data, CLK1 was markedly reduced in EC cells, while PSENEN showed elevated expression (Fig. 10I–J).

Discussion

Endometrial cancer remains a significant global health challenge, with its increasing incidence underscoring the need to understand environmental and dietary contributors to its pathogenesis [1]. Our study provides a comprehensive investigation into the potential oncogenic role of acrylamide, a probable human carcinogen prevalent in high-temperature cooked foods, in EC development. By integrating transcriptomic profiling, scRNA-seq, machine learning, and molecular docking, we identified four pivotal genes—ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN—as key mediators of acrylamide-induced EC progression. These findings align with emerging evidence linking dietary and environmental exposures to cancer risk and offer novel insights into acrylamide’s molecular mechanisms in EC.

Our integrative bioinformatics analysis identified nine consensus genes intersecting across GEO-derived DEGs, single-cell differential expression results, WGCNA modules, and acrylamide targets. These genes were significantly enriched in biological processes such as membrane protein ectodomain proteolysis and tetraspanin-enriched microdomains, as well as pathways including Notch, Wnt, and microRNAs in cancer. These pathways are well-documented in EC pathogenesis. Jonusiene et al. conducted a comprehensive review of the current research on Notch signaling in endometrial cancer, highlighting its potential role in the disease [24]. Fatima et al. discussed the Wnt activation mechanisms in endometrial cancer, emphasizing the latest advancements and challenges in developing drugs targeting the Wnt pathway [25]. The role of microRNAs in endometrial cancer has also garnered attention. Bogaczyk et al. discussed the involvement of miRNAs in the development of endometrial cancer, emphasizing their potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers [26]. Onuma et al. identified endometrial cancer-specific miRNA biomarkers from endometrial fluid using minimally invasive methods, further supporting their potential clinical application [27]. In breast cancer, acrylamide exhibits tumor-promoting effects by activating oncogenic pathways, including PI3K-Akt and HIF-1 signaling, which regulate cell survival, angiogenesis, and glycolytic metabolism under hypoxic conditions [28].In colorectal cancer, acrylamide preferentially enriches pathways linked to epigenetic dysregulation and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. Notably, aberrant activation of histone H2A deubiquitination may facilitate chromatin decompaction, thereby enhancing the transcription of oncogenes and accelerating tumorigenesis [29].

The identification of ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN as key genes through machine learning analyses underscores their potential as therapeutic targets. ROCK1, a serine/threonine kinase, plays a crucial role in the progression of endometrial cancer. Wen et al. found that endometrial cancer patients exhibit ECM stiffening, which is associated with increased ROCK1 expression. ROCK1 regulates the tumor microenvironment in endometrial cancer through the ROCK1/YAP1 axis, promoting tumor invasion and resistance [30]. Similarly, SIRT1, a histone deacetylase, promoted autophagy and proliferation of EC cells by reducing acetylation level of LC3 [31]. Lin et al. further discovered that SIRT1 enhances mitochondrial autophagy and drives hormone resistance through the deacetylation of FOXO3 [32]. The remaining two hub genes, CLK1 and PSENEN, have been less studied in endometrial cancer, although some studies have found their association with the development and progression of other types of tumors. Cdc2-like kinase 1(CLK1), a member of Cdc2-like kinase family [33]is upregulated in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinogenesis (ICC), and patients with high CLK1 expression have a poorer prognosis. CLK1 drives the initiation and progression of ICC by increasing YAP activity [34]. Yang et al. found that CLK1 mRNA is lowly expressed in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) tissues, and high expression of CLK1 predicts a significantly worsened prognosis in esophageal cancer patients, suggesting that CLK1 may promote tumor progression [35]. Presenilin enhancer gamma-secretase subunit (PSENEN) forms the protein complex γ-secretase with presenilin, nicastrin, and anterior pharynx defective 1 (APH1). γ-secretase can cleave proteins in transmembrane domains [36]. PSENEN is highly expressed in renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) and is associated with poor prognosis. PSENEN may be involved in regulating the immune microenvironment of KIRC, and oxidative phosphorylation may also play a role in its involvement in cancer development [37]. Previous studies show significantly upregulated levels of PSENEN expression in most human cancers compared to normal tissues [38]. Surprisingly, PSENEN is associated with survival and immune infiltration in low-grade gliomas [38].

We performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) to analyze immune cell infiltration and found that immune cell infiltration was increased in tumor tissues and correlated with the expression of key genes. The tumor microenvironment, particularly immune cell infiltration, plays a crucial role in cancer progression and response to therapy. The current study found that immune cell infiltration is associated with prognosis in endometrial cancer. Donkers et al. highlighted the importance of the tumor microenvironment in predicting immunotherapy efficacy in endometrial cancer, noting that high levels of T cell infiltration and increased PD-L1 expression are associated with better responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors [39]. Zhang et al. analyzed the role of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment of endometrial cancer, revealing the impact of immune dysregulation on the progression of endometrial cancer [40]. The results corroborate these findings, suggesting that immune cell infiltration can serve as a prognostic indicator and a potential target for immunotherapy in endometrial cancer.

Molecular docking experiments demonstrated strong binding affinities between acrylamide and the key proteins, providing molecular evidence for acrylamide’s oncogenic potential in EC. This finding is particularly relevant given the growing body of research on the role of environmental factors in cancer development. For instance, study found a potential link between exposure to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) and the risk of endometrial cancer. PFAS exposure may lead to oxidative stress, thereby creating conditions that facilitate the onset and progression of cancer [41]. A study explored the relationship between occupational pesticide exposure and endometrial cancer in women. The research found that exposure to certain pesticides may increase the risk of endometrial cancer [42].

While the current study provides valuable insights into the role of acrylamide in endometrial cancer, several areas require further investigation. First, the exact mechanisms by which acrylamide influences gene expression and signaling pathways need to be elucidated. Second, the clinical utility of mRNAs as biomarkers should be validated in larger cohorts. Third, the potential of targeting ROCK1, SIRT1, Notch, and Wnt pathways for therapeutic purposes warrants exploration. Finally, the role of immune cell infiltration in predicting response to immunotherapy should be further studied to optimize treatment strategies for endometrial cancer patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study elucidates the potential oncogenic mechanisms of acrylamide in EC through integrative transcriptomic, machine learning, and molecular docking analyses. The identification of ROCK1, CLK1, SIRT1, and PSENEN as key mediators, coupled with their interactions with acrylamide and influence on the TIME, provides novel insights into EC pathogenesis. These findings highlight the importance of environmental factors in cancer development and underscore the need for targeted therapeutic strategies. Future experimental validation and clinical studies are essential to translate these findings into preventive and therapeutic interventions.
