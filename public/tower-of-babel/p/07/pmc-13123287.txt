Reproductive Health Services in Abortion-Providing Clinics: A Scoping Review of Provision and Policy Contexts
Authors: Katherine M Mahoney, Licia Bravo, Arden McAllister, Kacie Bogar, Sean Hennessy, Courtney A Schreiber, Alice Abernathy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:Abortion-providing clinics often offer preventive reproductive health services. We conducted a scoping review to characterize provision of contraceptive care, sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing and/or treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and/or screening at U.S. abortion-providing clinics and to highlight policy drivers of variation in access to and provision of these services at the time of abortion by abortion-providing clinics.Data Sources:Researchers screened articles and extracted data from PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL from inception through March 2023.Methods of Study Selection:Articles were eligible for review if they related contraceptive care, STI testing and/or treatment, or cervical cancer prevention and/or screening to abortion access, demand, or provision. Within these articles, we closely examined any discussion of health policy, conceptualized broadly based on existing literature describing policy associations with reproductive health care to identify policy drivers of reproductive health care provision in abortion care settings.Tabulation, Integration, and Results:A total of 5,359 articles were screened; 74 were included in the review. Sixty-five were about contraceptive care, seven on STI testing and/or treatment, and two on cervical cancer prevention and/or screening. Drivers of variation in provision of preventive reproductive health services included insurance and billing policies and regulatory requirements on abortion-providing facilities and clinicians. Existing data on the association between policies that restrict or protect abortion access and provision of preventive health services by abortionproviding clinics on a national scale are limited.Conclusion:Abortion-providing clinics are an access point for reproductive health services. Legislative trends to constrain abortion access may have spillover effects on provision of preventive reproductive health care such as contraceptive care, STI testing and/or treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and/or screening.

While historical state-level restrictions on abortion provision created barriers to abortion care before the June 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision that struck down the constitutional right to abortion, the number of abortion-providing clinics in the United States fell precipitously after the Dobbs decision (Guttmacher Institute, 2022). State legislation restricting abortion, followed by abortion clinic closure, has outpaced legislation expanding abortion access (Center for Public Health Law Research, 2022; Guttmacher Institute, 2023).

Constrained abortion access affects public health through changes in birth rates, maternal morbidity and mortality, and provider training (de Londras et al., 2022; Peachman, 2022; Stevenson, 2021; Strasser et al., 2022; Zahedi-Spung et al., 2020). In addition to abortion care, abortion-providing clinics are also a site for other reproductive health care including contraceptive care (Kavanaugh et al., 2010; Roe & Bartz, 2019; Witwer et al., 2020), sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing and treatment (Frost, 2008; Kaiser Family Foundation, 2020), and cervical cancer prevention and screening (Frost, 2008; Lu & Slusky, 2014).

Even before Dobbs, the provision of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening by abortion-providing clinics may have been impacted by health policy context. Policies that create onerous regulatory and reporting requirements specific to abortion-providing clinics, restrict reimbursement of routine health services when provided at the time of abortion, and prohibit abortion in all or most instances may limit abortion-providing clinic capacity to deliver other health services (Abernathy et al., 2024). However, the intersection of abortion-related health policy and general reproductive care has not been thoroughly explored. Variation in health policies over time and by state may have resulted in differential changes in provision of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening at the time of abortion or by abortion-providing clinics. Thus, we conducted a scoping review to first understand the breadth of routine reproductive health care provided at the time of abortion or in clinics that provide abortion. Second, we sought to understand how various health policies have created barriers or facilitated delivery of comprehensive reproductive health care. We concluded with a synthesis of the literature to identify knowledge gaps in how health policy changes have affected delivery of such care in abortion care settings.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and Registration

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for scoping reviews. We registered the protocol with Open Science Framework.

Scope

We aimed to examine the extent to which comprehensive reproductive health services—defined as contraceptive care, STI testing and/or treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and/or screening—are offered at U.S. abortion-providing clinics and to highlight policy drivers of variation in provision of comprehensive reproductive health care in abortion care settings. The key questions addressed in this review are as follows:

Key Question 1 (KQ1): What is the current landscape and historic context of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening at the time of abortion or in clinics that provide abortion?

Key Question 1 (KQ1): What is the current landscape and historic context of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening at the time of abortion or in clinics that provide abortion?

Key Question 2 (KQ2): How have health policies created barriers to or facilitated delivery of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening in abortion care settings?

Key Question 2 (KQ2): How have health policies created barriers to or facilitated delivery of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening in abortion care settings?

Synthesize KQ1 and KQ2 to identify knowledge gaps regarding how health policy changes have affected delivery of such care in abortion care settings.

Sources: Search and Screening Strategy

We developed our search strategy in consultation with a librarian at the University of Pennsylvania, recommendations from team members with experience conducting scoping reviews, and published guidelines (Peters et al., 2020). We searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL from inception to March 24, 2023 (Table 1). We did not include clinicaltrials.gov in our searched databases.

Article Selection and Information Extraction

References were uploaded into Covidence (Covidence Systematic Review Software, 2024). Each title and abstract was reviewed by two of the five reviewers. The reviewers included collaborators trained in clinical provision of reproductive health care, an expert in reproductive health policy, and trained health services researchers. We required consensus between two reviewers for full-text review. If it could not be determined if an article met inclusion criteria based on abstract screen alone, it was included for full-text review. Conflicts were resolved by discussion until reaching consensus among all reviewers. One of five authors reviewed the full text and abstracted data. An additional author reviewed the extracted data before it was finalized.

Articles were eligible for review if they related contraceptive care, STI testing and/or treatment, or cervical cancer prevention and/or screening to abortion access, demand, or provision. Articles did not have to be studies to be included; for example, we included medical organization guidelines, expert updates, and systematic reviews. Articles on abortion access alone or those with a non-U.S. focus were excluded. We did not restrict by year of publication or study design because reproductive health care delivery has evolved over the past several decades. The definition of health policy context was intentionally broad because health policy related to routine reproductive health care, including abortion provision, has changed significantly over time. We considered U.S. policies such as abortion bans, abortion reporting requirements, restrictions on insurance coverage of abortion, restrictions on public funding of abortion, physician-only abortion provision requirements, and targeted regulation of abortion providers—that is, laws or rules that set more stringent requirements for abortion providers than for providers of other health care services with comparable levels of risk (Austin & Harper, 2019; Center for Public Health Law Research, 2022)—as well as changes in funding for and coverage of family planning services such as Title X and the Affordable Care Act (Swan, 2021). We did not include global family planning frameworks or metrics.

For all articles, we extracted article ID, title, authors, and aim/objective. For studies, we also extracted inclusion criteria, region of United States, methods, study design (cross-sectional study, randomized controlled study, cohort study, text/opinion, qualitative research, systematic review, other), start date, end date, population, participant features, and total number of participants. We identified if the study outcome was relevant to direct patient care, public health outcomes, or health policy. We organized data by reproductive health service delivered at the time of abortion (contraceptive care, STI testing and/or treatment, cervical cancer prevention and/or screening). We identified whether the article focused on (KQ1) additional reproductive health care provided at the time of medication or procedural abortion or services provided by abortion-providing clinics; or (KQ2) policy changes or policy environment that impacted care provided at the time of abortion or by abortion-providing clinics. We created a narrative synthesis of these findings to identify knowledge gaps regarding how health policy changes have affected delivery of reproductive health care in abortion care settings. We did not conduct a critical appraisal of the study findings, as this is not required for scoping reviews. Rather, we generated a narrative summary of results to identify future avenues of investigation that may capture the impact of health policy changes on these outcomes.

Results

A total of 5,359 articles were eligible for initial review. After excluding duplicates, non-U.S. articles, and articles that did not link contraceptive care, STI testing and/or treatment, or cervical cancer prevention and/or screening to abortion access, demand, or provision, 74 articles were included for full-text review (Figure 1). There were 65 articles on contraceptive care, seven on STI testing and/or treatment, and two on cervical cancer prevention and/or screening. Some articles addressed more than one of these services (Table 2). Publication years ranged from 1974 to 2023, and designs included randomized controlled trials, cohort and cross-sectional studies, qualitative studies, systematic reviews, and opinion pieces. We generated a timeline to summarize our results, in which we present the counts of articles included in the review, organized by reproductive health service provided and year, and notable health policies and frameworks for reproductive health care delivery (Figure 2).

Contraceptive Care

Provision of Contraceptive Care at the Time of Abortion

Contraceptive care was the most widely studied area: 65 articles examined contraceptive services. Most clinics reported offering contraceptive education, though one study noted most patients had already chosen a method of contraception by the time they were seen in an abortion-providing clinic (Iyer et al., 2022). Oral contraceptives were nearly universally available, but other methods including long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs; intrauterine devices [IUDs] and contraceptive implants) were not available at all abortion-providing clinics (Bryant et al., 2018; Hammerslough & Irizarry Mora, 1990; Kavanaugh et al., 2010; Kavanaugh, Jones, et al., 2011; Lerner et al., 1974; Lilja et al., 2021; Lindheim, 1979; O’Connell et al., 2009; Roe et al., 2019; White et al., 2019). Clinics that primarily provided abortion care were more likely to offer IUDs compared with primary care or general obstetrics and gynecology clinics (Lilja et al., 2021; Vohra-Gupta et al., 2022). Type of contraception desired at the time of abortion did not vary by urbanicity (Clement et al., 2022). Clinics that accepted insurance placed more LARCs, though clinic reimbursement for services associated with insertion, removal, and follow-up varied by insurance type (Bryant et al., 2018; Vela et al., 2018). LARCs were more likely to be placed at the time of procedural abortion than medication abortion (Laursen et al., 2017). Policies that restricted reimbursement for abortion or care provided at the time of abortion were associated with lower rates of LARC provision (Bryant et al., 2018; Cottrill et al., 2021; Lerner et al., 1974; Mastey et al., 2021; Thompson et al., 2011). We found overall high rates of patient satisfaction with contraceptive care at the time of abortion; satisfaction was especially high for those who previously experienced barriers to accessing contraceptive care, including patients without a longitudinal care provider (Kavanaugh, Carlin, et al., 2011; White et al., 2020) or for whom cost was a consideration (McNicholas et al., 2012; 2014; Sonalkar et al., 2013).

Many articles framed contraceptive care at the time of abortion or by abortion-providing clinics as a public health benefit that would reduce unintended pregnancies. For example, Ganti and Hillard (2019) noted access to abortion and contraceptive care reduced unintended or rapid repeat pregnancies in adolescents. Studies that modeled immediate LARC insertion estimated more than 70,000 unplanned pregnancies (Patil & Bednarek, 2015; Salcedo et al., 2013) and 20,000 repeat abortions could be prevented annually in the United States if 20% of patients opted for immediate post-abortion LARC (Reeves et al., 2007). Receipt of desired tubal occlusion at the time of abortion was found to reduce unintended pregnancies and improve quality-adjusted life years (Krashin et al., 2014). These articles were published when the Tiered Effectiveness Contraceptive Counseling Frame-work predominated. This framework considers method effectiveness to be the most important attribute and therefore encourages providers to begin patient counseling by describing the most effective reversible methods (i.e., LARC) first (Brandi & Fuentes, 2020). Thus, many articles at the time focused on LARC specifically and did not explore contraceptive access, interest, and uptake beyond LARC. Emergency contraception provision was found to be inversely correlated with birth and abortion rates in Utah (Turok et al., 2009). A higher number of publicly funded contraceptive clinics was associated with higher rates of abortion decline over time than a lower number of publicly funded contraceptive clinics (Madison et al., 2023).

Insurance and cost barriers limit post-abortion contraception access (Biggs et al., 2017; Bryant et al., 2018; Cottrill et al., 2021; Goodman et al., 2008; Goyal et al., 2017; Harper et al., 2015; Jerman et al., 2019; Kavanaugh et al., 2010; Kavanaugh, Jones, et al., 2011; Krashin et al., 2017; Langston, 2014; McNicholas et al., 2014; Postlethwaite et al., 2018; Rocca et al., 2016; 2018; Rodriguez et al., 2022; Roe et al., 2019; Schreiber & Traxler, 2015; Sonfield & Gold, 2014; Taylor et al., 2014; Thompson et al., 2011; Tsao et al., 2014; Vela et al., 2018; White et al., 2012; 2020; Witwer et al., 2020). Insurance coverage was associated with a 30–100% increase in LARC uptake (Biggs et al., 2017; Cottrill et al., 2021; Krashin et al., 2017; Rocca et al., 2016). Patients receiving abortion reported the primary reasons for not using a preferred contraceptive method were lack of insurance coverage and cost (White et al., 2020). Patients may choose not to use insurance for abortion-related care for confidentiality or logistical reasons (Rocca et al., 2016; 2018; Sonfield & Gold, 2014) and for this reason may be limited to more affordable contraceptives or forgo contraceptives due to cost constraints when insurance is not used for care (White et al., 2012). Most studies (Goyal et al., 2017; Krashin et al., 2017; McNicholas et al., 2014; Rocca et al., 2016; Rodriguez et al., 2022; Roe et al., 2019; Taylor et al., 2014), but not all (Schreiber & Traxler, 2015), found that when cost barriers are removed, effective post-abortion contraception uptake increases, suggesting that financial concerns might be a factor in contraception use among those who obtain abortions.

Insurance policies and contraceptive costs also limit abortion-providing clinics’ ability to provide contraceptive care. Many abortion-providing clinics do not accept insurance for abortion or post-abortion contraception (Bryant et al., 2018; Harper et al., 2015; Jerman et al., 2019; Kavanaugh et al., 2010; Kavanaugh, Jones, et al., 2011; Langston, 2014; Postlethwaite et al., 2018; Sonfield & Gold, 2014; Thompson et al., 2011; Tsao et al., 2014; Vela et al., 2018; White et al., 2012; Witwer et al., 2020). The reported reasons for not accepting insurance are related to broader policy barriers, including reimbursement issues (Jerman et al., 2019; Langston, 2014; Postlethwaite et al., 2018; Sonfield & Gold, 2014; Thompson et al., 2011; Tsao et al., 2014; Vela et al., 2018; Witwer et al., 2020), high upfront cost of LARC (Harper et al., 2015; Sonfield & Gold, 2014; Thompson et al., 2011; White et al., 2012), and limited experience with billing insurance (Sonfield & Gold, 2014). The percentage of clinics that do not accept insurance varied by clinic type and location, and state Medicaid coverage of abortion was associated with higher likelihood of clinics accepting insurance for contraceptive care and specifically LARC (Bryant et al., 2018; Kavanaugh et al., 2010; Kavanaugh, Jones, et al., 2011).

Methods recommended to reduce insurance-related barriers included specialized funding programs for post-abortion contraceptive care (Goyal et al., 2017), negotiating directly with pharmaceutical companies for reduced rates on contraceptive methods (Jerman et al., 2019), qualifying for 340B drug pricing and other federal or public funding programs (Jerman et al., 2019), and hiring financial counselors to address patient-level barriers (Jerman et al., 2019).

Impact of Abortion Policy on Contraceptive Access and Uptake

Restrictions on providing abortion care and decreased funding for family planning clinics disproportionately harmed residents in areas with already limited contraceptive access (Grossman et al., 2014; White et al., 2012). Access to the most effective contraceptive methods was reduced when abortionproviding clinics could not purchase contraceptives through 340B programs, a government program that allows certain clinics, including Title X family planning clinics, to purchase contraception at a discounted rate (White et al., 2012). Changes to the family planning budget in Texas redirected funding away from specialized family planning clinics and family planning clinics that provided abortion and toward health centers that focus on primary care. The funding shifts resulted in a reduction in the total number of contraceptive clients seen (Madison et al., 2023).

Rodriguez et al. (2023) reported that the number of female Medicaid enrollees living in counties with low contraceptive use and restricted abortion access was expected to increase 46% following the Dobbs ruling.

Thompson et al. (2011) found that states with contraceptive coverage mandates and states with Medicaid family planning expansion programs had higher rates of LARC use. Jacobs and Stanfors (2015) found state-level abortion accessibility and hostility did not significantly modulate contraceptive use compared with individual characteristics.

STI Testing and/or Treatment

Seven articles pertained to STI testing and/or treatment at the time of abortion or by abortion-providing clinics. Patients seeking abortion had a high prevalence of chlamydia and gonorrhea (Gokhale et al., 2018; Park et al., 2017), and Black women were four times more likely than white women to have multiple STIs when tested for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomonas (McNicholas et al., 2014). Among adolescents seeking abortion, 80% were screened for chlamydia and gonorrhea, and 22% tested positive (Lee et al., 2019). O’Connell et al. (2009) reported more than half of abortion clinics perform STI testing.

Lindsay et al. (1990) found that most patients were amenable to HIV testing at the time of abortion and that universal screening was associated with improved detection among those without reported risk factors for HIV acquisition. Newmann et al. (2013) recommended opt-out counseling and treatment for HIV at the time of abortion. Many abortion-seeking patients had indications for HIV Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP), though most expressed preference for starting PrEP with their primary care provider (Ralph et al., 2021). Satish et al. (2023) found other barriers to PrEP prescription at the time of abortion including low PrEP knowledge among patients, low willingness of providers unfamiliar with PrEP to prescribe PrEP, and limited financial and staffing resources that make prescribing and monitoring PrEP difficult.

Srinivas et al. (2022) was the only study that reported on the association between abortion policy and STI outcomes. The researchers found increased incidence of gonorrhea in counties with an abortion clinic closure relative to those without a closure.

Cervical Cancer Prevention and/or Screening

Ellison et al. (2021) found that following Ohio legislation that restricted public funding of organizations that provide or refer for abortion, increased driving time to the closest abortion clinic was associated with reduced likelihood of receiving a Pap test among all Ohio women ages 18–45 with household incomes <$50,000 (though this association was not as robust without imputation of key variables). O’Connell et al. (2009) reported that around half of abortion clinics perform Pap tests. Gerber et al. (2021) found that the abortion visit offers an important opportunity to start or finish the human papilloma virus (HPV) vaccine series, as participants in their single-clinic study were receptive to receiving HPV vaccines during abortion visits and reported no prior opportunity to receive the HPV vaccine.

Other Health Care Services

Although not the focus of the review, several articles addressed additional services beyond contraceptive care, STI care, and cervical cancer prevention and screening offered at abortion clinics, including pelvic exams, pregnancy tests, hematologic assessments (Lindheim, 1979), transgender-specific health care, mammogram and breast exams (Witwer et al., 2020), opioid use disorder care (Mastey et al., 2021), and other forms of psychological, medical, and social service counseling (Hammerslough & Irizarry Mora, 1990). Ellison et al. (2021) found that following Ohio restriction of funding for abortionproviding clinics, farther distance from abortion clinic was associated with decreased likelihood of receiving a mammogram or breast exam.

Discussion

Findings from this scoping review suggest contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening are acceptable to patients seeking abortion and that abortion-providing clinics are a point of access for these services. Such care is consistent with guidelines endorsed by the Society of Family Planning and American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG, 2021; Roe & Bartz, 2019). Despite this, abortion-providing clinics face many barriers to providing preventive reproductive health services with abortion care, including limitations of insurance coverage of abortion and care provided at the time of abortion, reimbursement issues when insurance is billed, and facility capacity to provide services at the time of abortion (Abernathy et al., 2024; Dehlendorf & Grumbach, 2008; Kavanaugh, Jones, et al., 2011; Thompson et al., 2011; Upadhyay et al., 2022).

Many patients who already experience structural and systemic barriers to health care may access contraceptive care, STI screening and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening through abortion-providing clinics. For this reason, it is critical to understand the impact of abortion-related policies on provision of routine reproductive health care. Though we anticipated variability in provision of preventive reproductive health care at the time of abortion or by abortion-providing clinics, we found few national studies of the relationship between abortion policy and provision of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening and few studies that compared provision of these services in states with different policy environments.

Implications for Practice and Policy

Abortion-providing clinics are an access point for reproductive health services. Abortion care is delivered through a combination of independent clinics and those funded by state and federal programs, which creates a complex health delivery network vulnerable to policy shifts that hinder abortion care provision and indirectly affect the concomitant services provided (Frost et al., 2014, Meier & McFarlane, 1994; McFarlane & Meier, 1998; Planned Parenthood Annual Report 2024). In most cases where abortion care was restricted, articles found worse public health outcomes, either related to preventive health service utilization or other related outcomes. However, the complex policy landscape and different state environments mean the magnitude of this association is difficult to ascertain from this review. As abortion care is restricted and abortion-providing clinics close, patients who would obtain care at a clinic providing abortion may either forgo services or obtain them elsewhere. Whether the current infrastructure for care delivery is sufficient to meet this shifting demand remains unknown and merits further investigation.

Conclusion

Research suggests that abortion-providing clinics serve as a source of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening and that patients who rely on these clinics for such preventive care will need to seek other providers as more abortion clinics close. Research on how policies that restrict or protect abortion access impact abortionproviding clinics’ provision of contraceptive care, STI testing and treatment, and cervical cancer prevention and screening on a national scale is limited. More research is needed to examine the public health impact of clinic closures after Dobbs. Attention should be paid to changing trends in contraceptive use, STI rates, and cervical cancer in regions where many abortion-providing clinics have closed, thus reducing access to reproductive health services.
