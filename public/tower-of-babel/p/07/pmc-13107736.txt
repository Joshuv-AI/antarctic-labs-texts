The effectiveness of interventions designed to enhance and maintain medical student wellbeing: a systematic review
Authors: Anita J. Horvath, Farhana Ahmed Shegufta, Muhammad Reza Chairilsyah, Kevin Wikanta, Nilakshi T. Waidyatillake, Katharine Reid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological well-being is crucial for medical students, who face high levels of stress throughout their training. This systematic review examines interventions designed to enhance the well-being of medical students, addressing gaps in previous research that often focused on specific interventions or included broader health professional populations.MethodsThis systematic review was undertaken according to PRISMA guidelines and registered with PROSPERO (CRD42018097172). The review analysed 33 studies identified from a search of Medline and PsycINFO, identifying a variety of interventions and outcome measures of wellbeing. Results were narratively synthesised and primary studies assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB-2) tool for interventional studies and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool for observational studies.ResultsThe most effective interventions were mindfulness-based programs, particularly when combined with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). These approaches helped students manage stress, anxiety, and depression, which occur frequently among medical students. Notably, online mindfulness programs were as effective as in-person sessions, offering flexibility for students with demanding schedules. Voluntary participation in these interventions also led to better outcomes, highlighting the importance of student engagement. Other effective interventions included positive psychology programs, which foster resilience, coping ability and gratitude, and physical exercise which reduced stress and improved overall well-being. In contrast, voluntary mentoring programs and health education initiatives showed limited effectiveness in reducing psychological distress, possibly due to inconsistent implementation or a lack of emotional support. Interestingly, the review found that the frequency and duration of effective interventions did not significantly affect their efficacy. This suggests that simply including and delivering wellbeing interventions in curricular may be more important than their length or frequency. Participants who engaged in mindfulness and other well-being interventions also maintained improvements in mental health during follow-up assessments, indicating long-term benefits.ConclusionVoluntary mindfulness interventions combined with CBT, along with positive psychology and exercise programs, were identified as the most effective strategies for enhancing the well-being of medical students. Future research should explore integrating these interventions into medical curricula to ensure lasting mental health support throughout students’ training.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08460-5.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines health as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, highlighting that health encompasses much more than just the absence of illness or disability [1]. Psychological well-being is influenced by many factors, including socioeconomic, biological, and environmental elements. 1 This holistic view is essential for enabling individuals to function effectively in their daily lives [2].

Psychological well-being encompasses both the experience of positive emotions and the ability to function effectively in daily life [2]. For sustained psychological wellbeing, individuals must be able to effectively process negative emotions and stressors, which are a part of daily life, in order to restore positive emotions [2]. Therefore it is vital that individuals are able to adopt effective strategies to enhance, sustain and restore the psychological wellbeing [1–3].

Medical students often face elevated stress levels throughout their training, resulting in lower psychological well-being compared to their peers in the general student population [4]. The medical curriculum presents unique challenges, including exposure to patient illness, demanding academic commitments, financial pressures, and the emotional weight of confronting death [3–5]. Additionally, the competitive and high-stress environment inherent in medical education further exacerbates these difficulties, making it essential to prioritize the well-being of students in this demanding field. Further, recent research shows a significant decline in medical student well-being since the COVID-19 pandemic, largely due to increased isolation and disruptions to course progression [6].

Medical students are reported to begin their studies with a higher level of psychological wellbeing compared to their age-matched peers; however, their mental health declines as they progress through their training to below that of their non-medical peers [4]. Such evidence highlights the importance of medical schools investing in interventions to improve or sustain the mental wellbeing of students during medical school [5, 7].

The health and wellbeing of medical students and interventions to enhance psychological wellbeing have increasingly been of interest to educators, therefore there is a need to identify effective wellbeing interventions that could be feasibly embedded in medical education curricular. To date, systematic reviews and meta-analysis of the literature have focused on specific wellbeing interventions such as mindfulness-based interventions [8, 9] and some have focused on specific wellbeing outcomes such as burnout, anxiety and stress [9–12], or included other health professional students, medical practitioners or students with pre-existing mental health concerns in addition to the general medical student cohort [13]. The external stressors that impact the broader student cohort may not reflect the complex pressures of the medical curricular therefore limiting the applicability of identified effective interventions. A recent umbrella review of well-being intervention reviews also highlighted the low quality of reviews available in this area [14]. To date, there has been no comprehensive systematic review evaluating the efficacy of wellbeing interventions specifically for medical students with no pre-existing or diagnosed mental health condition or examining current interventions and those applied prior to and since the COVID-19 pandemic.

This review aims to understand the effectiveness of wellbeing interventions, including but not limited to those based on mindfulness, for medical student cohorts. The review focuses on outcomes relating to psychological wellbeing such as stress, anxiety, depression as well as mental/emotional wellbeing including burnout. It captures the most recent research, from five years prior to the onset of COVID-19 to 2024. The review also examines if the length or frequency of the intervention, obligation to participate and the mode of delivery (online or in-person) has any impact on its effectiveness and if any identified improvements are sustained over time.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review was undertaken according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO [15]. Medline and PsycINFO were searched with an initial search in August 2023 and the final search was conducted in March 2025. Clinical Trial number: not applicable.

Search strategy and study selection process

Key search terms were formulated around the population of medical students with no pre- existing mental health conditions; the intervention terms included any form of wellbeing interventions; and the outcome of medical students’ wellbeing. Additionally, the search was limited to original articles that were written in English and an additional manual search was conducted of the reference lists of prior systematic reviews and in selected studies for this review.

Selection process

Search results were extracted using EndNote, then transferred to Covidence for screening, including deduplication. Papers written in languages other than English were extracted separately and translated into English using Endnote. The initial title and abstract screening of the studies was conducted by one reviewer (FAH), then checked by two other reviewers (KGW and MRC). In case of disagreement, the disagreement was resolved via discussion with AH and NW.

Data collection process and synthesis of results

A standardised Google Sheets Document was used to extract key characteristics of the included studies. Data extraction was performed initially by one reviewer (FAH), then confirmed by two other reviewers (KGW and MRC). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with AH and NW. The study design and settings, subjects’ characteristics, intervention and control, outcome measures, quantitative data, and author’s conclusion from each study was extracted. Thereafter, the information was narratively synthesised. Meta-analysis was not possible due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures.

Review quality appraisal

Quality assessments were performed by two independent reviewers (MRC and KGW) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB-2) tool for interventional studies [16] and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) tool for observational studies [17]. The RoB-2 quality appraisal results are presented using the robvis tool for risk of bias visualisation [18]. The quality appraisal result was then checked by the third reviewer (FAS). Any disagreements were resolved in discussion with AH and NW. RoB-2 assesses the risk of bias of interventional studies by evaluating the randomisation process, deviation from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported results. The studies were then categorised as having high, some concern, or low risk of bias [16]. Following the NOS tool guideline, the risk of bias of observational studies was rated numerically with the maximum of 4 stars for the selection category, 2 stars for the compatibility category, and 3 stars for the outcome category. The studies were then categorised as having high risk of bias if the stars range from 0 to 3, moderate risk of bias if the stars range from 4 to 6, or low risk of bias if the stars range from 7–9 [17].

Results

The comprehensive database searches yielded 3149 articles and three additional articles identified from manual searching of reference lists (Fig. 1). These were uploaded to COVIDENCE via EndNote and 87 duplicates were removed. The remaining articles were screened in COVIDENCE against the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1). Based on the title and abstract screening, 131 studies were identified for further review. All of these papers were published in English language journals, with none of the 84 articles published in languages other than English meeting the inclusion criteria. Finally, full text screening of 131 studies (excluding one study which was unable to be retrieved), led to the inclusion of 33 studies relevant for this review for data extraction. Of the 33 studies, 13 were randomised control trials 19–31, 12 were quasi experimental studies [32–43], and 8 were cohort studies [44–51]. The summary of the data extracted and characteristics of the 33 included studies is reported in Table 2.

Study year, location and participant demographic

The year of study publication ranged from 2014 [20, 50] until 2023 [21, 25, 48], with the highest number of studies published in 2020 [19, 34, 36–38, 40–42, 44]. The geographic location of the studies included Asia [22, 28, 41–43, 45, 47, 50, the United States of America [19, 20, 26, 37, 39, 40, 48, 49], Europe [21, 25, 27, 33, 35, 38, 42], South America [24, 29, 36, 46, 51], Australia [32, 34], New Zealand [23], and Canada [44] (Table 2).

Mental and emotional wellbeing

The sample size of the included studies varied from 44 to 374 participants [25, 41]. Further, within the study population, most studies reported a higher number of female medical students as participants; except for five studies where the number of male students was higher [20, 28, 30, 42, 46]. The mean age of the participants ranged from 18.3 to 26.7 years old [34, 43] across the studies, with two studies reporting age groups but not the mean age of participants [46, 48].

Due to the unique curricula of medical schools in different countries the years of medical training and the year in which interventions were included varied across the studies. For instance, six studies included 1 st and 2 nd year students [33, 37, 40, 42, 48, 49], while another six studies included only 1 st year students20,23,29,38,36,38. One study only included medical students attending foundation years[32], while another study only included students from the core clerkship years [19]. On the other hand, four studies did not specify the year of study [21, 27, 31, 47] (Table 2).

Intervention types, length and mode of delivery

The range of wellbeing intervention reported in the included studies are categorized as either mindfulness-based interventions (MBI) or other interventions (Fig. 2). Of the 33 studies in the final selection, a total of 15 studies had mindfulness-based interventions (MBI) [19–26, 32–35, 44–46] with the intervention duration ranging from four weeks [21, 45] to one academic year [19], and the sessions ranging from four sessions [19, 45] to twenty-six sessions [22]. Out of which, nine involved mindfulness meditation [20, 21, 24–26, 33–35, 44], three included courses on mindfulness practice [22, 45, 46], four involved yoga [20, 21, 33, 44], three were combined with CBT [22, 35, 45], three studies had mindful eating [21, 33, 44], and two incorporated physical exercise in the program [22, 45]. Moreover, four of the mindfulness-based interventions were online [21, 25, 34, 44], two studies integrated mindfulness teaching within the medical curriculum [19, 32], one was an app-based intervention [25]and one was a small group peer-support mindfulness program [22].

The remaining 18 studies involved other interventions such as mental health education programs27, 30, 36, 41, 42, 48, 50, physical activity40, 42, 48, positive psychology interventions (PPI) [31, 43, 51], meditation training [40, 47, 49], online CBT[36], electronic music [26], clinical hypnosis [38], narrative storytelling [27], sunrise alarm clock and smartphone deprivation intervention [37], and mentoring program [28]. These other intervention durations ranged from two weeks [37] to one year [28], and the frequency ranges from a single session [35] to sixteen sessions [39, 40].

Wellbeing outcomes

The effects of the interventions in the included studies assessed a variety of wellbeing outcomes (Tables 2 and 3). The most common reported were stress (n = 19) [20–22, 24–27, 29, 33–35, 37, 39–41, 45–48], anxiety (n = 16) [21, 23–25, 27, 29–31, 35, 36, 38, 39, 43, 45, 50, 51], depression (n = 16) [24, 25, 27–31, 35, 36, 39, 40, 42, 43, 45, 50, 51], quality of life (QoL) (n = 11) [22–24, 27–29, 36, 37, 40, 42, 50], resilience (n = 12) [19–23, 25, 30, 32, 39, 46, 49, 50], compassion (n = 5) [20, 25, 32, 34, 44], as well as mental and emotional wellbeing, which includes burnout (n = 6) [28, 35, 37, 41, 44, 46]. For this review, only quantitative data were extracted and the longest follow-up time post intervention (up to 6 months) was considered. Out of the 33 studies, only seven assessed outcomes at follow up, with three studies having a follow up at two-months [21, 25, 26], one study at three-months [29], one study at four-months [33], and two studies at six-months post intervention [20, 22].

Measures of wellbeing outcomes

Study authors utilized a wide range of tools to measure wellbeing outcomes in the included studies (Table 3). The most common tool for assessing stress was the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), used by fifteen studies [20–22, 26, 29, 33–35, 37, 40, 45–49]. To measure depression and anxiety, the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS) was most commonly used [24, 25, 29, 41, 45], which also measured stress. For QoL, the most commonly used measurement tool was the Perceived Health Questionnaire (PHQ) used by four studies [23, 28, 39, 40]. In addition, mental and emotional wellbeing included burnout and emotional stability which was measured using the Maslach Burnout Index (MBI) in five studies [28, 35, 37, 44, 46] and Difficulties in Emotional Regulation Scale (DERS) in two studies [21, 25]. The Resilience Scale (RS) was used to measure resilience in three studies [20, 21, 23], while the Self Compassion Scale (SCS) was used to measure compassion in four studies [20, 25, 34, 44].

Mindfulness based interventions (MBI) and wellbeing outcomes

The wellbeing outcomes for MBI interventions are summarized in Table 3 and the significance of these interventions presented graphically in Table 4. Of the fifteen MBI studies, eleven studies reported stress as an outcome [20–22, 24–26, 33–35, 45, 46] and seven studies showed a significant decrease in stress [20–22, 25, 26, 35, 45, 46] immediately post intervention. Furthermore, four MBI studies reported depression as an outcome [24, 25, 35, 45] with three reporting a statistically significant decrease [25, 35, 45], immediately post intervention. Out of the six MBI studies reporting anxiety [21, 23–25, 35, 45], four of them showed a significant decrease [21, 25, 35, 45] immediately post intervention. Moreover, seven out of nine MBI studies reported significant improvement in mental and emotional wellbeing, immediately post intervention [21, 22, 25, 26, 44–46]. Amongst the MBI studies reporting resilience, 3 of 7 studies reported significant increase in resilience, immediately post intervention [21, 32, 46]. Additionally, 4 of 5 MBI studies reported significant increase in compassion [20, 32, 34, 44], and 1 of 3 reported a significant improvement of QoL [21], immediately post intervention.

The six studies with MBIs had a follow up period ranging from one-months to six-months [20–22, 25, 26, 34]. Out of these studies, two of them showed a significant improvements of anxiety [21, 25], and four showed a significant improvement of emotional and mental wellbeing [21, 22, 25, 26]. One of them showed no significant improvement on depression [24] at follow-up, and the result for stress and resilience were mixed [20–22, 25, 26, 34].

The mode of delivery for majority of the MBI studies were in-person (n = 8) [19, 20, 22–24, 32, 33, 45], which when compared to the online or app-based (n = 7) [21, 25, 26, 34, 35, 44, 46] ones, resulted in similar number of significant improvements in the wellbeing domains of depression, anxiety and mental/emotional wellbeing, post intervention.

In terms of obligatoriness, 4 out of the 15 MBI studies were involuntary and yielded similar improvements in wellbeing as voluntary MBI studies [23, 24, 32, 46].

Non-MBI interventions and wellbeing outcomes

A broad range of other interventions that did not involve MBI and their impact on wellbeing outcomes were also examined (Table 3) and effects summarized (Table 5). Of the eighteen studies that implemented interventions other than MBI, eight studies reported stress as the outcome [27, 29, 37, 39–41, 47, 48], with six studies showing significant decrease immediately post intervention, which involved meditation training combined with physical activity, smartphone deprivation combined with sunrise alarm clock, mental health education, and emotional training [27, 37, 40, 41, 47, 48]. Furthermore, twelve other intervention studies reported depression as an outcome [27–31, 36, 39, 40, 42, 43, 50, 51]. Eight studies that involved mental health education, meditation, physical training, emotional training, clinician guided narrative storytelling, health discussion, and PPI reported a statistically significant decrease in depression, immediately post intervention.[28, 31, 36, 40, 42, 43, 50, 51]. Out of ten other interventions studies measuring anxiety [27, 29–31, 36, 38, 39, 43, 50, 51], six reported a significant decrease, involving mental health education, clinical hypnosis, emotional training, and PP [31, 36, 38, 43, 50, 51]. In addition, six of seven studies involving health education, PPI, emotional training, health discussions, physical activity and narrative story telling by clinicians showed a statistically significant increase in mental and emotional wellbeing, immediately post intervention [31, 42, 43, 48, 50, 51]. Four of nine other interventions studies reporting on QoL showed a statistically significant increase, which involved mental health education, meditation and physical training, immediately post intervention [36, 40, 41, 50]. There were four studies that included resilience as the outcome[30, 39, 49, 50], with three reporting a significant increase, that included intervention such as meditation, mental health education, physical activity and journaling [30, 49, 50]. In contrast with the MBI studies, none of the other interventions studies measured compassion.

Of these eighteen studies, ten were either curriculum-level or guided by either a trainer or a clinician [27–29, 36, 38, 39, 47, 49–51], while eight of them were voluntary individual level interventions that demonstrated similar improvements in psychological wellbeing compared to the mandated interventions (Table 5) [30, 31, 37, 40–43, 48].

Quality appraisal

The risk of bias assessment of included RCTs using ROB-2 have been summarized in Table 5. According to ROB-2 fifteen studies were rated as having high risk of bias [22, 26–28, 32–40, 42, 43] and eleven are rated as having “some concern” risk of bias [19–21, 23–25, 29–31, 41, 50]. All of the studies qualified as having “some concern” risk of bias on the Measurement of Outcome domain since they all rely on self-reported questionnaire when measuring their outcome [19–43, 50]. Ten studies did not give any explanation about their randomization process, therefore given high risk of bias on the Randomization Process domain [27, 32–37, 39, 42, 43]. Nine studies had high risk of bias on the Deviation from the Intended Interventions because they did not analyze the effect of adhering to intervention despite having low adherence rate [22, 26–28, 35, 38–40, 42]. Two studies were rated as having high risk of bias on the Missing Outcome Data domain due to having a large number of loss to follow-up participants [33, 40].

The quality assessment of included cohort studies using NOS have been summarized in Table 6. Based on the quality assessment using the NOS tool, four of the studies were rated as having moderate risk of bias [44, 48, 49, 51] and three had low risk of bias [43, 45, 47]. Most of the studies lose a star on the Ascertainment of Exposure and the Ascertainment of Outcome criterion due to most of them using self-report measurement methods [44, 45, 48, 49, 51]. The studies by Dhanvijay & Chandan (2018) and Keng, et al. (2015) were each given one stars due to controlling for other important compounding factors (history of major surgery, history of major illness, on medication, and suicidal) [45, 47]. In addition, only one study did not demonstrate adequate follow- up rate for their intervention [44] (Table 7).

Discussion

This systematic review included 33 papers. The review explored the published literature on interventions available to enhance and maintain medical student wellbeing and their effectiveness on wellbeing outcomes. A solid body of evidence exploring the impacts on medical student wellbeing at both the individual and medical curriculum level interventions were identified. However, heterogeneity in study designs, interventions and the wide range of tools that were used to measure wellbeing outcomes increased the complexity of assessing effectiveness of interventions.

Based on the results of the included studies, MBIs appeared to be beneficial in alleviating medical student’s stress, depression, anxiety, compassion while promoting mental and emotional wellbeing which also aligns with prior studies on effectiveness of MBI [5, 7, 52]. Furthermore, the duration and frequency of mindfulness- based interventions (MBIs) did not appear to influence the significance of the reported well-being outcomes, as shorter MBIs proved just as effective as longer ones. A similar conclusion was reached in a systematic review by Kaisti et al. [5]. A recent meta-analysis by Sperling et al. which examined the effects of MBI’s on stress among medical students demonstrated a moderate effect on stress reduction [12]. Systematic reviews by Kaisti et al., McConville et al., and Daya et al. also indicated that MBI results in sustainable wellbeing of medical students which supports the results of this review, as the improvement in wellbeing outcomes were mostly maintained and significant during the follow up period [5, 7, 14, 52].

Moreover, the previous systematic reviews differed from this review as they focused only on MBI with Kaisti et al. including only one MBI with CBT,5,26 McConville et al. focusing on all health science students rather than the medical student cohort separately [48] and Daya et al. reporting on studies prior to 2018 [7]. In contrast to Kaisti et al., online MBIs were found to be equally effective compared to in person MBIs and the obligatoriness of participation did not seem to have an impact on the effectiveness as per the included studies in this review [5]. Further, the MBI studies included in this review showed similar significant improvements in medical student wellbeing, compared to studies which employed MBI in combination with yoga, mindful eating or CBT.

Due to the variety of other interventions included in this review, it was difficult to compare the effectiveness across studies, although the interventions examined in this review showed improvements in wellbeing outcomes like stress, anxiety, depression, emotional and mental wellbeing, QoL, resilience and compassion and some were significant. Positive psychology [31, 43, 51], mental health education [27, 30, 36, 41, 42, 48, 50], meditation training [40, 47, 49] and physical activity [40, 42, 48] were some of the common themes amongst the other interventions. Furthermore, PPIs showed significant improvement in wellbeing outcomes for medical students which was through the development of an optimistic mindset and has been proven by previous literature [31, 43, 51]. Additionally, physical activity combined with meditation was effective in alleviating stress, depression and simultaneously increased QoL in medical students which is similar to studies reporting physical activity to be as effective as MBIs [40, 42]. However, formal peer mentoring intervention and internet based CBT appeared to be the least effective in terms of the improvements in anxiety, depression, stress, QoL and resilience not being significant [29, 39]. Akin to the current review, there have been studies reporting on mentoring programs not being successful in improving wellbeing with limited research on medical students and the intervention being mostly successful in improving medical resident wellbeing [53–55]. Similar to MBIs, the duration and frequency as well as obligatoriness of other interventions did not seem to impact the significance of wellbeing outcomes.

Limitations & further recommendations

The studies included in this review reported only self-reported measures therefore articles reporting physiological measures as well as a consistent measurement tool for wellbeing domains across studies would allow comparisons of effectiveness more reliable. Further, for subsequent intervention research, studies with long term follow up will support our understanding of the sustainability of these interventions. The impact of age, sex, gender, year level of intervention implementation and sample size on wellbeing outcomes were not analyzed in this review and should be explored in future studies of medical student wellbeing. Further the variability of the study designs, interventions and outcomes data did not allow us to conduct a meta- analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, positive psychological well-being of medical students is essential for not only successful completion of medical school but also for becoming compassionate and effective healthcare professionals who can care for both their patients and themselves. Evaluation of the diverse range of wellbeing interventions being utilized, demonstrates that mindfulness interventions—whether delivered online or in person or combined with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and other well-being programs that include physical exercise or positive psychology interventions (PPI), are the most effective in promoting and sustaining medical student well-being. However, the implementation of such interventions is further complicated by individual factors, such as student motivation and preferences, as well as logistical challenges, including the cost and resources required to integrate these programs into the medical curriculum. These factors must be carefully considered when designing strategies to support the mental health of medical students.
