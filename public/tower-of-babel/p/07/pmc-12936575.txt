Conflict and its global health consequences: a decolonial call to action
Authors: Jennie Gamlin, Ayesha Ahmad
Published: 2025

Introduction

Global health institutions hold the tradition of abstaining from political commentary when acting in response mode to contexts of humanitarian crisis, displacement or conflict, under the imperative of keeping access routes open to save lives. Yet if this community of practitioners and academics from the fields of medicine and public health or humanitarians concerned with the health of conflict-affected populations want a role in preventing the health and medical outcomes of social and ecological violence, they must not remain silent on the historical, geopolitical and contemporary determinants of war and conflict. This is a silence that in relation to the current situation in Gaza has been described as ‘morally unacceptable’.1

The coloniality of global political arrangements, territorial rights and injustices around natural resources is a structural faultiness that underlies many contemporary conflicts. Inequalities in geopolitical power also shape international trade relations, determining food supply chains at both local and global production levels with long-term consequences for the well-being of affected populations and the recovery of conflict-affected people. These processes belie the wounds that acute and emergency medicine treats in conflict situations. Stemming the bleeding without ‘cleaning out the wound’ by confronting its historicity is to be silent on the structural roots of trauma—and to silence the victims. As legal scholar Catalina Vallejo Piedrahíta argues, these ‘underlying causes tend to reappear and cause distress if they are not properly seen and transformed’.2 This is the work that is needed when devising conflict prevention strategies.

Food supply in times of conflict

Anthropologist Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing writes in her field defining monograph Friction. An ethnography of global connection that, ‘War has become a central force of capitalism’.3 Humanitarian healthcare in conflict settings is the space where violence meets healing and it is peculiar because while witnessing and treating the wounds of suffering, it is separated at both individual and geopolitical levels from meaning such as this. Here we speak of conflict in the broadest sense, including acute crises where there is an immediate threat to life as is the current situation in Gaza, and contexts of longstanding or irregular conflicts over identities or governance, land or resources including where international corporations, drugs cartels or armed militia are involved.

We do not see the lives that are annihilated through complicity and silencing in conflict. In Gaza, a narrative is unfolding where the weaponisation—global militarisation—of targeted starvation throughout the civilian population is strategised through forced famine and the killing of people seeking aid at food distribution points by Israeli forces and US contractors.4 At the time of writing, in the last 24 hours, five children have died from malnutrition and the UN World Food Programme has declared that hunger and malnutrition levels are the highest ever recorded, ‘with more than a third of the population going without food for days at a time and 500 000 people at the brink of famine’.4 While this emergency has finally been recognised by global leaders as reaching crisis and famine proportions, the historicity of the interruption and devastation of Gaza’s food supply chain has been little documented. In a recent study, Hassoun et al identify the social, environmental and economic dimensions of this, citing gender inequality, water pollution, energy shortages, access to food markets, displacement, interruption of food production, access to essential farming supplies such as seeds, fertilisers and machinery in addition to the knock-on effects on regional food supplies, as all factors in the destruction of Gaza’s food supply chains.5 Conceptualising this violence as ‘famine’, a closed time-frame conflict-related event, rather than continuous experiences that contain the ways that conflict structures the lives of affected populations is a reductionist framing that invisibilises historical and ongoing occupation of the region and its people. In parallel, the process of starvation and the choices that parents are having to make about who lives and who dies, or which child to prioritise by feeding, are not revealed through final descriptions of the totality of death, further blindsiding long-term lived realities. This stripping of personhood is another veil of violence which we must collectively lift for the healing of humanity. Biomedical conceptualisations have reduced prevention in conflict settings to bodies which require healing, and to figures for deaths of individuals disconnected from their history, identity and relationships with place. However, their wounded narratives are infused with transgenerational experiences or harm.

In the continued context of an emerging act of violence, globally critiqued to be an unfolding genocide, new-born babies in Gaza either orphaned or killed during Israel Defense Forces military interventions are now either living on, or buried in, land that has suffered a multi-generational trauma sited in and rooted to colonial violence. How medical communities conceptualise conflict and trauma, both physical and psychological, reverberates in geopolitical responses. We write this from concern with how dominant Global Health discourse has a tendency to view conflict as a localised occurrence, disconnected from global governance and its history. We seek to interrupt the coloniality of discourse around conflict by emphasising the continuity of its historical roots as a means of mitigating conflict-related experiences, including food shortages, and seeking to repair the harm at its source. To this end, we put forward the role of the Global Health community to be accountable in contributing to the personhood and identity of conflict-affected populations by making visible how the violation of people-land relations over time through both conflict and unequal power relations has both acute and longitudinal implications for populations’ health.

Slow violence and food sovereignty

Contemporary violences and conflicts largely unfold in a context of coloniality, by which we refer to the longstanding patterns of racialised and hierarchical power that emerged in the context of colonialism, and continue to be colonial in form.6 A decolonial approach to violence and conflict seeks to provide a critical counterpoint to dominant discourse by pointing out the coloniality of contemporary structures of power, and disassembling these by decolonising the knowledge, processes and institutions whose role it is to prevent it, that is, by ‘critically examining and reforming power imbalances’.7 Slow violence, a violence that unfolds gradually and unspectacularly,8 for example in the form of longstanding colonial relations that weaken food sovereignty through agrarian change or economic restructuring, is present in unequal power relations as they impact on forms of land use and territoriality. In Indigenous communities, colonial land relationships and divisions increasingly sit at the roots of conflict, in terms both of political and identity politics, and ecological or environmental conflict directly impacting food sovereignty. The institution of development, by treating land as a resource, is to Indigenous Ñuu Savi Edith Herrera a colonial mechanism for reproducing power imbalances, and she writes that extractivism, mining and megaprojects that pollute land, dry out waterbeds and create conflict are all presented as ‘development’, yet promote forms of land use that run counter to Indigenous territorialities and undermine Indigenous food systems.9 As Balvanera et al argue, colonialism ‘structurally shaped the un-sustainability’ of Indigenous food systems, as science and development discredited and replaced these with ‘new technological alternatives that boost yields at the expense of negative environmental and societal consequences’.10

Latin American Indigenous women increasingly politicise these relationships by speaking of body-territory, entangling the embodiment of territory with their bodies, where these ‘have become the primary political “territory”’, subjects of forced exploitation by drug cartels and political-economic alliances.11 Where land that was once used for the production of food is converted into the resource for drugs cartels, long-term food supply shortages and dependency on colonial food systems become entrenched. Beyond Latin America, Tak et al highlight the case of militarised violence on Indigenous food systems in Kashmir, demonstrating how access to land and land dispossession has had crucial implications for Kashmir diets.12 These are forms of slow violence that, by undermining food sovereignty, multiply the impacts of conflict and real-time violence on marginalised populations, creating protracted crises.12 They are a reminder that conflict and violence are rarely isolated in time and place and frequently reverberate as intergenerational trauma through the disruption of people-land relations. To critically reframe them, we must confront the coloniality of these violences, situate local, national and international conflicts within the mechanisms of global power, and recognise how historical structures and processes, such as development, trade relations, colonialism and white supremacy, generated these inequalities that underlie violences.

In a similar vein, reflecting on the cradles and graves of the conflict ravaging Gaza, demands a call on the global health community to ensure that the impunity of colonial powers ceases, is terminated and does not resurrect, as well as create embargoed spaces that are not occupied, but nurtured by the connections to land and community that will heal through well-being and shared relationships between the self and the soil.

What does this mean for addressing the global health impacts of conflict?

We need to communicate meanings about land and relations with it, to understand violence in its spatial and territorial origins and the repercussions of this for processes that sustain life, including the production of food. Time must be recognised as an enduring vector of conflict, opening space to consider the need to heal the harms to people-land relations that is necessary for sustainable solutions to well-being. These are not just historical wars playing out in contemporary times, but meanings grounded spatially that are vital to well-being. We appreciate that questions of access, safety and security for humanitarians in crisis settings can be dependent on their neutrality, although ‘political anaesthesia’13 serves no further purpose. Fassin has argued that ‘there is no inequality more disturbing than that by which we decide who can still interest us, and who no longer does’.13 Medicalised approaches do not prevent conflict, and the presence of international aid and development agencies can perpetuate stereotypes of violent communities in Lower and Middle Income Countries, with the LMIC label itself becoming an explanation of violence. Viewed from a decolonial perspective, the developmentalist paradigm is based on western and hence colonial notions of a linear pathway to modernisation, development and health, and the victims of violence are viewed through this lens. As Abimbola and Pai argue, “A future in which Global Health is decolonised would be one in which there are no longer pervasive supremacist remnants of colonialism within Global Health practice”,14 yet, recent cuts to foreign aid in parallel to increases in military spending in the UK, mirrored by the complete withdrawal of USAID, suggest both UK and US administrations do not take ownership of their role in creating and preserving the unequal relations that perpetuate conflict. In this Anthropocene epoch, when earthly limits are being tested, access to food and clean water is controlled by new political landscapes of supremacy. Wheat shortages and global price hikes brought this into UK and European households when the conflict in Ukraine hit supplies of this staple, but mostly Western nations’ purchasing power allows ambivalence towards local disruptions until these are brought to us in images of starvation.

Aid is not a solution to global inequality, yet it remains preferable to no aid at all, since its existence denotes a recognition that we inhabit an unjust world in which resources are unfairly distributed. Reductions in aid are a large step backwards for decolonising Global Health as it further removes from view the role of colonial nations in creating and enabling the conditions for conflict.

The call to action

Research which aims to contribute to conflict prevention should ‘use methods and generate data that reveal the impact of colonialism on everyday structures’ and lives.15 A decolonial methodology should disassemble discourse around underlying inequalities by redressing trauma, re-establishing healthy territorialities, naming historical injustices and evidencing their continuity.

Examples of decolonising approaches in situations of territorial conflict and war include:

Trans-disciplinary collaborations using artistic and participatory methods to explore what more just and sustainable local food systems would mean, connecting individual and collective agency with key leverage points within local food systems.10

These are localised initiatives and we need a real-time call to action. As Abimbola and Pai argue, “Equity and justice involve flipping every act of supremacy on its head”.14 One of these is how aid is delivered. Transforming it in such a way that it repairs injustice rather than replicating modes of domination. Considerable work is required to scale up efforts such as the above so that they are effective in preventing conflict and violence and to accompany them in making the colonial roots of conflict seen while actively constructing healthy environments. To do so requires shifting discourse to their colonial and historical origins and a commitment to decolonial actions within global and multilateral institutions. The responsibility for instigating these changes lies with the global north.

Conclusion

The case has been made clearly for decolonising global health.7 14 Our focus is on actioning the decolonisation of conflict prevention and its impacts on life-sustaining resources, and within this, nurturing and repairing people-land relations as a prerequisite for sustainable food supplies. As Fassin argues, critical perspectives become crucial ‘where violence disappears from our line of vision’.17 Refocusing our gaze and repositioning discourse addressing the health impacts of conflict and violence is a necessary step in transforming it into a platform for decolonial actions that address the permanence of the past in the present. Humanitarian and medical efforts that respond to conflict, while playing a vital role in maintaining life, also need to shift the paradigm of where conflict is managed, or negotiated, from bodies of pain to bodies of power.

As Global Health academics, we must use our positionality to positively influence public opinion so that we can shift discourse within the field toward unequal relationships of power. We can do this through research and teaching but also by calling on multilateral institutions to recognise the historicity of violent conflict, its broader impacts on supply chains and food sovereignty, and to commit to embedding decolonial understandings, addressing deep and historical causes of inequality in real-time so that contemporary conflicts do not become historical injustices.
