Wide-Field Optical Redox Imaging with Leading-Edge Detection Enables Assessment of Treatment Response and Heterogeneity in Patient-Derived Cancer Organoids
Authors: Amani Gillette, Shirsa Udgata, Alexa E. Schmitz, Jordan N. Stoecker, Jeremy D. Kratz, Dustin A. Deming, Melissa C. Skala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Label-free wide-field optical redox imaging is an accessible tool for monitoring changes in metabolism in patient-derived cancer organoids to reproducibly assess treatment response and heterogeneity in clinically relevant treatment studies.

Introduction

Precision medicine seeks to enhance cancer treatment by aligning the best therapies to individual patients, often relying on genomic mutations for decision-making (1–3). Although this approach works well for certain mutations, like EGFR in lung cancer and BRAF in melanoma, it has limitations across other cancers (4). Patient-derived cancer organoids (PDCO) are three-dimensional cultures created from fresh tumor specimens and retain key biological features of the individual patient’s cancer from which they were derived. This method provides functional insights into drug sensitivity, complementing genomic analyses. PDCOs effectively replicate the molecular, histopathologic, and phenotypic characteristics of the original tumors, offering accurate models for assessing patient drug responses (5–7). They also reflect the cellular diversity within tumors, which is crucial for understanding treatment failures related to resistant cell populations (8–10). In colorectal cancer, there are multiple molecularly defined subtypes with targeted therapy options, and resistance can develop through the acquisition or selection for clones with resistant molecular profiles (6, 10–12).Consequently, evaluating drug responses across multiple PDCOs is essential for capturing treatment response within a patient (6). However, traditional assessment methods used for two-dimensional cultures do not translate well to the three-dimensional nature of PDCOs. Most available PDCO assays measure the metabolic activity of the cells at the well level, limiting the ability to evaluate the heterogeneity of individual PDCOs and, therefore, heterogeneity within a patient (13–15). Additionally, longitudinal monitoring is often difficult as these assays are highly sensitive to baseline plating of the organoids, making it more challenging to control than with two-dimensional cultures (16–18). These challenges emphasize the need for nondestructive methods to quantify drug responses in PDCOs while accounting for cellular heterogeneity.

Cancer alters cellular metabolism, and many therapies aim to disrupt the altered metabolism to inhibit growth or trigger cell death (19). The autofluorescent properties of the metabolic coenzymes NADH, NADPH, and FAD offer a noninvasive way to monitor cellular metabolism in intact samples (20–26). NADH and NADPH have overlapping fluorescent properties and are jointly referred to as NAD(P)H. It is important to note that they do play different roles in metabolic pathways, and although the NADH/NAD+ ratio is generally more oxidized in cells compared with the NADPH/NADP+ ratio, the ratios are coupled. This suggests that changes in the combined NAD(P)H autofluorescence can be used to monitor changes in the reduced state of the individual cofactors (27–29). The fluorescence properties of FAD, an electron acceptor, and NAD(P)H, electron donors, are often combined into the optical redox ratio (ORR), which reflects the oxidation–reduction state of a sample. The ORR is sensitive to early metabolic changes induced by drugs, which can occur before noticeable shifts in PDCO size, cell proliferation, and cell death (30–33). However, most studies of autofluorescence in PDCOs have used expensive two-photon (2P) and/or fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopes that are not widely available (9, 30, 34, 35).

Label-free optical redox imaging (ORI) can also be performed using commonly available wide-field (WF) microscopes equipped with standard components, such as a broadband excitation source, a scientific monochrome camera, and standard 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) filter cubes to collect NAD(P)H and FAD, respectively (36, 37). Our group and others have previously used WF ORI at both cellular and tissue levels to evaluate oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and drug responses (23, 38–41). Although WF ORI cannot resolve individual cells as effectively as 2P microscopy due to the low signal of the autofluorescent coenzymes, it allows for the rapid imaging of large PDCO populations, which is crucial for studying subpopulations with varying treatment responses (10, 42, 43). Our prior work demonstrated that WF ORI is a practical tool for monitoring morphologic and metabolic changes in PDCOs, with single-organoid tracking enhancing sensitivity to drug responses (37). We found that the autofluorescence signal at the edge of the PDCOs was different from that of the core due to differing biological environments, such as the necrotic core (44), which generally has a high FAD signal (45). However, our prior work did not isolate the signal to the living cells on the edge of the PDCOs.

In this study, we expand upon prior work showing that an additional analysis step, leading-edge detection, improves sensitivity to treatment responses. Additionally, we performed a direct comparison between matched PDCOs imaged with both WF ORI and 2P ORI. Finally, we assessed the sensitivity of WF ORI to mutation profiles and targeted therapies in a blinded study of mixed populations of PDCOs. This work demonstrates that WF ORI is an accessible tool for monitoring changes in metabolism in PDCOs, capable of reproducibly assessing treatment response and heterogeneity in clinically relevant treatment studies.

Materials and Methods

Colorectal cancer PDCO isolation and organotypic culture techniques

All studies were completed following Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, with written informed consent obtained from subjects through the University of Wisconsin (UW) Molecular Tumor Board Registry (UW IRB #2014-1370) or UW Translational Science BioCore (UW IRB #2016-0934). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Colorectal cancer tissue was obtained from needle biopsies, endoscopic biopsies, or primary resections and processed as previously described (46, 47). After processing, PDCO suspensions were immediately mixed at a 1:1 ratio with Matrigel (Corning) and conditioned media. Droplet suspensions were plated and set for 3 to 5 minutes at 37°C and then inverted for at least 30 minutes to solidify the matrix and avoid the direct interface of PDCOs with the interface. Plated cultures were overlaid with 450 µL of media, DMEM/F-12 (Invitrogen) with added 1× GlutaMAX (Invitrogen), 10 mmol/L HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 50 IU/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen), and 50 ng/mL EGF (Invitrogen), mixed at a 1:1 ratio with WNT3a conditioned media (46). PDCOs were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2, and the medium was replaced every 48 to 72 hours.

Viability staining

PDCOs were collected from 24-well culture plates and passaged to single-well gridded 35 mm glass dishes (Cellvis). They were cultured for 120 hours prior to staining with calcein AM and Annexin V. Calcein AM was used at a working concentration of 5 μmol/L, and Annexin V Alexa Fluor 350 was diluted to a working concentration of 1:200 (v/v) in Annexin binding buffer and incubated with 500 μL of stain on the PDCOs for 2 hours, prior to washing once with Annexin binding buffer prior to imaging. Images were then acquired on a Nikon Ti-S inverted microscope using a 4× objective, as described below. Calcein AM was excited for 20 ms through a 480/30 nm filter at 100% power, and emissions were collected using a 505 nm dichroic mirror and a standard FITC (535/20 nm) filter. Annexin V Alexa Fluor 350 was excited for 1,000 ms through a 360/40 nm filter at 100% power, and emissions were collected using a 400 nm dichroic mirror and a standard DAPI (460/50 nm) filter.

Therapeutic studies

PDCOs were collected from 24-well culture plates and passaged to single-well gridded 35 mm glass dishes (Cellvis) or 24-well glass plates. They were allowed to recalibrate for 24 hours prior to image acquisition on a Nikon Ti-2E inverted microscope using a 4× objective prior to treatment, as described below. Pharmacologic agents were prepared at physiologic Cmax, and the duration of therapy was 48 hours for FOLFOX studies [10 μmol/L 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) + 5 μmol/L oxaliplatin; refs. 48, 49], followed by 48 hours in media prior to imaging, whereas panitumumab (1.6 μmol/L; ref. 50) PDCOs were treated for 48 hours and imaged at 48 hours. Pharmacologic agents were received from the UW Carbone Cancer Center Pharmacy, including 5-FU (Fresenius), oxaliplatin (Hospira), and panitumumab (Amgen). All therapeutic studies were performed with two to three independent technical replicates on one to two biological replicates; see Supplementary Table S1 for biological replicates per experiment.

Colorectal cancer mutation–specific PDCO mixing studies and predictions

Colorectal cancer PDCOs with known mutation profiles, wild-type (WT) or KRAS + PIK3CA double-mutant (DM) PDCOs, were cultured separately and then passaged via spiking, using a pipette tip to isolate a single PDCO (Supplementary Video S1), and plated into mixed population Matrigel droplets. The scientist culturing immediately collected and annotated bright-field (BF) images with the known mutation profile of the individual PDCOs in the image prior to collecting WF ORI for day 0. The image analysis scientist was not given the BF images and therefore was blinded to the mutation profile until after completing the image analysis and predictions.

WF ORI and leading-edge image analysis

WF ORI was performed using a Nikon Ti-S coupled to a SOLA light engine (300–650 nm, Lumencor). All images were collected with NIS-Elements software (RRID:SCR_014329; Nikon) using a 4× air, 0.13 NA objective (Plan Apo, Nikon) and a Hamamatsu Flash4 digital CMOS camera. NAD(P)H was excited for 3 seconds through a 360/40 nm filter at 40% power (0.25 mJ/cm2), and emissions were collected using a 400 nm dichroic mirror and a standard DAPI (460/50 nm) filter. FAD was sequentially excited for 3.5 seconds through a 480/30 nm filter at 40% power (0.531 mJ/cm2), and emissions were collected using a 505 nm dichroic mirror and a standard FITC (535/20 nm) filter. Five fields of view (FOV; image size = 3.34 mm × 3.34 mm; 2,048 × 2,048 pixels) were taken of each sample, with three replicates per sample condition; light doses remained below levels shown to keep PDCOs viable (36, 51). Images were acquired on day 0 prior to treatment and after treatment (day 4 for FOLFOX studies, day 2 for panitumumab studies) for all PDCOs, using the same settings.

NAD(P)H and FAD WF autofluorescence images were analyzed using Cell Profiler (RRID:SCR_007358; ref. 52). Due to the biological heterogeneity within PDCOs, which often contain necrotic cores, quiescent zones, and proliferating zones (Fig. 1A and B), especially in large PDCOs, a pipeline to limit ORI measurements to the proliferating zone of the PDCOs called “leading-edge analysis” was developed (Fig. 1C). PDCOs were segmented by hand using the NAD(P)H channel. The radius of the individual PDCO mask was then calculated by Cell Profiler, and 20 pixels (32 µm) were subtracted generating a core mask, which was subtracted from the original PDCO mask to create a leading-edge mask (Fig. 1C; Supplemental Analysis Pipeline). The ORR was then calculated for each leading-edge mask. We expect day-to-day variation in the WF system (e.g., minor drift, variation in exposure time) and recognize that illumination through the slightly autofluorescent Matrigel may affect intensity-based measurements. To account for these factors, we measured the average fluorescence intensity from five background (PDCO-free) locations within each image. Note that other definitions of the ORR have been used in prior articles (26, 53), and we choose this definition to be consistent with our previous work and so that the ORR decreases with effective treatments (33, 46). Treatment effects were measured by the change in the ORR (ΔORR), in which ΔORR = Normalized ORRorganoid_day4 – Normalized ORRaverage_day0. Here, “Normalized ORRorganoid_day4” is the background normalized ORR of individual PDCOs on day 4, and “Normalized ORRaverage_day0” is the background normalized average ORR of the same PDCO population measured on day 0 prior to treatment. For each condition, a minimum of 24 PDCOs were assessed with WF ORI; see Supplementary Table S1 for PDCO counts per condition and experiment. Unless otherwise noted, all ΔORR values are reported using the leading-edge technique.

2P imaging and analysis

NAD(P)H and FAD images were acquired on a 2P system by finding the bottom of a PDCO of interest and optically sectioning into the PDCO 20 to 40 µm to find a field of view with good cell nuclei and borders. Briefly, NAD(P)H and FAD were excited at 750 and 890 nm, respectively, using a tunable ultrafast pulsed laser (Coherent, Inc.), an inverted microscope (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ti), and a 40× water immersion (1.15 NA, Nikon) objective. Both NAD(P)H and FAD images were obtained for the same field of view. FAD fluorescence was isolated using an emission band-pass filter of 550/100 nm, whereas NAD(P)H fluorescence was isolated using an emission band-pass filter of 440/80 nm. Fluorescence lifetime images were collected using time-correlated single-photon counting electronics (SPC-150, Becker & Hickl) and a gallium arsenide phosphide (GaAsP) photomultiplier tube (H7422P-40, Hamamatsu). Images (512 × 512 pixels) were obtained using a pixel dwell time of 4.8 μs over a total integration time of 60 seconds, averaging 39 frames. A Fluoresbrite YG microsphere (Polysciences Inc.) was imaged as a daily standard for fluorescence lifetime. The instrument response function was measured using a second harmonic generation signal from urea crystals excited at 890 nm, with a full width at half-maximum of 240 ps. The lifetime decay curves were fit to a single exponential decay, and the fluorescence lifetime was measured to be 2.1 ns (n = 7), which is consistent with published values (9, 33).

Fluorescence intensity was computed for each image pixel using SPCImage (version 8.0, Becker & Hickl GmbH). To enhance the fluorescence counts in each decay, a 3 × 3 bin comprising nine neighboring pixels was employed (>1,000 photons/pixel; ref. 54). The background was thresholded, and the pixelwise decay curves were fit to a biexponential model convolved with the instrument response function, using an iterative parameter optimization to obtain the lowest sum of the squared differences between the model and data (weighted least squares algorithm). The decay curves were then integrated to generate the intensity of each fluorophore. Here, we only report the intensity values, and the lifetime parameters are not reported.

Whole-cell masks were generated using Cell Profiler, as previously described (55). Briefly, pixels belonging to nuclear regions were manually identified, and the resulting round objects were stored as a mask. Cells were identified by propagating out from each nucleus; an Otsu global threshold was used to improve the propagation and prevent it from continuing into background pixels. Cell cytoplasm was defined as the cell border minus the nuclei. All cells identified were counted for the 2P analysis as the images were acquired such that all cells imaged are part of the leading-edge area of the PDCO. Values for the intensity of NAD(P)H and FAD, as well as the ORR, were measured for each cell’s cytoplasm. For each variable, values for all cells within a treatment group were pooled together; 1,976 to 1,646 cells were analyzed per condition (see Supplementary Table S2 for cell counts per PDCO line per condition).

Statistical analysis

Differences in ΔORR between treatment and control and WT and mutant PDCOs were tested using a one-way ANOVA of control versus treatment (or WT vs. mutant), with a P value < 0.001 defined as significant. Due to the large sample sizes, almost all comparisons were found to be significant. Therefore, we also chose to calculate the effect size.

The absolute value of the treatment effect size was calculated using GΔ, which is defined here as follows:GΔ = |(μTx- μCtrl)σCtrl|,where μTx is the mean ΔORR of the treatment group, μCtrl is the mean ΔORR of the control group, and σCtrl is the SD of the ΔORR for the control group (56, 57). GΔ was used because comparisons of large sample sizes, such as those acquired here with single-cell and single PDCO measurements, almost always pass traditional significance tests unless the population effect size is near zero. Additionally, to make comparisons between effect sizes more robust, the 95% confidence interval of the GΔ effect size was calculated (58), using the method described in the book by G. Cumming and the provided workbook “ESCI-intro-Meta-Analysis-Sep-2-2016.xlsm” using the “d-two-groups” macro tab (59). Results are reported with the notation GΔ ± 95% confidence interval where appropriate.

Results

Leading-edge analysis improves sensitivity to redox change in treated PDCOs

PDCOs are known to have different biological zones that correspond to differing cell states primarily driven by nutrient access. The diffusion limit of most small molecules, such as oxygen and ATP, is around 100 to 150 µm into the organoid, which is similar to that in vascular tissue and tumor masses in vivo (60, 61). This means that the outermost edge of the PDCO, with the most exposure to nutrients, is dominated by proliferative cells with active metabolism. Cells that are exposed to high levels of metabolic waste (CO2 and lactate) and starvation due to a lack of nutrient diffusion exist in the necrotic zone, whereas the quiescent zone between the proliferative and necrotic zones contains live cells that are not highly metabolically active (Fig. 1A and B; refs. 44, 46, 61, 62). Therefore, ORR measurements were limited to the leading-edge of the PDCO, which contains only cells within the proliferating zone. This was achieved by segmenting the whole PDCO and then subtracting 20 pixels (32 µm) radially to generate a core mask. This core mask was then subtracted from the original PDCO mask to create a leading-edge mask of the outermost 32 µm of the PDCO (Fig. 1C). A 32 µm threshold was chosen to conservatively remove the quiescent zone and retain the most proliferative outer edge of each PDCO.

The ΔORR on day 4 after treatment compared with day 0 before treatment for control-treated versus FOLFOX-treated (10 μmol/L 5-FU + 5 μmol/L oxaliplatin) PDCOs is shown across two patient samples (Fig. 1D). There were statistically significant changes in all measured compartments, including the whole organoid, leading-edge, and core. However, the effect size of the leading-edge ΔORR was the largest (GΔ = 1.462 ± 0.23) compared with the whole organoid and core measurements (GΔ = 1.233 ± 0.22, GΔ = 0.982 ± 0.21, respectively). The lower GΔ in the core mask was likely the cause of the decreased GΔ in the whole organoid mask. Overall, these results indicate some benefit to isolating the ORR to the proliferating zone of the PDCOs imaged with WF ORI.

Assessment of leading-edge threshold

To validate the use of a 20-pixel, 32 µm leading-edge for future analysis, different leading-edge sizes ranging from 5 pixels to 100 pixels (8–160 µm) were characterized. Different mask widths were overlaid onto a representative day 4 FOLFOX-treated WF ORR image, which shows that increases in mask width capture more heterogeneous regions within the PDCOs (Fig. 2A). Quantifying the average ΔORR across various leading-edge widths further characterizes this heterogeneity, as the SDs increase from the 5 to 50 pixel mask size before leveling out (Fig. 2B). The GΔ effect size between control and FOLFOX conditions does not substantially increase after 30 pixels. Additionally, the leading-edge width of 20 pixels is the smallest mask width that meets a GΔ effect size threshold for intermediate response (GΔ = 0.5; ref. 46). Furthermore, a line plot profile of the WF ORR over the diameter of two distinct PDCOs shows stable ORR from 0 to 20 pixels, and thereafter, the ORR dips and demonstrates heterogeneity that suggests cell death (Fig. 2C and D). This is supported by a line plot profile of a PDCO stained with calcein AM (live cells) and Annexin V (dead cells; Fig. 2E and F), which shows that the calcein AM signal peaks in the leading-edge before the 20-pixel point, whereas the Annexin V stain continues to increase and peaks near the 70-pixel point, which corresponds to a depth of approximately 112 µm. Overall, the 20 pixels (32 µm) leading-edge was chosen to balance the needs to (i) minimize inclusion of dead cells while (ii) maximizing differences between treated and control conditions.

WF ORI resolves treatment effects across all PDCOs better than 2P ORI

Using gridded dishes, identical PDCOs were imaged on days 0 and 4 under control and FOLFOX treatment conditions for two patients, using both 2P and WF ORI. Representative day 4 NAD(P)H autofluorescence images of control and treated PDCOs show the difference in scale and PDCO detail that can be achieved with the two imaging modalities (Fig. 3A and B; Supplementary Fig. S1). 2P images were acquired such that all imaged cells are likely to fall within the 32 µm area considered the leading-edge by WF imaging (see Materials and Methods). Boxed PDCOs in the WF panels correspond with the representative 2P images shown. Tracking the same PDCO over time with treatment using both WF and 2P ORI indicates that both modalities show an overall decrease in the ORR of the PDCOs with FOLFOX treatment (Fig. 3C and D), a change that has been previously correlated with treatment response (33, 37, 46).

By correlating the ΔORR for both 2P and WF ORI of identical PDCOs (n = 16) over 4 days (Fig. 3E), we see a moderate correlation (R2 = 0.68), which is expected given the differences in resolution and optical sectioning between WF and 2P ORI. Importantly, WF ORI can rapidly image all PDCOs in a dish (40+ PDCO/dish/30 seconds), providing greater power to resolve treatment effects compared with the much slower 2P ORI (3–5 PDCO/dish/30 minutes). Specifically, WF ORI of individual PDCOs provided a ∼3× increase in effect size (GΔ = 1.462 ± 0.23) compared with single-cell 2P ORI (GΔ = 0.5402 ± 0.65) for FOLFOX treatment of the same samples (Fig. 3F). This improvement in GΔ is likely due to increased sampling of PDCOs with WF ORI and the greater representation of the cross-section of cells within each PDCO with the WF leading-edge. 2P ORI only captures the cells on the bottom leading-edge, whereas WF ORI includes the entire edge of the PDCO and overall captures signals from more total cells. This is significant because WF ORI can be performed with hardware present in most research and clinical labs, at a lower cost and higher throughput compared with 2P ORI, with increased sensitivity to treatment.

WF ORI distinguishes metabolic differences based on driver mutations in colorectal cancer PDCOs

Prior work has shown that ORI is sensitive to driver mutations in breast cancer cell lines (25, 63). To investigate whether colorectal cancer PDCOs with known driver mutations exhibit differing baseline metabolic characteristics, we used WF ORI to image PDCOs across multiple mutation profiles, including WT (four patients), KRAS (two patients), PIK3CA (two patients), or both KRAS and PIK3CA (two patients) mutants (Fig. 4A and B; Supplementary Fig. S2). Remarkably, these pretreatment ORR measurements showed differences between WT and PIK3CA-mutant PDCOs (GΔ = 0.546 ± 0.24) and KRAS + PIK3CA double-mutant (DM) PDCOs (GΔ = 1.24 ± 0.28). Differences are also apparent when comparing the normalized NAD(P)H and FAD intensities between WT and DM PDCOs (NAD(P)H: GΔ = 0.816 ± 0.27, FAD: GΔ = 0.519 ± 0.26; Supplementary Fig. S2). The ORR differences between WT and DM PDCOs were sufficient to identify the two populations in mixed culture (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Video S1). A random forest classifier (64, 65) trained on the ORR, NAD(P)H intensity, and FAD intensity from WT and DM PDCOs in pure culture and tested on dishes with a mixed population of WT and DM PDCOs achieved high sensitivity and specificity with an AUC of 0.87 when predicting WT versus DM PDCOs (Fig. 4D–F). The weights of the variables indicate that FAD intensity is the most important variable for making this distinction, which is confirmed by ROCs of individual WF ORI variables (Supplementary Fig. S3). The best single-variable ROC is achieved by using FAD intensity alone (AUC = 0.71), and the combination of either other variable with FAD intensity achieves a reasonable AUC of 0.78 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Overall, this indicates that in some cases, WF ORI can identify driver mutations before treatment and could be used as a tool to screen patients for resistant cell populations, especially in the case of therapies that target specific mutations.

WF ORI is more sensitive to panitumumab-resistant PDCOs in mixed population dishes compared with PDCO diameter

In colorectal cancer, K/NRAS mutations lead to clinical therapeutic resistance to EGFR monoclonal antibodies such as panitumumab (66). Here, we test whether WF ORI can identify treatment-sensitive WT versus treatment-resistant KRAS + PIK3CA (DM) PDCOs in mixed dishes (Fig. 5A). Dishes with both WT and DM PDCOs were treated with a physiologic Cmax of panitumumab (50) for 2 days, and the same PDCOs were tracked over time with treatment using both manual PDCO diameter measurements, as described in previous publications by our group (46, 67, 47), and WF ORI (Fig. 5A). Results show that there is an overall decrease in the diameter of the WT PDCOs with 2 days of panitumumab treatment that is not apparent in the DM PDCOs (Fig. 5B and C). Here, the effect size for the diameter change over 2 days of treatment for WT compared with DM PDCOs in the same dish is GΔ = 0.92 ± 0.52. This decrease in WT PDCO diameter has been previously correlated with treatment response (46, 47, 66).

Similarly, WF ORR shows a decrease in the ΔORR of the WT PDCOs with 2 days of panitumumab treatment that is not apparent in the DM PDCOs (Fig. 5D and E). A decrease in ORR has been previously correlated with treatment response (22, 37, 46). However, the effect size between WT and DM PDCOs in the same dish is greater for the ΔORR (GΔ = 1.39 ± 0.51) compared with the change in diameter (GΔ = 0.92 ± 0.52). This demonstrates that WF ORI may be able to rapidly assess PDCOs for mutation profiles that are resistant to therapy in mixed PDCO populations. These WF ORR drug response screens could be used to improve treatment selection for individual patients.

Discussion

Functional drug screenings using PDCOs reflect the diversity and drug sensitivity of tumors in vivo, making them valuable tools for predicting the most effective treatments and identifying new drug candidates in development (5, 6, 10, 68). However, current methods to evaluate drug response in PDCOs face several challenges: they can be destructive, overlook inter-PDCO heterogeneity, offer limited response metrics, or lack scalability. Common cell viability assays, such as tetrazolium and luciferase tests, assess metabolic function as a proxy for viable cell count but may introduce long-term toxicity, limit future use of precious samples, or fail to account for intratumoral heterogeneity (69–71). Histologic techniques yield detailed structural and molecular information but involve slow, destructive sample processing (72). Traditional molecular biology methods (such as RNA sequencing, qPCR, and Western blots) capture treatment-induced changes in protein and RNA levels but often require sample pooling to obtain sufficient material (72). Although measuring PDCO diameter from BF images allows for monitoring the same PDCOs over time, this method provides only a single-response metric (change in diameter) and can be subject to user bias due to manual execution (46, 73). Additionally, diameter changes are often preceded by more rapid metabolic responses (37).

Performing ORI in a WF configuration is well suited for high-throughput applications in PDCO screening because of its simple, widely available instrumentation, large field of view, micron-scale resolution, and fast acquisition. Although WF ORI of PDCOs with a 4× objective does not provide a readout at the single-cell level, unlike 2P ORI, due to the nature of WF illumination (lack of optical sectioning) with multiple cell layers comprising the organoids, there is autofluorescence emitted from out of plane that obscures the signal from single cells. Furthermore, we chose to use lower magnification (4×) to capture as many organoids as possible per patient, which decreases single-cell resolution. However, our results indicate that the PDCO-level readout may better represent treatment response as WF ORI of individual PDCOs provided a ∼3× increase in effect size compared with single-cell 2P ORI analysis for FOLFOX treatment of the same samples (Fig. 3F). This increase in effect size with WF ORI is likely due to the higher volume of cells interrogated with WF illumination compared with a single z-slice with 2P ORI. 2P z-stacks can be used to address this issue, with the caveat of lower throughput. WF ORI can screen >40 PDCOs/dish/30 seconds, providing greater power to resolve treatment effects compared with the lower-throughput 2P ORI that screens 3 to 5 PDCOs/dish/30 minutes. Crucially, for future dissemination of ORI measurements, WF ORI can be performed with hardware present in most research and clinical labs, at lower cost and higher throughput compared with 2P ORI.

Furthermore, the isolation of ORR measurements to the leading-edge of the PDCOs, which corresponds to the proliferative cells with active metabolism, increases the sensitivity of ORR treatment response measurements (Fig. 1D). This is likely due to the removal of nonviable cells from the ORR measurement as most chemotherapies target rapidly dividing cells (19, 61, 62). The components of FOLFOX, 5-FU and oxaliplatin (48, 49), both induce DNA damage, a common method for disrupting the growth of rapidly dividing cancer cells. Similarly, panitumumab targets the EGF receptor (74), which blocks a key pathway for cell growth. Though cells that are present in the core of the PDCOs will also be affected by treatment, the effect is diminished by the lack of rapid growth occurring in the internal PDCO niche as the cells are exposed to higher levels of metabolic waste (CO2 and lactate) and starvation due to a lack of nutrient diffusion (61). Additionally, the leading-edge ΔORR enhances differences in treatment response between responsive and resistant PDCOs within the same dish compared with more standard measurements of diameter change taken on the same PDCOs (Fig. 5). This resolution of single PDCOs is crucial as colorectal cancer samples are mutationally diverse and bulk sequencing does not capture rare alterations that often lead to tumor resistance (10, 66). Therefore, a tool that can quickly assess many PDCOs for driver mutations within days of PDCO culture (Fig. 4) and assess the efficacy of targeted therapies would be of great clinical use.

This study establishes WF ORI as a robust tool to assess multiple label-free quantitative variables at the single PDCO level. This methodology facilitates the evaluation of treatment response and the identification of PDCO subpopulations according to distinct mutational phenotypes. An important outcome of this technical advancement is the development of a standardized framework for assessing redox images, acquired on a conventional epifluorescence microscope, using background-normalized leading-edge analysis over multiple time points with open-source software. Although this proof-of-concept investigation was limited to a small cohort of patient samples with defined mutational profiles, additional studies are needed to confirm that these specific observations apply across a broader spectrum of mutation profiles and additional patients. However, these methods can be adopted by nonexperts to achieve reproducible label-free images and perform quantitative analyses of treatment response in PDCOs using widely available tools.

This study outlines a scalable framework for implementing WF ORI of PDCOs with the addition of leading-edge analysis that increases sensitivity to treatment response. WF ORI enables the acquisition of functional metabolic measurements that can be used to characterize PDCO heterogeneity. Furthermore, this technique can detect differences in both the baseline metabolism of PDCOs with altered driver mutations and differing responses to treatments with known mutational resistance. These findings support the broader application of WF ORI as a screening tool for PDCOs, with potential utility in preclinical drug development and individualized treatment planning.
