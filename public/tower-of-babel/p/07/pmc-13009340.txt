Macrophage polarization in inflammatory regulation: molecular mechanisms, therapeutic targets, and translational challenges
Authors: Wenjie He, Jingwen Xu, Xinyun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Macrophage polarization plays a vital role in regulating inflammation, and the balance of this process is crucial for maintaining tissue health and influencing disease progression. Recent studies have shown how macrophages can adapt their phenotypes in response to their surroundings, underscoring the importance of their polarization changes in various inflammatory conditions, such as infections, tumors, metabolic disorders, and autoimmune diseases. This review brings together significant advancements in our understanding of the signaling pathways involved in inflammation, the role of epigenetic factors, metabolic changes, and the development of targeted therapies, with the goal of offering new perspectives on treating inflammation-related diseases.

Introduction

Macrophages are essential components of the immune system, playing a significant role in regulating inflammation and immune responses. Their remarkable ability to adapt allows them to change their functions based on the surrounding microenvironment. This flexibility is best illustrated by a process known as macrophage polarization, where macrophages can transform into different types, mainly M1 and M2. M1 macrophages are typically involved in pro-inflammatory responses and are crucial for protecting the body against infections, while M2 macrophages are associated with anti-inflammatory responses and help in tissue repair. Maintaining a proper balance between these two types is important for keeping the body in a state of homeostasis and effectively managing inflammation. When this balance is disrupted, it can lead to various health issues, including chronic inflammatory diseases, metabolic disorders, and cancer [1, 2].

Recent studies have highlighted the multifaceted roles of macrophages, revealing that their functions extend far beyond traditional host defense mechanisms. For instance, macrophages are now understood to play crucial roles in the development of various diseases, including obesity, diabetes, and cancer. In the case of obesity, the buildup of M1 macrophages in adiposetissue contributes to chronic inflammation and insulin resistance, illustrating the connection between macrophage polarization and metabolic.

health [3]. Similarly, in cancer, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) can shift to a pro-tumor M2 phenotype, which not only supports tumor growth and spread but also hampers effective anti-tumor immune responses [4]. These insights highlight the pressing need to explore macrophage polarization as a potential therapeutic target in diverse clinical scenarios.

The regulation of macrophage polarization is a complex process that involves various signaling pathways and environmental cues. Key factors such as cytokines, growth factors, and metabolic signals play a vital role in guiding macrophage differentiation and determining their functional outcomes. For example, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) is known to promote M1 polarization, which is associated with pro-inflammatory responses, while interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) are instrumental in driving M2 polarization, which is linked to anti-inflammatory and tissue repair functions [5, 6]. Moreover, the metabolic state of macrophages significantly influences their polarization and functional capabilities, suggesting that metabolic reprogramming could be a promising strategy for developing new therapeutic approaches aimed at modulating macrophage behavior in various diseases [7].

Given the critical role of macrophage polarization in both health and disease, there is increasing interest in developing therapeutic strategies to modulate macrophage functions. Interventions aimed at shifting the balance from M1 to M2 polarization may provide new avenues for treating chronic inflammatory conditions and enhancing tissue repair [8, 9]. For instance, researchers are currently investigating pharmacological agents that target specific signaling pathways related to macrophage polarization, such as the NF-κB and STAT6 pathways, as potential treatments for diseases marked by chronic inflammation. Additionally, the application of biomaterials and nanotechnology for the targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to macrophages represents an innovative approach to modulate their polarization and improve treatment efficacy. The dynamic nature of macrophage polarization and its relevance to various diseases underscore the necessity for further research into the underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic strategies [10, 11]. By elucidating the factors that influence macrophage polarization, scientists can devise new methods to manipulate macrophage activities, ultimately enhancing clinical outcomes for a range of inflammatory and metabolic disorders. The study of macrophage polarization not only deepens our understanding of immune system regulation but also uncovers novel strategies for therapeutic intervention, positioning macrophages as vital targets in the pursuit of effective disease management.

Molecular mechanisms underpinning macrophage polarization​​

Canonical signaling pathways​

The polarization of macrophages is a crucial aspect of the immune response, significantly influencing inflammation and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis. Central to this process are key signaling pathways, particularly the JAK/STAT pathway, which plays a vital role in directing macrophage polarization (Fig. 1). The activation of STAT1 and STAT3 is essential for the differentiation of macrophages into M1 and M2 phenotypes, respectively. STAT1 is primarily associated with the pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage phenotype, which is activated by pathogens and inflammatory signals, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. In contrast, the activation of STAT3 promotes the M2 phenotype, characterized by its anti-inflammatory properties and involvement in tissue repair, often linked to the production of IL-10 and TGF-β. An imbalance in these signaling pathways can lead to pathological conditions, including chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases. Recent studies have highlighted the intricate relationship between these signaling pathways and their impact on macrophage function, suggesting that therapeutic strategies targeting the JAK/STAT pathway may effectively modulate macrophage polarization and improve treatment outcomes in inflammatory diseases [12, 13].

Epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulation​

During macrophage polarization, epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms form a complex multi-level network. Within this network, histone modifications (including acetylation and methylation) and DNA methylation dynamically regulate gene expression by altering chromatin accessibility [14]. Chromatin remodeling factors such as histone deacetylases (HDACs) and bromodomain protein 4 (BRD4) play central roles in this process [15]. Through epigenetic reprogramming, these factors enable macrophages to rapidly respond to microenvironmental stimuli without altering DNA sequences. Different polarized macrophages exhibit distinct dynamic features of NF-κB signaling and variations in chromatin openness. Although the specific mechanisms of long non-coding RNAs (e.g., MALAT1) in macrophage polarization remain incompletely elucidated, single-cell mRNA analysis provides a methodological foundation for studying how lncRNAs regulate chromatin states. At the post-transcriptional level, microRNAs achieve precise regulation of macrophage polarization by targeting key nodes in signaling pathways. For instance, miR-155 promotes M1 polarization by inhibiting negative regulators like SOCS1, while miR-146a drives M2 polarization through targeting TRAF6/IRAK1 [16, 17]. These regulatory mechanisms interact with epigenetic modifications, such as histone modifications regulating miRNA promoter activity and miRNA feedback modulating epigenomerase expression. In diseases such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (M2-type hyperpolarization) and tumor immune suppression (extracellular PKM2-mediated M2-type polarization), the imbalance of these regulatory networks plays a critical role [18]. Targeting these mechanisms through strategies like HDAC inhibitors to regulate epigenetic states and engineered vesicles to reprogram membrane protein expression can reverse pathological polarization states. These approaches have opened new research avenues for treating age-related macular degeneration and advancing tumor immunotherapy [19].

Metabolic features​

Metabolic reprogramming is a key aspect of how macrophages undergo polarization. M1 macrophages primarily use glycolysis for their energy needs, while M2 macrophages rely more on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation to carry out their immune functions [20]. The autophagy process plays a crucial role in maintaining the balance between the supply of metabolic substrates and the polarization state by removing damaged organelles, such as through mitochondrial autophagy, and degrading proteins [21]. In an inflammatory environment, M1 polarization is marked by a temporary increase in autophagy to fulfill the energy requirements for producing pro-inflammatory factors. However, if autophagy remains activated for too long, it can lead to further tissue damage. For example, β-glucan has been shown to inhibit autophagy while activating the Dectin-1/NF-κB signaling pathway, which promotes M1 polarization and can worsen conditions like atherosclerosis [22]. On the other hand, sialic acid interferes with autophagosome formation and increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby intensifying M1-related inflammatory responses [23]. In contrast, during M2 polarization, autophagy aids in lipid metabolism and helps resolve inflammation. Notably, glycosylated nano hydroxyapatite (GHANPs) has been found to significantly promote M2 polarization, as evidenced by increased expression of Arg-1, by stimulating mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and autophagy, which in turn accelerates the regeneration of bone tissue [20].

Intervention strategies targeting autophagy and metabolic pathways should be designed according to disease progression stages. Autophagy-promoting approaches can induce M2-type anti-inflammatory/regenerative responses: Kaempferol activates autophagy to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasomes, block M1-type polarization, and reduce corneal transplant rejection; PPARγ agonists (e.g., Bifenkangxie Tablets) suppress PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathways, enhance autophagy levels, promote M2-type polarization, and inhibit atherosclerosis development [24]. Autophagy-inhibiting strategies enhance M1-type anti-tumor/anti-infective responses: Chloroquine increases ROS-mediated MIF secretion by suppressing autophagy, driving macrophages toward M1 polarization to boost antitumor immunity. Meanwhile, mTOR activator MHY1485 promotes M1 polarization through autophagy blockade, thereby strengthening the immune response against HSV-1 virus [23]. Precision timing intervention strategies demonstrate that using NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 during the early inflammatory phase can reverse PD-1 inhibitor-induced M1-polarized myocardial injury [25]. In the chronic phase, rapamycin activates autophagy to counteract free fatty acid (FFA)-induced mTOR-autophagy axis inhibition, thereby correcting ketosis-associated M1-polarization in dairy cows [22]. In conclusion, the strategy targeting autophagy requires spatiotemporal specificity: promoting autophagy is effective for tissue regeneration (e.g., bone defects) and resolution of chronic inflammation (e.g., atherosclerosis), while inhibiting autophagy facilitates anti-tumor or acute infection treatments. Key nodes such as mTOR (metabolic-autophagy hub), NLRP3 (inflammatory body), and PPARγ (polarization switch) serve as core targets for optimizing therapeutic efficacy (Table 1).

Microenvironmental determinants of macrophage polarization​​

Tissue-specific niches​

The polarization of macrophages is influenced not only by cytokines but also by various non-cytokine factors present in the tissue microenvironment. In the context of tumor microenvironments, biomaterials like glycosylated nano hydroxyapatite can activate mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and autophagy by targeting mannose receptors (MR) on the surfaces of macrophages, which drives M2 polarization and subsequently promotes tumor progression [26]. In obesity-related adipose tissue microenvironments, free fatty acids (FFAs) play a significant role in promoting M1 polarization of macrophages and enhancing the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors by inhibiting mTOR-mediated autophagy pathways [27]. Furthermore, exposure to environmental toxins such as arsenic can induce mitochondrial autophagy, which facilitates the transformation of macrophages to the M2 phenotype, driving processes like epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tumor metastasis [28]. In atherosclerotic lesions, the metabolite sialic acid promotes M1 polarization and accelerates plaque formation by inhibiting the LKB1-AMPK-Sirt3 signaling pathway, which leads to increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and blocks the autophagy-lysosome system [29].

Regulation by pathogens and damage signals

The presence of pathogens and the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) play a significant role in influencing how macrophages become polarized. Both pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and DAMPs act as signals that encourage macrophages to shift towards the M1 phenotype, which is essential for combating infections and clearing away damaged tissues. For example, when an infection occurs, macrophages are triggered by PAMPs, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) found in the walls of bacteria. This activation leads to M1 polarization, characterized by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [21]. This polarization is essential for initiating an effective immune response. Furthermore, signals that indicate cellular damage, which are released from injured tissues, can also promote M1 polarization [30]. The interaction among these signals is intricate; while the presence of pathogens promotes M1 polarization, the resolution of inflammation and the healing of tissues frequently necessitate a shift towards M2 polarization [31]. This shift is influenced by various factors, such as the availability of nutrients and the presence of specific cytokines in the surrounding environment. For example, interleukin-10 (IL-10) plays a crucial role in promoting M2 polarization, which is vital for the repair of tissues following an inflammatory response [32]. Understanding these regulatory mechanisms is essential for developing therapeutic strategies that target macrophage polarization across various disease contexts, such as infections and chronic inflammatory conditions [24–26].

Targeting macrophage polarization intervention strategies

Small molecule inhibitors and metabolic regulators

Recent advancements in the development of small molecule inhibitors have shown promise as an effective strategy for regulating macrophage polarization, potentially leading to improved clinical outcomes in a range of diseases [33]. Macrophages exhibit significant plasticity, enabling them to shift between various functional states, primarily categorized as pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes. The polarization of macrophages is influenced by a range of factors, including cytokines, metabolic signals, and environmental stimuli. Small molecule inhibitors that target specific signaling pathways related to macrophage polarization have shown promise in altering the balance between these two phenotypes. Studies have indicated that 5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamid ribonucleotide (AICAR), a well-known activator of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), induces macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype by activating the AMPKα1 subunit [34]. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated models, AICAR treatment markedly increases the expression of M2 markers such as Arg1 and IL-10, while suppressing M1-associated factors like TNF-α and IL-6. Mechanistic investigations reveal that AICAR promotes AMPK-mediated fatty acid oxidation metabolism reprogramming and enhances mitochondrial function, thereby facilitating the M2 polarization process. Furthermore, synergistic effects are observed when AICAR is combined with Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors: In atherosclerosis models, AICAR, in combination with Tofacitinib, reduces foam cell formation by enhancing ABCA1-mediated reverse cholesterol transport [35]. Different JAK inhibitors exert varying polarization regulatory effects. Tofacitinib inhibits M2 differentiation and promotes the M1 phenotype in vitro, yet it increases the M2 proportion in apoE-/- mice by suppressing inflammatory factors and upregulating ABCA1. Methotrexate enhances IL-10 secretion through adenosine-A2 receptor pathways to induce M2 polarization. These findings underscore the central role of the JAK-STAT pathway in macrophage polarization, with its effects displaying inhibitor specificity and context-dependent characteristics. Immunological research has shown that the combination of histone deacetylase inhibitors and AMPK activators (e.g., AICAR) can enhance M2 polarization through epigenetic regulation, although this mechanism necessitates further preclinical validation [33]. In summary, small-molecule inhibitors that target macrophage polarization, including JAK inhibitors and AMPK activators, offer multidimensional therapeutic strategies for inflammatory diseases, metabolic disorders, and cancer treatment (Table 2).

Nanodelivery systems and gene editing

The advent of nanodelivery systems has markedly improved the precision of therapies aimed at macrophages, especially in the context of gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR [36]. These systems facilitate the efficient introduction of genetic material into macrophages, allowing for controlled modulation of their polarization states [37]. By engineering nanoparticles to specifically bind to macrophage surface receptors, researchers can ensure targeted delivery of CRISPR components to edit critical genes involved in polarization [38]. For example, lipid-assisted polymer nanoparticles (CLAN) have been used to deliver the CRISPR/Cas9 system, resulting in the specific knockout of the Ntn1 gene in macrophages and subsequent improvement in type 2 diabetes symptoms [36]. Another study employed mannose-modified neutrophil membrane vesicles to transport CRISPR/Cas9 RNP to target the NLRP3 gene, significantly reducing macrophage inflammatory responses and alleviating acute hepatitis [37]. Arginine nanoparticles (ArgNPs) were also utilized to deliver CRISPR components for the knockout of the SIRP-α gene, thereby enhancing macrophages’ phagocytic activity against tumor cells. Moreover, core-shell structured lipid nanoparticles (CSNs) have been used to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 systems targeting HK2 genes via inhalation, effectively managing macrophage glucose metabolism and decreasing inflammatory responses in acute lung injury [38].

Despite the promising therapeutic potential these strategies exhibit, their clinical application is beset with numerous safety and technical challenges. The CRISPR/Cas9 system may have off-target effects, potentially causing unintended gene mutations. There is also a need for thorough assessments of the biocompatibility, immunogenicity, and long-term toxicity of nanocarriers. While some studies have attempted to achieve macrophage-specific Cas9 expression using promoters like CD68, both targeting efficiency and cellular selectivity require further refinement. Additionally, in vivo delivery efficiency, particularly to macrophages in deep tissues, remains a significant hurdle. Consequently, future research should concentrate on refining vector design, improving gene editing precision, and conducting comprehensive validations of safety and efficacy across a broader range of preclinical models.

Dynamic regulation of the macrophage polarization spectrum and targeted therapeutic strategies

Macrophage polarization is a highly dynamic and plastic process that involves a continuum of functional states, rather than a simple binary classification [39]. The classic activated M1 type (pro-inflammatory) and the alternatively activated M2 type (anti-inflammatory) represent two extremes of the polarization spectrum. The M2 type can be further subdivided into subtypes such as M2a (induced by IL-4 or IL-13), M2b (induced by immune complexes with LPS), M2c (induced by IL-10 or glucocorticoids), and M2d (induced by adenosine or IL-6) [40]. These subtypes exhibit distinct functional characteristics under specific microenvironmental signals, such as cytokines, microbial products, or tissue injury factors, with some subtypes (e.g., M2b) displaying mixed phenotypes that can be considered intermediate polarization states. Dynamic transitions between polarization states are precisely regulated through epigenetic controls, cellular metabolic reprogramming (e.g., M1-dependent glycolysis and M2-dependent oxidative phosphorylation), and signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB and PPARγ) [41]. In disease treatment, targeting macrophage polarization has emerged as a pivotal strategy. For instance, in atherosclerosis, pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages exacerbate plaque instability, whereas IL-10 or glucocorticoids can induce M2c polarization to promote inflammation resolution and tissue repair. In tumor microenvironments, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) often exhibit M2-like phenotypes. Nanocarriers delivering IFN-γ or blocking IL-10 signaling can reprogram them into M1-like states, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses. Additionally, in obstetric pathologies such as placental implantation, M2-like macrophages promote abnormal trophoblast invasion through G-CSF secretion. Interventions targeting the G-CSF pathway can modulate polarization states and improve pathological progression [42]. These therapies achieve precise intervention in intermediate polarization types by regulating polarization-related signaling pathways, metabolic pathways, and microenvironmental factors, offering new therapeutic directions for inflammatory diseases, cancers, and cardiovascular disorders.

Natural products and traditional Chinese medicine components

Natural products and constituents derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) have garnered significant attention for their potential effects on macrophage polarization. Various phytochemicals have been identified as modulators of macrophage function, capable of promoting either M1 or M2 polarization depending on their chemical properties and mechanisms of action. For instance, compounds like curcumin and resveratrol exhibit anti-inflammatory effects by promoting M2 polarization while concurrently inhibiting M1 activation. This modulation occurs through the alteration of essential signaling pathways, such as NF-κB and STAT3, highlighting the intricate relationship between these natural substances and immune response regulation [43]. Furthermore, TCM preparations frequently comprise a complex blend of bioactive compounds that can synergistically target multiple pathways involved in macrophage polarization, thus offering a comprehensive therapeutic approach [44]. The integration of natural products into clinical practice offers an innovative approach to modulating macrophages and underscores the importance of understanding the complex nature of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in addressing multifaceted diseases. As research advances in elucidating the specific mechanisms by which these natural compounds influence macrophage polarization, there is significant potential for their application in creating new therapeutic agents designed to restore immune balance in various pathological conditions.

Challenges and future directions

Development of dynamic monitoring technologies

Technological advancements in the dynamic monitoring of macrophage polarization are still in their early stages, presenting significant challenges in the fields of immunology and therapeutic strategies. Accurately assessing macrophage polarization in real-time is crucial for understanding their roles in various pathological conditions, including inflammation, cancer development, and autoimmune diseases. Current methods mainly rely on static evaluations, which do not capture the dynamic responses of macrophages to their surroundings. This limitation hinders timely therapeutic interventions and the development of targeted treatment strategies. Future research should focus on improving the sensitivity and specificity of dynamic monitoring techniques, such as advanced imaging methods and biosensors that can provide real-time information about the state of macrophage polarization [45, 46]. Advancements in nanotechnology and microfluidics are paving the way for the development of portable devices designed for in vivo monitoring of macrophage activity. Additionally, integrating artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms to analyze the intricate datasets generated by these monitoring systems could enhance our comprehension of macrophage behavior in different biological contexts. Ultimately, the progress of dynamic monitoring technologies will play a vital role in connecting fundamental research findings with clinical applications, thereby improving disease management in situations where macrophage polarization is a significant element [47, 48].

Multi-Target combination interventions

The future of therapeutic strategies designed to tackle drug resistance and enhance treatment effectiveness lies in the development of multi-target combination therapies (Fig. 2). Traditional single-target approaches have often fallen short in addressing complex diseases, particularly those characterized by multiple underlying factors, such as cancer, metabolic disorders, and chronic inflammatory conditions. Research indicates that targeting several pathways at once can bypass the compensatory mechanisms that frequently make monotherapy ineffective. For instance, in cancer treatment, using agents that simultaneously target different aspects of tumor biology—such as cell growth, programmed cell death, and immune evasion—has shown considerable potential in both preclinical and clinical studies [49]. This methodology enhances the effectiveness of therapies while reducing the likelihood of resistance developing. Future research should aim to identify synergistic combinations of existing medications and explore new compounds that could be integrated into combination treatment protocols. Additionally, employing systems biology approaches to model the interactions between various therapeutic agents and their targets may provide important insights into the best combination strategies [50]. Personalized medicine strategies, which consider the unique characteristics of individual patients, are anticipated to play a crucial role in the effective implementation of multi-target interventions. Clinical translation bottlenecks

The field of translational medicine faces significant challenges that hinder the effective transition of research findings into clinical practice. One major concern is the lack of standardized protocols for evaluating the safety and efficacy of new therapies, particularly those that involve nanotechnology and elements from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). It is crucial to thoroughly assess the safety profiles of nanomaterials used in drug delivery systems to prevent any potential adverse effects on patients [51]. The complex nature of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) formulations, which often include a variety of active ingredients, makes it challenging to establish consistent quality control protocols and evaluate their therapeutic effectiveness. Standardizing TCM components is essential for ensuring that clinical outcomes are reproducible and reliable. Furthermore, regulatory frameworks need to evolve to address the unique characteristics of these therapies, which will help in their approval and integration into mainstream healthcare practices [52]. Collaborative efforts among researchers, healthcare professionals, and regulatory authorities are crucial for addressing these challenges. By developing clear guidelines for the clinical evaluation of innovative therapies, we can speed up the process of moving from laboratory research to clinical practice. This transition not only improves patient outcomes but also drives progress in the medical field.

The traps and timing of targeted polarization therapy

Targeted polarization therapy, which includes imaging or interventions utilizing the polar properties of cells or molecules, shows promise for disease management but also presents several pitfalls and hazards. For example, the improper design of carbon dots or probes used in polar targeting imaging can interfere with cellular physiological activities, such as altering mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), thereby inducing abnormal cellular behaviors like autophagy [53]. This underscores the importance of avoiding nonspecific interactions. Furthermore, the application of macrophage polarization-targeted therapy in atherosclerosis is challenging due to insufficient research, which may result in a lack of specificity, off-target effects, or unknown immune responses [54]. Although Proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) technology can degrade target proteins, off-target risks still require further evaluation. In terms of safety, while some nanomaterials, such as dual-emission carbon dots, demonstrate good biocompatibility in short-term use, long-term or large-scale applications may accumulate toxicity, causing organ damage or misdiagnosis risks—especially when polar-sensitive probes are used for detecting diabetic complications [55]. Regarding administration timing, these therapies should be initiated at critical stages of disease progression: intervention during macrophage polarization imbalance (M1/M2 ratio abnormality) in atherosclerosis progression, early treatment after detecting organ damage through lysosomal polarity changes in early diabetes stages, and implementation of targeted photothermal therapy when tumor microenvironment polarity abnormalities (such as hypoxia) occur [54]. Overall, while targeted polarization therapy holds promise, strategies must be optimized to minimize risks and undergo thorough preclinical validation.

Conclusion

Epigenetic alterations, metabolic changes, and microenvironmental elements impact macrophage function. Understanding these interactions is crucial for deciphering inflammatory diseases and formulating new therapies.

From an expert perspective, studying macrophage polarization is not just an academic pursuit; it has significant clinical implications. When exploring macrophage activation and polarization mechanisms, diverse research perspectives are important. For example, genetic and epigenetic research on macrophage roles shows how the environment can cause lasting changes in macrophage activity, while research on metabolic reprogramming in macrophages explains how energy production and nutrient availability affect inflammation.

Integrating these diverse perspectives is essential for a comprehensive understanding of macrophage biology. Focusing on only one aspect may overlook the dynamic and interconnected nature of these processes. Future research should use multi - omics technologies to evaluate genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic changes in macrophages simultaneously. This approach will deepen our understanding of macrophage polarization and how different regulatory layers interact in inflammatory diseases.

Formulating precise intervention strategies for macrophage polarization holds promise for treating inflammatory disorders. Current therapies often fail to address macrophage heterogeneity in different tissues. Multi - omics research can help identify specific molecular targets for more customized treatments. For example, pharmacological agents can be developed to steer macrophage polarization towards an anti - inflammatory phenotype in chronic inflammation.

The microenvironment where macrophages reside influences their function.
