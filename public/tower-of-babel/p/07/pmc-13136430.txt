Geospatial assessment of activity concentrations of natural radionuclides and absorbed dose rates in gidan-kwano, minna, nigeria using airborne radiometric data
Authors: H O Shittu, I O Olarinoye, M T Kolo, S F Olukotun, M Mathuthu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed the spatial distribution of natural radionuclides and the associated absorbed dose rates in Gidan-Kwano, Minna, Niger State, Nigeria, using airborne radiometric data acquired from the Nigerian Geological Survey Agency (NGSA). Activity concentrations of ²³⁸U, ²³²Th, and ⁴⁰K were determined, and geospatial contour and isodose maps were generated. Absorbed dose rates ranged from 18 to 69 nGy/h (mean: 32 nGy/h), below the global reference of 59 nGy/h, indicating low radiological risk. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation for ⁴⁰K (R = 0.93), a moderate correlation for ²³²Th (R = 0.62), and a weak correlation for ²³⁸U (R = 0.08), suggesting ⁴⁰K largely drives spatial variability despite a lower dose contribution. Comparison with previous in-situ measurements showed weak agreement, reflecting methodological and spatial-scale differences. The findings establish a regional radiological baseline and demonstrate the utility of airborne surveys for environmental monitoring and radiation protection planning.Keywords: Airborne radiometric data, Nigerian Geological Survey Agency (NGSA), Activity concentration, Isodose map, Contour map, Gidan-KwanoSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Natural background gamma radiation is ever-present in the environment, and humans are continuously exposed to both terrestrial and cosmic sources of radiation1,2. According to the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Atomic Radiation (UNSCEAR), natural background radiation constitutes the primary source of human exposure, with a global mean annual effective dose estimated at 2.4 × 10⁻³ Sv3,4. Evaluating gamma radiation from natural sources is therefore of considerable importance5–8. Radioactivity levels in the environment vary significantly depending on the type and mineral composition of the soil9–12.

Naturally Occurring Radioactive Materials (NORMs), including uranium (²³⁸U), thorium (²³²Th), and potassium (⁴⁰K), are major contributors to environmental radiation due to their natural presence in rocks and soils13,14. These radionuclides typically occur in trace amounts, with ²³⁸U ranging between 2 and 4 ppm, ²³²Th between 8 and 12 ppm, and ⁴⁰K at approximately 2.5% by weight15. However, activities such as mining, construction, and natural disasters can increase the mass fractions and redistribution of these radionuclides in the environment. Human exposure to radioactive substances primarily occurs through the inhalation of dust particles and the ingestion of contaminated food and water. Prolonged exposure to ionizing radiation may lead to adverse health effects, including radiation-induced diseases and cancer16. In recent years, there has been growing interest in evaluating environmental radiation levels and assessing the risks associated with exposure to low doses of natural radioactivity from various sources, such as rocks, building materials, food, and water17–25.

Airborne radiometric surveys have become increasingly valuable in mineral exploration, particularly in large, geologically complex, or inaccessible areas. This geophysical technique estimates the mass fractions (in ppm or weight%) of radioelements in rocks by detecting gamma rays emitted during radioactive decay. Previous studies have demonstrated that airborne radiometric data can be used to estimate the activity concentrations (in Bq/kg) of ²³⁸U, ²³²Th, and ⁴⁰K, making the technique applicable not only to mineral exploration but also to environmental radiological safety assessments26–32.

This study aims to assess the spatial distribution of naturally occurring radionuclides (²³⁸U, ²³²Th, and ⁴⁰K) and their associated absorbed dose rates in Gidan-Kwano, Minna, Nigeria, using airborne radiometric data. Recent illegal and artisanal mining activities, increasing population growth associated with university expansion, and the granting of a mining license to the University underscore the need for a baseline radiological assessment. Illegal and artisanal mining operations disturb naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORMs), including uranium-series, thorium-series, and potassium-40 radionuclides, potentially releasing radionuclide-bearing dust into the atmosphere. Activities such as excavation, crushing, and grinding enhance airborne particulate dispersion, a process documented in mining-impacted environments8,33–35.

Airborne radionuclides primarily affect human health through inhalation. Long-term exposure to radon progeny and radioactive particulates can increase the risk of lung cancer and other radiogenic effects within the university community, even at relatively low chronic doses8,14. Radon decay products are strongly associated with lung cancer, while prolonged exposure to uranium- and thorium-series radionuclides may contribute to bone cancer and leukemia through internal irradiation pathways8.

This study therefore evaluates the spatial distribution of the activity concentrations of ²³⁸U, ²³²Th, and ⁴⁰K and their associated absorbed dose rates using airborne radiometric data. The generated geospatial contour and isodose maps provide a regional radiological baseline and support future background radiation monitoring, land-use planning, and radiation protection strategies.

Study area

The study area is located in Chanchaga Local Government Area of Niger State, north-central Nigeria, between latitudes 9°33′N and 9°38′N and longitudes 6°28′E and 6°35′E (Fig. 1). It encompasses the Gidan-Kwano Campus of the Federal University of Technology, Minna (FUT Minna), which has witnessed increased human activities, including small-scale illegal mining operations. This has raised concerns about the environmental radiological safety of the area36.

Map showing the study area (ArcGIS Desktop: Release 10.3 (Esri Inc., Redlands, CA, USA; https://www.esri.com, 2014)).

The study area experiences a distinct seasonal wind regime, dominated by north-easterly Harmattan winds during the dry season and south-westerly moist winds during the wet season37,38. These seasonal wind systems strongly influence the transport, resuspension, and atmospheric dispersion of dust-bound radionuclides. During the Harmattan period, enhanced surface dryness, increased wind speed, and high dust loading promote the long-range movement of particulate matter originating from disturbed soils, exposed geological formations, and mining residues, thereby increasing the likelihood of inhalation exposure and surface deposition in nearby populated environments. Conversely, wet-season south-westerly winds, combined with precipitation scavenging, generally reduce airborne particulate concentrations and limit radionuclide dispersion, although localized redistribution may still occur. The integration of meteorological parameters, especially seasonal wind direction and dust dynamics, is therefore essential for accurate airborne radiological risk assessment and environmental monitoring in mining-impacted regions8,34.

This region consists of two primary geological formations: sedimentary rocks and the basement complex39. The southern part, which is primarily composed of sedimentary rocks, includes sandstone formations and alluvial deposits, especially around the Niger Valley and across large parts of Borgu, Mokwa, Bida, Lapai, Agaie, Gbako, Lavun, and Wushishi. The region also encompasses the floodplains of the Niger River, enhancing the state’s reputation as one of Nigeria’s largest and most fertile agricultural zones40. In contrast, the northern part is characterized by the basement complex, with granitic outcrops and inselbergs that shape the rolling landscape, particularly in Rafi, Shiroro, Minna, Mariga, and Gurara41.

Methods

Airborne radiometric survey data and evaluation of activity concentration levels of ²³⁸U, ²³²Th, and ⁴⁰K.

Radiometric data for the present study were obtained from the high-resolution nationwide airborne geophysical survey conducted by the Nigerian Geological Survey Agency (NGSA), which forms part of Nigeria’s national geoscientific mapping programme. Airborne gamma-ray spectrometry detects emissions primarily from the upper soil layer and therefore yields spatially averaged near-surface radionuclide activity concentrations rather than point-specific values. Consequently, the derived activity concentrations and absorbed dose rates may be influenced by flight-altitude variability, atmospheric attenuation, soil moisture, vegetation cover, and assumptions of secular equilibrium within the uranium and thorium decay series, potentially leading to localized underestimation or overestimation, as widely documented in international radiological assessment guidelines15,27.

To minimize these limitations, the survey was conducted using standardized flight-control procedures, calibrated detector, altitude and background corrections, while data interpretation emphasized regional spatial trends to ensure a reliable environmental radiological baseline assessment. The survey employed a 256-channel gamma-ray spectrometer equipped with NaI(Tl) crystals measuring 2″ × 2″, mounted on a fixed-wing aircraft flown at an average terrain clearance of approximately 80 m, with a flight-line spacing of 500 m and a nominal tie-line spacing of 2 km oriented in the NE–SW direction. Scintillation detectors recorded radiometric counts at 0.1 s intervals, with an average flight elevation of about 100 m42.

The equivalent uranium mass fraction was measured using the gamma-ray emissions of ²¹⁴Bi at 1765 keV within the standard uranium window (1660–1860 keV). The thorium (²³²Th) mass fraction was derived from the 2614 keV line of ²⁰⁸Tl (2410–2810 keV window), while potassium-40 (⁴⁰K) was measured directly at 1460 keV (1370–1570 keV window). These measurements were conducted in accordance with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) airborne spectrometry guidelines outlined in the Technical Report Series Nos. 309 and 32326,43. Potassium concentrations were recorded as weight percentages (wt%), while uranium and thorium mass fractions were expressed in parts per million (ppm)44.

The measured mass fractions (in ppm or wt%) of ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th in the study area were sorted, analyzed, and converted to activity concentrations (in Bq/kg) following IAEA guidelines. According to the IAEA, a potassium mass fraction of 1 wt% corresponds to an activity concentration of 313 Bq/kg, while 1 ppm of uranium and thorium corresponds to 12.35 Bq/kg and 4.06 Bq/kg, respectively26,27. Plotting of 238U, 232Th and 40K contours and isodose maps.

The datasets containing the evaluated activity concentrations of ²³⁸U, ²³²Th, and ⁴⁰K, along with the calculated absorbed dose rates and their corresponding coordinates, were used to generate contour maps for each radionuclide and Isodose map using ArcGIS version 10.3, a software tool for spatial analysis and mapping46. These contour maps illustrate the spatial distribution of activity concentrations for each radionuclide, as well as the associated absorbed dose rates and radiation exposure levels across the study area. The Kriging interpolation method was applied, which assigns decreasing weights to data points based on a semivariogram model of the survey locations5,18,47. The GPS data were referenced to the World Geodetic System (WGS) 1984 and aligned with the coordinates of the measurement points.

Descriptive statistics and comparative analysis.

Descriptive statistics such as maximum, minimum, mean, median, standard deviation (SD), standard error (SE), skewness, and kurtosis were computed for the obtained activity concentrations of ²³⁸U, ²³²Th, ⁴⁰K and the absorbed dose rate (D). In addition, Pearson’s correlation coefficient analysis was employed to evaluate the strength and direction of the linear relationships between the activity concentrations of the radionuclides and the calculated absorbed dose rates. The Pearson correlation coefficient (R) quantifies the degree of linear association between two variables, with values ranging from − 1 to + 1. A value of + 1 signifies a perfect positive correlation, −1 indicates a perfect negative correlation, and 0 implies no linear relationship48.

Furthermore, a scatter (X, Y) plot was used to compare the absorbed dose rate (D, in nGy/h) values obtained in this study with those reported by Shittu47, who employed an in-situ measurement technique for the same study area. This provides a visual representation of the relationship between the two datasets. Each point on the plot represents a paired observation: the value from this study (X-axis) and the corresponding value from Shittu47. A linear trendline was fitted to the data to assess the strength and direction of the relationship, and the coefficient of determination (R²) was calculated.

Results and Discussion

Distribution patterns of ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th in the study area

The contour maps (Figs. 2 and 3, and 4) illustrate the spatial distribution of activity concentrations for each radionuclide. Figure 2 shows the contour map for ⁴⁰K, which indicates relatively elevated activity concentrations toward the northwestern margin of the study area, while lower values dominate the southern sector. A diagonal belt of activity concentrations ranging from 350 to 470 Bq/kg trends from northeast to southwest. This pattern is consistent with the lithology of the study area, as potassium is predominantly hosted in feldspars and micas commonly associated with granitic and gneissic basement rocks. The higher concentrations observed in the northern and northwestern zones likely reflect the proximity of crystalline basement outcrops and weathered regolith derived from them. In contrast, the reduced ⁴⁰K activity in the south corresponds to sandstone formations, alluvial deposits, and floodplain sediments, where prolonged weathering, mineral sorting, and leaching processes typically deplete potassium-bearing minerals. The diagonal transition zone may further indicate geological contacts, sediment transport pathways, or redistribution of soil materials through anthropogenic disturbances such as excavation and informal mining.

For ²³⁸U, the contour map (Fig. 3) indicates generally low activity concentrations extending from the northwest toward the south, interrupted by a localized high activity concentration near the southern boundary and relatively higher values in the northeast. The localized southern high activity concentration may reflect secondary accumulation within sedimentary units or alluvial trapping zones, whereas the higher northeastern values are consistent with uranium-bearing accessory minerals such as zircon, monazite, and uraninite, commonly associated with granitic basement lithologies.

In contrast, ²³²Th exhibits comparatively uniform activity across most of the study area, with values largely constrained within a narrow range of approximately 19–21 Bq/kg, except for a distinct high activity concentration in the western sector (Fig. 4). Thorium is characteristically less mobile than uranium under near-surface environmental conditions and thus more directly reflects primary lithology rather than secondary transport. The western high activity concentration is therefore likely associated with thorium-rich accessory minerals, particularly monazite, within granitic or metamorphic basement rocks. The relative uniformity elsewhere suggests extensive sediment mixing and prolonged weathering, which tend to homogenize thorium concentrations in transported soils and alluvial materials.

Overall, the combined distributions of ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th demonstrate a clear geological control linked to the north–south transition between basement complex and sedimentary terrains, while also reflecting secondary influences from weathering, sediment transport, seasonal dust dynamics, and localized mining disturbances. Integrating these geological and environmental controls provides a more meaningful interpretation of the radiometric contour maps and strengthens the environmental radiological assessment of the study area.

Geospatial pattern of absorbed dose rates across the study area.

Figure 5 presents the isodose rate map showing the spatial distribution of absorbed dose rates across the study area. Elevated dose rates occur mainly in the western and northwestern regions, with additional hotspots in the southeast, corresponding to granite and gneiss formations enriched in naturally occurring radionuclides (⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th). The contour maps (Figs. 2, 3 and 4) further clarify the radionuclide sources of these spatial variations. High ⁴⁰K activity (350–470 Bq/kg) in the northwestern margin reflects potassium-rich feldspars and micas within granitic and gneissic basement rocks, whereas lower southern activities correspond to potassium-depleted sandstone and alluvial deposits. ²³⁸U shows generally low activity with localized enrichments near the southern boundary and northeastern sector, likely associated with sedimentary accumulation and uranium-bearing accessory minerals (e.g., zircon, monazite, and uraninite) within basement lithologies. In contrast, ²³²Th is relatively uniform (19–21 Bq/kg) but exhibits a distinct western high linked to thorium-rich minerals, particularly monazite, contributing substantially to elevated dose rates.

Isodose rate map (ArcGIS Desktop: Release 10.3 (Esri Inc., Redlands, CA, USA; https://www.esri.com, 2014)).

The overall north–south gradient in absorbed dose rates reflects the geological transition from crystalline basement rocks (granite, gneiss, and pegmatite), associated with higher radiation levels, to southern sedimentary terrains characterized by lower doses. This lithological influence on environmental radiation is consistent with previous studies, including reported absorbed dose ranges of 20–400 nGy/h in granitic regions of central France and elevated dose levels in granitic basement terrains of Niger State, Nigeria15,36. Collectively, the results indicate that the spatial distribution of absorbed dose rates is governed by the combined influence of radionuclide concentration, geological setting, and related environmental processes.

Summary of activity concentrations and absorbed dose rate.

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of the measured parameters. For ⁴⁰K, the mean (293 Bq/kg) was higher than the median (261 Bq/kg), indicating a positive skew, with substantial variability (SD = 204) and a slightly leptokurtic distribution (kurtosis = 0.2). The distribution of ²³⁸U appeared fairly symmetric, with the mean (15 Bq/kg) equal to the median (15 Bq/kg), moderate variability (SD = 5), and a mild positive skew (skewness = 0.9). For ²³²Th, the mean (21 Bq/kg) was equal to the median (21 Bq/kg), but the high kurtosis value (4.4) suggests a strongly leptokurtic distribution with the presence of extreme values. The absorbed dose rate (D) was right-skewed, with the mean (32 nGy/h) greater than the median (30 nGy/h), and a kurtosis value of 1.7, indicating a leptokurtic distribution with central clustering.

Importantly, the mean activity concentrations of ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th, as well as the absorbed dose rate, were all below the corresponding world average values of 400 Bq/kg, 35 Bq/kg, 30 Bq/kg, and 59 nGy/h, respectively, as reported by15. This indicates that the study area poses no significant radiological health risk.

Pearson correlation matrix of activity concentrations and dose rates.

The Pearson correlation matrix (Table 2) reveals the relationships between the activity concentrations of ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th (in Bq/kg), and the absorbed dose rates (D, in nGy/h). A strong positive correlation was observed between ⁴⁰K and D (R = 0.93), suggesting that variations in ⁴⁰K activity exert a strong influence on the spatial distribution of dose rates across the study area. However, it is important to note that due to its relatively low dose conversion factor, ⁴⁰K is not a dominant contributor to the absolute dose but serves as a key driver of spatial variability15. In contrast, ²³²Th showed a moderate positive correlation with D (R = 0.62), reflecting its more substantial contribution to the absorbed dose rates given its higher conversion factor. Meanwhile, ²³⁸U exhibited only a weak positive correlation with D (R = 0.08), indicating a limited role in influencing the overall dose levels. The inter-activity correlations revealed that ⁴⁰K and ²³²Th were weakly correlated (R = 0.38), ⁴⁰K and ²³⁸U were negatively correlated (R = − 0.22), and ²³²Th and ²³⁸U showed a negligible correlation (R = 0.06), suggesting largely independent activity concentrations.

Comparative analysis of obtained absorbed dose rates with a previous Study.

The scatter plot (Fig. 6) illustrates the relationship between the absorbed dose rate values (D, nGy/h) obtained in this study and those reported by Shittu47. A linear regression fit yielded the equation Y = 1.20X + 107.58, with slope and intercept uncertainties of ± 0.33 and ± 10.98, respectively. The coefficient of determination (R² = 0.16) indicates a weak positive correlation, suggesting that while both datasets exhibit a broadly similar trend, only about 16% of the variability in Shittu’s dataset can be explained by the current study.

Comparison of absorbed dose rates (D, nGy/h) between this study and Shittu45.

The remaining 84.28% of the variability may be attributed to methodological differences between the two studies. For instance, higher ground-level measurements can be explained by the inverse square law governing photon flux, where shorter source-to-detector distances result in stronger readings49. Airborne data, conversely, are subject to attenuation in air and interference from suspended particles, which may contribute to lower values. Furthermore, ground-based measurements may detect radiation from additional sources beyond the three naturally occurring radionuclides typically captured by airborne gamma spectrometry.

Geological outcrops and altitude differences also play a role. Airborne measurements, conducted at a fixed flight altitude, tend to record higher dose rates over elevated terrains compared to low-lying areas, where the distance from source to detector increases. These factors likely account for the differences and the weak correlation between the datasets. Nonetheless, while airborne surveys may not resolve small-scale variations, they remain valuable for regional-scale radiation mapping and long-term environmental monitoring30,32.

Conclusion

This study presents a geospatial assessment of natural radionuclide distribution and absorbed gamma dose rates in Gidan-Kwano, Minna, Nigeria, using airborne radiometric data. The spatial variability of ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, and ²³²Th reflects the geological transition between crystalline basement rocks and sedimentary formations, which governs the observed isodose pattern. Although the mean absorbed dose rate is below the global reference value of 59 nGy/h indicating generally low radiological risk, the presence of localized zones with relatively elevated dose rates highlights the importance of site-specific environmental evaluation. Crucially, the results provide a baseline for radionuclide distribution and absorbed gamma dose rates in view of ongoing illegal and anticipated mining activities within and around the university environment. Land disturbance, excavation, and dust resuspension associated with mining can redistribute radionuclide-bearing particulates, potentially affecting localized air quality and surface radiation levels even where regional averages remain low. Correlation analysis shows that ⁴⁰K drives spatial dose variability, with an additional contribution from ²³²Th, while ²³⁸U plays a minor role, indicating multiple geological and environmental controls. In summary, airborne radiometric surveying proves effective for regional radiological mapping, early detection of mining-related environmental impacts, and the establishment of a pre-development radiological baseline, thereby supporting continuous monitoring, regulatory planning, and evidence-based environmental protection within mining-influenced university settings.
