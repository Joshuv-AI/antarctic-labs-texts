Australian registry reports poor health and wellbeing in people living with ME/CFS
Authors: Natalie Eaton-Fitch, Sonya Marshall-Gradisnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionMyalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS) is a debilitating condition hallmarked by post-exertional malaise accompanied by an array of multisystemic symptoms. Consequently, people living with ME/CFS experience significant health impacts and report poor health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Living with a condition of an area of unmet need, there is a call for a greater understanding of the health impacts of ME/CFS in Australia. Despite growing recognition and awareness, large-scale national comparative investigations using ME/CFS cohorts have not been reported.MethodsThe National Centre for Neuroimmunology and Emerging Diseases houses a significant Australian registry of people with ME/CFS. Self-reported questionnaire data collected from people with ME/CFS and non-fatigued controls (NFC) between 2014 and February 2026 was exported from applications including Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) and LimeSurvey. Data was collected pertaining to the sociodemographic background, medical history, symptom presentation (for people with ME/CFS), and validated patient reported outcome measures to assess HRQoL. The questionnaire covered medical history, routine medications, symptom presentation, and HRQoL, using validated tools including the 36-item short form health survey (SF-36). Data was analysed using independent student’s t and chi-square tests, linear regression analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and k-means clustering. Data was presented as mean and standard deviation or percentages. Statistical analysis was completed using SPSS and RStudio with a significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsResponses were analysed from n = 797 NFC (41.72 ± 13.86 years, 62.6% female) and n = 2,873 people with ME/CFS (44.85 ± 13.77 years, 75.2% female). Participants with ME/CFS fulfilled Fukuda (39.0%), CCC (30.4%) and ICC (30.6%) case definitions. Analysis of symptom presentation differed in both severity and frequency according to the most stringent case definition met; however, this did not predict poor HRQoL outcomes. HRQoL was significantly impaired compared with NFC, and people fulfilling ICC were more likely to report poorer outcomes. Regression analysis revealed the impact of multisystemic symptoms on both physical and mental functioning. K means clustering returned four clusters which differed according to stringent case criteria met, severity and frequency of symptoms.ConclusionThis large-scale national investigation highlights the significant symptom burden and reduced HRQoL in people with ME/CFS. These findings emphasise the need for tailored clinical approaches and further research into symptom clustering for the purpose of identifying clinical subtypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08618-9.

Introduction

Complex chronic diseases of unknown aetiology present a significant burden on quality of life (QoL). Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS), a potentially severe and disabling multisystemic illness, consequently impacts occupational performance, participation in society, financial independence, and overall physical or mental wellbeing [1]. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is often used interchangeably with QoL; however, refers to the self-perceived health status of an individual [2]. Global research has consistently documented impaired HRQoL outcomes in people with ME/CFS. These findings are reflective of an individual’s severity as those considered mild, self-report an approximate 50% reduction in pre-ME/CFS activities, while severe cases are home- or bed-bound and constitute ~ 25% of people with ME/CFS [3]. It is estimated that 10–20 per cent of people with ME/CFS experience increased disability over time [4]; therefore, contributing to prolonged and potentially permanent health impacts [5]. However, to date, small cohort investigations have been undertaken to assess self-reported HRQoL outcomes in people living with ME/CFS [6–13], with limited research undertaken in larger cohorts [14]. The impacts on a person’s HRQoL are multidimensional and are influenced by factors outside health status. Specifically, social and emotional support, social isolation, socioeconomic factors, lifestyle behaviours, and healthcare factors [15]. The economic burden of ME/CFS has been investigated previously in an Australian cohort [16]. The cost attributed to ME/CFS was estimated at $14.5 billion (AUD) heavily influenced by loss of income, direct health care expenditure including attendances with medical and allied health professionals, cost of diagnostic tests, and prescription medications. Further, an investigation by the European ME/CFS Research Network conducted a review of the literature pertaining to general practitioner (GP) knowledge and understanding of ME/CFS concluding that disbelief and lack of knowledge remains widespread [17]. An Australian investigation, whereby ME/CFS formed a small proportion of female participants with chronic conditions, investigated central themes of healthcare experiences including barriers receiving diagnosis, healthcare experiences, understanding medical complexity, and coping with their symptoms [18]. Difficulties in receiving a diagnosis, and “medical gaslighting” and knowledge of the condition were emphasised by people living with ME/CFS [18].

Diagnostic barriers are attributed, at least in part, to ambiguity in clinical case definitions for ME/CFS, for which there are more than 20 in circulation [19]. Decades of evolving research have resulted in numerous diagnostic criteria of varying sensitivity contributing to ill-informed health practitioners and policy providers. Indeed, with the numerous case criteria in existence, this biases even the terminology of ME/CFS resulting in disconnect within communities of the understanding of the condition. Few case criteria acknowledge the multisystem nature of ME/CFS, emphasising the importance of both the Canadian Consensus Criteria (CCC) and International Consensus Criteria (ICC) that include symptom categories including neurosensory, autonomic, immunological, and thermoregulatory [20, 21]. Research into the spectrum of symptoms experienced by people living with ME/CFS has eluded to clinical subtypes [22, 23].

Most importantly, the multisystemic co-occurring symptoms have severe impact on HRQoL [24]. Given the many factors influencing health outcomes for people living with chronic conditions, multidisciplinary research in ME/CFS is essential. Therefore, maintaining accurate and up-to-date research on HRQoL outcomes in people living with ME/CFS will continue to raise awareness and provide resources aimed not only at education, but also to emphasise the importance of maintaining health policy to decrease the burden of illness. To the author’s knowledge, this investigation utilises the largest national cohort of people living with ME/CFS to investigate health and wellbeing in an Australian cohort.

Method

Setting and study population

This study follows a cross-sectional observational design of self-reported demographic and clinical data between 2014 and 2025. Patient level data was collected by the National Centre for Neuroimmunology and Emerging Diseases (NCNED) based at Griffith University located in Queensland, Australia. This study was approved by Griffith University Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 2022/666) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

A total of 5,509 questionnaire entries were exported for analysis (Fig. 1). Incomplete responses (n = 1,333), or individuals who did not meet inclusion criteria (n = 508) were excluded from final analysis resulting in 3,670 entries being analysed.

During the data collection period, participants were recruited using online advertisements via the NCNED website, radio, newspaper, and social media to ensure broad reach. To be eligible, participants were required to: (i) report a diagnosis of ME/CFS; (ii) be above 18 years of age; and (iii) understand written English. Participants were not excluded for comorbid diagnoses in order to capture the lived experiences of people living with ME/CFS. The impact of comorbid diagnoses will be addressed in future research. Paediatric ME/CFS was not assessed. Informed consent was obtained by agreeing to the terms and conditions disclosed online or by signing a hard copy sent via mail. Non-fatigued controls (NFC) were defined as participants without a diagnosis of ME/CFS and did not report a fatigue-related condition.

Data collection

Participants completed an online survey distributed by either LimeSurvey, RedCAP, or by mailed hard copy. Completed hard copies were returned to NCNED and entered into the online application by a member of the research team. Items in the survey were developed in accordance with the CDC CFS Symptom Inventory Questionnaire, with additions to incorporate the CCC and ICC case definitions of ME/CFS. The questionnaire was divided into four primary sections. Section A sorted sociodemographic characteristics including age at the time of response, gender identify (male, female or other), Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), highest level of education obtained, current working status, smoking status, residence, and ancestry. Section B reported on medical history including diagnoses received during childhood and adolescence, diagnoses received during adulthood, routine medication or supplements. Section C was completed only by people with ME/CFS to gather data on preceding events, symptom presentation including frequency (a little of the time, some of the time, a good bit of the time, most of the time, and all of the time) and severity (very mild, mild, moderate, severe and very severe). Alternatively, participants reported frequency or severity on a scale of one to ten, whereby ten indicates very severe or most frequent. These values were normalised and scaled from one to five using linear min-max transformation using excel. Symptom severity and frequency were then entered numerically rather than ordinally. Lastly section D, completed by all participants, incorporated a series of validated patient reported outcome measures (PROMs).

Statistical analysis

Data was analysed using both RStudio and SPSS. Descriptive statistics were calculated for categorical variables. For continuous variables, data are presented as mean, standard error of the mean (SEM) and standard deviation (SD). Demographic, QoL, and symptom characteristics were examined using Independent Student’s T test and Chi-square analyses using SPSS. Bivariate correlation analysis was performed examining correlation among symptoms and HRQoL using SPSS. For each SF-36 domain, QoL was modelled with symptom severity and frequency using multiple linear regression. Covariates age and illness duration (years) were entered, while symptom variables were subjected to stepwise entry/removal. Standardised beta (β) coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using symptom severity and frequency scores to determine symptoms explaining the most variance amongst the cohort [25]. All variables were standardised by z-score scaling. PCA was performed using prcomp function from the base R package. K-means clustering was applied to standardised symptom frequency and severity scores [26]. The silhouette function (cluster package) was used to determine the optimal number of clusters by maximising the average silhouette width across k = 2–8. The elbow method to determine optimal k was used as a comparator [27]. Missing data in symptom presentation was attributed to the absence of a symptom and denoted as a zero during PCA analysis. Significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Data was analysed from a total of n = 2,873 people with ME/CFS (78.28%) and n = 797 NFC (21.72%). Participant demographics can be found in Table 1.

There was a significant difference in the age and distribution of age between participants (p < 0.001). Specifically, people with ME/CFS were older compared with NFC (p < 0.001). Both ME/CFS and NFC cohorts skewed towards older demographics with the highest proportion of participants aged 50+. There was a significant difference in the BMI for people with ME/CFS compared with NFC (p < 0.001). Participants of the female gender constituted the majority of the study cohort, with the highest distribution of females reported in the ME/CFS cohort compared with NFC (p < 0.001).

Majority of participants reported residing in the state of Queensland followed by New South Wales given the close geographical proximity to the NCNED laboratories. Participants were asked to list at least two relevant ancestries, with the majority of participants reporting Australian and European ancestry. Several ancestry variables differed by group, especially Asian and Indigenous Australian ancestries. The NFC cohort had the highest postgraduate proportion, while people with ME/CFS reported higher high school or professional training qualifications. People with ME/CFS showed significantly higher unemployment rates with illness or disability as the dominant reason for unemployment.

Symptom Presentation

ME/CFS participants were more likely to fulfil Fukuda criteria, which was followed by ICC then CCC (X2 = 42.32, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Symptom presentation was acquired according to “yes” or “no” responses when asked if a particular symptom was a concern within the past month. Symptom frequencies are reported in Table 2 whereby beyond the core symptoms of ME/CFS, muscle pain (87.1%), muscle weakness (77.0%), neurosensory disturbances (82.3%), gastrointestinal disturbances (81.9%) and cardiovascular manifestations (78.0%) were commonly reported. Self-reported preceding events were reported by participants, whereby more than one relevant event at the time of their symptom onset could be reported. People were more likely to report post-infectious onset (53.3%), followed by stress (48.4%), chemical exposure (17.4%), and physical trauma (14.7%).

The frequency of symptom presentation was investigated in people with ME/CFS (Table 3). The frequency of all reported symptoms differed statistically between people with ME/CFS fulfilling Fukuda criteria compared with CCC and ICC (p < 0.001). People fulfilling ICC were more likely to report symptoms more frequently across all symptoms excluding, unrefreshing sleep and cardiovascular dysfunction which were slightly more frequent in the CCC cohort. Statistical differences between the ICC and CCC cohorts were observed in the frequency of joint pain (p < 0.001), unrefreshing sleep (p < 0.001), neurosensory disturbances (p = 0.003), muscle weakness (p < 0.001), sore throat (p < 0.001), gastrointestinal symptoms (p < 0.001), feverishness or chills (p < 0.001), respiratory disturbances (p < 0.001), genitourinary disturbances (p < 0.001) and temperature intolerances (p < 0.001).

Symptom severity in ME/CFS is reported in Table 4. People fulfilling Fukuda criteria consistently reported experiencing symptoms as less severe compared with CCC and ICC cohorts (p < 0.001). Statistical differences were reported between CCC and ICC cohorts in symptom severity for muscle pain (p < 0.001), joint pain (p < 0.001), sleep disturbances (p = 0.030), neurosensory disturbances (p < 0.001), muscle weakness (p < 0.001), sore throat (p < 0.001), gastrointestinal disturbances (p < 0.001), feverishness or chills (p < 0.001), respiratory disturbances (p < 0.001), and cardiovascular dysfunction (p < 0.001). People fulling ICC returned reported symptoms as more severe in all categories.

Poor HRQoL among people with ME/CFS

Significant differences were observed across cohorts for all QoL domains collection (p < 0.001), as shown in Table 5. Across all SF-36 domains, people with ME/CFS scored significantly lower compared with NFC. People with ME/CFS had significantly elevated WHO DAS scores compared with NFC (p < 0.001). Most effected domains included participation in society, life activities, and household participation. According to AMPKs and Dr Bell’s Disability Scale, functional capacity was significantly reduced in people with ME/CFS compared with NFC (p < 0.001). Impact of fatigue was determined using MFIS and was significantly increased in ME/CFS compared with NFC (p < 0.001).

People with ME/CFS were further divided into the most stringent case definition met, being Fukuda, CCC or ICC (Table 6). People with ME/CFS fulfilling the CCC or ICC definitions returned poorer QoL outcomes compared with participants only fulfilling Fukuda criteria. ANOVA revealed statistical significance in all domains excluding HADS. SF-36 domains revealed progressively worsening scores in participants fulfilling Fukuda followed by CCC followed by ICC. All SF-36 domains returned statistical significance between cohorts; however, no significant differences were reported between Fukuda and CC participants for role emotional and mental health. No significant differences were found between CCC and ICC participants in the mental health domain. WHO DAS domain scores were significantly different between cohorts for all domains and case definitions met.

No statistical differences were observed between participants fulfilling CCC and ICC criteria for AMKPs, Dr Bell’s CFID, MFIS scores and HADS scores. Participants fulfilling Fukuda criteria differed compared with both CCC and ICC participants for scores obtained by AMPKS, Dr Bell’s CFID and MFIS.

Impact of symptom presentation on physical and mental wellbeing

Given the most employed PROM to determine HRQoL in ME/CFS research is the SF-36, individual and total domain scores were employed for this analysis. The linear correlation between symptom presentation and HRQoL outcomes was confirmed (Supplementary Table 1). Linear regression analysis can be found in Tables 7 and 8. Prolonged illness duration was a significant positive predictor for higher QoL outcomes for role function (β = 0.089, CI = 0.052 to 0.301), role physical (β = 0.122, CI = 0.100 to 0.338), role emotional (β = 0.131, CI = 0.154 to 0.601), social functioning (β = 0.099, CI = 0.072 to 0.338), and total SF-36 score (β = 0.087, CI = 0.036 to 0.168). Age was a predictor for general health (β = 0.135, CI = 0.065 to 0.228), role functioning (β = -0.120, CI = -0.307 to -0.094), role physical (β = -0.091, CI = -0.242 to -0.037), mental health (β = 0.106, CI = 0.043 to 0.289), and social functioning (β = -0.081, CI = -0.258 to -0.029). Most stringent classification met was not a predictor of QoL outcomes.

Increased severity of fatigue (β = -0.096, CI = -3.498 to -0.344), muscle weakness (β = -0.110, CI = -2.743 to -0.516), and gastrointestinal upset (β = -0.172, CI = -3.254 to -1.247) were significant predictors of poor general health (R2 = 0.230, p < 0.001). Symptom Clusters and Dimensionality

Sampling was verified using Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure (KMO = 0.901) and Bartlett’s test (X2 = 116,079.40, p < 0.001), hence further analysis employed PCA (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 2). PCA did not exclude observations, and all participants were retained for clustering. As PCA retains total variance and no factor rotation was applied, only initial eigenvalues and proportions of variance are reported to demonstrative symptom structure (Table 9). All eigenvalues and proportions of variance can be found in Supplementary Table 3. PCA loadings for each symptom per component can be found in Supplementary Table 4. Nine components returned eigenvalue ≥ 1.0, among these the cumulative variance was 75.65%, demonstrating that these components represent majority of the data. All symptoms contributed comparatively to PC1, with fatigue (7.42%), cognition (7.15%), unrefreshing sleep (6.98%), sleep disturbances (6.90%), muscle pain (6.61%), and headache (6.41%) being the largest contributors (Supplementary Table 5). While the largest contributors to PC2 included temperature intolerances (12.08%), cardiovascular disturbances (10.70%), genitourinary manifestations (9.77%), gastrointestinal complaints (6.94%) and muscle weakness (8.92%) being the largest contributors (Supplementary Table 5).

First 10 component variance

First the silhouette method returned two clusters, whereby cluster 1 consisted of n = 1,922 participants with ME/CFS, while cluster 2 contained n = 951. K-means cluster centroids for each included variable can be found in Supplementary Table 6. Participants in cluster 2 were reported to be older (p = 0.003, t = -2.948) and had prolonged illness duration (p = 0.007, t = -2.685) (Fig. 2A). The distribution of cohort according to stringent classification fulfilled also difference significantly (p < 0.001), whereby participants in cluster 1 were more likely to fulfil Fukuda criteria while participants in cluster 2 were more likely to fulfil ICC. People assigned to cluster 1 were more likely to report symptoms at a higher severity and more frequent. Independent T test revealed significant differences in symptom severity and frequency between clusters 1 and 2 (all p < 0.001, Supplementary Table 7). There were significant differences in HRQoL between clusters 1 and 2 (Table 10). Importantly, SF-36 domains were more likely to be lower in Cluster 1 compared with Cluster 2, excluding role functioning and role emotional. Thus Cluster 1 was more likely to return poorer outcomes. Figure 2C demonstrates heatmap of mean symptom severity and frequency scores between clusters.

Subsequently, the elbow method returned k = 4, highlighting further heterogeneity to be explored in the current dataset. The final cluster centres can be found in Supplementary Table 8. ANOVA analysis returned significant differences between all four clusters (Supplementary Table 9). Participants in cluster 4 were of older age (45.97 ± 13.11 years) and reported a longer illness duration (13.98 ± 11.70) compared with other clusters (p = 0.005). Participants in cluster 4 reported higher symptom severity and frequency compared with all clusters excluding neurosensory disturbances and tender lymph nodes which were highest in cluster 2. SF-36 health domains were significantly lower in cluster 4 across all domains (p < 0.001), excluding role physical which was lowest in cluster 1 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

To the author’s knowledge, this current investigation reports on the clinical presentation and HRQoL impacts in the largest cohort of people living with ME/CFS internationally. This current investigation builds upon previous research undertaken in smaller cohorts, whereby frequency statistics accompanied by limited traditional regression and cluster analysis has been undertaken [24, 28, 29]. Therefore, this present investigation constitutes an important international contribution and relevancy to this area of research in ME/CFS.

This current study consisted of a cohort primarily of people who are female with a mean age of ~ 44 years, as also seen in epidemiological [30, 31] and biomedical investigations documenting potential contributors of sex differences in ME/CFS, including immune, autonomic and endocrine variability [32–34]. Although women are more likely to represent cohorts of ME/CFS in research [35], the healthcare-seeking practices or biological variability as the cause is yet to be elucidated. Moreover, sex or gender gaps in research and medical education may be a result of increased female participation and/or a representation in epidemiological research or clinic diagnosis rates. Nonetheless this is yet to be confirmed in ME/CFS research and the relevance of whether this increased proportion of women with ME/CFS in research translates into true societal prevalence.

Across all SF-36 domains, people with ME/CFS scored significantly lower compared with NFC, indicating reduced perceived physical and mental functioning. This is consistent with previous research findings [6, 28, 29]. Further, poorer HRQoL scores were reported by people with ME/CFS fulfilling ICC compared with the Fukuda criteria, and for limited HRQoL domains this includes the CCC. In an Australian study, researchers examined the HRQoL impacts of people living with ME/CFS patients fulfilling ICC or Fukuda criteria [36]. The authors report that participants fulfilling the more stringent ICC represent a more severe subgroup of ME/CFS believed to be attributed to the additional symptoms required to meet the ICC. HRQoL scores coupled with symptom severity and frequency scores in the present investigation support these previous findings.

The reproducibility of SF-36 domain data in ME/CFS research using a multiple linear regression analysis aimed to confirm predictors of poor HRQoL outcomes. Overall, HRQoL reflected multisystem physiological impairment with fatigue acting as the central symptom by consistently predicting poor HRQoL outcomes. In addition, muscle weakness, sleep dysfunction, autonomic complaints, gastrointestinal disturbances and genitourinary symptoms predict poor HRQoL outcomes further documenting the multisystemic impacts of ME/CFS. Previous research has highlighted the importance of correctly interpreting “fatigue” experienced by people with ME/CFS which encompasses cognitive difficulties, neuromuscular complaints, autonomic dysfunction, pain, flu-like symptoms and unrefreshing sleep/rest [37, 38]. The concurrent role of multisystemic symptoms on HRQoL outcomes provide further support for previous epidemiological research. Furthermore, the SF-36 pain domain was not only associated with the presence of more severe muscle and joint pain and muscle weakness, but also cognitive and sleep disturbances. This present data suggests that physical functioning is not only limited by energy impairment and the symptom of ‘fatigue’, but also neurocognitive, autonomic and pain manifestations. Further, mental wellbeing and role emotional domains were associated with severity of impaired memory, joint pain, unrefreshing sleep, sleep disturbances, as well as severity and frequency of muscle pain and weakness. Therefore, highlighting that functional impairment in ME/CFS is not of psychiatric origin.

In this present study, people with ME/CFS were more likely to report moderate-severe HADS scores compared with NFC, indicating significant impact to overall mental health. Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a primary psychiatric origin but rather, a consequence or a secondary manifestation association with multisystem symptom burden and HRQoL outcomes in people with ME/CFS. Previous investigations have employed HADS and SF-36 and reported no significant differences in people with ME/CFS by physical activity level [39]. While this current investigation did not control for physical activity level, these current findings further demonstrate that irrespective of activity level, physical functioning measures are essential and highlight the negative impact of ME/CFS. Importantly, this current large scale Australian study clearly demonstrates the impact of a chronic illness, being ME/CFS, and secondary impacts on mental health. Therefore, future investigations are aimed to elucidate the role of social factors, including accessing healthcare in HRQoL.

Further, this cross-sectional investigation restricts the ability to interpret the relationship between symptom burden and HRQoL as causal. Whilst longitudinal studies are required to clarify casual inference, a previous investigation completed by Weigel et al. reported on HRQoL over a 12-month period in a smaller cohort of Australian’s with ME/CFS [5]. This previous investigation reported that HRQoL was consistently reduced in ME/CFS over the study period. Importantly, these data highlight that the current HRQoL of Australian’s living with ME/CFS is likely attributed to the condition and emphasises the importance of documenting this cohort as a critical unmet need. Given this current investigation did not assess patient-reported experiences, individuals with positive physician communication may report better HRQoL outcomes. Future research may aim to report on patient-reported experience measures in conjunction with HRQoL.

Given this is the largest scale investigation into symptom types, this current investigation employed PCA for dimensionality reduction. However, it is important to highlight that missing data was attributed to the absence of a symptom and denoted as zero, as outlined in the statistical analysis. Consequently, symptoms with a higher degree of missingness including impaired memory, muscle weakness, urinary disturbances, and joint pain are at risk of weaker loadings within symptom clusters and may be underestimated by the PCA. Symptoms associated with the first component were comparable and accounted for a substantial proportion of total variance demonstrating shared symptom dimension. This suggests that the present cohort of ME/CFS is perhaps not consisted of clinical subtypes but rather demonstrates the heterogeneity in symptom presentation severity and frequency as a collective and further large scale investigations are required for confirmation. The high KMO value also reveals substantial shared variance across symptom frequency and severity in the present cohort, confirming a correlating symptom structure. These current findings underscore that ME/CFS is shaped predominantly by global symptom dimensions, challenging the notion of discrete disease subtypes and highlighting consistent, overarching patterns of illness.

Conversely, elbow analysis further classified the current data into four clusters, suggesting further heterogeneity of the data compared with analysis performed by silhouette analysis. The elbow method determines the cohesion of the data group within a cluster according to similarities, whilst the silhouette method incorporates not only cohesion, but also separation [27]. Silhouette analysis showed small separation between two or four clusters, with cluster two returning a higher silhouette width. While no model is considered more correct, the interpretation of four clusters demonstrates the role of symptom severity and frequency, not only symptom presence, in clinical subtypes. In this present study, core symptoms including fatigue, pain, and sleep disturbances were statistically relevant and additional key symptoms reported through regression and component analyses perhaps indicated a skewed dataset towards autonomic disturbances. Alternatively, muscle weakness, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular disturbances present opportunities to investigate clinical subtypes in the future. In regard to clinical utility, validation of the two-cluster model may provide a binary classification resource to enable physicians to stratify people with ME/CFS at need of additional services including multidisciplinary allied health practitioners and monitoring as a targeted patient-centred approach to improve health outcomes.

Prior investigations have consistently reported symptom clustering in ME/CFS that revealed broad, overlapping domains rather than discrete clinical subtypes [23, 24, 40]. One study of 60 Australians with ME/CFS identified four symptom clusters based on symptom presence and SF-36 and WHO-DAS domain scores [24]. Another investigation using hierarchical clustering in 84 participants reported five clusters shaped by symptom severity, comorbid fibromyalgia, and anxiety/depression; notably, although 36.9% screened positive for depression on the HADS, only 14.28% reported a formal clinical diagnosis [40]. A further study of 106 individuals meeting CCC criteria applied factor solution analysis and identified four symptom factors—inflammatory, pain, neurocognitive, and autonomic, and thus, capturing the major dimensions of illness experiences [23]. This current investigation supports these previously observed patterns. Taken together, these collective studies reinforce the symptom patterns demonstrated in the current investigation reporting clustering approaches that repeatedly highlight variations in overall symptom burden and domain-level patterns, rather than supporting the existence of distinct ME/CFS subgroups defined by unique clinical phenotypes.

Over the ten-year period captured in this dataset, diagnostic criteria for ME/CFS have evolved substantially, specifically from the broader Fukuda definition to the more stringent Canadian Consensus Criteria (CCC) and International Consensus Criteria (ICC). Because this study spans that evolution, participants meeting the Fukuda definition were retained rather than excluded. As expected, statistically significant differences emerged in both the frequency and severity of symptoms across groups meeting Fukuda, CCC, and ICC criteria, with the ICC group—reflecting the most rigorous case definition—differing most markedly from those classified under Fukuda. HRQoL outcomes also varied significantly by case definition, including between CCC and ICC classifications. Nevertheless, despite these definitional differences, the specific criteria met did not independently predict poorer HRQoL. Therefore, research and medical communities may endeavour to remain inclusive to ensure recognition of the potential spectrum of ME/CFS respecting individuals who received a diagnosis of ME/CFS according to previous criteria or with the evolution of the ICC and CCC may be reassessed.

This current investigation did not exclude other comorbidities to encompass the generalisability of the cohort to the wider community of Australians with ME/CFS. A previous investigation documented chronic overlapping pain conditions including fibromyalgia, interstitial cystitis, irritable bowel syndrome, temporomandibular disorder, and others, in ME/CFS cohort [41]. The authors reported that 76.1% of ME/CFS participants had one or more pain comorbidity. This current investigation acknowledges the importance of including the impact of comorbidities to determine the full extent of the impact of ME/CFS on HRQoL outcomes to improve education and knowledge therefore will be applied in future investigations. Furthermore, the extent of PEM as a symptom is difficult to accurately quantify in earlier research. Given PEM as a symptom arises from cognitive or physical exertion and other stressors, the completion of questionnaire responses may skew toward people who are not considered very severe. Consequently, it is also essential to acknowledge the limitation of participant selection and inclusion. Responders who engage in the online survey are those more likely to express interest in participating in biomedical research. Furthermore, consideration and acknowledgement of self-reported data bias due to individual interpretation of symptom presentation and responses to PROMS highlights the need for objective measures to be employed in future epidemiological and biomedical research.

The present investigation constitutes an important contribution, reporting clinical outcomes that may be generalisable to the broader ME/CFS population in Australia. However, the data included in this current investigation represents people living with ME/CFS located in urbanised areas, cities, and large towns. As a result, there is little inclusion of people living with ME/CFS from rural areas, and future research may aim to target this untapped demographic. Further, a European study of participants in the United Kingdom (U.K.) (n = 918) and The Netherlands (n = 1,392) measured symptom presence and HRQoL outcomes by country [42]. Interestingly, there were significant differences between people with ME/CFS from U.K. compared with The Netherlands. Accordingly, the results of this study may need to be considered and interpreted within the context of the Australian ME/CFS population.

Conclusion

This large-scale investigation provides further evidence of the profound consequences of ME/CFS on HRQoL outcomes in people living with ME/CFS. While fatigue was a significant predictor of poor HRQoL outcomes, muscle weaknesses, cognitive impairment, pain, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular manifestations were also contributors. These data documents the heterogeneity of multisystem burden of varying symptom severity and frequencies on ME/CFS clusters; however, further analysis is required to definitively identify distinct clinical subtypes. Future research will explore the impact of comorbidities and may integrate biomedical research measures to elucidate meaningful subgroup clusters for translatable clinical outcomes with the aim to improve health and wellbeing of people with ME/CFS.
