Effects of a high-intensity functional exercise program on menopausal symptoms, physical performance, and dual-task performance in postmenopausal women: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nazlı Hilal Gözütok, Filiz Eyuboglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to examine the effects of a high-intensity functional exercise program on menopausal symptoms, physical performance, and dual-task performance in postmenopausal women.MethodsThe study included 40 postmenopausal women aged 50–65. Participants were randomized into two groups: a high-intensity functional exercise (HIFE) group and a control group. Participants’ physical performance was assessed using a short physical performance battery (SPPB), and dual-task performance was assessed using simultaneous cognitive tasks during a 4-meter walk and a 30-second sit-to-stand test. The Menopausal Rating Scale (MRS) was used to assess the severity of menopausal symptoms, and the Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Scale (MSQOL) was used to assess health-related quality of life. Assessments were conducted at the beginning of the training and at the end of 8 weeks.ResultsThe HIFE group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in SPPB total score, including 4-meter walk and chair sit-to-stand performance, compared with the control group (p < 0.05, d = 1.97). In addition, significant improvements were observed in dual-task performance in the HIFE group compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Large effect sizes were observed for the 30-second sit-to-stand test (d = 2.36) and the 4-meter walk test (d = 2.76). Significant improvements were also observed in somatic and psychological subparameters and total MRS scores, as well as in all MSQOL subparameters except sexual problems (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the groups in SPPB balance, MRS urogenital symptoms, or MSQOL sexual problems (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe study found that the HIFE program increased physical performance, reduced dual-task costs, and decreased somatic and psychological symptoms in postmenopausal women, while also improving health-related quality of life. Our study demonstrated that HIFE is an effective exercise modality for improving physical performance, reducing menopausal symptoms, and enhancing cognitive-motor performance in postmenopausal women.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT07471620, Registration date: 10/03/2026.Keywords: Postmenopause, High-intensity functional exercise, Physical performance, Dual-task performance, Menopausal symptoms

Background

Menopause is a physiological process characterized by the permanent cessation of the menstrual cycle and the end of fertility, clinically defined as the absence of menstrual bleeding for twelve consecutive months [1]. During this process, known as the postmenopausal period, the rapid decrease in estrogen levels increases the risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis. During this period, a decrease in muscle mass and an increase in fat mass result in a decline in cardiorespiratory capacity, reduced physical performance, prolonged reaction times, and balance disorders [2, 3]. These physical and physiological changes make it difficult for individuals to perform their daily activities.

Estrogen is an important hormone that supports the formation of synaptic connections in the central nervous system beyond reproductive functions and increases synaptic plasticity. Therefore, it plays a critical role in processes such as learning, memory, and executive functions [4]. With the decrease in estrogen levels during menopause, a decline in cognitive functions such as attention, executive function, and memory may be observed. This situation significantly negatively affects individuals’ dual-task performance in daily life. A dual task is defined as an individual performing a cognitive task while simultaneously performing a motor function [5]. It has been reported that decreased dual task performance leads to consequences such as weakened balance reactions, increased risk of falling, slowed walking speed, and decreased functional independence [6].

Since both physical and cognitive changes are common in postmenopausal women, non-pharmacological approaches such as exercise are gaining increasing importance. In this context, the literature reports that aerobic exercises increase cardiorespiratory capacity, resistance exercises are effective on muscle strength and functional performance, and combined exercise approaches provide more comprehensive improvements in body composition and overall physical capacity [7].

High-intensity functional exercise (HIFE) is an exercise model consisting of multi-joint, high-intensity functional exercises that holistically target multiple physical parameters such as strength, balance, agility, coordination, and endurance within a single session [8]. HIFE is based on completing as many repetitions of a series of exercises as possible within a specific time frame or completing a specific number of repetitions of a series of exercises in the shortest possible time [9]. In addition to increasing strength and endurance, HIFE also significantly enhances balance and postural control. This contributes to more effective performance of motor tasks, such as maintaining different positions and making sudden changes in direction. In addition to improving walking and balance parameters, the HIFE protocol also supports individuals’ ability to respond quickly to environmental stimuli and coordinate movements [10].

The postmenopausal period is a process that requires a holistic approach. There is a growing trend toward non-pharmacological treatments to reduce symptoms in postmenopausal women. This trend is leading to increased interest in new treatments. Studies in the literature that address dual-task performance and postmenopausal symptoms together are limited. In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of HIFE on physical performance, menopausal symptoms, and dual-task performance in postmenopausal women.

Based on the aim of the study, the following hypotheses were established:

H1: High-intensity functional exercise improves physical performance in postmenopausal women.

H2: High-intensity functional exercise improves dual-task performance in postmenopausal women.

H3: High-intensity functional exercise reduces menopausal symptoms in postmenopausal women.

Materials and methods

Participants

Our study, planned as a randomized controlled trial, was conducted at the Üsküdar University Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation Application and Research Center (ÜSFİZYOTEM). The study included postmenopausal women aged 50–65 years who had been at least 12 months past their last menstrual period, had not received hormone replacement therapy for at least 3 months, and were able to walk independently. Individuals with neurological disorders, active vestibular dysfunction, severe cardiovascular disease, lower extremity surgery or serious orthopedic injury, severe arthritis and pain affecting walking, and cognitive impairment were excluded from the study. The study was approved by the Non-Interventional Ethics Committee of Üsküdar University on November 26, 2025 (meeting no. 12) and was registered retrospectively under clinical trial number NCT07471620.

Allocation concealment was ensured using a computer-generated randomization sequence (www.randomizer.org). The allocation sequence was generated prior to participant enrollment and was not accessible to the investigators involved in recruitment or outcome assessment. Group assignment was revealed only after participants completed baseline assessments.The study’s sample size was evaluated using G*Power 3.1 [11]. Dual-task performance was defined as the primary outcome measure and was used for the sample size calculation. With 95% power and 5% error margin, it was concluded that reliable findings could be obtained with a total of 40 cases (20 cases for each group) using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test with d = 1.26, a high effect size. Twenty-two patients were included in each group considering possible dropouts.

Patients who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were randomly assigned to the HIFE group or the control group. The study flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. Our study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed written consent was obtained from the participants. Patients in both groups were evaluated before and 8 weeks after the intervention. Outcome measurements at 8 weeks were performed by a blinded researcher.

Measurements

Patients’ demographic and clinical information, including age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), hip circumference, waist circumference, duration of menopause, and date of last menstrual period, was recorded. All participants were female and postmenopausal.

Short physical performance battery

The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) was used to assess the physical performance of the subjects. It is an objective measure that evaluates lower extremity physical performance. The total SPPB score is determined by three sub-parameters: balance test, 4-meter walk test, and sit-to-stand test. Standing for 10 s in three different positions was evaluated. In the balance test, standing for 10 s was evaluated in the feet-together, half-tandem, and full-tandem positions. Walking speed was calculated in the 4-meter walk test, and the time to sit and stand up 5 times consecutively was calculated in the sit-to-stand test. Each subtest was scored from 0 to 4. The lowest possible total score was 0, and the highest possible total score was 12. High scores indicate good lower extremity physical performance [12, 13].

Dual Task Assessment

To examine the effect of adding a simultaneous cognitive task on motor task performance, assessments were conducted under both single-task and dual-task conditions. The motor task was first administered under single-task conditions and then under dual-task conditions. Participants were administered a 4-meter walking test and a 30-second sit-to-stand test for assessment. During the 4-meter walk, participants were asked to count backward from 100 in increments of 4. In the 30-second sit-to-stand test, they were asked to name fruits and vegetables associated with a specified letter. Menopausal Rating Scale

The Menopausal Symptom Assessment Scale (MRS) was used to evaluate the menopausal symptoms of the cases. The MRS is an 11-item Likert-type scale. It is used to determine the severity of menopausal symptoms. Each item on the scale is scored from 0 to 4, where 0 indicates ‘none’, 1 indicates ‘mild’, 2 indicates ‘moderate’, 3 indicates ‘severe’, and 4 indicates ‘very severe’ symptoms. The lowest possible score on the scale is 0, and the highest possible score is 44. An increase in the score obtained from the scale indicates a corresponding increase in the perceived severity of menopausal symptoms [15].

Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Scale

The Menopause-Specific Quality of Life Scale (MSQOL) is a 29-item Likert-type scale that assesses in greater detail the physical, vasomotor, sexual, and psychosocial symptoms that affect quality of life during menopause. Each question is rated on a scale of 1 to 8 points. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms in the relevant areas [16]. The Turkish validity and reliability of the study were first developed by Şahin in 2007 [17].

Intervention

High-intensity functional exercise training group

Postmenopausal women in the high-intensity functional exercise training group participated in a face-to-face exercise program three times a week for 8 weeks. Each session consisted of a 5-minute warm-up, a high-intensity functional exercise program, and a 5-minute cool-down. The HIFE program consisted of the following functional exercises: jumping jacks, high knees, butt kicks, squats, lunges, side steps, lateral band walks, jump squats, seated resistance band rows, and wall push-ups. Exercise intensity was maintained at a perceived exertion level of 15–18, as measured by the Borg scale. The first two weeks of exercises were performed in sets of 20 s for 2 sets; weeks 3–4, in sets of 20 s for 3 sets; weeks 5–6, in sets of 30 s for 2 sets; and finally, weeks 7–8, in sets of 30 s for 3 sets. The exercise protocol was designed based on high-intensity training and progressed according to the principle of progressive overload [18, 19]. Before participation, all subjects underwent routine clinical evaluation to confirm their eligibility for high-intensity exercise training. All exercise sessions in the high-intensity functional exercise training group were supervised by an experienced physiotherapist. Exercise intensity was monitored using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion scale. Participants were instructed to discontinue exercise immediately if they experienced chest pain, dizziness, severe shortness of breath, or musculoskeletal pain. No serious adverse events were observed during the intervention period.

Control group

Postmenopausal women in the control group underwent a low-to-moderate intensity home exercise program consisting of warm-up, stretching, and lower extremity strengthening exercises, three days a week, for 30–40 min per session, targeting flexibility and strength. Participants received initial supervised training on the exercise protocol, and adherence was monitored weekly by researchers via telephone calls and exercise recordings [21–23]. Subjects who were unable to complete three consecutive sessions of the home exercise program were excluded from the study. Overall adherence to the home exercise program was assessed as satisfactory based on attendance records, telephone follow-ups, and exercise recordings.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The independent samples t-test was used for intergroup comparisons of normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied for intra-group comparisons. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 27.0 software.

Discussion

The effects of this high-intensity functional exercise (HIFE) on postmenopausal physical performance, dual-task performance, and menopausal symptoms. As a result of the eight-week exercise training, the HIFE group showed significant improvements in 4-meter walk test performance, sit-to-stand test performance, and total Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) scores compared with the control group.

Dual-task performance improved, and somatic and psychological symptoms assessed during the menopausal period decreased, along with health-related quality-of-life characteristics. No difference was observed in the parameter balance of SPPB or in urogenital data evaluated by menopausal assessment performance. The lack of a significant improvement in sexual symptoms may be linked to menopausal urogenital changes due to decreased estrogen levels [20].

Muscle mass in women begins to decrease from age 30 gradually, and after age 50, the decline accelerates [21]. The literature does not clearly establish whether the cause of muscle mass decline is sarcopenia or the decrease in estrogen levels associated with menopause. However, a study by Rolland et al. indicated that muscle mass decreases by 0.6% per year after menopause [22]. Studies have shown that muscle mass loss primarily affects Type-2 muscle fibers [23]. The HIFE protocol is known to support Type-2 muscle fibers through a high-intensity exercise program [18]. Therefore, in our study, we believe that the HIFE group showed an increase in short-distance explosive strength tests, such as the 4 m walk test and the sit-to-stand test, which are sub-parameters of SPPB, after high-intensity exercise training.

In the literature, walking speed is used not only to measure speed but also to assess overall health and functionality, and is considered the “sixth vital sign” [24]. Pereira et al. reported that even a 0.1 m/s increase in walking speed results in clinical improvement [25]. In the literature, Bean et al. (2002) state that muscle strength is a more decisive factor than muscle power in assessing functional independence [26]. In our study, participants who achieved a score of 4 on the 4 m walk test reported reaching a functional independence value consistent with the walking speed threshold of approximately 1.0 m/s required for independent movement, as reported in the literature [27]. The lack of significant change in balance performance may be due to the high initial performance level and the test’s sensitivity.

High-intensity functional exercise inherently requires the simultaneous execution of multiple functions. While performing high-intensity exercise correctly, it is also necessary to engage in cognitive tasks such as time tracking and planning the next exercise. Although limited in the literature, studies have shown that HIFE exercise programs improve cognitive function in postmenopausal women. Wilke (2020) noted in his study that the HIFE group had lower dual-task costs in acute effects compared to the aerobic exercise group and that HIFE is a suitable method for improving working memory [28]. In our study, the dual-task cost in the HIFE group decreased more than in the control group in the 4-meter walk and 30-second sit-to-stand tests. Exercise-induced cognitive improvement may be associated with increased brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression and enhanced synaptic plasticity, particularly through improved connectivity between the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex [29].

In our study, somatic and psychological symptoms, as assessed using the Menopausal Rating Scale, decreased. In contrast, health-related quality of life, as assessed using the menopause-specific quality-of-life questionnaire, improved. Bailey et al. (2016) reported that exercise approaches that improve cardiorespiratory capacity enhance thermoregulation, thereby reducing hot flashes after menopause and alleviating symptoms such as night sweats [30]. Additionally, studies in the literature indicate that exercise groups activating large muscle groups at high intensity produce a greater cardiovascular pumping effect than moderate-intensity exercise, thereby increasing peripheral blood flow [31, 32]. He et al. (2022) observed in their study that high-intensity exercise increases NO release from the endothelial wall and decreases endothelin-1 release [33]. We believe that increased tissue oxygenation contributes to reduced physical symptoms, including fatigue.

The study contributes to the literature by evaluating the effects of a high-intensity functional exercise program on physical and menopausal-related outcomes in postmenopausal women, although it should be noted that participants’ prior exercise history was not considered, the intervention assessed only short-term effects, and balance assessment may have been insufficiently sensitive for this population.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered. The clinical trial was retrospectively recorded, which may affect the initial transparency of the study design. The intervention duration was limited to 8 weeks, and the lack of long-term follow-up restricts the interpretation of the persistence of effects. A baseline difference in waist-to-hip ratio was observed between groups, but this was not a primary outcome measure and is unlikely to have significantly affected the main findings. Furthermore, dietary intake was not monitored during the study, which may have influenced the results. Finally, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the HIFE program, administered three days a week for eight weeks in postmenopausal women, increased walking speed and sit-to-stand test performance, thereby improving overall physical performance. It also reduced dual-task costs, somatic and psychological symptoms, and enhanced health-related quality of life. Our study demonstrated that HIFE is an effective exercise modality for improving physical performance, reducing menopausal symptoms, and enhancing cognitive-motor performance in postmenopausal women. Future studies examining the long-term effects of HIFE, incorporating large-scale, long-term measurements, may reveal the sustainability of the gains achieved.
