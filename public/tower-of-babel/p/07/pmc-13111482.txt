How do Australian workplaces support breastfeeding mothers in 2025? A content analysis to develop a ‘Supportive Workplace Checklist’
Authors: Bianca Blanch, Amanda R Cooklin, Nicole Bridges, Lisa H Amir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFew studies have described uptake and implementation of breastfeeding friendly workplace initiatives from employers’ perspectives in Australia. Our aim was to describe how Australian breastfeeding friendly workplaces report supporting breastfeeding mothers in paid employment. We explored the characteristics and/or workplace profiles of Australian BFWs listed on a publicly available Australian Breastfeeding Association (ABA) website. Each BFW provides: a lactation space, lactation breaks and a supportive workplace culture. Based on these profiles, systematic reviews, published literature, and information on the ABA website, we created a 46-item inventory of supportive workplace factors and counted the number of factors present per profile. On 1 October 2025, we identified 138 BFWs on the ABA website, most commonly from federal government and government agencies (n = 30; 22%) and hospitals and health care service industries (18; 13%). Fifty-seven (41%) organizations published a workplace profile. Of a possible 46, the median number of supportive factors or workplace benefits was 13 (IQR: 10–17.5). Three frequently reported factors: supportive workplace culture (93%), flexible work conditions (74%), and work–life balance (70%). Workplaces demonstrated many ways to support breastfeeding mothers. Future studies should focus on better understanding employers’ perspectives of providing workplace supports and whether the lived experiences of mothers returning to work match the purpose of each workplace initiative.Keywords: breastfeeding, organizational culture, workplace, mothers, women, working, lactation rooms, maternal employment

Background

The Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion highlights the importance of building healthy public policy, creating supportive environments (both physical and social) and strengthening community action via organizations promoting good health (World Health Organization 1987). A workplace can actively promote these principles or embed systemic barriers that reduce the likelihood of employees having good health practices. Globally, an increasing number of mothers with child(ren) aged up to 14 years participate in paid employment (hereafter ‘work’). In Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries between 2008 and 2021, the rate of mothers (with a child aged 0 to 14 years) participating in paid employment increased from 64% to 71% (OECD Family Database 2023). More than half of working mothers (54%) had full-time employment, 17% had part-time employment (OECD Family Database 2023), the remaining 29% of mothers possibly worked less than part-time hours, but this statistic was not reported. Workplaces providing lactation support for mothers returning to work are consistent with the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion because it promotes the health behaviour of continuing breastfeeding, which is important for maternal [e.g. reduced risk of breast (Zhou et al. 2015, Victora et al. 2016) and ovarian cancers (Feng et al. 2014, Victora et al. 2016)] and child health outcomes [reduced risk of all-cause mortality, respiratory infections, gastrointestinal infections, sepsis, and hospitalization (Victora et al. 2016)].

The World Health Organization (WHO) and Australia’s National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) infant feeding guidelines both recommend children are exclusively breastfed until 6 months of age and to continue breastfeeding until at least 12 months (National Health and Medical Research Council 2012) or beyond 2 years (World Health Organization 2025). For a mother to maintain adequate breast milk production and support responsive feeding, they require time throughout the day to express breastmilk or breastfeed their baby in accordance with their child’s feeding cues. If the mother is working, this will include paid working hours, whether they work during the day or night. The younger the child, the more time needed to maintain their breastmilk supply (Smith and Ellwood 2011).

A recent global meta-analysis found 25% of mothers return to work whilst breastfeeding (Dutheil et al. 2021). Five global reviews identified 13 ways workplaces support (breastfeeding) mothers returning to work after parental leave: supporting work–life balance (Franzoi et al. 2024), mental and physical health (Franzoi et al. 2024), promoting job-related wellbeing and working experience (Franzoi et al. 2024), flexible work conditions (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Nurjanah et al. 2023, Brugaillères et al. 2024) and supporting breastfeeding (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Brugaillères et al. 2024, Franzoi et al. 2024). Breastfeeding support was the most common way workplaces support mothers returning to work, examples include breastfeeding policies and accommodations (Litwan et al. 2021, Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Nurjanah et al. 2023, Brugaillères et al. 2024, Franzoi et al. 2024), lactation room (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Franzoi et al. 2024), lactation breaks (Litwan et al. 2021, Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Brugaillères et al. 2024, Franzoi et al. 2024), support from co-workers/supervisors (Litwan et al. 2021, Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Brugaillères et al. 2024), childcare at workplace (Franzoi et al. 2024), participation in breastfeeding support programmes (Franzoi et al. 2024), availability of breast pumps (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021), breastfeeding education/counselling (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021), and delaying return to work until 15 weeks post-partum (Nurjanah et al. 2023). Three reviews concluded supportive workplaces are associated with longer duration of breastfeeding and prevent early introduction of commercial milk formula (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Nurjanah et al. 2023, Franzoi et al. 2024 ). Two reviews supported a more tailored approach to breastfeeding support and concluded we need to better understand how breastfeeding workplace interventions work for mothers (Litwan et al. 2021, Brugaillères et al. 2024).

We found three published measures assessing workplace support for mothers of infants returning to work (Bai et al. 2008, Greene and Olson 2008, Yeşildere Sağlam et al. 2023). Each measure was developed via a literature review, subject matter expert review, and consultation with working mothers (Bai et al. 2008, Greene and Olson 2008, Yeşildere Sağlam et al. 2023). For two measures, the researchers conducted a factor analysis (Bai et al. 2008, Yeşildere Sağlam et al. 2023). Despite the measures being developed in different jurisdictions [Turkey (Yeşildere Sağlam et al. 2023) and the US (Bai et al. 2008, Greene and Olson 2008)], the themes across the measures were similar: environment, colleagues’ support, workplace culture, and, work tasks (Greene and Olson 2008, Yeşildere Sağlam et al. 2023). No measure included the employers’ perspective.

The benefits associated with being a parent-friendly workplace include having engaged, satisfied employees, promoting staff loyalty, increasing staff retention, reducing recruitment costs, maintaining corporate knowledge, attracting talent, and less workplace discrimination, which all lead to increased productivity and profits (Bai et al. 2012, Australian Human Rights Commission 2014, Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025b). Perceived negative consequences of parent-friendly workplaces include initiatives are expensive, time consuming, inefficient, and favours specific employees, which can lead to resentment (Bai et al. 2012). All these factors need to be explored to better understand employers’ motives and the potential impacts of creating a parent-friendly workplace culture.

Australian context

In 2022, 66% of mothers with a child aged under 4 years participated in paid employment (Australian Institute of Family Studies 2022). Three quarters of mothers return to work at least 4 months post-partum (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2017). In Oceania, one in three mothers return to work whilst breastfeeding (35%), which is higher than the global rate of 25% (Dutheil et al. 2021). In Australia, it is estimated that 43% of babies aged 12 months receive breastmilk (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2023). From these statistics, it is vital for workplaces to understand how to support breastfeeding mothers returning to work to enable optimal breastfeeding practices.

Australian Breastfeeding Association Breastfeeding Friendly Workplace accreditation programme

To support breastfeeding mothers returning to work, in the 1990s, Australian Breastfeeding Association (ABA; Australia's peak body for breastfeeding information, education and support) started a national BFW accreditation programme (Eldridge and Croker 2005). To be an ABA-accredited BFW, each organization provides:

The ABA programme is unique internationally. To our knowledge, no other programme defines criteria to be a BFW; quantifies programme uptake by listing all BFWs on a public website; and celebrates BFWs by giving this public recognition (Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025b). Despite the uniqueness of the ABA BFW programme and it being at least 30 years old, this is the first study to describe the characteristics of a national Australian sample of BFWs. In this paper, we provide a snapshot of BFWs on 1 October 2025. Methods

BFW programme inclusion criteria

Each organization pays for the BFW accreditation for 2 years based on the number of employees, i.e. workplaces with a higher number of employees pay a higher price. Each workplace must align with ABA’s Code of Ethics, e.g. clause 1.14 states no enterprise, which are ‘inimical’ (harmful) to the ABA are eligible (Australian Breastfeeding Association 2023). This includes any organization who is not compliant with the International Code of Marketing of Breastmilk Substitutes, i.e. organizations that market, sell, or manufacture commercial milk formula, feeding bottles, or teats (World Health Organization 2023). Each workplace must maintain three criteria (lactation space, lactation breaks, and supportive workplace culture), which are confirmed by ABA via yearly inspections (Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025b).

Within the ABA programme, a BFW can receive best practice (BP) status if they have been a BFW for at least 2 years and demonstrate an ongoing commitment to breastfeeding support (Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025d), e.g. strengthen their lactation support policies and procedures; embed inclusive and culturally safe practices; promote breastfeeding awareness and understanding among staff; share information about breastfeeding support; and/or, improve breastfeeding facilities, policies and support over time (Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025d).

Study inclusion criteria

We counted an organization as a BFW if their name was listed on either the BFW (n = 136) or BP (n = 50) web page on the ABA website (Fig. 1; Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025b, 2025d). Forty-eight workplaces were listed on both webpages and two were listed only on the BP webpage.

Summary of Australian Breastfeeding Association accredited breastfeeding friendly workplaces: 1 October 2025.

We counted a workplace as having BP status if the organization’s name was listed on the BP web page (n = 50) or they reported the year of BP accreditation in their workplace profile (n = 29). Two workplaces were not listed on the BP webpage but reported their BP accreditation year in their workplace profile. This may be a clerical error as ABA would likely remove inaccurate information from a workplace profile before publishing it on their website.

Data availability

All data were derived from sources in the public domain, available from these websites: https://www.breastfeeding.asn.au/community-workplaces/bfw/accredited-workplaces and https://www.breastfeeding.asn.au/community-workplaces/breastfeeding-friendly-workplaces/what-best-practice.

Data extraction/activities per objective

Objective 1: Describe BFW characteristics. From the ABA website, we extracted workplace name, industry (defined by the ABA), BP accreditation (yes/no), and workplace profile available (yes/no) (Fig. 1; Supplementary File S1). We stratified these results by the existence of a workplace profile. The template for each workplace profile affirms the organization is a BFW. The rest of the profile is bespoke. It was likely written by an employee at each organization to describe the workplace to a breastfeeding-supportive audience knowing it would be published and publicly available on the ABA website.

Objective 2: Perform a workplace profile inventory listing supportive practices from the BFWs’ perspective. We created a list of supportive workplace practices informed by published research, ABA website, and a directed content analysis of workplace profiles (Fig. 2). We then counted the number of supportive factors mentioned in each profile.

Development of the 46-item workplace profile inventory

Inventory item development

To quantify how BFWs support their employees, we developed an inventory using a directed content analysis approach (Hsieh and Shannon 2005) situated within a post-positivist paradigm. This paradigm acknowledges the existence of an external reality while recognizing that findings are shaped by theoretical assumptions and researcher interpretation. We generated a framework to guide initial coding and created additional categories inductively that were not present in the literature.

Item theme

For reporting purposes, we organized the 46-items into six themes: workplace characteristics; perceived benefits of a (breastfeeding) supportive workplace; BFW accreditation criteria; parent-friendly policies; financial support; and, returning to work support.

Inventory scoring

Each item described in the workplace profile scored 1 point. One supportive factor can tick multiple items. For example, workplace #8.27 states ‘flexible work arrangements (including job share, part-time, flexible hours, and working from home).’ They scored four points: flexible work conditions, job share, flexible/reduced hours, flexible location. If a profile mentions the same item multiple times, they only score 1 point. Workplace #9.2 states they provide ‘flexible working arrangements…staggered return to work option.’ The profile scored 1 point for flexible work conditions.

Coding process, interrater reliability, and consensus

The first author (B.B.) scored each workplace profile against the inventory and recorded the result for each item in an Excel spreadsheet.

We then conducted interrater reliability (IRR) via percentage of agreement, where the number of agreed items is divided by the total sum of the items. IRR is established when there is 90%–95% agreement (Burns 2014). The second (A.R.C.), third (N.B.), and fourth authors (L.H.A.) randomly selected five profiles each and independently scored each one against the 46-item inventory. The average percentage agreement across all pairs (first author and second/third/fourth author) was 87%, indicating IRR was not achieved. We met and discussed ambiguous items with the highest number of disagreements and changed four items’ definitions. All authors then reviewed five different profiles with the updated inventory. For this second round of scoring, the average percentage agreement across all author pairs was 90%, indicating IRR was established (Burns 2014). The first author then scored the other 27 (47%) profiles alone.

We ranked the frequency of each theme and item across all workplace profiles and defined three mutually exclusive categories: highly reported [present in at least 38 (>66%) profiles]; moderately reported [present in 19–37 (33%–66%) profiles]; and rarely reported [present in up to 18 (<33%) profiles].

Supplementary workplace policy search

If the workplace profile mentioned a policy and the type of workplace support was unclear, the first author conducted an internet search on the workplace’s website for further information about the policy. This information was counted in the profile score. For example, workplace #5.7 listed a ‘Restart Grant’ policy, which provides parental leave. Parental leave was not mentioned in the profile. Consequently, we scored the ‘parental leave’ item as present in this profile. We conducted this supplementary website search for six (11%) profiles.

Supportive Workplace Checklist

We reviewed the 46-items reported in the profile inventory and removed items not describing supportive factors, e.g. number of employees (Table 1).

Data presentation

We present all information in text, tables, figures, and supplementary files. We report employee quotes from workplaces’ profiles to highlight the lived experience of working in a supportive environment from both mother and manager perspectives (where available).

Organization names

In Supplementary File S1, we list all workplaces included in this study. We did not use workplace names in this article for two reasons. Firstly, our study focuses on the support workplaces provide, we are not focusing on specific workplaces. Secondly, to reduce perceived reporting bias, since all the authors’ workplaces are currently listed as BFWs, and three have a workplace profile.

BFWs were commonly reported in the following industries: federal government and government agencies (n = 30; 22%), hospitals and health care services (18; 13%), education (16; 12%), and state government (16; 12%). Of the 57 workplaces with a profile, federal government and education each represented 18% (n = 10) followed by state government (7; 12%).

Year of BFW accreditation

Forty-eight (84%) profiles reported the year of BFW accreditation. The median year was 2013 (IQR: 2010 to 2018–2019; range: 2007–2025). Of the 31 workplaces with a workplace profile and BP accreditation, 29 (94%) reported the year of BP accreditation, the median year was 2020 (IQR: 2019 to 2021–2022; range: 2019–2024).

Twenty workplaces reported the year of accreditation and the year of best practice accreditation. The median time between the two accreditations was 8 years (IQR: 6–10; range: 3–15 years). Of note, each workplace must be a BFW for 2 years before they are eligible for BP accreditation (Australian Breastfeeding Association 2025d).

The median word count for all workplace profiles was 261 words (IQR: 195–317; range: 49–1353). Thirty profiles (53%) included a website link to find out more information about the workplace.

Profile inventory items and results

The profile inventory includes six themes across 46 items. The median number of themes reported per workplace profile was 3.5 (IQR: 2–4; range: 1–5). Four themes were highly reported (workplace characteristics; BFW accreditation criteria; parent-friendly policies; returning to work support), one theme was moderately reported (perceived benefits of a breastfeeding supportive workplace) and financial support was rarely reported.

The median number of items present per article was 13 (IQR: 10–17.5; range: 2–31). Five (11%) of the 46-items were highly reported, nine (20%) were moderately reported and 32 (70%) rarely reported (Table 3). Despite only profiling BFWs, 11 (24%) inventory items related specifically to breastfeeding support. Of those, one (9%) was highly reported, three (27%) moderately reported and seven (64%) rarely reported (Table 3).

Theme 1: Workplace characteristics

Every profile (57; 100%) reported at least one item from this theme (Table 3). Fourteen items were included in this theme. Three (21%) items were highly reported, two (14%) moderately reported and nine (64%) rarely reported. Four (29%) items were specific to breastfeeding support (Table 3).

Every profile reported they participated in a breastfeeding support programme, the ABA BFW programme. Fifteen (26%) of the 57 profiles described the number of employees. The median was 1300 employees (IQR: 200–3300; range: 55–21 000).

Eleven profiles (19%) included the proportion of males/females in their workplace. Of these, seven reported the specific proportion of females [median: 50% (IQR: 27.5%–39.5%; range: 15%–85%)] and four profiles stated which sex dominated their workplace [female dominated (n = 3; 75%); male-dominated (n = 1; 25%)].

Six profiles (11%) affirmed why they sought BFW accreditation. Across the profiles the reasons were to: support/retain/attract staff (n = 3); increase breastfeeding rates (n = 2) or promote the importance of breastfeeding (n = 1).

Three profiles (5%) included quotes from staff members, either managers or mothers. The quotes demonstrate the benefits of being a supportive workplace for mothers returning to work.

Employers’ perspective:

‘By meeting these [breastfeeding at work] challenges we can meet the health, family and cultural needs of our women which in turn provides the [workplace] with the strategic and capability benefits of optimised recruitment and retention, reduced absenteeism, greater effectiveness and productivity, whilst demonstrating our commitment to workplace diversity’. (Workplace #6.3)

‘We really see ourselves as setting a standard in regard[s] to family-friendly and female-friendly working conditions. We want to demonstrate to other companies and organizations that they won’t lose out if they provide the right conditions for women to excel in the workplace. In fact, it will be quite the opposite’. (Workplace #2.8)

Mothers’ perspectives:

‘Negotiating lactation breaks wasn’t a problem… [I knew] my choice to breastfeed would be supported. The support has made returning to work enjoyable’. (Workplace #6.3)

‘I also know having a baby isn’t going to impact my career development, and that’s really important to me.’ (Workplace #2.8)

‘They provided an information package when I notified them of my pregnancy and also are there to help with any questions’. (Workplace #1.2)

‘Due to our breastfeeding policy I have been able to return to work earlier than expected, and this benefits me as well as [workplace name]’. (Workplace #1.2)

Theme 2: Perceived benefits of a breastfeeding supportive workplace

Thirty-four (59%) profiles reported at least one perceived benefit of being a supportive workplace (Table 3). Of the six themes, this was the fourth most reported theme, demonstrating workplaces more commonly reported employee benefits of being a supportive workplace than employer benefits.

Five items were included in this theme; one item was moderately reported and four were rarely reported. The two most reported items in this category were reputation/attracting talent (n = 24; 42%) and staff retention (n = 15; 26%) demonstrating retaining and attracting people are the main motivators for a supportive workplace.

Theme 3: Breastfeeding friendly workplace accreditation criteria

Fifty-three (95%) profiles reported at least one BFW accreditation criteria (Table 3). Seven items were included in this theme. One item was highly reported (supportive workplace culture), three items were moderately reported, and three were rarely reported. Four items were breastfeeding specific items.

To be a BFW, each workplace must demonstrate three factors: lactation space, lactation breaks and supportive workplace culture. However, only 16 (28%) profiles reported all three supportive factors.

Thirty-nine (68%) workplaces exceeded at least one criterion (lactation breaks, private space and/or supportive culture). Examples of exceeding the criteria included paid lactation breaks or a lactation break policy; breastfeeding facilities available for visitors; internal committees to advance equality/inclusion in the workplace, or recognition from an external body by awarding the organization a workplace culture award, e.g. Workplace Gender Equality Agency (WGEA) Employer of Choice.

Theme 4: Parent-friendly policies

Forty-three (77%) profiles reported at least one parent friendly policy (Table 3). Of the five items, two were moderately reported (parental leave; other supportive parenting policy) and three were rarely reported. Two items were breastfeeding support items.

The most reported item was paid parental leave (33; 58%). Fourteen profiles (25%) reported the length of parental leave, the median was 18 weeks (IQR: 17-19; range: 12-26).Twelve profiles (21%) reported parental leave duration of at least 15 weeks which is associated with longer duration of breastfeeding (Nurjanah et al. 2023). Of note, this parental leave is organization-funded so is in addition to the Australian federal government paid parental leave scheme, currently up to 20 weeks per family (18 weeks for a birthing parent and 2 weeks for a partner) (Services Australia 2025).

Twenty-four (42%) profiles provide other supportive policies to parents (birthing, non-birthing, surrogate or adoptive). This was commonly partner leave, bonding leave or carer’s leave to care for children after returning to work.

Nine workplaces (16%) provide fertility/pregnancy support which may include additional leave to attend pregnancy medical appointments; a pregnancy policy to give staff relevant workplace policy information and open the dialogue to raise any concerns about their working environment whilst pregnant; additional leave to participate in fertility treatment; flexible work location options whilst pregnant; and pregnancy loss leave.

Only two (4%) workplace profiles described breastfeeding resources including breastfeeding class or information.

Theme 5: Financial support

Twelve profiles (21%) reported at least one (of three) factors of financial support for families (Table 3). All three items were rarely reported. Workplaces provide free or discounted products and services: income protection insurance; health insurance; family care costs reimbursement; flu vaccinations; skin screenings; heart health assessments; tickets to movies, shows or events; or, zoo entry.

In the six profiles (11%) mentioning salary, the focus was on pay equality (n = 3), award wages (n = 2) or eligibility of salary increases whilst on parental leave (n = 1).

Theme 6: Returning to work support

Forty-three (77%) profiles reported at least one return to work support item (Table 3). Twelve items were included in this theme. One item was highly reported; one item was moderately reported; 10 were rarely reported. One item was specific to breastfeeding support.

Almost one quarter of profiles provide childcare assistance (23%). Examples of assistance include on-site childcare, a space to care for children at work or childcare subsidy.

Discussion

The main finding of our snapshot was demonstrating the plethora of supportive practices exhibited by Australian BFWs to support employees, predominantly in the past decade. Despite focusing on BFWs alone, only one-quarter (n = 11; 24%) of the items in the 46-item profile inventory were directly related to supporting breastfeeding employees, e.g. private lactation room or lactation break. Three quarters (35; 76%) of the items were generally supportive and potentially beneficial for all employees, e.g. work-life (or work/non-work) balance, supportive culture and flexible working conditions. This finding suggests breastfeeding support is one of a myriad of common workplace supportive practices present in Australian BFWs.

Our ‘Supportive Workplace Checklist’ identified 28 supportive workplace practices which is more than double the 13 factors identified across five global reviews examining how workplaces support mothers returning to work (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021, Nurjanah et al. 2023, Brugaillères et al. 2024, Franzoi et al. 2024). For 11 factors, we could compare the frequency in which each factor was reported in the literature and reported in Australian BFWs. For four factors, the global literature reported the factor more frequently than Australian BFWs, in descending order: lactation space [global result: 65% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. our result: 60%]; lactation breaks [41% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. 35%]; breastfeeding education [27% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. 5%]; and, providing breast pumps [11% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. 0%]. Of note, 100% of Australian BFWs did provide lactation space and breaks as they were requirements of being an accredited BFW, but many workplaces did not report these factors in their profile. For the other seven (64%) factors, Australian BFWs reported the practice more commonly than the global literature: participating in a supportive breastfeeding programme [global result: 87% (Franzoi et al. 2024) vs. our result: 100%]; supportive workplace culture [54% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. 93%]; flexible work conditions [8% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. 74%]; work-life balance [12% (Franzoi et al. 2024) vs. 70%]; mental or physical health [6% (Franzoi et al. 2024) vs. 40%]; job-related well-being [10% (Franzoi et al. 2024) vs. 28%]; and, breastfeeding policy [16% (Vilar-Compte et al. 2021) vs. 19%]. This result may be an artefact of our study design where we examined the workplace profiles of BFWs, which are likely more supportive than the average workplace. These workplace supportive practices are consistent with three Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion principles: providing a supportive workplace environment, building healthy public policy (via parental leave, supportive workplace policies) and strengthening community action (via breastfeeding support and flexible work arrangements) (World Health Organization 1987). Workplaces may also use the ‘Supportive Workplace Checklist’ as a method to elicit employees’ feedback on current and future desired supportive practices. Any of these activities would likely build or increase trust and loyalty in employees.

We identified three published, validated measures that were designed to explore mothers’ experiences returning to work (Bai et al. 2008, Greene and Olson 2008, Yeşildere Sağlam et al. 2023). Our checklist is the only one, to our knowledge, designed to capture the workplaces perspective, so it complements these existing measures. We identified similar themes (i.e. supportive workplace culture and physical breastfeeding space) indicating these factors are important for both mothers and workplaces simultaneously. The next logical step would be to compare the employer perspective and employees’ lived experience of these supportive measures within the same organization. This exploration would demonstrate if the supportive practices the workplace provides is appropriate for its employees, which may vary across workplace, sector, industry and/or jurisdiction. There can be a gap between a policy being in place and uptake.

We identified scant information on organizations’ motivations to be a supportive workplace. This is concerning, because if the benefits of being a supportive workplace are unknown, workplaces may not invest in these practices. In our study, just over half (60%) of the Australian BFWs reported a perceived benefit of being a supportive workplace. This is lower than expected considering half the workplaces have been a BFW for at least 12 years (median year of accreditation: 2013), which involves renewing every 2-years. It is unlikely workplaces would renew their accreditation if they did not perceive some benefit from it. In this theme, the most reported factor was reputational benefits and/or attracting new talent (42%). However, the other four items were rarely reported (staff retention, positive work outcome, increased engagements and loyalty). It is essential to define and report quantifiable metrics over time so we can establish if and how these practices improve workplace culture and staff retention. This evidence is currently lacking in the field.

Our study is the first globally to systematically explore the characteristics of a national sample of BFWs, across industries, and describe how workplaces support breastfeeding employees, and the perceived workplace benefits of being supportive. This study celebrates and recognizes the efforts of workplaces supporting breastfeeding employees and provides a practical, self-assessment checklist for all workplaces to review their supportive practices. The ‘Supportive Workplace Checklist' can be flexibly applied to individual workplaces.

Our study has several limitations. The BFWs are not a representative sample of Australian workplaces, we restricted our sample to organizations who voluntarily sought out and paid for BFW accreditation and they represent only 0.01% of Australian employing workplaces (138/999 161) (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2024). Our raw data (i.e. workplace profiles/testimonials) were written ‘freestyle’ (free text) by an employee from each organization for the ABA website. They were not written for research purposes. As there was no consistent profile template or set of prompts, each profile likely reflects each organization’s unique values and supportive practices written for a breastfeeding-interested audience, or to align with their public profile, rather than reflecting a study-specific data collection. Our inventory and checklist scores do not reflect the level of workplace support. Longer profiles would likely mention more items, attracting a higher score, compared to shorter profiles, despite our findings demonstrating no such relationship by industry. Scores should not be compared across workplaces. Instead, scores can be used to benchmark levels of support within a workplace over time. We did not confirm the accuracy of the supportive factors mentioned in the profile. We know there was under-reporting of workplace supportive factors. Every organization in this study provides a lactation space, break and a supportive culture, yet only 28% of profiles reported all three factors. The WGEA found 68% of organizations offer paid parental leave, and 87% of workplaces pay superannuation during parental leave (Workplace Gender Equality Agency 2025), but in the profiles we found only 58% and 4% (respectively) of profiles reported this support. Profiles could over-report supportive factors, i.e. report a factor they do not provide. It is not feasible to check the accuracy of each factor listed in each profile with each organization. Our study did not purposefully seek mothers' perspectives or lived experiences of returning to work. Every supportive workplace factor scored one point in our inventory/checklist, intimating that each factor is of equal value to mothers, employers etc. Some factors will be experienced as more supportive from mothers’ perspectives than others.

Our snapshot identified three major gaps in the literature, exploring:

Employer and employees’ perspectives, in the same organization, of the uptake and execution of supportive factors available;

How mothers returning to work value the support available in their organization. For example, a non-breastfeeding mother may put less value on a lactation space and breaks. Future research can explore which factors are highly valued by mothers, and whether a myriad of disparate supportive factors is experienced as more supportive than similar support practices. For example, Vilar-Compte et al. (2021) suggests the optimal way to support breastfeeding mothers returning to work is to provide support across four resource types: physical (e.g. private lactation room); organizational (flexible breaks, onsite childcare); education (e.g. organization-wide breastfeeding education); and workplace (support from co-workers and breastfeeding policy). No Australian BFW profile mentioned providing each of these types of support. This finding suggests an important question for future research is ‘How do these supportive factors work together to enhance mothers’ experiences of support when returning to work’?

Finally, the benefits of being a supportive workplace are largely unrecognized. No systematic reviews, and few studies, focus on the employer, their motivations, experiences, and perceived benefits or negative consequences of becoming a more supportive workplace either for all employees, or breastfeeding mothers. Public recognition, reputational benefits and an increase in social capital for supportive workplaces will encourage more organizations to support all their employees.

Conclusion

Our snapshot celebrates the 138 Australian breastfeeding friendly workplaces who support mothers to continue breastfeeding after returning to work to ensure returning to work is not a barrier or reason to stop breastfeeding. We identified four common supportive factors across industries (participating in a breastfeeding support programme; work-life balance; supportive workplace culture; flexible work conditions) that would mostly support all employees. We also identified the employer’s perspective is largely missing from the field. Future research should focus on the employers’ reasons for being a supportive workplace to ensure other workplaces understand the reasons to invest in its staff.
