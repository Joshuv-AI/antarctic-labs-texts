Prevalence and Predictors of Osteoporosis Among Saudi Patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Single-Center Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Samar Alharbi, Raghad Jan, Alaa Ahmed, Abdullah Albihani, Mahmood Alattas, Renad Alrehaili, Roaa Aljohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOsteoporosis (OP), a common comorbidity in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), is characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and an increased risk of fractures. The interplay between chronic inflammation, RA medications, and other contributing factors exacerbates bone loss. In this study, we sought to estimate the prevalence of OP and identify factors associated with OP in patients with RA.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using medical record data from patients diagnosed with RA at rheumatology clinics. The collected data included demographic details, clinical history, disease activity scores, medication use, and BMD measurements. Statistical analyses were performed to assess the prevalence of and identify significant risk factors for OP in this cohort.ResultsWe included 173 Saudi patients with RA (mean age: 46.29 years; 154 women, 19 men) in the study. Mean age was significantly higher in the OP group than in the normal-BMD group. Disease duration was significantly associated with low BMD; 35.4% of patients in the normal-BMD group had disease duration <2 years, compared with only 4.3% in the OP group, whereas 50% of patients with OP had disease duration >10 years.ConclusionOP affected 26.6% of patients with RA, indicating that bone fragility is common in this population. The discovery that advanced age and disease duration are major risk factors for high-risk groups emphasizes the importance of early screening and targeted preventive interventions.Keywords: bone mineral density, dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry, inflammation, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the joints that affects 1% of the general population and impacts women more frequently than men.1,2 Chronic inflammatory illnesses are commonly associated with systemic bone loss and have a complex pathophysiology involving several interconnected systems. The relationship between inflammation and bone loss has been well documented in both clinical and experimental settings.3–6 Osteoporosis (OP) is a common extra-articular feature of RA characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD). OP affects approximately twice as many individuals with RA as the general population.7–9

The prevalence of OP among patients with RA varies with age, sex, disease activity, and geographic region but is generally higher than that in patients without RA. A previous meta-analysis estimated the global prevalence of OP in patients with RA to be approximately 33%, with an additional 60% classified as osteopenia.10 In a Norwegian registry-based study, Haugeberg et al demonstrated that patients with RA had significantly more vertebral deformities and lower BMD.11 These findings highlight the substantial role of systemic bone loss in RA, particularly in postmenopausal women. The pathophysiology of OP is complex, and chronic systemic inflammation is a key element. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin (IL)-1, and IL-6, facilitate osteoclast development through the receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK)/RANK ligand pathway, thus boosting bone resorption.12 Although long-term glucocorticoid therapy is often necessary to control disease activity, it directly disrupts osteoblast function and increases osteocyte death, resulting in reduced bone formation and increased bone resorption.13 Other contributing factors include physical inactivity due to joint pain and deformities, which limit the mechanical stimulation necessary for bone maintenance, and common nutritional deficiencies, such as vitamin D insufficiency, which are associated with lower BMD and higher RA disease activity.14

Several risk factors for OP have been identified in patients with RA. These factors include advanced age, female sex, postmenopausal status, low body mass index (BMI), elevated disease activity, extended illness duration, tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, and total dosage and duration of glucocorticoid use.15,16 Vitamin D insufficiency is particularly common in individuals with RA and leads to bone demineralization and increased inflammatory activity.14 Consequently, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) screening and early preventive measures are recommended, particularly in high-risk populations.

Given the high burden of OP in RA and the influence of demographic and clinical characteristics on BMD, each region needs to understand the local epidemiology. Therefore, we sought to estimate the prevalence of low BMD among patients with RA in Saudi Arabia and to examine its association with established clinical and laboratory risk factors. Identifying these patterns within a local context can support timely detection and inform preventive strategies to reduce fracture risk and improve long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

Study Design

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study conducted using medical records of patients diagnosed with RA. The study population comprised adult patients with RA attending the rheumatology clinics during the study period.

Study Setting and Population

The study was conducted at King Salman bin Abdulaziz Medical City (KSMC), Medina, Saudi Arabia, a secondary referral center with selected tertiary care services providing specialized rheumatology services to patients from Medina and the surrounding regions. The rheumatology clinics receive referrals from primary and secondary healthcare facilities, as well as direct consultations for complex inflammatory rheumatic diseases.

Patients with RA who attended the rheumatology clinics at KSMC between February 2025 and May 2025 were screened for inclusion. RA was diagnosed according to the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria.17

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible participants were patients aged ≥18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of RA. Patients aged <18 years and those with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Ethics Approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of KSMC (protocol code IRB 25–018, approved on March 9, 2025) and Taibah University and conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample Size Calculation

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of OP in patients with RA. Based on a previous study in Saudi Arabia by Abdulkhaliq et al, 13.5% of the patient cohort had RA with OP.18 To achieve a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 5% (type 1 error, 0.05), and a power of the study of 80% (type 2 error, 0.20), a minimum sample size of 180 participants was required. The sample size was calculated using Epi Info software (version 7.2.5.0).

Data Collection

Demographic data, including age and sex, were collected. Clinical variables included disease duration, comorbidities, 28-item tender joint count (TJC), 28-item swollen joint count (SJC), and RA-specific treatments, including glucocorticoids, conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and biologic therapies. Disease activity was defined using the Disease Activity Score 28 (DAS28) with erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).19 The patients were categorized into groups according to the DAS28: remission (<2.6), low disease activity (2.6–3.2), medium disease activity (3.2–5.1), and high disease activity (>5.1). Laboratory parameters collected as part of routine clinical care are described separately below.

Clinical and Laboratory Assessments

Baseline laboratory investigations obtained as part of routine clinical care were collected, including C-reactive protein (CRP), ESR, rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (anti-CCP), serum calcium, magnesium, uric acid, and vitamin D levels. Serum vitamin D was measured as total 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D]. All laboratory tests were performed at the hospital’s central laboratory according to standardized protocols and reference ranges. Laboratory results were interpreted using the reference ranges reported by the hospital’s central laboratory.

Bone Mineral Density Assessment and DXA Pathway

BMD was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) at the lumbar spine. DXA assessments were performed as part of routine clinical care based on individual risk assessment and were not mandated by the study protocol. DXA results were reviewed by the treating rheumatologist, and management decisions were initiated according to routine clinical practice.

Osteoporosis was diagnosed according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria using T-scores: normal (T-score > −1), osteopenia (T-score between −1 and −2.5), and osteoporosis (T-score ≤ −2.5).20

Statistical Analysis

Tests of normality were performed for numerical variables. Data were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Comparisons of numerical variables among groups were performed using one-way ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. The chi-square test and Monte Carlo test were used to compare categorical variables, as appropriate. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify predictors of osteopenia and osteoporosis among patients with RA. Two-sided p-values ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study Population and Demographic Characteristics

We initially identified 180 patients with RA; 7 were excluded due to incomplete medical records. The study flow is summarized in Figure 1. The mean age (±SD) of the patients was 46.29 (±11.44) years, and the majority of patients were women (n = 154, 89.0%). Sex distribution did not differ significantly across BMD groups (p=0.121); however, women predominated across all groups, with the highest proportion observed in the normal bone density group (95.8%), followed by osteopenia (88.6%), and OP (82.6%). The mean age increased significantly and progressively from the normal group (47.4±11.7) to the OP group (59.1±12.1; p<0.001), particularly between the normal and both osteopenia and OP groups. The BMI distribution showed no significant difference among the groups (p=0.313); smoking was rare and showed no significant association with the DXA classification. No alcohol use was reported. Comorbidities and Bone Mineral Density Status

Regarding comorbidities and low BMD, malignancy was infrequent across all groups (3.5% overall), and there were no significant differences among the groups (p=0.826). Diabetes mellitus was more common in the osteopenia group (25.3%) than in the normal and OP groups, but the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.407). Similarly, hypertension was slightly more prevalent in the osteopenia (26.6%) and OP (23.9%) groups than the normal group (12.5%; p=0.166). Thyroid disorders were more frequently observed in the OP group (26.1%) than the normal (10.4%) and osteopenia (13.9%) groups (p=0.09; Table 2). Clinical Disease Characteristics

Regarding clinical and laboratory features, RF was positive in 60.2% of the cohort, with no significant difference across groups (p=0.343). Positive anti-CCP antibodies were reported in 43.2% of patients, with no significant differences among the three groups (p=0.416). Similarly, the TJC and SJC distributions were not significantly different (p=0.797 and p=0.181, respectively), with the majority showing no joint involvement. The DAS28 values did not significantly differ among the groups (p=0.946), and over half of the patients in each group were in remission (<2.6). However, disease duration was significantly associated with low BMD (p<0.001); 35.4% of patients in the normal group had a disease duration <2 years compared with only 4.3% in the OP group, whereas 50% of patients with OP had a disease duration >10 years.

Laboratory Findings

Laboratory investigations did not differ significantly between the DXA classifications (normal, osteopenia, OP) for any measured parameter. Overall, CRP was positive in 57.8% of patients, with no statistically significant differences among groups (p=0.543). Similarly, serum calcium and magnesium levels were within a normal range in most patients (81.5% and 57.2%, respectively), with no significant group differences (p=0.290 and p=0.696, respectively). Vitamin D deficiency was common across all groups (65.3% overall), particularly in patients with OP (69.6%), without a significant difference (p=0.676). Uric acid levels were abnormal in 59.5% of the study population, with no significant differences among groups (p=0.885). Moreover, ESR values were similar across all groups (p=0.740; Table 3).

The RA and OP treatment types are shown in Table 4. Factors Associated with Osteopenia and Osteoporosis

Binary logistic regression analysis identified disease duration and age as significant predictors of low BMD (osteopenia and OP) in patients with RA (Table 5). Compared to those with disease duration <2 years, patients with a disease duration of 2–10 years had 4.18-fold higher odds of developing osteopenia or OP (odds ratio [OR]=4.183; 95% confidence interval [CI]; 1.622–10.790; p=0.003), whereas those with a disease duration >10 years had a 6.77-fold greater risk (OR=6.768; 95% CI, 2.257–20.295; p<0.001). Additionally, increasing age was a significant independent predictor, with each additional year associated with a 5.5% increase in the odds of bone loss (OR=1.055; 95% CI, 1.022–1.090; p=0.005). In contrast, vitamin D supplementation did not show a statistically significant protective effect (OR=0.655; 95% CI, 0.289–1.484; p=0.311).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we found that the prevalence of OP in Saudi patients with RA was 26.60%. A previous cross-sectional study conducted at Dr. Soliman Fakeeh Hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, examined 408 patients with RA between May 2018 and August 2020. Among those patients, 13.5% had OP; this percentage was lower than that observed in our study.18 Although both studies were conducted in Saudi tertiary centers, several differences likely account for the higher OP prevalence observed in our cohort. Our patients were, on average, older adults and had longer disease duration, both of which are well-established predictors of bone loss in RA. Differences in DXA screening indications, referral patterns, and body composition may also have contributed to this discrepancy. The previous study included a younger RA population and used a broader recruitment strategy, which may have underestimated true OP prevalence. These factors collectively explain why our OP prevalence was nearly twice that previously reported, despite similar clinical settings. Nonetheless, our findings align closely with global estimates. Meta-analysis data indicate that the global cumulative prevalence is approximately 27.6% (95% CI, 23.9–31.3%), based on 57 studies that included 227,812 patients with RA. The rates in Africa (46.1%) were significantly higher than those in the Americas (19.5%) and Europe (25.6%).10 Another global analysis revealed a slightly lower accumulated prevalence of 21.5% (95% CI, 16.5–27.0%), indicating that the studies were significantly dissimilar and that the prediction ranges varied from 1.4% to 56.1%.10 A Korean multicenter cohort found site-specific prevalence rates of 6.3% to 36.3% in the hip and 12.3% to 38.9% in the spine, depending on the anatomical location examined.21 The aggregate prevalence of patients with RA in Iran is 42.2%, with 50.7% of patients being women aged >45 years and 14.3% being women aged ≤45 years.22 Additionally, a high prevalence (50%) of OP was observed in a large Italian multicenter cross-sectional study that included 925 female patients with RA.23 Moreover, according to the fourth Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database, 39.1% of Korean women aged ≥50 years had OP, and 43.4% had osteopenia.24 In another study of postmenopausal women and men aged ≥50 years with seropositive RA in South Korea, 121 (52%) of 234 patients had OP.25

Collectively, these findings indicate a significant degree of variation among demographic groups and locations; however, our observed prevalence of 26.6% is consistent with the global average. Our findings are in accordance with international reports, despite the fact that there are discrepancies between regions, which can be attributed to the inclusion of several age categories in the study populations, the menopausal status of the participants, and the use of glucocorticoids.

The prevalence of OP in patients with RA is influenced by several factors, with the most significant being continuous inflammation, glucocorticoid and DMARD use, and diminished physical activity due to old age and disability.26 In this study, we found that older age and longer disease duration were associated with an increased risk of OP and low BMD in patients with RA. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies reporting similar findings.27,28 Many studies have found that older age is a consistent and independent risk factor for low BMD and OP in patients with RA.29 A large retrospective cohort study conducted in South Korea on male patients with RA aged >50 years found a significant association between advanced age and lower femoral neck and hip BMD. However, this association was reduced in multivariate analyses that included additional variables, such as BMI and disease activity.30 In the multicenter RA registry (KORONA), older age was independently associated with OP at all skeletal locations, along with low BMI, long illness duration, and glucocorticoid use.31 Similarly, Hauser et al found statistically significant relationships among age, menopausal length, and lower BMD in female patients with RA, highlighting the critical role of aging in bone fragility.32 Another study published in 2024 discovered that older age was a significant risk factor for OP and fragility fractures in RA, even after controlling for the inflammatory burden and treatment effects.33,34

Patients with RA who have a long disease duration are more likely to develop OP and concomitant fractures. Lindner et al demonstrated that the prevalence of OP in patients from a German RA registry more than quadrupled after 5 years of the disease, increasing from 1.4% to 3.7%. This finding demonstrates a clear trend in bone loss over time.34 Additionally, comprehensive reviews and registry analyses have validated that bone mass generally diminishes with prolonged RA duration, even with advanced DMARD therapy, and that the risk escalates over several years.35 Moreover, prolonged disease duration is an independent predictor of osteoporotic fractures in patients with RA, as indicated by a community-based cohort study in Sweden (n=1,928). This was particularly evident in individuals with a disease duration >5 years. Conversely, fracture rates were not substantially elevated in individuals with early RA (<1 year) compared to the general population.36

Choi et al demonstrated that the FRAX criteria identified disease duration, age, glucocorticoid dose, and BMI as independent risk factors for fractures in patients in a comparable study.21 This demonstrates a clear trend in bone loss over time. These consistent data confirm that disease duration is a substantial risk factor for OP in RA, emphasizing the importance of early identification and extended skeletal monitoring. Although vitamin D deficiency was common across all groups, particularly in patients with OP (69.6%), it was not statistically significant. Patients with RA typically have vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency, which is strongly associated with a lower BMD and an increased risk of OP. Observational studies and meta-analyses have shown significantly lower serum 25(OH)D levels in patients with RA than in healthy controls, with deficiencies related to increased disease activity and disability, both of which promote bone loss.37 In our study, vitamin D supplementation showed a statistically significant difference (p=0.041), with lower supplementation rates among patients with OP than in the normal group; however, it did not show a statistically significant protective effect in the binary logistic regression analysis.

The role of vitamin D in preventing glucocorticoid-induced OP and reducing the fracture risk in inflammatory rheumatic diseases, including RA, is further supported by expert evaluations.38 However, further studies are required to confirm this association.

We did not find any significant association with the development of OP in patients with RA, nor with the presence of RF or anti-CCP antibodies. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies; large tertiary care cohort studies have indicated no significant correlation between the prevalence of OP and RF or anti-CCP positivity after adjusting for age, sex, disease duration, or treatment factors.32 Serological status, particularly the presence of RF and anti-CCP antibodies, has been studied as a potential independent risk factor for decreased BMD in patients with RA. Although some studies have reported an association, particularly with anti-CCP, the data for RF are less reliable. In early treatment-naïve RA, anti-CCP positivity, particularly at elevated titers, has been independently correlated with osteopenia at the femoral neck and Ward’s triangle. Even after controlling for age, sex, and illness duration, RF showed no independent association in these analyses.39

Our study found no association between RA disease activity and an increased risk of OP, consistent with a previous Saudi study.18 Hafez et al examined patients with RA and found that although changes in BMD were present, they did not consistently correspond with clinical disease activity, as assessed by the DAS28.40 Similarly, another study found that OP or decreased BMD, rather than higher disease activity or joint damage, was associated with longer symptom duration in patients with early RA.41

In a South Korean cohort of male patients with RA aged ≥50 years, multivariate analysis revealed that DAS28-ESR >3.2 was an independent predictor of OP (OR=3.85, p=0.032), along with low BMI (≤22 kg/m2).30 Another Romanian study of postmenopausal women with RA found that a reduction in lumbar spine and femoral BMD was significantly correlated with elevated disease activity (DAS28 score), physical disability, and advanced RA stage.42 Moreover, Ketabforoush et al demonstrated that increased DAS28 scores and CRP levels were independently associated with reduced BMD in patients with early RA (<1 year of disease), highlighting that inflammation induces early bone loss, regardless of disease duration.22 The inconsistent results of the relationship between disease activity and BMD status in RA are most likely due to methodological and biological heterogeneity, which includes differences in study designs, disease activity definitions (single-timepoint versus cumulative measures), patient populations (age, sex, menopausal status, glucocorticoid exposure, comorbidities), site-specific BMD assessment (lumbar spine versus hip), and insufficient control for confounding factors, such as vitamin D status or physical activity. Furthermore, the evolution of RA therapeutic strategies, such as early diagnosis, treat-to-target methodologies, and widespread use of DMARDs and biologics, can rapidly reduce inflammation, potentially alleviating bone loss and obscuring correlations in modern populations.

Based on our findings, if the prevalence of OP in our Saudi patients with RA is 26.6%, it indicates that just over one in four patients in this population already meets the diagnostic criteria for OP. This represents a substantial burden, particularly given that RA cohorts often include younger individuals than the general OP population. When compared with the prevalence rates in the general Saudi population, typically lower in younger age groups, and in RA populations in other countries (often ranging from 20 to 50%), this figure suggests a significant level of skeletal fragility. Clinically, this underscores the importance of incorporating routine OP screening, risk stratification, and preventive strategies in RA management. From a public health perspective, it signals a considerable risk of future fractures, associated morbidity, and increased healthcare utilization. We recommend that routine BMD measurements and other factors influencing bone health be included in RA treatment, especially in older patients and those with long disease duration. Prompt diagnosis and treatment will prevent associated problems, such as fractured bones and disabilities.

This study has several strengths. It is one of the few Saudi studies to examine OP risk in patients with RA, offering valuable regional data on the prevalence and associated factors. The use of standardized diagnostic criteria for RA and objective BMD measurements by DXA enhances the reliability of the findings. Additionally, a broad range of demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables was examined, enabling a comprehensive assessment of potential associations.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to determine temporal or causal relationships between risk factors and OP. Single-timepoint measures of disease activity may not accurately capture cumulative inflammatory burden. The study did not include a healthy control group; therefore, direct comparisons with the general population could not be performed within the same setting. Additionally, the study was conducted at a single center, which potentially limits the generalizability of the findings. Certain lifestyle and clinical factors known to influence BMD—such as cumulative glucocorticoid dose, detailed fracture history, prior bone-directed therapy, hormone replacement therapy status, menopausal status, calcium intake, and physical activity levels—were not fully captured, allowing for residual confounding.

Conclusion

In this single-center cross-sectional study, 26.6% of patients with RA had OP, indicating that bone fragility is common in this population. Longer disease duration and advanced age were independently associated with lower BMD in adjusted analyses, highlighting the need for early screening and specialized prevention measures for high-risk groups. While causal relationships cannot be inferred due to the study design, these findings support the inclusion of routine bone health assessments in the holistic treatment of RA to reduce fracture risk and improve long-term outcomes.
