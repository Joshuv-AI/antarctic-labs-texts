The struggle of dealing with uncertainty: a qualitative, phenomenological study about how Dutch novice physical therapists experience their transition from student to professional
Authors: B L H Biemans, N Koenders, R A de Bie, J M Sieben, T J Hoogeboom
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explain how Dutch novice physical therapists experience their transition from student to physical therapist in private practice.MethodsA qualitative, phenomenological study was performed in The Netherlands to collect personal experiences from novice physical therapists who graduated <1 year ago. Data were collected with semi-structured interviews and analyzed using interpretative phenomenological analysis.ResultsThe transition experience from student to novice physical therapist is a personal, complex, and context-dependent phenomenon. Sixteen novice physical therapists (of whom 10 were still working in private practice) said their transition was liberating, stressful, insecure, unexpected, chaotic, and challenging. The variety in experiences occurred from the fact that the impact of an experience varies from one individual to the other, depending on their previous (life) experiences, uncertainty tolerance, coping, and personal and professional environment. Four overarching themes emerged: (1) ‘Suddenly, I was on my own’, (2) ‘I was unprepared for the hassle and demands of clinical practice’, (3) ‘I couldn’t make the impact I expected’, and (4) ‘I had to find a new me’.ConclusionsA novice physical therapist’s transition is a context-related phenomenon, unique for each individual and determined by individual experiences and coping strategies. Novice physical therapists described a feeling of ‘faking’: they had to pretend to know what they were doing [while treating a patient] while in reality, they felt like they did not. For some novice physical therapists, a primary reason to leave the profession.Keywords: Transition, novice physical therapists, experiences, uncertainty tolerance

Introduction

In health professions, the transition from student to healthcare practitioner has been described as a challenging and overwhelming period, often associated with substantial levels of stress [1–3]. Duchscher defined three different stages of transition (doing, being, and knowing) and constructed a theoretical framework for the Transition Shock in novice nurses [1]. The Transition Shock framework presents the professional role transition experience as a nonlinear process where the novice nurse engages in a professional role for the first time and is confronted with physical, intellectual, emotional, developmental, and sociocultural changes [4]. According to Duchscher, the experiences of a transition shock often arise during the first year of practice [1]. Going through a rough transition period can lead to a loss of professional competence, patient care quality [1,5], and job dissatisfaction of novice healthcare professionals [6]. The latter can even lead to them quitting their job [5,6], which is problematic because the healthcare system already is under pressure due to the aging population [7] and the demand for physical therapists will soon exceed the growth of supply [8]. For this reason, it is necessary to provide novice physical therapists a smooth transition towards their professional career, to minimize dropout rates and relieve pressure on the healthcare system.

The first year of practicing is experienced as a crucial period for novice physical therapists to develop their competencies [5]. This period of learning, identity formation, and role transition can be challenging since novice physical therapists must overcome a shift in caseload volume, case complexity, and accountability, and need to modify their expectations about practicing their new profession [3]. Especially in private practice settings, novice physical therapists may feel not sufficiently prepared because the setting of a private practice expects them to operate independently, often with minimal support of colleagues [6,9,10]. Since the support of colleagues is found to be a crucial determinant for growing self-efficacy, work-readiness, and facilitating the transition, this feeling of unpreparedness is understandable [3,9,11]. Additionally, internship supervisors often support their physical therapy interns assuming specific work duties (such as administration tasks) or by allowing interns to spend more time with the patient than would typically be reimbursed, meaning novice physical therapists are not fully prepared to work autonomously after graduation [3]. Starting in practice, novice physical therapists expect themselves to perform at the same level as their more experienced colleagues, which causes insecurity [3,12]. Along with having to deal with greater independence, responsibility, and managing changes in personal and professional identity, the transition can have a great impact on the lives of novice physical therapists [3]. As a result, they start doubting their own qualities, competence, identity, and even their choice to become a physical therapist [3,5]. All together, during transition novice physical therapists often experience feelings of insecurity, stress, shock, and even burn-out [3,13,14].

Although there is a growing body of literature on the transition experiences of novice physical therapists [2,3,5,9–12], there are several knowledge gaps. First, previous studies primarily focused on employed physical therapists, while largely ignoring the experiences of the ones who leave the profession [2,3,5,9–12]. Exploring the perspectives of both groups will help to gain a more complete picture of what such transitions look like. Second, most studies exploring transition experiences employed focus-groups or surveys. We believe that in-depth interviews with rigorous analysis will further increase our understanding of how novice physical therapists experience their transition. This understanding might give us new insights into how to adequately prepare physiotherapy students for the currently demanding healthcare system and decrease the amount of novice physical therapists leaving the profession. Therefore, the aim of this study is to explain how Dutch novice physical therapists experience their transition from student to physical therapist in private practice.

Methods

Study design and research paradigm

An interpretative phenomenological study was performed to explain the lived experiences of Dutch novice physical therapists during their transition from student to physical therapist [15]. Standards for reporting qualitative research were followed [16] and a completed version of the checklist is provided in Appendix A. The Radboudumc Ethical Committee (dossier number 2022-13674) judged that this study does not fall under the scope of the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). This study adhered to the general principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. All participants signed informed consent for both study participation and publication of coded participant characteristics.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The research team consisted of five researchers of whom four had a background in physical therapy. The interviews were performed by two researchers (BB and NK), both certified physical therapists and researchers. BB experienced an overwhelming transition as a novice physical therapist and left the profession after three years of working in a private practice to start the master Human Movement Sciences. NK experienced a pleasant transition by continuing to work in the hospital after his internship and combining this with an MSc. TH works as an assistant professor and experienced an insecure and unexpected transition, as a result, he left the profession within the first year of practice and he continued on as a researcher. JS, movement scientist and educationalist, works as a (qualitative) researcher in the fields of physical therapy and education but is not a practitioner. She contributed with an outsider perspective to the discussions. RB has worked as a physical therapist but became more and more convinced that daily practice did not deliver the answer needed to enhance practice so pursued further studies and a research career in the field of physical therapy.

Sampling strategy

Participants were selected through purposeful sampling in the first stage and snowball sampling was used for participants with specific traits in a second stage. Sampling purposefully provided a wide variety of novice physical therapists ranging in age, gender, months of working experience, university attended, pre- and/or post-education, and employment status.

To adequately address the research goals and to provide comprehensive transition experiences, both novice physical therapists who were working in a private practice and those who left the profession were included. The latter group was included to prevent survivorship bias. Notably, our selection criteria specifically targeted individuals engaged in private practice settings to reflect: (a) the majority of physiotherapy graduates’ career trajectories within this domain (for more context regarding Dutch private practices, please see Appendix B) and (b) since previous literature suggests that novice physical therapists feel unprepared in private practice context, as they need to operate independently, often with minimal support of colleagues [6,9,10]. Finally, we aimed at sampling a heterogenous group of participants in terms of lived experiences, perspectives, and visions.

Participants were eligible if they graduated <1 year ago, work(ed) in a private practice, and had a maximum work experience of 12 months. We chose this cut-off, as previous research suggests that the first year of practice has the greatest impact on the transition of a health care professional [1,5]. Participants were recruited through professional social media accounts, universities of applied sciences in the Netherlands (offering physical therapy curricula), Dutch regulatory body for physiotherapy (KNGF), and networks of the researchers. Potential participants received study information by email, followed by an informed consent and a short questionnaire including age, contact information, work experience and duration, graduation date, university, and qualifications, through Castor electronic data capture [17].

Data collection and processing

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted between April and July 2022 and took ∼1 h. While interviews were audio-recorded, field notes were taken, and afterwards transcribed verbatim by a third party. An interview guide (see Appendix C) provided lead- and possible follow-up questions. Four pilot interviews were conducted, reviewed, and analyzed, but not included for data analysis, by two researchers (BB and NK). Subsequently, minor changes were made to the interview guide, and consensus about the use of the interview guide was ensured. Researchers continued sampling and collecting data until no new concepts arose from the interviews, also known as data saturation [18].

Data analysis

Since the goal of this study was to explain the individual experiences of novice physical therapists about their transition from student to physical therapist, data were analyzed with interpretative phenomenology analysis which strives to elicit rich, detailed, and first-person experiences of the phenomena [15]. Interpretative phenomenology analysis focuses on particular experiences of the individual and generalization can only be done by careful examination of the experience on a case-by-case basis. Findings were aggregated and analyzed using ATLAS.ti software (version 9.1.6 Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin). This was done in six steps [15], where steps one to five were performed independently by the data analysts (BB and NK):

Individual case analysis: a transcript was analyzed by reading, re-reading, while simultaneously listening to the recording.

Techniques to enhance trustworthiness

To enhance credibility, member checking was performed by sending a summary of the interview to the participant for approval or adjustment of the interviewers’ interpretation. Credibility was further improved by engaging in a phenomenological attitude; meaning the research team maintained a continuous and systematic reflection of research question, analysis procedure, sampling strategy, characteristics of the participants, and the researchers’ individual experiences and prejudices. Lastly, transferability was optimized and improved by providing thick descriptions of context and study results.

Results

Participants

The 16 novice physical therapists included in this study graduated from four different universities of applied sciences. They were interviewed between two and fourteen months after graduation, and their working experience ranged from two to ten months. Of the 16 participants, ten were working in a private practice, five had left the physical therapist profession, and one had transitioned to physical therapy in secondary care. Phenomenon under study

The transition from student to novice physical therapist is a personal, complex, and context-dependent phenomenon. Four themes emerged from the data analysis: (1) ‘Suddenly, I was on my own’, which illustrates experiences of freedom, autonomy, insecurity, a perceived lack of knowledge, and the need to pretend; (2) ‘I was unprepared for the hassle and demands of clinical practice’, which illustrates experiences of chaos and unexpected responsibilities; (3) ‘I couldn’t make the impact I expected’, which illustrates reflecting on individual practice and doubts about the profession; and (4) ‘I had to find a new me’, which illustrates self-reflection on professional and personal identity.

Theme 1: Suddenly, I was on my own

Novice physical therapists noted that supervisors were always present during their internships, either inside or just outside the room, and were someone they could rely on. Upon beginning their physical therapy careers, they suddenly found themselves having to rely solely on their own expertise. This was described by some as a positive experience, while others talked negatively about it.

Some participants expressed a sense of relief about being left alone with their patients and being solely responsible for their care. The transition gave them a sense of confidence and independence because, finally, there was no supervisor to judge or criticize their decisions. This resulted in feelings of freedom and autonomy.

“[Working without supervision] does give a feeling of freedom and trust. That if something happens or something goes wrong, you can try and solve it yourself without immediately being reprimanded or something.” (Participant 15)

On the other hand, others found working without supervision to be stressful because it evoked feelings of uncertainty; no-one else was present to confirm or rebut their decisions. These novice physical therapists felt that patients expected a high standard of treatment and a direct solution for their pain, which they believed they could not provide.

“During my internship, at some point, I did have this feeling that yes, I was making mistakes, but it was not my responsibility, in the sense that I could fall back on someone. This isn’t the case anymore. Now I don’t have someone to fall back on.” (Participant 05)

Novice physical therapists who were concerned about their own expertise and capabilities felt as if they had, figuratively speaking, lost their knowledge and experienced a constant urge to refer back to the specialist or ask colleagues for confirmation. Moreover, some were afraid of missing red flags or working unsafely. Because of this uncertainty, in combination with not having a back-up to confirm their actions, novice physical therapists felt they needed to put on a façade for their patients, pretending they knew what they were doing, while in reality they felt unsure.

“You’re immediately pushed out of your comfort zone. In the beginning, it’s very scary. You also have to provide patients with information straight away. Normally, your internship supervisor could fix that up for you. The first week, I often found myself thinking: ‘I feel like I’m just winging it here, what happened to my knowledge?’” (Participant 15)

[In answer to the question: ‘What do you do when you’re with a patient and you don’t actually know what to do?’]: “Yes, well, you fake it till you make it.” (Participant 14)

Theme 2: I was unprepared for the hassle and demands of clinical practice

The transition from student to novice professional physical therapist represented a period of hassle and organizing, further complicated by time-management issues. It was also described as messy and chaotic because of the requirements of starting work; for instance, requesting official registrations and organizing pension contributions.

“We were not given any explanations on how to proceed once we’ve graduated […]. We were kind of just released out into the world and told: ‘Here’s your diploma. Good luck figuring it all out!’. So that can make the transition [from student to professional] very confusing, especially if you’re not sure that you actually like the work at this point. I thought that made the transition difficult.” (Participant 13)

During the transition, some novice physical therapists realized they had been sheltered by their supervisor during internships as they had not been responsible for tasks beyond patient care. Time management was a new challenge for them because as an intern they could spend more time with a patient if needed, finishing the treatment or administrative work while their supervisor took care of the next patient. Novice physical therapists noted a gap between the competences needed as an intern and those needed as an independent physical therapist. Ancillary tasks described as unfamiliar were: (1) navigating the (unknown) administration system; (2) making time for administrative tasks; (3) working in a 25- or 30-min schedule; (4) writing letters to specialists; (5) calling general practitioners; (6) answering questions about insurances; and (7) referring to other healthcare disciplines.

“There was so much to do. […] In a way, everything that happens behind the scenes as a physiotherapist, things that I was not necessarily told about during my internship, all of that I suddenly had to do. And I thought it was all a bit much. […] It caused stress, which in turn made me feel guilty towards the patient. […] Yeah, that’s no fun.” (Participant 16)

Due to the general time shortage in private practice, the novice physical therapists felt uncomfortable interrupting colleagues to ask for help, whether during their treatment of another patient or during their breaks. The participants highlighted time constraints as a significant stressor, which was further compounded by the requirement to attend meetings or complete a treatment in as few sessions as possible because of insurance requirements. Additionally, some felt insecure and inexperienced about meeting the expectations imposed both by themselves and their workplace. However, most novice physical therapists eventually managed to cope with the demands of clinical practice by establishing a routine and becoming familiar with the tasks through trial and error.

Theme 3: I couldn’t make the impact I expected

Novice physical therapists who left the profession felt they were unable to help patients in the ways they had expected. Two reasons were mentioned for this: (1) not being able to spend more time with a patient, which they believed impacted the quality of care, and (2) uncertainty about their influence on patient outcomes after realizing recovery is determined by multiple (external) factors. Both reasons contributed to doubts about the profession and job dissatisfaction.

[In answer to the question: ‘What experience led you to no longer want to be a physical therapist?’] “I think it was what we were talking about just now, what you said. About how [health care] is organized. That what you really believe would be good for people…is not something you can actually give them.” (Participant 12)

“What I experienced when working in a private practice– and that is also the reason I left – is that you have so little time for patients. It is so much like assembly-line work, and there is so much administration, that I had no energy left to do anything extra. […] Lots of administration, a fully packed working schedule. Little time to provide any extras. That was what I minded most, that as a healthcare provider, you find yourself saying: ‘I’m sorry, our time is up.’ […] That was something I really struggled with.” (Participant 10)

As mentioned in the first theme, novice physical therapists frequently experienced a sense of uncertainty and inadequacy, leading them to resort to ‘pretending’ or ‘faking’ their knowledge; however, they soon realized that their treatment was effective because their patients improved over time. This positive reinforcement increased the self-confidence of most participants, who subsequently felt that occasional uncertainty about what to do was acceptable.

“In your communication towards patients, you notice it is really important to create a feeling of trust. You don’t necessarily have to show that you don’t know what you’re doing, because at that moment you are doing what you think is best. You have such a good basis that you couldn’t really do anything really wrong anyway.” (Participant 02)

Some novice physical therapists were confused by the fact that patients improved over time, finding it difficult to credit this improvement to their own efforts due to ‘faking’ their knowledge, which they believed was unfair to the patient. They questioned whether the improvement was the result of natural recovery, placebo effects, or the provided intervention. Some participants stated that they expected to have a greater impact on patient recovery and that this anti-climactic experience contributed to their decision to leave the profession.

[In answer to the question: ‘Did the feeling of faking contribute to you quitting your job as a physiotherapist?’] “Yes. I also felt that it was not fair to the patients. The fact that I was thinking: ‘They don’t know it, but I’m just winging it.’ And I noticed that a lot of my peers who had also just started working…were saying that in the first few months, you just have to fake it, and at some point you will get it… Even that gave me an unpleasant feeling.” (Participant 14)

“Not that I would bluff my way out of it, saying: ‘Oh, it’s clearly this [physiotherapeutic diagnosis]’ […] It could have been a hundred things, and I may have just tried one thing, and maybe that will work, but at the end of the day… it’s that you really don’t know for sure… Did I actually make a difference, or would it have solved itself anyway?” (Participant 13)

Lastly, novice physical therapists had different expectations about the challenges of clinical practice. Some experienced practice as more challenging than their internship because of more complex cases; however, some stated practice was easier than expected because they felt like a personal trainer rather than a physical therapist, or felt like a masseur when they were ‘just rubbing a lower back’.

“But then, there you are, I don’t know, pouring a glass of milk, while you actually studied physiotherapy for four years. You know? […] It just happens too often, to make a long story short, that you’ve got Mrs Janssen, 80 years old, and she should flex her knee ten times, and then we’re done, and you find yourself thinking: ‘Anyone could do this.’” (Participant 12)

Theme 4: I had to find a new me

As students, the participants did not consider their professional identity as it was easy to copy that of their supervisor, but as novice physical therapists they noticed a shift in the ways that patients, colleagues, and experts communicated with them. They were now considered experts in the field, which they had never been before, making them reflect on their own identity. Central to this transition was the exploration of how to present oneself as a professional while adjusting to a new rhythm in their professional and personal lives. This process raised questions, such as ‘Who am I as a physical therapist?’ and ‘What is my expertise?’.

“What I find typical [of the transition] is this sense of searching. Searching for your sense of your own value, and for your own plan of action. And figuring out what kind of physiotherapist you are. […] But also organizing your life around your work, because you really do have to adjust your personal life to accommodate your working life. I found that to be a challenge too.” (Participant 03)

Some participants said it was challenging to identify a specialism and define the competencies in which they wanted to become proficient. In addition to discovering their professional identity, they also had to modify their personal lives. Juggling busy patient schedules, adapting to new roles, and taking on additional responsibilities left many novice physical therapists feeling fatigued during the transition, which forced them to decline social activities and/or sports activities.

“I was really exhausted in the beginning, to the point that on a Friday night, I wouldn’t even feel like going to see friends, or going out, or whatever. And I exercised less. I used to be really fanatical about it, but I didn’t have the energy for it anymore.” (Participant 05)

The participants who felt insecure about their new role as a physical therapist described how their feelings of doubt and the ‘quest for a new me’ particularly influenced their professional and personal lives.

“Especially when [the burden of transition] became really difficult, I would socially isolate myself more, which meant that I also didn’t get that relaxation from my private life anymore. If at some point you notice that you don’t enjoy it, or that you feel insecure, or incompetent, or you find yourself wondering whether this is what you want, or whether you’re doing it right, well, these were not really times when I wanted to see my friends, or have them ask me how I was doing, or how things were at work.” (Participant 16)

Discussion

The findings from this study provide insights into how novice physical therapists experienced their transition from student to professional. Four themes emerged: (1) ‘Suddenly, I was on my own’; (2) ‘I was unprepared for the hassle and demands of clinical practice’; (3) ‘I couldn’t make the impact I expected’; and (4) ‘I had to find a new me’. These themes reflect a wide variation of experiences, from joyful and liberating to stressful and challenging, demonstrating that the transition from student to physical therapist is not a universally shared phenomenon; rather, it appears to be context- and person-dependent.

By collecting individual transition experiences, we found that the impact of a specific experience or feeling could vary from one individual to another. For example, feeling the need to ‘fake’ or ‘pretend’ during a treatment could be experienced as a heavy burden by some novice physical therapists, while others viewed it more positively. Those participants who had left the profession described this feeling of ‘faking’ or ‘pretending’ as a source of guilt, which could be linked to imposter syndrome, defined as ‘a behavioral health phenomenon described as self-doubt of intellect, skills, or accomplishments among high-achieving individuals’ [19]. By contrast, the novice physical therapists who remained believed that ‘faking’ their knowledge was a necessary part of their job. When their ‘pretended’ treatment led to improvements in the patient’s condition, some participants felt a sense of accomplishment in helping their patient, while others felt confused and doubted their impact on the treatment. It is not surprising that an important determinant of physical therapist job satisfaction is ‘helping people’ [20]. In our study, participants who had left the physical therapy profession felt they could not help patients as well as they had expected, citing reasons, such as time constraints, patient expectations, and the perceived need to ‘fake’ their knowledge. This new insight adds to the existing literature on the transition experiences of novice physical therapists.

Previous studies on the turnover of healthcare professionals have applied the job-embeddedness theory, which authors have deemed a valuable perspective to evaluate employee retention in this context [21,22]. The job-embeddedness theory comprises three main aspects: links, fit, and sacrifice, which predict the intent to leave the job and explain the discrepancy between job satisfaction, organizational commitment, job alternatives, and job searches [21]. Our findings show parallels with the main aspects of the job-embeddedness theory. First, ‘links’ are described as the formal or informal connections between a person and institutions or other people that influence a person’s ideas about the job [23]. The novice physical therapists did not mention extensive formal or informal links with their job yet as they only recently graduated. More importantly, they spoke about their struggles to build meaningful links within their organization, as most of their time they worked independently in their own treatment room [3,24]. Second, ‘fit’ is described as the compatibility with an organization or the environment, where it is important that an employee’s personal values, career goals, and plans must complement the goals of the organization, as well as fit their job knowledge, skills, and abilities [23]. In the present study, the novice physical therapists who left the profession were unable to cope with the feeling of ‘faking’ their knowledge and skills, leaving them doubting their abilities. Additionally, we found that novice physical therapists can feel unprepared for their chosen profession [3,10,25], experience struggles with their professional and personal identity [5,13], and feel like they could not make the impact they expected. Third and finally, ‘sacrifice’ is described as the perceived cost of material or psychological benefits that may be forfeited by leaving a job [23]. The novice physical therapists described the great pressure and hassle (time management, managing one’s own agenda, low salary, administrative tasks) of working in private practice [10], which meant the decision to switch, for example to secondary care, was easily made. Such a switch would not feel like a sacrifice as it would likely mean a clearer pension plan, more interaction with colleagues, and/or interesting side projects [6]. In summary, from the perspective of the job-embeddedness theory, one might conclude that novice physical therapists have rather weak links with, a limited fit to, and a low sense of sacrifice regarding their job when working in a private practice, which could potentially drive staff turnover in private practices [26]. From a positive angle, the links, fit, and sacrifice have the potential for improvement, providing an opportunity to retain novice physical therapist staff in private practices.

The transition from student to novice physical therapist is a context-related and personal process involving positive and negative experiences. Positive aspects of the transition included feelings of freedom, autonomy, success, establishing a professional identity, building self-confidence, and working in a safe environment. On the other hand, the negative experiences associated with the transition were feelings of stress, chaos, isolation, doubt, fatigue, not being able to align personal characteristics with a professional identity, and, as mentioned earlier, feeling the need to ‘pretend’ or ‘fake’. This study confirms the existing literature that the transition period can significantly impact the professional and personal well-being of new graduates [5]. Additionally, other studies also showed that novice physical therapists experience feelings of insecurity, uncertainty, anxiety, and unpreparedness during their transition, and that the overall transition experience is influenced by the specific context in which it occurs [1–3,9,10,12]. When entering practice, novice physical therapists must adjust their expectations of their capabilities as graduates [3], including their skills in autonomous clinical reasoning, caseload management, administration, communication, and interpersonal interactions [10]. Novice physical therapists will manage double the caseload they had as students, often comprising more complex cases [2,9]. These factors explain the sense of unpreparedness experienced by many novice physical therapists as they transition into practice as new graduates.

We found that physical therapy students can feel that their internship supervisors shelter them from the actual workloads and tasks expected of them as novice physical therapists, which aligns with previous studies of physical therapists [3], nurses [27], and occupational therapists [28]. Although feeling unprepared can cause stress, doubt, and job dissatisfaction [2,3,5,6], not all novice physical therapists experienced the transition negatively. The transition could also result in positive feelings, including freedom, growing self-confidence, and personal development, as previously documented [2,5,12]. In contrast with previous studies, we found that novice physical therapists experiencing uncertainty, due to a lack of knowledge and/or affirmation, perceive the practice of their clinical skills as ‘pretending’ or ‘faking’. Coping strategies vary among individuals and are closely related to one’s tolerance of uncertainty, which is the extent to which a person can tolerate the probability, ambiguity, and complexity of a situation [29]. The individual’s perception of an uncertain situation is determined by their response, which can be positive or negative. For example, individuals who perceive an uncertain situation as a threat and start to doubt themselves would want to avoid the perceived difficulty, while others might interpret the uncertain situation as an opportunity for growth, which stimulates their curiosity to seek more information and confront the challenge. If we are able to help physical therapy students to realize that uncertainty is an inherent feature of their future job, they may be less likely to make decisions out of fear, doubt, or stress. Moreover, this realization might enhance their humility towards practice, a trait associated with expert physical therapists [30].

The struggle that novice physical therapists experience during their transition into an autonomous healthcare professional is well documented, as it is for other health professions, such as occupational therapists, doctors, and nurses [2]. Although novice healthcare professionals share several challenges, including a lack of self-confidence, feeling unprepared, high workloads, and having to adjust expectations, one topic might be unique for physical therapists. We found that not being able to cope with or tolerate the constant uncertainty in combination with the high workload might cause novice physical therapists to leave the profession. It is important to realize that physical therapists are prone to burnout, although none of our participants reported having had one despite feeling high levels of stress during the transition period. High burnout and turnover levels have been reported for physical therapists in several countries, including Canada, Australia, and the United States of America [13,31–34]. Supporting novice physical therapists to increase their resilience during their education, as well as through their transition, may reduce negative transition experiences [35] and prevent the early attrition of physical therapists [33].

Implications for clinical practice

Our study has several clinical and practical implications. Various stakeholders could contribute to a smoother transition for novice physical therapists, including educational institutions, physical therapy associations, physical therapy colleagues, and practice owners. To address the unpreparedness reported by novice physical therapists, educators, and internship supervisors should focus more on enhancing students’ readiness for clinical practice by encouraging them to work more independently and solve problems on their own [36], and by familiarizing them with caseload management, administration work, and referrals to other disciplines. Students should be encouraged to ‘think out of the box’ and face more complex cases so they are not surprised by the non-linear, complex, and multifactorial conditions they will face in practice as novice physical therapists [10]. These changes will ensure students are less sheltered during internships, encouraging them to evolve into resilient health professionals. Resilience training and teaching students how to deal with uncertainty should be part of physical therapy education, as it provides students with strength, focus, endurance, and the ability to overcome difficult circumstances [37,38]. Additionally, the uncertainty tolerance of the students could be increased during classes and internships by frequently exposing them to uncertain situations in a safe environment, and letting them evaluate and reflect on their responses [29]. Colleagues and practice owners may try to create a safe environment where novice physical therapists feel free to ask questions or discuss difficulties [6,9]. Scheduling regular breaks between therapeutic sessions will give novice physical therapists enough time to keep up with their administration, reflect on their own practice, and/or read up on medical literature. The practice owners could provide a buddy or mentorship program for colleagues with the same specialization, for which weekly meetings are scheduled in advance, enabling novice physical therapists to ask questions and discuss important issues without feeling they are interrupting others [24,35]. Lastly, policy staff at physical therapy associations may support the transitions of novice physical therapists by informing them about what to expect and providing advice about the process. Additionally, the associations should inform novice physical therapists how to request official registrations, retirement information, quality labels, and insurances for those who will be self-employed to minimize the hassle that already comes along with the transition period.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study was the inclusion of novice physical therapists who had left the profession, which provided a comprehensive overview of outcomes compared with previous studies investigating the transition from student to novice physical therapist. The comprehensive overview was also facilitated by the interpretative phenomenological approach of the study, which has led to in-depth understanding of experiences. Furthermore, we used member checking to confirm the analysts’ interpretations and minimize researcher bias, while purposive sampling was used to ensure a rich group of participants.

Some limitations were also present in this study. The majority of participants were recruited from a single region in the Netherlands and were employed in private practice, which limit the transferability of our findings to other regions, countries or other settings, such as secondary care. Moreover, the data were collected at one timepoint, which does not provide insight into the progression of a transition experience and professional development. Future research should therefore use a longitudinal, interprofessional, mixed-method study to obtain more insights into how a transition experience changes over time.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the transition from student to novice physical therapist is context-related and unique to each individual. Some novice physical therapists experienced positive outcomes, such as freedom, autonomy, success, and the development of their self-confidence and professional identity, while others experienced stress, chaos, isolation, doubts, fatigue, and felt the need to ‘fake’ or ‘pretend’ their clinical skills. Those who struggled with negative experiences felt they were unprepared for the demands of clinical practice, had expected to make a greater clinical impact, and/or found it more difficult to establish a new professional identity. The ways in which novice physical therapists deal with the experienced uncertainties can influence their job satisfaction and job embeddedness. We recommend that educators provide extra support to novice physical therapists to enable them to grow into happy and resilient colleagues. This could include (a) supporting them with the realistic challenges of the demanding healthcare system, and (b) guiding them to accept uncertainty, as this is inherent to providing care as a novice physical therapist.

Context of private physiotherapy practice

In the Netherlands, individuals can directly access physiotherapy services without the need for a referral from a physician. Physiotherapy practices are typically privately owned and operated, with many physiotherapists managing their own clinics. Employment terms for physical therapists working in private practices are not legally defined and are determined by the practice owner themselves. Practices sign contracts with health insurance companies so that patients can have their physiotherapeutic sessions reimbursed. The costs for a physiotherapy session are determined by health insurance companies themselves. Physiotherapy care is not covered by the legally required basic insurance, meaning patients can only have physiotherapy treatments reimbursed if they have supplementary insurance [39].

In the Netherlands, physiotherapy education is organized within accredited universities of applied sciences, offering bachelor’s degree programs in physiotherapy. These programs typically span four years. Upon successful completion of the bachelor’s program, graduates are eligible to register with the Dutch regulatory body for physiotherapy, enabling them to practice as licensed physiotherapists. Additionally, some universities offer postgraduate programs and continuing education courses for physiotherapists looking to specialize in specific areas of practice or advance their professional development.
