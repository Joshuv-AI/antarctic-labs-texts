Extracellular Vesicles From Fungal Infection in Humans: A Key Player in Immunological Responses
Authors: Caroline P de Rezende, Patrick W S Santos, Renan A Piraine, Virgínia C Silvestrini, Julio C J Barbosa, Fabiana C P Valera, Edwin Tamashiro, Guilherme G Podolski‐Gondim, Silvana M Quintana, Rodrigo Calado, Roberto Martinez, Taicia P Fill, Márcio L Rodrigues, Fausto Almeida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFungal infections cause approximately 1.6 million deaths annually. Diagnosing and treating fungal infections is difficult due to limited access to diagnostic tests and rising antifungal resistance. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) facilitate interactions between fungal cells and hosts, significantly influencing the pathogen‐host relationship. Owing to the complexity of fungal EVs and the lack of clinical studies on their roles in human infections, we analysed EVs from serum and urine samples of patients with infections caused by Candida albicans, Cryptococcus neoformans, and Paracoccidioides brasiliensis to determine their roles. Using mass spectrometry, we identified sterols, sphingolipids, and fatty acids as key metabolites in the EVs. We quantified cholesterol and ergosterol, confirming the presence of both host and fungal EVs in clinical samples. Our research investigated whether these EVs could modulate the host immune response. We observed a proinflammatory response in murine and human macrophages, characterized by increased cytokines, such as tumour necrosis factor‐α, interferon‐γ, and interleukin‐6, and elevated expression of the inducible nitric oxide synthase gene, a marker of M1 macrophage response. Thus, circulating EVs in patients with fungal infections likely play a role in disease pathophysiology. These findings enhance our understanding of EVs in fungal infections, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for systemic mycoses.Keywords: clinical samples, extracellular vesicles (EVs), fungal metabolites, immunomodulation, systemic mycoses

Introduction

Fungi are the least studied and understood microorganisms among all classes that can infect humans, especially in immunocompromised individuals (Brown et al. 2024). The high incidence of fungal infections, therapeutic limitations, and lack of attention from health agencies drive the development of new immunological interventions (Chechi et al. 2023). Fungal infections are caused by a wide range of pathogenic fungi classified by the World Health Organization into three priority groups: critical, high, and medium. Candida albicans (C. albicans) and Cryptococcus neoformans (C. neoformans) are critical priority fungi, whereas Paracoccidioides brasiliensis (P. brasiliensis) is a medium priority fungus (World Health Organization 2022). Current antifungal therapy is considered effective in many cases; however, the emergence of strains resistant to the four classes of antifungal drugs (azoles, polyenes, echinocandins, and flucytosine) promotes new strategies such as extracellular vesicles (EVs) (Martins‐Santana et al. 2023; Houšť et al. 2020).

EVs consist of spherical lipid bilayers, whose composition depends on the origin of the cell and environmental and metabolic conditions (de Toledo Martins et al. 2019). EVs facilitate bidirectional communication between the pathogen and host, influencing the host immune responses (Liebana‐Jordan et al. 2021), and they carry lipids, carbohydrates, proteins, and nucleic acids (de Toledo Martins et al. 2019). Biological fluids, such as blood and urine, are important sources of EVs, providing a promising platform for identifying diagnostic biomarkers (Alberro et al. 2021; Marr et al. 2020). Monocytes and dendritic cells are modulated by fungal EVs (Ikeda et al. 2018; Johansson et al. 2018), such as those derived from the fungal pathogens C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis, which change the macrophage functions in vitro by modulating the host immune response during infection (Da Silva et al. 2016; Oliveira et al. 2010).

High mortality rates of pathogenic fungal infections are due to underdiagnosis and limited treatment options. The emergence of multidrug‐resistant fungal pathogens necessitates the rapid and accurate diagnosis of causative fungi for effective treatment (Fang et al. 2023). Diagnostic tests, including the gold standard test, have various limitations, such as low sensitivity, an invasive collection process, the need for qualified professionals, and a slow response time, emphasizing the need for new microbiological diagnostic tools with greater specificity and sensitivity for the detection and treatment of patients in clinical settings (Malhotra et al. 2014; Pasqualotto and Denning 2005).

Current studies emphasize the importance of understanding the interactions between the host and pathogenic fungi, demonstrating that the identification of specific fungal metabolite profiles can facilitate the development of rapid and non‐invasive diagnostic tools and personalized treatment (Brown et al. 2024; Wangsanut and Pongpom 2024). The secretion of molecules that aid in nutrient acquisition, immune evasion, and infection is fundamental to fungal pathogenicity. In particular, the release of EVs plays a role in cellular communication and the progression and/or eradication of fungal infection (Reveglia et al. 2025). Despite many studies on the production of EVs by fungal pathogens (Da Silva et al. 2016; Hatanaka et al. 2019; Bitencourt et al. 2018; Almeida et al. 2017), to date, no studies have characterized the roles of EVs in fungal infections using human samples. Despite their impact on human health, the mechanisms of fungal infection and their interactions with human physiology are still under investigation (Reveglia et al. 2025).

Here, we identified the presence of EVs in the serum and urine samples of patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis, suggesting the potential of fungal EV–host interactions for the development of new therapeutic strategies. Evaluation of metabolite composition in EVs via mass spectrometry (MS) analysis revealed the presence of lipids, especially sterols, sphingolipids, and fatty acids, suggesting their crucial roles in the physiological functions of EVs. Furthermore, EVs derived from infected patients polarized the human (THP‐1) and murine (AMJ2‐C11) macrophages towards a proinflammatory immune response profile in vitro. Taken together, our results suggest that both the host‐ and pathogen‐derived EVs are involved in the eradication of fungal infections via modulation of the host immune response. Therefore, our study provides new insights into the modulatory mechanisms of EVs and their potential use in novel therapeutic approaches for systemic mycoses.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Statement and Patient Consent Statement

The procedures performed with patients follow the principles proposed by the National Research Ethics Commission and the Research Ethics Committee of the University Hospital, Ribeirao Preto Medical School at the University of Sao Paulo (HCRP number 4.277.966). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical Samples

Blood and urine samples from healthy individuals and patients with fungal infections caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis were obtained from University Hospital, Ribeirao Preto Medical School, University of Sao Paulo, SP, Brazil (HC‐FMRP, USP). Sample collection was carried out in collaboration with Prof. Dr Roberto Martinez and Prof. Dr Silvana Maria Quintana (HC‐FMRP). Blood samples were collected in a collection tube with gel and clot activator (Firstlab) and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min (Thermo Fisher Sorvall Legend RT Centrifuge) to obtain serum. Urine samples were collected from the midstream of the first morning urine in a sterile universal collector (Fisrtlab).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria of the Patients

Female and male patients of any age were considered eligible for this clinical study. The inclusion criterion was the presence of fungal infections caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans, or P. brasiliensis in the acute phase. Clinical samples from patients with candidiasis were derived from vulvovaginal manifestations. Exclusion criteria included acute bacterial and/or viral infections, as well as prior treatment for fungal infections (Table S1). It is important to note that exclusion based on HIV positivity or AIDS diagnosis was not applied to patients with cryptococcosis, since the global burden of this infection is significantly higher among immunocompromised individuals. Consequently, including such cases is essential for developing therapeutic strategies that reflect real‐world clinical conditions (Chechi et al. 2023; Mourad and Perfect 2018).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria of Healthy Individuals

Healthy female and male participants of any age were eligible for inclusion. The inclusion criterion was the absence of acute infections and immunocompetence. Exclusion criteria included the presence of acute bacterial, fungal, or viral infections and the use of medications that could potentially influence study outcomes (Table S1).

Murine Alveolar Macrophage Cell Line (AMJ2‐C11)

The murine alveolar macrophage cell line (AMJ2‐C11) was obtained from the Rio de Janeiro Cell Bank (BCRJ). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) with high glucose (Gibco‐Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with foetal bovine serum (10%), sodium bicarbonate, and penicillin/streptomycin (1%), and incubated in CO2 (5%) at 37°C. Cells were cultivated until confluence and detached from the flask bottom with PBS pH 7.4 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher) and 0.5 M EDTA pH 8.0 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were plated in 24‐ or 48‐well culture plates, depending on the assay performed.

Human THP‐1‐Derived Macrophages

The human monocytic cell line THP‐1 (American Type Culture Collection ATCC TIB202) is derived from an acute monocytic leukaemia cell line. The cells were maintained in a suspension culture dish (Corning Inc., Sigma Aldrich) with RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco‐Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with foetal bovine serum (10%), sodium bicarbonate, and penicillin/streptomycin (1%), and incubated in CO2 (5%) at 37°C. THP‐1 monocytes were differentiated into macrophages as previously described (Park et al. 2007). THP‐1 cells were stably differentiated into macrophages by phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA) (Sigma‐Aldrich) at 5 ng/mL over 48 h. Cells differentiated into macrophages were plated in 6‐well plates for the assay.

Standardization of Extracellular Vesicle (EV) Extraction in Clinical Samples

EVs were isolated as previously described (Liu et al. 2018; Rodrigues et al. 2007), with some modifications. First, the collected blood samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min (Thermo Fisher Sorvall Legend RT Centrifuge) to obtain the serum. Serum (2 mL) and urine (15 mL) samples were subjected to centrifugation at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove cells and debris (Thermo Fisher Sorvall Legend RT Centrifuge). In turn, serum samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g, 30 min, 4°C (Thermo Fisher Sorvall Legend RT Centrifuge), while urine samples were concentrated through a polystyrene membrane in an Amicon ultracentrifugation system with a 100 kDa cutoff (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C (Thermo Fisher Sorvall Legend RT Centrifuge). Subsequently, the supernatants obtained from serum and urine samples were filtered through a 0.22 µm filter and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C (Optima TLX Ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter). The pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of ultrapure water.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

Size distribution analysis and quantification of EV preparations were performed on a NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) equipped with rapid video capture and particle tracking software. Vesicles purified from clinical samples were diluted in 1 mL of ultrapure water. Each sample was injected into the sample cubicle of the device for analysis. Fluorescence scattering and capture settings (such as focus, camera, and gain settings) were optimized to make particle tracks visible, and then measurements were obtained in triplicate and analysed using NanoSight NS300 software (version 3.2.16). Data on EV sizes were expressed as the calculated means ± SD of size distribution.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Serum and urine EV samples from healthy individuals and patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis were incubated with 2% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 4 h at a temperature of 2°C to 4°C and subsequently ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g at 4°C for 1 h. The pellet obtained from the samples was resuspended in 200 µL of sodium cacodylate buffer. Images were captured with a JEOL JEM 100CXII transmission electron microscope with a Hamamatsu ORCA‐HR digital camera.

Western Blotting of Classical EVs Markers

Western blotting of EVs from the serum and urine of healthy individuals and patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis was performed as previously described (Silvestrini et al. 2020), with some modifications. Serum and urine EVs were lysed with RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. The samples were sonicated for 1 min at 60% amplitude (10 on and 10 off pulses), centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for quantification. Protein concentration was determined by the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit method (Thermo Scientific, Catalog numbers 23225 and 23227), separated by SDS‐PAGE, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for 1 h. The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in 0.05% Tween/TBS and incubated with the specific antibodies. ALIX (E47TU‐Cell Signaling), Anti‐CD63 (SAB4301607‐Sigma Aldrich), and Anti‐CD9 (EPR23105121‐Abcam) were used at a dilution of 1:1000. The protein‐antibody complex was detected using ECL Western blot detection reagents (GE Lifesciences), and signals were detected using a CCD camera (Image Quant LAS 4000 mini, Uppsala, Sweden).

MS

Extracellular vesicles obtained from the serum and urine of healthy individuals and patients diagnosed with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis were subjected to extraction using 200 µL of methanol, followed by drying in a stream of N2. The extracts obtained were prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and subjected to analysis via liquid chromatography‐high resolution MS/MS (LC‐HRMS/MS) using a Thermo Scientific Q‐Exactive Quadrupole‐Orbitrap hybrid instrument. The analyses were conducted in both positive and negative modes, with a m/z range of 100–1500, a capillary voltage of 3.4 kV, an inlet capillary temperature of 280°C, and a voltage of 100 V. Chromatographic separation employed a Thermo Scientific Accucore C18 2.6 µm (2.1 mm × 100 mm) column. The stationary phase consisted of MilliQ water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B). Chromatographic separation was performed using the following gradients: 0–5 min, 5% B; 5–10 min, 40% B; 10–12 min, 45% B; 12–18 min, 98% B; 18–20 min, 98% B; 20–22 min, 5% B; and 22–24 min 5% B. The injection volume of each sample was standardized at 10 µL. MS data were collected in data‐dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, capturing MS/MS spectra of the five most intense ions in 5‐s windows. The Orbitrap analyser resolution was set as 70,000 with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1e6 and a maximum injection time (IT) of 100 ms. MS/MS spectra were obtained in centroid mode, maintaining a resolution of 35,000, with a target AGC of 1e5 and a maximum IT of 100 ms. Collision energies in normalized collision energy (NCE) mode were applied at 20, 35, and 45 eV. MS data were processed with Xcalibur software (version 3.0.63) developed by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Molecular Networks and Metabolomics Analysis

The raw files (.raw) were converted via MSConvert (Chambers et al. 2012) to the mzXML format and analysed in the MZmine 3.9.0 software. The processing parameters used are in Table 1. The .mgf files and the quantification tables obtained by MZmine were used to perform feature based molecular networking (FBMN), where the fragmentation tolerance parameters of the library data were 0.02 Da, and the cosine score for the chromatograms that presented corresponding peaks was 0.7 (70%). Pre‐established libraries were used for putative annotations according to the feature table generated by MZmine 3.9.0 (Schmid et al. 2023). The annotated compounds were classified according to Schymanski et al. (2014): Compounds confirmed by comparison of MS/MS profiles with reference standards (in this study reference standards for cholesterol, ergosterol and D‐erythro sphinganine were used)—Level I annotation; compounds annotated through comparison of the MS/MS fragmentation profile with spectra present in the GNPS libraries—Level II annotation; compounds annotated through simulated spectra used were generated by SIRIUS 5.7.0—Level III annotation; compounds not annotated but with molecular formula unequivocally determined through information from LC‐HRMS analyses—Level IV annotation. The molecular networks, as well as the annotations made against the GNPS databases, are available at: https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=3396730133064ed6a81bb5cfa59a0208. The FBMN molecular networks were visualized using Cytoscape (v.3.10.1). In total, 533 compounds were annotated according to the GNPS MS/MS libraries. All MS1 and MS/MS spectra data, together with their metadata in mzXML format, have been deposited in an open format in the public data repository GNPS/MassIVE (Dührkop et al. 2019). Finally, the areas of the differential ions noted through univariate and multivariate comparisons were calculated manually using Thermo Xcalibur 3.0.63 software (Copyright 1988–2013 Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.), and correlation plots of the calculated areas were constructed to analyse the abundances of these compounds in the different sample groups, using Prisma 8.0.1 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Quantification of Cholesterol and Ergosterol in Serum EVs by LC‐MS

Cholesterol and ergosterol standards were used for quantification. Stock solutions of these compounds were prepared at 10 and 1 mg/mL in MeOH. The calibration curves of these compounds were used to quantify these compounds in serum EV samples from patients with fungal infections caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans and P. brasiliensis. The linear ranges (n = 6) were 0.005 to 0.01 µg for cholesterol and 0.002 to 0.1 µg. The LODs and LOQs were calculated as 3.3 and 10 SD/S, respectively, where SD is the standard deviation of the intercept and S is the angular coefficient of the equation. The compounds were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC (Waters, Milford, MA) using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 µm; Waters, UK). The conditions were: binary mobile phase—water (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B); gradient elution: 0–7 min, 5% B; 8–9 min, 95% B; 9.1–13 min, 5% B. The injection volume was 10 µL, and the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min. For the detection of ergosterol and cholesterol, a Xevo Triple Quadrupole—TQD (Waters MS Technologies, Manchester, UK) with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in positive ionization mode was used. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used to confirm and quantify ergosterol and cholesterol. For ergosterol, transitions were monitored from the precursor ion at m/z 379.4 to the product ions at m/z 253.17 and 125.06. For cholesterol, transitions were monitored from the precursor ion at m/z 369.30 to its product ions at m/z 109.09 and 95.05. In both cases, the dwell time was set at 0.025 s.

Immunobiological Activity of Murine and Human Macrophages After Incubation With EVs From Clinical Samples

Murine alveolar macrophages (AMJ2‐C11) and human macrophages (THP1) were plated in 48‐ and 6‐well plates, respectively (1.5 × 106 cells/mL), and maintained at 37°C, 5% CO2, overnight. AMJ2‐C11 and THP1 cells were stimulated with pools of EVs from the serum and urine of patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis at concentrations of 1 × 106 and 1 × 109 EVs/mL. As a control, EVs from serum and urine from healthy individuals were used at a concentration of 1 × 106 EVs/mL, Pam3CSK4 (1 µg/mL), and culture medium alone. The cells were incubated for 24 and 48 h at 37°C with 5% CO2. The supernatant was collected to measure TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, IL‐6, and IL‐10 using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with a BD Biosciences kit (San Diego, CA, USA), as described by the manufacturer. The absorbance value was determined at 450 nm on a spectrophotometer for microplates (SpectraMax 190; Molecular Devices).

Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (qPCR)

AMJ2‐C11 were plated in 24‐well plates (2 × 106 cells/mL) and stimulated with EVs from serum (1 × 1010 EVs/mL) and urine (1 × 109 EVs/mL) from patients with fungal infections, EVs from serum and urine from healthy individuals (1 × 106 EVs/mL), Pam3CSK4 (1µg/mL), IL‐4 (50 ng/mL), or medium alone for 24 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells were used for RNA extraction using the Illustra RNAspin Mini RNA isolation kit (GE Healthcare) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and quality were measured using a nanophotometer (Implem). The reaction for cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg of RNA, following the instructions available in the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems). Real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was conducted in a StepOnePlus Real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) to analyse the relative expression of the genes of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and arginase‐1 (Arg‐1). The β‐actin gene (Actb) was used as an endogenous reference control. The primer sequences for these macrophage‐polarization markers are listed in Table 2. The conditions used in the qPCR reactions followed the protocol available in the qPCRBIO SyGreen Mix kit (PCR Biosystems). All samples were analysed in triplicate. Relative expressions were calculated by comparing Threshold Cycle (Ct) values of β‐actin and targeted genes according to the 2−ΔΔCT method described by Livak and Schmittgen (2001).

Statistical Analysis

The results were expressed as the mean ± SD (standard deviation). Statistical analyses of data with more than three experimental groups were performed using the one‐way ANOVA test and Sidak's multiple comparisons test with a single pooled variance, using the Prisma 8.0.2 program (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Statistical differences are p < 0.05.

Results

Identification and Characterization of EVs From the Serum and Urine Samples of Healthy Individuals and Patients With Fungal Infections

In this study, we collected serum and urine samples of healthy individuals (n = 20) as well as from patients infected with C. albicans (n = 10), C. neoformans (n = 6), and P. brasiliensis (n = 20) for EV identification and characterization using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight; NS300), TEM, and western blotting. Healthy individuals (n = 20) were randomly pooled into groups of five samples each and served as non‐infected controls (Figure 1A,B). The samples from infected individuals were also pooled in groups of five samples each from patients with candidiasis (Figure 1C,D) and paracoccidioidomycosis (Figure 1G,H), and all six samples from patients with cryptococcosis (Figure 1E,F). The EV concentration in the serum from healthy individuals corresponded to 2.97 × 1012 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 164 nm; mode: 123 nm) (Figure 1A), while concentrations for infected patients were 2.94 × 1011 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 255 nm; mode: 196 nm) for candidiasis (Figure 1C), 3.29 × 1012 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 150 nm; mode: 108 nm) for cryptococcosis (Figure 1E), and 1.7 × 1012 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 215 nm; mode: 197 nm) for paracoccidioidomycosis (Figure 1G). Urine samples had mean vesicle concentrations of 3.55 × 1010 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 182 nm; mode: 114 nm) for healthy individuals (Figure 1B), 4.14 × 1010 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 211 nm; mode: 112 nm) for candidiasis (Figure 1D), 3.26 × 1010 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 131 nm; mode: 87 nm) for cryptococcosis (Figure 1F), and 6.95 × 1010 EVs/mL (mean diameter: 236 nm; mode: 123 nm) for paracoccidioidomycosis (Figure 1H). Therefore, patients with candidiasis exhibited lower concentrations of larger sized EVs in the serum than those with cryptococcosis and paracoccidioidomycosis. These results demonstrate the presence of EVs in the serum and urine samples of patients with fungal infections, mainly those caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis.

The spherical shape of EVs allows the calculation of their diameter using various techniques, such as TEM that shows the resolution in nanometres (Rikkert et al. 2019). The classic cup‐shaped morphology of EVs can be used to distinguish different classes of vesicles in the same samples (Jung and Mun 2018; Szatanek et al. 2017). The presence of EVs in the serum and urine samples of healthy individuals and patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis was visualized using TEM. The capture fields from TEM images revealed that EVs exhibited the classic cup‐shaped morphology with a diameter of 200 nm (Figure 1I), thus confirming the characteristics of EVs from the serum and urine samples of patients with fungal infections.

EVs From the Serum and Urine Samples of Patients With Candidiasis, Cryptococcosis, and Paracoccidioidomycosis Present Classic EV Markers

For the characterization of EV proteins, the International Society of Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) recommends the evaluation of the expression of at least one protein from the following classes: transmembrane, anchored in glycosylphosphatidylinositol associated with plasma membranes and/or cytosolic, and heat shock proteins (Gandham et al. 2020). Here, we analysed the presence of three classic protein markers, CD9, CD63, and ALIX, in the serum and urinary EVs of healthy individuals and patients with fungal infections caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis using western blotting (Figure 1J). For CD9 and CD63 labelling, the tetraspanin family, which consists of membrane proteins regulating the selective trafficking of associated proteins possibly via membrane compartmentalization, is crucial for EV characterization (Ghossoub et al. 2020; Oksvold et al. 2014). Here, we performed ALIX labelling of EVs from the urine samples of healthy individuals and patients with fungal infections (Figure 1J). Notably, EVs isolated from the clinical samples were characterized according to the standards proposed by ISEV.

Statistical Analysis and Molecular Networking in the Serum EVs of Healthy Individuals and Patients With Fungal Infections

EVs from the serum samples of healthy individuals and patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, or paracoccidioidomycosis were prepared as dry extracts of lyophilized EVs and resuspended in MeOH at a concentration of 1 mg/mL for UHPLC‐MS/MS analysis. The data obtained were analysed using principal components analysis (PCA). The main components of PCA showed a clear separation between the analysed serum EVs, corresponding to 27.2% of the total data variance, 14.6% for PC1, and 12.6% for PC2 (Figure 2A). This distinctive separation was fundamental in distinguishing metabolic patterns associated with different health state conditions. FBMN was applied to analyse the chemical composition of the EVs under the studied conditions (Supplementary Material 2). The fragmentation tolerance parameters of the library data were 0.02 Da, and the cosine score for the chromatograms that presented corresponding peaks was 0.7 (70%) (Supplementary Material 3). The annotation resulted in a total of 80 compounds (annotation levels I and II) using the MS/MS libraries from GNPS and analytical standards. Among these, notable metabolites from the classes of sterols, sphingolipids, and fatty acids were annotated (Figure 2B).

Metabolite Annotations of Serum EVs in Patient Samples

Metabolomics is a systems biology approach that aims to profile metabolites in cells, biofluids, and tissues (Li et al. 2018). In the present study, the profiles of metabolites in serum EVs from patients infected with C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis were analysed using UHPLC‐HRMS/MS analysis, and serum EVs from healthy individuals were used as non‐infected controls (Figure 3). The detection of cholesterol and ergosterol, which are compounds found in cell membranes, is important for understanding the origin of EVs and the relationship between pathogens and the hosts. Cholesterol (C27H46O) was predominantly found in serum EVs from healthy individuals (Figure 3A), with a peak area of 2.2 × 104, and with lower abundance in EVs from patients with fungal infections (Figure 3B), measured at m/z 369.3520 [M + H − H2O]+, and with high similarity with the spectral library (Supplementary Material 3A) and further confirmed with an analytical standard. Ergosterol (C28H44O), measured at m/z 379.3344 [M + H − H2O]+, was detected in the serum EVs of patients with fungal infections and was detected below the quantification limit in healthy individuals. EVs of C. albicans (peak area of 6.7 × 102), C. neoformans (peak area of 1 × 103), and P. brasiliensis (peak area of 1 × 103) showed a significant difference compared to EVs derived from healthy individuals (Figure 3C,D; Supplementary Material 4).

In EVs from the serum of patients with candidiasis and cryptococcosis, we identified compounds such as phytosphingosine (C18H39NO3, m/z 318.2987 [M + H]+; Figure 3E,F) and identified sphingosine (C18H37NO2, m/z 302.3041 [M + H]+; Figure 3G,H) using an analytical standard as a comparison (Supplementary Material 3C,D). These compounds are present in the sphingolipid pathway, with phytosphingosine being an intermediate metabolite and sphingosine being an 18‐carbon amino alcohol derived from the cleavage of the ceramide process (Le Stunff et al. 2007).

Oleic acid (cis‐9‐octadecenoic acid), commonly known as omega‐9 fatty acid, has 18 carbons with a cis double bond at the 9th carbon position (C18H34O2). This fatty acid was detected in the serum EVs from healthy individuals and patients infected by pathogenic fungi (Figure 3I), with a significant increase in the concentration of EVs from patients with cryptococcosis compared to healthy individuals (Figure 3J). The measured m/z for oleic acid was 265.2517 [M + H − H2O]+, and most of the major fragments of the compounds matched the fragmentation patterns in the GNPS database (Supplementary Material 3E). Putative notes detected a compound from the sphingolipid class in the EVs obtained from serum of patients infected by C. neoformans and P. brasiliensis; however further studies are required to confirm its identity and its chemical structure (Supplementary Material 3F). Thus, our results demonstrate the presence of lipid compounds in the serum EVs of patients with fungal infections.

Quantification of Cholesterol and Ergosterol in Serum EVs From Healthy Individuals and Patients Infected With C. albicans, C. neoformans, or P. brasiliensis

The detection of cholesterol and ergosterol in serum EVs from patients with fungal infections led us to hypothesize that circulating EVs, originating from both the host and the fungus, are heterogeneous in their composition. In this sense, we quantified cholesterol and ergosterol in serum EVs from healthy individuals and patients infected with C. albicans, C. neoformans, or P. brasiliensis (Figure 4) using LC‐MS/MS. In healthy individuals, the mean concentration of 1.61 × 1011 EVs/mL resulted in a cholesterol concentration of 0.68 ppm (parts per million) (Figure 4A) and an ergosterol concentration below the detection limit of the quantification curve (Figure 4B and Supplementary Materials 5 and 6). Serum EVs from patients with candidiasis (1.74 × 1011 EVs/mL), cryptococcosis (1 × 1011 EVs/mL), and paracoccidioidomycosis (2.29 × 1010 EVs/mL) showed mean cholesterol concentrations of 0.67, 0.63, and 0.64 ppm, respectively (Figure 4A and Supplementary Material 5). The ergosterol concentration was 0.24 ppm for candidiasis and 0.33 ppm for both cryptococcosis and paracoccidioidomycosis (Figure 4B and Supplementary Material 6). Our results demonstrated an estimated cholesterol/ergosterol ratio in EVs of 2.86 for patients with candidiasis, 1.99 for patients with cryptococcosis, and 2.02 for patients with paracoccidioidomycosis (Figure 4C and Supplementary Materials 5 and 6). Therefore, it is possible to infer a heterogeneous composition of EVs.

Host and/or Fungal EVs From the Serum and Urine Samples of Patients With Candidiasis, Cryptococcosis, and Paracoccidioidomycosis Stimulate AMJ2‐C11 Cells to Produce Proinflammatory Cytokines

To understand the inflammatory properties of EVs from patients with fungal infections, murine macrophages (AMJ2‐C11) were stimulated with 106 and 109 EVs/mL from the serum and urine samples of patients infected by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis for 24 and 48 h. AMJ2‐C11 cells showed a significant increase in the expression levels of interferon (IFN)‐γ and tumour necrosis factor (TNF)‐α at concentrations of 106 and 109, respectively, with serum EVs of patients with candidiasis after 24 h (Figure 5A,E) and 48 h (Figure 5B,F) compared to those in healthy individuals. Serum EVs of patients infected by C. albicans also exhibited significantly increased production of interleukin (IL)‐6 (106 and 109 EVs/mL) after 24 and 48 h (Figure 5I,J). Stimulation with serum EVs from patients with cryptococcosis showed a significant increase in the expression levels of IFN‐γ (106 EVs/mL) and TNF‐α (106 and 109 EVs/mL) after 48 h (Figure 5N,R). Moreover, analysis of cytokine production in AMJ2‐C11 cells stimulated with urinary EVs revealed a significant increase in IFN‐γ levels at concentrations of 106 EVs/mL (24 h) and 109 EVs/mL (48 h) for C. neoformans (Figure 5C,D) and TNF‐α levels for patients with candidiasis compared to healthy individuals (Figure 5G,H). Urinary EVs of patients with cryptococcosis also promoted a significant increase in TNF‐α and IL‐10 levels 24 and 48 h (Figure 5K,L,O,P) and IL‐6 levels 48 h after stimulation (Figure 5T). In turn, urinary EVs from patients infected by P. brasiliensis only showed an increase in TNF‐α expression levels (109 EVs/mL) after 24 h of stimulation (Figure 5U). Our findings suggest that the heterogeneity of serum and urinary EVs in patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis induces murine macrophages to produce proinflammatory mediators.

THP‐1‐Derived Macrophages Show Heterogeneous Expression of Cytokines After Stimulation With Host and/or Fungal EVs From Patients Infected by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis

The THP‐1 monocytic cell line can differentiate into macrophages and is often used as an in vitro model of human macrophages because of its metabolic and morphological similarities (Park et al. 2007). Here, the same experimental conditions described above were used to verify the similarity of the immunological activity of these cells to that of murine macrophages (AMJ2‐C11). THP‐1‐derived macrophages stimulated with EVs from the serum of patients with candidiasis exhibited increased production of IFN‐γ (109 EVs/mL) (Figure 6A) and IL‐6 (106 EVs/mL) for serum EVs of patients with cryptococcosis compared to those from the healthy individuals after 24 h (Figure 6E). In the 48‐h stimulus, EVs from patients infected by C. neoformans increased the production of TNF‐α and IL‐10 at both concentrations tested (Figure 6J,N). IFN‐γ and TNF‐α (106 and 109 EVs/mL) levels were significantly increased in THP‐1 macrophages after stimulation with serum EVs from patients with paracoccidioidomycosis (Figure 6Q,R,U,V). Stimulation with urinary EVs resulted in the heterogeneous production of cytokines in THP‐derived macrophages. EVs from the urine samples of patients with candidiasis significantly increased IL‐10 production (109 EVs/mL) after 24 h compared to those from healthy individuals (Figure 6C). Increased production of IFN‐γ (106 EVs/mL) and TNF‐α (106 and 109 EVs/mL) was observed after stimulation with EVs from the urine samples of patients with cryptococcosis in both stimulus times (Figure 6H and 6L), along with elevated IL‐6 (109) and IL‐10 expression (109) after 24 and 48 h, respectively (Figure 6O,P,T). These results show the prevalence of inflammatory mediators, mainly TNF‐α and IFN‐γ, in THP‐1‐derived macrophages. IL‐10 production was also observed in the serum EVs of patients with candidiasis and serum and urinary EVs of patients with cryptococcosis.

C. albicans EVs Induce the M1 Phenotype in AMJ2‐C11 Macrophages

Here, the production of proinflammatory cytokines by EVs from the serum and urine samples of patients with fungal infections suggests the polarization of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype. To further analyse this process, AMJ2‐C11 cells were stimulated with EVs from the serum (1010 EVs/mL) and urine (109 EVs/mL) samples of patients for 24 h, followed by the relative quantification of transcripts of M1 (inducible NO synthase [iNOS]) and M2 (Arg‐1) polarization markers. Compared to those in healthy individuals, expression levels of iNOS mRNA were increased by 7.7‐fold in the serum EVs (Figure 7A) and 59‐fold in the urinary EVs (Figure 7B) of patients with candidiasis. In terms of Arg‐1 mRNA expression, an 8‐fold increase was observed in C. albicans (Figure 7C), whereas a 3.2‐fold and 1.8‐fold increase were observed in the serum and urinary EVs of C. neoformans (Figure 7C,D). In contrast, urine samples of P. brasiliensis showed a 1.7‐fold decrease in Arg‐1 mRNA expression (Figure 7D). These results indicate that EVs of patients with fungal infections are involved in the polarization of macrophages towards the M1 phenotype.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the presence of EVs, most likely originating from both the host and the fungal pathogens, in serum and urine of healthy individuals and patients with fungal infections caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis. The identification and characterization of EVs were performed using ultracentrifugation, NTA, TEM, and western blotting methodologies (Théry et al. 2018). Supporting our methods, Fusco et al. showed that ultracentrifugation and TEM are the most commonly used techniques in clinical studies for EV isolation and characterization (Fusco et al. 2024). Furthermore, Takov et al. demonstrated that ultracentrifugation provides greater purity in EVs isolation with minimal lipoprotein contaminants, as indicated by APOB quantification, compared to size exclusion chromatography (Takov et al. 2019). The size distribution of circulating EVs in these patients was similar to that reported in previous studies in in vitro models, with a size variation of 50–100 nm, with some peaks between 350 and 500 nm for C. albicans (Dawson et al. 2020; Vargas et al. 2015), 50–250 nm for C. neoformans (da Silva et al. 2018), and 20–200 nm for P. brasiliensis (Vallejo et al. 2011). Collectively, these data suggest the heterogeneity of EVs among different fungal genera. The presence of EVs in the urine of patients with fungal infections (candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis) also corroborates the patent by Marr and collaborators, who investigated molecules present in EVs excreted in the urine of patients with invasive aspergillosis and are being explored in diagnostic methods for aspergillosis (Marr et al. 2020).

EVs from human cells are generally enriched in sphingomyelin, cholesterol, phosphatidylserine, and glycosphingolipids (Chen et al. 2019). Here, we observed the presence of cholesterol, which is important for structural rigidity and resistance to physicochemical changes, in the EVs of healthy individuals. This result was expected, as cholesterol accounts for 25%–50% of the lipid content in mammalian cell membranes (Crane and Tamm 2004). In fungal cell membranes, ergosterol is crucial for regulating membrane fluidity, protein folding, localization, and cell cycle (Rodrigues 2018). Ergosterol has been detected in the EVs of C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis in vitro (Rodrigues et al. 2007; Vargas et al. 2015; Vallejo et al. 2012) and detected below quantification limits in healthy patients, corroborating our findings on patient samples in this study. The cholesterol/ergosterol peak area ratio may suggest the presence of EVs derived from both the host and the pathogens C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis during fungal infection in humans.

The identification of specific components, such as ergosterol, in EVs from clinical samples of patients with fungal infections is of great relevance, given its participation in physiological processes such as fungal growth, proliferation, adaptations to stress, and cellular detoxification (Bhattacharya 2021). Fungal ergosterol was considered by Koselny et al. (2018) to be immunologically active, being important for the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies (Koselny et al. 2018). The identification of ergosterol in EVs provides an improved view of host‐pathogen interactions and is a potential diagnostic biomarker. Furthermore, the main targets of current antifungals correspond to the fungal cell membrane, cell wall, or intracellular components; however, they are limited to only four main classes of drugs with complications including host toxicity, unfavourable pharmacokinetics, and a limited spectrum of activity (Lee et al. 2023; Reddy et al. 2022). Thus, the increase in fungal resistance promotes the need for new treatment approaches for systemic mycoses (Loh and Lam 2023).

The heterogeneity of serum EVs from patients with fungal infections was demonstrated by quantifying cholesterol and ergosterol, allowing the indirect estimation of host‐fungal EV ratios. The prevalence of human EVs may be related to the severity of fungal infection and immune responses. Fungal pathogens communicate with the host through complex mechanisms of interactions, in which host immune cells are the main participants in immune escape strategies and fungal control (Munhoz da Rocha et al. 2020). The heterogeneity of human and fungal EVs is an important mediator in the host response to infection, especially at different stages of disease progression (Tiberti et al. 2023). Furthermore, studies involving the characterization of human EVs in tuberculosis patients (Mehaffy et al. 2017; Kruh‐Garcia et al. 2014) detected mycobacterial macromolecules such as proteins, lipids, and lipoproteins, suggesting that these EVs contained the cellular content of the host and the pathogen (Schorey et al. 2021).

The production of EVs by the host and fungal pathogen during infection can also be suggested due to the detection of the sphingolipid class in the EVs of infected patients with candidiasis and cryptococcosis, particularly sphingosine and phytosphingosine, compared to the EVs of healthy individuals. Sphingosine and phytosphingosine are compounds present in the cell membranes of mammals and yeast, with the inclusion of specific mammalian tissues such as epidermis, small intestine, and myelin, respectively (Kondo et al. 2014). Furthermore, these compounds have been identified in pathogenic strains of C. albicans (Li et al. 2018) and C. neoformans (Singh et al. 2017) and in the EVs of C. albicans (Trentin et al. 2023). Previous studies revealed the importance of cholesterol and sphingomyelin in controlling the host immune response in phagocytic cells (Bryan et al. 2021; Qureshi et al. 2010). Qureshi et al. showed the production of different species of sphingomyelin in phagocytic cells and lymphocytes and its high levels at the site of fungal infection, providing insights into the roles of sphingomyelin in host‐controlled infection processes (Qureshi et al. 2010). However, further studies are warranted to investigate the association between sphingomyelin‐derived metabolites and EVs.

The presence of oleic acid in the EVs of patients infected by P. brasiliensis has also been detected in vitro using the Pb18 strain (Vallejo et al. 2012). The significant increase in oleic acid in the EVs of patients with cryptococcosis can be explained by the fact that lipids from the host contribute to the growth, virulence, and modulation of the immune response (Kretschmer et al. 2012). This hypothesis is supported by various in vitro and in vivo studies that showed the upregulation of genes related to the lipid metabolism of C. neoformans (Fan et al. 2005) and attenuated virulence of mutant strains of C. neoformans that do not use fatty acids as a carbon source for β‐oxidation in a nutrient‐scarce phagosome (Hu et al. 2008). Furthermore, Nolan and collaborators showed that the presence of oleic acid increases the replication rate of C. neoformans, where the fungus is able to eliminate, store, and use lipids in a beneficial way for its survival (Nolan et al. 2017).

Owing to their immunobiological activity, host and fungal EVs from patients with candidiasis, cryptococcosis, and paracoccidioidomycosis stimulated the murine alveolar macrophages (AMJ2‐C11) and human monocyte‐derived macrophages (THP‐1), promoting their polarization towards a proinflammatory immune response by increasing the levels of cytokines, such as TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, and IL‐6, and expression of the M1 gene marker characterized by iNOS. Our results are consistent with those on C. albicans EVs in vitro, which increased the production of NO and cytokines, such as IL‐12, TNF‐α, transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β, and IL‐10, and enhanced the survival of Galleria mellonella upon treatment with EVs after fungal challenge (Vargas et al. 2015).

Cryptococcosis is more prevalent in immunocompromised individuals, who are more susceptible to persistent pulmonary infection and dissemination to the central nervous system, culminating in cryptococcal meningitis (Pruitt et al. 2025). The acute vulnerability of AIDS patients to C. neoformans infection results from impaired CD4⁺ T cell immunity (Tugume et al. 2019). Therefore, mimicking immunodeficient conditions is essential to better understand the immune cell subtypes involved in protection and infection control in these contexts (Chechi et al. 2023). Our results demonstrated that EVs isolated from the serum and urine of cryptococcosis patients stimulated the production of both pro‐inflammatory (IFN‐γ, TNF‐β, IL‐6) and anti‐inflammatory (IL‐10) cytokines in AMJ2‐C11 and THP‐1 cells. In agreement with previous studies, EVs derived from C. neoformans have been shown to enhance TNF‐α and nitric oxide (NO) production, as well as promote phagocytosis of fungal cells. However, these vesicles also carry virulence factors, such as the capsular antigen glucuronoxylomannan, which has immunosuppressive effects on macrophages, increasing the production of anti‐inflammatory cytokines such as TGF‐β and IL‐10 (Oliveira et al. 2010; Retini et al. 2001). These findings support the dual immunomodulatory role of C. neoformans‐derived EVs in host‐pathogen interactions.

Here, serum and urinary EVs of patients with paracoccidioidomycosis stimulated the production of TNF‐α and IFN‐γ in the serum EVs of THP‐1 macrophages. The classical inflammatory activation profile (M1) promoted by P. brasiliensis EVs is characterized by the production of NO and proinflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐12p70). Incubation of alternatively activated macrophages (M2), which were subsequently treated with EVs from this fungus, changed their phenotype to an M1 profile (Da Silva et al. 2016). Furthermore, EVs from the attenuated variant of the Pb18 strain significantly increased the expression of inflammatory mediators (NO, TNF‐α, and IL‐6) compared to those from the virulent strain (Octaviano et al. 2022). To the best of our knowledge, this is a pioneering study on the identification and characterization of EVs from the clinical samples of patients with fungal infections caused by C. albicans, C. neoformans, and P. brasiliensis, providing new insights into the roles of EVs in humans and their possible use in therapeutic strategies for systemic mycoses.
