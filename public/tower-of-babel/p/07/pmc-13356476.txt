Reproductive outcomes in women with Hodgkin's lymphoma in a Danish fertility preservation cohort
Authors: Lotte Berdiin Colmorn, Elisabeth C. Larsen, Anette Tønnes Pedersen, Stine Gry Kristensen, Kirsten Tryde Macklon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe risk of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) after Hodgkin's lymphoma depends on female age, type, and dose of gonadotoxic treatment. Treatment with ABVD rarely results in POI and impaired fertility, whereas treatment with BEACOPP or stem cell transplantation is associated with higher risks of ovarian failure. Reports on parenthood rates are reassuring, although some studies report reduced fertility after treatment. In this study, we evaluate the reproductive outcomes after Hodgkin's lymphoma and the risk of POI after different types of chemotherapy.Material and MethodsThis cohort study was conducted at the Fertility Clinic at Rigshospitalet University Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark. Data was collected retrospectively from medical records from 1999 to 2020 and prospectively from 2020 to 2023. We included all female patients with Hodgkin's lymphoma, referred for fertility preservation between 1999 and 2022. End of follow‐up was December 31, 2023. Information about diagnoses, gonadotoxic treatment, and gynecologic and obstetric history was retrieved from medical records.ResultsA total of 59 women were included: 21 received low‐dose, 15 moderate‐dose, and 20 high‐dose chemotherapy (3 unknown). Ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) was done for fertility preservation in 86%. Mean age at diagnosis was 23.9 (SD 4.9) years. While no women developed POI after low‐dose chemotherapy, 4 (27%) did after moderate‐dose and 10 (50%) after high‐dose chemotherapy. Among 38 women with a pregnancy wish, 30 achieved at least one livebirth after cancer. Mean age at first pregnancy was 29.7 (SD 4.0) years. The chance of delivery after high dose was lower compared with low‐dose chemotherapy (OR 0.06, 95% CI:0.01–0.68) (p = 0.020). Among 33 women with OTC, 21 achieved pregnancy and delivery without ovarian tissue transplantation (OTT). Nine women underwent OTT of whom four delivered (44%) a total of five children.ConclusionsThe likelihood of livebirth and risk of POI depended on the type of chemotherapy, with the least number of deliveries and the highest risk of POI after high‐dose chemotherapy. Pregnancy rates were reassuringly high even among women with a single ovary after OTC, with only 20% returning for OTT. Our findings support that fertility preservation in Hodgkin's lymphoma patients should primarily be offered to those receiving moderate‐ to high‐dose chemotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

In the Nordic countries, the incidence of Hodgkin's lymphoma (HL) in the female population has been stable around 2/100.000 females for the past many years, with a reassuringly high 5‐year survival rate of 95%. 1 However, many are diagnosed before the age of 40, when they may not have completed, or even begun, achieving their desired family size. For some, the successful treatment of their cancer may come with some serious long‐term side effects such as reduced fertility. The risk of premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) after HL is related to female age, type, and dose of gonadotoxic treatment. Where low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy regimens like ABVD (Adriamycin‐Bleomycin‐Vinblastine‐Dacarbazine) rarely result in POI, treatment with moderate‐ to high‐dose alkylating chemotherapy regimens like BEACOPP (Bleomycin‐Etoposide‐Adriamycin‐Cyclophosphamide‐Oncovin‐Procarbazine‐Prednisolone) and allogenic stem cell transplantation (ASCT) are associated with a higher risk of ovarian failure even in younger women below the age of 40. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 In recent register‐based Nordic studies, the parenthood rate in HL is comparable to a matched background population, though they have a higher rate of assisted reproductive treatment. 7 , 8 Age at diagnosis is a predictor of risk of POI posttreatment, but more studies with structural follow‐up on repeated measurements of ovarian function markers in relation to clinical outcomes are needed to improve fertility preservation counseling for this patient group. 6 , 9

In the Nordic countries, healthcare is free, and fertility preservation and assisted reproductive technology (ART) come with no cost to the patient. Over the past 2–3 decades, fertility preservation programs have been established in all the Nordic countries, and patients with cancer or other life‐threatening diseases can be referred for pretreatment fertility counseling and fertility preservation when deemed necessary. Initially, patients with cancer were referred to our specialized clinic regardless of cancer type or planned treatment, and many were offered fertility preservation. However, little is known about the spontaneous fertility rate and the use of ART with the stored tissue/gametes in women with HL. The purpose of this study was to assess the reproductive outcomes in women referred to a single, specialized fertility center due to a diagnosis of HL.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The study is a single‐center, cohort study including all female patients referred to our clinic for fertility preservation due to a diagnosis of HL between 1999 and 2023. Data about diagnosis, cancer treatment, gynecological, obstetrical, and medical history was collected from paper‐based and electronic medical records from public Danish hospitals, retrospectively from 1999 to 2023, and prospectively in women visiting the clinic from 2020 to 2023. Follow‐up ended on December 31, 2023. Female characteristics such as age (years), antral follicle count (AFC, n), and anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH, pmol/L) were collected as continuous variables.

Patients were grouped by type of chemotherapy into low, moderate, or high‐dose regimens in relation to the risk of POI by the expected total cyclophosphamide equivalent dose (CED) from the protocols described in the medical records. High‐dose chemotherapy included regimens with expected total CED≥10 g (ASCT or other high‐dose regimens), moderate risk total CED 5‐9 g (BEACOPP), and low risk, total CED <5 mg (ABVD). 10 , 11

Outcome measures were the rate of POI after chemotherapy, defined as at least two values of follicle stimulating Hormone (FSH) ≥25 IU/L 4–6 weeks apart and amenorrhea or irregular cycles for at least 4 months in women aged <40 years.

Live Birth Rate (LBR) was defined as the number of women with at least one live birth per woman with a pregnancy wish.

The total fertility rate (TFR) was defined as the number of live births per woman with a pregnancy wish.

Information about pregnancy wish was retrieved from medical records based either on direct documentation of a pregnancy wish, information on pregnancies and deliveries, or attempts to conceive through ART.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were made in SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.4. Fisher's exact and T‐test were applied for comparison of groups and means, while logistic regression analyses were applied for binary outcomes (pregnancy, delivery) with continuous variables such as age, AFC, and AMH. In case of different variances, we have used Satterthwaite tests or Welch tests. Descriptive statistics are used to describe the population.

The age at referral varied from 14 to 32 years, with a mean age at diagnosis of 23.9 (4.9) years. Of the 59 women, 21 received low‐risk chemotherapy (36%), 15 moderate‐risk (25%), and 20 high‐risk chemotherapy (34%) (Table 1). In three women, the type of chemotherapy could not be identified from the available medical records and was unknown. The mean age in the group of women with low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy (25.6 (4.1) years) was higher than in the groups with moderate (22.4 (3.8) years) and high‐dose chemotherapy (22.3 (5.7) years) (p = 0.04). The overall mean follow‐up time was 8 years, varying from 1 to 21 years. The mean age at the end of follow‐up (EOF) was 31 years (15–50 years), with 7 patients younger than 21 years at EOF. A total of 5 women died from HL before EOF, all within 1 to 3 years from diagnosis. All after high‐dose alkylating chemotherapy. Two women emigrated within 2 years from diagnosis and were lost for follow‐up. One after low dose and one after moderate‐dose alkylating chemotherapy.

AMH at the time of diagnosis was available in 14 patients (24%) (low dose: n = 1, moderate dose: n = 8, high dose: n = 5). All AMH values were within normal levels in relation to age at time of diagnosis except for two women who had AMH values below 5 pmol/L; one woman was 29 years old with oligomenorrhea but premenopausal levels of FSH (<25 IU/L); the other woman was 31 years old with an intrauterine hormonal device, regular periods, and a normal FSH (<25 IU/L). Both developed POI after chemotherapy. In this historical cohort, 86% (n = 51) of the patients had ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC): 85% in the group with low‐dose (n = 18), 80% with moderate‐dose (n = 12), and 95% with high‐dose alkylating chemotherapy (n = 19) (Table 1). In two women with OTC before gonadotoxic treatment, the type of chemotherapy was unknown. The reason most women were offered OTC instead of IVF for fertility preservation was lack of time before the planned start of gonadotoxic treatment. Among the eight women who did not have OTC, two had medical ovarian protection with GnRH agonist during chemotherapy only (1 low dose and 1 moderate dose), and six had no fertility preservation before gonadotoxic treatment (2 low dose, 2 moderate dose, 1 high dose, and 1 unknown). Two women with HL in adolescence had fertility preservation with IVF and oocyte/ embryo‐vitrification in later life due to low ovarian reserve after gonadotoxic treatment.

Low‐dose chemotherapy

A total of 20 women were available for follow‐up. The follow‐up time varied from 2.5 to 17.5 years (mean (SD): 9.2 (4.1) years). One woman was lost to follow‐up due to emigration directly after the end of chemotherapy. No women developed POI during follow‐up in the group with low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy (Table 2). Out of the 21 women, 18 had a documented pregnancy wish, and 1 had an unwanted pregnancy that was terminated. Among those with a pregnancy wish, 17/18 (94%) achieved at least one pregnancy and live birth with their own gametes, resulting in a TFR of 1.7 per woman (Table 3). The one woman who did not fulfill her pregnancy wish had a hysterectomy due to cervical cancer about 2 years after the end of chemotherapy. A total of 41 pregnancies and 30 deliveries were observed, with 85% of all pregnancies and 83% of all deliveries following natural conception, and 12% of all pregnancies and 17% of all deliveries following ART (Table 3). All but one woman, who delivered at gestational age (GA) 34 weeks, delivered at term. The mean age at first pregnancy after chemotherapy was 31.0 years (SD 3.1, 22–35). Among women with a pregnancy wish, all but three had OTC for fertility preservation before chemotherapy (Table 4). They all achieved pregnancy without the need for OTT (Table 3). No women treated with low‐dose chemotherapy had OTT during follow‐up (Table 4).

Moderate‐dose chemotherapy

Among the 15 women, one emigrated 1.5 years after diagnosis and was lost to follow‐up. Follow‐up time varied from 0.8–18.3 years (mean (SD): 7.6 (5.8)). During follow‐up, 4 of 15 women developed POI (Table 2). The risk of POI was 27% (95% CI 8.8–55.1), which was significantly higher than after low‐dose chemotherapy (0%) (p = 0,022) (Table 2). In this group, 12 (80%) women had OTC for fertility preservation before chemotherapy, one had GnRH agonist treatment only, and 2 had no fertility preservation procedures (Table 1).

Nine women had a pregnancy wish, resulting in 17 pregnancies in nine women and 12 live births in eight women (Table 3). The chance of having a live birth was 89% per woman with a pregnancy wish, with a TFR at 1.5 per woman with a pregnancy wish. One woman had two preterm deliveries at GA 35 weeks, and the rest delivered at term. Among women with a pregnancy wish, eight had OTC before chemotherapy (Table 3). Of these, three women (38%) had OTT after gonadotoxic treatment due to low ovarian reserve and unfulfilled pregnancy wish (Table 4). All three got pregnant after OTT, and two had a live birth (Table 4). The remaining five women with OTC before chemotherapy all got pregnant and delivered without the need for OTT. Among the women with a pregnancy wish, one had no fertility preservation before chemotherapy. She had two live births and one delivery after insemination during the study period. A total of 17 pregnancies and 12 deliveries were observed, with 77% of all pregnancies and 83% of all deliveries following natural conception, and 23% of all pregnancies and 17% of all deliveries following ART (Table 3). The mean age at pregnancy after chemotherapy was 28.8 years (SD 3.2, 25–32) (Table 3). One woman without a pregnancy wish had an unwanted pregnancy that was terminated in the first trimester (not included in Tables 3 and 5).

High‐dose chemotherapy

Out of 20 women, four died within 1 year from diagnosis, and one died 2.5 years after diagnosis. For these women, no information about POI was available. The follow‐up time in women surviving more than 1 year after diagnosis varied from 1.0 to 11.3 years (mean (SD) 5.2 (3.4) years). Among the 16 women in whom information about ovarian function was available, 63% (95% CI 35.4–84.8) developed POI (n = 10), representing a significantly increased risk of POI compared to low‐dose treatment (p < 0.0001) (Table 2). The risk of POI did not differ significantly between groups with moderate‐ and high‐dose chemotherapy (RR 2.3, 95% CI 0.9–5.9; p = 0.073, data not shown).

Eight women had a pregnancy wish. Out of these, five conceived (75%) and three (38%) had a live birth, with a total of eight pregnancies and six live births (Table 3). The TFR was 0.75 per woman (Table 3). All eight women had OTC before chemotherapy, and six of these (75%) had OTT after gonadotoxic treatment (Table 4). In five women, the indication for OTT was POI, while one woman had OTT due to an unfulfilled pregnancy wish and low ovarian reserve after gonadotoxic treatment (Table 4). Among the six women with OTT, four got pregnant and two delivered (Table 3). Further, one woman got pregnant and delivered after egg donation, following two unsuccessful OTTs (pregnancy outcome not included in Tables 3 and 5). The two women who did not have OTT remained premenopausal during follow‐up; one had three naturally conceived pregnancies and deliveries (follow‐up 11 years), while the other had an unfulfilled pregnancy wish (follow‐up 3 years). A total of eight pregnancies and six deliveries were observed, with 75% of all pregnancies and all deliveries (100%) being achieved from natural conception, and 25% of all pregnancies, but no deliveries, following ART (Table 3). The mean age at pregnancy after chemotherapy was 26.8 years (SD 5.6, 21–36) (Table 3).

AMH, AFC, age, ovarian function, and pregnancy after chemotherapy

Of all the women in this cohort, 38 expressed a pregnancy wish (Table 5). The mean time from diagnosis to first pregnancy was 57 months (4.8 years; range 17–165).

Overall, the mean age at diagnosis was significantly lower in pregnant than in non‐pregnant women (24.4 ± 4.2 vs. 29.4 ± 2.9 years), with a linear association between increasing age at diagnosis and lower pregnancy probability (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.37–0.99; p = 0.044). However, no relation was found between the age at diagnosis and the chance of delivery (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.64–1.02; p = 0.078). No significant difference in the mean age at first delivery was observed between the different groups of chemotherapy (p = 0.09) (Table 3). We were not able to identify any association between AMH or AFC at the time of diagnosis and the chance of pregnancy or delivery (Table 5).

Women with POI after chemotherapy had a lower chance of childbirth compared to premenopausal women (Table 5). Across chemotherapy groups, the risk of POI was not associated with age, AMH, or AFC at diagnosis. However, there was a trend towards a lower AMH level at diagnosis in women who developed POI compared to those who remained premenopausal during follow‐up (AMH 6.9 pmol/L [95% CI (3.2 to 9.7)] vs. 15.5 pmol/L [95% CI (7.9 to 22.6), p = 0.07]). (Table 2).

Most women (27/38) conceived naturally, accounting for 83% (n = 60) of all pregnancies and 85% (n = 46) of all deliveries (Table 5). This was consistent across chemotherapy groups. A total of thirteen women (37%) underwent ART without egg donation, resulting in 11 pregnancies in eight women and 7 deliveries in five women. The LBR after ART was 39% (5/13), and the TFR was 54% (7/13) without oocyte donation. In one woman, the mode of conception was unknown. Eight women had unsuccessful ART: four later conceived naturally, one had egg donation and three did not get pregnant before EOF.

Fertility preservation and pregnancy

Among women with a pregnancy wish, 33 (87%) had OTC before gonadotoxic treatment, one had medical ovarian protection with GnRH agonist only, and four (11%) had no fertility preservation (Table 5). Of the 33 women with OTC before chemotherapy, 22 achieved a pregnancy and livebirth without OTT (67%), and nine women returned for OTT (27%) to fulfill their pregnancy wish. One woman had a first delivery without OTT but developed POI and returned for OTT due to secondary infertility.

Among the nine women who returned for OTT, five had POI, and four had ongoing ovarian function (Table 4). In the four women, OTT was performed because of low ovarian reserve or advanced maternal age to increase the pregnancy chance by adding younger oocytes.

The overall return rate for OTT was 18% (9/51), and 20% (9/46) when excluding deceased women (n = 5) and those younger than 18 years (n = 1) at the EOF. Among women with POI, the return rate was 64%. Two women underwent two OTT procedures—one due to an unfulfilled pregnancy wish after the first procedure and one because of recurrent POI. One woman had three OTTs, all due to persistent unmet desire for pregnancy, but she never conceived.

The median time to first OTT was 2.5 years (IQR 1.5–8.2) from diagnosis, ranging from 1.3 to 15.4 years. The interval between first and second OTT varied from 1.8 to 4.5 years (median 2.2, IQR 1.9–4.5). In total, seven women achieved nine pregnancies after OTT, of whom four had five deliveries (Table 4). All deliveries followed natural conception. Three women had unsuccessful IVF after OTT. Across chemotherapy groups, the LBR after OTT was 44% (4/9 women), with a TFR of 0.55 (five deliveries to nine women).

DISCUSSION

This cohort study reports a total of 72 pregnancies and 54 deliveries in 33 of 52 adult HL survivors, giving a chance of at least one live birth of 63% per adult woman and 87% among women with a known pregnancy wish. In existing literature, the proportion of HL survivors giving birth is heterogeneous. In studies with significantly reduced delivery rates, Anderson et al. reported a hazard ratio of 0.56 (0.49–0.64) for livebirth in HL survivors compared to a matched cancer‐free general population control group, and van der Kaaij et al. reported an OR at 0.64 (95% CI: 0.52–0.77) for live birth in female HL survivors compared to a matched general population control group and a 76% chance of at least one live birth among women with a pregnancy wish. 12 , 13 Other studies report delivery rates comparable to the background population like the Danish register study by Øvlisen et al., who reported parenthood rates of 64% among HL survivors vs. 61% in the background population, and a Canadian register study by Luong et al. with RR at 0.99 (95% CI: 0.75–1.07) for childbirth after HL compared to matched cancer‐free controls from the general population. 8 , 14 In our study, the delivery rate after chemotherapy in the subpopulation of women with a pregnancy wish was reassuringly high, with 87% of women having at least 1 live birth after cancer. This is consistent with other studies where 75%–81% had at least one live birth after cancer. 9 , 12 , 15 Discrepancies in rates could result from underestimation of numbers if women without a pregnancy wish are included, which was the case in the register‐based studies by Anderson et al., Øvlisen et al. and Luong et al. and in the case–control study by van der Kaaij et al. 8 , 12 , 13 , 14 However, in the study from van der Kaaij et al., they also did a subgroup analysis, where the chance of live birth was evaluated in HL survivors with a pregnancy wish only. 12 In our study, 15% of all deliveries came from ART compared to about 11% in the Danish background population (www.Sundhedsdatastyrelsen.dk). This is a little more than reports from the existing literature, where the proportion of deliveries after ART in female HL patients varies from 2 to 12%. The difference between studies can reflect differences in the availability of reimbursement for ART locally.

In our study, the chance of at least one live birth after cancer was related to the type of chemotherapy, with reduced rates after high‐dose compared to low‐dose alkylating regimens. No difference was seen between low‐dose and moderate‐dose regimens. This is consistent with reports by Øvlisen et al. in which neither ABVD (low dose) nor BEACOPP (moderate dose) decreased the parenthood rate in female HL survivors compared to the general population. 8 Likewise, another Scandinavian register study by Entrop et al. showed that women treated with BEACOPP had a similar cumulative incidence of childbirth as those treated with ABVD 10 years after diagnosis. 7 Information about a pregnancy wish was not available in the register studies. An older study by Kiserud et al. reported a reduced chance of parenthood after high‐dose chemotherapy for HD (27%) compared to low‐dose chemotherapy (55%) and in women above 30 years at diagnosis with attempt of pregnancy after chemotherapy (18% >30 years vs. 77% ≤30 years). 9 In the study by van der Kaaij et al., the chance of pregnancy after cancer in women with a pregnancy wish decreased with increasing doses of alkylating agents, second‐line therapy (OR 0.27, p < 0.001), and in women above 35 years at diagnosis (OR 0.13, p < 0.001). 12 In our study, no relation was seen between the chance of delivery and age or ovarian reserve markers at diagnosis. We have not been able to identify any studies that evaluated the association between the chance of delivery and ovarian reserve markers at diagnosis in HL survivors. The lack of an association with age in our study may be explained by the fact that most studies report differences in reproductive outcomes primarily among women diagnosed after the age of 30, with most of our patients being younger than 30 years old at the time of diagnosis, the oldest being 32.

OTC was the most common form of fertility preservation in this historical cohort, mainly due to lack of time to perform other types of fertility preservation before start of gonadotoxic treatment. In our study, the majority of the women with a pregnancy wish had a single ovary due to OTC at diagnosis. Among these, 67% delivered without the need for OTT. In the low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy group, 17 of 18 women with a documented pregnancy wish achieved a live birth after completion of chemotherapy, out of whom 15 had OTC before chemotherapy. Together with the absence of POI in women after low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy, this has led to a change in our national recommendations, where fertility preservation is no longer recommended to women with planned low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy. However, as previous studies have reported lower live birth rates among women diagnosed at >30–35 years of age, fertility preservation in this group can be considered before chemotherapy, given the risk of age‐related fertility decline compounded by treatment‐related delays. 9 , 12 In our cohort, OTT was performed only in women with a pregnancy wish after moderate‐ and high‐dose alkylating chemotherapy. The usage rate of frozen ovarian tissue was about 20% in adult women of reproductive age, which is higher than in the total Danish population of women with frozen ovarian tissue and general reports of utilization rates in the literature. 16 , 17 In our population of HL survivors, the LBR after OTT was 44%, which is similar to what we observed in the total population of women after OTT from our center, where the clinical pregnancy rate was 56% and the total LBR 0.4. 17 It is higher than the pooled PRs (37%) and LBRs (28%) after OTT reported in the metanalysis and review from 2022 by Khattak et al. 18 In another study by Lotz et al. from the German FertiPROTEKT network, the pregnancy rate after OTT was 35% in women with HL, which was identical to the pregnancy rate reported (33%) in the total cohort of women with OTT (33%). 19 The higher chance of pregnancy and delivery observed in our study in women with HL compared to the general OTT population reported in the literature may reflect that women with HL are most often younger at diagnosis and time of OTC, which is known to be related to better reproductive outcomes. 18 , 19

In our cohort, GnRH agonist was given as the only fertility preservation procedure to two women. Although this is not the standard practice in Denmark, it is sometimes given due to the patient's wish after thorough information about the controversies of the efficacy in the literature. The efficacy of GnRH agonists as medical ovarian protection in fertility preservation is continuously debated in the literature, and evidence for protection comes from retrospective analyses only. 20 , 21 , 22

In this cohort, no women developed POI after low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy (ABVD), whereas about 27% and 63% with known POI status developed POI after moderate‐ and high‐dose alkylating regimens, respectively. No association was seen between the risk of POI and the age or ovarian reserve markers at diagnosis. In a study by Falorio et al., 25% of women with HL had POI 7 years after treatment. 20 In the study by van der Kaaj, POI was seen in 20% of HL patients 16 years after treatment, with an increasing risk with increasing dose of alkylating chemotherapy and increasing age. 6 The relation to age is also seen in a study by Behringer et al., where the risk of POI was increased in women older than 30–35 years at diagnosis. 23 In our cohort, only 4 patients were older than 30 years at diagnosis, which can possibly explain why we did not find any association between the risk of POI and age at diagnosis. Age is related to ovarian reserve, and studies in women with breast cancer have shown that the risk of POI can be associated with age and AMH at diagnosis. 24 However, we have not been able to identify studies that evaluated the association between the risk of POI and ovarian reserve markers in women with HL. In our study, women who developed POI after treatment had a 50% lower AMH value at diagnosis as compared to the values seen in women with ongoing ovarian function after chemotherapy, but the results did not reach the level of significance, probably due to the low numbers.

The strength of our study is a long follow‐up period and access to national medical patient records ensuring information about all treatments and deliveries in Danish public hospitals. However, the number of pregnancies and miscarriages can be underestimated because some women are treated/followed for early miscarriages in the private sector. Also, no structural follow‐up on ovarian function was planned back in time, which explains why we did not have uniform follow‐up time or detailed information about the time of onset of menopause, and thus could not assess the reproductive lifespan with time to, or age at menopause after the end of chemotherapy. Due to the small sample size and the young age of the population at diagnosis, we were unable to evaluate the impact of ovarian reserve markers or provide recommendations about which women are at increased risk of POI based on these markers or their age at diagnosis. Larger prospective studies are needed to enable clearer recommendations and provide more precise counseling for our patients.

CONCLUSION

The risk of POI differs in relation to the type of chemotherapy, with expected higher rates after high‐dose compared to low‐dose alkylating regimens. No women developed POI after low‐dose alkylating chemotherapy, and fertility preservation is therefore no longer recommended for this group based solely on the planned chemotherapy. However, if time allows, fertility preservation with embryo‐ or oocyte vitrification may be considered in women aged >35 years at diagnosis, due to the expected age‐related decline in fertility during the time it takes to undergo chemotherapy and the subsequent recovery after treatment. Even though we were not able to assess the reproductive lifespan in women after chemotherapy, we found that the pregnancy rates were reassuringly high, with deliveries in about 3 out of 4 HL survivors, despite the majority of the women having only one ovary due to OTC at diagnosis. The chance of delivery was higher after low‐dose compared to high‐dose chemotherapy. Only women treated with moderate and high‐dose chemotherapy needed OTT to conceive, with one in five women with POI after chemotherapy returning for OTT. Larger numbers are needed to evaluate the association between the risk of POI and age, AMH, and AFC at baseline in the different treatment groups.
