Healthcare, caregiver and human capital costs associated with anxiety and depression among Indonesian youths
Authors: Karen Arulsamy, Elmeida Effendy, Sarah Mardhiyah, Mustafa M Amin, M Surya Husada, Vita Camellia, Anne-Claire Stona, Eric Andrew Finkelstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Indonesia, at least 5% of youths aged 10–17 report having a diagnosed mental health condition. Despite evidence from other countries showing substantial economic costs associated with youth mental health conditions, data on their broader economic impact in Indonesia remain limited. This study estimates the economic costs of anxiety and depression among Indonesian youths aged 10–17.MethodsThis study employs a low-cost web panel approach to estimate economic costs, providing a replicable model for countries lacking data. We administered a cross-sectional online survey to 3038 Indonesian citizens (adult proxies) aged at least 18 years via a web panel. Respondents filled out the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) on behalf of youths in their household to capture prevalence rates, resulting in data on 4328 youths. Proxies of youths who screened positive for anxiety and/or depression symptoms based on the PHQ-4 (N=174) were then asked about mental healthcare utilisation (medication use, outpatient visits, and ED visits and hospitalisations), days missed from work by caregivers to provide care, and days missed from school and reductions in productivity resulting from mental health symptoms. Healthcare utilisation and caregiver costs were monetised and extrapolated based on unit costs/average wages, prevalence rates within the sample and national youth population counts.Results10.6% reported symptoms consistent with anxiety and/or depression, yet 68.9% were never formally diagnosed, indicating a substantial diagnosis gap. Direct healthcare costs averaged IDR1 319 872 (US$83.15) per youth. Lost productivity of caregivers due to missing work to take care of these youths averaged IDR7 510 576 (US$473.17) per youth. These costs generate total annual medical costs of IDR5577.54 billion (US$0.35 billion) and total caregiver costs of IDR27 771.06 billion (US$1.75 billion). Overall, the total annual economic burden is IDR33 348.60 billion (US$2.1 billion). Youths with these symptoms also missed 187.1 hours of school per year and experienced a 45% decline in school performance.ConclusionsEvidence-based interventions should be adopted to address the significant health and economic burden associated with these symptoms.

Background

A significant proportion of mental health conditions emerge early in life.14 A recent meta-analysis found that the global onset of symptoms for the first mental disorder occurs before age 18 in nearly half of all cases.5 Among these conditions, depression and anxiety are the most common. They contribute to over 10 million disability-adjusted life-years annually.6 Experiencing these conditions early in life is associated with unhealthy behaviours, adverse physical health outcomes and increased risk of suicide and suicide attempts.712 Youths with these conditions also experience poorer economic outcomes later in life, including less educational attainment, lower earnings, greater unemployment and lower retirement preparedness.1319 In Indonesia, at least 5% of youths between 10 and 17 years old report a diagnosed mental health condition, although evidence on their wider impact is limited.2022

Cost-of-illness studies, which typically account for both direct costs (eg, healthcare utilisation) and indirect costs (eg, productivity losses, caregiving burden), estimate the overall economic burden of a disease at individual, family and/or societal levels. These studies support priority setting by allowing policy-makers to compare the costs of different illnesses and allocate resources accordingly. Evidence on the broader economic costs of youth anxiety and depression remains limited, with most studies focusing on high-income countries, while such cost-of-illness evidence remains scarce in Indonesia, an upper-middle-income country.23 Overall, this evidence reveals significant costs for these conditions, both in terms of healthcare utilisation and lost labour market productivity for caregivers. Bodden et al24 examined a sample of depressed adolescents (aged 12–21) in the Netherlands and estimated an average annual family cost of US$20 329 and total cost of US$39.6 million.24 Cost-of-illness studies assessing similar cost attributes focusing on anxiety disorder estimated annual costs of US$3878 per Dutch child with an average age of 12 (total cost of US$21 million) and up to US$6405 per American child between 0 and 17 years old.2529 In Singapore, anxiety and depression among youths (aged 4–21) resulted in a per capita cost of US$7642 (total cost of US$0.9 billion) with over 190 hours missed from school per year and reduced school performance.30 These studies highlight substantial indirect costs from productivity losses incurred by parents or caregivers providing care to a child with symptoms and increased school absences.24 29 Increased school absences and potential reductions in school performance can affect human capital accumulation and subsequently impact university completion, employment choices and income, leading to greater long-term economic costs.

Evidence on the overall monetary cost of anxiety and depression among youths in low-income to middle-income countries is scarce and unavailable for Indonesia. Large-scale household surveys are the gold standard for estimating economic burden but are often too time-consuming and costly. While estimates from other countries exist, they may not be generalisable to Indonesia due to differences in culture, help-seeking behaviours and access to mental healthcare. In Indonesia, there is evidence of public stigma toward mental health conditions, including cases where individuals have been shackled (a practice known as ‘pasung’), which may further discourage help-seeking.3133 Low mental health literacy appears to be a contributing factor, with stigma decreasing among those who have had personal contact with someone experiencing mental health issues or who have a family member with such conditions.31 Psychiatric consultations are covered by Indonesia’s National Health Insurance following referral from a general practitioner, but multiple referrals may be necessary to access clinical psychologist services for free. Further to covered services, individuals may also choose to pay out-of-pocket, although these costs can be large, especially for lower income households.34 The availability of public insurance for specialist mental healthcare in Indonesia is notable, as the cost-of-illness studies cited previously are mostly based on countries with partial or limited public insurance coverage for specialist services. However, despite public insurance availability, mental healthcare supply remains limited, and its impact on waiting times is unclear. For example, Indonesia has a ratio of psychiatrists of 1:121 000 compared with WHO recommendations of 1:30 000. Out of Indonesia’s 514 districts, only 216 have psychiatric services, and just 62 districts employ at least one clinical psychologist in public primary care.3436 These factors likely contribute to a high treatment gap—approximately 10% of youths with symptoms receive formal mental healthcare in Indonesia.37

Our study addresses this gap by quantifying the economic burden of diagnosed and undiagnosed anxiety and depression in terms of mental healthcare utilisation among youths and associated caregiver burden in Indonesia. To do so, we employ a low-cost and expedient web panel survey designed to also capture overall prevalence rates. The survey was first fielded to a broader sample (N=3038) to capture prevalence rates and a smaller sample (N=174) who reported symptoms of anxiety and/or depression.303840 In this study, prevalence estimates are based on the validated Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) which assesses core symptoms of anxiety and depression, answered by an adult proxy.41 Our economic burden estimates comprise two key measures: healthcare expenditures based on monetising the value of proxy-reported mental healthcare utilisation and absenteeism based on the market value of self-reported days missed from work to provide care for youths due to their anxiety and depression symptoms. We also present results on the human capital burden of anxiety and depression captured via days missed from school and reduced school performance. The approach is easily replicable in countries lacking current data and provides a model that can be adapted.

Methods

Participants

We fielded a cross-sectional online survey from 14 August 2024 to 18 November 2024 to Indonesian citizens aged at least 18 years old who are part of an online platform hosted by TGM Research.

TGM Research is a market research company with a strong presence in Southeast Asia through its online panels. In Indonesia, their panel includes over 270 000 active users—representing nearly 10% of the total population. These respondents were asked to fill out the PHQ-4 on behalf of all youths aged 10–17 years old in their household. The PHQ-4 consists of the 2-item PHQ-2 and the Generalised Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2). The PHQ-2 and GAD-2 are shown to have high sensitivity (83% and 88%, respectively) and specificity (90% and 82%, respectively) in detecting symptoms of anxiety and depression.41

Due to its brevity and ease of administration, the PHQ-4 is commonly used as a screener for anxiety and depression, including in clinical settings.42 Respondents are asked to rate the extent to which youths in their household were bothered by the following problems in the past 2 weeks: feeling nervous, anxious or on edge; not being able to stop or control worrying; feeling down, depressed or hopeless; little interest or pleasure in doing things based on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). If a youth screened positive for symptoms of anxiety or depression (ie, following the recommended cut-off point of ≥3 on either the PHQ-2 or GAD-2), the respondent was then directed to a longer survey which included questions on mental healthcare utilisation, schooling absenteeism and performance, and caregiving responsibilities in relation to the youth.41 If more than one youth screened positive, the oldest child took priority. Due to consent laws in Indonesia, we relied on adult proxies to report on youths rather than surveying youths directly.

Measures

The approach detailed above provides us with four main measures as discussed below:

Prevalence rates of anxiety and/or depression

Prevalence rates are estimated by dividing the number of youths who scored 3 or more on the PHQ-2 or GAD-2 by the total number of youths across all households in the sample. Proxies are also asked questions on whether youths in their household have been diagnosed with anxiety and depression by a physician. By comparing responses to these questions and the PHQ-4, we also estimate the diagnosis gap in the sample.

Mental healthcare utilisation

To capture healthcare utilisation, we asked questions on the use of medication, extent of outpatient visits to healthcare providers and number of serious medical events modelled on the Medical Expenditures Panel Survey.43 Proxies are asked whether the youth is currently taking any medications for symptoms of anxiety and depression and the frequency and duration of consumption. Specifically, respondents were asked the following: Is your child currently taking any prescription medications to treat his/her depression/anxiety symptoms?; How often does your child take prescription medications to treat their depression/ anxiety symptoms? How long has your child been taking prescription medications to treat their depression/ anxiety symptoms?. In terms of outpatient visits, we captured visits to non-specialist providers (eg, General Practitioner, Family Medicine), psychiatrists and psychologists in the past 3 months. The number of visits was multiplied by four to obtain the estimated number of annual visits. Specifically, respondents were asked: In the last 3 months, has your child visited (including tele-visits) any of the following healthcare providers (non-specialist, psychiatrists, psychologists) in an outpatient setting because of his or her depression/anxiety symptoms? If yes, please enter the number of visits. For visit types that have more than one healthcare provider listed, select all that apply. Proxies were also asked whether the youth was hospitalised or visited an emergency department (ED) in the past 12 months and the number of nights and visits, respectively.

To estimate the costs of healthcare utilisation associated with anxiety and depression, unit costs were applied to each type of service based on case-based tariffs obtained from the Regulation of the Ministry of Health in Indonesia (Permenkes Number 3/2023). For our analyses, we take a conservative approach and use the midpoint of the relevant tariffs. Although we collected data on the number of nights an individual was hospitalised, we did not collect information on the number of hospitalisation events. In Indonesia, hospitalisation costs tend to remain consistent regardless of the length of stay. Therefore, for those who reported any hospitalisation, we assumed one hospitalisation event per year to estimate associated costs.44 The unit costs are provided in online supplemental Material 1. To obtain per-person healthcare cost estimates, we averaged costs across all respondents in the sample including those who report no healthcare utilisation. This approach accounts for the fact that not all youths with anxiety and depression seek treatment.

Total healthcare cost estimates are calculated by multiplying estimated youth citizen population counts by the estimated prevalence rate of anxiety and depression and the average per-person cost estimates. In terms of population size, we focus on those aged 10–19 years old based on the most recently released census data in 2020 and exclude foreign citizens (approximately 10%), resulting in 39 866 235 youths.45

School absenteeism and performance

The human capital burden of anxiety and depression is assessed through school absenteeism and performance based on an adapted version of the Workplace Productivity and Impairment Questionnaire: Specific Health Problem V2.46 School absenteeism is estimated by multiplying the estimates of weekly hours missed from school due to depression and anxiety symptoms by the typical number of weeks in a school year (ie, 38 weeks). Schooling performance is captured by asking proxies the extent to which the youth’s symptoms of anxiety or depression affect their performance at school on a scale of 0–10 with 0 being ‘no symptoms or symptoms had no effect on the youth’s school performance’ and 10 being ‘symptoms completely prevented the youth from attending school’.

Caregiving burden

Proxies are asked who cared for the youth when they stayed home due to symptoms of anxiety and depression and the total number of hours missed from work for all caregivers in the past week due to caregiving responsibilities. Specifically, respondents were asked: During the past 7 days, how many hours did the caregiver(s) miss from work because of problems associated with your child’s depression/anxiety symptoms? Include hours missed on days they did not work at all, days they went in late, days they left early, etc because they cared for the child (eg, accompanied them to their doctor appointments, visited hospitals or clinics, or stayed with them while at home). Do not include time missed to participate in this study. Based on these questions, we estimate total hours lost annually due to caregiving responsibilities (multiplying weekly hours lost by 48 weeks) and monetise this based on average hourly wages. Hourly wages are calculated by dividing the average monthly income in Indonesia (IDR3 040 719) by 160, the typical number of working hours in a month.47 We chose to use the national average monthly income rather than the income reported in our sample, as it provides a more representative measure of the population. Total caregiving costs are calculated by multiplying average person costs with the estimated prevalence rate, proportion of youths with symptoms who missed school and had caregiver support in the sample (87.5%), and Indonesian youth population counts.

We applied winsorisation at the 95th percentile on all continuous healthcare utilisation, school absenteeism and performance, and caregiver burden to limit the effect of outliers while minimising sample loss.48 This method involves replacing values larger than the 95th percentile with values at the 95th percentile. We present burden estimates for anxiety and depression combined as opposed to separate estimates for each condition due to the high degree of comorbidity between these conditions and high variance in the per-person estimates.

Two pilot tests with over 50 respondents from TGM Research’s online panel were conducted to refine the survey before full fieldwork. These respondents were broadly representative of national gender and age distributions. Based on the criteria detailed above, if a child in the household screened positive for symptoms, the main respondent was routed to a longer survey covering healthcare utilisation, caregiving burden and school absences. Following insights from the pilot data, we added a logic check question and removed counsellors/social workers from the list, as this category was rarely selected by respondents.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of the research.

Results

Prevalence of anxiety and depression

Table 1 shows the overall sample characteristics, prevalence rates of anxiety and depression, and diagnosis gap. Among 4328 youths, 10.6% had symptoms consistent with anxiety and/or depression. Among those with symptoms, a majority of youths had symptoms of anxiety only (4.1%), followed by depression (3.4%) and comorbid anxiety and depression (3.1%). Comorbid cases represent nearly 30% of all cases in our sample. Among youths with symptoms of anxiety and depression, nearly 70% had not previously been diagnosed by a physician, indicating a large diagnosis gap. 11.7% of girls had symptoms of anxiety and depression compared with 9.5% of boys. This difference of 2.2 percentage points is statistically significant at the 5% level and is consistent with prior studies that find a gender gap in psychological distress among Indonesian youths.21 49 50

Mental healthcare utilisation

Table 3 presents mental healthcare utilisation among youths in the sample. Over 50% visited a healthcare provider, either in person or online, in the past 3 months for symptoms of anxiety and depression. Among these youths, 85.3% consulted a non-specialist provider, 77.5% consulted a psychiatrist and 68.6% consulted a psychologist. On average, respondents reported at least four visits to a healthcare provider. Nearly 50% were currently taking prescription medications for their symptoms. Most youths take these medications daily and have been taking them for between 1 and 5 months. 42% reported serious medical events in the past year—31.6% reported direct hospitalisation without an ED visit, 28.7% reported an ED visit, and 19% reported an ED visit with hospitalisation. On average, proxies reported at least 3–5 nights/visits in relation to serious medical events.

School absenteeism and performance

School absenteeism and performance are calculated based on the sample of youths (N=159) who are currently attending classes in an academic setting. Proxies reported that youths missed an average of 4.9 hours (SD=5.9) of school in the past week due to symptoms of anxiety and depression. This adds up to 187.1 hours missed per year and over 20 days missed from school in a typical school year due to symptoms. Proxies reported a ‘presenteeism’ score of 4.5, meaning that youths with symptoms were 45% less productive at school.

Caregiver burden

Among proxy respondents who reported that their child missed school due to mental health symptoms, 80.8% reported that they cared for the youth during that time. 33% reported that their spouse or co-parent provided care, and 19.2% reported that another family member or friend provided care. A majority (52.5%) reported relying on a single source of care, while 34.9% reported at least two sources of care and the rest did not report a particular source. Proxy respondents reported that caregivers missed on average 8.2 hours of work (SD=8.3) in the past week to provide care for the youth. This adds up to 395.2 hours missed per year on average, assuming 48 weeks worked.

Economic burden of anxiety and depression

Table 4 presents the breakdown of per-person and total costs by direct costs (healthcare) and indirect costs (caregiving) and the overall economic cost of anxiety and depression among youths in Indonesia. Direct mental healthcare costs due to symptoms of anxiety and depression averaged IDR1 319 872 (US$83.15; IDR1=US$0.000063) per youth. These costs are multiplied by the prevalence rate of 10.6 and population counts to obtain overall estimated healthcare costs of IDR5577.54 billion (US$0.35 billion). Workplace absenteeism costs incurred by caregivers to provide informal care to youths with symptoms averaged IDR7 510 576 (US$473.17) per youth. These costs are extrapolated following a similar approach used for healthcare costs resulting in a total cost IDR27 771.06 billion (US$1.75 billion). Summing these costs yields a total cost attributable to anxiety and depression among youth of IDR33 348.60 billion (US$2.1 billion) per year. Caregiver costs account for the majority of costs (83.3%) and mental healthcare utilisation costs account for the remaining 16.7%.

Discussion

In this study, 10.6% of youths exhibited symptoms of anxiety and depression. This prevalence is higher than the prevalence rate of depression (5.1%) documented in the National Health Survey 2018 (Laporan Nasional Riskesdas) among 15–17 years and the prevalence rate of mental disorders (5.5%) in the National Adolescent Mental Health Surveys conducted among 10–17 years in 2021. This is plausible as both these surveys used diagnostic tools, the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview and Diagnostic Interview Schedule for Children, to capture prevalence rates.2022 While commonly used to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression, the PHQ-4 is not an official diagnostic tool. Therefore, among those who reported symptoms based on the PHQ-4, not all youths may meet the criteria for an official diagnosis, although they may still incur costs and school absences due to their symptoms. Our survey was also administered online, whereas the National Health Survey and National Adolescent Mental Health Surveys were administered face-to face by an interviewer. Prior studies show that under-reporting for potentially sensitive information may be more of a problem among interviewer-administered as opposed to self-administered surveys.51

This is the first study to estimate the economic burden of anxiety and depression among Indonesian youths. Mental healthcare costs averaged IDR1 319 872 (US$83.15) per youth with these symptoms while caregiver costs averaged IDR7 510 576 (US$473.17) per youth. Based on a prevalence rate of 10.6% in the sample extrapolated to the population level, anxiety and depression resulted in IDR33 348.60 billion (US$2.1 billion) in costs annually. The overall economic burden accounts for 0.15% of Indonesia’s GDP as of 2023 (US$1.37 trillion).52 Prior cost-of-illness studies have largely focused on high-income countries and find that costs for depression and anxiety among youths account for up to US$20 329 per family within a year.2430 In comparison, our per youth cost estimate is lower and may reflect a lower exchange rate and higher treatment gap. However, the total national economic burden exceeds estimates from studies conducted in higher-income countries and likely reflects a larger population base and greater labour productivity losses due to caregiving.

Within the overall sample, nearly 70% were not formally diagnosed, potentially indicating a large diagnosis and treatment gap. While substantial, the treatment gap reported in our sample is lower than the over 90% figure reported in other studies focusing on Indonesia.21 53 This could be because our youth sample comes from households with more educational attainment and income than the general population and may, therefore, be more aware of and can access formal mental healthcare. Further research is needed to understand factors that contribute to the treatment gap. Notably, over 50% of proxies reported serious medical events which are associated with significantly higher costs, including ED visits and hospitalisations that may be avoidable with early intervention. Proxies also reported that youths missed over 20 days school due to their symptoms, with significant impairments in school performance. These findings suggest substantial human capital losses, potentially leading to long-term negative educational and economic outcomes.

This study estimates the economic burden of anxiety and depression using an affordable and expeditious approach. However, there are significant limitations. The main limitation is the reliance on an online panel and proxy reports. compared with the general population, this sample is of higher income and educational attainment than the general population and thus may have greater awareness of and access to mental healthcare. Therefore, we cannot guarantee that our sample with depression and anxiety symptoms is representative of the broader population with these symptoms. Due to consent laws in Indonesia, we relied on adult proxies to report on youths rather than surveying youths directly. Moreover, obtaining valid and ethically acceptable parental consent, followed by asking youths to complete the survey, presents significant challenges. Moreover, as minors are considered a vulnerable population in research, proxy reporting reduces the ethical risks of asking children directly about potentially distressing topics such as anxiety, depression or suicidality—especially in the absence of a face-to-face interviewer who could provide support. Depending on their age, youths may also struggle to understand the survey without in-person guidance. While the use of proxy reports addresses these issues, they may not accurately capture the extent of mental health issues among youths. Proxy reports, as opposed to young people’s own accounts, may both overestimate and underestimate mental health symptoms and associated costs, making it difficult to determine the direction and magnitude of this bias.

There is also a possibility of selection bias where proxies for youths with greater levels of distress and/or mental healthcare use were more likely to fill out the longer survey. Finally, we excluded other costs that may be relevant, including diagnostic costs, other sources of care such as spiritual healers, complementary medicine, etc and transportation costs. We also did not capture the burden of unpaid caregiving or the opportunity costs associated with caregiving related to these symptoms. Although respondents were instructed to report only work absences related to caregiving for youth with symptoms of anxiety and depression, they may have also included hours missed for caregiving related to other reasons. We assumed that symptoms lasted for the entire year, but it is possible that for some youths, the symptoms only persisted for a portion of the year. Results indicate that per capita costs increase with higher educational attainment and income. However, it is difficult to draw firm conclusions due to the smaller sample sizes within education and income subgroups. Future studies should aim to corroborate these results using alternative methods, potentially relying on larger and more representative samples and merging data with official medical records to obtain objective measures of healthcare utilisation. Future research can also explore factors such as stigma that contribute to the high treatment gap and delayed diagnosis, as well as the potential benefits of early identification. Face-to-face surveys may help reach lower-income households in Indonesia; however, they typically incur considerably higher costs compared with online surveys. Longitudinal studies can also provide evidence on the long-term impact of anxiety and depression on economic outcomes and the effectiveness of prevention and treatment strategies.

Conclusions

Among 10.6% of youths with symptoms, nearly 70% had not been formally diagnosed by a healthcare provider, indicating a large diagnosis and treatment gap. This study estimates that anxiety and depression among youths cost the economy IDR33 348.60 billion (US$2.1 billion) per year. Results indicate not only short-term losses due to mental healthcare utilisation and caregiver costs but also potentially longer-term consequences due to increased school absenteeism and performance. This highlights the need for evidence-based strategies to address the large health, human capital and caregiver burden of common mental health conditions among youths in Indonesia.
