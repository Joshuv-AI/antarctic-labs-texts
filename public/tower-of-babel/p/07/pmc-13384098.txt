Clinical Characteristics and Prognostic Factors of Patients with Acute Poisoning: A Retrospective Observational Cohort Study of Diquat, Single-Drug and Mixed-Drug Exposures
Authors: Hongyu Han, Lihua Gao, Han Cui, Dan Li, Yanan Hou, Jing Jiang, Hongna Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute poisoning remains a major emergency and toxicology problem, but its clinical pattern differs by toxin type and local availability. Diquat poisoning is increasingly reported where paraquat use has been restricted, whereas metamizole exposure remains relevant in regions where dipyrone remains clinically available.ObjectiveThis retrospective observational cohort study described clinical characteristics, treatment patterns and prognostic factors in acute poisoning involving diquat, single-drug pharmaceutical exposure and mixed-drug exposure.MethodsWe analysed 385 adults with confirmed acute poisoning treated at a single tertiary toxicology centre from January to June 2025. Patients were classified as Group A (diquat, n = 125), Group B (single-drug poisoning, predominantly metamizole, n = 143) or Group C (mixed-drug poisoning, n = 117). Baseline features, laboratory findings, interventions and 30-day outcomes were compared. A presentation-based logistic regression model assessed mortality predictors, and early haemoperfusion was analysed separately in the diquat subgroup.ResultsGroup A had the highest 30-day mortality (28.8%) compared with Group B (5.6%) and Group C (14.5%) (p < 0.001). Liver injury, renal dysfunction, cardiac biomarker elevation, lactate elevation and acidosis were most pronounced after diquat exposure. In the diquat subgroup, haemoperfusion within 6 hours was associated with lower mortality than delayed haemoperfusion (17.1% vs 47.6%, p < 0.001), including after propensity score matching. Diquat poisoning, delayed presentation, low Glasgow Coma Scale score, hypotension, elevated lactate, renal dysfunction, acidosis and early multi-organ dysfunction were associated with mortality.ConclusionAcute poisoning showed heterogeneous clinical patterns by toxic agent category. Diquat poisoning carried the greatest mortality and multi-organ injury, whereas mixed-drug poisoning showed intermediate severity. Early haemoperfusion was associated with improved outcomes in diquat poisoning, but causal interpretation is limited by the retrospective design; prospective multicentre validation is needed.

Introduction

Acute poisoning continues to be a major global public health concern and a leading cause of emergency department (ED) presentations and intensive care utilisation. Recent epidemiological data indicate that acute poisoning accounts for approximately 1–3% of all visits to specialised poisoning units and 4–40% of intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, with reported mortality rates of approximately 3–6% in severe cohorts.1,2 The burden of poisoning differs across regions according to drug availability, agricultural practices, regulatory policies, patterns of self-harm and access to specialised toxicology services.

Acute poisoning encompasses pharmaceutical, agricultural, recreational and industrial compounds. Among agricultural toxins, bipyridine herbicides such as diquat and paraquat are particularly important because of their high toxicity and limited antidotal options.3,4 In China and several other agricultural settings, restrictions on paraquat availability have been accompanied by increased use of diquat as an alternative herbicide, and recent clinical reports have described increasing numbers of severe diquat poisoning cases.3–8 Although diquat differs from paraquat in tissue distribution and does not accumulate preferentially in pulmonary tissue, severe exposure may rapidly produce acute kidney injury, hepatic injury, myocardial injury, central nervous system injury and multi-organ dysfunction.5–8

Single-drug poisoning, particularly involving common analgesics and antipyretics, represents another substantial category of acute intoxication. In the present cohort, metamizole (dipyrone) is the predominant agent in the single-drug group. Metamizole has been withdrawn or restricted in some countries because of the risk of agranulocytosis, but it remains available by prescription or over the counter in many regions, making its poisoning burden geographically uneven.9 Toxic exposure may cause hypotension, allergic reactions, haematological toxicity and organ injury, and its clinical management differs from that of both herbicide poisoning and mixed-drug intoxication.

Mixed-drug poisoning, also known as polysubstance poisoning, has emerged as an increasingly prevalent and complex clinical challenge. Contemporary data indicate that polysubstance exposure accounts for a substantial proportion of drug overdose presentations and is involved in many drug-related deaths.10,11 Polydrug use may reflect intentional self-harm, recreational use, drug substitution or unintentional exposure through drug combinations and adulteration.12,13 Pharmacological interactions between drug classes complicate diagnosis and increase the risk of respiratory depression, cardiovascular collapse, delayed toxicity and death.

The comparison of diquat poisoning, single-drug pharmaceutical poisoning and mixed-drug poisoning is clinically meaningful because these groups represent common but distinct toxicological pathways encountered in specialised poisoning practice: high-lethality agricultural herbicide exposure, predominantly single-agent pharmaceutical exposure and complex polysubstance exposure. These categories differ in toxicokinetics, organ injury patterns, treatment pathways and prognosis. The present study, therefore, aims to compare clinical characteristics, laboratory abnormalities, treatment strategies and prognostic factors across these three poisoning categories while using a cautious observational interpretation of treatment–outcome associations.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care centre with an established medical toxicology service and a dedicated poisoning ICU for patients requiring critical care. No primary centres participated in the study; all included cases were managed at the same tertiary hospital. The study protocol received approval from the institutional ethics committee (approval no. LBNET-ERC-EC-2026-D-004), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of data collection. All patient data were anonymised and handled in accordance with relevant data protection regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Population and Enrolment

The study population comprised all adult patients with confirmed acute drug or chemical poisoning who presented to the ED and were managed by the toxicology service between 1 January and 30 June 2025. Patients requiring organ support or close monitoring were admitted to the poisoning ICU, whereas less severe cases were managed in monitored toxicology beds. Therefore, the ICU admission rates reported in the treatment-modality table refer to formal ICU admission during hospitalisation, not to the eligibility criterion. Systematic case identification was performed through electronic medical record review using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification codes T36–T50 and T51–T65.

Patient Grouping Strategy

Enrolled patients were stratified into three clinically distinct groups based on the type and number of toxic agents involved. Group A comprised patients with confirmed diquat poisoning, identified through a clinical history of herbicide exposure and confirmed by positive qualitative or quantitative diquat detection in blood or urine specimens using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. Group B included patients with single-drug poisoning, defined as intoxication involving one pharmaceutical agent; metamizole was the predominant agent in this group, followed by sedative-hypnotics, acetaminophen, antidepressants and opioids. Group C consisted of patients with mixed-drug poisoning, defined as concurrent or sequential exposure to two or more distinct pharmaceutical agents or drugs of abuse; the median number of substances in this group was three (interquartile range [IQR]: 2–4; range: 2–7).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible patients were 18 years or older, had documented acute drug or chemical exposure within 24 hours before hospital presentation, had poisoning confirmed by clinical presentation and toxicological testing or reliable exposure evidence and had complete data for the primary outcome and key baseline variables required for group classification and mortality analysis. Patients were excluded if they had severe pre-existing chronic diseases that could confound outcome assessment, including advanced malignancy, Child–Pugh class C liver disease or severe chronic kidney disease with an estimated glomerular filtration rate below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2; if medical records were insufficient to determine exposure group or 30-day outcome; if they were pregnant or lactating; or if they were transferred after major resuscitative interventions at another facility.

Clinical Data Collection

A standardised data extraction form was developed and piloted before formal data collection to ensure consistency and completeness. Trained research personnel conducted a systematic review of the electronic medical records, nursing documentation and laboratory information systems to extract relevant clinical information. Baseline demographic characteristics, including age, sex, body mass index, comorbid medical conditions and relevant social history, were recorded. Detailed poisoning-related data were collected, including (1) circumstances of poisoning, such as intentional self-harm, accidental exposure or recreational use; (2) specific toxic agent identification, with the quantity ingested when available; (3) time interval from poisoning exposure to ED presentation; and (4) pre-hospital interventions administered, including emesis induction or activated charcoal administration by emergency medical services.

Clinical assessment parameters obtained at hospital presentation to the poisoning ICU included vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature and oxygen saturation); level of consciousness evaluated using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS); and systematic physical examination findings, with particular attention to the cardiopulmonary, gastrointestinal and neurological systems. Comprehensive symptom documentation included, but was not limited to, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, altered mental status, seizures, respiratory distress and cardiovascular manifestations.

Laboratory Investigations

Standardised laboratory evaluation was performed at hospital presentation and serially thereafter according to clinical indication. Tests included complete blood count with differential, serum electrolytes, renal function indices, liver function tests and glucose and, when clinically indicated, arterial blood gas analysis, cardiac biomarkers, inflammatory markers and coagulation studies. Arterial blood gas analysis was available for 285 patients (74.0%), including 116 in Group A, 86 in Group B and 83 in Group C; these results were analysed as an available-case laboratory subgroup because arterial sampling was guided by clinical severity rather than performed universally.

Cardiac biomarkers, including high-sensitivity troponin I and creatine kinase-MB, were measured in patients presenting with chest pain, electrocardiographic abnormalities or suspected myocardial injury. Inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein and procalcitonin, were measured when infectious complications were suspected. Coagulation studies, including prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time and international normalised ratio, were performed in patients with bleeding manifestations or in those receiving anticoagulation for extracorporeal therapies.

Toxicological analysis is a critical component of laboratory assessment. Qualitative urine drug screening was performed using immunoassay techniques for commonly abused drugs, including opioids, benzodiazepines, amphetamines, cocaine metabolites and cannabinoids. Confirmatory testing using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry or liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry was conducted for positive screening results or when clinical suspicion persisted despite negative screening results. Quantitative measurement of specific toxins was pursued when available and clinically relevant, including serum drug levels of acetaminophen, salicylates, lithium and valproic acid, as well as herbicide concentrations in patients exposed to diquat.

Treatment Protocols

Standardised treatment protocols were implemented according to institutional guidelines for patients with poisoning, with modifications based on specific toxin characteristics and individual patient factors. Initial stabilisation followed the Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability and Exposure approaches, with particular emphasis on airway protection and ventilatory support. Patients with a GCS score ≤8 or those demonstrating inadequate airway protective reflexes underwent endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation with lung-protective ventilation strategies.

Gastrointestinal decontamination procedures were selectively employed based on the timing of presentation and the nature of the ingested substance. (1) Gastric lavage was performed in patients presenting within 1 hour of life-threatening poisoning ingestion using a large-bore orogastric tube, with return of gastric contents until the effluent became clear. (2) Activated charcoal administration (1 g/kg body weight, maximum 50 g) was provided to patients presenting within 1–2 hours of ingestion, provided that airway protection was adequate and the ingested substance was adsorbed by charcoal. Multiple-dose activated charcoal was considered for patients who ingested substances with enterohepatic recirculation or delayed absorption. (3) Cathartic agents, including sorbitol and magnesium citrate, were administered judiciously to enhance gastrointestinal transit, with careful monitoring of electrolyte disturbances and volume depletion.

Extracorporeal blood purification techniques were implemented according to poison-specific indications and timing considerations. (1) Haemoperfusion was performed using activated charcoal- or resin-based cartridges in patients with confirmed diquat poisoning, severe theophylline toxicity or poisoning with other highly protein-bound substances that are amenable to adsorptive removal. The timing of haemoperfusion initiation was recorded, with particular attention paid to whether treatment commenced within 6 hours of exposure. Sessions typically lasted 4 hours, with blood flow rates maintained at 150–200 mL/min, and repeat sessions were performed based on clinical response and repeat toxin levels, when available. (2) Intermittent haemodialysis was employed for patients with severe lithium toxicity, methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning or severe salicylate toxicity, utilising high-efficiency dialysers with ultrafiltration coefficients of >10 mL/h/mmHg and blood flow rates of 250–300 mL/min. (3) Combined haemoperfusion and haemodialysis were used in select cases in which both adsorptive removal and diffusive clearance were deemed beneficial.

Specific antidotal therapy was administered when available and clinically indicated. N-acetylcysteine was administered for acetaminophen poisoning according to standard nomogram-based or empirical dosing protocols. Naloxone was administered for opioid-induced respiratory depression, with appropriate monitoring of withdrawal symptoms. Flumazenil was selectively used for benzodiazepine-induced central nervous system depression in the absence of seizure risk or chronic benzodiazepine dependence. Other antidotes, including fomepizole for toxic alcohol poisoning, sodium bicarbonate for tricyclic antidepressant toxicity and lipid emulsion therapy for severe local anaesthetic or lipophilic drug toxicity, were employed according to clinical necessity.

Supportive care measures were tailored to individual patient requirements and patterns of organ dysfunction. (1) Haemodynamic support included intravenous crystalloid resuscitation guided by clinical assessment and invasive or non-invasive haemodynamic monitoring. Vasopressor and inotropic agents, including norepinephrine, vasopressin and dobutamine, were titrated to maintain a mean arterial pressure of >65 mmHg and adequate tissue perfusion. (2) Respiratory support ranged from supplemental oxygen via a nasal cannula or face mask to invasive mechanical ventilation with strategies to minimise ventilator-induced lung injury, including low tidal volume ventilation (6 mL/kg predicted body weight), plateau pressure limitation (<30 cm H2O) and permissive hypercapnia when appropriate. (3) Renal replacement therapy was initiated for acute kidney injury with severe metabolic derangements, refractory hyperkalaemia or uraemic complications, utilising continuous renal replacement therapy for patients who were haemodynamically unstable and intermittent haemodialysis for patients who were stable.

Outcome Assessment and Definitions

The primary outcome measure was 30-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause within 30 days of hospital admission. Secondary outcome measures included the following: (1) length of hospital stay, calculated from ED admission to hospital discharge or death; (2) ICU admission requirement and length of stay; (3) development of specific organ dysfunction and complications categorised as hepatic injury (aspartate aminotransferase [AST] or alanine aminotransferase [ALT] elevation greater than five times the upper limit of normal), acute kidney injury (defined according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes criteria as an increase in serum creatinine by 0.3 mg/dL or more within 48 hours or an increase to 1.5 times or more from baseline), myocardial injury (elevation of high-sensitivity troponin I above the 99th percentile, with clinical features consistent with cardiac injury) and respiratory failure (requirement for invasive mechanical ventilation or PaO2/FiO2 ratio <300); and (4) clinical disposition at discharge, categorised as complete recovery (return to baseline functional status without residual organ dysfunction) or partial recovery (persistent mild organ dysfunction or disability requiring ongoing medical management).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post hoc tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H-test with Dunn–Bonferroni post hoc tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons when the overall test was significant.

Univariate logistic regression was used to examine variables associated with 30-day mortality. The main multivariable model was designed as a presentation-based prognostic model and included variables available at presentation or within the first 24 hours, including poisoning type, time to presentation, GCS score, systolic blood pressure, lactate, creatinine, arterial pH and early multi-organ dysfunction. Post-baseline interventions, including mechanical ventilation and haemoperfusion timing, were not included in the main prognostic model to avoid mixing treatment decisions with baseline risk prediction. The timing of haemoperfusion was assessed separately in the predefined diquat subgroup analysis.

Variables with clinical relevance and p < 0.10 in univariate analysis were considered for multivariable modelling. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and variables with a variance inflation factor >10 were not entered together. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Receiver operating characteristic curve-derived cut-offs were used for exploratory risk stratification and were interpreted as cohort-specific thresholds rather than externally validated clinical decision rules.

The primary outcome and key baseline variables required for the main analysis were complete for all included patients. Laboratory tests obtained according to clinical indication, including arterial blood gas analysis and selected cardiac biomarkers, were analysed using available cases, and the number of patients with available measurements is reported in the relevant tables. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, because this was a retrospective observational cohort including all eligible patients during the defined 6-month period; the precision of estimates was therefore evaluated using 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses restricted to patients with complete laboratory data showed the same direction of association for the main prognostic factors.

For the predefined diquat subgroup analysis, propensity score matching was used to reduce imbalance between patients who received early haemoperfusion (within 6 hours of exposure) and delayed haemoperfusion (more than 6 hours after exposure). Propensity scores were estimated using age, sex, estimated ingested dose, time to presentation, GCS score, systolic blood pressure, lactate, creatinine and arterial pH. One-to-one nearest-neighbour matching without replacement was performed with a calliper of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Balance after matching was assessed using standardised mean differences, with values below 0.10 considered acceptable Treatment-effect estimates from the matched cohort were interpreted as exploratory subgroup findings rather than causal estimates.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Demographics

During the 6-month study period, 412 patients with suspected acute poisoning were managed by the toxicology service at the tertiary centre. Twenty-seven patients were excluded because of incomplete exposure verification, insufficient outcome documentation, pregnancy or lactation, severe pre-existing diseases or transfer after major treatment elsewhere, leaving 385 patients for the final analysis. Group A comprised 125 patients with diquat poisoning (32.5%), Group B included 143 patients with single-drug poisoning (37.1%) and Group C consisted of 117 patients with mixed-drug poisoning (30.4%). In Group B, metamizole accounted for most cases, whereas Group C involved a median of 3 substances (IQR: 2–4). Clinical Outcomes and Complications

The clinical outcomes demonstrated marked variations across the three poisoning groups (Table 4 and Figure 2). The overall 30-day mortality rate for the entire cohort was 15.8% (61 deaths among the 385 patients). Mortality was substantially higher in Group A (28.8%, 36/125 patients) than in Group B (5.6%, 8/143 patients) and Group C (14.5%, 17/117 patients) (p < 0.001). Among the individual continuous predictors, serum lactate level demonstrated strong prognostic value, with an AUC of 0.847 (95% CI: 0.803–0.891, p < 0.001). The optimal lactate cut-off value for mortality prediction was 4.2 mmol/L, yielding a sensitivity of 78.7% and specificity of 81.2%. The GCS score showed an AUC of 0.823 (95% CI: 0.776–0.870, p < 0.001), with an optimal cut-off of ≤8, providing a sensitivity of 73.8% and specificity of 84.6%. Serum creatinine at presentation demonstrated an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI: 0.745–0.847, p < 0.001), with an optimal cut-off of 2.8 mg/dL achieving a sensitivity of 68.9% and specificity of 82.7%.

The pH value obtained from arterial blood gas analysis yielded an AUC of 0.809 (95% CI: 0.761–0.857, p < 0.001), with an optimal cut-off of 7.26, providing balanced sensitivity (75.4%) and specificity (78.9%). Time from exposure to hospital presentation, when analysed as a continuous variable, demonstrated an AUC of 0.718 (95% CI: 0.658–0.778, p < 0.001), with diminishing survival probability as time to treatment increased beyond 6 hours.

A comparison of AUCs using the DeLong method revealed that the multivariate prediction model significantly outperformed individual predictors (all pairwise comparisons, p < 0.01), supporting the clinical utility of integrated risk assessment incorporating multiple prognostic factors rather than relying on single parameters. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded non-significant results (χ2 = 8.34, p = 0.401), indicating good calibration of the final multivariate model, with observed outcomes closely matching predicted probabilities across deciles of risk.

In the crude subgroup comparison, mortality was lower in the early haemoperfusion group than in the delayed haemoperfusion group (17.1% vs 47.6%, p < 0.001). In the propensity score-matched analysis, early haemoperfusion remained associated with lower mortality (adjusted OR: 0.29, 95% CI: 0.14–0.60, p = 0.001). The median length of hospital stay was shorter in the early haemoperfusion group (10 days, IQR: 6–15) than in the delayed group (14 days, IQR: 9–22; p = 0.003), and multi-organ dysfunction involving three or more organ systems was less frequent (32.9% vs 61.9%, p = 0.002).

Among patients who survived to hospital discharge, renal recovery was more frequent in the early haemoperfusion group, with 78.3% of survivors returning to within 20% of baseline serum creatinine by discharge compared with 54.5% in the delayed group (p = 0.012). These findings support an association between earlier haemoperfusion and improved short-term outcomes in diquat poisoning, although residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded in this retrospective analysis.

Correlation Analysis: Laboratory Parameters and Outcomes

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between key laboratory parameters obtained at hospital presentation and clinical outcomes, including hospital length of stay and number of organ systems involved in dysfunction. Peak serum creatinine level during hospitalisation demonstrated a strong positive correlation with length of hospital stay (ρ = 0.687, p < 0.001), indicating that more severe renal injury necessitated prolonged hospitalisation for monitoring and management. Serum lactate concentration at presentation showed a moderate positive correlation with the number of organ dysfunctions (ρ = 0.564, p < 0.001), supporting its role as a marker of illness severity and tissue hypoperfusion.

Liver transaminase elevations, specifically peak AST levels, were moderately correlated with the development of hepatic encephalopathy (ρ = 0.492, p < 0.001) and coagulopathy (ρ = 0.478, p < 0.001). Interestingly, time from exposure to the first blood purification treatment demonstrated a significant negative correlation with survival (ρ = −0.421, p < 0.001), reinforcing the importance of timely intervention. Among the cardiac biomarkers, peak troponin I levels were correlated with both the development of arrhythmias (ρ = 0.536, p < 0.001) and the need for vasopressor support (ρ = 0.612, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This retrospective observational analysis of 385 patients with acute poisoning managed at a tertiary toxicology centre provides clinically relevant information on the heterogeneous presentation, management and outcomes of diquat herbicide poisoning, single-drug pharmaceutical poisoning and mixed-drug poisoning. Diquat poisoning was associated with the greatest severity and mortality, whereas mixed-drug poisoning showed an intermediate risk profile characterised by frequent neurological and respiratory compromise.

The findings are consistent with contemporary hospital admission patterns in which acute poisoning imposes a considerable clinical burden and intentional self-harm represents a major mechanism of exposure.14 The general principles of acute poisoning management apply to most cases,15 but high-toxicity herbicides and polysubstance exposures often require specialised toxicology assessment, rapid risk stratification and close organ function monitoring.

The intermediate mortality rate observed in the mixed-drug poisoning group (14.5%) reflects the increasingly prevalent and challenging nature of polysubstance exposure. Risks associated with combinations of opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol and other central nervous system depressants include enhanced respiratory depression, unpredictable pharmacokinetic interactions and difficulty in clinical assessment.16 The co-ingestion of multiple substances complicates both diagnosis and treatment because synergistic effects may produce toxicity greater than that predicted from individual agents alone. The higher rates of altered mental status (49.6%) and respiratory distress (27.4%) in Group C than in the single-drug poisoning group support this interpretation.

Comparative toxicology of mixed-substance exposure presents unique challenges in emergency management. When patients present with polysubstance intoxication, the clinical picture may be atypical, and the pathophysiology may involve simultaneous effects on several organ systems.17 This complexity supports comprehensive toxicological screening, monitoring for delayed effects and preparedness for unpredictable clinical deterioration. Multi-organ dysfunction in 45.3% of mixed-drug poisoning cases highlights the need for intensive supportive care in selected patients.

Early haemoperfusion within 6 hours was associated with lower mortality in the diquat subgroup. This finding is biologically plausible and consistent with principles of extracorporeal blood purification in toxicology, which emphasise that treatment is more likely to be effective before extensive tissue distribution of removable toxins has occurred.18,19 However, because haemoperfusion timing was not randomised, the observed association should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of causality.

The mechanistic rationale for early haemoperfusion relates to the pharmacokinetics of diquat absorption and distribution. Following oral ingestion, diquat undergoes rapid gastrointestinal absorption, and early circulating toxin may remain accessible to adsorptive removal before more extensive tissue distribution and oxidative injury develop.20 In the present cohort, early haemoperfusion was associated with lower crude mortality than delayed treatment, but unmeasured differences in dose, presentation pathway or treatment selection may have influenced outcomes.

Extracorporeal therapy in acute poisoning may contribute through direct toxin elimination, correction of metabolic derangements and support of organ dysfunction.19,21 In this cohort, 94.4% of patients with diquat poisoning underwent haemoperfusion, with 64.4% treated within 6 hours. These findings support the practical importance of early toxicology consultation, rapid transfer pathways and readiness to initiate blood purification when indicated, recognising that optimal timing and modality require prospective validation.

The prognostic model showed high apparent discrimination, with an AUC of 0.912. This performance should be interpreted cautiously because the model was developed in a single-centre cohort with 61 deaths and may be vulnerable to optimism and overfitting. The model is best viewed as an exploratory risk-stratification tool that identifies clinically plausible high-risk features rather than as a validated prediction rule ready for external implementation.

Severity scoring in poisoning remains challenging because general critical illness scores may not fully capture poison-specific factors such as agent identity, dose, time to presentation and availability of toxin-directed treatment.22,23 The current presentation-based model emphasises variables that are rapidly available in clinical practice, including poisoning category, GCS score, blood pressure, lactate, creatinine, arterial pH and early organ dysfunction. Its high negative predictive value suggests potential value for identifying patients at lower risk, but external validation is required before the model can be used for triage decisions.

Recent investigations have explored biomarkers for outcome prediction in acute poisoning. The prognostic value of lactate in this cohort complements this literature because lactate integrates tissue hypoperfusion, mitochondrial dysfunction, respiratory compromise and impaired hepatic clearance. For diquat poisoning specifically, early biomarker assessment, including procalcitonin within 24 hours, has also shown prognostic utility.24

Multi-organ dysfunction involving two or more organ systems was the strongest clinical marker of mortality in the multivariable model. This finding reflects the convergence of direct toxin-mediated injury, systemic inflammation, tissue hypoperfusion and maladaptive compensatory responses. In the present cohort, 78.4% of patients with diquat poisoning developed hepatic injury, 68.0% developed acute kidney injury and 42.4% developed myocardial injury, underscoring the systemic nature of severe intoxication.

The mechanisms underlying diquat toxicity involve redox cycling and reactive oxygen species generation, leading to oxidative stress and cellular injury across multiple organ systems.20 Unlike paraquat, diquat does not accumulate preferentially in pulmonary tissue; however, this difference does not prevent severe multi-organ toxicity. The high prevalence of combined hepatic and renal injury in the diquat group supports the need for comprehensive organ support rather than isolated organ-directed therapy.

Prevention and management of multi-organ dysfunction should be prioritised in severe poisoning. Early recognition of patients at high risk, supportive care to maintain perfusion and oxygenation, timely blood purification when indicated and serial monitoring of organ injury are central components of care.19 Integration of toxicology expertise with emergency and intensive care services may improve outcomes and optimise healthcare resource utilisation.25

Metabolic Derangements and Outcome Prediction

Serum lactate levels exceeding 4 mmol/L at presentation were associated with increased mortality odds, highlighting the prognostic importance of metabolic assessment. Elevated lactate in acute poisoning may reflect impaired oxygen delivery, cardiovascular or respiratory compromise, mitochondrial dysfunction, enhanced anaerobic glycolysis and impaired hepatic clearance. As an integrative biomarker of illness severity and tissue hypoperfusion, lactate provides readily obtainable information that may guide triage, monitoring intensity and early supportive treatment.

Arterial pH showed prognostic value, with an exploratory cut-off of 7.26 in ROC analysis. Metabolic acidosis may result from lactate accumulation, impaired renal acid excretion, direct acidifying effects of toxins or bicarbonate loss through the gastrointestinal route. The combination of lactate elevation and acidosis was associated with poor outcomes, supporting careful correction of underlying hypoperfusion, renal failure and respiratory compromise rather than reliance on any single laboratory threshold.

The strengths of this study include its focus on a dedicated toxicology service, a relatively comprehensive evaluation of diquat poisoning and metamizole-predominant single-drug poisoning and the inclusion of clinical, laboratory, intervention and 30-day outcome data. Several limitations should be considered. The retrospective single-centre design is vulnerable to selection bias, information bias and residual confounding. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the 6-month enrolment period may limit seasonal and regional generalisability. The poisoning categories were heterogeneous, especially the mixed-drug group. Some laboratory variables, including arterial blood gas measurements, were obtained according to clinical indication rather than universally. The haemoperfusion analysis may be affected by confounding by indication despite propensity score matching, and the prognostic model requires external validation.

Future research should prioritise prospective multicentre studies with standardised treatment protocols, complete toxicological characterisation, including quantitative drug levels, and extended follow-up of functional outcomes and quality of life. Randomised or carefully emulated comparative-effectiveness studies of haemoperfusion timing and modality would provide stronger evidence for treatment decisions. Studies addressing prevention, access restriction, mental health referral and safe storage of high-risk substances are also needed to reduce the incidence of severe diquat and pharmaceutical poisoning.

The practical implications of these findings are that hospitals managing acute poisoning should maintain clear triage pathways for high-risk exposures, rapid access to toxicological testing and protocols for early consultation regarding blood purification. For diquat exposure, clinicians should prioritise immediate decontamination when appropriate, early organ-function assessment and timely consideration of haemoperfusion. For metamizole and other pharmaceutical poisonings, prevention should emphasise safe prescribing, patient education, medication storage and mental health screening. For mixed-drug poisoning, clinicians should anticipate synergistic toxicity and use broad toxicological screening and prolonged monitoring when clinically indicated. At the policy and managerial level, poison-control networks, referral pathways, staff training and public education on the safe storage of herbicides and high-risk medications may reduce preventable morbidity and mortality.

In conclusion, acute poisoning in this tertiary toxicology cohort showed marked heterogeneity by toxic agent category. Diquat herbicide poisoning was associated with the highest mortality and rapid multi-organ dysfunction, whereas mixed-drug poisoning showed intermediate severity with frequent neurological and respiratory compromise. Early haemoperfusion in diquat poisoning was associated with lower mortality, but this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective observational design. Presentation delay, impaired consciousness, hypotension, metabolic derangement, renal dysfunction and multi-organ injury were associated with fatal outcomes and may help guide early risk stratification and management.
