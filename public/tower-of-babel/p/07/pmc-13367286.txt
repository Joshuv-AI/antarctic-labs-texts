Plasma-catalytic dry reforming of methane to syngas using a planar plate DBD reactor packed with Ni/SiO2 fiber catalyst
Authors: Nongnuch Rueangjitt, Arisa Thamaporn, Waratthaya Ananrat, Napida Hinchiranan, Prasert Reubroycharoen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of this research was to investigate the syngas production from dry reforming of methane (DRM) in a planar plate dielectric barrier discharge (DBD) plasma reactor packed with Ni-based catalyst on SiO2 fiber (SF) and SiO2 porous (SP) supports. The SiO2 fiber support was synthesized by sol–gel and electrospinning techniques. Fresh Ni/SiO2 catalysts were characterized by XRD, N2 adsorption–desorption, SEM-EDS, TEM-EDS and XPS. The effects of full and partial packing configurations of the catalyst, Ni loading on SF and SP supports, and applied voltage were investigated on the DBD plasma-catalytic DRM performance. The results showed the partial packing configuration of the catalyst into this studied DBD plasma reactor and the increasing applied voltage gave a positive effect in improving performance. Moreover, the Ni loading and SiO2 support type (SF and SP) had a significant role on the overall system performance. The conditions with partial packing of the 10%Ni/SF catalyst and an applied voltage of 15 kV exhibited good results based on CH4 and CO2 conversions, syngas yield, hydrogen-to-carbon monoxide molar ratio and energy efficiency.

Introduction

The International Energy Agency (IEA) has reported that global energy-related carbon dioxide emissions, one of the main components of greenhouse gases, increased to 37.8 billion tons in 2024 due to high energy demand and extremely rapid economic recovery after a disruption.1 Furthermore, the most recent IEA study found that overall energy-related carbon dioxide emissions increased by 0.8% in 2024 and reached a new record high of 37.8 Gt.1 For this reason, numerous attempts have been devoted to reducing these pollution problems, and researchers are developing new routes to convert them into useful and valuable products. The reforming of methane (CH4) with carbon dioxide (CO2), known as dry reforming of methane (DRM) (eqn (1)), has attracted significant interest as a sustainable alternative due to its ability to reduce CH4 and CO2 greenhouse gases and produce a valuable synthesis gas (H2 + CO), which is used as the feedstock in chemical processes such as Fischer–Tropsh synthesis and methanol synthesis.1CH4 + CO2 → 2CO + 2H2 ΔH298K = +247 kJ mol−1

Due to the limitation of thermodynamic equilibrium and the difficulty in activating stable CH4 and CO2 molecules, a highly endothermic DRM reaction must be carried out at high operating temperatures (above 800 °C). Two serious problems that hinder operational conditions are high energy consumption and rapid catalyst deactivation caused from metal sintering and coking/carbon deposition. The formation of carbon on the catalyst surface occurs by side reactions of methane decomposition (eqn (2)), CO disproportionation/Boudouard reaction (eqn (3)) and carbon monoxide reduction (eqn (4)).2,32CH4 ↔ C + 2H2 ΔH298K = +75 kJ mol−132CO ↔ C + CO2 ΔH298K = −172 kJ mol−14CO + H2 ↔ C + H2O ΔH298K = −131 kJ mol−1

Non-thermal plasma (NTP) assisted DRM reaction is considered a challenging and upcoming approach to overcome thermodynamic limitations because this process can be carried out at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure. Plasma can generate various energetic species and charged particles in the plasma zone at low temperatures, including electrons and radicals with high energies. DBD (dielectric discharge barrier), a type of non-thermal plasma, has been intensively investigated for plasma-catalytic DRM reactions.4–13 The integration of low-temperature plasma with heterogeneous catalysis is regarded as synergistically enhancing the CH4 and CO2 conversions, and improving product selectivity, stability and sintering tolerance.

Ni-based catalysts are the most common ones employed in catalytic DRM reaction, due to their promising activity and selectivity, inexpensive price and widespread availability.2 Many researchers have been conducted to improve catalyst deactivation and increase catalytic performance on Ni-based catalysts3 and SiO2-supported Ni catalyst.14–18 SiO2 fiber (SF) is an attractive support material due to its non-porous structure, contributing to easy accessibility and exposed active sites. Fiber-like structure supports e.g. SiO2 fiber,18–21 CeO2 fiber22 and ZSM-5 fiber23 were studied in previous researches. Wen et al.18 reported the SiO2 nanofibre confined Ni catalyst can improve Ni dispersion, leading to slightly higher initial activity and much better stability than Ni on commercial SiO2 powder support for CO2 reforming of methane process. Mhadmhan et al.20 reported that Ni/SF catalyst gave higher catalytic performance, hydrogen production and stability than the conventional Ni on SiO2 porous (Ni/SP) catalyst in ethanol steam reforming. Ni/SiO2 fiber catalyst has much strong metal-support interactions (SMSI) and more abundant basic sites, leading to improved coke resistance property. Herminio et al.22 reported that Ni/CeO2 fibrous catalyst for CO2 reforming of methane showed high resistance against carbon formation and enhanced catalyst stability after 30 hours at 700 °C. Besides, Harnsinudom et al.11 demonstrated, for the first time, the full and partial packing of nickel catalyst on commercial glass wool support in a planar plate DBD plasma reactor for simulated biogas reforming. The result revealed that the DBD plasma system with partially catalyst packing obviously better performed than the ones with fully catalyst packing in the plasma zone-occurring at a gap between two planar plate stainless steel electrodes.

In this research, the syngas production from dry reforming of methane was investigated in a planar plate DBD plasma reactor packed with Ni/SiO2 fiber catalyst at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, with no additional heating. The effects of packing configurations of catalyst, amount of Ni loading on SiO2 support, catalyst support type, and applied voltage were evaluated.

Experimental

SiO2 fiber (SF) preparation

SiO2 fiber was synthesized by sol–gel assisted electrospinning technique using tetraethyl orthosilicate (99.0% TEOS, Si(OC2H5)4) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich as a Si source. TEOS was dissolved in deionized water (DI water) with a 1 : 2 ratio followed by 37% HCl and 99.9% ethanol with a 0.01 : 2 ratio under stirring at room temperature. After that, the solution was continuously stirred and heated at 55 °C to form the gel. Finally, the mixed gel was transferred into a syringe with a 0.55 mm diameter needle for SF synthesis by electrospinning technique. The electrospinning method was carried out by controlling a distance of 15 cm between the needle tip and electrode, an injection rate of 10 mL h−1 and an applied electric voltage of 15 kV. The obtained SF support was dried overnight at 110 °C and calcined at 500 °C for 2 hours, as shown in Fig. 1.20

SiO2 fiber (SF) preparation by sol–gel assisted electrospinning technique.

Catalyst preparation

Ni catalysts on SiO2 fiber (Ni/SF) and SiO2 porous (Ni/SP) supports with Ni loading of 5, 10 and 15 wt% were prepared by the incipient wetness impregnation method. SP support was a commercially obtained material (Fuji Silysia) with a diameter range of 0.85–1.70 mm. SF support was synthesized by sol–gel and electrospinning techniques. Nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich as a Ni precursor was dissolved in DI water, and then the solution was impregnated into the SF and SP supports. The sample was dried overnight at 110 °C, and then calcined at 500 °C for 2 hours. The obtained Ni/SF catalysts were labeled as 5%Ni/SF, 10%Ni/SF and 15%Ni/SF, and Ni/SP catalysts were marked as 5%Ni/SP, 10%Ni/SP and 15%Ni/SP.

Catalyst characterization

The textural properties of the catalyst, including specific surface area, pore volume and pore size were measured by N2 adsorption–desorption analyzer (3P Instruments, Micro 300). The specific surface area was measured using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, while the pore volume and size of the catalyst were determined using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method. The crystalline structures in the catalysts were recorded by X-ray diffractometer, XRD (Rigaku, SmartLab) equipped with Cu Kα radiation in a scanning 2θ range of 10–70°. The crystal size of nickel metal was calculated using Scherrer equation. The surface chemical composition and chemical valence states of fresh catalysts were obtained by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, XPS (Kratos Analytical, AXIS Supra+). The surface morphology and nanoparticle distribution of fresh and spent catalysts were analyzed by Field Emission-Scanning Electron Microscope, FE-SEM (JEOL, JSM-7610F), and the Ni and C content was determined by an Energy Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS). The image of carbon formed on the spent Ni/SF catalyst was determined by TEM-EDS (JEOL, JEM 2100 Plus); the particle size distribution of fresh catalysts was detected by Transmission Electron Microscope, TEM (JEOL, JEM 2100). Thermogravimetric analyzer, TGA (PerkinElmer, TGA 8000) was performed to evaluate the carbon deposition on the spent catalysts after the plasma assisted-DRM reaction. The types of carbon on spent catalysts were identified by Raman spectroscopy (Horiba, LabRAM HR Evolution).

Plasma-catalytic performance in dry reforming of methane

The experimental set-up of the DBD plasma-catalytic system for dry reforming of methane reaction is shown in Fig. 2. A planar plate DBD plasma reactor is made of acrylic material with a 21.5 cm length, a 9.5 cm width and a 3.85 cm thickness. The two planar-shaped electrodes were fabricated from stainless steel sheets with a 3 mm thickness, and a glass plate with a 2 mm thickness serves as the dielectric material. The electrode gap distance of 3 mm was fixed. The simulated feed gas of methane and carbon dioxide with a CH4/CO2 ratio of 70 : 30, was specially manufactured for this research by Linde (Thailand) Public Co., Ltd with a total feed flow rate of 35 cm3 min−1 controlled by a digital mass flow controller (New-Flow, TLFC). The DBD plasma reactor was supplied by an AC high voltage power source with a high-side voltage of 13 kV and an input frequency of 300 Hz to operate the discharge of plasma generation. The voltage and frequency were manipulated by a function generator (GW Instek, GFG-802OH). The power, current and output voltage at the low-side voltage of power source were measured and monitored by a power analyzer (Lutron, DW6090). The gaseous products was analyzed by a gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, GC-14B) equipped with a Unibeads C packed column and a thermal conductivity detector (TCD)—which is capable of detecting CH4, CO2, CO, H2, O2 and N2, with using the standard gas mixture manufactured from Air Liquide America Specialty Gases LLC (USA) for GC calibration.

Results and discussion

Catalyst characterization

The textural properties of the fresh catalysts are presented in Table 1. The specific surface area and pore volume of Ni/SF catalysts were significantly smaller than Ni/SP catalysts, as clearly shown on SF without Ni loading. The SF support had the lowest specific surface area of 2.892 m2 g−1 and pore volume of 0.011 cm3 g−1, indicating that the obtained SF was a non-porous structure. The increase in Ni loading on SF support resulted in an increase surface area and pore volume while decrease pore radius. The highest surface area of 28.104 m2 g−1 and pore volume of 0.068 cm3 g−1 were obtained at Ni loading of 15 wt% on SF, owing to the characteristics of non-porous materials. Thus, increasing the Ni loading enhances the specific surface area of catalyst.6 However, increasing Ni loading on SP support were significantly decrease the textural property of Ni/SP, certainly caused by the deposition of nickel oxide (NiO) within SP pores. Furthermore, the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm curves are shown in Fig. 3. The Ni/SF catalysts displayed a linear isotherm, confirming a non-porous material. The Ni/SP catalysts exhibited type IV isotherms with H1-type hysteresis loops, indicating a mesoporous structure.

Fig. 6 shows the XPS survey spectra of all fresh catalysts. For the Ni/SF catalysts (5%, 10%, and 15%Ni/SF), the survey spectra revealed the characteristic photoelectron peaks corresponding to Si 2p (∼100–105 eV), C 1s (∼285 eV), O 13s (∼530 eV), and Ni 2p (∼855 eV), confirming NiO impregnated onto the SF support. The presence of C 1s signal in the Ni/SF catalysts is attributed to adventitious carbon contamination, which is commonly observed in XPS measurements and serves as an internal binding energy reference. Notably, the Ni 2p peaks in the Ni/SF catalysts are clearly observable and exhibit increasing relative intensity with higher Ni loading on the fiber surface. The XPS survey spectra of the Ni/SP catalysts (5%, 10%, and 15%Ni/SP) show the prominent peaks for Si 2p and O 1s, but the Ni 2p signals are absent. This observation implies that Ni species in the Ni/SP catalysts are likely incorporated within the internal pore channels of the porous SiO2 structure, so limiting the detection of Ni by the surface-sensitive XPS technique, which typically probes only the outermost 5–10 nm of the sample surface.

Plasma-catalytic dry reforming of methane

Effect of catalyst packing configuration

Fig. 7 displays the full packing of Ni/SF catalyst on the performance in DRM at an applied voltage of 13 kV. When compared to the partial packing of the Ni/SF catalyst illustrated in Fig. 8, it was found that the results of full catalyst packing were consistent with the previous topic on the effect of Ni/SF catalyst partial packing in a plasma reactor, and it was clearly demonstrated that increasing Ni loading on SiO2 support improves DRM performance. The maximum CH4 and CO2 conversions are achieved by fully packing the 10 wt% Ni/SF catalyst combined with plasma. In addition, it had the highest syngas product yield and H2/CO molar ratio. However, the full catalyst packing gave a lower conversion of CH4 and CO2 than the partial catalyst packing in all studied conditions. This is mainly due to the limitations of the plasma discharge area, which has an impact on plasma generation behavior when catalysts are fully packed into the plasma reactor. The decreasing electrical field and plasma discharge density directly affect the frequency of collisions of highly energetic electrons or radicals with stable reactant molecules. Previous research explained that packing 25% of catalyst into the discharge gap results in an excellent combination of microdischarges and surface discharge on the catalyst. However, the generation of microdischarge is significantly reduced in the fully packed catalyst. Moreover, the electrical signals of plasma discharge suggest that partially catalyst packing is similar to the electrical signal profile of without catalyst, and still exhibits strong microdischarges.10–12

Effect of Ni loading amount

The effect of Ni loading amount on the performance of DRM over Ni/SF catalyst under partial packing at an applied voltage of 13 kV is displayed in Fig. 8. The CH4 conversion was greater than the CO2 conversion in all experimental conditions because the bond dissociation energy (BDE) of methane gas (CH3–H; 4.55 eV) is lower than that of carbon dioxide (CO–O; 5.52 eV).24 Compared to the plasma dry reforming of methane reaction without a catalyst, the presence of glass wool which is commonly used to hold catalysts in the reactor has slightly effect on the conversion of CH4 while no effect on the conversion of CO2. The combination of plasma and Ni/SF catalysts significantly enhances the conversion of CH4 and CO2 compared to the plasma DRM in the absence of catalyst.25 Additionally, increasing the Ni loading on the SF support from 0 wt% to 10 wt% resulted in an increase in the trend of both CH4 and CO2 conversion. However, increasing the Ni loading to 15 wt% on SF support led to a slight decrease in the reactant conversion. The CH4 and CO2 conversions reached a maximum of 9.12% and 8.29%, respectively, using partially packed with 10 wt% Ni/SF catalyst. Moreover, Fig. 9 shows the effect of Ni loading on the SP support. The CH4 and CO2 conversions increased when increasing the Ni loading from 0 wt% to 5 wt% on SP support, and the both conversions decreased when increasing the Ni loading to 15 wt%. Considering the SP support, the combination of plasma and the 5 wt% Ni/SP catalyst provides the highest CH4 and CO2 conversions of 8.10% and 6.27%, respectively. The results demonstrate that the partial packing of Ni/SF and Ni/SP catalysts in the plasma DRM reaction at atmospheric pressure and room temperature improves reactant conversion. The synergistic effect in DRM process is attributed to the plasma-catalyst interaction, as well as the specific role of the catalyst in reducing the activation energy of process and providing an alternate pathway for converted CH4 and CO2 to the desired syngas production.4–13

Fig. 8b and 9b indicate the selectivity of the syngas product on the effect of Ni loading on SF and SP supports. The presence of Ni/SF and Ni/SP catalysts significantly decreases the H2 and CO selectivities as compared to plasma DRM without catalysts. This is expected to result from the formation of C2–C3 hydrocarbons and coke through side reactions.11,26 However, the H2 selectivity was higher than the CO selectivity when using these catalysts in the plasma reactor, as contrasted with using plasma without the catalyst and plasma with glass wool. For SF support, the 10 wt% Ni/SF catalyst had the highest H2 selectivity (53.79%), while the 0 wt% Ni/SF catalyst had the highest CO selectivity (43.89%). Meanwhile, the 15 wt% Ni/SP catalyst shows the highest H2 and CO selectivities (51.91% and 45.49%, respectively).

Fig. 8c and 9c illustrate the yield of syngas product produced at various Ni loadings on SF and SP supports. The partial loading of Ni/SF and Ni/SP catalysts in plasma reactor enhanced H2 and CO yield, especially for Ni/SF catalysts, as well as H2/CO molar ratio and energy consumption of system. The 10 wt% Ni/SF catalyst achieved the highest syngas product yield and H2/CO molar ratio. Additionally, it was able to reduce energy consumption when compared to other studies. Thus, the Ni loading on catalyst, catalyst properties, and the plasma discharge behavior play an important role in the DRM performance.

Effect of catalyst support type

The influence of catalyst support type on catalytic performance as shown in Fig. 8 and 9. Overall, the Ni/SF catalyst can improve the reactant conversion for DRM reaction under plasma system when compared to the Ni/SP catalyst due to the physical and chemical suitability of SF catalytic support. According to the literature, Ni supported on SF catalysts demonstrated greater catalytic activity and stability due to the extremely open structure of SF support, which solved the mass transfer limitation problem and improved reactant-active site accessibility.20 In addition, the electrospinning synthesis of a Ni on SiO2 nanofiber catalyst indicated enhanced Ni dispersion, metal-support interaction and catalyst coke resistance, resulting in increased activity and stability in CO2 reforming of methane.20 From all results, the Ni/SF catalyst was chosen to investigate the effects of catalyst packing configuration and applied voltage. As observed the electrode surface in the plasma reactor after DRM reaction, it was found that the physical properties of the packed catalysts affected the plasma discharge behavior. The partially loaded Ni/SF catalyst in the plasma reactor have a larger plasma discharge area than Ni/SP catalyst, which is expected to improve plasma discharge behavior.

Effect of applied voltage

Fig. 10 presented the effect of applied voltage (10, 13 and 15 kV) with partial packing of SF support and 10 wt% Ni/SF catalyst on the DRM reaction. The increased applied voltage promotes the conversion of CH4 and CO2 under the SF support and Ni/SF catalyst. The applied voltage was increased from 10 to 13 kV, resulting in a significantly increase in reactant conversion, whereas rising to 15 kV resulting in a slightly improve in the conversion under the same type of catalyst. Increasing the applied voltage strengthens the electrical field, which is directly proportional to the increase in the average energy of electrons, density of electrons and gas temperature in the plasma zone.26 An excessive increase in applied voltage could cause coke formation on the electrode surface during the DRM process, which can be clearly observed when cleaning the plasma reactor after reaction.

Characterization of spent catalyst

Fig. 11 showed the TGA profiles of spent 10 wt% Ni/SF and 5 wt% Ni/SP catalysts under an applied voltage of 13 kV. From room temperature to 100 °C, the weight of 5 wt% Ni/SP catalyst decreased by almost 1% due to the weight loss of moisture adsorbed on the porous catalyst surface. Meanwhile, the 10%Ni/SF catalyst observed no weight loss in this region, indicating that moisture adsorption did not occur on the non-porous SiO2 fiber surface. In addition, heating both catalysts to 1000 °C resulted in no significant effect on weight loss. It can be inferred that the Ni catalyst on SiO2 support in this study did not have coke deposited on the catalyst surface. In general, the carbon deposits can be clearly observed on the catalyst surface, which may contribute to the blocking of active sites. The Raman spectra of the spent 15%Ni/SF catalyst are presented in Fig. 12. Two prominent bands at approximately 1350 cm−1 (D band) and 1580 cm−1 (G band) are detected at both measurement points of a and b. The D band is associated with defective or disordered graphitic carbon, while the G band corresponds to the stretching vibration of sp2-hybridized carbon in ordered graphitic structures. The relatively sharp and well-resolved D and G bands indicate the formation of well-defined graphitic-type carbon on the Ni/SF surface during reaction. Similarly, the XRD patterns of the spent catalyst (Fig. 13) did not show a peak at 25°, which referred the crystalline structure of carbon. However, the intensity of the structural NiO crystal planes of the spent catalyst decreased as compared to the fresh catalysts. It can be expected that the spent catalyst has a reduced amount of Ni particles or sintering of the catalyst. Moreover, the particle size of NiO crystals were presented in Table 2. The NiO particle size of the spent catalyst increased due to an aggregation of metal particle on the catalyst, which can cause to the deactivation of catalyst. TEM-EDS analysis also revealed that the C species were detected in the two selected regions on the spent 15%Ni/SF catalyst after DRM reaction, as shown in Fig. 14.27

Conclusions

The comprehensive study on the planar plate DBD plasma-assisted dry reforming of methane over Ni/SiO2 fiber catalyst clarified the factors affecting overall system performance. Notably, catalysts supported on SiO2 fiber exhibited superior catalytic performance compared to those on porous SiO2 pellets. Furthermore, the packing configuration within the plasma reactor was highly influential, with a partial packing of the catalyst in the plasma zone demonstrating a significant enhancement in system performance. This improvement is attributed to superior discharge distribution and stable plasma behavior. The applied electrical voltage strongly impacted both system efficiency and plasma discharge stability. Post-reaction characterization via TGA, Raman spectroscopy, XRD, and TEM-EDS confirmed the negligibly small level of coking on the catalyst surface and the structural integrity of the silica fiber support under plasma discharge conditions. The optimum operational conditions for syngas production—achieving maximum CH4 and CO2 conversions, syngas yield, H2/CO molar ratio, and energy efficiency—were identified as 10% Ni/SF catalyst partially packed within the plasma reactor zone at an applied voltage of 15 kV.
