Use of medicinal herbs in an Iranian population: cross-sectional findings from the Fasa PERSIAN Cohort Study
Authors: Seyed Hamdollah Mosavat, Alireza Kamrani Poor, Reza Homayounfar, Mohammad Mehdi Naghizadeh, Mojtaba Farjam, Neda Nayebi, Ayeh Naghizadeh, Fatemeh Amini, Mehdi Salehi, Mohammad Hashem Hashempur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo determine the prevalence, patterns and correlates of medicinal herb use in a rural Iranian population and to evaluate demographic and clinical predictors using adjusted regression models.DesignCross-sectional analysis of baseline data from the Fasa Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran Cohort Study.SettingSheshdeh, a rural district in southern Iran.Participants10 143 adults aged 35–70 years enrolled between 2017 and 2019.Primary outcome measuresPrevalence of self-reported medicinal herb use during the past year and its associations with demographic variables and non-communicable diseases (NCDs).ResultsOverall, 84.7% of participants (95% CI 83.9% to 85.5%) reported herb use. In multivariable logistic regression, higher educational attainment was positively associated with herb use (university education vs. illiterate: adjusted OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.88). No significant adjusted associations were observed between herb use and major NCDs including diabetes, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease or depression. The most frequently used herbs were Zataria multiflora, Echium amoenum and Matricaria chamomilla, most commonly for anxiety/neurasthenia (81.6%), gastric pain (59.6%) and common cold (49.8%).ConclusionsMedicinal herb use is highly prevalent among adults in southern Iran. Educational level, but not chronic disease status, was associated with herb use. These findings highlight the need for integrated public health strategies regarding safe and evidence-based use of medicinal herbs.Keywords: COMPLEMENTARY MEDICINE; Health policy; Herbal medicine; Delivery of Health Care, Integrated; Traditional Persian medicine; Iran

Introduction

Extensive use of traditional and herbal medicine in ancient eastern countries has a history of millennia.1 Traditional Persian medicine (TPM) is one of the oldest systems of medicine, rooted in Zoroastrian teachings from thousands of years ago and has evolved over time through the contributions of numerous scholars.2 3 Although largely supplanted by modern medicine over the past century, the knowledge and practices of TPM have been preserved and passed down through generations. In recent years, Iran has seen a resurgence of interest in researching this field.4 This is in concordance with the worldwide trend to use complementary and complementary medicine (CAM), with herbal medicine being the most popular modality.5 6

Across various countries, for example, the USA, the UK, Australia, South Africa and the United Arab Emirates, there has been a marked increase in CAM usage among the general population over the past few decades.7,13 Despite this growing interest, few population-based studies have explored the prevalence and use of complementary and traditional medicines, including herbal medicine.14 15 In Iran, most studies have focused on specific patient groups.16,20 For example, a large study in southwest Iran found that 68.1% of women attending a healthcare centre had used herbs in the past year, while a community-based study in central Iran reported that 62.5% of participants used CAM, with herbal medicine being the most common choice.1 21

The growing popularity of herbal medicine raises concerns about the lack of data on its risks and benefits. In a national survey of clinical oncologists in the USA, 41.0% of doctors discussed the use of herbal supplements with their patients.5 22 Some studies reported high rates of self-medication with herbal medicines, ranging from 72.6% to 80.0%, while others report much lower percentages, such as 20.0%. These disparities might be due to ethnic and racial differences within populations.14 23 24

In Iran, the use of herbal remedies is deeply rooted in culture and history, formally recognised as TPM. In recent years, the Iranian Ministry of Health has integrated TPM into the national health system, establishing TPM clinics and PhD programmes for medical doctors. Despite these policy advances, a significant portion of herbal use remains informal and self-prescribed. While the WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy emphasises the integration of traditional and complementary medicine, data on the actual prevalence and patterns of use in specific rural populations remain fragmented. We hypothesised that herbal medicine use in this population is driven by socio-cultural factors, specifically education and age, and sought to determine if such use correlates with the presence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs).22 25

The absence of large national surveys obscures the significant health and economic impacts of medicinal plants use. Understanding the prevalence, attitudes and behaviours surrounding herbal medicine through population-based studies is crucial for scientists, clinicians, educators and policymakers. The present study investigated the prevalence, patterns and correlates of medicinal plants use in one of the local centres of the Iranian nationwide cohort study, the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN), conducted by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education in Iran.

Materials and methods

Study population and settings

Participants were recruited through a census-based household recruitment strategy. Study staff visited households, explained the study objectives and procedures, and invited all eligible individuals aged 35 to 70 years residing in the target districts to participate. Eligible participants who consented were enrolled at the local cohort centre and registered using their national identification codes. This recruitment approach was designed to include the full eligible population of the selected rural areas and enhance representativeness of the local cohort. The cohort profile has been previously described in detail.26

Participants were recruited using a household sampling method. Study protocols and objectives were explained by experts who visited participants’ homes. Eligible individuals aged 35 to 70 years were invited to the local cohort centre for enrolment, where written informed consent was obtained. Out of 11 097 individuals within the target age range, 10 622 were eligible and registered using their national identification codes. The age range was selected to capture established behavioural habits and lifestyles, mental and physical ability to comply with study requirements, and sufficient longevity for the observation of NCDs.

Sample size and sampling

This study is a secondary analysis of data from the Fasa Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran Cohort Study (FACS). The cohort was established using a census-based sampling strategy, meaning that all eligible individuals within this age range in the target districts were invited to participate. The sampling was not based on random selection but on the full population of the selected areas to ensure that the study sample was representative of the local rural population. The eligibility criteria for participants included Iranian nationality, at least 1 year of residence in the area, and verbal communication ability.26 Since this study uses secondary data, no additional sampling was required. The original cohort design was powered to assess a wide range of health risk factors, including the prevalence of chronic diseases and demographic associations with health outcomes.

Data collection and measurement

Baseline data were collected as part of the FACS. As described in the published cohort profile, FACS used standardised questionnaires administered by trained interviewers and structured quality-control procedures (including supervision and periodic calibration of instruments). This manuscript presents a secondary cross-sectional analysis of baseline (registration-phase) data.

Questionnaire development and validation

The baseline questionnaires, including items related to medicinal herb use, were implemented within the FACS registration protocol. The herb-use items were developed using common domains applied in prior herb-use/CAM surveys (use/non-use, commonly used products, form of use and self-reported indications) and were adapted to the local rural context. The herb-use questions underwent expert review for face/content validity by specialists in TPM and epidemiology, and the interviewing workflow was piloted during the cohort registration process before the full implementation.

Herb use was defined as self-reported consumption of medicinal herbs by participants during the past year of data collection. Participants were asked whether they used herbal remedies and, if applicable, the reasons for their use. Because respondents could report more than one indication, these items were analysed as multiple-response variables; therefore, percentages for indications may exceed 100%. The denominators used for all herb-use analyses are explicitly stated in the tables.

Herb-use ascertainment and herb identification

Herb use was assessed by asking participants whether they had used any herbal preparation for a health condition during the past year and, if yes, collecting the most frequently used herb name, method of consumption (eg, decoction, infusion, distillate, powder) and self-reported reason(s) for use. To support accurate reporting, interviewers applied standardised probing (including local/common herb names when clarification was needed) while allowing open-ended responses. Reported medicinal plants were subsequently authenticated and matched to scientific nomenclature using standard herbal references. An expert authenticated mentioned medicinal plants according to major herbal references such as ‘Dictionary of Medicinal Plants’, ‘Matching the Old Medicinal Plant Names with Scientific Terminology’, ‘Dictionary of Iranian Plant Names’ and ‘Indian Medicinal Plants’’.27,29 All data were digitised, stored in a secure, codified database and prepared for analysis.

Training and quality assurance

In line with the cohort protocol, interviewers were recruited locally and received standardised training by national cohort experts to administer questionnaires. Interviewers were preferentially assigned based on relevant expertise (eg, nurses for disease/medication-related items). Quality assurance included field supervision and routine checks to reduce measurement error.

Missing data

For this secondary analysis, we described participant flow and handled missing data using complete-case analysis for models requiring full covariate information. Participants with missing herb-use status and/or key covariates were excluded from the corresponding analyses. The number excluded and the final analytic sample size are reported to ensure transparency and consistency.

Assessment of NCDs

NCDs included in this study were defined based on previously documented physician diagnoses recorded in the cohort dataset, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease, and objective measurements performed during the cohort visit (eg, fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL for diabetes; systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg for hypertension).

Participants and exclusions

Out of 11 097 residents in the target area, 10 622 individuals aged 35 to 70 years were eligible and registered. For the current analysis, 479 participants (4.5%) were excluded due to incomplete data regarding medicinal herb usage or missing values for key covariates (education, body mass index (BMI) or NCD history). The final analytic sample consisted of 10 143 participants.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS for Windows, V.15.0 (SPSS). Descriptive statistics were represented as frequencies and percentages. The χ² test was used to analyse differences in categorical sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. The bar chart presented the prevalence of herb usage based on years of education. To aid in comprehension, a second-degree polynomial trend line was added. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For age-stratified herb-specific analyses involving multiple comparisons, p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Adjusted p values are reported where applicable. Other analyses with a limited number of comparisons were not adjusted and are interpreted cautiously.

Patient and public involvement

Participants or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study.

Multivariable analysis

In multivariable logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, gender, marital status and BMI, educational attainment remained a significant predictor of medicinal herb use. compared with illiterate participants, those with a university education had significantly higher odds of using herbs (adjusted OR=1.41; 95% CI 1.11 to 1.88, p=0.006). Conversely, no significant adjusted associations were found between herb use and the presence of diabetes, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease or other assessed NCDs (p>0.05 for all), confirming the unadjusted findings (detailed in the online supplemental table S1).

Discussion

This study highlights a high prevalence of herb use in a rural area of Fars province, among an Iranian population with predominantly Persian ethnicity. Consistent with findings from other cultures with strong traditions of herbal medicine, such as China (86%) and Jamaica (72.6%), our analysis showed that a majority of participants (84.66%) reported using medicinal herbs over the past 12 months, reflecting the continued importance of traditional medicine in these communities, despite the widespread availability of modern healthcare.23 30 31

Moreover, our findings are consistent with studies from other Middle Eastern settings, including Turkey, Jordan and Saudi Arabia, which also report high community reliance on herbal medicine for common symptoms and psychosomatic complaints. In rural Iranian communities, cultural continuity, trust in traditional remedies, limited access to specialist care and perceived safety of herbs often shape health-seeking behaviour. These contextual factors may explain the high prevalence observed in FACS.

Our study also found that the use of medicinal herbs was significantly more frequent among individuals with higher levels of education, aligning with findings from other research.32 33 This finding may suggest that educational attainment can be linked to a greater awareness or acceptance of CAM practices, including herbal medicine. It is possible that individuals with higher education levels are more informed about the potential benefits and applications of medicinal herbs, leading to more frequent use. Our study observed a trend towards more frequent use of medicinal herbs among younger adults, which may reflect a broader cultural shift towards wellness and preventive health. This observation aligns with studies from other regions suggesting that younger populations are increasingly inclined to explore alternative health practices, such as herbal medicine, as part of a holistic approach to well-being.34 35 However, the reasons for this pattern are complex and may involve multiple sociocultural factors, including greater openness to CAM among younger generations, a greater emphasis on natural remedies or even increased access to herbal products via social media and online health communities.7

It is important to consider this finding as a hypothesis rather than a firm conclusion. We suggest that further research, possibly using qualitative methods, could help elucidate the motivations behind younger adults’ use of herbs. Moreover, this generational shift might reflect broader global trends in health-seeking behaviour, where there is a growing interest in self-care and natural health solutions. In some societies, this shift is linked to concerns about the side effects of pharmaceutical treatments and a desire for more natural alternatives, which may be perceived as safer or more holistic. Additional studies that explore cultural, educational and economic factors influencing these trends would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the role of medicinal herbs in modern health practices.

It is important to note that the age-stratified analyses for individual herbs involved multiple statistical comparisons, increasing the potential risk of type I error. Therefore, the significant differences observed in the usage of specific herbs across age groups should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, warranting confirmation in focused future studies. Interestingly, while no significant associations were found with other demographic features, our study did observe a trend towards more frequent use of medicinal herbs among younger adults and slightly more among females. The higher prevalence among younger adults in our study may reflect a growing interest in natural and holistic health approaches among the younger population, which could be driven by a broader cultural shift towards wellness and preventive health. It appears that the use of CAM, including herbal medicine, is more common among younger adults, women and those with higher education levels, as reported by several studies.36,39 However, the findings have been inconsistent across different populations. Some studies, in line with our results, have not found significant relationships between herbal medicine use and demographic factors.39 40 Conversely, other studies have reported an increase in herbal medicine use with advancing age.32 The observed patterns in our study, particularly the higher use of medicinal herbs among younger adults, might indicate a shift in generational attitudes towards traditional medicine. Younger individuals might be more inclined to explore alternative therapies as part of a broader trend towards integrating traditional practices with modern healthcare approaches. This could also reflect a cultural continuity where younger generations maintain traditional practices learnt from older family members, adapting them to contemporary contexts. Moreover, the slight increase in herbal medicine use among females might be linked to women’s roles in family health management, as well as a greater likelihood of women seeking natural remedies for common health concerns such as anxiety, digestive issues and colds, which were among the top reasons for herb use in our study. The interplay between gender, age, education and herbal medicine use is complex and likely influenced by a combination of cultural, social and individual factors that warrant further investigation.41,44

Our survey did not find a significant association between herbal use and any specific medical condition. This finding aligns with other studies that have similarly reported no meaningful correlation between chronic illnesses and the use of medicinal plants.38 One hypothesis to explain this lack of association is that for there to be a significant link between the use of specific herbs and a chronic disease within a population, there should be both scientific evidence supporting the effectiveness of those herbs and a concurrent popularity of their use in that community. Although some studies have found significant associations between herbal medicine use and chronic conditions, it is important to consider the specific populations studied. Many of these studies have been conducted among patient groups rather than the general population, and patients with acute or chronic diseases are often more likely to seek out CAM treatments compared with healthier individuals.36 38 45

Moreover, in the context of Iran, where TPM specialists—who are medical doctors with additional PhD-level training—play a role in prescribing medicinal plants, there is an opportunity to enhance the safety and effectiveness of herbal medicine use. Despite this, many medicinal plant users tend to self-prescribe rather than consult with TPM practitioners or other CAM professionals. This self-prescription practice raises concerns about the potential for misuse or suboptimal use of herbal products, which underscores the need for more rigorous national policies and the establishment of national guidelines and educational programmes. Developing strategies that integrate TPM into the public healthcare system more fully could help ensure that herbal medicines are used safely and rationally, providing a valuable complement to modern medical practices.46,48

Given that over half of the study population reported using Shirazi thyme (Zataria multiflora Boiss) (69.5%), Iranian borage (Echium amoenum Fisch & Mey) (54.2%) or chamomile (Matricaria chamomilla) (53.5%) within the past year, it is crucial to address the quality control of these commonly used medicinal plants in this region. Collectively, there is a necessity of developing policies to deal with a number of issues. First, the importance of quality control becomes evident. Many herbal products may suffer from contamination or variability in quality, which can increase the risk of adverse effects.49 Second, there is a pressing need to enhance public awareness and educate practitioners about the safe and rational use of these herbs. This includes understanding proper dosing, potential drug interactions and contraindications associated with commonly used herbs.50 Third, investing in research to address these issues is essential. High-quality, evidence-based studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of herbal remedies, considering factors such as geographical origin, harvest time and preparation methods. Although many herbs are known for their therapeutic effects, their safe use requires rigorous scientific validation. Despite the generally positive therapeutic outcomes reported, potential adverse effects and interactions with other medications warrant careful consideration. Therefore, long-term studies to assess the safety and efficacy of herbal treatments are necessary.51 Fourth, with advanced laboratory technologies and the ability to analyse active plant compounds, there is a significant opportunity to develop new pharmaceuticals from natural sources and enhance the integration of traditional and modern medical practices.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its design. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes any inference of causality between herb use and health outcomes; we cannot determine if herb use preceded the onset of NCDs. Second, data on herb use were self-reported covering the past year, introducing the possibility of recall bias. Social desirability bias may also have influenced responses, although the cultural acceptance of TPM in this region likely minimises underreporting. Third, while we assessed the presence of NCDs rigorously, some degree of misclassification is possible. Fourth, our survey did not capture the frequency, dosage or duration of herb consumption, nor did it assess concurrent use with specific conventional medications, limiting our ability to evaluate potential herb-drug interactions or toxicological risks. Although multiple-comparison adjustment was applied to age-stratified herb-specific analyses using FDR correction, other analyses were not adjusted; therefore, findings should be interpreted cautiously. Because herb use was self-reported over a 12-month period, recall bias and social-desirability bias are possible. The use of trained interviewers, standardised interviewing procedures and structured probing was intended to reduce misreporting; however, these strategies cannot eliminate recall-related error.

Conclusions

Medicinal herb use is highly prevalent in this rural Iranian population and is strongly patterned by educational attainment. The absence of adjusted associations with major NCDs suggests that herbs are used primarily for symptomatic relief rather than chronic disease management. These findings underscore the need for strengthening public education, practitioner training and the regulatory framework governing herbal products. Future research should incorporate longitudinal analyses, detailed exposure assessment and evaluation of herb–drug interactions.
