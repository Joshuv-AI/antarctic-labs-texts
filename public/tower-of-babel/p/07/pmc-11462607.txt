Salutogenic mechanisms in nature-based work: fostering sense of coherence for employees with limited capability for work
Authors: Sanne Renske Hiemstra, Jenneken Naaldenberg, A de Jonge, Lenneke Vaandrager
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHaving a job is important for the well-being and inclusion of people with limited capability for work (LCW) due to physical and/or mental disability. This study explores salutogenic mechanisms that contribute to work-related sense of coherence (Work-SoC) of employees with LCW, i.e. perceiving their work in nature as manageable, comprehensible and meaningful. Semi-structured interviews (26 in total) were conducted with employees with LCW, employee supervisors, job coaches and foresters, all working in nature management. Interviews were held at four worksites of a governmental organisation in the Netherlands that provides permanent jobs for people with LCW. Employees with LCW contributed to the design and analysis of the study as co-researchers. Thematic analysis was used and member checks were carried out on preliminary findings. We identified six salutogenic mechanisms that contribute to nature-based Work-SoC of employees with LCW: (i) having constructive working relationships, (ii) experiencing structure and clarity, (iii) receiving practical and emotional support, (iv) support in the creation of meaning, (v) experiencing and learning in practice and (vi) physical activity and (absence of) stimuli. Identified mechanisms can create a positive effect when leveraged, thereby boosting (new) salutogenic mechanisms. Our findings illustrate that nature-based work can provide resources that promote Work-SoC of employees with LCW through the identified mechanisms. However, employees with LCW, colleagues, supervisors and stakeholders must recognize, mobilize and use these resources to leverage identified mechanisms in order to facilitate health-promoting workplaces for people with LCW. This, in turn, can contribute to sustainable inclusion through enablement.Keywords: limited capability for work, mechanisms, work sense of coherence, salutogenesis, disability, health-promoting environments

INTRODUCTION

Paid jobs are vital for the well-being of people with limited capability for work (LCW) due to physical and/or mental disability (Saunders and Nedelec, 2014; Robertson et al., 2019). Additionally, paid jobs contribute to human rights to participate equally in society (United Nations, 2006). People with disability comprise approximately 16% of the global population (World Health Organization, 2022). For this large, diverse group, participation in paid work lags behind. Europe’s employment rate for working-age individuals with disability is 50%, compared to 75% for people without disability (European Council of the European Union, 2023). Employment policies and interventions often focus on enhancing the individual skills and competencies of people with disability to facilitate inclusion in paid work. However, the individual focus must be broadened for sustainable inclusion of people with disability, as barriers extend beyond individual capacities (Hall and Wilton, 2011; Gallagher et al., 2015).

Social and physical work environments in which jobs are performed influence the extent to which employees with disability are enabled to participate. Adapted work processes, social acceptance and physical accessibility can support employees with LCW (Kocman and Weber, 2018; Vornholt et al., 2018). Therefore, to complement approaches focussing on individual capacities, scholars advocate for research and practice that considers workplace context to enable work opportunities for people with LCW (Gould et al., 2020). The salutogenic perspective offers a complementary approach, central to the current study. The salutogenic model of health (SMH) emphasizes the importance of health-promoting workplaces that cultivate comprehensible, manageable and meaningful work for individuals with and without disabilities. This experience is defined as a ‘work-related sense of coherence’ (Work-SoC) (Vogt et al., 2013). Work-SoC enables individuals to thrive at work, thereby contributing to one’s ability to sustainably participate in paid work (Vaandrager and Koelen, 2013; Jenny et al., 2022), and therefore, one’s possibilities to benefit from the positive effects of (paid) work for general well-being and inclusion in society.

In a recent study, we showed that employees with LCW working for a governmental organisation had strong Work-SoC and scored high on indicators for health and employability (Hiemstra et al., 2024). As the target group’s work participation lags behind in general and research and practice necessitates a context-specific, complementary perspective, the current study aims to explore how this strong Work-SoC of people with LCW develops within a specific context offered by this governmental organisation: nature-based work settings for people with LCW. Semi-structured interviews are used and an inclusive approach is applied to incorporate experiential knowledge of people with LCW in this study’s design and analysis (Frankena et al., 2019, Glade et al., 2020).

Our approach contributes to a better understanding of how employment can enable Work-SoC from the perspective of employees with LCW and people who work closely with them. This knowledge can be used to facilitate healthy workplaces for people with disabilities, supporting their opportunities for sustainable employment. Additionally, we contribute to the theory of salutogenesis by providing new insights into how Work-SoC evolves for this specific target group. Finally, this research contributes to the body of knowledge about the connections between nature, health and employment for people with disability.

Defining limited capability for work

In the context of work, we refer to limited capability when one’s ability to function in paid work is limited (but not impossible) due to intellectual, psychological, physical, sensory or neurological (health) disability. From a biomedical perspective, a limited capability for work is based on a combination of medical diagnosis and an assessment of one’s ability to perform regular work (see e.g. Disability Rights UK, 2024) and is often guided by diagnostic tools such as the International Classification of Diseases (World Health Organization, 2001) or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). In a Dutch context, it is estimated that, by 2025, a heterogenous group of 305,000 people will be assessed as having LCW based on physical, mental, intellectual and/or learning disability and functional limitations to participate in regular work (Fris, 2020).

From a social model of health perspective, disability refers to individual constraints when social and physical environments do not accommodate needs resulting from a biomedical condition (Barnes, 2019). The impact of disability on capability for work depends on individual characteristics (e.g. type of disability, skills, capacities, previous experience), context (e.g. social and physical surroundings) and intended function (e.g. functional (social) job requirements), thereby stressing the importance of social and physical context for successful work participation of people with LCW.

Health-promoting workplaces for people with LCW: a salutogenic orientation

Health-promoting workplaces provide a supportive environment. They offer social and personal support, facilitate the development of employees’ skills and enable individuals to work in good health while remaining employment overtime (Kuhn and Chu, 2022). To facilitate health-promoting workplaces for all, knowledge about health-promoting mechanisms for people with LCW is essential. The SMH provides an approach that allows the identification of these mechanisms.

The SMH defines health as a dynamic process. An individual’s health moves on a continuum of ease/disease (physical, social and emotional) and is influenced by an individual’s interaction with their social and physical environments (Antonovsky, 1996). At work, people with LCW engage with job characteristics and the surrounding social and physical work environment which affects capabilities to function in work. For example, social support and feedback on performance can function as job resources (Kocman and Weber, 2018; Ybema et al., 2020). These resources support one’s capability to deal with work-related challenges or job demands. What is perceived as a job resource can vary per person (Demerouti et al., 2001; Bakker and Demerouti, 2007; Jenny et al., 2022). Interaction between job demands and resources also leverages mechanisms that influence the way work is experienced as coherent, meaning that work is comprehensible, manageable and meaningful (Work-SoC). Therefore, we define salutogenic mechanisms as ‘the processes through which [Work-SoC] was brought about’ (Hedström and Ylikoski, 2010).

Work-SoC reflects the extent to which a specific workplace is health-promoting: Work-SoC enables an individual to deal with work-related challenges and provide opportunities to ‘thrive’ at work, reaching personal goals, personal growth and performance (Bauer and Jenny, 2022). Additionally, a strong Work-SoC is related to health and other work-related well-being and employability indicators in regular working populations (i.e. work-related fatigue (Van der Westhuizen, 2018) and work engagement (Vogt et al., 2015; Van der Westhuizen, 2018; Grødal et al., 2019)).

Nature-based work for people with LCW

To explore salutogenic mechanisms in the context of work for people with LCW, this study focuses on nature-based work in the Netherlands. Within this setting, employees with LCW are offered a diverse range of arrangements matched with individual employee needs, such as conditions of employment, contract terms, performance standards and specific tasks. Employees are guided by a supportive supervisor and receive work-related training. Besides these social and organisational arrangements, another unique characteristic of this work setting is its natural environment.

Studies on vocational rehabilitation, which focus on the enablement of individuals with disability towards employment, have shown that a green environment can contribute to sustainable work participation. This contribution is due to health-promoting qualities (e.g. fresh air, physical work, etc.), as well as to the interaction of individuals with nature as a work environment. Social contacts within those natural settings, the emergence of meaningful experiences and the abilities that nature-related activities provide to adjust work to specific needs and abilities have been indicated as important resources for employment (Nordh et al., 2009; Pálsdóttir et al., 2014b; Hassink et al., 2017). In addition, spending time in nature can have a positive impact on health and well-being (Hartig et al., 2014; Martyn and Brymer, 2016; White et al., 2019, 2023; Martin et al., 2020; Konijnendijk et al., 2023). Nature-based work environments can therefore be a job resource in itself, contributing to a health-promoting environment. However, this has not yet been studied in a natural setting where people with LCW are engaged in paid (structural) employment.

METHODS

Study design

This study was part of a larger study on how work in natural environments impacts the health and sustainable employability of people with LCW in the Netherlands. The longitudinal study is carried out in partnership with a governmental organisation that provides inclusive jobs in e.g. forestry, facility management and archive editing for people who meet the LCW criteria of the Dutch Participation Act and the Job Agreement (Ministry of Social Affairs and Employment, 2023). This heterogeneous target group consists of people who are limited in their ability to function in (regular) paid work without additional support due to various disabilities of varying severity and are therefore eligible for these specific jobs. The study focused specifically on employees with LCW working in forestry, using a qualitative design with semi-structured interviews. Interviews were held with employees with LCW (12), their supervisors (6), job coaches (4) and foresters (4) at four worksites in forestry in the Netherlands in May 2022. Focus sites were selected based on variety in geographical location, type of area, available accommodations, team count and tenure. The Wageningen University Research Ethics Committee approved the study in October 2021.

Setting

Employees with LCW (hereafter: ‘employees’) are stationed at management units of the Dutch State Forestry Service. These management units are responsible for the maintenance of nature reserves in the Netherlands. At these sites, employees work in teams of 8–10. The teams are guided by a supervisor and work closely together with regular foresters for the execution of tasks. Work-related training is provided to employees to become skilled in specific tasks, and (external) job coaches are available for additional support for employees and their supervisors in relation to work-related or organisational challenges (see Table 1 for a more specific explanation of the different roles). Conditions of employment, such as contract terms, performance standards and specific tasks, are matched with individual needs (Dutch State Forestry Service, 2019a, b). After an initial probationary period, employees with offered a permanent contract with the same legal rights as employees without LCW.

Inclusive approach

To actively include people with LCW in research about their work health, this study followed an inclusive approach. Three co-researchers with LCW, already involved in the overarching project, were engaged throughout the study’s design. By adhering to inclusive research guidelines (Frankena et al., 2019) and engaging in regular reflective practices, we were able to tailor the inclusive practices, ensuring both feasibility and value for both the co-researchers and the research objectives. All involved co-researchers contributed to probing interview questions after the identification of key topics for the interviews, ensuring that the questions were relevant to the studied work setting. One co-researcher (A.d.J.) also contributed to the analysis, which proved to be burdensome: learning to apply this skill on a large data set was demanding in terms of time and energy, as it ‘hits close to the home’ (Cahill, 2004, p. 274).

We decided to tailor the analysing process, i.e. by working with pre-selected data fragments and limiting the coding time for the co-researcher. This yielded an inclusive process in which the co-researcher (i) inductively coded selected data fragments during an allocated amount of time (four half days of work) and (ii) provided experience-based reflections on identified mechanisms. This resulted in a coding scheme that helped the first author to further analyse the transcripts and to cluster the identified themes. A final contribution to better understanding participants’ perspectives was the member checks with our participants, their colleagues and the foresters at the participating focus sites.

Materials

The interview guide was based on literature (SMH), observations and co-researcher input. The guide addressed (i) perceived job demands, referring to the aspects of a job that require effort and are associated with mental and physical strains for employees, and job resources, which are aspects that help employees to achieve work goals and stimulate growth, and (ii) Work-SoC, meaning that work is experienced meaningful, comprehensible and manageable, and how they evolved. Interview questions were adapted for each respondent group to gather perspectives on how work environments affect the employees (see Table 2).

Sampling and participants

Participating employees were recruited through purposeful sampling via supervisors. This method enabled the careful identification of representative and information-rich participants within the research population while ensuring the consent of individuals to participate (Palinkas et al., 2015). As individuals with LCW differ not only in type of disability but also in other dimensions such as gender, education level, age and employment history, we aimed to include a diverse sample representing the broad diversity of this target group to include multiple perspectives. Variety in (i) age, (ii) gender, (iii) educational level and (iv) duration of employment was considered in order to select two employees per team. All participants are eligible for accommodated paid work based on the Dutch Participation Act. Participant ages varied from 20 to 56, with a majority being male (10 male, 2 female). Educational backgrounds spanned from lower vocational education to university degree, and the duration of employment ranged from 6 months to 5 years. Finally, supervisors involved with these employees and foresters and job coaches related to their teams were invited to participate in interviews.

Procedure

Semi-structured interviews with employees, foresters and job coaches were conducted face-to-face during their work. Employees were interviewed independently. Supervisor interviews were held via MS Teams. Interviews were led by the first author, with assistance from a master’s student who conducted a portion of the employee interviews independently. Before each interview, the interviewer confirmed the participant willingness, checked informed consent clarity and addressed the participant’s questions. All interviews took ~1 h and were audio recorded with participant permission. After the interviews, the recordings were transcribed verbatim.

Analysis

Salutogenic mechanisms were identified through a thematic content analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006) in MaxQDA 2022. First, the first author familiarized herself with the data by reading and re-reading the transcripts and selecting relevant parts. Next, significant fragments were coded inductively, staying close to the wording of the participants. Attention was given specifically to three facets: (i) job demands and job resources, (ii) Work-SoC and (iii) processes through which Work-SoC emerges (mechanisms). Codes that reflected similarities in meaning were merged and rephrased into more concise descriptions. Then, these codes were clustered in themes to achieve a higher level of interpretation. Finally, these themes were clustered into overarching salutogenic mechanisms (e.g. ‘employees get to know each other well’ became part of ‘facilitating working relationships’).

RESULTS

Resources in context

Identified resources were person and situation-dependent. Examples of identified resources are supervisor presence, work with colleagues, social and physical safety, appropriate work conditions, training, job coach availability and work within nature. However, the analysis showed that the creation of health-promoting workplaces is a collaborative responsibility: the availability of, and responsibility for, resources and the leverage of mechanisms is the result of a dynamic interplay between different (f)actors present within the teams, the forest management unit and the natural work environment. As is visualized in Figure 1 and explained by a supervisor:

The physical and social work setting, inspired by Dahlgren and Whitehead (2021).

Supervisor: ‘ It’s a combination of factors that make them feel at home. I give them responsibility...let them discover things for themselves and I have found that they appreciate that. It helps them look at their work in a certain way...by giving them the freedom to make mistakes, they realise that they can achieve a lot. And then you get the feeling of ‘hey, I’m not just here to do tasks, my insights matter’. And then you just work pleasantly. So it gives them an environment where they feel at home and appreciated. And of course the Dutch State Forestry Service is important. That [employees] are also appreciated by them.’ (Site2_Supervisor1: 216-263)

Social setting: the team

Within the team of employees, two essential resources were identified. First, the supervisor is an important resource. As a cooperative foreman, the supervisor gives instructions and explains tasks to employees. Participants emphasized that, subsequent to this guidance, the supervisor’s availability for practical and social support enables employees to do their jobs. Second, employees stress the importance of direct colleagues with LCW themselves, providing social contact, fun encounters and practical and emotional support.

Social setting: forest management unit

For employees, working in collaboration with foresters from the management unit also provided resources. First, clear assignments provided by the management unit help supervisors and employees to carry out their tasks. Second, the employees’ explicit positioning as colleagues by the forest management unit has value for those employees. They are provided with the same uniforms, professional equipment and workplace facilities, which further underscores this feeling. Third, being positioned as colleagues enables employees and foresters social interaction. Finally, the foresters of the management unit actively provide opportunities for personal development of employees, e.g. by offering training or internships.

Physical setting: natural work environment

When asked about the nature-based work settings’ value, employees emphasized their beauty. Additionally, natural work environments offer a great diversity of (reoccurring) tasks and opportunities to adapt the work to the employee’s needs and capabilities. Furthermore, physical work provides concrete tasks which enable employees to see direct results.

The analysis further identified that the interaction between employees and the aforementioned (f)actors in the social and physical environment leveraged six salutogenic mechanisms that support Work-SoC for employees. Identified salutogenic mechanisms and their resources

Having constructive working relationships

Informal contact contributes to knowing each other’s needs and being able to act upon them:

Employee: ‘We know from each other how to deal with each other.’ (Site3_Employee1: 681-689)

This type of contact occurs during the workday when employees work with colleagues, supervisors or foresters, for example while moving from one location to another, during breaks or at organized gatherings, such as barbecues.

By knowing each other, supervisors and employees can facilitate work arrangements that help employees execute specific tasks (e.g. by linking employees who have difficulty reading maps with employees who can do this well) or match people and work based on character and behaviour. Thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of tasks and, by extension, a healthy atmosphere at work.

In relation to foresters in the work setting, employees express the importance of equal treatment. This conception of equality involves respectful exchanges, regardless of differences in personal characteristics and circumstances or formal roles. For example, employees indicate that being seen as full colleagues by the foresters and being part of important moments within the management unit (e.g. anniversaries or staff outings) stimulates a sense of belonging.

The nature of intrapersonal communication, both within the team and in relation to foresters, is fluid and in flux. The dynamic can change quickly due to employees’ personal circumstances or work situations. Such a dynamic requires conscious navigation between individual and group interests. Supervisors manage this navigation by mediating between different perspectives, actively seeking solutions when conflicts arise and deliberately fashioning employee-specific tasks.

Experiencing structure and clarity

The work offers a rhythm and structure to daily life, providing fixed benchmarks such as work days, weekends and holidays. An informal start to the day allows employees to share (private) concerns with colleagues and supervisors and to focus on the tasks introduced by their supervisors. During the day, allocated time for specific tasks or areas and breaks mark the transition from one task or area to another (‘Blocks’). Employees like to understand their jobs and tasks and to take pleasure in work:

Employee : Yes, yes, As long as I can keep on working. And as long as I don’t have to wait for too long. Just really, just working pleasantly together, without delays. Therefore, the work needs to be clear. If so, a day can work out extremely beautifully. (Site1_Employee3: 48–63)

Employees emphasize the importance of clear instructions based on what should be done, by whom, how, with what material, where and under what conditions, as well as on the availability of materials and transportation. Forestry work provides a sense of structure, as different types of natural areas and changing seasons bring diverse yet recurrent activities:

Employee : No, that’s right, you don’t get bored because you do many different things, but with the same focus. So you do get better at knowing what you’re going to do. But it is always in a different place or a different job. You always meet other people. It’s never the same, but it’s structured. (MC.1)

Supervisor availability ensures continuous support. Additionally, the permanent contract that employees have (or can attain) assures them that they are accepted and welcomed and have a stable income.

Supervisors, foresters and job coaches added that, to provide the aforementioned resources, they need a constructive collaboration with management unit foresters. Forest managers provide the overall assignments and are the primary source of practical information for supervisors, who then translate information to employees.

Receiving practical and emotional support

Working in nature allows the opportunity to tailor work to the abilities and needs of employees. The work environment offers a diversity of tasks that can be adjusted in terms of difficulty or intensity:

Employee : Yes, and the physical part of work also becomes easier if you do it more often. And you can also do plenty of tasks within the same assignment if you physically feel less well that day. For example, if we are going to replace poles at fences, you can walk with the poles, put them into the ground with a hammer or the earth drill, or pull the cramps out of the poles and smash them in. That sort of thing. (Site4_Employee1: 305–307)

Supervisors proactively match tasks with needs and capabilities. Their (physical) presence is crucial, according to employees. Supervisors actively observe what is needed and ‘tune in’ with employees to proactively become aware of potential challenges or triggers, sometimes even before employees themselves encounter them. Supervisors provide additional or repeated instructions and intervene if things go wrong on the job, stemming difficult challenges at work and making them easier for employees to address. Additionally, supervisors offer help with non-work hurdles, i.e. by providing time and space to finish school assignments or by assisting participants with private preoccupations, such as dentist calls.

On an emotional level, supervisors provide an ‘empathetic ear’ to employees when dealing with emotional struggles (inside or outside of work). Having an open conversation in which the supervisor is non-judgemental and takes employees’ thoughts seriously while asking reflective questions helps employees to let go and take a fresh perspective. However, sharing this type of information with a supervisor requires a trusting relationship that acknowledges that employees don’t want to share everything:

Employee: I kind of built a wall for myself. That’s the boundary. That boundary of not getting too close…When it gets too sensitive, too intense for me. Then it’s done… then, I can very easily switch in my head to joking or another topic or something… and then they also stop… they notice that from me too. (Site4_Employee3: 238–260)

Furthermore, a supervisor’s (expressed) confidence in employees helps motivate them to tackle challenges and develop (new) skills. Employees also expressed how the presence of colleagues with LCW helped both at practical and emotional levels as they received practical help and had people to talk to who understand the reality of working with a disability. Finally, employees expressed that foresters, while working together, also provided practical help and instructions.

Support in the creation of meaning

Meaningfulness of tasks occurs through understanding the rationale of tasks and specific working methods. For the most part, the supervisors provide this contextual information. Employees expressed that when foresters with a specialist background provide additional background, allowing for questions and exchanging ideas and knowledge, further help employees appreciate the work. However, the meaning of work can be overshadowed. Employees can become occupied by thoughts or emotions that occur due to events or situations at work or within their private lives. Examples include a colleague expressing something negative about an employee, an assignment turning out different than expected, or private situations evoking fierce emotions or insecurities. In such cases, the supervisor helps employees put things in perspective.

Having meaningful work experiences further highlights work’s meaning. Employees value fun at work and enjoyment of tasks, specifically with colleagues:

Employee : Just being busy all day with something I enjoy... Yes, you have to take pleasure in your work.... And you need to have nice colleagues, because otherwise there is no need to be here. (Site4_Employee3: 88–95)

For employees, being expected by the nature management unit to deliver results, being trusted as a responsible colleague and receiving appreciation make work worthwhile. Furthermore, forestry work sheds light on the results of employees’ efforts when revisiting different areas during the year. Appreciation by supervisors and colleagues is essential, but employees emphasize the value of explicit appreciation of foresters and visitors of the nature reserves. Employees noted that their work results are also valuable for nature and its resident organisms, thereby contributing to a sense of meaningful participation in society, which is also strengthened by the forester uniform: employees’ status as civil servants is visible to outsiders, which influences how they are viewed by others, although not all employees attribute this value to the uniform.:

Employee: To me, it’s just corporate clothing. (MC.2)

Experiencing and learning in practice

The social and physical work setting provides opportunities to discover talents, develop ambitions and gain knowledge and skills in a safe setting. Employees feel allowed to make mistakes, to learn and to try again, which invites them to:

Employee : …bring out talents that others don’t know about. (MC.5)

The natural work environment offers ample opportunities to learn in practice. For example, employees indicate that the aforementioned structured workdays help with work familiarity, to the extent that recognition and practice (and, thus, skill acquisition through repetition) are neither monotonous nor boring.

Employee : Something positive. Yes, I do learn from it every day. Yes. But not every day but many days. Then I learn the same thing, and then I learn something new. Or that day, I learn something more extensive. (Site4_Employee4: 310)

Supervisors share knowledge with employees throughout the working day and demonstrate specific tasks and safe behaviour. Supervisors use the natural work environment’s flexibility to deliberately schedule tasks so that employees can develop specific skills, and receiving help from supervisors or foresters with professional knowledge.

Besides opportunities for experiential learning, employees receive formal work-oriented training. This training is provided because professional certificates are required for specific activities in nature (e.g. chainsaw use and forest mowing). Employees follow courses mainly at the start of their careers to gain practical work-related skills. Additionally, employees can participate in Dutch vocational education levels 2–4 or a specialist course, which can help in future careers.

Physical activity and (absence of) stimuli

Working in nature additionally contributes to (extra) physical activity of employees, which strengthens physical condition. Physical fitness helps employees deal with the physical aspects of the work, such as controlling a brush cutter or a chainsaw consists of physical actions, and moving branches and material takes and requires strength. Being in nature also enables employees to recover from and reflect on work and life. As one employee said during one observation prior to this study’s interviews:

Observation report : The forest mowing is of course a lot of noise. But there is also a lot of silence and something beautiful. Nature helps him to come to himself and to keep calm. Because of the beautiful things, you are distracted and do not think about difficult things, but you process it. (O.4, Pos. 31)

As this employee explains, the presence of stimuli can help one ‘let go’, while work situations with fewer stimuli can desensitize.

Social and physical work environment: the importance of context

The six salutogenic mechanisms influence the extent to which employees find their work understandable, manageable and meaningful. When employees experience a feeling of ‘work sense of coherence’, a workflow is experienced in which employees work pleasantly. When ‘everything is right’, employees understand what they need to do (and why), as well as that they can handle the requested work and that their work is worthwhile. This understanding evolves through continuous interaction between various (f)actors and mechanisms within this work setting. Consider the following fictional composite example, based on interviews with employees, supervisors, foresters and job coaches:

An employee does not help his colleagues put up a fence and distances himself from the work. The work supervisor signals this and ‘tunes in’ to the worker: he says he noticed the worker has not been working for a while and seems busy with something else. The employee tells the supervisor he has received good news: he can finally get his own apartment. He is excited about this, but it also raises many questions. This keeps him very occupied, so he fails to concentrate on his work and the cooperation with colleagues. The work supervisor listens to the employee’s concerns. The employee tells his story, and together, they think about practical solutions, who can help, and what questions the employee will ask that person. Then, the work supervisor offers the employee a small concrete job: digging holes for poles at places his colleagues have measured out. The supervisor explains that in that way, the employee can contribute to the group and to the task of putting up the fence to protect specific frogs. The employee goes back to work.

DISCUSSION

This study was the first attempt to unravel salutogenic mechanisms contributing to the experience of work as understandable, manageable and meaningful (Work-SoC) for employees with LCW. Six salutogenic mechanisms were identified, leveraged by interaction between employees and their social and physical work environment. Supervisors appeared essential for the Work-SoC of employees with LCW. Supervisors provide instructions, steer social processes, lend a sympathetic ear, motivate employees and facilitate progress in work and life by proactively anticipating obstacles and providing (learning) opportunities. As hypothesized, the natural work environment proved to be a resource for salutogenic mechanisms. Nature contributes to meaningfulness and provides a ‘learning environment’: the diversity and variety in forestry offer numerous chances to tailor work to employees’ needs and learning opportunities for employees.

While how Work-SoC for people with LCW develop has not been researched yet, our findings align with research on other underlying mechanisms of positive work-related outcomes for people with LCW, such as job satisfaction (Akkerman et al., 2016), work engagement (Ybema et al., 2020), work participation (Kinn et al., 2014), work performance and the ability to stay at work (Van Hees et al., 2022). Much like our own study, these studies emphasize the dynamic interplay between employees with LCW and their work environment, stressing the importance of an open and trustful organisational climate, meaningful supervisor and colleague relationships, support of supervisors and colleagues and opportunities to adjust the work(load) in order to facilitate health-promoting work environments for people with LCW. Schaap et al. (2022) further highlighted the significance of enjoyable and varied work, equal relationships and enabling work conditions for employees with LCW. They also stressed the importance of available supervisors who provide explicit instructions, (emotional) support, appreciation and facilitation of social processes and learning opportunities.

Research on Work-SoC is in its early stage. Our study’s findings align with previous research in general working populations that showed that, for example, role clarity, social support from supervisors and colleagues and opportunities for development and learning contribute to the emergence of Work-SoC (Broetje et al., 2020). Notably, during this study’s member checks, foresters expressed that the identified mechanisms were also crucial for their own experience of work to be manageable, comprehensible and meaningful. This remark raises the question of what differentiates resourceful health-promoting work environments and salutogenic mechanisms for people with LCW. On a more abstract level, we suggest that Work-SoC develops through the identification of potential resources and learning how to use these resources through reflective learning about work-related situations (Super et al., 2016). For people with LCW, these processes are not straightforward. For example, understanding social dynamics or overseeing specific situations is difficult because of functional limitations.

The findings indicate that one’s ability to participate sustainably in paid work depends on the social and physical context of employees (Cieza et al., 2018). The proactive role of supervisors in the studied setting seems to enable salutogenic mechanisms for employees with LCW, which relates to what Koelen and Lindström (2016, p. 34) have called the ‘Sense FOR Coherence’ of professionals: the ability ‘to improve the sense of coherence for the people we work for’. In other words, supervisors’ ability to recognize and provide resources for employees and enable them, in turn, to recognize and use these resources to leverage salutogenic mechanisms that contribute to Work-SoC. Thus, although the identified salutogenic mechanisms seem generic and broad enough to benefit everyone, it appears to be crucial to make these mechanisms’ resources inclusive for people with LCW. Therefore, proactive tailoring of work settings and resources to individual talents and needs seems essential to facilitate health-promoting workplaces for this target group (Lezwijn et al., 2011).

The natural environment and the nature of the work offer abundant resources which can be customized to meet individual needs and abilities for salutogenic mechanisms. The wide variety of tasks allows supervisors and employees to adapt in terms of intensity and difficulty, a phenomenon which is consistent with previous research on vocational rehabilitation settings (Nordh et al., 2009; Pálsdóttir et al., 2014b; Hassink et al., 2017). Additionally, nature-based work provides structure and learning opportunities that enable employees to gradually strengthen their work-SoC through recurring, recognizable, non-monotonous tasks and challenges that foster task completion and increased competence. This has also been shown in studies with different target groups (i.e. young people (Kogstad et al., 2014) and people with depressive symptoms (Salomon et al., 2018)). Furthermore, achieving (visible) outcomes for nature and for visitors enjoying the beauty of these places, and being able to marvel these places themselves, contributed to the extent to which employees found their work worthwhile. Moreover, social interaction within the studied setting proved to be a meaningful and important resource for the health-promoting effects of nature-based interactions (Hassink et al., 2017; Salomon et al., 2018; Grigoletto et al., 2023). Finally, working in nature offers physical exercise which contributes to physical health, as well as both low-stimulation and high-stimulation environments which provide employees with rest or distraction when needed, helping them to stay balanced (Korpela et al., 2014; Pálsdóttir et al., 2014a; Hassink et al., 2017). In summary, the natural work environment proved to be supportive, resourceful and thereby health-promoting. However, the interplay between the physical environment and social actors leverages salutogenic mechanisms that contribute to Work-SoC and workplace health for employees.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the combination of semi-structured interviews, member checks and an overall inclusive approach. By conducting semi-structured interviews and member checks with employees with LCW and those working closely with them, the study gained in-depth exploration and valuable insights into the participants’ experiences. Additionally, experiential knowledge was included in the research approach through a collaboration with three co-researchers who are experts by experience. These co-researchers actively contributed to the study’s design and analyses. Our inclusive approach, incorporating experiential knowledge, helped us better comprehend the interaction between employees with LCW and their social and physical work environments.

Another important strength is the use of the salutogenic model of health, which enabled the researchers to complement the biomedical perspective of disability with a perspective that includes the interaction between individual characteristics and the social and physical environment. However, the focus on salutogenic mechanisms left out existing pathogenic mechanisms (Jenny et al., 2022). During the analyses, we noticed that when the identified six salutogenic mechanisms are not (appropriately) present, Work-SoC seems to stagnate or weaken. Salutogenic mechanisms might help to maintain and restore balance, i.e. due to ‘gain spirals’, which enable the recognition and use of resources and leverage other salutogenic mechanisms (Grødal et al., 2019).

Our approach also had its limitations. Co-researchers and researchers collaborated in an inclusive research team in which decisions were made together through discussions on both processes and outcomes. This sometimes resulted in co-researchers having a less active role when a specific task, for instance reading interview transcripts, was very straining. By applying inclusive research guidelines (Frankena et al., 2019) we reflected on these decisions and their influence on the research and its outcomes. This means that we included experiential knowledge of people with LCW in our study, as intended with our approach while recognizing the burden of inclusive practices for co-researchers (Frankena et al., 2016; Di Loreti, 2018). In addition, we found that our collaboration led to learning and empowerment processes for co-researchers, which is a positive trade-off and is consistent with findings from other inclusive practices (Walmsley et al., 2018). For example, learning to apply scientific analysis provided the involved co-researcher with new skills and knowledge.

Another limitation lies in our sampling method for the semi-structured interviews. The study’s participants were selected based on supervisor identification, which might have caused a selection bias. We intentionally chose this approach, with supervisors serving as the primary point of contact for reaching employees with LCW. Due to their roles and training, they could consider their employees’ interests. They explicitly emphasized the voluntary nature of participation and the acceptability to decline the invitation. As supervisors are a trusted sounding board for employees, our approach gave participants the space to ask questions and make a considered decision to participate. To minimize selection bias, we used pre-defined selection criteria developed with our co-researchers to guide supervisors’ selections. Additionally, we conducted member checks with all participating worksite employees to validate our findings and mitigate the potential effects of the aforementioned limitations.

Implications for practice and research

An explicit focus on health-promoting work settings that enable people with LCW in paid work can help complement individual approaches to stimulate sustainable work participation for people with LCW. The identified six salutogenic mechanisms provide a starting point to further design and evaluate interventions and health-promoting settings in dialogue with people with LCW, for example through ‘resource-oriented capacity building’ at work (Hoffmann et al., 2014). Longitudinal studies could further contribute to understanding the relationship between resourceful work conditions, salutogenic mechanisms, Work-SoC and sustainable inclusion of people with LCW in paid employment.

The findings indicate that the identified mechanisms are broad enough to benefit everyone. The suggested universality of the mechanisms contributing to Work-SoC implicates that active facilitation of sustainable approaches for everyone might be more beneficial than a fragmented approach targeting specific populations (Eck, 2022; p. 183; Shore et al., 2018). However, the study revealed that identified mechanisms do need to be made inclusive for employees with LCW through the proactive tailoring of work settings and resources to their talents and needs. A Sense For Coherence of supervisors seems to leverage mechanisms supporting the movement towards sustainable inclusion through the enablement of employees with LCW. In salutogenic literature, a sense of coherence is often portrayed as an individual orientation that helps to identify and mobilize resources, implying active individual agency. Our findings call for a better understanding of the sense FOR coherence as a construct, and its relation to the sense of coherence for people with and without LCW.

Finally, the study provided insights into the role of nature-based work settings. Although these settings offer resources for meaningful occupations, the main takeaway is that it is not only the natural environment that contributes to salutogenic mechanisms. Key opportunities arise from the interaction between nature, employees with LCW, their supervisor and other stakeholders.

CONCLUSION

Our findings illustrate that nature-based inclusive employment promotes Work-SoC for people with LCW through the identified mechanisms. However, these resources must be recognized, mobilized and used by employees, their colleagues, supervisors and other close stakeholders. This need stresses the importance of a supportive social and physical work environment that (flexibly) matches the needs and capabilities of people with LCW to facilitate health-promoting workplaces for this target group. This, in turn, contributes to sustainable inclusion through enablement.
