Surgical intervention as a driver of new angiogenesis in tumors—time to consider minimally invasive surgeries?
Authors: Vimalraj Selvaraj, Saravanan Sekaran, Sureh Kumar Rajamani Sekar
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

Surgical resection remains the primary therapeutic approach for the overwhelming majority of patients diagnosed with localized solid tumours. Angiogenesis is a crucial part of the recovery process after surgery, but its tumour-promoting effects might provide a therapeutic difficulty. Consistent with this, numerous pro-angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and angiopoietin 2, were discovered to have considerably raised plasma levels following surgical tumour excision. It is unclear whether minimally invasive surgery is preferable to open surgery in terms of avoiding the pro-angiogenesis response, and increasing levels of these pro-angiogenic factors seem to correspond with the amount of surgical wounds. These pro-angiogenic factors, which may originate from a wide variety of cell types including endothelial cells, cancer cells, fibroblasts, and immunological cells, tend to rise after surgery and stay at a high level for up to four weeks. The presence of these proteins not only helps sustain a tumour-promoting microenvironment but also shields tumour cells from the effects of standard chemotherapy. Anti-angiogenesis medicines have so been advocated for use in the immediate postoperative period, ahead of the administration of standard chemotherapy1,2. Further research is needed on the long-term effects and safety concerns related to early postoperative anti-angiogenic therapy because of the possible impact on wound and anastomotic healing.

VEGF has been utilized as a surrogate marker to evaluate the angiogenic response after a number of different types of surgery because of its central role in angiogenesis. Variations in sample types (wound fluid, plasma, serum), tumour biology (benign vs. malignant), and surgical approaches (open vs. minimally invasive) contribute to the wide range of reported durations and intensities of this response3. However, enhanced free VEGF levels (compared to preoperative samples) indicate an angiogenic response that may begin as early as postoperative day 1 and last for up to 4 weeks. Compared to circulating VEGF (plasma and serum), VEGF levels in wound fluids accurately represent the local angiogenic response. Surgery-related angiogenesis seems more apparent in patients with malignant tumours than in benign equivalents, as shown by case-control studies. It has been demonstrated that prolonged surgery is linked with a higher angiogenesis response4, therefore the degree of surgical resection also impacts the postoperative angiogenesis response.

Studies have shown that minimally invasive surgery is preferable to open surgery for preventing the postoperative angiogenic response, perhaps as a result of less traumatic injury to the patient. VEGF was considerably greater in the minimally invasive group compared with open surgery, although other studies have shown inconsistent outcomes. Therefore, it is unclear whether the size of the surgical wound or the biology of the tumour has a larger role in determining the magnitude of the postoperative angiogenic response. However, the precise causes and origin of angiogenic factors remain unknown, despite the well-established postoperative angiogenesis response after major surgery. Cancer cells and normal cells (such as immune cells and fibroblasts) may both produce angiogenic factors in response to surgery-induced hypoxia, metabolic abnormalities, and inflammation. Genetic changes in cell growth pathways (such as, Ras-ERK, PI3K-AKT) leading to persistent and uncontrolled cell growth signalling5,6 have been found in virtually all cancer cells by recent genome-wide genetic characterization studies. To promote production of downstream angiogenic factors like VEGF-A and Ang-2, these oncogenic growth signals converge at hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) with hypoxia signals in remaining cancer cells after surgery5. These results suggest that cancer cells are a substantial source of angiogenic factors because they are more resistant to hypoxia than normal and benign tumour cells. To a lesser degree than in individuals with benign tumours, this may explain why cancer patients often exhibit more strong postoperative angiogenic responses. A worse prognosis in cancer patients has been linked to tissue hypoxia, which is caused by surgical operations. Example: tissue hypoxia may have an effect on the long-term prognosis of a cancer patient due to extended vascular clamping during hepatic surgery to limit perioperative blood loss5,6. Anaemia, caused by the loss of red blood cells after surgery, decreases oxygen supply to the body’s tissues and organs (hypoxia). Furthermore, the mobilization of bone marrow in response to anaemia may help accidentally to enhance the systemic angiogenic response. Patients with cancer have a worse chance of surviving when their haemoglobin levels are low3.

Tumours are more vulnerable to the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy once they undergo vascular normalization induced by the loss of myeloid-cell VEGF. Considering the wide-ranging effects of VEGF on tumour biology, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the postoperative angiogenic response is critical for transiently expanding residual cancer cells and constructing an initial tumour-supportive niche. This is especially true given that it occurs during a window of time in which conventional adjuvant therapy is not typically administered. Therapy and operations aimed at preventing further angiogenesis. Blocking the VEGF/VEGFR axis, which plays a crucial role in angiogenesis, is thought to eradicate tumour vasculature and, in theory, cure cancer. Indeed, in many clinical contexts, the use of three anti-angiogenic medications (bevacizumab, sunitinib, and sorafenib) has dramatically increased cancer patient survival7. All three drugs block either the VEGF ligand or the VEGF receptor, preventing VEGF from transmitting signals. Recent mouse investigations, however, have shown fascinating evidence that, although reducing the development of the original tumour, VEGF-targeted treatment actually decreases overall lifespan by increasing tumour spread8. Exploiting such anti-angiogenesis medications to provide a long-lasting therapeutic effect, however, necessitates more study7,8. In addition, these medications may also inhibit physiological angiogenesis, which is essential for wound healing, organ regeneration, and postoperative recovery. When an appropriate interval of time is allowed between surgery and therapy, surgery in patients receiving VEGF-targeted therapies seems to be safe9. It is proposed that anti-angiogenic therapies be started in the early postoperative period prior to the start of conventional chemotherapy because cancer patients are exposed to high levels of pro-angiogenic factors during a time period when no conventional adjuvant therapy is usually applied. However, further research is needed on the potential dangers and the “gains and losses” in terms of long-term survival.
