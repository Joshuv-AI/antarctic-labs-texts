Career Intentions in Academic Medicine: Factors Influencing Pursuit and Gender Disparities
Authors: Valentine Bour, Anne Congard, Corentin Gonthier, Élisa Sarda, Pierre-Antoine Gourraud, Patricia Lemarchand, Aurore Deledalle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionA decline in intentions to pursue careers in academic medicine (AM) has been observed, despite their importance for healthcare systems and scientific advancement. Persistent gender disparities further highlight the need to understand the factors shaping these career choices.MethodsThis cross-sectional online survey study examined factors associated with AM career intentions among 1,641 french medical residents (63% women) who completed an online survey assessing individual (e.g. self-efficacy, professional interest), contextual (e.g. mentorship, perceived stress), and background variables (e.g. residency year, research experience). Regression analyses identified factors, while mediation and moderation analyses explored gender-related mechanisms.ResultsConsistent with prior research, interest in research, prior research training (MSc/PhD), and mentorship were associated with academic medicine career intentions. Additional factors included work-related expectations (higher work centrality, stimulation, and lower conformity/autonomy) and intrapreneurial self-capital. Gender differences were partly explained by research-related factors (57.06%) and work centrality (15.61%), with no moderating effects observed.DiscussionPromoting AM careers requires enhancing early research exposure, providing structured mentorship, and fostering organizational flexibility to accommodate diverse professional goals. Strengthening supportive environments (especially for women) by improving mentorship access and workplace safety could sustain engagement and promote equity in academic medicine.Keywords: medical students, academic medicine, career intentions, research experience, mentorship, gender differences, intrapreneurial self-capital, research interest

Background

Academic medicine (AM), combining research, clinical practice, and teaching, remains essential to university hospitals but struggles to attract interest. While nearly half of medical students show interest in teaching/research,1,2 only 13.9% consider AM careers, reflecting a global recruitment challenge3 that emerges during residency.4

The challenge of recruiting future AM practitioners is linked to gender disparities. Despite women making up 55% of hospital practitioners,5 they hold only 31% of academic positions. Gender biases in recruitment contribute to this under-representation,6 with disparities appearing early in medical training.7 For instance, only 6% of female residents consider an AM career, compared to 20% of male residents.8 The evolution of professional expectations among medical students appears to be dynamic and variable throughout their academic journey.9 The present study aimed to explore a broad range of potential factors associated with academic medicine career intentions.

Understanding of AM Career Intentions

This section presents the individual, institutional, and societal factors that may influence the choice of an academic career and that motivated this research, drawing on Lent’s Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT),10 one of the most widely used career development frameworks. SCCT posits that career development is shaped by self-efficacy beliefs, outcome expectations, goals, and contextual supports and barriers and it is particularly relevant for examining both individual cognitive factors and contextual experiences during medical education.

Medical specialty, medical school location, residency stage, results in the National Ranking Examination (ECN) standardized test, parental occupational status and parental status could all shape residents’ experiences and opportunities, potentially influencing AM career intentions. Results in the National Ranking Examination (ECN), a nationwide standardized exam taken by medical students in France at the end of medical school. The score determines each student’s national ranking, which is then used to choose their specialty (such as surgery, pediatrics, etc.) and residency location.

The intention to pursue an academic medicine career is primarily driven by professional interest in research, as research is central to such careers. In this context, professional interest in research (defined as a preference for research-related activities) plays a key role in career choice. Together with mastery research experiences, it contributes to residents’ decision to pursue an AM career.11,12

Academic perceived and experienced affect the intention to engage in an AM career. For example, having or not a mentor or role model and perceiving an academic medicine career as accessible are key factors influencing the intention to pursue such a career.13,14 Professional stress experienced in the workplace tends to be intense during residency,15 and this high pressure could contribute to disengagement from the hospital setting in later career choices. Experiencing sexism in the workplace may also shape career choice goals, especially for women.

The extremely heavy workload on tenured hospital staff16 and the work-centric culture in AM17 can present obstacles to this career. As a result, the desire to balance personal and professional life may hinder commitment to AM career. Work centrality may directly affect the willingness to prioritize professional obligations over personal well-being and affect the expectation of return on personal investment when engaging in an AM career track. Second, the professional values of an individual, although less studied, have been named as an essential factor that deserves more attention.12 Intellectual stimulation in particular has been “the most consistently identified personal value which draws trainees to AM”18 Conversely, the perceived lack of autonomy is among the least attractive factors of AM according to students.19

Voluntary research experiences, such as completing an MSc or PhD, as well as informal research participation, significantly influence the decision to pursue an academic medicine career which can influence self-efficacy and outcome expectations.20 As medical studies are reputed to be stressful and demanding,21 study-related stress could also affect career aspirations. The concept of person-environment congruence (alignment between personal values and professional environment) applies well to medical residents, given that a misalignment between personal values and learning environment could reduce AM career intentions.22

Self-efficacy (defined as beliefs about one’s capacity to engage in specific behaviors) is widely recognized as an important factor influencing interest in career-related activities and choices.23 This may be particularly relevant in relation to intrapreneurial resources, which have also been associated with career development and professional engagement, especially among women.24 In turn, intrapreneurial self-capital, defined as the set of perceived personal resources that enable individuals to cope with challenges, adapt to change, make decisions, and transform constraints into opportunities,25,26 appears particularly relevant in the context of medical education. Medical students must navigate demanding training environments, uncertainty, and complex, highly competitive career choices. As such, these personal resources may play an important role in how students manage career-related challenges and shape their professional aspirations, particularly toward demanding pathways such as academic medicine. Belief in one’s own personal resources appears essential for engaging in a research career, regardless of gender.

Possible Explanations for the Gender Gap

Women face multiple disadvantages in pursuing academic medicine (AM) careers. Despite research participation encouraging AM career intentions,27 women often lack adequate mentors and role models28 and exhibit lower self-efficacy.8 They also frequently encounter discrimination and gender biases.27 Evidence is mixed regarding women’s interest in research and work-life balance concerns.27 Additionally, there is a mismatch between AM culture and women’s experiences.29 While these factors may explain the gender gap in AM careers, existing studies often rely on qualitative methods with exclusively female samples,30 thereby limiting the ability to infer a direct effect of these factors on career intentions. Further research is needed to examine whether these factors mediate or moderate the association between gender and academic medicine career intentions, or whether their effects are similar across genders.

Rationale for the Present Study

The current study aimed to investigate the factors associated with residents’ intentions to pursue an academic medicine (AM) career, particularly among women.2 This study investigated the individual, institutional, and societal factors involved in the development of academic medicine (AM) career intentions described in the previous section, with particular emphasis on cognitive variables from Lent’s Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT), one of the most widely used career development frameworks.

More specifically, the first objective was to identify the factors significantly influencing intention to pursue an AM career and to assess their unique contribution using multiple regression (Objective 1). The second objective was to examine the mechanisms that may explain women’s lower intention to pursue an AM career, and to identify potential barriers and facilitators specific to men and women (Objective 2).

Based on previous literature and the Social Cognitive Career Theory (SCCT) framework, we hypothesized that residents’ intentions to pursue a career in academic medicine (AM) would be shaped by a combination of research-related experiences, academic perceptions, and individual psychosocial factors. First, we expected professional interest in research, prior research training, and participation in research activities to be positively associated with AM career intentions. In line with previous findings, we also hypothesized that academic experiences and perceptions would significantly influence career intentions, with mentorship, the presence of role models, and perceiving academic medicine as an accessible career path being positively associated with AM intentions, whereas professional stress and experiences of sexism would be negatively associated. Second, we hypothesized that individual characteristics and resources would contribute to AM career intentions. More specifically, person–environment congruence, self-efficacy, and intrapreneurial self-capital were expected to be positively associated with stronger intentions to pursue an academic medicine career. We also expected personal and work-related values to play a significant role, with some factors acting as facilitators—such as work centrality and the need for stimulation—while others, such as a stronger value placed on autonomy, might be associated with lower AM career intentions. Finally, based on the literature on gender disparities in academic medicine, we expected women to report lower intentions to pursue an AM career than men. We further hypothesized that these gender differences would be partly explained by differential exposure to research, mentorship opportunities, and work-related perceptions. Consistent with these hypotheses, professional interest in research, prior research training (MSc/PhD), and mentorship were significantly associated with AM career intentions. Additional factors included work-related expectations—particularly higher work centrality and stimulation, alongside lower conformity and autonomy—and intrapreneurial self-capital. Gender differences were partly explained by research-related factors and work-related perceptions, while no moderating effects were observed.

Method

Sampling Procedure

Participants were recruited through Email using institutional mailing lists. Social media using online advertisements were used too. They completed a pseudo-nymous, self-reported questionnaire via Lime Survey, which was accessible from November 2022 to February 2023. Completion of the questionnaire required approximately 25 minutes. Participants were not compensated. In view of their educational level, no minors were enrolled in this study. All residents affiliated to French medical schools were eligible for this study. There were no exclusion criteria beyond not providing informed consent or not completing the questionnaire. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The consent form provided a comprehensive description of the study objectives, data storage and data-sharing procedures, and the participants’ right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments, or comparable ethical standards, and were validated by the Ethics Committee for Non-Interventional Research (CEDIS) of Nantes University, France (ethics committee approval No. 26072022). OSF preregistration for this study is available at https://osf.io/9yseq/?view_only=1c72743f542b402ba67beed6908e597d.

Materials

Choice Goal

Intention to pursue an AM career was assessed using a single-item measure: “To what extent do you wish to pursue a career in academic medicine?” (1 = Not interested at all; 2 = Slightly; 3 = Moderately; 4 = Fairly; 5 = Very interested). This single-item measure is a commonly used approach for assessing AM career intentions and has been widely adopted in prior research.19

Professional Interest

Interest in research was assessed using the Interest subscale of the Attitude toward Research Scale,31 selected for its focus on intrinsic engagement in research activities and its conceptual alignment with professional interest in research. The scale was translated and adapted following Hambleton et al’s guidelines32 for transcultural validation. It was translated into French and back-translated into English by independent bilingual experts. The scale consists of eight items assessing general interest in research (eg., “I enjoy doing research” and “Research is essential in my professional life”). Factor analysis supported a one-factor structure representing research interest (64% explained variance; factor loadings ranging from.63 to.89), with excellent reliability (ωt =0.92). Participants responded using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with an additional “does not apply” option. Participants who selected “does not apply” for more than three items were excluded from the study (n = 29).

Self-Efficacy

Self-Efficacy beliefs were measured with the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE),33 a 10-item self-reported instrument that asks participants to rate the degree of agreement with items reflecting optimistic self-beliefs (eg., “I can always manage to solve difficult problems if I try hard enough”), on a 4 point-Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all true) to 4 (Exactly true). Reliability was very high for this scale in the current data (ωt =0.90). General Self-Efficacy Scale is the most widely used scale in scientific articles for assessing Self-Efficacy beliefs.

Outcome Expectations

Outcome Expectations Related to Work Centrality

Health studies are particularly demanding, and academic medical careers are associated with substantial professional investment. In this context, we chose to examine work centrality. Work centrality reflects the extent to which individuals view work as a source of meaning, identity, and personal value. This construct appears especially relevant in academic medicine, where work occupies a central role in both professional and personal trajectories. This dimension was assessed using the Work Centrality Scale.34 The scale was translated and cross-culturally adapted.32 It was first translated into French and then back-translated into English by independent bilingual experts. This 12-item scale, rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), assesses the extent to which individuals believe that their involvement in work will provide them with personal benefits. Items 8 to 12 are direct translations of the original scale (eg., “I believe that my sense of personal worth will stem from my work and the strength of my commitment to it”), whereas items 1 to 7 were adapted to introduce a work–life balance dimension. Accordingly, the scale captures both commitment to work and aspirations for personal time and activities outside work (eg., “I would like to have a job with working hours that allow me to pursue activities outside of work”). Scores for the adapted items were reverse-coded so that higher mean scores reflected stronger expectations regarding the personal outcomes of work involvement. Factorial analysis supported a one-factor structure representing work centrality (52% of explained variance; factor loadings ranging from 0.43 to 0.87), with high internal reliability (ωt = 0.91).

Outcome expectations related to values were assessed with the Work Values Questionnaire (WVQ).35 This scale provides a theoretically robust and comprehensive framework for assessing work-related values. It is grounded in Schwartz’s extensively validated model of basic human values,36 one of the most widely used frameworks in values research, and specifically adapted to capture values within the professional sphere. The scale comprises 28 items assessing 8 work-specific values: Recognition, Humanism, Benevolence, Conformity, Empowerment, Achievement, Stimulation, and Autonomy. Each item asks participants to choose between two options representing two different values (which means each value is assessed by seven items: eg., Stimulation - “I like having complex problems to solve” or Autonomy - “I prefer a hierarchy that gives me freedom of action”); the score for each value is the number of times this value was chosen. This structure makes it impossible to compute a reliability coefficient.

Learning Experiences

Complementary research experiences were assessed by asking respondents about their research experiences, categorized into levels reflecting the progression of research involvement in France: 0 = no additional research experience, 1 = 1st year of MSc (elective courses only), and 2 = 2nd year of MSc or PhD (advanced research involvement).

Participation in research outside of complementary training was assessed with a single-item question asking participants if they had engaged in research activities (eg., conducted a study, written or co-written a research protocol or article, etc)., with a 5-point scale coded as follows: 0 (Never), 1 (Rarely), 2 (Sometimes), and 3 (Often).

Satisfaction with studies was assessed with the Scale of Satisfaction with Studies (SSS),37 a 5-item self-reported scale used to measure academic satisfaction. Participants responded to the items using a 7-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 7 (Strongly agree). The scale had very high reliability (ωt = 0.88).

Study-related stress was assessed with the Short Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), developed as a short form of the initial 14-item PSS scale by selecting four items from the self-reported questionnaire.38 Participants responded to the items using a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (Almost never) to 5 (Always). Reliability was high in this sample (ωt = 0.84).

Congruence between person and environment during studies was assessed with the subjective person/environment congruence scale in an academic context.39 The scale assesses how well the training environment aligns with students’ personalities, aspirations, and values, especially in their relationships with peers and teachers. The scale comprises 13 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (Not at all true) to 5 (Completely true) focusing on personal interests, skills, motivations, and social relationships in relation to the demands and opportunities of the study environment. Reliability was very high (ωt = 0.89).

Trait anxiety was assessed using the French version40 of the 20-item State–Trait Anxiety Inventory – Trait subscale.41 Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always).

The STAI-T measures trait anxiety, defined as a relatively stable individual tendency to perceive situations as threatening and to respond with heightened anxiety across contexts. This construct is particularly relevant in medical students, as anxiety is highly prevalent throughout medical training and is closely associated with academic pressure, uncertainty regarding career pathways, emotional burden, and increased risk of psychological distress and burnout. The STAI is one of the most widely used and extensively validated instruments for anxiety assessment in both clinical and non-clinical populations. The French validation study reported excellent internal consistency for the trait subscale (Cronbach’s α = 0.91),40 supporting its reliability for assessing stable anxiety dispositions in this population.

Contextual Influences Proximal to Choice Behavior

Mentoring was assessed using a yes/no question:

Within your professional network, do you have a mentor (a knowledgeable and experienced guide or advisor, someone you hold in high esteem, whom you can emulate, and/or who provides you with their expertise and experience)?.

Participants also specified the mentor’s gender and status (Hospital Practitioner, Associate Professor, Full Professor, or other, with an option to specify).

Relationship with the mentor was assessed using a mentoring perception scale,42 which consists of 37 items rated from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree) provides a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of the mentoring experience. In the present study, mentorship was considered a key proximal contextual influence of Academic medicine career intentions. Rather than simply documenting the presence of a mentor, this scale captures how mentorship is experienced and perceived by the mentee across several core relational and developmental dimensions. Its multidimensional structure makes it particularly relevant in medical education, where mentoring relationships extend beyond academic supervision and often contribute to professional socialization, identity formation, personal support, and career development. In the original validation study, the authors identified a five-factor structure through principal component analysis, comprising Reflection Stimulation, Mentor Presence, Response to Personal Situations, Stimulating Conditions and Perspective on Behavior Change, and Professional Identity Development. However, the French validation of the scale43 identified a more parsimonious three-factor structure: (1) Quality of the Proximal Relationship, reflecting the relational and interpersonal aspects of mentoring; (2) Academic and Strategic Support for Career Development, capturing guidance and support in the construction of career plans; and (3) Ethical and Developmental Dimension, referring to the mentor’s role in fostering personal growth, professional values, and reflective development.

Workplace stress factors were measured using the Job Stress Survey (JSS)44 which evaluates various sources of stress, such as work conditions, physical strain, relations with colleagues, etc. This measure is particularly relevant in medical training and academic medicine, where stress is shaped by multiple interacting demands, including workload, emotional burden, and team dynamics. Its multidimensional approach provides a comprehensive assessment of workplace stress and is especially useful for examining how the professional environment may influence well-being, career development, and professional engagement. The scale includes 30 items rated, each rated for both severity (on a scale from 1 to 9) and frequency (on a scale from 1 to 5); total scores were computed as the sum of (severity x frequency) for each item. Total reliability for the scale was high (ωt = 0.91, computed on the 30 severity x frequency products).

Exposure to sexism was measured with an ordinal question45 coded as 0 (no exposure to sexism), 1 (exposure to sexist remarks/behaviors only), or 2 (exposure to unwanted sexual advances or more). This measure captures a continuum of gender-based experiences within the professional environment, from everyday sexism to more explicit forms of sexual misconduct. It is particularly relevant in medical training, where gender-based discrimination can shape well-being, professional identity, and career trajectories.

AM career accessibility was measured with a yes/no question: “Do you think an AM career is accessible to everyone?”.

Background Contextual Affordances

Background contextual affordances included socio-demographic factors: parental status of the student (0 = No children vs. 1 = One or more), and parental occupational status (coded as 1 if at least one parent was a university professor, academic physician, or hospital practitioner and 0 otherwise). We also assessed academic factors through the following variables: year of residency and medical specialty, result in France’s national ranking examination (ECN), and location of the medical school (0 = Provincial vs. 1 = Paris region).

Person Inputs

Gender was assessed with a question asking participants “You are…”; response options were “a man” (coded as 0), “a woman” (coded as 1), or “none-binary” (excluded from analysis, as detailed above).

Personality was measured using the Intrapreneurial Self-Capital Scale.26 This scale assesses intrapreneurial personality, defined as the set of self-perceived positive personal resources that enable individuals to mobilize their abilities toward self-development and the pursuit of ambitious professional goals. Intrapreneurial Self-Capital (ISC) capturing perceived psychological resources related to adaptation, initiative, and career self-management, this measure provides valuable insight into factors that may influence students’ professional aspirations and career trajectories in healthcare.

The Intrapreneurial Self-Capital Scale a 28-item scale rated from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). The scale is designed to assess seven dimensions: self-core evaluation (eg. “Sometimes when I fail I feel valueless”), hardiness (eg. “By studying with strong commitment, you can always reach you goal”), creative self-efficacy (eg. “I am able to solve problems creatively”), “resilience” (eg. “I am able to achieve objectives despites obstacles”), goal mastery (eg. “One of my goals in training is to learn as much as I can”), decisiveness’ (eg. “It’s simple for me to decide”), and vigilance (eg. “When I must make decision, I like to stop and consider all possible options”) but also supports the calculation of a global score, which was used in the current study. Total reliability for this scale was high (ωt = 0.95).

To further assess the quality of the measurement model, standardized factor loadings, Composite Reliability (CR), and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) were calculated for all latent variables. Data Analysis

Before testing the primary predictors based on the SCCT model, preliminary analyses were conducted to verify three key assumptions observed in prior literature: (i) women report lower AM career intentions than men;2,8 (ii) AM career intentions decline across academic years;2,4 (iii) this decline in AM career intentions is more pronounced for women as compared to men.7,46

We then conducted a series of simple regression analyses to predict the intention to pursue an AM career from each potential determinant based on SCCT model. Since the intention to pursue an AM career was measured on an ordinal scale, we used an ordinal regression model (with a cumulative probability family, a logit link, and parallel coefficient terms), using rms package47 in R (Version 4.3.0, 2023). All predictors were standardized before analysis to make odds ratios more comparable and allow for interpretation of effect sizes.

Given the large number of predictors, two constructs being measured with a large set of multiple variables are presented as separate analyses for clarity: medical specialty (comparing the most frequent specialties in our sample: General Practice, Pediatrics, Internal Medicine and Clinical Immunology, Psychiatry, Anesthesiology, Cardiovascular Medicine, each representing more than 3% of the sample) and professional values (including eight values). The effect of features related to mentorship was also analyzed separately in the subsample of students who reported having a mentor. Only participants who identified their mentor as a Hospital Practitioner, Associate Professor, or Full Professor were included in this analysis, as these roles are directly associated with hospital practice and academic activities.

After identifying determinants with significant simple effects separately, penalized multiple regression using a Lasso penalty was applied to select the most relevant predictors. Lasso regression is similar to a classic multiple regression except that it is designed to select significant predictors among a large set of variables. It applies a lasso penalty to minimize multicollinearity and retain only the predictors with the most substantial unique effects on the dependent variable. This analysis was conducted in R statistical software using the ordinalNet package.48 For parsimony, we retained the lambda parameter with the highest value associated with a log-likelihood of model fit within the standard error of the model with the best fit.49 This analysis was performed on all variables with a significant effect in simple regressions. To avoid biasing the effect of other predictors, the analysis was conducted separately for the three variables (interest in research, participation in research, and complementary research training experiences) potentially acting as a proxy for career intentions rather than a determinant as such, which could mask the effect of other predictors. After the multiple Lasso regression selected the relevant predictors associated with the dependent variable, we refit an ordinal multiple regression without the lasso penalty to estimate the OR effect sizes of each individual predictor using rms.

Finally, to further explore the dynamics of AM career intentions as a function of gender, mediation analyses were conducted using lavaan R Package50 to identify factors contributing to women’s lower intention to pursue an AM career. Moderation analyses using rms R Package also examined possible gender-based barriers and facilitators (moderators), focusing on interactions between gender and key predictors. Prior to these two last analyses, gender differences in key variables were examined using Welch’s t-tests and chi-square tests to provide a preliminary overview of disparities and to report numerical data, including mean values and percentage distributions.

Mediation analyses indicated that the total effect of gender on AM career intentions (β = −0.19, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001) was partly mediated by Interest in research and by Complementary research experiences in the first model. The indirect effects via these mediators accounted for 57.06% of the total effect. In the second mediation model, including factors of AM career intentions not directly related to research, only two mediators revealed a significative indirect effect: Work Centrality and the value of Autonomy. For Work Centrality, the indirect effect via work centrality was negative (β = −0.03, SE = 0.01, p < 0.001), as gender had a negative effect on work centrality (β = −0.05, SE = 0.03, p = 0.030), while work centrality had a positive effect on AM career intentions (β = 0.29, SE = 0.05, p < 0.001). This indicates that gender disparities in AM career intentions were partly explained (15.61%) by differences in work centrality, controlling the role of the other mediators. For Autonomy, gender had a significant negative effect on autonomy (β = −0.05, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001), meaning that women reported lower autonomy expectation than men. Autonomy, in turn, had a significant negative effect on AM career intentions (β = −0.22, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001), suggesting that individuals with higher autonomy expectation were less inclined to pursue an AM career. As a result, the indirect effect of gender on AM career intentions via autonomy was positive (β = 0.01, SE = 0.01, p < 0.001) reducing the total effect of gender. These findings suggest a paradoxical effect in which autonomy expectations weighed more heavily on men because women were less affected by this expectation.

For the moderation analyses, none of the interactions were statistically significant (all ps >0.05) suggesting that the effects of all variables on AM career intentions were similar for men and women. In other words, women were disadvantaged for a number of psychological variables of AM career intentions, but the effect of all variables on AM career intentions was broadly similar in men and women.

Discussion

The results are consistent with previous studies, most students reported little or some intention to pursue an AM career overall, with intentions being even lower in women. This confirms the extent of low attractiveness of this AM career path, as outlined in prior studies.2,3 We also replicated the finding that AM career intentions declined over the course of the residency,4 although this was only observed when additional research-related learning experiences were statistically controlled (reflecting the fact that AM career intentions did not decline in students who engaged in complementary research experiences such as a PhD). The vast majority of residents (86.3%) acknowledged that an AM career is not accessible to everyone, which underscores the widespread awareness of barriers to entering an AM career. The results also revealed a number of psychological variables of this low attractiveness of AM careers.

Factors Associated with Career Intentions in Academic Medicine

Overall, the present study underscores the multifactorial determinants of academic medicine career attractiveness among medical students. Intentions to pursue an academic medicine career appear to be influenced by a combination of research-related experiences, individual-level characteristics, and broader perceptions regarding work and professional trajectories. Interest in research was the factor most strongly associated with academic medicine career intentions, with an odds ratio nearly three times higher than the second strongest contributor. This result was expected, given that professional interests are the most proximal variable to actual career choice goals. Complementary research experiences were the second strongest contributor, aligning with prior systematic reviews.11,12 Students involved in research activities were more than twice as likely to consider an academic medicine career, while additional research experiences were associated with a 2.5 fold increase in the likelihood of envisioning this career path. While participation in research outside of complementary experiences was also associated with academic medicine career intentions, this association did not remain significant in the multivariable analysis. This finding suggests that both interest in research and engagement in complementary research training play an important role in shaping intentions to pursue a career in academic medicine, in line with previous research.19,51

Apart from professional interest (interest in research) and learning experience (complementary research experiences), other multiple variables influencing AM career intentions included gender, intrapreneurial self-capital, mentorship, work centrality, and professional values like stimulation and conformity.

Overall, the observed relationships were consistent with the literature. As expected, women reported lower intentions to pursue a career in academic medicine.7 Perceiving oneself as having an intrapreneurial personality was also associated with a more favorable orientation toward an academic medicine career (eg. taste for challenge, perseverance, adaptability, and the ability to pursue ambitious goals). Intrapreneurial Self-Capital refers to a set of perceived personal resources, such as the ability to cope with challenges, adapt to change, make decisions, or transform constraints into opportunities.25,26 In the demanding context of medical studies and academic careers, these resources may play a role in how students envision their professional future and project themselves into particularly demanding career paths. More specifically, the perception of being able to mobilize one’s own resources may contribute to sustaining professional aspirations despite the uncertainty, competition, and workload associated with these trajectories. This dimension may be particularly relevant to explore among women, for whom these resources are sometimes less valued or less socially recognized.

Furthermore, the presence of a mentor appeared to be a favorable factor for engagement in an academic medicine career, consistent with previous works.13 Mentorship plays an important role in career choices by influencing how individuals envision themselves within a given professional path, with students receiving mentorship showing an approximately 48% greater likelihood of considering this career path. Interestingly, a higher proportion of men reported having a mentor compared with women (43.7% vs. 37.7%), suggesting potential gender differences in access to mentoring opportunities. Furthermore, the positive association with AM career intentions was stronger when mentors held more senior positions, indicating that mentor status may enhance the perceived attractiveness or attainability of an academic career path. In contrast, neither the mentor’s gender nor the perceived quality of mentorship was significantly associated with AM career intentions. More broadly, having an experienced person who can guide, advise, and support students throughout their training (both academically and in reflecting on their professional trajectory) seems to facilitate the clarification and consolidation of career choices. A more detailed understanding of the mentor–mentee relationship14 deserves particular attention, as its effectiveness depends on multiple factors involving both parties: the mentor’s availability and guidance, as well as the mentee’s initiative, reflexivity, and engagement in the relationship. This issue appears especially important for women in medicine, among whom the lack of female mentorship is regularly identified as a major barrier to engagement in certain academic careers.

At the level of perceptions, a high level of work centrality was more strongly associated with the intention to pursue an academic medicine career,17,52 as were certain professional values,12 particularly a high need for stimulation,18 a low need for conformity (which is consistent with the fact that conformity is opposed to stimulation in the circumplex model of professional values35), as well as a low need for autonomy. The role of a low need for autonomy was the most unexpected finding: this may reflect the fact that bureaucracy and lack of professional control are important barriers to engagement in an academic medicine career,19 or it may indicate a preference among residents interested in this career path for more structured environments offering greater mentorship and clearer supervisory frameworks.

Among the psychological variables examined for their association with academic medicine career intentions, many showed significant effects in simple regression analyses but were no longer significant when considered jointly with other variables. This does not necessarily undermine their relevance, as predictors that lose significance in multivariable models may reflect more distal associations. This was the case for self-efficacy, whose effect may be partly captured by having an intrapreneurial personality (which correlated r = 0.69 with self-efficacy), as well as for study-related stress, job-related stress, satisfaction and congruence with studies, which all correlated with intrapreneurial personality. The detrimental effect of sexism may, unfortunately, be largely captured by the effect of gender.

Factors Associated with the Gender Gap in Academic Medicine Career Intentions

The results examine factors associated with lower intentions among women to pursue a career in academic medicine and aim to identify potential barriers and facilitators specific to both men and women. Our findings lead to three main conclusions.

First, women were disadvantaged compared to men on virtually all variables contributing to AM career choices. This was true for the key variables identified in the multiple regression: women had lower interest in research, were less likely to engage in complementary research experiences, were less likely to have a mentor, reported a lower feeling of work centrality, a lower need for stimulation, and a personality less oriented towards facing challenges. This was also true for almost all variables with a significant effect only in the simple regressions (these results were not detailed here for simplicity): women also experienced higher levels of work-related and study-related stress, higher anxiety, lower self-efficacy, lower satisfaction with studies, and of course more exposition to sexism (all ps < 0.001).53

Second, the gender gap in AM career intentions was largely explained by women’s lower research interest, fewer research experiences,27 and reduced work centrality. Interestingly, women’s lower autonomy needs served as a protective factor. Third, no gender-specific moderators were found, suggesting that career intention factors affect both genders similarly. Thus, women should not be treated as a distinct group regarding AM career choices, as the primary psychological variables of AM career intentions influenced men and women in a similar way.

Finally, the vast majority of students (86.3%) considered that a career in academic medicine is not accessible to everyone. This finding highlights a widely shared perception of barriers to entering this career path, whether related to competition, academic demands, workload, or the challenge of balancing the clinical, academic, and personal dimensions of such a career. This collective perception deserves particular attention, as it may shape professional aspirations early in training and contribute to the self-selection of certain profiles.

Implications and Practical Recommendations

Overall, this study emphasizes that the attractiveness of academic medicine careers develops progressively at the intersection of academic, personal, psychological, and social factors. A better understanding of these determinants appears essential for developing targeted strategies aimed at making these careers more visible, accessible, and attractive throughout medical training, and ultimately supporting the renewal of future generations of physician-scientists and academic clinicians.

Fostering interest in research and offering opportunities for additional research training are the first two catalysts to consider, although they both require the investment of institutional resources (eg. to implement a course familiarizing students with the added value of research for a medical practitioner). Integrating research to a greater extent into physicians’ hospital-based medical practice would be a way to foster professional interest in research even among residents aiming for a non-academic hospital career, and would be more productive than presenting clinical and academic careers as strictly distinct professions.

Creating an institutional process to formally make it possible for students to request mentors to accompany an AM career path is also an option supported by the present data, and one that may be particularly cost-effective. Although the constellation of variables related to anxiety, stress and satisfaction with studies only reached significance in simple regressions, promoting a supportive study and work environment can only be helpful to encourage AM career tracks.

Regarding our second objective, there are few reasons to treat women as an entirely distinct group, and most interventions outlined above can be expected to be equally effective for men and women. Promoting interest in research and ensuring that AM careers are compatible with lower work centrality should be especially helpful. It is noteworthy that the lower engagement of women in additional research experiences, and their lower probability of having a mentor, may both be due to perception that these options are less accessible to women (eg., due to gender stereotypes).

Finally, workplace sexism was significantly associated with academic medicine career intentions. The marked gender disparities observed across different forms of sexism are particularly concerning. Compared with men, women reported substantially higher exposure to sexist remarks and behaviors, unwanted sexual advances, and threats or negative consequences related to sexual conduct. These experiences appear to undermine women’s willingness to pursue a career in academic medicine. Notably, more than two-thirds of women reported experiencing sexism in the workplace, and one in four reported unwanted sexual advances. The association between workplace sexism and reduced academic medicine career intentions should be considered within the broader context of structural inequities in medicine.54 Sexism operates through institutional and cultural mechanisms that shape access to opportunities, mentorship, recognition, and career advancement. These overlapping systems of disadvantage may contribute to a diminished sense of belonging and lower interest in academic careers among affected individuals, highlighting the need for comprehensive institutional strategies to promote equity and inclusion. These findings highlight the urgent need for institutions to address sexism and gender-based discrimination within academic and clinical environments. Creating safer and more inclusive workplaces is a fundamental prerequisite for enabling women to envision and pursue academic medicine careers. Beyond preventing discriminatory behaviors, institutions should actively foster supportive environments by improving access to mentoring opportunities, promoting a culture of respect and benevolence, and strengthening policies that advance gender equity in academic medicine. Such efforts are essential not only to enhance career aspirations among women but also to ensure a more diverse and equitable academic workforce. Creating safer work environments could indirectly encourage more women to consider AM careers.

Limitations and Perspectives for Future Studies

Our study has three major limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits understanding of temporal dynamics during residency Our study has three major limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits understanding of temporal dynamics during residency.55 A longitudinal analysis of the factors identified in the present study, combined with flow diagram approaches, would help illustrate how interest in academic medicine careers evolves throughout medical training.56 Second, as recruitment was conducted through open dissemination (social media and Email distribution), the total number of individuals who received the survey invitation could not be determined, and therefore no response rate could be calculated. This recruitment strategy may have introduced self-selection bias. Third, academic medicine career intention was assessed using a single-item measure, which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of career aspirations. Future studies should consider using validated multi-item instruments to provide a more comprehensive assessment of this construct.

Conclusion

This study highlights the multifactorial nature of the attractiveness of academic medicine careers among medical students. Consistent with previous literature, interest in research, participation in research activities, prior research training (Master’s or PhD), and mentorship emerged as the strongest predictors of intentions to pursue a career in academic medicine. Other individual factors were also associated with this orientation. Perceiving oneself as having resources related to Intrapreneurial Self-Capital was associated with a greater likelihood of considering an academic medicine career. Similarly, a stronger sense of congruence with one’s studies, greater satisfaction with medical training, and higher self-efficacy were positively associated with this intention, whereas higher levels of perceived stress and anxiety were negatively associated with it. Work-related expectations also appeared to play an important role, particularly high work centrality, a stronger need for stimulation, and lower needs for conformity and autonomy. These findings suggest that students attracted to academic medicine particularly value intellectually stimulating and highly demanding professional environments. Finally, our results suggest that the psychological determinants underlying career intentions in academic medicine are broadly comparable between men and women, indicating that women may not differ as a distinct group in terms of the mechanisms shaping these career choices. By shifting the focus from individual choices to structural conditions, our findings provide actionable insights for institutions aiming to foster a more inclusive and appealing academic career landscape. Although centered on AM careers, these results could also resonate in other high-demanding fields, offering a lens to think about other career.
