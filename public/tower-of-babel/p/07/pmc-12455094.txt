Theta-shaking mitigates cognitive-emotional decline via subiculum and ventral septum metabolic plasticity
Authors: Runhong Yao, Kouji Yamada, Hirohide Sawada, Takeshi Chihara, Naoki Aizu, Kazuhiro Nishii
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging-associated cognitive decline remains a major challenge in gerontology; few non-invasive interventions provide both mechanistic insight and translational feasibility. We investigated whether low-frequency “theta-shaking” whole-body vibration (5 ​Hz) could modulate cognitive function, emotional behavior, and metabolic plasticity in a senescence-accelerated mouse model. Senescence-accelerated mouse prone-10 mice were exposed to theta-shaking stimulation for 30 weeks. Spatial memory was assessed using Y-maze spontaneous alternation test, and anxiety-related behavior was evaluated using marble burying test. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were conducted to assess neuronal density and protein expression in specific brain regions. Theta-shaking subjected mice exhibited delayed yet significant improvements in spatial memory at 20 (p ​= ​0.017) and 30 (p ​= ​0.018) weeks. Anxiety-related behavior shows a biphasic pattern: an initial increase at 20 weeks (p ​< ​0.001) followed by stabilization at 30 weeks. Histological analysis revealed preserved neuronal density in the subiculum (p ​< ​0.001) and elevated proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1α) expression in the Cornu Ammonis 1, subiculum, and lateral septum (all p ​< ​0.05). Notably, mitochondrial biogenesis appeared to be intervention's primary target, as shown by robust PGC1α upregulation, while brain-derived neurotrophic factor revealed a trend-level increase (p ​= ​0.062), and neurotrophin-3 expression remained unchanged. Frequency-tuned mechanical stimulation induced region-specific neural neurometabolic adaptations, supporting theta-shaking as a non-pharmacological, low-exertion strategy to counteract brain aging. These findings offer promising translational potential, especially for individuals with limited mobility.Keywords: Theta-shaking, Aging, Cognitive decline, Vibration, Hippocampal circuits, Metabolic plasticity

Introduction

Neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease and late-life anxiety syndromes, are imposing increasing socioeconomic burdens worldwide.1,2 Cognitive-emotional decline in aging is driven not only by synaptic loss but also by mitochondrial vulnerability within key neural circuits, particularly the hippocampal-septal axis.3 Conventional interventions—such as pharmacological treatments (e.g., Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma agonists) and physical exercise—face important limitations: systemic drugs often fail to adequately cross the blood-brain barrier and may cause off-target effects, while adherence to exercise protocols tends to decrease in older individuals due to mobility limitations and comorbid conditions.4 These challenges highlight pressing need for noninvasive, low-exertion strategies that directly address the metabolic fragility underlying cognitive-emotional dysfunction—an aspect frequently overlooked in traditional approaches.

Hippocampal-lateral septum circuits: a nexus of metabolic vulnerability

The hippocampus-lateral septum (HPC-LS) axis plays a central role in spatial memory and emotional regulation. These regions communicate via theta oscillations (4–8 ​Hz), enabling the integration of cognitive and affective signals.5,6 Aging disrupts this dynamic through three interconnected mechanisms.

a.Mitochondrial attrition: Hippocampal Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) production declines by approximately 33 ​% by 18 months of age in rodents, compromising synaptic plasticity.7

b.Neurotrophic withdrawal: Age-related reductions in hippocampal brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels have been documented in both humans and animals.8,9

c.Circuit desynchronization: Theta coherence between the hippocampus and lateral septum diminishes with age, impairing information flow.10,11

Although current interventions often aim to restore BDNF levels—via aerobic exercise or Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) agonists—their delayed onset of action limits their ability to address acute energy deficits in metabolically fragile neurons.12 This underscores the importance of targeting mitochondrial resilience directly, a function mediated by transcriptional coactivator peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1α), a key regulator of oxidative phosphorylation and antioxidant defense.13,14

Mechanoneurobiology: a paradigm shift in metabolic rescue

Recent evidence highlights mechanical stimuli as potent modulators of cellular energetics. In peripheral tissues, pulsatile shear stress activates PGC1α through Piezo1-mediated calcium influx, bypassing classical growth factor signaling.15 Applied to the brain, vibratory stimulation offers a unique means of engaging metabolic pathways independently of neurotrophic support. For example, low-intensity shaking improves cognition in aged rodents by upregulating fibronectin type III domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5)/irisin, a myokine linked to mitochondrial biogenesis.16 However, several key gaps remain.

b.The timing between mitochondrial adaptation and neurotrophic changes remains unclear, hindering mechanistic prioritization.

c.Real-time biomarkers to monitoring vibratory efficacy—such as PGC1α+ neuron counts—have not been validated.

Objectives and innovation

We hypothesize that theta-frequency mechanical stimulation (5 ​Hz) induces spatiotemporally distinct metabolic plasticity the HPC-LS axis, with early PGC1α-mediated ATP buffering preceding delayed neurotrophic changes. Using the SAMP-10 model,17 which mimics human neuroinflammatory aging, we addressed three translational challenges.

a.Regional Specificity: Are vibratory effects localized to HPC-LS subregions with high bioenergetic demand, such as the subiculum (a memory integration hub) and ventral lateral septum (LSv), involved in anxiety regulation?

b.Temporal Dynamics: Does mitochondrial rescue precede and stabilize synaptic plasticity before BDNF/neurotrophin-3 (NT3)-driven remodeling?

By addressing these points, we redefined vibratory stimulation as subregion-targeted metabolic therapies, offering a mechanistically distinct alternative to conventional neurotrophic strategies.

Methods

Animals

We used the senescence-accelerated prone mouse strain (SAMP10), a well-established accelerated ageing model.18,19 Although 9-week-old SAMP10 mice are young in chronological age, they are genetically predisposed to exhibit early-onset and progressive ageing phenotypes, including hair loss, skin lesions, increased lordokyphosis, reduced activity, and a shortened lifespan (median survival ∼10 months). Importantly, SAMP10 mice display age-associated neurodegeneration, including cerebral cortex atrophy, limbic system shrinkage, and reductions in both the number and size of neurons, leading to early impairments in learning and memory. Thus, the present study represents a preventive intervention in an accelerated ageing model, rather than a treatment for advanced-stage ageing. All animal procedures were conducted by the guidelines of the National Institute of Health (Bethesda, MD, USA). The protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Fujita Health University (Toyoake, Japan; approval number, APU19120). Mice were housed under controlled (25 ​± ​1 ​°C; 12-h light/dark cycle, lights on from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.) with free access to food and water. Eight-week-old male SAMP-10 (SAMP-10/TaSlc; SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) were randomly assigned to one of two groups: shaking (n ​= ​14) or control (n ​= ​14).

Method details

Exercise protocol and theta-shaking exercise

Theta-shaking stimulation was initiated at 9 weeks of age and continued until week 30; given the accelerated ageing trajectory of SAMP-10, this protocol constitutes a preventive paradigm aimed at delaying age-related neural and behavioral decline (Fig. 1A). Mice in the shaking group were placed individually in separate compartments of a shaking platform (NR-3; TAITEC Co., Ltd., Saitama, Japan) that generated a unique horizontal rotational motion (5 ​Hz; 50 ​mm displacement) applied uniformly across 360°. This 5 ​Hz frequency was selected to align with the natural theta rhythm of the rodent hippocampus, distinguishing it from conventional vibration therapies that typically employ high-frequency (>30 ​Hz) vertical or multidirectional oscillations. Sessions lasted 30 ​min per day, three times per week, for 21 consecutive weeks. During sessions, animals remained isolated and did not interact.

Experimental Timeline and Brain Subregion Parcellation

A. Longitudinal design: SAMP-10 mice received theta-shaking (5 ​Hz horizontal vibration, 30 ​min/day, 3 days/week) or no intervention from 9 to 30 weeks of age. Behavioral tests (Y-maze, marble burying) were conducted at 8 (baseline), 20, and 30 weeks, followed by tissue collection for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and ELISA. B. Brain regions analyzed included: suprapyramidal (sDG) and infrapyramidal (iDG) blades of the dentate gyrus, proximal/distal CA1 (pCA1/dCA1) and CA3 (pCA3/dCA3), subiculum, and lateral septum subregions (LSc, LSr, LSv).

Behavioral tests

Y-maze test

The Y-maze consisted of three identical arms (BRC. LE847, W300 mm ​× ​D600 mm ​× ​H150 mm) made of opaque acrylic. Each mouse was placed at the maze center under ambient illumination (20 lx) and allowed to explore freely for 5 ​min. An arm entry was recorded when all four paws entered an arm. Total entries and correct alternations (three consecutive entries into different arms) were evaluated. Alternation (%) ​= ​[Number of correct triads/(Total entries ​− ​2)] ​× ​100.

Marble-burying test

Mice were individually placed in cages with 5 ​cm of fresh bedding for a 30-min test session. Twenty-five marbles were evenly spaced across the surface prior to testing. At the end, marbles buried ≥2/3 were counted; borderline marbles (∼2/3 or less) were excluded. All tests were conducted in a quiet environment to minimize stress.

Tissue preparation

After the final behavioral test, all animals underwent a thoracotomy under general anesthesia (isoflurane), followed by perfusion with phosphate-buffered saline and brain extraction. Tissues for biochemical analysis were snap-frozen and stored at −80 ​°C. Samples for histology were fixed in 10 ​% neutral-buffered formalin and paraffin-embedded using an automated system. Of the 14 mice, seven were allocated for immunohistochemistry and seven for Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA).

Immunohistochemistry

Sagittal sections (20 ​μm) containing the central zone of the hippocampus and lateral septum (interaural coordinates ​= ​1.00 ​mm) were obtained using a microtome. Primary antibodies included anti-FNDC5 (ab181884; Abcam PLC, Cambridge, UK) and anti-PGC1α (N-terminal; ab191838, PCL, Cambridge, UK). Both were used at 1:100 dilution. The secondary antibody—biotin-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H ​+ ​L) (BA-1000; Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA)—was applied at 1:1000. Color development was performed using enzyme substrate (PK-6100; VECTASTAIN Elite ABC Standard Kit; Vector Laboratories) and diaminobenzidine (cat. no. 40651; Muto Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). All steps followed standard protocols.

The HPC was subdivided into four regions: dentate gyrus (DG), Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3), Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1), and subiculum. For detailed analysis, the DG was further divided into upper (suprapyramidal) and lower (infrapyramidal) blades. CA1 and CA3 were each separated into proximal (closer to DG) and distal (farther from DG) subfields. The LS was divided into three subregions: dorsal (LSc), rostral (LSr), and ventral (LSv),20 allowing for a more granular assessment of neuronal activity (Fig. 1B).

Neuronal counts were manually performed in the dorsal HPC–LS regions and quantified using ImageJ software (1.52a; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Quantification included both the percentage of PGC1α-positive neurons and total neuronal counts per region.

ELISA

Brain samples from hippocampal and non-hippocampal regions were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, pulverized and lysed using a radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy). After protein quantification and normalization, lysates were centrifuged and supernatants collected. Protein levels of BNDF and NT3 were measured using commercial ELISA kits (BDNF: BEK-2211-1P; NT3: BEK-2231; Biosensis Pty Ltd., Thebarton, Australia). Sample dilutions were optimized, and all procedures followed the manufacturer's protocols.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Behavioral data (Y-maze and marble-burying tests) were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. Immunohistochemistry and ELISA data were analyzed using unpaired t-tests (for normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U tests (for non-normal data). Statistical significance was set at p ​< ​0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS v25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Spatiotemporal specificity of cognitive enhancement

Delayed spatial memory improvement

Spontaneous alternation percentages in the Y-maze were measured at 8 (57.72 ​± ​14.08 ​% vs. 57.12 ​± ​12.93 ​%), 20 (51.19 ​± ​16.07 ​% vs. 65.79 ​± ​14.03 ​%), and 30 weeks (45.93 ​± ​24.48 ​% vs. 63.99 ​± ​8.05 ​%) for control vs. theta-shaking groups, respectively. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant group effect (F[1,25] ​= ​9.949, p ​= ​0.004), but no effect of time (F[2,50] ​= ​0.370, p ​= ​0.693) or group-time interaction (F[2,50] ​= ​2.635, p ​= ​0.090). Post hoc Bonferroni analysis showed significantly higher alternation in the theta-shaking group at 20 (p ​= ​0.017) and 30 weeks (p ​= ​0.018). These findings indicate improved spatial memory in the theta-shaking group at later stages (Fig. 2A).

Preserved exploratory motivation amid aging

The number of arm entries in the Y-maze was recorded at 8 (31.29 ​± ​7.81 vs. 31.50 ​± ​6.39), 20 (14.93 ​± ​7.81 vs. 21.92 ​± ​5.80), and 30 weeks (10.14 ​± ​1.91 vs. 16.69 ​± ​4.80) for control vs. theta-shaking groups. Biphasic emotional adaptation

Marble-burying behavior was assessed at 8 weeks (0.00 ​± ​0.00 vs. 0.21 ​± ​0.44), 20 weeks (acute phase: 0.36 ​± ​0.50 vs. 4.93 ​± ​3.31), and 30 weeks (adaptive phase: 0.43 ​± ​0.51 vs. 0.23 ​± ​0.44) for control vs. theta-shaking groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of group (F[1,25] ​= ​18.767, p ​< ​0.001), time (F[1,27] ​= ​22.472, p ​< ​0.001), and their interaction (F[1,27] ​= ​19.604, p ​< ​0.001). Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc analysis revealed that, in the theta-shaking group marble-burying significantly increased from baseline to 20 (p ​< ​0.001) and again from 20 to 30 weeks (p ​< ​0.001). In controls, a significant difference was found only between 8 and 30 weeks (p ​= ​0.021). The animals in the theta-shaking group buried significantly more marbles than the control animals at both acute (p ​< ​0.001) and adaptive phase (p ​= ​0.018). These results suggest that theta-shaking induces a biphasic trajectory of anxiety-like behavior. (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Spatiotemporal metabolic plasticity driven by theta-shaking

CA1, subiculum and LS as metabolic hubs: subregion-specific PGC1α upregulation

Cognitive-emotional decline in aging is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction within the hippocampal-septum axis—a circuit essential for integrating spatial memory and emotional regulation.13,21 Our findings demonstrate that theta-shaking induces subregion-specific metabolic remodeling in SAMP-10 mice, with PGC1α upregulation serving as a central mediator (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The most robust effects were observed in the subiculum and ventral lateral septum.

In the subiculum, theta-shaking led to a 1.87-fold increase in PGC1α+ neurons (32.28 ​% vs. 60.32 ​%, p ​= ​0.040) and significantly elevated neuronal density (25.14 vs. 84.71, p ​< ​0.001). These changes coincided with improved Y-maze performance (alternation: 45.93 ​% vs. 63.99 ​%, p ​= ​0.018), suggesting enhanced synaptic energy buffering and neuroprotection in this metabolically sensitive region.

Although pCA1 showed a modest increase in PGC1α expression (3.06 ​% vs. 5.12 ​%, p ​= ​0.038), this may support adjacent hippocampal circuits involved in memory processing.

The LSv showed a striking 14.9-fold increase in PGC1α expression (p ​= ​0.002), which paralleled reduced anxiety-like behavior in the marble-burying test (0.23 vs. 0.43, p ​= ​0.018). This underscores LSv's role in theta-driven septo-hippocampal synchrony and stress regulation. Other LS subregions also responded to theta-shaking: LSr showed a robust effect (p ​= ​0.005), while LSc exhibited a smaller but significant increase (p ​= ​0.026). This gradient of metabolic plasticity (LSv ​> ​LSr ​> ​LSc) may reflect functional hierarchies in septal contributions to theta rhythm entrainment.

This anatomical specificity contrasts with conventional exercise protocols, which typically diffuse BDNF upregulation throughout the hippocampus.22 In contrast theta-shaking selectively targets subiculum, pCA1, and LS—regions with high mitochondrial demands—suggests a mechanotransduction mechanism that prioritizes energetically vulnerable circuits.

Behavioral-metabolic temporal coupling: from anxiety priming to stabilization

The biphasic behavioral pattern—acute anxiety elevation at 20 weeks followed by stabilization at 30 weeks—paralleled the delayed PGC1α upregulation in LS subregions (Fig. 3). This trajectory resembles clinical findings in deep brain stimulation for obsessive-compulsive disorder, where transient anxiety precedes therapeutic improvements via ventral striatal modulation.23,24 We propose that early LS hyperactivity reflects a phase of metabolic priming, during which PGC1α-mediated mitochondrial biogenesis progressively restores inhibitory control over septo-limbic projections.

Mechanistic dissociation: mitochondrial rescue precedes neurotrophic remodeling

Theta-shaking dissociated mitochondrial adaptation from classical neurotrophic signaling.25,26 While hippocampal BDNF showed a non-significant 18 ​% increase (p ​= ​0.062), NT3 levels remained unchanged (p ​> ​0.1) (Fig. 5). This divergence may reflect two complementary mechanisms.27,28

First, mitochondrial remodeling via PGC1α may precede neurotrophic responses—occurring between weeks 20–30—whereas BDNF may require longer stimulation, as seen in studies reporting its elevation only after ≥40 weeks of aerobic exercise.29 Second, mechanostimulation may selectively activate mitochondrial biogenesis through Piezo1–calcium–PGC1α pathways, bypassing TrkB-dependent BDNF signaling. This pathway specificity has been reported in low-intensity vibration models targeting muscle regeneration.30,31

Frequency-dependent mechanotransduction: positioning theta-shaking in the field

Prior research has demonstrated that the systemic and cellular effects of mechanical stimulation depend critically on frequency, magnitude, and application mode. For example, low-magnitude high-frequency (LMHF) vibration at 60 ​Hz inhibited prostate cancer growth via osteocyte–endothelial interactions,32 low-intensity vibration (LIV) at 100 ​Hz enhanced mesenchymal stem cell proliferation and collagen synthesis in bone marrow analogs,33 and LIV-induced proliferation in CHO cells involved AKT phosphorylation.34 Whole-body vibration training (WBVT) improved cognition in sedentary individuals and synergized with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS).35

Our theta-shaking protocol differs in applying horizontal rotational oscillations at 5 ​Hz—matching hippocampal theta rhythms—yielding subregion-selective effects (e.g., subiculum, LSv) and a dissociation between metabolic and neurotrophic responses. These distinctions suggest that high-frequency, low-magnitude vibrations may be optimal for peripheral mechanotransduction, whereas theta-range, ultra-low-magnitude oscillations could preferentially engage central nervous system circuits, potentially via different mechanosensitive pathways such as Piezo1-mediated calcium influx. This parameter-specific targeting may represent a novel avenue for preventive neuroprotection in aging models.

Mechanistic insights and translational potential

Circuit-specific targeting: mechanotransduction in high-energy demand regions

Identifying the subiculum, CA1, and LS as responsive hubs challenges the traditionally DG-centered view of hippocampal plasticity. While DG neurogenesis predominates in exercise studies,30 theta-shaking preferentially targets regions that act as metabolic gatekeepers for cognitive-emotional integration.

The subiculum, as the hippocampus's primary output, integrates cortical input from memory retrieval via glutamatergic projections to the entorhinal cortex.36, 37, 38 Its high ATP demand to sustain long-term potentiation renders it especially vulnerable to mitochondrial decline with age. Theta-shaking's 1.87-fold increase in PGC1α expression (∗p ​= ​0.040) may buffer these energy deficits, supporting improved memory performance (Y-maze: 45.93 ​% vs. 63.99 ​%, ∗p ​= ​0.018).

Proximal CA1 functions as an energy-efficient relay between hippocampal subregions. Located between CA3 and the subiculum, pCA1 transmits processed information with lower energetic demands (PGC1α+ neurons: 32.28 ​% vs. 60.32 ​%, ∗p ​= ​0.040). Its intermediate plasticity may explain the more modest response to theta-shaking, possibly supporting neighboring regions rather than acting as a primary metabolic hub.

The lateral septum modulates anxiety via GABAergic projections to the hypothalamus39,40—a process that paradoxically, requires high ATP expenditure, as inhibitory neurotransmission consumes more ATP than excitatory signaling.41 The 14.9-fold increase in PGC1α in LS (∗∗p ​< ​0.05) suggests enhanced mitochondrial efficiency, potentially stabilizing septo-hypothalamic inhibition despite age-related metabolic stress.

Protocol optimization: benchmarking 5 ​Hz parameters for safety and efficacy

The 5 ​Hz/30 ​min stimulation paradigm proved effective without inducing adverse effects (e.g., stress hormone elevation), establishing a preclinical benchmark. Biomechanical modeling suggests this frequency aligns with human hippocampal theta rhythms (4–7 ​Hz),42 supporting its translational relevance.

Scope, limitations, and future directions

While vibration-based or mechanical exercise paradigms can broadly improve systemic health,32, 33, 34, 35 the present study demonstrated localized neuronal preservation and PGC1α upregulation in hippocampal–lateral septum regions—circuits particularly susceptible to age-related decline.43 These findings suggest that the preventive effects of theta-shaking may involve both general well-being and targeted neuroprotection, with the latter potentially offering added value in aging models.

Several limitations should be considered to guide future research and enhance translational relevance. First, this study included only male rodents, leaving potential sex-specific effects unexamined. Anatomical differences between species—particularly between the rodent's LSv region and the primate bed nucleus of the stria terminalis—also pose challenges for direct translation.

Mechanistically, while our data demonstrate region-specific increases in PGC1α-positive neurons and preserved neuronal density—findings consistent with enhanced metabolic resilience—we acknowledge that direct evidence of mitochondrial biogenesis (e.g., ATP production, oxygen consumption rate) is required to definitively establish metabolic plasticity. Although we performed histological assessments of PGC1α expression in the hippocampus and lateral septum, we did not evaluate neurogenesis-specific markers such as DCX or BrdU. Future studies incorporating these assays are needed to determine whether theta-shaking also promotes structural plasticity through neurogenesis. Future studies employing conditional PGC1α knockout models44 or in vivo mitochondrial PET imaging (e.g., 18FBCPP-EF)45 could help clarify this causal relationship. Notably, neurotrophic responses remained modest, with only a marginal BDNF increase and no significant change in NT3.46,47 This pattern may reflect either: (1) a temporal lag in neurotrophic response given our 30-week endpoint, or (2) a preferential activation of mitochondrial pathways over classical neurotrophic signaling by theta-shaking, as observed in other low-intensity vibration paradigms.

Technically, future studies should incorporate automated, AI-assisted tools such as QuPath to improve stereological precision. In parallel, systematic testing of other vibration frequencies (e.g., 3 ​Hz, 10 ​Hz), along with biomechanical scaling, will be essential for adapting the protocol to non-human primate and eventual human clinical translation.

Conclusion

Theta-shaking shows promise as a novel, non-pharmacological strategy to mitigate age-related cognitive and emotional decline, with our data suggesting its effects may be mediated through enhanced mitochondrial resilience in the hippocampal-septum axis. Our findings indicate that this intervention selectively upregulates PGC1α expression in the CA1, subiculum, and lateral septum—key hubs for integrating memory and affect, consistent with metabolic plasticity. Unlike conventional exercise approaches primarily reliant on neurotrophic signaling, theta-shaking appears to engage mechanotransduction pathways, potentially offering a more targeted means to stabilize energetically vulnerable circuits.

This work repositions vibratory interventions from global hippocampal modulation to subregion-specific metabolic rescue, bridging mechanobiology and gerontology. The demonstrated safety and efficacy of the 5 ​Hz protocol provide a strong preclinical basis for translational. Future directions include frequency personalization and development of mitochondrial biomarkers for clinical monitoring. By prioritizing metabolic over neurotrophic pathways, our study highlights the therapeutic promise of physics-based, exertion-independent strategies for treating brain aging in vulnerable populations.
