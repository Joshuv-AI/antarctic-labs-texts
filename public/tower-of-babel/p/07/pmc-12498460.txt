Astragalus polysaccharide-based nano-platforms loading PTX to boost chemo-immunotherapy for triple-negative breast cancer with intrinsic GLUT-targeting ability and immunoregulatory activity
Authors: Jinshuai Lan, Wenlong Nie, Zhijun Bi, Ruifeng Zeng, Zhe Li, Tong Zhang, Yue Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemo-immunotherapy shows great promises for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) treatment, including PTX. However, the chemo-immunotherapy of PTX is limited, due to its upregulation of PD-L1 expression on tumor cells and bone marrow suppression. In addition, nano drug delivery systems of PTX encounter substantial challenges against tumors, such as inefficient encapsulation and inadequate tumor targeting efficacy. Here, PTX-loaded nanoparticles (APS-PTX NPs) based on astragalus polysaccharide (APS) was successfully developed to address these challenges. In APS-PTX NPs, APS could form amphiphilic polymers to highly load PTX, in which APS worked as the carrier, active targeting ligand, immunoregulator and chemotherapy adjuvant at the same time. Compared to PTX or Lipusu® (PTX-Lipo), APS-PTX NPs increased cellular uptake by binding to high expressed glucose transporter of 4T1 cells, and synergistically enhanced cytotoxicity of PTX. Meanwhile, APS-PTX NPs induced immunogenic cell death to induce DC maturation and decreased PD-L1 expression of 4T1 cells. In the TNBC model, APS-PTX NPs synergistically activates a potent systemic anti-tumor immunity, effectively inhibiting tumor growth and lung metastasis, and alleviating the reduction of white blood cell induced by PTX. Overall, the multifunctional nanoplatforms based on APS could overcome the harsh tumor biological barriers, and boost chemo-immunotherapy for TNBC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03708-0.Keywords: Chemo-immunotherapy, Immunogenic cell death, Polysaccharide nanocarriers, GLUT-targeting, Paclitaxel

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) constitutes approximately 15 − 20% of all breast cancer cases. It is distinguished by its highly aggressive phenotype, frequently resulting in early recurrence and distant metastasis [1, 2]. Although chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of systemic treatment for TNBC, patient outcomes are suboptimal, with a median overall survival of merely 13.3 months [3, 4]. Emerging evidence indicates that chemotherapy can enhance the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy through modulating immune responses [5, 6]. Notably, certain chemotherapeutic agents, such as paclitaxel (PTX), doxorubicin, and oxaliplatin, have been demonstrated to induce immunogenic cell death (ICD). ICD is a process that stimulates adaptive antitumor immunity in immunocompetent hosts [7–9]. ICD represents a distinct form of programmed cell death, which elicits a robust immune response against tumor cells [10, 11]. This process is mediated by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including adenosine triphosphate (ATP), calreticulin (CRT), and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) from dying cancer cells [12–14]. These DAMPs function as potent immunostimulatory signals, facilitating the recruitment and activation of dendritic cells (DCs), which subsequently initiate T-cell mediated antitumor immunity [15]. However, the therapeutic potential of chemotherapy-induced immune activation is often limited by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (ITME) [16], such as the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD - L1, CD47, CTLA4) [17, 18] and the overexpression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO) [19].

Paclitaxel (PTX), a potent microtubule-stabilizing agent, demonstrates significant antitumor activity against TNBC. However, its first-generation formulation, Taxol®, is associated with severe clinical side effects, limiting its therapeutic efficacy and safety [20–22]. To address these limitations, nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have emerged as a promising approach. Examples include Abraxane® (albumin-bound PTX, USA) and Lipusu® (PTX-Lipo, China) [23]. Despite considerable progress in preclinical research, the clinical translation of nanomedicines remains largely confined to liposomal and polymeric micelle formulations, while multifunctional and targeted nanotherapeutics have not yet been clinically applied [24]. A major obstacle in nanoparticle-based cancer therapy is the reliance on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for tumor targeting, which is frequently compromised by elevated interstitial fluid pressure within the tumor microenvironment [25]. This pressure gradient severely limits the penetration and uniform distribution of nanoparticles, reducing their ability to reach and accumulate in deeper tumor regions [26]. Furthermore, conventional nanocarriers lack tumor targeting, and those designed for active targeting often require sophisticated modifications, such as conjugation with anti-PD-L1 antibodies [27]. These modifications not only involve complex synthetic processes and stringent optimization of reaction conditions to achieve efficient antibody-nanocarrier coupling but also significantly increase production costs [28]. Additionally, prolonged administration of PTX in clinical settings can induce leukopenia, impairing patients’ immune function and hindering their recovery from postoperative tissue damage and inflammatory responses [29, 30]. In light of these crucial challenges, there is an immediate requirement to engineer an innovative delivery platform for PTX that can comprehensively and efficiently overcome these limitations.

Polysaccharides feature diverse functional groups on their sugar rings, including hydroxyl, amino, carboxylic acid, and ester groups, enabling easy modification into various derivatives [31, 32]. Among them, chitosan is widely used as a nanocarrier due to its excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, and ease of modification [33]. Polysaccharide-based nanoparticles enhance tumor-specific accumulation by interacting with glucose transporters (GLUTs), which are overexpressed in cancer cells [34]. However, rare polysaccharides can activate the immune cells. Among the different polysaccharides, plants polysaccharides have received attention due to their diverse therapeutic properties and immunostimulatory activity [35]. Radix Astragali is a valuable medicinal and edible plant that is widely distributed in China, northern Korea, Mongolia, and other temperate regions worldwidely. Radix Astragali have health benefits and high safety, which can be classified as medicinal or food plants and used as general food ingredients. Astragalus polysaccharides (APS) is a key bioactive component of this leguminous plant [36]. In China, APS injections are used to boost immune responses in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy. Studies show that APS induces ICD in cancer cells and downregulates programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on breast cancer cells [37–39]. Additionally, APS promotes dendritic cell (DC) maturation by increasing interleukin-12 (IL-12) secretion and upregulating CD40, CD80, and HLA-DR expression [38, 40]which might just compensate for the deficiency of the ICD effect caused by PTX. These attractive advances suggest that the combination of PTX and APS can modulate the tumor microenvironment. In addition, as a natural polysaccharide, it also possesses many favorable properties, including good biodegradability, excellent biocompatibility, non-toxicity, and low production cost [41]. All these properties indicate its potential applications in drug delivery. However, to date, no studies have explored it. Here, APSs were used as candidates for the construction of multifunctional platforms and tested for their efficiency in self-assembly with hydrophobic drugs, tumor targeting, self-assistance and immune activation.

Based on this inference that the multifunctional nano-platform based on astragalus polysaccharide promotes chemo-immunotherapy. We innovatively used APS as a novel nano-platform and synthesized APS-PTX NPs by a one-step sonication method. As shown in Fig. 1, the hydrophobic anti-cancer drug PTX was encapsulated in APS through the interaction between the hydrophobic functional groups of PTX and APS without any chemical modification, significantly improving the solubility of PTX. Subsequently, the APS-loaded PTX nanoparticles (APS-PTX NPs) could rapidly attach to 4T1 cells through GLUT-mediated targeting, thereby enhancing the chemotherapeutic effect. At the tumor site, APS could reduce the expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells, enhance the PTX-induced ICD effect, and trigger an anti-tumor immune response. After the synergistic treatment of PTX and APS, the “cold tumor” with low immune infiltration was transformed into a highly reactive and infiltrative “hot tumor”. In the TNBC model, it synergistically activates a potent systemic anti-tumor immunity, effectively inhibiting tumor growth and lung metastasis, and alleviating the reduction in white blood cell count induced by PTX. The key innovation of this study was to explore the feasibility of APS as a novel nano-platform and its natural immune-activating ability, so as to improve the efficacy of chemo-immunotherapy synergy.

Materials and methods

Materials

APS were obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). PTX, pyrene and coumarin-6 (C6) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PTX-Lipo were purchased from Nanjing Luye Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.

CCK-8 assay kit, Annexin V-FITC apoptosis assay kit, and Calcein-AM/PI assay kit were provided by Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Antibodies used for flow cytometry (FCM) immunocytological cell sorting were obtained from BD Biosciences Co., Ltd. (USA).

Mouse breast cancer cell Line 4T1 and bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were preserved in RPMI 1640 or DMEM medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and cultured in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37mineEsco Lifesciences Group, Singapore).

Animals

BALB/c female mice weighing 16–18 g and Sprague Dawley rats weighing 200–220 g were purchased from Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. PZSHUTCM2312010007). All procedures involving animals were conducted in compliance with the guidelines for the ethical treatment and use of laboratory animals.

Preparation of APS-PTX NPs

APS-PTX NPs were prepared by a simple one-step ultrasonic method. First, 10 mL of APS solution (10 mg/mL) was added to 0.5 mL of a PTX ethanol solution (20 mg/mL), and the suspension was stirred at 1000 rpm for 2 min. Subsequently, the suspension was ultrasonicated using a SCIENTZ-IID ultrasonic processor (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) for 60 min (3 s on/2 s off; 100 W). After removing ethanol by rotary evaporation, the sample was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was APS-PTX NPs. The fluorescent nanoparticles used subsequently were prepared as same way, just replacing PTX with C6 and DID. All the nanoparticles were stored at 4 °C for further use.

Characterization of APS-PTX NPs

The physicochemical properties of APS-PTX NPs, including particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential, were analyzed using a Malvern Zetasizer ZEN3600 Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Morphological features were examined via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (SEMC, USA). UV-visible absorption spectra were acquired over a wavelength range of 200–500 nm. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 on a Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (Thermo, USA) using the KBr particle method. Structural properties were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD). Formulation stability was evaluated by monitoring changes in particle size, zeta potential, and the presence of precipitates. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of PTX were quantified using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) coupled with the minicolumn centrifugation technique.

The CMC of APS

The fluorescence emission spectra of pyrene were acquired using a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The excitation wavelength was set to 334 nm, with emission and excitation slit widths of 2.5 nm and 5.0 nm, respectively, and a scanning speed of 240 nm/min. Spectra were recorded for pyrene in APS solutions at varying concentrations. The I1/I3 intensity ratio (I(373 nm)/I(384 nm)) was plotted against the logarithmic concentration of APS to determine the critical micelle concentration (CMC).

Cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate the impact of diverse formulations on cell viability, 4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1*106 cells/mL. The experimental groups included APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, and APS-PTX NPs. After incubating with varying concentrations of these formulations for 48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. Subsequently, after 1 h of Further incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured. The cell viability was calculated, and the IC50 values were determined using GraphPad Prism 10.

Cell apoptosis

Cell culture and apoptosis analysis

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL and treated with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, and APS-PTX NPs for 24 h. The PTX concentration was maintained at 200 ng/mL, while APS concentrations matched those in APS-PTX NPs. After treatment, cells were washed, trypsinized, and centrifuged to collect the cell. The cell was resuspended in 0.5 mL of buffer and stained with 2 µL of Annexin V-fluorescein and propidium iodide in the dark. Apoptosis rates were quantified using FCM.

In vitro inhibition of migration and invasion

To assess the anti-metastatic potential of APS-PTX NPs, a wound healing assay was performed to evaluate cell migration. 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 90% confluency. A straight scratch was made using a sterile pipette tip, and dislodged cells were removed by washing with PBS. The cells were then treated with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, or APS-PTX NPs (PTX concentration: 200 ng/mL; APS concentration equivalent to APS-PTX NPs). Scratch closure was monitored and imaged at 0, 6, and 12 h using a microscope.

Cell invasion was evaluated using a transwell system. 4T1 cells were treated with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, or APS-PTX NPs (PTX concentration: 200 ng/mL; APS concentration equivalent to APS-PTX NPs) for 24 h, followed by seeding into the upper chamber of the transwell. After 24 h of incubation, cells that migrated to the lower chamber were fixed with cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. The stained cells were washed with PBS and imaged under a microscope. Migration and invasion rates were quantified using Image J software.

In vitro cellular uptake and verification of the GLUT-targeting mechanism

In vitro cellular uptake study

The cellular uptake of blank nanoparticles was assessed using 4T1 and HC11 cells. Fluorescent nanoparticles (APS-C6 NPs) were prepared similarly to APS-PTX NPs, with C6 replacing PTX. Cells were seeded in culture dishes and incubated with APS-C6 NPs (C6 concentration: 50 ng/mL) in RPMI 1640 medium for 4, 8, and 12 h. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI. Fluorescence distribution within cells was visualized using CLSM. For quantitative analysis, cells were seeded in 6-well plates, treated with APS-C6 NPs for 4, 8, and 12 h, trypsinized, centrifuged, and analyzed by flow cytometry (FCM, Beckman Coulter).

GLUT-targeting mechanism validation

To verify GLUT-mediated targeting, 4T1 cells were pretreated with BAY-876 (2.5 ng/mL) for 4 h to inhibit GLUT activity. Cells were then incubated with APS-C6 NPs (C6 concentration: 50 ng/mL) for 8 h. Fluorescence distribution was observed using CLSM. For quantitative assessment, cells were seeded in 6-well plates, treated as described, trypsinized, centrifuged, and analyzed by FCM.

Hemolysis assay

Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from Sprague Dawley (SD) rats. Aliquots of 1 mL each of distilled water, saline, and APS-PTX NPs were added to the RBC suspensions. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 3500 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatant. Hemolysis was quantified by measuring the UV absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm using a UV-visible spectrophotometer, and the hemolysis rates were calculated.

Immunogenic cell death inducing ability

ICD in tumor cells was assessed by evaluating intracellular high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1), cell surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT), and extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) secretion. CRT expression was analyzed via immunofluorescence. Briefly, 4T1 cells (1*106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 80% confluency. Cells were treated with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, or APS-PTX NPs (PTX concentration: 200 ng/mL; APS concentration equivalent to APS-PTX NPs) for 24 h. After PBS washing, cells were incubated with an anti-calreticulin fluorescent antibody for 30 min and analyzed by FCM.

Extracellular ATP and HMGB1 levels were quantified using assay kits. 4T1 cells (1*106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, or APS-PTX NPs (PTX concentration: 200 ng/mL; APS concentration equivalent to APS-PTX NPs) for 24 h. Supernatants were collected, and ATP and HMGB1 concentrations were measured following the protocols.

In vitro influence of APS-PTX NPs on PD-L1 expression

4T1 cells were seeded in culture dishes at a density of 1*106 cells/well and treated with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, or APS-PTX NPs (PTX concentration: 10 µg/mL; APS concentration equivalent to APS-PTX NPs) in RPMI 1640 medium for 12 h. Cells were then washed with PBS, stained with an anti-PD-L1 fluorescent antibody and Hoechst 33342, and visualized using CLSM. For quantitative analysis, cells were seeded in 6-well plates, treated as described, and incubated with an anti-PD-L1 fluorescent antibody for 30 min after PBS washing. PD-L1 expression was quantified using FCM.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study

Pharmacokinetic study

Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water and randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group). The free PTX, APS-PTX NPs were administered via tail vein injection (PTX dose: 3 mg/kg). Blood samples (0.25 mL) were collected at 3, 5, 10, 15, and 30 min, as well as 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-injection into heparinized tubes. Plasma was isolated by centrifuging the blood at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The plasma samples were processed according to the methods of previous authors [42]. The final supernatant was analyzed using UPLC-tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) (Table S1 and Figure S3, Supporting Information). Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using DAS 2.0 software.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of APS-PTX NPs

APS-PTX NPs were prepared by a simple one-step sonication method. After process optimization (The optimization process is shown in Table S2-S4), the average particle size of APS-PTX NPs was 191.7 ± 4.6 nm (Fig. 2A), with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.15 ± 0.03 and a zeta potential of −18.34 ± 0.43 mV (Fig. 2B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images showed that APS itself could form white spherical self-assembled nanoparticles in aqueous solution, accompanied by the Tyndall effect (Fig. 2C1); free PTX had a black prismatic crystal structure and precipitated in aqueous solution (Fig. 2C2), while APS-PTX NPs consisted of black spherical nanoparticles as the core and white material as the shell, indicating that APS-PTX NPs is formed by APS encapsulation of PTX, and exhibited a more obvious Tyndall effect than the APS aqueous solution (Fig. 2C3). CMC is one of the important parameters to measure the ability of amphiphilic polymers to form nanoparticles [43] and therefore it is necessary to determine the CMC value of APS. In this study, as shown in Fig. 2F two straight lines were obtained (Y1 = 0.56401-0.53398x; Y2 = 0.61841 + 0.01894x), and the intersection point was calculated through Y1 and Y2 to get the CMC. The CMC of APS was 0.79 mg/mL, which meant APS had micelle-like properties. This indicates that after simple co-sonication, APS can encapsulate the hydrophobic chemotherapeutic drug to form new nanoparticles. Interestingly, APS exhibited a solubilization function similar to that of Liposomes. The solubility of free PTX in aqueous solution was 8.78 ± 0.76 µg·mL−1, while the concentration of PTX in APS-PTX NPs was 941.96 ± 29.28 µg·mL−1, with a 107-fold increase in solubility (Figure I). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and the drug loading (DL) of PTX in APS-PTX NPs were 99.47 ± 0.18% and 9.66 ± 0.12%. UV–visible spectrophotometry of APS-PTX NPs and free APS and PTX at the same concentration was performed. As shown in Fig. 2D, free PTX exhibited maximum absorption peaks at 230 nm. In contrast, the absorption intensity of APS-PTX NPs significantly decreased at 230 nm, indicating that PTX were successfully loaded into APS-PTX NPs. The FTIR results (Fig. 2E) showed that the absorption peak of hydroxyl group stretching vibration of APS was at 3394.27 cm−1, and the vibrational absorption peak of free carboxyl group in APS galacturonic acid was at 1637.04 cm−1. The peak at 1732.02 cm−1 corresponded to the carbonyl group (C = O) stretching vibration of PTX, whereas, in the APS-PTX NPs, the The peak at 3394.27 cm−1 is shifted to 3376.35 cm−1, the absorption peak at 1637.04 cm−1 is shifted to 1645.04 cm−1 and the absorption peak at 1732.02 cm−1 is shifted to 1715.93 cm−1. These results indicate that the carbonyl band of PTX and the hydroxyl band of galacturonic acid and the methylated free carboxyl band in APS have undergone significant changes after assembly, suggesting that an assembly has occurred between APS and PTX. XRD analysis was performed to evaluate the crystalline structure of APS, PTX, and their physical mixture (Fig. 2H). PTX exhibited characteristic diffraction peaks at 5.6°, 8.8°, 10.0°, 12.2°, 12.5°, 15.8°, 18.6°, and 19.6°, while APS showed no distinct peaks. The physical mixture retained the sharp peaks of PTX, whereas these peaks disappeared in APS-PTX NPs, demonstrating the amorphous state of PTX within the NPs. This XRD pattern shift further confirmed the effective encapsulation of PTX by APS, highlighting the protective role of APS in maintaining PTX in an amorphous form.

Furthermore, APS-PTX NPs demonstrated minimal drug leakage over a 60-day period, as detailed in Table S5 (Supporting Information). To assess their stability and biocompatibility within the circulatory system, in vitro evaluations were conducted. Figure 2G illustrates that the particle size of APS-PTX NPs remained stable in PBS supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C for 24 h, suggesting their potential stability during blood circulation. Additionally, hemolysis assays were performed, with results presented in Table S6 and Figure S6. The hemolysis rate of APS-PTX NPs was consistently below 5%, and erythrocyte morphology remained unaffected, confirming negligible hemolytic toxicity at the tested concentrations. These findings underscore the favorable stability and safety profile of APS-PTX NPs for systemic applications. Finally, the in vitro release of APS-PTX NPs was performed in PBS solution containing 3% SDS (pH = 7.4). As shown in Figure S5, cumulative PTX release from APS-PTX NPs was significantly lower than that from PTX at all time points, indicating a sustained release behavior.

In vitro cell uptake

To evaluate the targeting capability of APS-PTX NPs, coumarin 6 (C6)-loaded APS nanoparticles (APS-C6 NPs) were successfully synthesized. As depicted in Fig. 3A1, the APS-C6 NPs group displayed stronger and more widespread green fluorescence signals in the cytoplasm of 4T1 cells compared to the free C6 group, indicating enhanced cellular uptake efficiency. We also conducted cell uptake tests in HC11 cells (Mammary Epithelium) (Fig. 3A2), which showed no significant difference in FITC fluorescence intensity between HC11 and 4T1 cells for free C6. However, APS-PTX NPs exhibited significantly higher uptake in 4T1 cells than in HC11 cells (Fig. 3B), a finding further confirmed by FCM analysis (Fig. 3C). In addition, tumor cells overexpress GLUT1 to Fuel their rapid proliferation and metabolism. APS is rich in glucose residues. We hypothesized that APS could selectively target 4T1 cells through GLUT1. In order to further prove that the tumor targeting of APS-PTX NPs is correlated with GLUT, we used BAY-876, a highly selective and cell-permeable GLUT1 inhibitor. At a safe concentration, when BAY-876 blocked GLUT1 in 4T1 cells, the uptake rate of APS-PTX NPs dropped from 55.28 to 47.64% (Fig. 3D, E). The result was further validated by FCM analysis (Fig. 3F). This suggests that APS-PTX NPs, having numerous surface glucose residues, are endocytosed via GLUT1. APS can actively target tumor cells through specific binding to GLUT1, achieving precise drug delivery while reducing damage to normal tissues. Hence, the findings collectively confirmed the efficacy of APS-PTX NPs in selectively target tumor cells rather than normal cells.

In vitro anti-tumor effects of APS-PTX NPs

The APS-PTX NPs promoted drug uptake by 4T1 cells, so we further studied the effect of APS-PTX NPs on PTX cytotoxicity. To compare the antitumor activity of APS-PTX NPs with free PTX and the marketed preparation PTX-Lipo, we incubated 4T1 cells with APS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, and APS-PTX NPs. The cytotoxicity of APS was weak, while the cytotoxicity of APS-PTX NPs was the strongest, significantly higher than that of free PTX and PTX-Lipo. The IC50 of free PTX, PTX-Lipo, and APS-PTX NPs were 116.27 ± 8.08 ng/mL, 68.64 ± 2.15 ng/mL, and 42.14 ± 1.82 ng/mL, respectively (Fig. 4A and B). According to the cell uptake results, free PTX is taken up by cells through passive diffusion; PTX-Lipo enters cells through endocytosis and exhibits stronger cytotoxicity, while APS-PTX NPs are taken up by cells through GLUT1-mediated endocytosis and thus exhibit the strongest cytotoxicity. In addition, we also analyzed the apoptosis of cells under different treatments. The result showed that APS did not take obvious effect, and the total apoptosis rates of APS, PTX, PTX-Lipo and APS-PTX NPs were 0.10 ± 0.04, 63.51 ± 2.15%, 72.17 ± 1.20%, and 75.41 ± 3.75%, respectively (Fig. 4C and Figure S7). These results were consistent with the trends observed in the cytotoxicity experiments.

PTX, a widely used chemotherapeutic agent, is known to trigger ICD in tumor cells [44]. To assess the ability of APS-PTX NPs to induce ICD, the externalization of CRT in 4T1 cells induced by APS-PTX NPs was first investigated using flow cytometry. In comparison to the control group, PTX, PTX-Lipo, and APS-PTX NPs were all capable of significantly inducing CRT expression. Notably, the induction effect of APS-PTX NPs was markedly more potent than that of PTX-Lipo. This could potentially be ascribed to the augmented endocytosis efficiency and elevated cytotoxicity, as depicted in Fig. 5D and H. High mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) were quantified with the utilization of relevant kits. The release of HMGB1 and the secretion of ATP in the APS - PTX NPs group were found to be higher than those in the other groups, as shown in Fig. 5I and J. These results indicate that APS-PTX NPs can induce ICD through the synergistic effect of APS and PTX and the enhanced endocytosis effect targeting GLUT.

DCs are crucial for triggering antigen - specific anti - tumor immune responses. They identify damage - associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that are discharged during ICD. Subsequently, DCs present the antigens to T cells, thereby playing an essential part in kick - starting the anti - tumor immune process. Given that APS-PTX NPs induce CRT exposure and the secretion of ATP and HMGB1 in tumor cells, their potential to stimulate DC maturation was Further investigated. 4T1 cells were treated with PBS, free PTX, PTX-Lipo, APS, and APS-PTX NPs, and their supernatants were used to culture bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that all PTX-based treatments significantly enhanced DC maturation compared to the control, with APS-PTX NPs exhibiting the most potent effect (Fig. 5C, G). Notably, APS could also induce DC maturation, which may be related to the release of DAMPs induced by APS. These results are consistent with the ICD detection.

However, high expression of PD-L1 in breast cancer tumor cells is often associated with poor prognosis in patients [45, 46]. PTX can further induce an upregulation of PD-L1 expression [47]. APS may ingeniously circumvent the drawback of PTX-induced PD-L1 upregulation. To demonstrate whether APS-PTX NPs can reduce the PTX-induced upregulation of PD-L1 expression, 4T1 cells were cultured with PBS, PTX, PTX-Lipo, APS, and APS-PTX NPs. Then, an anti-PD-L1 fluorescent antibody was added, and the samples were detected by FCM. Compared with the control group, PTX and PTX-Lipo could induce an upregulation of PD-L1 expression. APS could significantly reduce the PD-L1 expression on the surface of 4T1 cells. Compared with free PTX and PTX-Lipo, APS-PTX NPs could significantly decrease the PD-L1 expression (Fig. 5A, B, E, F). According to previous reports, APS inhibit PD-L1 expression in 4T1 cells via the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [48]. Therefore, we investigated the role of the AKT/mTOR/p70S6K signaling pathway in the reduction of PD-L1 expression by APS and APS-PTX NPs through western blotting experiments. The results showed that treatment with APS and APS-PTX NPs decreased the phosphorylation levels of AKT, mTOR, and p70S6K, indicating that APS and APS-PTX NPs treatment downregulated the AKT/mTOR/p70S6K pathway in 4T1 cells (Figure S8). These findings suggest that the reduction of PD-L1 by APS and APS-PTX NPs in tumor cells is associated with the downregulation of the AKT/mTOR/p70S6K pathway. The above results indicate that APS, as a novel drug carrier, can significantly reduce the PTX-induced high expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells, which is not achievable with common liposomes.

In vivo pharmacokinetics and tumor targeting evaluation of APS-PTX NPs

The rapid clearance in systemic circulation and lack of tumor specificity often necessitate high drug doses, leading to adverse effects. The in vivo targeting ability and pharmacokinetic properties of APS-PTX NPs remain to be further investigated. The influence of APS coating on the pharmacokinetics of PTX in nanoparticles was determined by monitoring the plasma drug concentration in the body and the results were shown in Fig. 6A, B and Tables S7 (Supporting Information). Compared with free drugs, pharmacokinetic parameters of PTX in APS-PTX NPs, such as average blood concentration, peak concentration Cmax, half-life t1/2, mean retention time (MRT 0−∞), and area under the curve (AUC0−∞) were significantly improved or extended, highlighting the advantages of APS-PTX NPs. This further indicates that APS as a nanocarrier can enhance the in vivo long circulation of PTX. The tumor targeting ability and tissue biodistribution of APS-PTX NPs were Further evaluated in 4T1 tumor bearing mice. The fluorescence intensities of the tumor sites are shown in Figs. 6C and D. From 0 h to 24 h, the fluorescence intensities of each group continuously increased. Specifically, only a small amount of free DID reached the tumor site. In contrast, the fluorescence intensity of DID-Lipo at the tumor site was significantly higher than that of free DID, mainly attributed to the EPR effect. Moreover, compared with DID-Lipo, starting from 3 h, APS-DID NPs exhibited a stronger fluorescence signal at the tumor site due to the targeting ability of APS. Additionally, at 24 h, the mice were sacrificed, and relevant tissues and organs were collected. The results in Fig. 6E, F indicated that the fluorescence intensity in the major organs (tumor, liver, heart, spleen, lung, kidney and all organs) of each group was far lower than that at the tumor site. The total fluorescence intensity of APS-DID NPs was the strongest, indicating that their clearance effect in vivo was the lowest. Among all the total fluorescence intensities, the ratio of APS-DID NPs to free DID was 2.45, while the ratio in tumor tissues was 4.03, which demonstrates that APS-DID NPs possess significant tumor-targeting ability. At the tumor site, the accumulation of free DID was the lowest, followed by DID-Lipo, and APS-DID NPs showed the highest accumulation, with significant differences among the groups. Compared with the free DID group, the fluorescence intensity of DID-Lipo was 2.26-fold higher, and that of APS-DID NPs was 4.03-fold higher. This suggests that the GLUT-mediated targeting can significantly enhance the accumulation of APS-DID NPs at the tumor site. Compared with DID-Lipo, the fluorescence intensity of APS-DID NPs was 1.78-fold higher, which was mainly due to the enhanced accumulation resulting from the targeting of APS to the tumor site. The above experimental results demonstrate that APS-DID NPs can actively target the tumor site through APS, which is beneficial for the treatment of nanoparticles at the tumor site.

Disscussion

Over the past decade, breast cancer has experienced a rising incidence, accompanied by an increase in metastases and recurrences within five years [54]. TNBC represents the most invasive subtype among breast cancers. It is characterized by a high likelihood of metastasis, and its mortality rate has been rising annually [2]. Notably, TNBC lacks distinct therapeutic targets and effective treatment strategies. The unique TME of TNBC is highly complex and heterogeneous, which can be designated as a marker [55, 56]. In recent years, numerous approaches have been investigated to address the challenges of poor tumor accumulation and limited permeability of the nano-delivery system. These approaches involve modifications with functional ligands or the incorporation of environment-responsive materials. Although several elaborately designed nano-carriers have achieved remarkable results in animal studies, the larg -scale production and clinical translation of these functionalized nano-drugs remain arduous tasks. In addition, the complex preparation procedures and high costs pose significant obstacles. This alteration can impact its stability and biocompatibility, and there is also a risk of triggering immune responses within the body [57]. Significantly, to date, no actively targeted liposome product has been approved. This is mainly due to its complex formulation ingredients, multi-step preparation processes, and its inability to meet the therapeutic expectations in clinical trials [58]. In this study, a novel intelligent PTX nanoparticle, APS-PTX NPs, was prepared for the first time, and it was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo. APS-PTX NPs is a simple nano-drug formulation composed of APS and PTX. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that, compared with traditional PTX liposomes, APS-PTX NPs possess superior tumor accumulation and penetration functions. It can reduce the level of PD-L1, reverse immunosuppression, activate the immune response, and improve PTX-induced myosuppression, thereby enhancing the anti-tumor efficacy.

The CMC serves as an indicator of the thermodynamic stability during micelle formation [59]. A lower CMC value implies that micelles can be more readily formed. The CMC of APS was 0.79 mg/mL. This value is substantially lower than that of typical small - molecule surfactants. For instance, the CMC of sucrose esters is 2.5 mg/mL [60] and that of sodium dodecyl sulfate is 2.3 mg/mL [61]. This suggests that APS has a strong tendency to self-assemble into micelles in aqueous solutions, even at relatively low concentrations. The observation of nanoparticles via TEM further corroborates the formation of micelles. Some studies have shown that non-polar groups can be grafted into natural polysaccharides. This modification makes the polysaccharide have amphiphilic structure. Therefore, these modified polysaccharides can self-assemble to produce micelles [62]. APS is rich in glucose residues and can target the overexpressed GLUT1 in cancer cells without additional modification. In contrast, chitosan-based carriers typically require chemical conjugation with targeting ligands (e.g., RGD peptides, antibodies) to achieve specific tumor accumulation, which increases synthetic complexity and production costs. However, our study shows that APS itself is amphiphilic. This property, combined with its biocompatibility, positions it as a promising material for the development of advanced drug delivery systems and other biomedical applications. Currently, polyoxyethylene castor oil is commonly used as a solubilizer for PTX in clinical practice [63]. Although it can increase the solubility of PTX, polyoxyethylene castor oil is highly irritating and likely to trigger a series of adverse reactions during clinical use, such as allergic reactions, neurotoxicity, and cardiovascular toxicity [64–66]. Our research shows that APS-PTX NPs can efficiently encapsulate PTX to form stable nanosystems, increasing the solubility of PTX by hundreds of times. This solubilization strategy not only avoids the irritation of polyoxyethylene but also ensures that PTX can reach an effective therapeutic concentration in vivo.

Chemotherapy plays a vital role in anticancer treatment [67]. Early studies have shown that chemotherapy can induce bone marrow toxicity or myelosuppression [68, 69], which are the primary complications associated with chemotherapy. Chemotherapy exhibits significant haematopoietic toxicity and has the potential to disrupt the haematopoietic microenvironment (HME). Exploring the underlying mechanisms and searching for new drugs to counteract the side effects of chemotherapy is crucial for chemotherapy treatment [70]. APS is a traditional Chinese medicine that has been used for centuries to enhance e side effects of chemotherapy is crucial for chemotherhaematopoietic functions inhibited by chemotherapy has been highlighted in clinical applications. Astragalus-based herbal medicine has been used in combination with chemotherapy to treat tumors such as non-small cell lung cancer and TNBC [71, 72]. In addition, it is particularly effective in patients with toxicity grades III-IV. Grade III/IV leukocyte toxicity was significantly reduced in patients receiving combination therapy [72]. In our study, APS-PTX NPs exhibited a protective effect against PTX-induced suppression in mouse bone marrow cells, without inducing any detectable toxicity. This unique attribute of APS underscores its superiority over other drug delivery systems as a promising carrier for chemotherapeutic agents.

Finally, corresponding to the enhanced ICD induction and anti-immunosuppressive activity in vitro, among all the treatment groups, APS-PTX NPs exhibit the strongest effects in reversing immunosuppression and activating anti-tumor immunity in vivo. This result can be interpreted from two aspects. On one hand, compared with traditional liposomes, APS-PTX NPs possess a significantly higher cellular uptake rate and a stronger ability to penetrate tumors. The treatment with APS-PTX NPs remarkably increases the accumulation of PTX at the tumor site, which in turn enhances the ICD of tumor cells, promotes the maturation and antigen presentation of DC cells, and thus activates the anti-tumor immune response mediated by CD8+ T cells. On the other hand, APS-PTX NPs significantly reduce the expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells, and inhibit the recruitment and immunosuppressive capabilities of immune regulation-related cells (M2 macrophages and Treg) in the tumor tissue, leading to the reactivation of CD8+ T cells.

The main chain of APS had a 1,3-linked-β-D-galactose residue, mainly including β-glucose, 1,6-linked-α-galactose, 1,5-linked-β-xylose, 1,4-linked-β-galactose, β-D-galactose, 1,2-linked-α-rhamnose and 1,2,4-linked-α-rhamnose [73]. A large number of literatures have shown that GLUT1 is highly expressed in breast cancer [74]. The glucose GLUT1 belongs to the major facilitator superfamily and is responsible for the constant uptake of glucose [75]. Compounds with glucose side chains interact with GLUT1 via hydrogen bonds between their glycosyl chains and the amino acid residues of GLUT1 [76]. However, mannose, arabinose, xylose, glucuronic acid and rhamnose have low affinity for GLUT or are not transported through GLUT [75]. In addition, we used BAY-876, a highly selective and cell-permeable GLUT1 inhibitor. At a safe concentration, when BAY-876 blocked GLUT1 in 4T1 cells, the uptake rate of APS-PTX NPs dropped from 55.28 to 47.64%. Therefore, the glucose residues of APS are mainly involved in the active targeting of GLUT1 in tumor cells.

APS is a heteropolysaccharide with a very complex molecular structure. Studies have isolated APS-I and APS-II fractions from APS, with the backbone mainly composed of major α-(1→3) glucose and a few 1→4, 1→6 glucose, and the side chain composed of arabinose and xylose [77]. This indicates that there are natural hydrophobic microdomains in the primary structure of APS: the main chain of APS is dominated by α-(1→3) glucose and a few 1→4, 1→6 glucose, which are rich in hydroxyl groups. These polar groups strongly interact with water molecules through hydrogen bonds, forming a hydrophilic framework. The side chains contain neutral sugar residues, whose furanose/pyranose structures, due to fewer hydroxyl groups and large steric hindrance, have weak interactions with water molecules, forming relatively hydrophobic regions. This might be the reason for the amphiphilicity of APS. In our study, the critical micelle concentration (CMC) of APS was 0.79 mg/mL, indicating a strong tendency for self-assembly. However, the current study has not yet clarified the specific distribution of hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions within the APS molecule, nor how these regions drive the self-assembly process through intermolecular forces (such as hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, etc.). It has been reported that the higher the degree of branching in hyperbranched polysaccharides, the more easily aggregates are formed [78]. So the self-assembly behavior of APS correlate with its degree of branching is deserved to be furtherly invested in future studies.

Conclusion

In this study, an intelligent PTX nanoparticle based on a multifunctional APS carrier (APS-PTX NPs) was developed and evaluated. Compared with the traditional PTX-Lipo, APS-PTX NPs possess a variety of excellent properties. These properties include a significant increase in cellular uptake and cytotoxicity in vitro, as well as enhanced accumulation and penetration within the tumor by targeting the tumor region through GLUT, thus remarkably improving the antitumor efficacy. Furthermore, APS-PTX NPs can not only reduce the PD-L1 expression in tumor cells but also synergize with PTX to induce the release of DAMPs. These DAMPs can further act as promoters to initiate DC maturation, macrophage polarization, and T-cell response, leading to the conversion of “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. Importantly, APS-PTX NPs can ameliorate the bone marrow suppression induced by PTX and can be easily prepared via a one-step sonication method without further modification. The current research findings are expected to provide a new nanomedicine to enhance the antitumor efficacy, and at the same time, they also demonstrate the potential of APS-PTX NPs as a more effective immune adjuvant.
