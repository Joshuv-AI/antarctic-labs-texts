Determinants of service utilization and satisfaction with sexual and reproductive health services among displaced and migrant populations in Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Moréniké Oluwátóyìn Foláyan, Roberto Ariel Abeldaño Zuñiga, Veronica Akwenabuaye Undelikwo, Babatunde Adelekan, Oluwaranmilowo Amusan, Mohammed Abdullahi, Olujide O. Arije, Idayat Uthman, Fadip Kpahnim Kwamkur, Fatimah Jajere, Gideon Bokkos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding the determinants of access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services in humanitarian settings, and their links to client satisfaction, is essential for effective programming. The primary objective of this study was to describe the levels of utilization of services providing modern contraceptives, STI services, cancer screening, and GBV management among women and men facing humanitarian crises. The secondary objective was to determine the associations between clients’ perceived ease of access and their satisfaction with the services received.MethodsThis was a secondary analysis of the data of a cross-sectional study conducted in five Nigerian states (Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, Benue) in December 2024. The study participants were refugees, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and migrants. Data were collected via interviews using a structured questionnaire. Utilization of five SRH services (modern contraceptive counseling, STI screening/treatment, cervical and prostate cancer screening, and GBV management) was assessed. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with service utilization, and cross-tabulations examined the link between perceived ease of access and satisfaction.ResultsThe study included 1,194 sexually active adults (842 women, 352 men). Service utilization was highly gendered. Compared to refugee women, IDP women had significantly lower odds of utilizing contraceptive (AOR: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.34–0.72) and STI services (AOR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.44-1.00), while migrant women had higher odds of utilizing cervical cancer screening (AOR: 3.04; 95% CI: 1.70–5.44) and GBV management (AOR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.09–4.09). For men, migrants had higher odds of utilizing STI services (AOR: 2.75; 95% CI: 1.37–5.54) and prostate cancer screening (AOR: 11.63; 95% CI: 3.47–38.98) compared to refugees. A strong, consistent association was found between ease of access and satisfaction. Over 93% of clients reporting ‘easy’ access were satisfied, while satisfaction plummeted to 20.8–33.3% among those reporting ‘uneasy’ access.ConclusionUtilization of SRH services is shaped by gender, displacement status, and service type. The link between perceived ease of access and client satisfaction underscores that the process of obtaining care is as critical as the clinical service itself. SRH programs must adopt tailored, client-centered approaches that address unique sub-population barriers and systematically reduce logistical friction to improve both service utilization and quality of care.

Introduction

In humanitarian settings, the confluence of displacement, overcrowding, poverty, and health system collapse acutely amplifies sexual and reproductive health (SRH) needs [1]. Women and girls face disproportionately elevated risks of unintended pregnancy, maternal mortality, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and gender-based violence (GBV) [2]. Yet access to essential SRH services remains a persistent challenge, frequently deprioritized during initial emergency responses [3]. Elucidating the determinants of both service access and client satisfaction is therefore fundamental to designing effective, equitable programs in these complex environments.

Access extends beyond mere physical availability. The World Health Organization conceptualizes access as encompassing availability, affordability, acceptability, and geographic accessibility [4]. In crisis contexts, barriers are multifaceted, spanning supply-side deficiencies—such as inadequate supplies and trained personnel—to demand-side constraints including social norms, stigma, and direct costs [5]. Critically, these barriers are not uniformly experienced; factors including legal status, gender, marital status, and age differentially shape the healthcare-seeking trajectories of refugees, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and migrants [6].

Previous research has identified multiple factors influencing SRH service uptake among displaced populations. Predisposing factors associated with higher utilization include female gender [6], higher educational attainment [7], and married or partnered status [8]. Enabling resources such as geographic proximity [9], possession of legal documentation [10], and peer networks or community health worker engagement [11] facilitate access, whereas cost [12], lack of culturally competent providers [13], and stigma, particularly surrounding contraception and STI treatment, pose substantial barriers [14]. Need factors, including prior GBV exposure [15], perceived STI risk [16], and pregnancy-related concerns [17], also drive service-seeking behavior. Within African humanitarian contexts specifically, displacement disrupts traditional support systems and introduces access barriers related to security, language, and fragmented service delivery across camp and host community settings [6, 18]. However, existing studies have largely examined these factors in isolation or focused predominantly on women, leaving gaps in understanding how determinants operate differentially across sub-populations and by gender, particularly within Nigeria’s humanitarian landscape.

Importantly, service availability alone does not guarantee utilization or optimal health outcomes. Client satisfaction serves as a key indicator of healthcare quality and a critical determinant of service uptake and adherence [19]. When services are perceived as difficult to access, unwelcoming, or of poor quality, utilization declines even when services are physically present [20, 21]. Thus, understanding the relationship between perceived accessibility and satisfaction is essential for ensuring that services are not only provided but also valued and effectively used by affected communities.

To comprehensively analyze the factors shaping SRH service access and satisfaction, we employ Andersen’s Behavioral Model of Health Services Use as our theoretical framework [22]. This model posits that health service utilization is a function of predisposing characteristics (demographic and social factors), enabling resources (logistical means to obtain care), and need (perceived or evaluated health conditions). In this study, predisposing characteristics encompass participant status (refugee, IDP, migrant), marital status, and educational level. Enabling resources are operationalized through clients’ perceived ease of access, a subjective measure of service accessibility aligning with the model’s emphasis on logistical feasibility. Outcomes are examined at two levels: utilization (actual receipt of SRH services) and post-utilization satisfaction, with the latter conceptualized as an outcome influenced by enabling factors. This expanded framework permits robust examination of the entire care pathway [23], and we hypothesize that enabling resources, particularly perceived ease of access, will strongly predict both service utilization and subsequent satisfaction.

Despite growing recognition of SRH as a priority in crises, research simultaneously examining access predictors and satisfaction determinants, particularly through a gender-disaggregated lens, remains scarce. This study addresses that gap by investigating sociodemographic factors associated with access to key SRH services and analyzing the relationship between perceived ease of access and client satisfaction among women and men in Nigerian humanitarian settings. The primary objective was to describe the utilization of modern contraceptives, STI services, cancer screening, and GBV management among IDPs, refugees, and migrants by gender. The secondary objective was to determine associations between clients’ perceived ease of access and their satisfaction with services received.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional dataset collected in December 2024. The primary study was conducted across five states in Nigeria (Borno, Taraba, Yobe, Cross River, and Benue), selected for their high concentrations of refugees, IDPs, and migrants. These states represent diverse humanitarian contexts in Nigeria, driven by conflict, violence, and environmental disasters. Details of the primary study methodology had been reported comprehensively by Undelikwo et al. [24, 25].

Study participants

The analysis for this sub-study focused on a subset of sexually active adults aged 18 years and above, who were recruited during the primary survey. Participants were included if they self-identified as either an IDP, refugee, or migrant, based on internationally recognized definitions. IDPs are persons forced to flee their homes but who remain within their country’s borders. Refugees are persons who have crossed an international border due to a well-founded fear of persecution. Migrants are people who relocate, primarily voluntarily, in search of employment or improved living conditions [26]. Individuals with severe health issues impairing their ability to participate were excluded from the primary study.

Community leaders confirmed participants’ status. This confirmation process was implemented to triangulate self-reported status, as displacement documentation (refugee identity cards or IDP registration papers) is not universally available across all study settings in Nigeria. Community leaders, including camp coordinators, settlement heads, religious leaders, and recognized elders, were consulted based on their long-standing knowledge of community members’ histories and circumstances. To ensure consistency across the five study states, pre-data collection stakeholder meetings were held in each state with representatives from State Emergency Management Agencies, the National Commission for Refugees, Migrants, and IDPs, camp management officials, and local community-based organizations. These meetings established consensus on local terminology and classification criteria, enabling standardized application of definitions across culturally and linguistically diverse settings [24].

Sampling and recruitment

To access these often-hidden populations, the primary study employed a mixed non-probabilistic sampling approach combining venue-based and controlled snowball sampling. First, venue-based sampling was conducted at designated camps and settlements identified through state-level stakeholder meetings to ensure initial contact with a diverse range of individuals across the five states. Second, to expand the sample size beyond formal settlements, a controlled snowball sampling method was used. To mitigate bias from homogeneous social networks, several measures were implemented: (1) a diverse group of initial participants (seeds) was recruited during the venue-based phase, representing varied displacement statuses, locations, ages, and genders; (2) enrolled participants received a limited number of coded coupons to invite peers from outside their immediate social circle, limiting referral chains; and (3) data collectors, supported by state-assigned monitors and the study statistician, tracked the geographic origin and referral patterns of participants to identify and correct for potential oversampling from any single network or location [24].

Data collection and variables

Data were collected through in-person interviews or tablet/phone-assisted self-interviews, using a structured questionnaire uploaded to a secure, encrypted online platform. The survey was administered in English, Yoruba, and Hausa, with real-time translation for other dialects by a professional translator who conducted forward and backward translations from English to the dialect and back to English [24]. For this analysis, the key variables extracted from the data are below. These variables were selected and categorized in accordance with Andersen’s Behavioral Model [22].

Dependent variables

Utilization of five key SRH services in the humanitarian setting, defined as binary (Yes/No) outcomes: (1) Modern contraceptive counseling assessed as receiving advice or information on modern contraceptive methods. (2) STI screening and treatment assessed as being screened and/or treated for sexually transmitted infections. (3) cervical cancer screening assessed as being screened for cervical cancer in women. (4) Prostate cancer screening assessed as being screened for prostate cancer in men. (5) Gender Based Violence (GBV) management services assessed as utilizing services for gender-based violence, including counseling, medical care, or psychosocial support.

Independent variables

The primary independent variable was respondent type (IDP, Refugee, or Migrant) and sex (male, female).

Confounders

These were the socio-demographic characteristics. Age (treated as a continuous variable), marital status (Single, Married, Cohabiting, Separated, Divorced, Widow/Widower), and educational level (No formal education/Pre-school, Primary, Quranic only, Secondary, Tertiary).

Ease of access and satisfaction with services

Following the administration of the core survey on SRH service access, a subset of participants who reported using a specific service were asked two additional questions to gauge their experience. First, they were asked to rate the ease of access to that service, typically on a Likert scale (Easy, Neutral, Uneasy). Subsequently, they were asked to rate their overall satisfaction with the service received, categorized as Satisfied, Neutral, or Dissatisfied. A three-point Likert-like scale was used as the study sample included individuals with varying levels of formal education, from none to tertiary. A three-point scale is more accessible for respondents with lower literacy levels, as it is easier to explain visually and verbally during interviews, thereby making the data collection process more inclusive.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata version 18.0. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations (SD) for age. Bivariate analyses using Chi-squared tests (for categorical variables) and t-tests (for age) were conducted to examine the unadjusted associations between socio-demographic characteristics and utilization of each SRH service.

Multivariable logistic regression models were built to identify factors independently associated with utilization of each of the five SRH services. Separate models were run for women and men. Each model included the independent variables and was adjusted for the confounders. The results are presented as Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with their 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 31 and RStudio 2026.01.1.

For the secondary study objective, descriptive statistics were computed for each SRH service, a cross-tabulation was created to display the frequency and percentage of clients in each satisfaction category, stratified by their reported level of ease of access, and a graphic representation of the findings was drawn, allowing for a direct comparison of how satisfaction levels shift as perceived accessibility changes.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval

for the primary study was obtained from the Institute of Public Health, Obafemi Awolowo University (IPHOAU/12/2408), and the respective State Ministries of Health in all five states. The study adhered strictly to ethical guidelines for research in humanitarian settings. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality was maintained by collecting data anonymously without personal identifiers, using unique codes instead. Data security was prioritized through SSL-encrypted data transmission and storage.

Results

As shown in Table 1, the study included 1,194 individuals (842 women and 352 men). The female cohort was comprised of 441 (52.4%) refugees, with an age range of 18–68 years and a median age of 30 years (IQR = 14). In addition, 385 (45.7%) women were married, and 339 (40.3%) had a secondary level of education. The male cohort included 199 (56.5%) refugees, was older than the female cohort (age range of 18–94 years and a median age of 34 years (IQR = 17), and 161 (45.7%) had a secondary education.

Service utilization among women was highest for modern contraceptive counseling (462, 54.9%), followed by STI services (225, 26.7%), cervical cancer screening (78, 9.3%), and GBV management (71, 8.4%). Significant disparities were observed by respondent type: refugee women had the highest contraceptive uptake (272, 61.7%; p < 0.001), while migrant women had the highest levels of cervical cancer screening (30, 19.4%; p < 0.001) and GBV service utilization (21, 13.5%; p = 0.003). Marital status was also a significant factor; cohabiting women had the highest contraceptive uptake (27, 79.4%; p = 0.027), divorced women had the highest STI service utilization (14, 43.8%; p < 0.001), and widows utilized GBV services the most (17, 16.5%; p = 0.022). Age was significantly associated with service use; women utilizing contraceptives were younger (median 29 (IQR = 12) vs. 31 (IQR = 16) years; p < 0.001), while those utilizing cervical screening were older (median 35 (IQR = 12) vs. 30 (IQR = 13) years; p < 0.001).

Service utilization among men was lower than among women: 99 (28.1%) utilized modern contraceptives, 65 (18.5%) utilized STI screening, 25 (7.1%) utilized prostate screening, and 22 (6.3%) utilized GBV management. Migrant men had the highest utilization of STI services (20, 32.8%; p = 0.006) and prostate cancer screening (12, 19.7%; p < 0.001). Marital status was significantly associated with contraceptive utilization (p = 0.011), with widowers having the highest utilization (6, 75.0%). Unlike the findings for women, age was not a significant factor for men in utilizing modern contraceptives, STI screening, prostate screening, or gender-based violence (p > 0.05). A significant association was found between education and GBV service utilization (p = 0.043), though the small cell sizes in some categories necessitate cautious interpretation.

The analysis presented in Table 2 identifies several key predictors of SRH service utilization, with distinct patterns for women and men. For women, respondent type was a significant predictor for the use of three of the four services. Compared to refugee women, internally displaced persons (IDPs) had 50% lower odds of receiving counselling on modern contraceptives (AOR: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.34–0.72; p < 0.001), while migrant women had 36% lower odds (AOR: 0.64; 95% CI: 0.43–0.94; p = 0.025). IDPs also had significantly lower odds of utilization of STI services (AOR: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.44–1.00.44.00; p = 0.049). Conversely, migrant women had three times the odds of being screened for cervical cancer compared to refugees (AOR: 3.04; 95% CI: 1.70–5.44; p < 0.001). Furthermore, migrant women had significantly higher odds of utilizing GBV management services (AOR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.09–4.09; p = 0.026).

For men, the patterns differed. Migrant men had 2.75 times higher odds of utilizing STI services than refugee men (AOR: 2.75; 95% CI: 1.37–5.54; p = 0.005). For prostate cancer screening, both IDP (AOR: 4.78; 95% CI: 1.32–17.26; p = 0.017) and migrant (AOR: 11.63; 95% CI: 3.47–38.98; p < 0.001) men had significantly higher odds compared to refugees. Respondent type was not a significant factor for men’s utilization of contraceptive or GBV services.

Figure 1 (and Supplemental File 1) reports on the analysis of clients’ satisfaction levels for SRH services, segmented by the clients’ perceived ease of access to those services. The data demonstrates that perceived ease of access is a determinant of client satisfaction for both female and male clients.

Among women, those who reported easy access to services were satisfied with the care they received. This was most pronounced for cervical cancer screening, where 100% of women who found access easy were satisfied. Similarly, high satisfaction rates were reported for easy access to modern contraceptive counselling (95.4%), STI services (97.0%), and GBV management (93.7%).

In contrast, women who reported neutral or uneasy access had lower satisfaction. For instance, among those who found access to modern contraceptives uneasy, only 20.8% were satisfied, while the majority (62.5%) were dissatisfied. A similar pattern was seen for STI services, where 60.0% of those with uneasy access were dissatisfied. Neutral perceptions of access were mainly associated with neutral satisfaction ratings.

Among men, the pattern was identical. Satisfaction was near-universal among men who reported easy access, ranging from 94.2% for modern contraceptives to 100% for GBV management. As with women, reports of neutral or uneasy access were linked to a decline in satisfaction. For example, all two men who found access to modern contraceptives uneasy were dissatisfied, and only one of three men (33.3%) with uneasy access to STI services or prostate cancer screening was satisfied.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study of individuals in a humanitarian setting in Nigeria reveals two critical, interconnected narratives. First, it demonstrates that the utilization of SRH services is shaped by gender, displacement status, and the specific service. For instance, while being a refugee was a positive predictor for women’s utilization of contraceptive and STI services with IDP and migrant women having significantly lower odds of utilization these services compared to refugees, it was a negative predictor for men’s utilization, as both IDP and migrant men had significantly higher odds of utilizing STI services and prostate cancer screening compared to refugee men. Second, the data provide evidence that the perceived ease of access is a determinant of client satisfaction. Across all service types and for both genders, clients who reported easy access to services showed very high satisfaction rates, whereas those reporting uneasy access exhibited a sharp increase in dissatisfaction and neutral feelings.

A key strength of this study is its relatively large and diverse sample, allowing for a granular analysis of service utilization determinants across sub-groups. The use of multivariable analysis to calculate adjusted odds ratios further strengthens the findings by controlling for potential confounders. However, the findings must be interpreted considering several limitations. The cross-sectional design captures a snapshot in time and cannot establish causality between the identified factors and service utilization or satisfaction. The reliance on self-reported data may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Furthermore, the non-probabilistic sampling method limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the distinctions between the categories of migrant, refugee, and IDP can be blurred in a complex and fluid humanitarian context like Nigeria. While we used community leader verification to minimize this, the potential for some conceptual overlap or cross-contamination between these groups remains and should be considered when interpreting the results. Furthermore, although the study’s sample size was adequate for its primary descriptive aims, exceeding the minimum requirement of 385 (with a 95% confidence level, a margin of error of 5%, and a conservative prevalence estimate of 50% to ensure robustness across variously accessed services), statistical power was limited for specific analyses. The number of users of less-utilized services, such as for male prostate cancer screening, was too low for reliable multivariable modeling. Similarly, analyses of user experience were constrained by very small subgroups, limiting the interpretability of findings for these specific scenarios. In addition, the male cohort of 352 participants was slightly below the calculated minimum but remained substantial, yielding a margin of error of approximately ± 5.1%, which is acceptable [27]. Despite these limitations, the study provides information that can help improve the design of SRH responses for those facing humanitarian crises in Nigeria.

First, our findings underscore that the determinants of SRH service utilization are highly gendered and service-specific, a complexity often masked in aggregate data. The observed disparity, where being a refugee was a positive predictor for women’s utilization of contraceptives and STI care but a negative predictor for men’s utilization, can be understood through the model’s predisposing characteristics. This is consistent with a global pattern of SRH programs being historically structured around maternal and child health, inadvertently sidelining men [3]. The feminization of SRH services in Nigeria creates a structural and normative barrier for men that discourages service use [28]. Men are often promoted as supporters of the female use of contraceptive services. This barrier is rooted in gender roles that define reproductive health as a predominantly female domain. This rooting is, however, not a global phenomenon. In Lebanon, healthcare providers provided information on SRH to significantly more male clients than female [29].

Men’s uptake of reproductive health services can also be limited by the high cost of services, the geographical inaccessibility of health facilities, particularly in rural areas, fear, embarrassment, and insecurity related to self-esteem and reputation, negative experiences, such as being ridiculed by peers, cultural and religious backgrounds that treat discussing sexual matters as a taboo, coupled with concepts of masculinity that are threatened by services like vasectomy or medical male circumcision, limited knowledge of the available SRH services and their benefits, and negative healthcare system experience [30]. Further studies are needed to understand the gender distinction reported in this study.

Second, the significant disparity in service utilization, where migrant men demonstrate more proactive use of STI and prostate cancer screening than refugees, may stem from differences in their displacement trajectories and resulting health-seeking profiles. Migrants often constitute a self-selected group with higher pre-migration educational attainment and health literacy, which fosters a greater understanding of preventive care [31]. Their health-seeking behaviour may be further driven by a concept of productive masculinity where maintaining health is directly tied to economic productivity [32], a driver that may be absent for refugees in aid-dependent camp settings where traditional masculine roles are undermined. Furthermore, migrants typically possess greater enabling resources, including the economic agency and mobility to travel to clinics and afford associated costs, whereas refugees are often geographically and financially confined to camp-based services [33]. Migrants also benefit from more integrated social networks in urban settings that provide practical guidance for navigating health systems, a resource often lacking in the informational isolation of camps [34]. Furthermore, their perceived needs differ; migrants’ environments can heighten their sense of vulnerability to specific health risks like STIs, and their migration as a future-oriented project aligns with preventive behaviours like cancer screening [35]. In stark contrast, the protracted uncertainty of refugee life often fosters a present-centered survival mode, where long-term health planning becomes a secondary concern [36]. Understanding these distinct pathways to SRH services for men facing humanitarian crises is essential for designing targeted strategies that effectively address the unique barriers faced by the different sub-populations.

Third, the lower overall utilization of ancillary, preventive SRH services like cervical and prostate cancer screening, compared to more established services like contraception, highlights a gap in the enabling resources provided by the humanitarian health system. These services are often deprioritized due to funding constraints and operational challenges, limiting their availability and utilization [37]. The fact that migrant women and men had the highest use of cancer screening could point to the role of prior health literacy, or experiences with organized preventive healthcare before displacement, factors known to influence the recognition of need and the ability to navigate health systems in crisis-affected populations.

Fourth, and central to our analysis, is the consistent link between the enabling resource of perceived ease of access and the outcome of client satisfaction. The drop in satisfaction for women’s contraceptive services when access was perceived as uneasy provides quantitative validation that enabling resources are a determinant of the care experience [38, 39]. Long wait times, complex referral pathways, and unwelcoming facilities degrade the enabling environment, impacting the client experience irrespective of the clinical quality of care. Our results confirm that this relationship holds across services, suggesting that the process of obtaining care is as critical to satisfaction as the care itself, a finding that reinforces the expanded Andersen model, which links system characteristics to outcomes [23].

Finally, the high rate of neutral satisfaction among those with uneasy access to GBV services, rather than direct dissatisfaction, is a particularly nuanced finding. It likely reflects the influence of predisposing characteristics such as stigma and fear, which interact with enabling resources. In this context, a neutral rating may not indicate ambivalence but a complex state where a client, deterred by these formidable barriers, may not feel empowered to express overt dissatisfaction. This echoes research on sensitive services where a lack of trust and safety, rather than the technical quality of care, can lead to disengagement [40]. This underscores that for GBV services, enabling resources must extend beyond logistical ease to include confidentiality, psychological safety, and trusted provider-client relationships. We therefore hypothesize that clients accessing GBV services in humanitarian contexts who perceive access as difficult may report higher rates of neutral satisfaction compared to users of other SRH services, as the stigma and safety concerns inherent to GBV create a barrier to expressing overt dissatisfaction.

Collectively, these results affirm that effective SRH programming must move beyond a one-size-fits-all approach. A refugee seeking prostate screening faces a different constellation of predisposing (gender norms) and enabling (lack of male-friendly services) factors than a woman IDP seeking contraception (mobility restrictions) or a single woman facing barriers to GBV support (stigma). This calls for a differentiated service delivery model, an approach gaining traction in global health, which tailors services to the specific needs of sub-groups. Therefore, interventions must be multi-pronged, targeting both the supply and demand sides of the health equation. On the supply side, programs must bolster enabling resources by creating more client-friendly facilities. On the demand side, they must address predisposing characteristics through targeted community engagement and education to shift norms, particularly around male engagement and the acceptability of seeking care for sensitive issues like GBV and STIs. This aligns with the growing focus on human-centered design in public health, which seeks to understand and address the real-world experiences of users to reduce barriers [41].

Conclusion

This study identified that the utilization of SRH services among forcefully displaced persons and migrants is not uniform, but is influenced by an individual’s gender, displacement status, and the type of service sought. Notably, women who are refugees had better utilization of contraception and STI services compared to IDPs and migrants, while men who are refugees faced barriers, and men who are migrants utilized services more readily. This points to a gap in SRH programming for men, especially refugees. In addition, the findings demonstrate that the process of obtaining care is as vital to the client experience as the clinical quality of the service itself, and that logistical barriers directly undermine trust and perceived quality of services provided. To address these issues, a shift in approach from a one-size-fits-all model to programs tailored to the unique needs of specific sub-populations is needed. This includes creating male-friendly SRH services, addressing the distinct barriers faced by IDPs and migrants, and dismantling procedural and logistical friction points that hinder service utilization and access. Achieving equitable and high-quality SRH in crises requires a dual focus on both what services are provided and how they are delivered, ensuring programs are responsive, dignified, and leave no one behind.
