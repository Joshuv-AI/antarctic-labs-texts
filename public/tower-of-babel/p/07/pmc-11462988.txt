The role of factor XIII in patient blood management

Introduction

Blood management is an unavoidable aspect of modern healthcare, as it seeks to optimize patient outcomes while minimizing the risks associated with blood transfusions [1]. Factor XIII (FXIII), a plasma transglutaminase, is a coagulation factor that plays a crucial role in blood clotting and has garnered increasing attention in recent years. It is best known as the final enzyme that crosslinks fibrin strands in the coagulation cascade [2]. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated that FXIII targets a wide range of additional substrates that have an essential role in health and disease management in an extensive range of clinical conditions. It plays an important role in hemostasis, especially in posttraumatic patients, patients undergoing surgery or obstetrics [3]. Additionally, FXIII is involved in wound healing and tissue repair. FXIII deficiency has also been described as a rare condition which can either be inherited or acquired. Extensive research has shown that FXIII deficiency is a rare coagulopathy that leads to longer bleeding time, perioperative and postoperative complications and slower wound healing [4,5].

This article aims to overview the diverse functions of FXIII and to highlight its role in patient blood management.

FXIII structure and FXIII role in hemostasis

Structure

Factor XIII, the fibrin stabilizing factor, is a crucial component of the coagulation cascade. Despite its vital role in ensuring blood clots’ proper formation and stability, it has only recently started raising interest among scientists and researchers. Overall, FXIII is considered a zymogen, although it differs from all other zymogen clotting factors since its active form (FXIIIa) is a transglutaminase that forms crosslinks between two polypeptide chains. It is a complex protein composed of two subunits, A and B, and is activated by thrombin proteolytic cleavage of the A subunit. This then results in the formation of an active heterotetrameric enzyme [2].

The A subunit of factor XIII is a pro transglutaminase. It is known as the only transglutaminase with a dimeric structure. It consists of 732 amino acids and is composed of four distinct domains (β-sandwich, catalytic core, β-barrel 1, and β-barrel 2), plus an NH2-terminal activation peptide. For the activation to occur, the NH2-terminal activation peptide region is cut off by thrombin. The activation process is then followed by thrombin cleaving the propeptide at the Arg-37 position, which exposes an active site thiol group on the A subunit [4].

The B subunit of factor XIII is a smaller protein comprising of 641 amino acids and plays a significant role in stabilizing the active heterotetrameric enzyme. It has a typical mosaic structure composed of 10 short tandem repeats, called sushi domains or glycopeptide-1 (GP-1) structures, each containing around 60 amino acids, which are then held together by a pair of internal disulfide bonds. The same structures have also been found in over 20 other proteins, including β2-glycoprotein. The B subunit binds to the A subunit through a series of noncovalent interactions, which help anchor the enzyme to the fibrin clot [2,4].

Role in hemostasis

FXIII is the final enzyme in the coagulation cascade and plays a key role in maintaining the functional integrity of fibrin clots. During the final stage of the coagulation cascade, thrombin converts fibrinogen into fibrin. Activation of FXIII occurs in the final phase of the clotting cascade by binding to calcium ions, which trigger a conformational change in the enzyme, leading to its activation. The first step of its activation is thrombin cleavage from the NH2 terminus of the FXIII-A subunit. Calcium ions then dissociate the FXIII-B subunit, allowing the FXIII-A dimer to assume an enzymatically active conformation [2,5].

The main role of plasma FXIII in haemostasis is providing mechanical stability to thrombi by cross-linking the α and γ-chains of fibrin. It also protects against fibrinolytic breakdown simply by cross-linking fibrinolytic inhibitors to fibrin. It has been shown that its multiple lysine residues alleviate the binding of plasminogen and tPA to the fibrin network, thus lowering fibrinolysis. FXIII also improves resistance to fibrinolytic degradation by compacting the fibrin fibre diameter and increasing fibre density in the clot itself since thinner clots with smaller pores were previously shown to possess enhanced resistance to fibrinolysis [6,7].

Factor XIII deficiency

The deficiency of FXIII is a very rare coagulation deficiency with inherited prevalence in one of 2–3 million live births. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern and is more common in areas where consanguineous marriage is prevalent. It is difficult to recognize in daily clinical praxis. Moreover, the laboratory results such as international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time are usually normal [7].

FXIII deficiency can be either congenital or acquired. The main cause of congenital/inherited deficiency is usually a missense mutation in either FXIII-A or B subunit genes. Acquired FXIII deficiency can be caused by autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis, malignancy, monoclonal gammopathy and certain medications such as isoniazid. Moreover, it can be caused by hyper-consumption of coagultion factors in patients undergoing a surgical procedure, sepsis, inflammatory bowel disease, thrombosis or Henoch-Schönlein purpura. Furthermore, it can be caused by hypothesis in patients with liver diseases and leukaemia [8,9].

The Diagnosis and Treatment of Trauma-Induced Coagulopathy (DIA-TRE-TIC) study, which was conducted in 2017 as a single-centre, prospective, cohort study including adult trauma patients admitted to Trauma Center at Innsbruck Medical University Hospital to determine the characteristics of coagulation abnormalities in severe blunt polytrauma showed that many patients undergoing severe trauma or polytrauma also suffered from FXIII deficiency and higher than normal Von-Wilenbrandt factor (vWF) levels. The study was conducted on 334 traumatized patients. Out of these, 274 exhibited multiple injuries. Nearly half of the patients showed FXIII levels below the reference range of 70%. Twenty-seven per cent of the study population even presented with FXIII plasma levels less than 60%, a value, which is associated with decreased fibrin polymerisation and increased bleeding in surgical patients [10,11].

At the ICU at Freiburg University Medical Centre conducted a retrospective study, which included a 19 patient control group and 20 patient intervention group (a total of 39 patients) demonstrating that venovenous ECMO therapy leads to acquired coagulation disorders that cannot be diagnosed using routine coagulation tests. FXIII deficiency was identified as the leading syndrome in 88% of all patients under extracorporeal support. Of all patients measured for FXIII activity, 88% developed FXIII deficiency and 81% of them within the first 7 days of ECMO therapy [12,13].

Another retrospective study conducted at the Department of Neurosurgery at Johann Wolfgang Goethe-University in Frankfurt/Main and at Department of Medicine at University of Leipzig showed a statistically significant correlation between decreased levels of FXIII and postoperative bleeding. The study included 1264 patients who underwent an intracranial operation. A total of 20 patients (1.6%) suffered from a major postoperative haemorrhage. FXIII deficiency was defined as less than 60% and was found in eight patients all suffering from major postoperative bleeding [14].

Low plasma FXIII levels were proven to be connected with prolonged and abnormal bleeding in patients undergoing surgery and traumatized patients. Prolonged reduction in plasma FXIII levels, regarded as FXIII deficiency, following surgery, has been demonstrated to increase postoperative complications including disturbed wound healing. FXIII deficiency has also been associated with impaired healing of nonsurgical wounds, such as leg ulcers, burns and pressure sores. Due to its tremendous benefactor role in patient blood management and wound healing, FXIII supplementation proved to be helpful in patients diagnosed with FXIII deficiency. Increasing the awareness of FXIII testing could help identify these patients and provide better healthcare results [9,15].

Clinical use of factor XIII

Besides its crucial role in haemostasis, FXIII plays an important part in various other physiological and pathological processes such as tissue repair, wound healing and immune response to infections. Clinical use of FXIII replacement therapy indicates a larger variety of conditions beyond congenital FXIII deficiency (shown in Fig. 1).

Clincal use of FXIII.

Perioperative and postoperative bleeding

A study conducted at the Institute for Anesthesiology and Institute for Clinical Chemistry and Hematology in Kantonsspital, St. Gallen, Switzerland, included 226 patients who underwent elective surgery compared patients with unexplained intraoperative bleeding to those without intraoperative bleeding. Patients included in the study underwent different types of surgery in general surgery, neurosurgery, orthopaedics and urology departments. In total, 8.8% of patients showed “unexplained” bleeding. These patients all presented with significantly lower levels of FXIII activity during and after surgery. The study discovered that in some patients, reduced clot firmness was detected before the bleeding occurred [16]. This led to the premise that early administration of FXIII may benefit patients with a risk of perioperative bleeding. This approach was then prospectively evaluated in a randomized, placebo-controlled trial and found highly beneficial [17,18].

Furthermore, several case reports suggested that FXIII supplementation is beneficial for managing bleeding in patients undergoing all types of major surgery. For example, a case report featured in a case collection by CliniCum, Wien medical Journal has shown that the supplementation of FXIII proved beneficial in a patient undergoing major abdominal intervention for liver rupture. The addition of FXIII concentrate for the correction of low preoperative FXIII activity (lower than 50%) led to normalizing its activity and prevented secondary bleeding [19]. Another case report taken from the same case collection by Clinicum focuses on the surgical treatment of a polytraumatized patient. FXIII deficiency was treated by administration of FXIII concentrate. In addition to bleeding prevention, additional FXIII concentrate administration was used to prevent or lower generalized inflammation, sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation [20,21].

In some cases, FXIII deficiency occurs due to an inhibitory process. In the case report presented by Hayashi et al. [23], a 75-year-old patient with an enlarged subcutaneous haematoma following tooth extraction was diagnosed with low FXIII activity. It was revealed that the cause for deficiency was the presence of immunoglobulin bound to the FXIII-A subunit. The bleeding tendency in this patient was later controlled by infusion of FXIII concentrate combined with immunosuppressive therapy (oral prednisolone) [22,23].

Wound healing

There is convincing and ever-increasing evidence that the role of FXIII extends much further than just controlling haemostasis in surgical patients. It was discovered that impaired wound healing was more common in patients with congenital FXIII deficiency. Slower wound healing due to FXIII deficiency also presented as a reason for more frequent postoperative bleeding and infections [23].

A study by Wozniak et al.[24], conducted on 54 patients with leg ulcers, noted decreased FXIII activity in patients with either postphlebitic syndromes, postphlebitic ulcers, varicose leg ulcers or mixed leg ulcers compared with those without venous disease. Thirty patients with chronic leg ulcers in the study received topical treatment with FXIII concentrate which significantly reduced ulcer size compared with placebo [24].

Another retrospective descriptive analysis compared patients with large surface burn wounds and FXIII deficiency, with patients with normal FXIII activity. This study by Burkhardt et al. [27] has revealed a significant correlation between FXIII and a longer hospital stay. However, there seemed to be no significant association between recombinant FXIII supplementation and the number of required surgeries. The study also suggested that acquired factor XIII deficiency in burn patients can be considered a negative prognostic marker [21,25].

Bacterial infections

FXIII also plays an important role in host defence against invasive bacteria. Due to FXIII's presence, the bacterial cells can get entrapped into plasma clots, thereby lowering the bacterial potential [20].

Some results from patient biopsies suggested evidence that FXIII plays a role in the immobilization of streptococcal bacteria, especially in patients who suffered from necrotizing fascitis caused by Streptococcus pyogenes[26,27].

An additional study by Wang et al. has shown that FXIII also cross-links fibrin to surface proteins from other bacterial species such as Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus[28]. Regarding the context of infectious bacterial disease conditions, FXIII has been linked to the entrapment of various bacteria and has been shown to interact with the immune system components. However, further clinical trials and studies are needed to fully understand the role of FXIII regarding bacterial infections and its potential beneficial factors [29].

FXIII assays

Rotem and teg

Rapid point-of-care tests were the standard for assessing hemostatic disorders and patient blood management for years. TEG (thromboelastography) and ROTEM (rotational thromboelastometry) are the most widely used. It is still the most frequently used method for assessing hemostatic disorders in clinical praxis. ROTEM has proven to be useful in transfusion algorithms alone or in combination with other rapid POC (point-of-care) tests such as whole blood platelet count and multiple electrode aggregometry (MEA). This has allowed a more goal-directed hemostatic therapy to take place with the administration of nonerythrocyte blood products to address specific deficiencies. Compared to standard laboratory tests (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin clotting time, fibrinogen concentration and platelet count), which usually take 45–60 min, ROTEM and MEA measurements are available within 15–20 min, which is beneficial in severely bleeding patients. ROTEM technology provides real-time analysis of the viscoelastic properties of clot formation and dissolution in whole blood. Diagnosis and treatment algorithms incorporating ROTEM and TEG analysis for bleeding patients in various clinical situations have been developed and are still in development. There is some evidence that these algorithms seem to reduce transfusions and improve clinical outcomes, but more studies are needed [30,31].

ROTEM and FXIII

It is known that low fibrinogen and FXIII levels affect blood loss negatively. A study conducted on patients who underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) used point-of-care-supported coagulation analysis (ROTEM, impedance aggregometry) has shown that dependence of blood loss on ROTEM, Multiplate, factor I and FXIII concentration was only weak. For the first time, it was proven that fibrinogen and FXIII levels correlated with each other on all measured time points. It was concluded that based on the measured levels of fibrinogen, an assessment of FXIII levels is likely possible. Based on these findings, it would be possible for the clinical staff to make an assessment of FXIII based on the fibrinogen values, which would allow the integration of FXIII into the coagulation monitoring during surgery. However, further studies are required [32].

Most hospitals and medical centres are using the standardized TEG or ROTEM method for measuring certain haemostatic and coagulation disorders. Since some studies have shown a correlation between fibrinogen levels and FXIII concentration the use of TEG or ROTEM has currently proven to be adequate and beneficial for assessing FXIII activity due to FXIII deficiency being a fairly rare condition and the high price of specific assays [30,33].

Specific FXIII assays

Diagnosing FXIII deficiency is much more complicated than diagnosing other coagulopathies.

Therefore, qualitative and quantitative FXIII assays are available to facilitate the diagnosis of FXIII deficiency. Qualitative assays are clot solubility tests based on the fibrin crosslinking activity of FXIII. Quantitative assays, on the other hand, provide an accurate measure of FXIII activity or antigen levels [34,35].

Qualitative assays also known as clot solubility assays were traditionally used as the initial screening test due to the limited availability of quantitative assays in some institutions. Following the addition of a clotting reagent (thrombin and/or calcium), the dissolution of the clot in the presence of a solubilizing agent (concentrated urea, acetic acid and monochloroacetic acid) within a prespecified time may suggest the presence of FXIII deficiency [36,37].

Fibrinogen concentration plays a major role in the sensitivity of the tests. False positives may also occur due to the activation of plasma pepsinogen in acidic environments. The test has a trend of meagre detection limit of 0.5–5% FXIII activity; therefore it can potentially miss out on clinically relevant deficiencies [36].

Quantitative FXIII antigen assays are based on immunological methods, ELISAs are used in most cases. They determine FXIII-A, FXIII-B or FXIII-A2B2 antigen concentrations, which allow the correct classification of the FXIII deficiency. Commercial assays are available and generally have a high sensitivity of detecting FXIII antigen concentrations less than 1%. FXIII-A antigen assays can also be utilized in platelet lysates to quantify cytoplasmic FXIII-A [37].

However, although it proved useful for measuring FXIII activity, the price point of specific FXIII assays remains a drawback. Due to its complex structure and its seldom use in clinical praxis, the price for a single specific FXIII assay ranges from 500$ upwards [35].

FXIII replacement therapy

Indications

Factor XIII replacement therapy is considered for patients with diagnosed factor XIII deficiency. In patients with inherited and/or chronic factor XIII deficiency, long-term prophylactic treatment with FXIII concentrates is advised. Furthermore, replacement therapy should also be considered in patients undergoing surgery with FXIII activity levels lower than 60%. FXIII deficiency has also been described in patients with severe trauma or polytraumatized patients. Although some studies have shown that replacement therapy in these cases could be beneficial, there is no certain indication for replacement (shown in Fig. 2) [38,39].

Indication, contraindications and side effects.

Contraindications

The definitive contraindication for FXIII replacement is in patients allergic to FXIII or any medicine made with human plasma. Replacement therapy is also not advised and should be individually judged in patients with known liver disease. It is unknown whether factor XIII will harm an unborn baby; therefore, consultations with an obstetrician should be made if the patient is pregnant or plans to become pregnant while using this medicine. It is also not known whether factor XIII passes into breast milk or if it could harm a nursing baby; therefore, consultation with the doctor is required. Factor XIII is made from human plasma which may contain viruses and other infectious agents. Although donated plasma is tested and treated to reduce the risk of it containing infectious agents and to reduce anaphylactic reactions, there is still a small possibility those could occur [39,40].

Side effects of FXIII replacement therapy

A descriptive analysis of over 20 years of pharmacovigilance data was made and it indicates that the rate of adverse side effects following the administration of FXIII concentrate across diverse clinical settings is low. The corresponding rate of side effects was very rare [one case for every 15 700 standard dosages (20 IU/kg)]. A total of 12 cases were determined as anaphylactic or hypersensitivity reaction events corresponding to approximately one case reported for every 98 400 doses. In some rare situations, the development of inhibitory antibodies against FXIII in patients receiving FXIII supplementation has been observed. Since the development of inhibitors to FXIII in patients complicates the basic management of the patient, the incidence of inhibitor development was also evaluated. A low rate of inhibitor development was observed, with a total of five cases reported, corresponding to one case for every 236 200 doses.

A total of 20 cases of transmission of pathogens were reported, which is approximately one case for every 59 100 doses. A lack of correlation was described in six cases or one case for every 196 800 doses. In 29 cases of patients treated with FXIII concentrate, there was no specific classification since they were not classified as anaphylactic, allergic, thromboembolic, virus-related, involving inhibitor development or lack of effect. Those were classified as unspecified event investigations (11 cases), general disorders and administration site conditions (10 cases), followed by injury, poisoning and procedural complications (eight cases) [40,41].

Management and guidelines

Routine prophylaxis

FXIII replacement therapy, as a part of routine prophylaxis, is usually administered intravenously. Corifact and Tretten are artificially made proteins produced to replicate the naturally occurring FXIII in the body. Currently, no biosimilar are medicines licensed for the treatment of haemophilia and other coagulation disorders. Development of biosimilar medicines for some of the newer modified products or nonreplacement products for haemophilia A and B or recombinant factor XIII, remains years away, due to its market exclusivity [22,42].

It is important to note that administration of the drug should be given only by or under the supervision of your doctor or other healthcare professional. In routine prophylaxis in adult patients with Corifact, the initial dose should be 40 IU/kg intravenously, infused at a rate not exceeding 4 ml/min. Subsequent doses should be administered every 28 days and are tailored to maintaining FXIII activity level at approximately 5–20%. If using Tretten, the initial dose should be 35 IU/kg intravenously, infused at a maximum rate of 1–2 ml/min. Further dose adjustment is needed if adequate coverage is not achieved at the initial dose. It is also important to note that the use of Tretten is not indicated in congenital factor XIII B-subunit deficiency.

The routine prophylaxis protocols in pediatric patients do not differ from adults (shown in Fig. 3) [42,43].

Management of perioperative bleeding

Managing perioperative bleeding involves multiple assessments and strategies to ensure appropriate patient care. It is important to identify patients with an increased risk of perioperative bleeding. Correcting preoperative anaemia and stabilizing macrocirculation and microcirculation are needed to optimize the patient's tolerance to bleeding. Finally, targeted interventions should reduce intraoperative and postoperative bleeding and prevent subsequent morbidity and mortality [43,44].

It is therefore worth measuring FXIII activity in cases with unexplained postoperative bleeding. If its activity is decreased, replacement should be considered at a 20 IU/kg dose. If immediate measurement of FXIII is unavailable, a standard dose of 20 IU/kg of XIII concentrate is justified if a bleeding tendency persists despite adequate active haemostatic therapy (shown in Fig. 3) [38].

The dosages should be individualized and based on FXIII activity level, type of surgery and clinical response. Monitoring FXIII levels during and after surgery is also needed.

When administrating FXIII concentrates, the dose must be properly adjusted considering the last prophylactic dose. An additional dose may not be needed if the last dose was administered within 7 days of the operation. If the prophylactic dose was given within the last 8–21 days, then an additional full or partial dose may be needed based on FXIII activity level. If the last prophylaxis administration was 21–28 days ago, the patient needs a full prophylactic dose before surgery [43,44].

European Society of Anaesthesiology guidelines suggest that viscoelastic hemostatic assays (VHA) should be used for intraoperative rapid diagnosis of bleeding causes. Early targeted treatment of coagulation factor deficiencies is recommended in all patients with any diagnosed deficiency. Coagulation factor concentrates are suggested for the primary treatment of acquired coagulation factor deficiency due to their high efficacy and minimal infectiousness [37].

Levels of FXIII above 3–5% are usually sufficient to prevent spontaneous bleeding. Since FXIII has a long plasma half-life of 7–12 days, prophylaxis is highly feasible. A 10–20 U/kg dose every 4–6 weeks should provide adequate plasma levels in most patients. However, the dose and frequency should be tailored to each patient's plasma levels and clinical efficacy. Because of the heterogeneous availability of the plasma-derived concentrate, regular prophylaxis has not become the standard care in congenital FXIII deficiency [38,44].

Following the British guidelines for rare coagulation disorders, it is suggested that patients undergoing minor surgery or with a known history of mild bleeding tranexamic acid are administered in doses of 15–20 mg/kg or 1 g four times per day. For severe bleeding or patients undergoing major surgery, administration of additional FXIII concentrate in doses of 10–40 IU/kg is advised depending on the interval since last prophylaxis and the severity of bleeding.

It is recommended that pregnant women with FXIII deficiency on standard prophylaxis therapy with FXIII concentrate are monitored closely throughout pregnancy. Prophylaxis should be increased in frequency to every 14–21 days. This will help to maintain FXIII activity more than 0.2 IU/ml. Additional FXIII concentrates in 10–40 IU/kg doses are advised once the patient is in established labour or before caesarean section [43,44].

Management of traumatized and polytraumatized patients

Acquired low levels of FXIII have been found in patients with major trauma and coagulopathy. Monitoring factor XIII levels and replacement below a certain level is suggested as part of coagulation support algorithms. However, the need for a defined optimal level of FXIII replacement in major trauma patients has not been determined. Following the updated guidelines for managing severe perioperative bleeding, the administration of FXIII concentrate is suggested in the presence of bleeding and a FXIII level lower than 30%. The replacement of FXIII with FXIII concentrate in major trauma patients has resulted in major reductions in transfusion requirements and improved clinical outcomes, including a reduction in the duration of stay in the ICU, organ dysfunction and hospital mortality in one study [10,38].

In major trauma patients with ongoing bleeding and with FXIII activity lower than 60%, administration of FXIII concentrate in 15 IU/kg doses led to a reduction in the proportion of patients requiring massive transfusions and an overall reduction of red blood cell and fresh frozen plasma transfusion. However, due to changes to acute trauma care over time, including novel goal-directed coagulation management strategies and modified surgical approaches, the specific contribution of FXIII to improved outcomes can not be confirmed (shown in Fig. 3) [43].

Conclusion

The FXIII plays an important role in patient blood management, even though its use in regular praxis is very seldom, depending on the physician and it is currently considered as off-label use only.

FXIII deficiency is a relatively rare blood clotting disease that is usually diagnosed in patients with severe trauma, patients undergoing major surgery or spontaneously due to other coagulation deficiencies. Specific qualitative and quantitative assays for FXIII deficiency have proven to be useful. However, considering the rareness and the high cost of specific assays, there are currently no standard screening tests in use. Due to the very low number of studies regarding the clinical correlation between fibrinogen and FXIII, using only TEG or ROTEM to assess FXIII activity may lead to excessive treatment with FXIII concentrate in patients with adequate levels of FXIII even though their levels of fibrinogen might be lower than usual, therefore further studies should be required.

Replacement of FXIII has shown to be useful and a valid asset in patient blood management in patients undergoing major surgery, severe trauma or in patients with congenital deficiency. FXIII replacement therapy is also gaining importance in routine prophylaxis. Due to the increasing amount of studies being made about FXIII replacement therapy and its use in various clinical conditions, its importance has not gone unnoticed in various haematological and transfusion guidelines. There is yet to be a certain guideline for the use and administration of FXIII; therefore, the use of factor XIII concentrate and replacement therapy is still considered as off-label use and is dependent on the physician.

Certain studies have shown that measurements of the exact levels of FXIII should be made before replacement therapy. On top of that, the economics and benefits of the diagnosis and treatment should be taken into consideration before administrating the drug. Regarding the prospective that biosimilars for FXIII deficiency and their wider use remain in an uncertain future, the price point of current FXIII replacement therapy drugs is suspected to stay stably high. Further studies are still required to determine the exact use and benefits of FXIII replacement therapy.

To summarize, the future of patient blood management and the importance of coagulation factor XIII is showing great promise. With its probable wider use in general praxis and possible inclusion into future guidelines, rapid development, studies and a wider range of indications are expected, potentially lowering the costs of diagnosis and treatment.
