Integrating wearables into the modern electrophysiology practice: A proposed framework for the use of wearables and creation of a remote digital clinic
Authors: Krishna Pundi, Jason G Andrade, Neal Bhatia, Thomas Deneke, Hamid Ghanbari, Matthew Kalscheur, Peem Lorvidhaya, Jennifer N Avari Silva, Amit J Thosani, Michael A Rosenberg, Gerilynn M Schott, Amy G Tucker, Niraj Varma, Omair Yousuf, Peter A Noseworthy
Published: 2025

The integration of wearables in cardiac electrophysiology practice represents a unique scenario where technology adoption is outpacing clinical evidence, guidelines, and best practices. Consumers and patients are already widely using wearables capable of assessing cardiac rhythm, forcing clinicians to adapt in real time to this changing landscape.1 Today’s wearables vary widely in the signals and data they collect, ranging from those that use photoplethysmography (PPG) waveforms as surrogates for cardiac rhythm to single- and multilead electrocardiogram (ECG) recordings and multimodality data collection. Limited evidence from studies such as the Apple Heart Study,2 the Fitbit Heart Study,3 and the Huawei Heart Study,4 along with smaller studies in individual practices and health systems, has begun to shed light on the utility and limitations of these technologies, but many challenges and unknowns remain.

Meanwhile, clinicians who have been early adopters of this technology have begun to recognize pitfalls in clinical utility. These include inconsistent and sometimes poor-quality tracings, an overwhelming influx of patient-generated data, a lack of unified data management systems, and an absence of well-established practice norms.5 These challenges highlight the need for robust data management systems and clear clinical practice guidance tailored to discrete clinical scenarios. Electrophysiology providers are especially well suited to take on this challenge, given their experience with medical-grade monitoring devices that have established algorithms for detection, verification, and response to arrhythmias.6

This white paper aimed to address these needs by compiling the insights and reflections of members of the Heart Rhythm Society Digital Health Committee who are navigating these complexities. Although we currently lack the data to formulate definitive recommendations, this document seeks to initiate a critical conversation within our field. By collectively examining these issues, we hope to take the necessary steps toward developing thoughtful, well-justified guidance for integrating wearables into our clinical practices. This effort is crucial as we strive to harness the potential of wearable technology to improve patient outcomes while managing its challenges in a clinically sound manner. In addition, there is a need for substantial guidance of patients in regard to the potential information a specific wearable and data transmission plans may offer in relation to individual clinical scenarios.

Ideal data platform

Although wearable devices have become increasingly popular among patients and consumers for health monitoring, their integration into clinical practice, particularly in cardiology, poses real challenges. Clinics often lack the infrastructure to efficiently process and integrate data from wearables into medical records. Despite these challenges, most health care providers who responded to a Heart Rhythm Society survey in 2020 had already recommended the use of a digital health device to facilitate management of atrial fibrillation (AF).7 This gap creates a disconnect between the potential of wearables for enhancing patient care and the current capabilities of health care systems to leverage these data effectively. The issues include difficulty in data ingestion, annotation, identification of relevant information, distinguishing important signals from irrelevant data, and generating concise reports for the medical record. Moreover, managing data from various sources is time consuming and not adequately supported by existing billing and reimbursement models, which sometimes makes the technology more burdensome than beneficial. From a patient perspective, the need to purchase and manage one’s own device has the potential to exacerbate existing health care disparities, particularly in patients with lower technological literacy.

Data integration with EHR and clinical workflow

Once developed, the well-engineered platform requires integration into a workflow that facilitates patient care. Such a workflow would start with a decision between a provider and a patient to initiate monitoring with a wearable device. An order would be placed by the provider that signals to the ingestion engine that a patient may start transmitting data. The patient then agrees to send that data and links their device (or devices) to that ingestion engine. After the connection is made, tracings or other discrete data points flow to the ingestion and processing platform. The technology to make these connections exists, but specific applications to enable the ingestion of all types of wearable data require development. This initial ingestion likely involves preprocessing using machine learning models to reduce the rates of inconclusive tracings and improve diagnostic accuracy.

Those preprocessed results would then pass into the second part of the platform to be displayed, cataloged, and prioritized for evaluation by clinical staff at the health care organization caring for the patient. Order generation is a key function in this processing portion of the platform. Creation of an order allows 2 key functions: (1) the result can be filed into the health care organizations’ medical record, allowing that result to be viewed across the organization, and (2) order placement facilitates billing. The clinical staff—likely nurses or certified technicians—review those preprocessed, prioritized tracings, creating a preliminary result. The preliminary results are finalized by attending physicians, billing is performed, the result is stored in EHR and the ingestion/processing platform, and the result is sent to the patient via the health care organization’s patient portal. This workflow overlaps in many ways with those already in place for ambulatory monitors—typically processed in ECG laboratories/heart stations—and remote monitoring for implanted devices. Therefore, the location of this clinical evaluation may vary between health care organizations.

Current state

Current commercial platforms are being developed to meet these requirements, but there is no dominant system that has been integrated at scale. Based on provider surveys, perceived barriers to adoption include limited awareness of billing codes available for interpretation of device-recorded ECGs,7 skepticism and uncertainty regarding the accuracy of the automated diagnoses from wearable devices, lack of understanding of the advanced data science techniques that lead to and improve those diagnoses, uncertainty regarding clinical utility, and data privacy concerns as we enter into a patient-centric rather than a provider-centric model for data.7,8 Obtaining and installing third-party software to help manage patient-generated data come with an expense. Therefore, the onus is on vendors to demonstrate how the system can overcome existing barriers and lead to improved clinical outcomes. However, the current system that often relies on individual providers reviewing tracings sent through patient portals without preprocessing or mechanisms for formalizing interpretations and billing is not sustainable.

Clinical scenarios for wearable monitor use in clinical practice

Primary AF screening

AF is an important and common cardiovascular disease, and oral anticoagulation (OAC) constitutes an effective, accepted treatment to prevent stroke in patients at high risk of thromboembolic events.9 The given incidence of new AF3,10,11 in the general population can vary owing to factors such as the monitoring modality, the population studied, the sensitivity of the monitoring method, and the duration of follow-up (Table 1).

What is the monitoring going to achieve?

Monitoring will detect subclinical or asymptomatic AF episodes, allowing for the initiation of guideline-directed anticoagulation and AF-directed therapies to prevent complications such as stroke, heart failure, and cognitive decline.

Potential approach

Screening modalities that accurately identify patients at high risk of developing AF are likely to be the most clinically relevant and cost-effective. Several risk scores for predicting incident AF have been reported,12 but most have not been replicated in large, diverse cohorts with various ethnicities. The CHARGE-AF risk score, which incorporates factors such as age, race, height, weight, blood pressure, smoking status, antihypertensive medication use, diabetes, and history of myocardial infarction and heart failure, has been tested on a large number of individuals across different ancestries, providing greater generalizability than other risk scores for AF prediction.13 These predictive models can be used to select high-risk individuals for trials of primary prevention of AF14 or intensive monitoring for AF detection and help select candidates for more aggressive risk factor modification. Promising screening techniques include machine learning based models to predict the development of AF based on sinus ECGs.15

Potential pitfalls

Developments in wearable technologies allow for more extended intensive monitoring. These advancements frequently lead to the detection of more paroxysmal AF episodes with a shorter duration, potentially representing a lower AF burden and a more benign prognosis. The US Preventive Services Task Force and other professional societies believe that there is insufficient evidence to determine whether systematic ECG screening for AF will do more good than harm and recommend adequately powered randomized clinical trials to resolve this issue. Despite the potential benefits of screening for AF, appropriate care pathways for confirming the diagnosis and potential initiating guideline-directed treatment in individuals with positive findings have not been established.

Wrist-based wearables use PPG sensors to measure pulse and process data frequently and passively during wear time. Although smartwatches continuously sample the PPG signal, most PPG-based algorithms only analyze periods when the user is not active.2,3 Therefore, effective screening is reliant on opportunistic detection over long periods of wear time, including during sleep. When worn consistently, wearables have demonstrated the capacity to detect most clinically relevant episodes of AF (ie, AF episode duration >6 minutes).16 Although PPG-based approaches for AF detection are comparable with ECG-based approaches for AF diagnosis when there is adequate signal quality,17 further testing might be required to confirm AF.

Secondary AF

Beyond clinical AF, digital health holds promise in patients with secondary, or “triggered- induced” AF. These include patients who have new-onset AF in the context of postoperative cardiac surgery, acute pulmonary disease, acute coronary syndromes, or sepsis. However, some patients have abnormal substrate and are at risk of future AF episodes, and their long-term need for anticoagulation is unclear.18 Recent data show that continuous cardiac monitoring after cardiac surgery identifies previously unrecognized AF compared with usual care.19 Digital wearables might provide better insight to patients who would benefit from long-term anticoagulation and rhythm control and help avoid unnecessary bleeding risk for others.

What is the monitoring going to achieve?

Current multisociety guidelines provide limited guidance for management in this subset of patients, given that the net clinical benefit for anticoagulation after secondary AF has not been established.9 Wearable-based monitoring in this population can inform clinicians as to whether AF is truly provoked or whether it has unmasked substrate for AF that needs to be addressed, which may include anticoagulation or rhythm control.

Potential approach

A patient-tailored approach can optimize the positive and negative predictive value of wearable monitoring. For instance, in patients after cardiac surgery, PPG-based continuous rhythm monitoring may be ideal given the high likelihood of recurrent AF episodes. Meanwhile, in patients with sepsis and normal cardiac substrate, spot-check monitoring with ECG-based devices can serve as a high-specificity approach and reduce false positives. Those patients who are confirmed to have further AF episodes can then be evaluated for anticoagulation and rhythm control.

Potential pitfalls

Although wearable devices have been evaluated in prediagnostic AF screening capacities, their true performance in postoperative patients or patients with provoked AF is not known. Patients with recent cardiac surgery may be at higher risk of atrial ectopy,20 which may be falsely detected as AF. There is also limited knowledge on how long after an inciting event to still consider an AF episode trigger induced. Acknowledging these limitations is important for effective shared decision making when treating such patients.

Patients at high risk of stroke or recurrent stroke

The relationship between AF and ischemic stroke has long been established, with clinically detected AF increasing the risk of stroke more than 5-fold.21 In many patients, AF may be asymptomatic, with up to 20% of patients presenting with stroke as the first clinical manifestation of previously undiagnosed AF.22 As such, it has been proposed that rhythm monitoring may be a valuable tool for identifying AF in patients who would benefit from stroke prevention therapies,23 such as those at high objective risk of stroke or those who previously experienced an embolic stroke of unknown source.24, 25, 26 In these groups of patients at risk of adverse AF-associated outcomes, prolonged cardiac rhythm monitoring with wearable devices may improve the diagnostic yield by detecting actionable AF.

What is the monitoring going to achieve?

The goal of prolonged rhythm monitoring is the detection of actionable episodes of AF. In effect, the detection of occult AF would allow the consideration of stroke prevention therapies with the goal of reducing the incidence of AF-associated strokes, which are associated with greater long-term disability and a significantly higher mortality than non-AF-associated strokes.27, 28, 29

Potential approach

AF detection is proportionate to the duration and intensity of arrhythmia monitoring, with intermittent rhythm monitors missing a substantial proportion of AF episodes.30 As a result, the ideal device for AF detection in high-risk individuals is a noninvasive device capable of real-time continuous rhythm monitoring over a prolonged duration of follow-up. Ideally, this should occur passively, meaning that the wearer is not required to perform an action to trigger the rhythm assessment (eg, rhythm strip activation). In this regard, an Internet-enabled smartwatch equipped with a PPG sensor and linked to real-time advanced rhythm analysis offers the best balance of convenience, continuous observation, and immediate notification. In individuals at high risk of AF, the detection of AF would be considered actionable and would suggest the need for OAC initiation for stroke prevention. In this circumstance, the relative balance of high pretest probability (eg, high risk of AF) would offset the relative inaccuracy of the PPG sensor, limiting the rate of false-positive detections necessitating ECG confirmation.

Potential risks and pitfalls

There are 2 concerns regarding the use of wearable cardiac monitors for AF detection and initiation of stroke prevention therapy. First, the current PPG-enabled devices are limited by a relatively low accuracy (∼95%), which is in part caused by a high proportion of uninterpretable recordings (eg, during exercise) and missed diagnosis owing to a lack of use (eg, patients not wearing the devices during sleep). Second, we do not yet know the threshold for clinically relevant AF episode duration or burden on wearable devices. Within the cardiac implantable device literature, there is debate regarding the biological relevance of shorter episodes of AF detected by continuous monitoring. Although these short AF episodes have been temporally linked to ischemic stroke events,31 the association is strongest for those episodes lasting longer than 24 hours.32 As such, it may be reasonable to consider an alternate approach of daily ECG rhythm strip assessment in lieu of continuous smartphone monitoring, at least until the accuracy of PPG monitoring improves.

Research priorities and evidence gaps

The major areas in need of research are (1) determination of the relationship between wearable device-detected AF and clinical outcomes (eg, stroke); (2) the relationship between AF episode duration (or AF burden over a certain period of time) and stroke risk, ideally with determination of a minimum duration of AF that increases the risk of stroke; (3) a randomized trial comparing OAC therapy for wearable detected AF; and (4) a pill-in-the-pocket OAC therapy for wearable detected AF (currently being tested in the REACT trial).33

Monitoring of known AF

What is the monitoring going to achieve?

Wearable monitors were initially validated to serve as prediagnostic tools for new AF via irregular pulse notifications from PPG.2,3 However, these tools can play a potent role in prevalent AF management from identifying lifestyle factors and triggers to informing medical or procedural intervention. They may also be valid tools for postintervention rhythm monitoring and have the potential for directing treatment options in this cohort during the early and late follow-up period.

Multiple lifestyle factors have known associations with AF severity and symptomatology. For many patients, the convergence of wearable monitoring with other digital technologies may have an important role in reducing morbidity and mortality. Hypertension is one of the most frequently attributed risk factors for developing AF,34 and patients who self-monitor blood pressure to titrate antihypertensives were shown to achieve better blood pressure control than those assigned to usual care.35 Improved glycemic control may also help reduce the risk of AF recurrence36; this can be facilitated by application-based diabetes management. Large prospective studies have demonstrated that, in individuals with obesity, weight loss can significantly reduce AF burden and symptomatology.37,38 Sleep disordered breathing and obstructive sleep apnea are associated with AF severity and AF-associated mortality, and continuous positive airway pressure therapy with improved sleep may ameliorate these risks.39,40 In a randomized controlled trial with a series of n-of-1 participants, alcohol intake was associated with AF episodes and higher AF burden.41 Although wearable monitors have long recorded and summarized heart rate trends during wear periods, some devices now combine PPG-based AF detection to also provide estimates of daily AF burden.42 Monitoring these trends can provide real-time feedback to both patients and clinicians on lifestyle effects and inform appropriate interventions.

Potential approach

For clinicians seeking to use information from wearable monitors to guide patient management, wearable monitors can now demonstrate hour- and day-level heart rate trends, allow for point-of-care ECG measurements, and report trends in AF burden before and after clinical intervention. In patients with low-burden AF or chronic persistent/permanent AF on rate control alone, even simple wearable monitors can provide sufficient PPG-based heart rate trends to ensure that patients are achieving goal heart rate parameters. In patients with symptomatic AF, including those with previous catheter ablation or on antiarrhythmic therapy, symptom-triggered ECG recordings sent to a clinician can be useful in separating AF episodes from other arrhythmias or nonarrhythmic symptoms. For asymptomatic patients, maximizing wear time with the AF detection features enabled on wearable monitors can facilitate the detection of AF recurrence. Finally, more sophisticated consumer monitors can provide multilead ECGs, and these may be helpful in attempting to localize arrhythmias that are clearly not AF.

Potential pitfalls

When using wearable monitors for AF management, it is important to inform patients that AF detection is not enabled by default and must be turned on. As noted earlier, AF detection also relies on wear time, including during sleep, and irregular rhythms during activity periods may go undetected. For some wearables, ECGs must be obtained during a perceived episode and therefore may not be well suited for passive monitoring. Signal quality and interpretation algorithms also vary between devices, and there have not been head-to-head studies to compare their performance. Based on these issues, the decision on the specificities of data recorded by an individual wearable must be included in the device selection process.

Research priorities and evidence gaps

Prospective studies are needed to validate these and other potential features of wearable monitors in AF management, including titration of rate control, evaluation of AF episode duration and burden on rhythm control, and identification of potential adverse effects of medications. QT measurements from wearable monitors are not validated and should not be used as a proxy for true 12-lead ECG measurements.

Symptom-rhythm correlation or undifferentiated arrhythmias believed to be low risk

What is the monitoring going to achieve?

Perhaps one of the most important use cases for wearables is in those patients who have intermittent palpitations but are either unwilling or unable (ie, owing to skin sensitivities) to use patch-based prescribed or implantable devices. In these patients, the use of direct-to-consumer wearables can provide a patient-centric approach to the acquisition of heart rate and ECG tracings that can guide diagnosis. A few subgroups of patients are ideally suited to this type of diagnostic approach: (1) patients with a high clinical index of suspicion for arrhythmia but infrequent symptoms and (2) patients with a low clinical index of suspicion for arrhythmia. In both patient groups, the ability to acquire heart rate and/or ECG tracings at the time of the event is important for having diagnostic certainty moving forward. In these cases, the diagnostic goal is to document symptom-rhythm correlation, which can then help guide therapy. These tracings can help support medical decision making and determine whether reassurance, medication, or referral for invasive diagnostics and treatment is most appropriate.

Potential approach

Shared decision making between the physician and patient when deciding on appropriate device selection is ideal. The role of the provider is to understand exactly what data are needed for diagnostic accuracy: does the device have to provide a heart rate or an ECG? Will a single lead suffice or are multiple leads indicated for diagnosis? What is the patient able to reasonably accomplish, both physically and financially?

Addressing these questions forwardly, coupled with understanding the patient’s level of digital literacy before device acquisition, empowers the patient to purchase the most appropriate device to address the clinical question. Once data have been acquired, the next step is interpreting and sharing the data. Edge-based computing using proprietary algorithms can provide the patient with a relatively instantaneous interpretation of the data. Certain technologies have a subscription model that patients can opt in to that provides a physician overread for data. However, ensuring capture and presentation of the relevant data that are used in medical decision making remains an important hurdle that needs to be addressed. Although institutions may have local solutions to support this interoperability, this is not yet broadly available and will be an important step in implementing these technologies.

Potential pitfalls

Although the raw data gathered from these devices are often of high quality, the current algorithms have opportunities for improvement across a broader patient age range and more varied arrhythmia detections. In addition, appropriate signal quality is needed to make diagnostic decisions.

Limitations of wearables

Consumer-grade wearable devices are not currently recommended for the diagnosis of suspected arrhythmic syncope with high suspicion of either a tachy- or bradyarrhythmia. For high-risk syncope (eg, unheralded syncope, syncope during exercise, syncope in the setting of known infranodal conduction disease, or syncope during high-risk activities including driving and operating heavy machinery), those cases requiring symptom-rhythm correlation after appropriate clinical cardiovascular evaluation are best suited for continuous implantable or prescription-grade monitoring. Owing to the potential consequences of not capturing the heart rhythm associated with these high-risk events (including but not limited to recurrent syncope, injury, or death), wearables are not recommended at this time given the required element of patient willingness and compliance with wearing and reporting important findings documented by a consumer-grade device. Medical-grade continuous monitoring is better suited to these situations, including ECG patch monitors, mobile cardiac telemetry, or implantable loop recorders, depending on the frequency of symptoms and specific clinical scenario. Each of these prescription or implantable options allows for direct feedback to the prescribing provider, minimizing the risk of missing an important rhythm finding requiring further treatment including cardiac pacemaker or transvenous defibrillator implantation.

One important consideration of wearable technology relates to the validity of the data that are returned, which includes an understanding that many of the clinical measures returned by wearables are the product of black-box computer algorithms applied to noisy raw data from sensors. For example, the PPG sensor measures patterns of light absorption across the skin, from which clinical metrics such as oxygen saturation, pulse rate and rhythm, or blood pressure are derived. The specific algorithms used in this process, whether well validated or not, are not directly available at the time of interpretation, nor are the raw data they process in deriving the clinical measure. The result is that many clinical measures reported by wearables carry a level of uncertainty that must be addressed if and when this information is reported back to the patient. A computer interpretation of an ECG tracing might falsely indicate AF owing to noise, but a clinician can directly inspect the raw tracing to confirm or refute the diagnosis. In contrast, a wearable PPG sensor can falsely indicate AF owing to motion artifact, but there is no mechanism for the clinician to review the raw data to make an equivalent assessment.

In other words, the process of creating derived clinical measures from raw sensors in wearables and the inherent uncertainty and opacity within this process have several clinical ramifications. First, clinicians are largely at the mercy of the proprietary black-box algorithms used to process the derived clinical measures from sensor data. Unlike with an ECG tracing, they do not have access to the raw data to confirm or refute the data provided, nor would they be expected to identify such confounding in the same manner using transmission data from the PPG sensor. Follow-up studies or care plans must be developed that account for this inherent uncertainty. Second, companies have generally sought to expand the range of the derived clinical measures to include many that may not fall in the purview of the treating clinical team. For example, in September 2018, Apple released the fall detection measure within its Apple Watch Series 4, which was designed to alert users and responders to the possibility of a fall leading to loss of consciousness. A clinic seeking to implement monitoring using wearables might find itself in the situation of needing to handle this information if it comes from a patient who is enrolled. In less extreme situations, clinicians focused on wearables for arrhythmia detection might find themselves involved in discussions about sleep patterns or stress and anxiety levels that have been derived from the same sensors used to detect AF. Within an environment of increasing competition between makers of wearables to add clinical measures to their respective device, it is expected that clinics will need to develop policies or guidelines on what information can be useful clinically and how they will handle information that may or may not be clinically valid or actionable.

Appropriate reimbursement for managing wearable device information must be included in the health care system and is a prerequisite for widespread, effective use. Electrophysiology and cardiac implantable electronic device clinics have existing infrastructures for remote monitoring and evaluation of external ambulatory event monitors. Depending on staffing availability within these clinics, incorporating data flow from consumer-grade devices may primarily require added person-hours within these clinics. The direct cost-effectiveness of incorporating wearable monitors into these clinics can be difficult to ascertain. However, factors to consider include reductions in the cost of hospitalization and subsequent care for early detection of AF, leading to timely anticoagulation and thus lowering the risk of stroke.

Simultaneously, patient costs vary widely depending on the devices being used. Currently, the 1-lead Kardia device is $79. The basic Apple Watch Series 9 is $399. Others (eg, Fitbit, Samsung, and Withings) are comparable with or slightly less expensive than the Apple Watch. Currently, there is no systematic way to alleviate the cost of the devices through the patients’ coverage. Certain centers report success with the use of the patients’ Flexible Spending Accounts or Health Savings Accounts. Others (eg, University of Michigan) internally issued Kardia devices for patients who underwent AF ablations at no cost as long as the patients transmitted a minimum number of recordings within a defined period of time.

Many insurance companies in the United States (eg, United, Aetna, Cigna, Anthem BlueCross BlueShield, and Humana) have subsidized programs for the Apple Watch or Fitbit as long as the patients are enrolled in their wellness programs (eg, Motion, Attain, Active&Fit, Blue365, and Go365) and complete or maintain a certain number of hours of physical activities over a certain period of time. This is another viable mechanism to support patients with their home monitoring needs.

Conclusion

This white paper summarizes the perspectives and recommendations of members of the Heart Rhythm Society Digital Health Committee on integrating wearable monitors into clinical practice through a patient-centered, indication-specific, and evidence-guided approach. Owing to the dynamic nature of monitoring technologies, data processing, and clinical management, these recommendations should serve as an organizing framework that continuously responds to technological innovation and advances in clinical research. In the future, end-to-end encrypted data streams between patient wearables and remote monitoring clinics with automated interpretation and robust reimbursement schemes can serve to accelerate guideline-directed cardiac rhythm care, improve patient engagement, and reduce health care costs.
