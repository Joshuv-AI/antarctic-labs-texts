Age‐Friendly Healthcare: An Evolutionary Concept Analysis
Authors: Anna Zisberg, Nosaiba Rayan‐Gharra, Alexandra Danial‐Saad, Amos Rogozinski, Paule‐Sarah Fraiman, Dikla Segel‐Karpas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsAging populations require adapting healthcare systems for older adult's specific needs. Numerous initiatives to improve older‐patient care have emerged, but the field lacks a unified framework. The current study aims to provide a systematic concept analysis of ‘age‐friendly healthcare’, examining its characteristics, components and structure.DesignRodger's evolutionary concept analysis.Data SourcesSearches were conducted in ProQuest, CINAHL, PubMed and Scopus databases between November 2022 and October 2023, utilising the PRISMA 2020 reporting checklist.MethodsA literature search using specific terms relevant to age‐friendly healthcare retrieved 1407 articles. After screening for duplicates and relevance, 140 articles were examined for eligibility based on inclusion criteria for age‐friendly care, language and full‐text availability. Following full‐text screening, 65 articles were included for data extraction by multiple researchers to synthesise theoretical, methodological and design elements.ResultsOur findings highlight key attributes of age‐friendly healthcare: Respect for older adults' autonomy and needs; leadership and organisational knowledge and support; Proactive policies and processes of care; holistic care environments; and communication and follow‐up with awareness of challenges and barriers as well as prioritisation of continuity‐of‐care.ConclusionThe concept of age‐friendly healthcare is still developing, with much research focused on development and implementation rather than evaluation of real‐world patient and health‐system outcomes. Our analysis of the concept may help unify the field and clarify future research directions through identification of areas requiring further study and enable development of improved practices and policies for implementing age‐friendly healthcare in a variety of settings.No Patient or Public ContributionThis concept analysis did not include any patient or public involvement.Reporting MethodThis study utilised the PRISMA reporting checklist.

Introduction

Aging global populations, particularly in developed countries, have drawn attention to the increasing need to adapt healthcare environments, including both primary care and acute hospital care, to the specific needs of older adults. There is a growing need for age‐appropriate care for older adults that focuses on multimorbidity, continuity of care, and functional outcomes, while also prioritising the autonomy and goals of older adults and their families (Lesser et al. 2022). Current healthcare systems are mostly designed to care for relatively young and single‐disease patients (Chiou and Chen 2009; Liu et al. 2023; Ungar et al. 2024) and have often proven inadequate or unable to meet the specific healthcare needs of older adults (Zisberg et al. 2022). Navigating through contemporary healthcare structures has substantial challenges for older adults and their families or caregivers, a situation which has only been further exacerbated in the aftermath of the COVID‐19 pandemic (Cacchione 2020; Rojo‐Perez et al. 2022; Sadarangani et al. 2022). Notably, specialisation in geriatrics receives suboptimal priority from health systems and professionals (general practitioners, nurse practitioners, social workers), leaving a significant gap as the bulk of older adult care falls under the purview of general health providers (Ungar et al. 2024; Waters et al. 2022). Furthermore, evaluations of healthcare providers' attitudes and knowledge have demonstrated poor results, indicating significant insufficiencies in healthcare providers' education concerning the needs of older adults (Burhenn, Ferrell, and Johnson 2016; Schapmire et al. 2018; World Health Organization 2017). In response to these pressing challenges, there is a growing recognition of the need to meet the distinctive needs of older adults in modern healthcare systems.

Background

The Development of ‘Age‐Friendly Care’

The term age‐appropriate care or age‐friendly care has been used to describe a variety of multifactorial principles and approaches by which hospitals, primary care providers, and other healthcare professionals, institutions and allied health professionals can improve patient and family care for older adults. This includes reducing healthcare‐associated harm and complications that adversely and disproportionately affect older adults (Ungar et al. 2024; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014; World Health Organization 2008, 2015). Numerous similar terms, such as ‘elder‐friendly care’, and a variety of plans and guidelines to improve healthcare systems' readiness to meet the needs of ageing populations have been proposed. As early as 2002 the World Health Organization (WHO) advocated for the creation of an ‘Age Friendly Hospital Network’ (Kuo and Chen 2019). The WHO's Global strategy and action plan on aging and health, adopted in 2016, includes the development of age‐friendly environments in both the community and the healthcare setting as one of its five strategic objectives (World Health Organization 2017). Likewise, a strategic goal of the United Nations' Decade of Action on Healthy Aging (2021–2030) is to ‘align health systems to the needs of older populations’ (United Nations 2020). However, a distinction can be made between community‐focused age‐friendly initiatives, such as those focusing on age‐friendly cities, public access, design, architecture, services and accessibility in public and community spaces, and those age‐friendly models and initiatives which focus on healthcare systems more specifically (World Health Organization 2017; Liebzeit et al. 2023). Innovative models for the development, promotion and implementation of various approaches to age‐friendly care have been developed and tested within various care settings, with many focused on acute in‐patient hospitalisation (Huang, Larente, and Morais 2011; Parke and Stevenson 1999; Parke et al. 2014; Rashmi, Kasthuri, and Rodrigues 2016; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014).

Age‐friendly hospital initiatives have emerged from various sectors, including professional groups, NGOs, academic institutions, individual hospitals and health organisations. One early example was the International Network of Health Promoting Hospitals and Health Services (HPH), established in the 1990s (World Health Organization and Regional Office for Europe 2007). The Canadian healthcare system pioneered other early initiatives such as British Columbia's ‘Elder‐Friendly Hospital’ in 1997, aimed at creating a healthcare culture that ‘will (1) recognize the risk of adverse consequences for older adults when they are admitted; and (2) work to predict and prevent remediable problems that may occur when the hospital fulfills its responsibility to provide acute and episodic healthcare services’ (Parke and Stevenson 1999). Likewise, Ontario's 2010 Senior Friendly Hospital Framework (SFHF) established age‐friendly healthcare guidelines across five domains (organisational support, processes of care, emotional and behavioural environment, ethics in care and research and physical environment) and addressed clinical priorities, including functional decline, delirium and care‐transitions for hospitalised older adults (Tsang et al. 2018). Additional domains were later added (Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014) solidifying the framework's comprehensive approach to enhancing age‐friendly healthcare. The similar Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) model is primarily focused on preventing hospital‐associated delirium and functional decline (Hshieh et al. 2018).

Another foundational model for the development of age‐friendly healthcare was Acute Care for the Elderly (ACE) units, which aim to prevent functional decline and other harmful sequela and iatrogenic conditions associated with hospitalisation (Fox et al. 2013; Flood et al. 2018). Initially developed and tested in the 1990s, it created separate, dedicated inpatient units only for older adults. ACE units were designed to meet older adults' unique medical and environmental needs through utilisation of clinical best practices. These included physical and environmental adaptations not present in general units different intake and discharge protocols, emphasis on interdisciplinary care, and specialised clinical standards (Landefeld et al. 1995). The impact of ACE units on patient outcomes and their influence on other age‐friendly healthcare models has been widely documented, including in recent systematic reviews, emphasising the important foundational status of ACE units to the development of age‐friendly healthcare as a unique concept, and the incorporation of ACE best practices into broader age‐friendly healthcare (Fox et al. 2012, 2013; Flood et al. 2018; Norman and Sinha 2022; Rogers et al. 2022).

The Nurses Improving Care for Health systems Elders (NICHE) programme was created in 1992 by New York University and influenced by the Yale Geriatric Care Model (Inouye et al. 1993). NICHE assists nursing and interdisciplinary teams' organisational goals through provision of education, support and resources for geriatric care and meeting older patients' needs. NICHE promotes evidence‐based clinical interventions and has been successful in enhancing the quality of care for older adult patients (Inouye et al. 1993; Rosenfeld and Glassman 2019). NICHE focuses on training unit‐based nurses and gerontology nurse specialists to become leaders for age‐friendly healthcare changes within their organisations. It also includes assessment and outcome measures (Boltz et al. 2008; Fulmer et al. 2019; Gilmartin 2023; Rosenfeld and Glassman 2019; Rosenfeld, Kwok, and Glassman 2018; Squires et al. 2021; Zisberg et al. 2022).

The 4Ms model of age‐friendly healthcare (Medication, Mentation, Mobility and what Matters) is a widely used approach and may have contributed to the popularisation of the term ‘age‐friendly’ in recent years. The model was developed as part of the larger Age‐Friendly Health Systems (AFHS) initiative by a cooperative research consortium including the John A. Hartford Foundation, the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), the American Hospital Association (AHA) and the Catholic Health Association. It builds on existing models and frameworks meant to address geriatric care across all healthcare settings (Lesser et al. 2022). This approach is a synthesis of many earlier elements and components of age‐friendly models and theories. Programme developers have already certified > 600 US hospitals as ‘age‐friendly health systems’ using the 4Ms and > 2000 further hospitals seeking certification (Emery‐Tiburcio et al. 2021).

Each of these models and approaches incorporates numerous aspects and applications of age‐friendly care. However, the breadth of scope of the field may in‐and‐of‐itself inadvertently create confusion or lack of clarity around the concept of age‐friendly care. It should be noted that while age‐friendly concepts may have initially begun with a focus on community care (as per early WHO guidelines), many current age‐friendly programmes and initiatives began at the hospital level and focused on inpatient care, with some later adapted or widened to include community care, such as the 4Ms model and the Canadian SFHF. Many of these programmes have since come full circle, with expanded efforts to improve suitability for entire healthcare systems, to improve community care, continuity of care and other components. However, these expanded applications have led to a degree of fragmentation with a wide range of definitions and approaches and thus a need to unify the understanding of the concept. As such, this paper delves into the concept of age‐friendly healthcare to unravel its theoretical foundations and practical implications in addressing the evolving healthcare needs of globally expanding older adult populations.

The Study

Aim

The purpose of this study was to define and explain the concept of age‐friendly healthcare using Rodger's evolutionary concept analysis model to examine the essential characteristics, components, structure and contextual usage of the concept and to synthesise the disparate definitions and applications of the concept into a more comprehensive conceptual framework.

Design

A concept can be defined as the mental image or understanding of a phenomenon, idea or construct and represents the building blocks of a theory (Walker and Avant 2005). This study was conducted using Rodger's ‘evolutionary cycle’ approach to concept analysis (Rodgers and Kathleen 2000). Rodger's model utilises a non‐linear methodology which may double back and circle between the various steps in the process. It acknowledges the changing, developing and evolving nature of a given concept over time, which may vary according to time, setting and use (Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm 2010). This approach is particularly useful when examining a widely used term like ‘age‐friendly’ care, for which there has not been a single agreed‐upon definition or description. Rodger's model has thus been applied to a range of nursing and healthcare concepts and can be especially useful for healthcare topics that are complex or difficult to define (Connor et al. 2023; Jackson and Sadler 2022).

Rodger's model incorporates several well‐defined steps: The process begins with the identification of a concept to study and its surrogate terms (terms or names which have been used synonymously or interchangeably with the concept), related terms (similar terms or models which share some characteristics with the concept but lack key features or differ in significant ways), and a search for all uses and contexts of the term. Researchers will also identify the key components of the concept: (1) Antecedents: elements or factors that precede the concept or are prerequisites or precursors for it, or which have been previously associated with it; (2) Attributes: essential and key defining characteristics of the concept; and (3) Consequences: the result or impact of the concept implementation. From these components a new definition of the concept can be identified and developed (Connor et al. 2023; Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm 2010). The use of Rodger's model allows for a comprehensive understanding of age‐friendly healthcare and will aid in organising existing knowledge in the field. Through this approach, we aim to develop a unified framework and definition of the concept.

Data Sources

Search Terms

An initial literature search was conducted in ProQuest CINAHL.

PubMed and Scopus databases using the following search terms: ‘age‐friendly’ or ‘aged friendly’ or ‘senior friendly’ or ‘elder friendly’ or ‘dementia friendly’ or ‘older adult friendly’ or ‘older people friendly’ or ‘geriatric friendly’ and ‘care’ or ‘system’ or ‘systems’ or ‘health system*’ or ‘healthcare’ or ‘health care’ or ‘health service*’ or ‘social service’ or ‘long‐term care’ or ‘assisted living’ or ‘in‐home care’ or ‘nursing home’ or ‘primary care’ or ‘hospital’ or ‘hospitals’ or ‘acute care’ or ‘in‐patient care’ or ‘in‐home care’ or ‘long term care facility’. We excluded the terms ‘city’ or ‘cities’ or ‘urban’ or ‘communities’ or ‘workplace’ or ‘work place’ or ‘university’ or ‘universities’ or ‘urban planning’.

The study was publicly pre‐registered with the Center for Open Science OSF registry (Registration DOI https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/U5T2Q). The study utilised the PRISMA 2020 reporting checklist. See supplementary file 1.

Search Outcomes

The initial screening procedure yielded 1407 articles. Using EndNote reference management software we eliminated 651 duplicate articles, leaving a total of 756 articles for title and abstract screening (see Figure 1 for breakdown by database). Of these, an additional 59 duplicate articles were manually identified and excluded. A further 557 articles were removed due to irrelevance, language (articles not in English) or due to missing abstracts or full text. The remaining 140 articles were examined by the research group to determine eligibility for inclusion in the concept analysis.

Inclusion criteria were articles specifically relating to age‐friendly healthcare, written in English, and with full text available. Exclusion criteria were articles that focused on topics that were beyond the scope of this analysis (i.e., referring to policies for an entire country generally, or conversely referring to a very specific and narrow aspect of age‐friendly care, or very specific medical topics and procedures). This includes excluding age‐friendly topics outside of healthcare, such as age‐friendly technology, architecture, workplaces, city planning/urban development, public transportation, or age‐friendly government and social support programmes outside of the healthcare system. We also excluded articles specifically on age‐friendly dentistry and dementia‐friendly care, as these were identified by the group as being distinct concepts. We further excluded age‐friendly models that were not based on a fully integrated approach, such as ACE units, geriatric inpatient units, segregated age‐friendly ER sub‐departments and other approaches which segregate older adults into separate care structures outside of the general provision of care.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Full‐text screening of 140 articles was conducted using the Covidence systematic review tool, with access provided via an institutional licence. Articles were assessed based on quality, relevance to the concept and according to Rodger's model for evolutionary concept analysis (Rodgers and Kathleen 2000). The initial full‐text screening revealed a degree of repetition in the literature, in which numerous papers covered the same topic or model (i.e., for multiple different audiences or specialties) using the same operational or theoretical definitions of age‐friendly care. As the concept analysis model calls for researchers to examine all uses of the concept and given the large number of articles included in our full‐text screening sample, it was decided to exclude certain articles based on repetition of content which did not add new uses, definitions or additional information to the concept analysis. A minimum of two researchers assessed each article and voted for inclusion or exclusion based on the study inclusion/exclusion criteria. All researchers were experts in older adult healthcare from the fields of nursing, gerontology or occupational therapy. As per the Covidence software, in cases of disagreement, a third researcher casts a ‘tie‐breaker’ vote, with the other researchers having an opportunity to review their initial assessment. This resulted in the exclusion of 75 articles. The remaining 65 articles underwent data extraction using a data extraction table created by the researchers, which tracked study characteristics, methodological and design elements (see Tables 1, 2, 3) as well as allowing for thematic analysis of the key components of Rodger's evolutionary concept analysis method.

A majority of the papers were from English‐speaking countries, including Canada (Cetin‐Sahin et al. 2021), the United States (i.e., Cacchione 2020; Guth et al. 2020; Herbert and Sibley 2022; Kochar et al. 2022; Rogers et al. 2022; Wolfe et al. 2021), Australia and Great Britain (Schwartz et al. 2020). It is notable that most of the studies took place in acute care hospital inpatient or emergency care settings, despite some of the earliest descriptions of age‐friendly healthcare having specifically described the primary or community care setting (World Health Organization 2008).

Definition

Age‐friendly care is a comprehensive, integrated approach to healthcare that intends to identify the specific and unique needs of older adults (ages 65 and above) across various healthcare settings, to adapt to, and meet their special needs, to promote optimal function and well‐being in this population, to reduce barriers to optimal care and to prevent complications of medical care. It emphasises patient choice, autonomy and active involvement of older adults in their own healthcare and promotes patient participation, choice and agency in the care process. Age‐friendly healthcare integrates proactive policies, supportive and holistic care environments, effective communication, continuity of care and preventative care to meet these needs.

Surrogate Terms

Surrogate terms are conceptualised as specific words or phrases that describe a concept, analogous or synonymous to the primary term of interest (Rodgers and Kathleen 2000; Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm 2010). Several surrogate terms for age‐friendly healthcare were found. These include: ‘geriatricizing’ (Casey et al. 2020), ‘senior‐friendly’ (Kelley et al. 2011) and ‘elder‐friendly’ (Cetin‐Sahin et al. 2021; Parke and Stevenson 1999). Furthermore, several models and frameworks have been identified that align with this concept, such as the ‘Senior Friendly Hospital Accelerating Change Together in Ontario (SFHACTION)’ (Ryan, Zeh, Tsang, et al. 2022); ‘Senior‐friendly Hospital Framework’ (D. Ryan et al. 2011); ‘Age‐friendly Hospital (AFH)’ (Chiou and Chen 2009); ‘Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP) (Hshieh et al. 2018; Inouye et al. 1993)’; ‘Age‐Friendly Health System’ (Lesser et al. 2022); ‘Age‐Friendly Public Health Systems’ (Wolfe et al. 2021); the “‘8Ms’ Framework” (Bhattacharyya et al. 2023); and Collaboration for Hospitalised Elders Reducing the Impact of Stays in Hospital (CHERISH) (Mudge et al. 2022) and others.

Related Terms

The term ‘age‐friendly’ has been applied in a wide range of contexts, including age‐friendly pharmacies (Cameron and Morris 2008; Malet‐Larrea et al. 2019), age‐friendly public transportation (Reinhard et al. 2018; Steels 2015), age‐friendly technology and apps (Bernardo et al. 2022; Duh et al. 2010; Kavandi and Jaana 2020), age‐friendly dentistry (Marchini 2023) and more. While closely related to age‐friendly healthcare and potentially tangential to its implementation, these related terms remain distinct from the concept. Notable related cases include the following.

Age‐Friendly Cities and Communities

The development of environments and communities designed to meet the needs of older adults has been prioritised by many governmental agencies and international organisations, including the European Union, the WHO, the US Environmental Protection Agency and more (Steels 2015). In 2002 the WHO first published a policy framework on active aging which emphasised the need for age‐friendly design and environments as a key component of public policy. This was followed in 2007 by the publication of the WHO's widely influential and implemented guide to age‐friendly cities (Liu et al. 2023; World Health Organization and Regional Office for Europe 2007). Age‐friendly communities differ from age‐friendly healthcare in that they take a broader approach and consider a far wider range of components and characteristics which impact older adults' lives. Their scope includes but is much larger than, and not limited to, healthcare. This includes social and economic factors, such as addressing that older adults may experience higher rates of poverty, loneliness and isolation. They also address environmental, structural and many other factors that impact older adults' overall quality of life, civic engagement, activity and well‐being within the community setting (Liebzeit et al. 2023).

Dementia‐Friendly

Dementia‐friendly care originally developed as an extension of the movement for age‐friendly environments, design, and communities. It promotes the creation of environments and support structures both in communities and within healthcare settings that support independence and accessibility (Innes, Smith, and Bushell 2021; Mitchell and Burton 2010; Munsterman et al. 2024). Dementia‐friendly healthcare is aware of, and proactive towards, the implications of dementia on patients' healthcare outcomes, including patients' ability to manage their own care. It promotes patient and caregiver empowerment, inclusion and agency in healthcare decision‐making and treatment (Innes, Smith, and Bushell 2021; Shannon, Bail, and Neville 2019). The concept recognises an increase in patients with dementia and highlights the need to provide medical care specifically tailored to the needs of this growing population (Shannon, Bail, and Neville 2019; van Corven et al. 2022). A 2024 concept analysis identified five domains of dementia‐friendly healthcare which overlap with some elements of age‐friendly healthcare and proposed a conceptual definition for the term. However, neither these domains nor the proposed definition are specific for the care for older adults (Munsterman et al. 2024). Although age‐friendly care includes and prioritises the needs of older adults with dementia or cognitive decline, it applies only to older adults. Conversely, dementia‐friendly care is not limited by patient age; younger adults and even children may also suffer from forms of dementia or cognitive decline. As patients of any age can be included within the scope of dementia‐friendly care, it is thereby distinguished as a distinct though related concept to age‐friendly healthcare, both in our analysis and in the recent analysis by (Munsterman et al. 2024).

Antecedents

Antecedents are factors that preceded the existence or application of the concept and may include elements that must be addressed in order for the concept to be applied or brought into fruition (Jackson and Sadler 2022; Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm 2010). Our analysis identified several key antecedents which must be recognised, valued and prioritised as a prerequisite for age‐friendly healthcare:

Older Adults Requiring Healthcare Services

A primary driver for the development of age‐friendly care models and practices has been ageing populations (primarily defined as adults over age 65). Healthcare systems and institutions facing increasing numbers of older adults with specific and unique healthcare needs have been a central impetus for the development of the field (Wolfe et al. 2021).

Leadership, Organisational Readiness and Support for Age‐Friendly Care

To provide effective and appropriate care for older adults, organisations and leaders within healthcare systems must first be cognisant of, recognise and support the premise that older adults have specific healthcare needs and preferences which differ from other patient populations (Dolansky et al. 2023; Hanson et al. 2017; Parke et al. 2014; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014).

Age‐Friendly Healthcare Knowledge, Education and Research

Age‐friendly healthcare cannot be implemented without sufficient knowledge and education on the needs of older adult patients among healthcare providers and other health system stakeholders. Insufficient knowledge and unfamiliarity with age‐friendly healthcare models have been identified as barriers to age‐friendly healthcare (Lesser et al. 2022). Knowledge prerequisites may include specialised staff education and training in geriatrics (Chiou and Chen 2009; Kuntz et al. 2023; Schwartz et al. 2020; Waters et al. 2022), interprofessional training to improve care for older adults (Schwartz et al. 2020) and knowledge of age‐friendly care protocols (Cetin‐Sahin et al. 2021). Prior education is required to ensure healthcare professionals have the confidence, knowledge and skills necessary to provide age‐friendly care. Likewise, sufficient pre‐existing research into older adults' healthcare is necessary for the formulation and implementation of age‐friendly healthcare (Ryan, Zeh, Tsang, et al. 2022; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014).

Environmental Suitability

Age‐friendly healthcare can only occur when the environment of the healthcare setting is conducive to the needs of older adults (Chiou and Chen 2009; Hanson et al. 2017; Woo, Mak, and Yeung 2013). An age‐friendly healthcare environment should be ‘appropriate, pleasant, supportive, respectful, and accessible’ (Kuo and Chen 2019). This includes the physical design, architecture and accessibility. It also encompasses the emotional, social, cultural and behavioural components, including issues such as language, socio‐economic status and equity disparities, ageism and attitudes of staff and caregivers towards older adults, technological accessibility and usability (Santos, Lima, and Zucatti 2016).

Recognition of and Appropriate Care Processes for Older Adults' Health Needs

Age‐friendly healthcare prerequisites the development and implementation of care processes and practices (i.e., mobility promotion, medication reconciliation, etc.) that address the specific and critical healthcare needs of older adults. Age‐friendly healthcare must therefore be predicated on recognition and understanding of the complex and challenging healthcare needs of older adults, as well as care processes and policies that specifically address these needs (Kochar et al. 2022; Parke et al. 2014).

Respect for Older Adults' Autonomy and Needs

Age‐friendly healthcare places fundamental importance on respect for older adults at all stages of the care process. It values patients' active and collaborative participation in healthcare decisions (Herbert and Sibley 2022; Morgan et al. 2022). This is evidenced in several age‐friendly care models, for example, the 4Ms model includes the ‘what matters’ component (Carbonell et al. 2022b; Lesser et al. 2022), and the Canadian Senior Friendly Hospital model emphasises respect for older adults' patient experience, independence and needs (Ryan, Zeh, Tsang, et al. 2022; Tsang et al. 2018). Age‐friendly healthcare is characterised by prioritisation of older adults' care goals, autonomy and values as related to their healthcare (Cogan et al. 2023; Dolansky et al. 2021; Guth et al. 2020; Herbert and Sibley 2022; Lesser et al. 2022; Pohnert et al. 2023; Powers and Penaranda 2022). Critically, age‐friendly healthcare works to reduce and eliminate effects of ageism, prejudice and negative attitudes about older adults among healthcare providers (Huang, Larente, and Morais 2011; Issahaku and Sulemana 2021; Morgan et al. 2022; Motsohi et al. 2020; Santos, Lima, and Zucatti 2016; Zisberg et al. 2022).

Leadership and Organisational Knowledge and Support

Age‐friendly healthcare is characterised by organisations and leadership which recognise, are knowledgeable of and support age‐friendly practices and policies (Farrell et al. 2023; Hanson et al. 2017; Ryan, Zeh, Tsang, et al. 2022; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014). This can apply to the macro‐level, such as governmental health policies, accreditation systems, large healthcare systems or international organisations such as WHO guidelines, and at the micro‐level, with individual hospitals, clinics or other facilities and providers recognising the need and value of age‐friendly healthcare. In practice, this may include prioritising staffing and patient‐to‐staff ratios, education, interprofessional cooperation, resource availability and resource management for age‐friendly healthcare (Dolansky et al. 2023; Hanson et al. 2017; Kuo and Chen 2019; Parke et al. 2014; Powers and Penaranda 2022; Severance et al. 2023; Shih et al. 2022; Waters et al. 2022). Age‐friendly healthcare should also prioritise and encourage further academic research into the unique health needs of older adults (Kochar et al. 2022) to facilitate further improvements in knowledge at all levels of the healthcare system.

Proactive Policies and Processes of Care

Age‐friendly proactive policies and processes of care are adapted to the special needs and concerns of older adult patients. Chief among these are medication reconciliation and polypharmacy (Berish et al. 2023; Guth et al. 2020; Kuntz et al. 2023; Morgan et al. 2022; Severance et al. 2023; Zonsius et al. 2022), multiple morbidities (Chodos, Cassel, and Ritchie 2020; Soley‐Bori et al. 2021), disease complexity (Chodos, Cassel, and Ritchie 2020; Coll et al. 2022), patient frailty and functional decline (Inouye et al. 1993; Kim et al. 2017; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014) cognitive decline, dementia and delirium (Huang, Larente, and Morais 2011; Kuntz et al. 2023; Mack et al. 2022; Mudge et al. 2022; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014), mobility challenges, fall prevention, skin integrity promotion and other issues (Kuo and Chen 2019; Woo, Mak, and Yeung 2013). Age‐friendly care also prioritises preventative care and health maintenance, with an emphasis on safeguards to prevent or reduce iatrogenic harm and complications among older adults (i.e., falls, nosocomial and hospital‐acquired injuries, overprescribing, etc.) as well as mobility, nutrition, social and emotional needs and health promotion.

Holistic Care Environments

Age‐friendly healthcare is characterised by holistic care environments which meet the physical, social, cultural, behavioural and technological needs of older adults. The holistic age‐friendly care environment is multifaceted and complex. For example, a holistic healthcare setting is characterised by physical accessibility to older adults, with consideration for mobility limitations, visibility of signage and resources, physical distances patients must walk, adequate seating, fall prevention infrastructure like handrails and other physical accomadations (Chiou and Chen 2009; Kelley et al. 2011; Kim et al. 2017; Morgan et al. 2022; Parke et al. 2014; Ungar et al. 2024). Age‐friendly holistic care environments also address usability and uptake issues related to healthcare technology and the use of technology to improve age‐friendly healthcare and increase older adults' access to healthcare resources (Adler‐Milstein et al. 2020; Cross and Adler‐Milstein 2022; McQuown et al. 2023; Southerland et al. 2023).

Communication and Follow‐Up

Age‐friendly healthcare integrates the needs of older adult patients into the entire healthcare system through effective and responsive communication across the full range of healthcare settings and allied health professionals. Continuity of care and follow‐up is facilitated through interprofessional cooperation, including case management, discharge planning to ease transitions between healthcare settings, and an awareness of barriers and obstacles faced by older adult patients. Communication and collaborative work between varied professionals and settings underpin this attribute (Cacchione 2020). Age‐friendly healthcare systems promote new technologies to improve communication, documentation, follow‐up and smooth transitions between care environments, while also addressing potential challenges that older adults might face in the utilisation of such means (Adler‐Milstein et al. 2020; Chiou and Chen 2009; Cross and Adler‐Milstein 2022; Farrell et al. 2023; Hanson et al. 2017; McQuown et al. 2023; Southerland et al. 2023).

Consequences

Within Rodger's concept analysis model, consequences are defined as the results or impact of the concept or the result of its implementation. Consequences can include measurable outcomes such as performance metrics. They may also represent observable implications or sequela which take shape or come into being as a result of the concept (Jackson and Sadler 2022; Tofthagen and Fagerstrøm 2010).

In the initial screen, 53 articles were identified as potentially including outcomes. However, these were often focused on the development and adoption of age‐friendly guidelines and principles to improve patient care, rather than specifically offering possible areas in which outcomes could be measured. It should also be noted that some researchers have specifically identified the lack of sufficient data and outcome measures as a problem in the field that should be addressed (Burke et al. 2022; Pearce, Rogers‐Clark, and Doolan 2011; Sadarangani et al. 2022). This need is further emphasised by studies that have developed tools and measurements to assess elements of age‐friendly healthcare within a given environment, such as the Geriatric Institute Assessment Profile (GIAP) developed for use with the NICHE programme, and others (McCusker et al. 2012; Rashmi, Kasthuri, and Rodrigues 2016; Shih et al. 2021; Zisberg et al. 2022).

Organisational outcomes of age‐friendly healthcare were measured either at the hospital level, such as total direct costs (Breda et al. 2023; Leite et al. 2022), or more commonly as process‐related outcomes. These included fall‐risk screening (Casey et al. 2020), care evaluation (McCusker et al. 2012), improvement in staff communication, simplification of medication regimens (Guth et al. 2020), advanced care planning, mobility goals (Berish et al. 2023) and documentation of specific age‐friendly healthcare actions, such as documenting 4Ms model components in healthcare records (Powers and Penaranda 2022; Southerland et al. 2023). Improvements in staff education, knowledge levels and attitudes were also identified as measurable outcomes of age‐friendly healthcare (Berish et al. 2023; Bhattacharyya et al. 2023; Burke et al. 2022; Cetin‐Sahin et al. 2021; Farrell et al. 2023; Herbert and Sibley 2022; Kelley et al. 2011; Kuntz et al. 2023; Ryan, Zeh, Rozo, et al. 2022; Santos, Lima, and Zucatti 2016; Schwartz et al. 2020; Severance et al. 2023). A total of 15 studies reported measures of patient outcomes, which ranged from patient experiences and perceptions (i.e., quality of life and mental health, satisfaction and self‐perception of the stay) to measures of clinical outcomes such as fall risk (Parke et al. 2014; Swauger and Tomlin 2002), length of stay, readmission rates (Breda et al. 2023; Leite et al. 2022), delirium prevalence (Mudge et al. 2022), incontinence, dementia, functional decline and catheter use (Berish et al. 2023; Mudge et al. 2022; Swauger and Tomlin 2002) and similar. Fourteen studies examined healthcare professionals' outcomes, including knowledge and perspectives (i.e., Casey et al. 2020; Hanson et al. 2017; Lesser et al. 2022; Schwartz et al. 2020), compliance with age‐friendly policies or processes and documentation (i.e., Adler‐Milstein et al. 2020; Powers and Penaranda 2022) and other measures. Only two studies provided outcome measures for healthcare students (Herbert and Sibley 2022; Severance et al. 2023), despite the importance of age‐friendly staff knowledge being widely emphasised. An additional eight studies measured outcomes from mixed populations, including healthcare staff, patients, family caregivers or other stakeholders (i.e., Dolansky et al. 2021; Kelley et al. 2011; Rashmi, Kasthuri, and Rodrigues 2016).

Age‐friendly healthcare outcomes can also be examined through systematic reviews (Pearce, Rogers‐Clark, and Doolan 2011) or by looking at entire health systems (Geng et al. 2022; Shih et al. 2022). For example, a review by Tsang et al. (2018) on the implementation of the Canadian Senior Friendly Hospital (SFH) initiative found that SFHs resulted in improvements across the province in a number of elements across all SFH domains (Tsang et al. 2018). Attempts to measure outcomes according to a specific model have had mixed results (Powers and Penaranda 2022). A scoping review examined the outcomes of the 4Ms model on medication (overprescribing), mobility and mentation but noted that none of the 37 studies included in the review assessed the ‘mentation’ M, indicating that even when applied in practical settings it may be difficult to assess measurable outcomes for current age‐friendly healthcare models (Wang et al. 2023). Similarly, a study on implementation of the NICHE programme at a single hospital was able to identify measurable outcomes only in some specific factors, such as restraint use reduction (Swauger and Tomlin 2002; Figure 2).

Discussion

Our analysis crafted a comprehensive definition of age‐friendly healthcare which reflects the central principles of several models and approaches. This is a synergistic definition which builds on the existing literature, in which most definitions or theoretical conceptions of age‐friendly healthcare were specifically embedded within their own models. Pre‐existing definitions were thus constrained by the scope and application of their respective models, each unable to fully explain the overall concept of age‐friendly healthcare as applied across diverse healthcare settings and needs within broader healthcare systems, including hospitals, long‐term care and community‐based primary care. While existing models all incorporate elements of age‐friendly healthcare, they conceptually differ in their objective, scope or other fundamental components. This resulted in a fragmented understanding of age‐friendly healthcare without an overall unifying definition. This lack of clarity may impact the effectiveness of age‐friendly healthcare models when implemented (Adler‐Milstein et al. 2020; Geng et al. 2022). Establishing an overarching definition for age‐friendly healthcare integrated into multiple care environments may streamline identification and implementation of optimal clinical practices tailored specifically to its principles. Our concept analysis has successfully identified the key antecedents, attributes and consequences of age‐friendly care. These elements have been utilised to craft a comprehensive definition that not only elucidates the concept but also distinguishes it from other related or similar concepts previously discussed.

In terms of the methodology, Rodger's evolutionary concept analysis model incorporates a non‐linear approach. We see the strength and applicability of this model for examining age‐friendly care as a concept, as we observed overlap and non‐linear relationships between many of the antecedents, attributes and consequences of the concept in both practice and theory. Our analysis has identified several elements which fall into multiple categories within the theoretical framework. These are elements which, perhaps counterintuitively, must already either be in place or be prioritised as prerequisites for age‐friendly healthcare, thereby making them antecedents to the concept but also represent essential attributes as well as consequences of age‐friendly care.

A prime example of this is a supportive, holistic and suitable environment, which we identified as functioning not only as an attribute but also as an antecedent and a consequence of an age‐friendly approach to healthcare. Our analysis found that age‐friendly healthcare requires older adult patients to be fully integrated into healthcare environments that meet their physical, emotional, social and cultural needs (Carbonell et al. 2022a; Parke and Stevenson 1999; Powers and Penaranda 2022) and requires all‐inclusive integration of appropriate environments into the wider healthcare system as a whole. As such, a holistic age‐friendly environment is an antecedent in that it must be in place for age‐friendly healthcare to exist but also represents an essential attribute of that care. Furthermore, we also observe that the creation of suitable, supportive and holistic environments for age‐friendly healthcare (including cultural, social, physical and other components of the environment) is a consequence of age‐friendly healthcare in settings where it has not previously been prioritised (Hanson et al. 2017; Huang, Larente, and Morais 2011; Parke et al. 2014; Santos, Lima, and Zucatti 2016; Saravana‐Bawan et al. 2021; Zisberg et al. 2022). Understanding the non‐linear complexities that environments play in age‐friendly healthcare is essential for advancing age‐friendly research and interventions. The transition to an age‐friendly environment poses significant challenges for healthcare systems and institutions, and the development of suitable holistic age‐friendly environments may be impractical or logistically unworkable in many care settings, even when given theoretical priority. Complexities and challenges in adapting physical environments to meet the needs of older adults include issues such as lack of resources or funding for physical modifications, lack of organisational prioritisation and insufficient knowledge. This is a major barrier to age‐friendly care that must be addressed, and an example of just one critical step for any research, policy‐making or interventional implementation aimed at transitioning to an age‐friendly healthcare system (Hanson et al. 2017; Lehning, Smith, and Dunkle 2014; Parke et al. 2014; Rojo‐Perez et al. 2022; Saravana‐Bawan et al. 2021; Shih et al. 2021; Tsang et al. 2018; Ungar et al. 2024; Wolfe et al. 2021). Although the specific physical needs and environmental special needs may vary between care settings, these factors are applicable in all healthcare environments, including acute hospitalisation, long‐term care and primary care (Cacchione 2020; Rojo‐Perez et al. 2022; Bhattacharyya et al. 2023).

Similar non‐linear complexities also arise when considering the role of knowledge and education in age‐friendly healthcare. Successful implementation of age‐friendly healthcare in all care settings requires adequate knowledge and understanding of the healthcare needs of older adults. Insufficient levels of evidenced‐based knowledge and education have been identified as significant barriers to effective age‐friendly policies, practices and care processes (i.e., Burhenn, Ferrell, and Johnson 2016; Hanson et al. 2017; Kochar et al. 2022; Parke et al. 2014; Waters et al. 2022; Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014; World Health Organization 2015). Conversely, adequate and appropriate knowledge and education can also be seen as an attribute of age‐friendly healthcare, with staff educational and training initiatives representing an important component in many age‐friendly healthcare programmes. Furthermore, levels of knowledge and successful educational metrics on geriatric care issues may also be considered as an outcome measure when evaluating the structure and efficacy of age‐friendly healthcare systems or specific intervention models (Lesser et al. 2022; Ryan, Zeh, Tsang, et al. 2022; Severance et al. 2023; Zisberg et al. 2022). Implementation of staff education programmes can also be examined as an outcome measure at the hospital or system‐wide level, as has been done in the Canadian Senior Friendly Hospital initiative (Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014), or in the development of age‐friendly primary care networks in Taiwan (Shih et al. 2021). Taken together, this circular relationship emphasises the key role of appropriate age‐friendly education and indicates that age‐friendly healthcare systems must provide and require sufficient training for healthcare teams as a prerequisite, characteristic and consequence of their ability to work within an age‐friendly healthcare setting.

To accomplish this, healthcare educators, administrators and policymakers must examine current educational systems and certification standards for all healthcare employees. Shortages of healthcare providers with adequate training in the needs of older adults have been cited as a barrier to age‐friendly healthcare (Chodos, Cassel, and Ritchie 2020). Current educational frameworks in many countries do not provide sufficient focus and prioritisation on geriatrics and gerontology and do not include them as integral parts of the healthcare curriculum (Cetin‐Sahin et al. 2021; Farrell et al. 2023; Herbert and Sibley 2022; Santos, Lima, and Zucatti 2016). Research has shown a problematic lack of interest in specialising in geriatrics or gerontology among many healthcare students, as well as a ‘critical deficit’ of hospital staff with the necessary training and experience to provide age‐friendly healthcare (Huang, Larente, and Morais 2011). Hospitals have also reported difficulty in recruiting employees with adequate training in the field (Wong, Ryan, and Liu 2014). This may be particularly problematic in long‐term care settings, many of which face significant staff education and recruitment concerns (Bhattacharyya et al. 2023). Ageism and negative stereotypes about ageing have also been identified as barriers to age‐friendly care that must be addressed in all healthcare settings, from primary to acute care (Santos, Lima, and Zucatti 2016; Issahaku and Sulemana 2021; Motsohi et al. 2020). Improved geriatric education may help improve healthcare providers' attitudes towards older adults and potentially improve their readiness to work with older adult patients (Ross, Jennings, and Williams 2020). These complexities emphasise the interdependent and interdisciplinary nature of gerontology, in which multiple factors affect students' intentions and professionals' readiness to work with older adults or to study gerontology. Age‐friendly healthcare cannot exist if providers have neither the interest nor the education required; it is therefore critical that issues relating to age‐friendly education and the integration of gerontology and geriatrics into healthcare curriculums be more broadly addressed both in research and by policymakers and healthcare educators. For age‐friendly healthcare to be effectively integrated into all areas of a healthcare system, age‐friendly healthcare knowledge, principles, standards and policies must be incorporated into basic education frameworks and licensing requirements of all healthcare professions. While this process has begun to some degree in certain jurisdictions, there is a need for a comprehensive overhaul of how healthcare curriculums for all healthcare professions educate students on issues related to older adults, geriatrics and gerontology (Bhattacharyya et al. 2023; Breda et al. 2023; Cetin‐Sahin et al. 2021; Farrell et al. 2023; Herbert and Sibley 2022; Innes, Smith, and Bushell 2021; Kelley et al. 2011; Kuntz et al. 2023; Schwartz et al. 2020; Severance et al. 2023; Waters et al. 2022).

Taken together, our concept analysis of age‐friendly care reveals that while there is a great deal of literature in the field, it remains in a relatively early stage of development. Much of the existing literature is focused on the development of age‐friendly healthcare frameworks and criteria, with less research available on actual patient and health system outcomes. Furthermore, there has yet to emerge an agreed‐upon consensus as to a unified definition, understanding or standards of implementation for age‐friendly healthcare. Much of age‐friendly research remains focused on the acute care setting, despite some of the earliest developments of age‐friendly healthcare having been focused on primary and community care (World Health Organization 2008). Greater research focus needs to be placed on integrating age‐friendly healthcare into the entire healthcare system, with priority placed on research into primary care and transitions and continuity‐of‐care between healthcare environments. Additionally, a significant majority of scholarship in the field has been conducted in the ‘anglosphere’, with research and models from the United States and Canada predominating in the literature. This is an interesting phenomenon, particularly as populations in those nations are not ageing as quickly as populations in Europe and some parts of Asia, where healthcare systems face larger and more urgent challenges in response to rapidly ageing populations. Future research should emphasise practical aspects related to implementation of age‐friendly healthcare in all types of care settings, including resources, care processes and the development of tools and measures to better evaluate outcomes. As much of the current literature remains highly theoretical there is also a need to provide more complete and quantifiable measures of age‐friendly outcomes to improve understanding of the impact of age‐friendly healthcare models on actual clinical practice for both patients and healthcare providers.

Limitations

Age‐friendly healthcare remains a developing and rapidly expanding field, with many new articles released each year. Given the wide breadth and disunification of the field it is not possible to include all age‐friendly healthcare articles in this analysis due to the large number of search results and limits on ability to analyse the ever‐expanding body of literature on the field. This is particularly challenging as the term age‐friendly continues to be applied in a wide and expanding range of settings, with new uses frequently added. As such there will be a need for future revisions to any concept analysis conducted in such a dynamic and rapidly evolving subject. Furthermore, additional systematic review may enable greater exploration of these topics.

The role of funding and healthcare costs in making age‐friendly healthcare accessible for all older adults has been noted in the literature (Chodos, Cassel, and Ritchie 2020; Coll et al. 2022) but cannot be fully discussed within the scope of this article, as systems vary substantially between countries. As such, economic accessibility to age‐friendly healthcare has received only limited considered in this study.

Conclusions

The field of age‐friendly healthcare remains highly fragmented, with multiple competing and converging models all vying to offer an effective path towards the broad establishment of age‐friendly healthcare as a recognised and effective healthcare model. Recognising the need for a more unified understanding, this study proposes a comprehensive definition of age‐friendly healthcare which emphasises the need to identify and address the specific needs of older adults across different healthcare settings. Key components include promoting optimal function and well‐being, reducing barriers to care and preventing medical complications. Moreover, age‐friendly healthcare should prioritise patient choice, autonomy and active involvement in healthcare decisions while integrating proactive policies, supportive care environments, effective communication, continuity‐of‐care and preventative measures. The development of this more unified definition, which synergises the key characteristics of the prevailing models and theories into a more unified concept, will help advance research in the field and contribute to healthier ageing.
