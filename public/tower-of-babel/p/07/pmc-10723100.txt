A Multicenter Retrospective Database Evaluation of Pediatric Patients Diagnosed With Tinea Capitis
Authors: Lauren Mazur, Zizhong Tian, Katherine Shedlock, Adrian D Zurca, Shouhao Zhou, Neal J Thomas, Conrad Krawiec
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTinea capitis is a common pediatric superficial dermatophyte infection associated with lower socioeconomic status, overcrowded environments, and poor hygiene internationally. Nevertheless, to the authors’ knowledge, no studies in the United States have reported an association between tinea capitis diagnoses and diagnostic codes for social determinants of health (SDOH). The objectives of the present study were to analyze the diagnostic and treatment approach and frequency of SDOH diagnostic codes in order to assess the presence of racial disparities in the treatment of pediatric patients aged 0 to 18 years diagnosed with tinea capitis.MethodsThis study comprised a retrospective analysis using the TriNetX electronic health record database of de-identified pediatric tinea capitis data in ambulatory and emergency settings. The data evaluated demographics, SDOH diagnostic codes, medication codes, and procedure codes.ResultsAnalysis of 19,677 patients (17,471 [88.8%] ambulatory and 2206 [11.2%] emergency encounters) demonstrated that a low frequency of patients had a confirmatory test for tinea capitis (ie, potassium hydroxide prep or fungal culture; 5.5%), prescription for dual therapy (25.2%), or SDOH diagnostic codes (5.5%). Patients with races classified as Black (odds ratio = 0.48, 95% confidence interval = 0.41–0.57, p < 0.001) and “other” (odds ratio = 0.52, 95% confidence interval = 0.33–0.81, p = 0.004) had a lower likelihood of having an ambulatory encounter, but a higher likelihood of receiving dual therapy.ConclusionsThis study found that diagnostic testing, dual therapy, and SDOH diagnostic codes were underutilized for pediatric patients diagnosed with tinea capitis. In addition, patients of races classified as Black and “other” were more likely to be diagnosed in emergency encounters, but had a higher likelihood of receiving dual therapy regardless of encounter type. Further research is needed to determine how to improve the management of tinea capitis and better understand its relationship with SDOH.

Introduction

Tinea capitis is a superficial dermatophyte fungal infection of the scalp. 1 It is the most common pediatric dermatophyte fungal infection worldwide, impacting primarily prepubertal children. 1,2 When contracted, its prognosis is good, as most patients are cured after a course of treatment. 1,3

For the efficient treatment of this condition, definitive diagnosis of tinea capitis through direct microscopy and potassium hydroxide (KOH) preparation is recommended while the patient is empirically treated. 1 This allows clinicians to rule out alternative diagnoses and to ensure that systemic antifungal agents are used judiciously. 1,3–5 Topical antifungal treatments are available, but they do not penetrate the root of the hair follicle. Thus, they are administered concomitantly with oral antifungal treatments to reduce complications, limit spread, and increase cure rates. 2,3 Early recognition and definitive treatment are essential, as cutaneous complications such as permanent hair loss and scar tissue formation can occur. 2,5,6 In addition, alterations in appearance can occur leading to psychosocial stress. 7

Despite the risks associated with tinea capitis infections, fungal cultures and/or KOH preparations are rarely used to confirm diagnosis and organism species. 3,8 Tinea capitis is also not always treated with the most suitable agent. For example, more than 90% of infections in the United States are due to Trichophyton, which is best cured with the antifungal terbinafine rather than griseofulvin. 6,8 In addition, it is not completely understood why tinea capitis is present in certain populations. Tinea capitis is reported to have its highest prevalence in prepubescent African Americans. 1,9 International studies have often linked higher infection rates to lower socioeconomic status, overcrowded environments, and poor hygiene. 10–12 Nevertheless, to the authors’ knowledge, no studies in the United States have reported an association between tinea capitis diagnoses and diagnostic codes for social determinants of health (SDOH). An understanding of how tinea capitis is diagnosed, its associated risk factors and treatment approaches, and the impact of race disparities can improve the care of pediatric patients with tinea capitis.

The objective of this study was to examine the diagnostic and treatment approaches to tinea capitis, analyze the frequency of SDOH diagnostic codes, and evaluate the possibility of racial disparities in the treatment of pediatric patients aged 0 to 18 years diagnosed with tinea capitis. The a priori hypotheses were that diagnostic testing would be underutilized, that tinea capitis infections would be primarily treated with single therapies (either oral or topical), that there would be a high frequency of SDOH diagnostic codes, and that race would not have an impact on the type of encounter or treatment provided.

Methods

Study design

This is a retrospective observational cohort study using the TriNetX electronic health record (EHR) database of pediatric patients aged 0 to 18 years who were diagnosed with tinea capitis (according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition [ICD-10], diagnostic code B35.0), were prescribed an agent for the treatment of tinea capitis (ie, oral or topical therapy), were listed as having an ambulatory or emergency encounter, and were not listed as deceased. The TriNetX global health federated research network is primarily based in the United States and has the ability to collect aggregated EHR data elements, including diagnoses, medications, procedures, and laboratory values for approximately 68 million patients in 56 large health care organizations. These data elements are de-identified and made available within a user-friendly browser-based software accessible in real-time. TriNetX, LLC, is compliant with the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) and any additional data privacy regulations applicable to the contributing health care organizations. TriNetX is certified to the ISO 27001:2013 standard. It maintains an information security management system to meet HIPAA Security Rule requirements and to protect the health care data to which it provides access. The patient-level data provided in a data set generated by the TriNetX platform contains only de-identified data in accordance with the de-identification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. Data de-identification processes that are performed within TriNetX are attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b) 1 of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. The Penn State Health Institutional Review Board predetermined this study to be nonhuman research because no protected health information is provided.

Data collection

Data for 73,557 patients from 56 health care organizations were obtained on July 5, 2022, from the TriNetX Research Network. Specifically, the following EHR data were analyzed: age, sex, race, ethnicity, ICD-10 diagnostic codes associated with tinea capitis and SDOH, medication codes of common agents used to treat tinea capitis, procedure codes (Common Procedural Terminology [CPT] and Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System [HCPCS]) to determine if the patient underwent KOH preparation or fungal culture evaluation, and encounter codes. The data provided were de-identified; thus, no dates of birth were provided, and therefore, ages are approximate. For example, if a patient born in 2000 was reported to have undergone KOH preparation laboratory testing on January 1, 2018 (ie, the first day of patient care services), the patient was determined to be 18 years of age. Patients who were deceased were excluded from analyses to ensure that all data related to the diagnosis and management of tinea capitis were available for review. Race and ethnicity were included in this analysis to examine similarities or differences in the diagnostic frequencies, types of encounters, and treatment practices for tinea capitis among the different groups. Because of the multicenter, de-identified nature of the database, it is unknown how race and/or ethnicity data were obtained and whether they were self-reported. Patients were noted to have an SDOH diagnostic code if the code was present in the database before the tinea capitis diagnosis. The study population was divided into 2 cohorts: patients diagnosed with tinea capitis during an ambulatory encounter and during an emergency encounter.

(Please see Supplementary Tables 1, 2, and 3 for definitions of the diagnostic, medication, and procedural codes that were analyzed in this present study.)

Statistical analysis

Patient demographic factors, disease diagnoses, medications, and therapies are reported in this study in terms of summary statistics for the ambulatory and emergency encounter groups. A 2-sample t-test was applied to compare the ages between the ambulatory group and the emergency group, and Pearson’s χ2 tests were applied for the categorical demographic characteristics, therapy type, KOH status, and SDOH status.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regressions were conducted to investigate associations between the primary outcomes of interest and the SDOH status. In a data preprocessing step, the missing data on sex and race were assumed to be missing completely at random and were removed. In addition, to improve model interpretability, the race categories Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander were combined into a single category labeled “A/AI/AN/NH/OPI” (or “other”) because of their small proportions in the data. The primary outcomes 1,2 are the encounter type (ambulatory or emergency) and the therapy type (dual or single). For each outcome, the following procedures were performed in parallel: First, univariable logistic regression models were fit for race, SDOH status, sex, ethnicity, and regional patient location to estimate the unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the multivariable analyses, because the question of whether the effects of race would be different for those with and without SDOH codes was of interest, a likelihood ratio test for the interaction between race and SDOH status was conducted for each outcome’s multivariable model. The results are reported in terms of the adjusted ORs with 95% CIs estimated from the multivariable logistic regression models paired with the univariable results .

The authors used the statistical software R v.4.2.2 to preprocess the data, conduct the statistical tests, and perform the logistic regression analyses. In the statistical results, p values of less than or equal to 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 19,677 patients (17,471 [88.8%] ambulatory and 2206 [11.2%] emergency) met inclusion criteria for the study. A majority of patients were noted to be male and Black. The overall distributions of sex, race, ethnicity, and age were all significantly different between the patients who were diagnosed with tinea capitis in the ambulatory group and those who were diagnosed in the emergency group (p < 0.001; Table 1).

Frequency of potassium hydroxide preparation or fungal culture testing

A KOH preparation or fungal culture was used to assist with diagnosis in 1014 (5.8%) ambulatory encounters and 51 (2.3%) emergency encounters (p < 0.001.)

Frequency of common agents used to treat tinea capitis

Overall, the most common agent prescribed was griseofulvin (82.5% of encounters), followed by ketoconazole, a topical therapy (26.5%). Among systemic therapies, the second most commonly used medication was terbinafine (5.6% of encounters). The second most commonly prescribed topical agent was selenium sulfide (8.0% of encounters; Table 2).

Frequency of single versus double agent treatment for tinea capitis

A lower frequency of patients were prescribed both oral and topical agents, and more patients received dual therapy during ambulatory encounters than during emergency encounters (4462 [25.5%] in the ambulatory group vs 500 [22.7%] in the emergency group, p = 0.037).

Presence of SDOH diagnostic codes before initial tinea capitis diagnosis

An SDOH diagnostic code was present prior to the initial tinea capitis diagnosis in 1077 encounters (5.5%). The category present most frequently was “Other problems related to primary support group, including family circumstances” (369, 1.9%). The presence of any SDOH diagnostic code was more prevalent in the ambulatory group (5.8%) than in the emergency group (3.3%; p < 0.001). Association of an ambulatory encounter and dual therapy for tinea capitis with patient data

According to the likelihood ratio tests, the overall effects of race on both the encounter group and the therapy type were not significantly different when the SDOH codes were present or absent (p = 0.360 for encounter group and p = 0.695 for therapy type). After controlling for sex, ethnicity, age, and the presence of SDOH diagnostic codes, the analysis indicated that patients in the Black (OR = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.41–0.57, p < 0.001) and A/AI/AN/NH/OPI (OR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.33–0.81, p = 0.004) race categories had lower likelihoods of having an ambulatory encounter than patients categorized as White. Additionally, patients with an SDOH diagnostic code also had a higher likelihood of having an ambulatory encounter (OR = 1.76, 95% CI = 1.36–2.27, p < 0.001). After the analysis was controlled for sex, ethnicity, age, and the presence of SDOH diagnostic codes, it was found that, compared to White patients, patients in the Black (OR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.72–2.13, p < 0.001) and A/AI/AN/NH/OPI (OR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.08–2.13, p = 0.017) race categories had higher likelihoods of receiving dual therapy (Table 4).

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to use a national EHR database to investigate how clinicians approach tinea capitis in pediatric patients and the frequency of use of SDOH diagnostic codes in association with tinea capitis, in order to assess the presence of racial disparities in the type of encounter and therapy provided for this common pediatric infection. The main findings of this study indicate that a low number of patients had a confirmatory test for tinea capitis (ie, KOH preparation or fungal culture), a low number of patients received both an oral agent and a topical agent, and non-White patients were more likely to be diagnosed with tinea capitis in the emergency setting. Additionally, it was found that a low number of patients had an associated SDOH diagnostic code prior to their tinea capitis diagnosis. These findings may have substantial implications for pediatric patients, particularly in terms of how clinicians approach tinea capitis.

Tinea capitis remains a global public health concern, with a frequency of 3% to 15% of US children per year. 13–15 If tinea capitis is not treated, disease progression can result in permanent hair loss, scarring, secondary bacterial infections, and the spread of disease. 2,5,6 Tinea capitis can also impact patients and families psychologically and economically. Patients may be subjected to bullying or ridicule as a result of alterations in their appearance, which may be permanent. 7,16 Patients and caregivers have also expressed fear of being stigmatized or called unhygienic. 9 The cost of long treatment courses and the need for prophylactic treatment for family members of those with infections to limit its spread can also have a negative financial impact on those in need of treatment. 16,17 To prevent these complications, reduce long-term psychosocial impacts, and limit disease spread, it is imperative that tinea capitis be properly diagnosed and managed.

According to the Red Book, published by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the ideal approach to tinea capitis should include both an oral agent and adjuvant treatment with an antifungal shampoo, such as selenium sulfide or topical ketaconazole. 5 Because topical treatments alone do not penetrate the root of the hair follicle, 18 oral treatment is necessary to effectively eradicate tinea capitis. 3 Topical treatment is still administered, however, because it is useful as an adjunct to systemic therapy to reduce the transmission of spores to others and increase cure rates. 5,18 This study found that a minority of patients were prescribed both an oral and a topical agent, whereas a majority of patients received systemic monotherapy. In addition, patients in the Black and A/AI/AN/NH/OPI race categories were found to have a higher likelihood of receiving dual therapy regardless of the encounter type. Given current recommendations, the authors expected that more patients would receive dual therapy. It is possible that the fact that this expectation was not met is due to lower practitioner knowledge and experience surrounding tinea capitis infections. Additionally, patients may have been advised to purchase adjuvant treatment over-the-counter, a possibility that was not captured in the data collection. Prescribers may have assumed that these over-the-counter treatments would not be covered by insurance. Whereas previous studies have focused on only pediatrics-based clinics and settings, the TriNetX database includes emergency as well as adult-, family-, and pediatrics-based ambulatory settings. This study was thus able to have a more encompassing look at how tinea capitis is treated and diagnosed across the United States. Because tinea capitis is typically seen in the pediatric population, 19 it is possible that adult practitioners may be less experienced with the best method for treating this infection. With tinea capitis continuing to be the most contagious fungal infection worldwide, 1 consistent best practice guidelines as outlined by the American Academy of Pediatrics across all disciplines could help reduce the further spread of this infection and increase practitioner confidence when they encounter patients with tinea capitis.

Among the encounters analyzed in this study, griseofulvin was the most common treatment agent used, whereas terbinafine was the fourth most common agent used. Whereas griseofulvin has long been used as a first-line treatment for tinea capitis, newer oral antifungal agents such as terbinafine have had higher complete cure rates and similar adverse effects in children who acquired Trichophyton species, while allowing for shorter treatment courses. 3,5,6,8,20 Griseofulvin is still the treatment of choice for tinea capitis caused by Microsporum species, but the literature has consistently shown that more than 90% of US tinea infections occur from Trichophyton species. 1 Therefore, diagnostic testing is necessary to determine the causative species and make the most effective, patient-centered decision regarding treatment. This study showed that, despite widespread recommendations to confirm organism species with KOH preparation microscopy or a fungal culture, a lower frequency of patients are receiving these diagnostic tests, particularly in the emergency setting. 1,3,21 Without confirmatory testing, it is difficult to determine the best treatment option, which could increase the chance of complications for patients and allow for the spread of infection to others. Additionally, forgoing diagnostic testing could lead to increased health care costs resulting from multiple visits and insufficient medication.

In this study, it was found that patients in the race categories of Black or Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (denoted herein as A/AI/AN/NH/OPI) had a lower likelihood of being diagnosed in the ambulatory setting. Previous studies have shown that Black individuals make up a majority of those diagnosed with tinea capitis infections in the United States. 22,23 In addition, although tinea capitis infection rates have increased within the past 10 years across all races in the United States, they have disproportionately increased among the Black population. 12,14,24 Because of the limitations of the TriNetX database, however, it is less clear why these racial groups have lower likelihoods of being diagnosed in the ambulatory setting. Tinea capitis infections have also been associated with lower socioeconomic status. Because of systemic racism, Black patients are likely to suffer from poverty, decreased access to health care, and other negative social conditions. 23,24 Not only can such conditions increase the likelihood of acquiring tinea capitis infections, but it can also result in families being burdened by the costs associated with treating tinea capitis, thereby leading them to delay medical care and/or use emergency departments as part of usual care. 23,24 Further study is needed to examine this association.

Because patients with lower socioeconomic status are at risk for tinea capitis, careful screening for possible underlying environmental causes (ie, SDOHs) may be necessary to try to reduce their impact. Interventions for those experiencing health care and socioeconomic inequities may limit the recurrence of tinea capitis, among other socially influenced diseases. Despite SDOH diagnostic codes being associated with negative health care outcomes and higher health care utilization costs, the frequency of SDOH diagnostic code documentation continues to be poor. 25 In this regard, this study was consistent with other studies evaluating the use of SDOH diagnostic codes. 25–27 Further study is needed to understand the reasons for the low usage rates of SDOH diagnostic codes and to develop strategies to improve their use.

This study has several limitations. First, the clinical data were limited to those available within the TriNetX database. Because of the de-identified nature of the study, it is not possible to determine specifically how tinea capitis is approached in adult vs pediatric settings, by the type of specialist, or by clinical setting (ie, community vs academic). Additionally, it was not possible to evaluate how often patients used over-the-counter treatments or insurance. This study was observational; thus, the associations identified do not represent a cause-and-effect relationship. Further, the fact that the TriNetX database is limited to certain regions and centers could have contributed to an undercollection of data from smaller and under-represented communities. To improve model interpretations, the authors combined 3 different races, but such an approach can mask different health-related behaviors as well as how diseases such as tinea capitis impact specific groups. Documentation bias for SDOH diagnostic codes may be present in patients with ambulatory encounters, as they are established patients and may see the same practitioners multiple times, who may be more likely to screen and record any positive results. Finally, because of the retrospective, multi-institutional nature of this study, policies regarding screening and documentation of SDOH could not be assessed, and it is unknown how race and ethnicity data were obtained.

Conclusion

In this study, it was found that diagnostic testing for in the setting of tinea capitis was underutilized, a majority of patients received monotherapy, Black patients were more likely to be diagnosed in an emergency setting, and a low frequency of patients had a documented SDOH diagnostic code at the time they received a tinea capitis diagnosis. These data suggest that practitioners may be missing opportunities to screen for and intervene on SDOH for their patients. Further research is needed to better elucidate the relationship between social determinants of health and tinea capitis infections.
