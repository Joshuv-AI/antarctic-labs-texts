Perceived barriers to timely diagnosis among Iranian women diagnosed with advanced breast cancer: a qualitative study
Authors: Maryam Janatolmakan, Marziyeh Lashkari, Rebecca Lehto, Reza Negarandeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackground and objectiveTimely diagnosis of breast cancer plays a vital role in improving treatment outcomes and increasing patient survival. However, contextual factors such as cultural beliefs, healthcare system structural limitations, and socioeconomic challenges can contribute to diagnostic delays. The qualitative study explores the barriers to timely diagnosis from the perspectives of Iranian women diagnosed with advanced breast cancer.Design and settingA qualitative study was conducted using in-depth, semistructured interviews to explore perceived barriers to timely diagnosis among Iranian women with advanced breast cancer. Data analysis were performed using MAXQDA software, V.20.Participants15 Iranian women [mean age 50.6±9.4 (range 38–72)] years with breast cancer diagnosed at an advanced stage.ResultsSeven participants reported diagnostic delays that exceeded 7 months. Key factors contributing to perceived delays included poorly managed appointments and medical care, care not meeting patient expectations, deceptive marketing practices, cost of diagnostic procedures, lack of knowledge, fears, transcendent beliefs, competing priorities, insufficient support and diagnostic errors.ConclusionsUnderstanding perceived barriers to timely diagnosis in women with advanced breast cancer is key to creating effective strategies that reduce preventable negative outcomes. Study findings have implications for patient education, healthcare quality, and enhancement of communication between providers and patients for delayed diagnosis prevention in respective treatment settings.Keywords: Breast tumours, Patient Participation, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent cancer among women, accounting for 31% of new cancer diagnoses in females in 2023.1 Recent research evaluating the global burden of cancers among women from 2020 to 2040 identifies the impact of breast cancer, which continues with the highest incidence and mortality rates among women across the lifespan compared with other cancers, a trend that is projected to persist through 2040.2 In Iran, a 2024 review reported rising trends for breast cancer across incidence indicators.3

Causes for rising breast cancer rates are complex. A recent review identified that genetic predispositions such as BRCA mutations, hormonal influences, lifestyle, reproductive patterns, age, family history and environmental factors are all linked with breast cancer.4 In Iran, factors increasing the risk of breast cancer include family history, genetics, lifestyle choices, reproductive history and the Arg/Arg genotype.5

Early diagnosis and effective treatment are highly critical towards halting breast cancer progression and improving survival rates.6 Thus, screening for breast cancer with regular mammography and teaching women about breast health and self-examination is important towards early detection. However, obstacles faced by patients contribute to late diagnoses,7 which can significantly reduce patient survival rates. For instance, a study reported a 36% reduced survival rate for patients diagnosed at an advanced stage.8 Barriers to timely diagnosis vary and are reported to include factors such as a lack of access to diagnostic and treatment facilities, religious and cultural beliefs, medical errors, competing priorities, economic challenges, lack of awareness and the use of complementary medicine.9,11

These recognised obstacles are documented in epidemiological research and may vary depending on healthcare systems, geographical location and religious and cultural differences, contributing to disparities among countries.8,16

Several studies conducted in Iran between 2014 and 202212,17 have identified the critical need for understanding factors associated with delays in breast cancer diagnoses. However, this research has not provided the patients perspective. Articulating the experiences of those who have faced late diagnoses is an effective approach to gaining a better understanding of barriers impacting the lack of timely diagnosis in patients from their lived experience. Therefore, this study explores perceived barriers to timely diagnosis from the perspectives of Iranian women diagnosed with advanced breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Study design

A qualitative approach following the conventional content analysis methodology was used to conduct the study.18 The approach allowed for inductive analysis of interview data to identify thematic data patterns directly emerging from participants’ experiences without imposing preconceived categories. Semistructured, in-depth interviews were employed whereby rich, detailed data were derived via concurrent data collection and analysis of data following the conventional content analysis method.18 This study was reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research.19

Participant selection

Participants were selected through purposive sampling using the maximum variation to ensure a diverse representation of patients who were referred to the cancer clinic of the hospital complex in Tehran, Iran. 15 women meeting the study criteria described next were recruited.

Diagnostic delay was defined as a period exceeding 1 month from the onset of unusual breast symptoms or discovery of a concerning mass to the time of initial biopsy and confirmation of breast cancer diagnosis.17 20 Inclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed diagnosis of breast cancer; (2) a history of diagnostic delay as defined previously; (3) age 18 years or older; (4) able to communicate verbally; (5) willingness to participate in the study and (6) absence of cognitive impairments that would hinder meaningful participation in interviews.

Ongoing treatment for breast cancer was not an exclusion criterion given many patients with advanced breast cancer are receiving some form of treatment during the course of their care. However, per our study protocol, in the case that a participant experienced physical or mental decline or distress, the interviews would be discontinued and the participant disenrolled from the study.

The researcher was present in the clinic daily and collaborated with the attending physician to identify eligible participants by reviewing medical records and conducting initial interviews. After introductions and explaining the voluntary nature of the study, the researcher scheduled interviews at convenient times for the participants. Reminder calls were made 1 day prior to each scheduled session.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to each interview, with assurances of confidentiality and the use of data solely for research purposes. Similar to previous qualitative studies, we anticipated that approximately 12–15 interviews would be sufficient to capture the broad perspective needed and to attain data saturation. Thus, the final sample size of 15 was determined during the process of data collection and analysis, once it was evident that no new information was emerging.

Data collection

Data were collected through semistructured, in-depth individual interviews. Open interview questions were developed that were based on a comprehensive literature review and lengthy consultations with the multidisciplinary research team. Examples of questions included: ‘How was the breast cancer discovered?’ and ‘what factors contributed to the late diagnosis?’ Follow-up prompts such as ‘could you elaborate further?’, ‘why?’, ‘how?’ and, if appropriate, ‘what made you decide not to seek medical attention earlier?’ were used to gain deeper insights while also maintaining sensitivity to participants’ emotional states. The standardised interview guide is provided as online supplemental file 1.

Data collection method

After obtaining written informed consent, semistructured interviews were conducted in a quiet, private room within the Cancer Institute of Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex in Tehran, Iran—a dedicated space designed specifically to facilitate cancer-related research activities. Each interview lasted between 40 and 50 min and was conducted by a nurse researcher with formal training in qualitative research methodology. All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data saturation was attained following 15 interviews as no new concepts were emerging. The time length of the diagnostic delay was the participants’ accounting with medical record corroboration.

To ensure methodological rigour and transparency, the research team consisting of experts with both research and clinical expertise in breast cancer care and treatment, qualitative methods, cancer nursing and illness adaptation collaboratively developed the interview guide. Data review and transcript verification were performed collectively. Final coding and thematic analysis were also reviewed and approved by the study team.

Interview questions were posed with neutral, non-judgmental language to encourage open reflection without inducing blame. Participants were already aware of their diagnostic timeline through previous clinical consultations. The study objectives were clearly explained before participation, emphasising that the goal was to understand diagnostic experiences broadly, without assignation of personal responsibility. Participation was voluntary, and many expressed a desire to share their stories to help others.

To protect participants’ well-being, the interviewer monitored verbal and non-verbal cues and redirected the conversation if distress arose. No participant withdrew or reported psychological harm related to the interviews.

Regarding reflexivity, a multiperspective approach was applied. The study interviewer maintained a journal throughout the study and documented her assumptions, emotional responses and evolving interpretations. As a clinician familiar with oncology settings, the interviewer was particularly attentive to how her background might influence data collection and analysis. The research team engaged in regular reflective discussions that aimed to address their personal, disciplinary and cultural assumptions, balancing local familiarity with critical distancing. Integrating diverse professional and epistemological viewpoints strengthened the credibility and reflexive depth of the study.18

Data analysis

The conventional content data analytical approach involves deriving codes and categories directly and inductively from the raw data, without pre-existing theory.21 Thus, data collection and analysis occurred simultaneously based on Elo and Kyngäs’s methodology.18 22 The approach consisted of the following steps:

Open coding: The interviews were read multiple times to gain a general understanding. Codes were then assigned to semantic units while reading the text using MAXQDA V.20 software.

Creating categories: Codes were compared with identifying differences and similarities, and those with similar meanings were grouped to form common content primary and sub-categories.

Abstraction: Based on the developed codes and the content within categories and subcategories, higher-level labels were generated. During the process of data analysis, the research team continuously assessed whether new insights were emerging. By the 14th and 15th interviews, the data had become repetitive, and no new codes or themes were identified. This observation indicated that data saturation had been achieved,23 and therefore, conducting additional interviews was deemed unnecessary.

Trustworthiness: To assess study rigour, Lincoln and Guba’s approach incorporating credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability was used.24 25 To enhance data credibility, the participants and the research team reviewed the data and provided additional comments to confirm its accuracy. To ensure data dependability, the researcher sought feedback from participants and had the findings reviewed by the research team and two experienced colleagues in qualitative studies. To improve confirmability, the researcher made a concerted effort to thoroughly describe all stages of the research, including data collection, analysis and theme formation, to allow for external scrutiny. Lastly, in order to improve data transferability, the researcher aimed to involve participants of diverse age, educational, cultural and social backgrounds. This diverse representation and its detailed description enhance the transferability of the findings to other contexts.

Patient and public involvement

Patients with advanced breast cancer participated in in-depth interviews to share their personal experiences, forming the core data of this qualitative study. Although patients were not involved in the initial design or conduct of the study, their perspectives were central to the research objectives. The findings will be disseminated primarily through publication in scientific journals. Additionally, pending appropriate permissions from the journal, the main findings may be shared with the relevant department of the Ministry of Health to inform the development of educational materials for women in the community.

Results

A total of 15 women participated in the study. The average age of the participants was 50.6±9.4 (range 38–72) years. More than half (n=9, 60.0%) had formal education below a diploma level. Categories and subcategories related to the experience of diagnostic delay in women with advanced breast cancer

Poorly managed appointments and care

Participants indicated that scheduled appointments at diagnostic and treatment centres were significant challenges. Specifically identified were lengthy waiting times to obtain appointments as well as repeated cancelling of appointments.

Lengthy waiting times to obtain appointments

Participants found long waiting times for appointments frustrating, which significantly impacted their satisfaction and resulted in non-compliance with follow-up appointments.

I waited in line for about a month to have a biopsy done by a resident doctor at a government hospital. Then I waited for weeks for the pathology results. This delayed the process of diagnosing my disease.(Participant2)

I was on the waiting list for mammography and ultrasound for several weeks until I finally managed to have them done. (Participant 3)

Repeated cancelling of appointments

Participants mentioned that the frequent cancelling of their medical appointments was frustrating. It reduced their energy and motivation to pursue symptoms and timely diagnosis.

I had two appointments for a biopsy with a female surgeon, but they were both canceled because she was on holiday, causing a delay in the diagnosis. (Participant 9)

My mammogram appointment was canceled two weeks in a row, resulting in a one-month postponement of my mammogram. (Participant 12)

Care not meeting patient expectations

The diagnostic and therapeutic services offered at the centres did not meet several of the participants’ expectations. They mentioned that the lack of consideration for individualised priorities such as having female providers. Patients also reported a lack of trust in government health facilities as contributing factors.

Gender non-conformity in providers

Participants emphasised the importance of gender compatibility between patients and doctors in diagnostic and treatment centres. They pointed out that the lack of female radiologists and surgeons was a reason for their diagnostic delays.

I declined to have a diagnostic ultrasound performed by the male radiologist and postponed the follow-up until a female radiologist was available. (Participant 4)

I waited weeks for a female surgeon to perform a biopsy, which caused a delay. (Participant 9)

In our city, most of the doctors were men, and I did not want to be examined by them to perform a clinical examination. For this reason, I postponed the follow-up of my symptoms. (Participant 13)

Lack of trust in government health facilities

The participants mentioned that the delay in diagnosing their disease was influenced by the conditions of public hospitals, such as overcrowding, noisy environments, the teaching atmosphere for providers and the inexperience of intern doctors.

I was advised to go to a government hospital for a biopsy. However, I hesitated because I knew that most of the work in these hospitals is done by students. As a result, I didn’t take immediate action and postponed following up on my symptoms for a while (Participant 4).

When I was referred to the government hospital, I encountered a large number of patients. I chose to postpone my follow-up for a few weeks in order to have the follow-up done in more suitable conditions (Participant 9)

Deceptive marketing practices

Participants mentioned the prevalence of misleading advertisements on social media about the effectiveness of alternative medicine, as well as the suggestion not to undergo certain diagnostic tests. They stated that such advertising made it difficult for them to make timely decisions and seek prompt medical attention. The phrases marketing for traditional medicine and misconceptions about diagnostic testing were derived from these discussions.

Marketing for traditional medicine

Some participants mentioned that they had come across numerous advertisements for herbal medicines claiming to be effective in treating cancer symptoms on virtual communication networks. Influenced by these ads and the perceived safety and accessibility of such strategies, they initially tried herbal remedies. After recognising that the remedies were ineffective, they sought conventional medical treatment.

I preferred traditional medicine over modern medicine because of media advertisements. As a result, I first consulted with traditional medicine doctors and implemented their recommendations, which led to a delay in my diagnosis by a few months. (Participant 10)

I have seen many times the positive effects of traditional medicine in reducing stomach pain, colds, and food poisoning in the media. Therefore, I initially used Nora, apple juice, and the recommended medicines from traditional medicine for my breast pain. Unfortunately, this delayed my diagnosis for three months. (Participant 12)

Misconceptions about diagnostic testing

Some participants also attributed delays in undergoing mammography, ultrasound and diagnostic biopsies to negative social media regarding the risks associated with these procedures.

I saw on social media that the radiation and pressure from the mammography machine can irritate breast tissue cells and make them malignant. That’s why I didn’t have my annual mammogram, and as a result, my breast mass was evaluated in the final stages. (Participant 8)

I had seen on social media that it was recommended not to undergo a diagnostic biopsy because it could cause the mass to spread throughout the body, so I postponed my diagnostic biopsy for a long time. (Participant 11)

Cost of diagnostic procedures

Financial difficulties in paying for diagnostic and treatment services were a barrier to timely symptom detection and follow-up for some participants. Two subcategories were identified:high costs for services and insufficient health insurance coverage.

High cost for services

Participants highlighted the significant expenses associated with screening methods such as ultrasound, mammography and biopsy in both non-governmental and governmental centres which created a challenge for timely diagnosis.

The cost of ultrasound and mammography is prohibitive, even at government centers, making it unaffordable for me. This is why I neglected my symptoms and sought medical attention late. (Participant 5)

The cost of diagnostic procedures was too high for me, so I didn’t follow up. (Participant 12)

Insufficient health insurance coverage

The inadequacy of both primary and supplementary insurance coverage was identified as a barrier to prompt symptom follow-up and timely diagnosis.

My social security insurance covers only a small portion of my diagnosis and treatment expenses, which posed a significant challenge for me. (Participant 1)

I didn’t seek treatment for my symptoms because I lack supplemental insurance to cover most of my healthcare costs, and my basic insurance is inadequate. (Participant 9)

Lack of knowledge

The participants’ limited knowledge about breast cancer symptoms and diagnostic methods contributed to delays in seeking medical attention. The subcategories misattribution of symptoms and late self-detection were identified.

Misattribution of symptoms

Some participants mentioned that they delayed seeking immediate follow-up due to misinterpreting symptoms and attributing them to previous surgeries, underlying diseases, daily chores, menopause, breastfeeding or pregnancy.

After my thyroidectomy, I experienced pain and swelling in my right breast area, but I attributed the symptoms to my medication. I didn’t follow up on the initial symptoms. (Participant 1)

I thought the numbness and swelling in my hand were due to my high blood pressure and diabetes. I had regular checkups for these, not realizing these symptoms were related to breast cancer. (Participant 8)

Late self-detection

Limited knowledge about screening such as regular breast self-examination and annual mammography contributed to diagnostic delay.

I didn’t perform breast self-examination at all, and I wasn't aware of it until this lump appeared and grew. Perhaps if I had conducted breast self-examination, I would have discovered it earlier. (Participant2)

Women over 40 are required to have a mammogram. I hadn’t, and when I noticed the lump, it had grown so large that it had also involved the lymph nodes in my armpit. (Participant9)

Fears

Participants did not track their symptoms in a timely manner due to fears of pity, stigma and losing their femininity.

Fear of receiving pity and sympathy

Avoidance of others’ pity and sympathy was identified by some participants to be a deterrent to seeking follow-up for their symptoms.

I always disliked pity and sympathy, and I didn’t like anyone treating me differently because of my illness. That’s why I hid my symptoms and didn’t seek help for a long time.(Participant 11)

Society often perceives cancer patients with pity, and I delayed seeking medical attention to avoid encountering their pity. (Participant 13)

Fear of stigmatisation

The participants also cited the fear of being stigmatised by others as a reason for not seeking prompt diagnosis for their symptoms.

I had heard people with this disease being told in a pitiful tone that they had cancer. When I discovered my symptoms, I didn’t follow up to avoid hearing that tone. (Participant 9)

In our society, a cancer patient can be treated like a contagious leprosy patient. I ignored my symptoms and avoided follow-up to escape this. (Participant 15)

Fear of losing femininity

Participants’ identity and femininity surrounding the breast as a crucial symbol of motherhood and marriage also contributed to hesitancy in seeking medical attention.

I was afraid of getting a mastectomy and ignored my symptoms, thinking about how I could present myself as a bride and a woman without my breasts. (Participant 9)

I was terrified of losing my breasts or having to use a prosthesis. My breasts were a significant part of my identity as a woman, and I ignored my symptoms to avoid questioning my femininity. (Participant 15)

Transcendent beliefs

The participants’ adherence to their personal beliefs such as fate, God’s will and miracles contributed to not seeking diagnostic testing.

Belief in fate and god’s will

The participants mentioned accepting cancer as part of their fate and cited it as a reason for not following up on their symptoms.

When I was diagnosed with breast cancer for the second time, I made a promise to myself that I would accept my fate instead of resisting and treating it, and that’s why I put off tracking my symptoms. (Participant 5)

When this issue arose, I accepted it as God’s will. I did not seek immediate treatment, saying that if it was my time to die, I had no objection. (Participant 12)

Belief in miracles as a result of prayer

Some participants, with strong religious beliefs, expressed hope that through prayer and divine intervention, their breast lump or symptoms would go away. This hope sometimes contributed to delays in seeking medical treatment.

After feeling a painful lump in my breast for months, I prayed for it to disappear, but it didn’t. My cancer continued to progress. (Participant 1)

When I found the small lump, I prayed for it to disappear. But over time, it grew larger and more aggressive despite my continuous prayers. (Participant 13)

Competing priorities

Participants noted that management of work and family responsibilities, important life events and unexpected situations like the COVID-19 pandemic contributed to putting off health priorities.

Multiple roles

Some participants, in their roles as mothers, wives and children, neglected their personal care due to their responsibilities and did not seek timely follow-up care.

I postponed following up on my symptoms and diagnosis until after my disabled father’s death while I was caring for him. (Participant 4)

I postponed following up on my symptoms until after my daughter’s entrance exam to ensure her comfort. (Participant 6)

Unexpected life events

The participants mentioned attending and hosting ceremonies such as birthday and marriage celebrations, as well as memorial services for their loved ones, as competing factors preventing their ability to move forward with healthcare.

At that time, my brother passed away, and I was engrossed in arranging his funeral and memorial services, which led to me neglecting my symptoms. (Participant 9)

I had to prepare for my sister’s wedding, so I ignored my symptoms for a long time, also resulting in a delayed diagnosis of my disease. (Participant 15)

Insufficient support

Participants mentioned that a lack of support from people around them, including medical staff and the community, including low family support and a lack of empathy in health providers, was contributing factors.

Low family support

Some participants expressed disappointment in receiving support from family and spouses.

My husband constantly blamed me for my chronic illnesses. Given his mindset, I ignored my symptoms when I suspected breast cancer. (Participant 4)

This is the second time I have been diagnosed with breast cancer. Due to my husband’s lack of support during my first diagnosis, I chose not to seek follow-up care this time. (Participant 5)

Lack of empathy in health providers

Participants mentioned that the lack of professional behaviours of service providers was a barrier to their timely follow-up.

I didn’t have a good memory of my brother’s oncologist’s behavior and words. When I got sick myself, I chose not to follow up to avoid witnessing the harsh and inflexible treatment from doctors again. (Participant 9)

During the initial examination of my symptoms, my doctor behaved inappropriately, which prevented me from seeking immediate follow-up care. (Participant 13)

Diagnostic errors

Several participants attributed their delayed diagnosis to what they perceived as errors in the diagnostic process. These perceptions were shaped by their personal experiences and interactions with healthcare professionals. While these accounts reflect participants’ interpretations, they highlight critical areas where perceived miscommunication or system inefficiencies may have contributed to diagnostic delays. On further analysis, three main subcategories emerged within this theme: perceived insufficient training of healthcare professionals, perceived false referrals and perceived issues with diagnostic tools.

Perceived insufficient training

Some participants believed that inadequate training and experience of healthcare professionals, particularly doctors, contributed to delays in their diagnosis.

If the radiologist who performed my mammogram had been more experienced, she would have diagnosed my malignant mass earlier. (Participant 1)

My radiologist said the mass was benign, and based on her statement, I didn’t follow up for two years. This led to a delayed diagnosis. (Participant 12)

Perceived false referrals

A few participants shared their view that they were not referred appropriately by healthcare providers, which they felt contributed to delayed diagnosis.

Unfortunately, my family doctor did not initiate a plan after my breast lump was found and I wasn’t referred to a specialist or a more equipped treatment center. (Participant 12)

In the initial assessment of my mass, my primary care physician prescribed vitamins and advised that I did not need to follow up. However, as the mass grew, it became evident that I should have been referred to an oncologist and a radiologist. (Participant 14)

Perceived issues with diagnostic tools

Some participants expressed their perceptions regarding the inefficiency of existing ultrasound and mammography devices, which they felt contributed to delays in timely diagnosis.

The ultrasound machine used for my initial evaluation was old, and I believe the radiologist was unable to accurately determine the nature of my mass with its images. (Participant 9)

I had my mammogram at a center where the machine used was of poor quality and unable to detect the malignant angles of my lump. (Participant13)

Discussion

This study explores the barriers to timely diagnosis from the perspective of Iranian patients with advanced breast cancer. The findings revealed that participants reported several obstacles to timely diagnosis, including poor appointment management, inadequate care, misleading marketing practices, high costs of diagnostic procedures, lack of knowledge, fears, fatalistic beliefs, competing priorities, insufficient support and based on the accounts of some participants, diagnostic.

The research identifies poorly managed appointments as a key issue in the Iranian health system regarding the scheduling of appointments between doctors and patients. Participants mentioned long waiting times for appointments and frequent cancellations by the health system as reasons for delays in diagnosing breast cancer. Zewdie et al noted in a systematic review that health system problems contribute to diagnostic delays in patients with breast cancer in Ethiopia.11 Additionally, Saldaña-Téllez et al found that long waiting times and difficulties in making appointments hinder the early detection of breast cancer in the Mexican community.26 Abo Al-Shiekh et al highlighted the long time required to perform diagnostic procedures, such as biopsies, as a factor in diagnostic delays of breast cancer in Palestinian women.27 Meanwhile, Knolhoff et al reported that 16.8% of missed cancer clinic appointments in America are related to patient-related factors.28 It appears that both patient-related and health system-related factors can contribute to diagnostic delays caused by appointment disruptions. To address this issue, interventions such as improving appointment scheduling systems, increasing diagnostic service capacity and implementing patient navigation programmes could help reduce delays caused by appointment disruptions.29 30

In the present study, unacceptable care was identified as an issue that had negative consequences. This includes gender non-conformity and lack of trust in government health facilities leading to patients not following up on their symptoms. Khakbazan et al highlighted the impact of healthcare system service quality on women’s decision-making in following breast cancer symptoms in their meta-ethnographic synthesis study.16 Additionally, Maggi et al found that the lack of female specialists was a concern for women with breast cancer, affecting the follow-up process of patients’ symptoms.31 Gbenonsi et al identified health workforce and health system conditions as influential factors in the diagnostic and treatment intervals of women with breast cancer in sub-Saharan Africa32 in their systematic review study. Furthermore, Saldaña-Téllez et al found that patients’ lack of trust in health personnel was a barrier to early detection of breast cancer.26 The study suggests that patients’ assessment of medical services significantly influences their decision to seek follow-up for symptoms, as also mentioned by Khakbazan et al.16 Improving patient trust through respectful communication, increasing the availability of female healthcare providers and strengthening service quality in public facilities could possibly help address these healthcare system barriers and encourage timely follow-up.33 34

Misleading marketing practices were also identified as important. Social media advertisements promoting the effectiveness of traditional medicine and misconceptions about diagnostic tests are cited as reasons for delaying timely diagnosis. Saldaña-Téllez et al highlighted the use of traditional medicine, seeking advice from non-specialists and following their opinions as obstacles to early breast cancer diagnosis.26 Additionally, Darré et al identified the use of traditional medicine or self-medication as a factor in the late diagnosis of some patients.35 Despite the findings, it is evident that advertising can play a significant role in influencing individuals' health. However, it is crucial to emphasise the necessity of regulatory oversight to ensure the dissemination of accurate and evidence-based information, thus mitigating the risk of misleading or harmful content.36

The high cost of diagnostic procedures is a significant factor in the delay of timely diagnosis of symptoms, often due to the expense and inadequate insurance coverage. This finding is consistent with research by Subedi et al, Momenimovahed et al and Sobri et al, who identified low socioeconomic status as a common barrier to early diagnosis.9 10 15 Additionally, studies by Saldaña-Téllez et al, Devi et al, Mirfarhadi et al and Foroozani et al found that the cost of medical care and lack of insurance coverage hinder early cancer detection, particularly in relation to breast cancer.13 17 26 37 Dianatinasab et al also highlighted the impact of income on early diagnosis, showing that low income is associated with later-stage diagnosis.14 Furthermore, Farooqi et al identified the poor socioeconomic status and poverty of women as factors contributing to delayed breast cancer diagnosis.38 Regardless of the specific study, economic factors play a significant role in either enabling or hindering patients from seeking timely diagnosis and treatment for their symptoms. To address this barrier, interventions such as expanding insurance coverage, subsidising diagnostic services and offering financial support to low-income patients could help reduce economic delays in diagnosis.39

The present study addresses the lack of knowledge as a contributing factor to diagnostic delay among participants. In Bergin et al’s studies, Farooqi et al also emphasised the lack of knowledge among women about breast cancer symptoms and the misconception of a painless mass as non-threatening.38 40 Additionally, the lack of knowledge about mastectomy conditions has been identified as a factor contributing to the diagnosis delay of patients. Subedi et al, Zewdie et al, Momenimovahed et al, Sobri et al and Khakbazan et al found that women’s misinterpretation of breast cancer symptoms, failure to recognise the necessity of breast self-examination and the lack of awareness about screening contribute to the delay in early detection.9,1115 16 Darré et al identified the absence of breast self-examination as a factor in the late diagnosis of some patients.35 Devi et al and Saldaña-Téllez et al also highlighted the lack of knowledge about breast cancer, its symptoms and associated risks as obstacles to early detection.26 37 In a study conducted in Iran by Mirfarhadi et al, it was found that lack of knowledge about breast cancer symptoms predicts diagnostic delays in women with breast cancer.17 It appears that the delay in diagnosing breast cancer in women may be due to a lack of awareness, failure to conduct breast self-examinations, misinterpretation of symptoms and considering symptoms unimportant or unrelated to breast cancer. Addressing this knowledge gap through targeted public health education campaigns, community outreach programmes and training healthcare providers to promote breast cancer awareness and self-examination can help reduce diagnostic delays.41

The participants in our study identified fears such as receiving pity, being socially stigmatised and losing their femininity and identity as important barriers to timely breast cancer diagnosis. In the Iranian context, several cultural factors, including low awareness, modesty, fear of stigma and reliance on traditional beliefs, contribute to delayed medical consultation.42 These challenges are also present in many non-Western countries. For instance, research from Kenya, India and indigenous communities in Mexico has shown that gender norms, social judgement and cultural expectations often discourage women from seeking early medical attention.26 31 43 On the other hand, most studies included in the systematic review by Ferreira et al were conducted in Western developed countries and focused on minority populations. These studies reported different types of barriers such as lack of trust in the healthcare system, concerns about privacy, language limitations and the preference for same-gender healthcare providers, which also hinder timely screening and diagnosis.44 Although the specific expressions of these barriers differ across regions, common elements such as fear, stigma and lack of knowledge about breast cancer symptoms are shared globally. Comparing these findings highlights the need to consider both universal and culturally specific factors when designing interventions to promote early diagnosis. In particular, Iran’s unique sociocultural environment calls for strategies that are culturally sensitive and locally adapted. Addressing these barriers requires culturally sensitive interventions such as community education programmes to reduce stigma, training healthcare providers in culturally competent care and creating safe spaces where women feel comfortable discussing their concerns and seeking timely medical attention.45

The current research has identified transcendent beliefs as an important factor which is connected to participants’ beliefs about death and the occurrence of miracles. Farooqi et al discussed the health beliefs of Indian Punjabi women who believed that God would heal their symptoms and that the healers’ faith would help them. They also mentioned the acceptance of death for unmarried girls with cancer.38 Momenimovahed et al highlighted the role of patients’ beliefs about death, as well as their religious and cultural beliefs, as factors influencing breast cancer diagnostic delays.15 Additionally, social beliefs about breast cancer, as demonstrated in the study by Khakbazan et al, were found to be influential in early detection.16 It appears that patients’ adherence to these beliefs may contribute to delays in seeking evaluation. Providing culturally appropriate health education and involving the community can help patients overcome these beliefs and seek medical help sooner, reducing delays in diagnosis.

Participants, due to their multiple roles and unexpected life events, placed the follow-up of their symptoms as a lower priority and often neglected it given these competing personal demands. Similarly, other research, such as Bergin et al, has identified addressment of other life priorities as reasons for delaying the planning to follow-up on breast cancer symptoms. 40 Additionally, Foroozani et al and Dianatinasab et al noted in their studies that individuals with a history of chronic disease experienced longer delays in diagnosis, prioritising their chronic disease over seeking timely diagnosis and treatment.13 14 Moreover, Momenimovahed et al emphasised that patients tended to prioritise life issues over the follow-up of symptoms. This tendency was identified as a significant factor contributing to the diagnostic delay of patients.15 It seems that prioritising seeking medical assistance is crucial for the timely follow-up of symptoms. Neglecting to do so due to work or family commitments can result in diagnostic delays.16 To address this barrier, implementing patient navigation programmes that help individuals manage competing life priorities, coordinate timely care and overcome logistical or socioeconomic barriers may support more timely diagnosis.46

One of the important findings of the current study is insufficient support for patients. They cited the lack of empathy of healthcare providers and the lack of family and community support as the reasons for not following up on their health. This is consistent with previous studies by Farooqi et al and Saeedi et al, who highlighted the influence of social support and social factors on diagnostic delays in breast cancer patients.12 38 Momenimovahed et al also emphasised the lack of social support and its effect on diagnostic delays.15 Khakbazan et al similarly identified the role of social interactions in influencing timely detection and tracking of symptoms.16 It seems that insufficient support from family and society is one of the main concerns of breast cancer patients and it strongly affects their decisions about follow-up and treatment. Enhancing empathy training for healthcare providers and developing community-based support programmes could improve patient follow-up and reduce diagnostic delays.47

Participants’ accounts indicated that what they perceived as diagnostic errors played a key role in delaying their breast cancer diagnosis. Although these perceptions reflect subjective experiences, they provide valuable insights into how patients interpret breakdowns in the diagnostic process. These perceived errors fell into three main categories: insufficient training of healthcare professionals, false or unnecessary referrals and issues related to diagnostic equipment or procedures. Consistent with these perceptions, studies by Farooqi et al and Gakunga et al also reported negligence, misdiagnosis and repeated unnecessary referrals as factors contributing to delayed breast cancer diagnosis. These issues are frequently associated with systemic challenges within healthcare systems.38 43 Furthermore, Sobri et al identified false-negative diagnostic test results as a contributing factor to delays.10 In addition, Abo Al-Shiekh et al highlighted poor planning for follow-up of benign tumours, inadequate systematic referral pathways and limited training among primary care physicians as further contributing factors.27 Overall, while participants’ experiences suggest that medical errors may adversely affect timely and accurate care, further research is needed to objectively validate these findings.

The findings indicate that various factors such as beliefs, culture, social and economic differences among participants can contribute to delayed diagnosis of breast cancer. To improve early diagnosis, public health policymakers need to address these obstacles through targeted interventions. These may include raising awareness about breast cancer symptoms and the importance of timely diagnosis via mass media campaigns, improving appointment management systems to reduce delays, enhancing healthcare providers’ training to minimise diagnostic errors, expanding insurance coverage to lower financial barriers, and promoting patient-centred care with female healthcare professionals to build trust. Additionally, strengthening social support networks and community-based programmes can empower women to prioritise their health despite competing life demands. Implementing such multifaceted strategies can help reduce both patient-related and healthcare system-related barriers, ultimately facilitating earlier diagnosis and better outcomes.26 48

Like all studies, this research has some limitations. Although it was conducted at the national breast cancer referral centre in Tehran, which receives patients from across the country and thus provides cultural and geographical diversity, conducting the study at a single centre may limit the transferability of the findings. However, this site was chosen because it gave access to a wide range of patient experiences, which aligns with the qualitative aim of gaining in-depth and contextual understanding rather than broad statistical generalisation. The age range of participants was between 38 and 72 years, representing a relatively diverse group. However, it is plausible that perspectives from much younger or older patients were underrepresented. All participants had experienced a diagnostic delay of more than 1 month. The study aimed to explore the reasons behind diagnostic delays rather than to precisely measure or compare the length of delay periods. All patients were diagnosed with advanced breast cancer, defined in this study as stage III or higher. This definition includes both locally advanced (stage III) and metastatic (stage IV) disease. Focusing on this group allowed us to better understand factors contributing to late-stage diagnoses but may limit the applicability of findings to patients diagnosed at earlier stages. Additionally, the study primarily focused on barriers to timely diagnosis and did not specifically examine facilitators that might have helped some patients receive an earlier diagnosis. Exploring such factors could be a useful direction for future research. Finally, although in-depth interviews and probing techniques were used to enhance recall accuracy, variations in time since diagnosis among participants may have influenced how they remembered and described their experiences. Despite these limitations, the study’s rigorous methodology, sample diversity and transparent analytic process contribute to the trustworthiness and richness of the findings.

Conclusions

This study identifies multiple, interconnected factors contributing to delayed timely diagnosis of advanced breast cancer from the perspective of Iranian women. The primary perceived barriers reported by participants included poor appointment management and substandard care, discrepancies between care provided and patient expectations, misleading marketing practices, high costs of diagnostic procedures, insufficient knowledge, fear, spiritual beliefs, competing life priorities, inadequate social support and diagnostic errors. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive strategies that address both individual and systemic obstacles. Given the complexity and interrelated nature of various barriers, existing interventions may not sufficiently address cumulative underlying factors. Further research is essential to develop and rigorously evaluate culturally sensitive, targeted public health interventions to improve healthcare access, strengthen patient–provider communication and enhance public awareness about breast cancer screening and prevention. Moreover, investigating the broader experiences of women, including those who received timely diagnoses, could provide insights into facilitators of early detection, helping to inform effective community-based health initiatives. Such studies are crucial for filling current knowledge gaps and ultimately improving breast cancer outcomes among Iranian women.
