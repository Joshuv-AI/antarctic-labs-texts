Exploring the prognosis of rictor(+) in high-risk population: lasso regression for enhancing overall survival in primary gastric adenocarcinoma
Authors: Wenhua Cheng, Yu Chang, Jinfeng Ma, Yifan Li, Ruifang Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify optimal methods for predicting survival in high-risk Rictor (+) gastric adenocarcinoma patients post-gastrectomy.Methods676 patients diagnosed with gastric adenocarcinoma and Rictor (+) status who underwent radical gastrectomy at three medical centers between May 2005 and December 2022 were analyzed. The prognostic variables linked with overall survival were meticulously evaluated through the sophisticated lenses of multiple Cox Proportional Hazards regression and Lasso regression analyses.ResultsThe Cox regression model included gender, age, positive lymph node count, and biomarkers (HER2, P27, P53), achieving a C-index of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.723–0.796). The Lasso regression model incorporated age, pT stage, lymph node involvement, neural invasion, and tumor diameter, demonstrating superior predictive accuracy (C-index: 0.797; 95% CI: 0.729–0.865). Both models outperformed the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system (C-index: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.659–0.722). Calibration curves revealed strong concordance between predicted and observed survival outcomes. Decision curve analysis affirmed clinical utility, while risk reclassification metrics showed significant improvements over AJCC staging: net reclassification improvement (NRI) reached 0.218 (P < 0.01) and 0.178 (P = 0.033) in training and validation cohorts, respectively, with integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) values of 0.085 (P < 0.01) and 0.059 (P = 0.028).ConclusionOur findings underscore the enhanced predictive accuracy of Lasso regression over the traditional Cox regression model for identifying high-risk gastric adenocarcinoma patients with Rictor (+) status post-gastrectomy. This novel analytical approach may represent a pivotal advance in the stratification of patients at elevated risk, potentially guiding more precise preventive and therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04043-2.Keywords: Gastric adenocarcinoma, Rictor, Overall survival, Nomogram, Risk stratification

Introduction

Gastric cancer poses a major global health burden, ranking as the fifth most common cancer and third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. While D2 lymph node dissection and postoperative chemotherapy have become standard treatments, they yield only modest improvements in overall survival [2–4]. These persistent limitations underscore the urgent need for novel targeted therapies, especially for patients with advanced or recurrent disease [5]. Promising strategies include targeting human epidermal growth factor receptor-2 (HER2) and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), both of which have shown clinical benefit [6, 7]. Additionally, inhibition of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway—where Rictor serves as a core component of the rapamycin-insensitive mTORC2 complex—represents an emerging therapeutic avenue.

We found Rictor expression in 49.8% of gastric adenocarcinoma tissues, significantly higher than in adjacent non-malignant tissues, suggesting its potential role as a prognostic biomarker. This aligns with previous reports, such as that of Wang et al., who observed Rictor positivity in 51.6% of gastric cancer samples [8]. Multivariate analyses have consistently identified elevated Rictor expression as an independent predictor of poor survival, supported by studies linking Rictor to disease progression and unfavorable outcomes [9]. To address the heterogeneity in patient survival, we developed a predictive model incorporating Rictor expression for improved risk stratification. Combining Cox and Lasso regression models offers a more precise and biologically informed prognostic tool than AJCC staging alone. These models integrate clinicopathological and molecular data to enable individualized risk assessment and guide personalized treatment strategies. This approach aims to optimize therapeutic allocation—identifying patients who may benefit from targeted interventions while avoiding overtreatment—ultimately advancing precision medicine in gastric cancer management.

Methods

Data collection

This study retrospectively reviewed 1,366 patients who underwent radical gastrectomy for gastric adenocarcinoma between March 2006 and December 2022 at Shanxi Cancer Hospital, Hongtong County Hospital, and Yuanqu County Hospital. Immunohistochemical analysis identified 676 patients with Rictor-positive tumors. These patients were randomly allocated into a training cohort (n = 471) and a validation cohort (n = 205) in a 7:3 ratio. Inclusion criteria comprised: histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma, availability of complete clinicopathological and follow-up data, absence of major postoperative complications, and no history of other malignancies or non-cancer-related mortality. Patients with systemic malignancies, incomplete records, prior palliative procedures, or non-gastric cancers were excluded. Tumor staging was performed according to the AJCC 8th edition TNM classification system. The study was approved by the Shanxi Cancer Hospital Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent, and data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Patient selection and study methodology are outlined in Figs. 1 and 2. This retrospective analysis included patients with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma who underwent R0 resection with curative intent. We collected comprehensive clinicopathological data including: patient demographics (gender, age at surgery), pathological features (vascular invasion, neural invasion, pT stage, number of positive lymph nodes, Lauren classification), tumor characteristics (size, gastrectomy type), and biomarker expression profiles (AE1/AE3, CK20, CDX-2, SATB-2, SYN, P27, P53, CGA, CD56, MLH1, PMS2, Her-2, MSH2, MSH6). Overall survival (OS) was determined from surgery to death using electronic medical records and oncologist consultations. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional review board guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

Immunohistochemistry(IHC)

To evaluate Rictor expression, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections from 676 gastric cancer cases were analyzed by immunohistochemistry (IHC). A qualified pathologist first re-examined all H&E-stained tumor sections. Tissue sections were dewaxed in xylene and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating for 20 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary mouse monoclonal anti-Rictor antibody (sc-271081, Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:700 dilution). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse/rabbit immunoglobulin (PV-6000, ZSGB Biotechnology) for 20 minutes at room temperature, and finally developed with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB). Rictor immunostaining was assessed independently by two pathologists using a semi-quantitative scoring system. For each section, ten high-power fields (HPF) were evaluated. A score was derived from the sum of two parameters: the percentage of positively stained cells (0: none; 1: <10%; 2: 10–50%; 3: >50%) and the staining intensity (0: none; 1: light yellow; 2: pale brown; 3: dark brown). The final immunoreactivity score (IRS) ranged from 0 to 6. An IRS of 0–2 was defined as negative for Rictor expression, while a score of 3–6 was considered positive.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. The data were split into training and validation sets. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify independent prognostic factors, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables with p < 0.05 were included in nomograms predicting 3- and 5-year overall survival. The model was internally validated using 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Model performance was assessed using Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent AUC, with discrimination interpreted as: 0.5–0.7 (poor), 0.7–0.9 (moderate), and > 0.9 (excellent). Calibration was evaluated with calibration curves and clinical utility using decision curve analysis (DCA). The net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were used to quantify incremental predictive value. An NRI or IDI > 0 indicates improved reclassification or discrimination, respectively, of the new model compared to the reference. Patients were stratified into risk groups based on nomogram scores, and survival differences were assessed with Kaplan–Meier curves. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Analyses were performed using R (v4.3.2) and SPSS (v25.0).

Results

Essential characteristics of the training cohort and validation cohort of rictor (+)

We assembled a training cohort of 388 gastric cancer patients with concurrent adjacent tissue infiltration and a validation cohort of 171 patients with similar invasion patterns. The cohorts were balanced for confounding variables (Table 1). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed Rictor expression in 49.5% (676/1,366) of tumor specimens, with staining localized predominantly to the tumor cell cytoplasm (Fig. 3). In contrast, Rictor was detected in only 21.9% (112/512) of non-neoplastic gastric tissue samples. Multivariate analysis confirmed Rictor expression as a strong, independent predictor of postoperative survival in gastric adenocarcinoma (Table 2, Model 3).

Development and validation of the prediction model of OS for rictor (+)

To identify factors influencing overall survival in gastric cancer, we analyzed a cohort of 371 patients. Key prognostic variables—including gender, age, lymph node status, and expression of Her-2, P27, and P53—are detailed in Table 3. We integrated these factors into a nomogram to predict 3- and 5-year survival probabilities (Fig. 4). The model demonstrated powerful predictive value for patients with Rictor-positive tumor. The nomogram achieved a concordance index (C-index) of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.723–0.796), outperforming the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system (C-index 0.69, 95% CI: 0.659–0.722). This indicates that our multidimensional model provides a more accurate prognostic tool for the management of gastric cancer.

Nomogram model designed to predict the 3-year and 5-year OS of patients based on cox regression

To identify key determinants of overall survival (OS) in gastric adenocarcinoma, we applied Lasso regression to select the most predictive variables for our prognostic model. Combined with Cox regression, this analysis refined the covariates to a concise set of six significant parameters: age, pT stage, lymph node status, neural invasion, and maximum tumor diameter (Fig. 5). We integrated these variables into a nomogram to predict 3- and 5-year OS probabilities for Rictor-positive patients (Fig. 6). To predict prognosis in Rictor-positive gastric adenocarcinoma, we developed a scoring system based on five clinicopathological variables selected by Lasso regression. Points were assigned for each variable as follows: age (20 years = 0 points; 52 years = 100 points), pT stage (T1/T2 = 21; T3 = 22), number of positive lymph nodes (0 = 22; 3–6 = 80), neural invasion (negative = 22; positive = 45), and maximum tumor diameter (1 cm = 8; 8 cm = 44). The total score, calculated by summing individual points, was positively correlated with overall survival and can be used to estimate predicted survival probability using a corresponding nomogram axis. Validated on our training cohort, the nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.797 (95% CI: 0.729–0.865) for OS, significantly outperforming the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system (C-index: 0.683, 95% CI: 0.618–0.747). The Lasso model’s superior predictive performance was further confirmed by a Net Reclassification Index (NRI) of 0.231 (p < 0.01) and an Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) of 0.086 (p < 0.01) in the training cohort (Table 4). This enhancement was replicated in the validation cohort (NRI: 0.178, p = 0.033; IDI: 0.059, p = 0.028; Table 4), demonstrating its robust clinical applicability.

Internal validation showed AUCs of 0.809 (95% CI: 0.709–0.909) for 3-year OS and 0.830 (95% CI: 0.749–0.950) for 5-year OS. Corresponding external validation results were 0.767 (95% CI: 0.670–0.841) and 0.718 (95% CI: 0.528–0.820), respectively (Fig. 8C, D). The Lasso model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy across these time points.

Our analysis demonstrates the prognostic utility of the Cox and Lasso regression models integrated into the nomogram. The tool robustly predicted long-term survival in gastric carcinoma patients, with consistent performance across validation cohorts. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate the clinical net benefit of the nomogram for predicting 3- and 5-year overall survival (OS) compared to the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system. The Cox regression model achieved a C-index of 0.759 (95% CI: 0.723–0.796) in internal validation, surpassing the TNM staging system (C-index: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.659–0.722). External validation yielded a C-index of 0.735 (95% CI: 0.681–0.788) for the Cox model, compared to 0.666 (95% CI: 0.618–0.714) for TNM staging (Fig. 9A-D). The Lasso regression model showed similarly improved performance, with internal and external validation C-indices of 0.797 (95% CI: 0.729–0.865) and 0.754 (95% CI: 0.647–0.860), respectively (Fig. 9E-H). These results confirm the superior predictive accuracy of both regression models over conventional TNM staging.

Risk scoring of the stratification system of OS for rictor (+)

Using a Cox regression-based nomogram, we calculated individualized risk scores to stratify patients into distinct prognostic categories. The optimal cutoff for overall survival (OS) was determined by X-tile analysis in a training cohort of 388 patients. A log-rank test confirmed significant survival differences between the resulting risk strata. Applying a score threshold of 136.98, we stratified the entire study population (n = 676) into low- and high-risk groups (Fig. 10A). The low-risk group included 323 training and 74 validation patients. In comparison, the high-risk group comprised 129 training and 126 validation patients. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significantly divergent survival curves between risk groups (p < 0.001) across the entire cohort, training set, and validation set (Fig. 10A–C). The low-risk group exhibited a median survival of 123 months in both the whole cohort and the training set. In contrast, the high-risk group showed consistently shorter median survival, ranging from 36 to 38 months across all cohorts. This clear stratification confirms the nomogram’s discriminative power in predicting survival outcomes.

The Lasso and Cox regression analyses yielded divergent risk stratifications. Using a threshold score of 206.29, we stratified the entire cohort (n = 676) into distinct low- and high-risk groups (Fig. 10D–F). The low-risk group comprised 322 patients from the training set and 130 from the validation set. The high-risk group included 149 training and 75 validation patients. Survival curves demonstrated significant stratification between risk groups across all cohorts (p < 0.001; Fig. 10D–F). Notably, median survival was not reached in any low-risk subgroup. In contrast, high-risk patients showed a median survival of 40 months in both the whole cohort and training set, and 36 months in the validation set. This clear separation confirms the model’s discriminative capacity for risk assessment.

Discussion

Rictor is a core component of the rapamycin-insensitive mTORC2 complex, where it serves as a scaffolding protein essential for mTOR’s catalytic activity [10]. This 192 kDa protein shares significant sequence homology with yeast AVO3 and is highly conserved across eukaryotes, underscoring its fundamental biological role. Although the precise functions of its structural domains remain unclear, Rictor contains a conserved ~ 200 amino acid region and a distinctive extended C-terminal segment that differentiates it from related proteins [11]. Elevated Rictor expression has been correlated with clinical outcomes in gastric adenocarcinoma, though its prognostic value remains controversial [12, 13]. For instance, while Bian et al. suggested that Rictor alone may not serve as a reliable prognostic marker [9], Cao et al. reported that it promotes tumor progression via Akt-mediated Cav-1 activation and is associated with poorer survival [14]. Rictor gene amplification has also been proposed as a potential therapeutic target in gastric cancer [15]. Furthermore, Lang et al. identified an Akt-mTORC2 feedback loop that suppresses Rictor in gastric and pancreatic cancer models [16], and Wang et al. linked high Rictor expression to adverse prognosis in gastric cancer patients [17].In our previous retrospective analysis, Rictor positivity was an independent predictor of reduced overall survival [18]. Here, with extended follow-up, we aimed to clarify the prognostic significance of Rictor in gastric adenocarcinoma and develop a refined predictive model for overall survival based on Rictor expression status.

P27, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, regulates cell cycle progression by blocking the G2/M phase transition, thereby inducing apoptosis or promoting quiescence (G0) [19]. Its dysregulation can lead to uncontrolled proliferation [20], and it also functions as a tumor suppressor involved in differentiation, drug resistance, cell migration, and inflammatory responses [21]. Reduced P27 expression is associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer [22–24]. The tumor suppressor p53 acts as a transcription factor that restrains tumorigenesis by coordinating cellular responses to stress—such as DNA damage, oncogene activation, or oxidative stress—triggering cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, or differentiation [25, 26]. Mutations or reduced expression of p53 contribute to gastric cancer development and progression [27–29]. Both P27 and P53 have emerged as predictive markers in Rictor-positive gastric cancer. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the functional and prognostic interplay between these proteins in this context.

In 1996, the challenges posed by high-dimensional data led Robert Tibshirani to introduce the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)—an embedded feature selection method designed to improve prediction accuracy and identify relevant predictors. The LASSO algorithm performs variable selection by applying an L₁-penalty term to the least squares regression, shrinking some coefficients and setting others to zero. The degree of shrinkage is controlled by the tuning parameter λ, which is optimized during the regularization process. A key advantage of LASSO is its ability to enhance both prediction accuracy and model interpretability through effective variable selection and coefficient shrinkage [30]. In physics and numerical mathematics—fields where regularization originated—regression model fitting can be viewed as an inverse problem, wherein model parameters are estimated from observed data. Such issues are often ill-posed, as small fluctuations in the data can lead to unstable parameter estimates (e.g., overfitting), non-unique solutions, or complete ill-conditioning. Regularization mitigates these issues by introducing stabilizing constraints, reducing sensitivity to noise, and improving generalization. Notably, the intercept (bias term) is typically excluded from regularization, as it is not subject to the same overfitting concerns as slope coefficients [31].

To improve prognostic stratification in gastric cancer, we developed and compared predictive models using Cox and Lasso regression analyses based on clinical, pathological, and molecular data. A multivariable Cox model incorporating gender, age, HER-2 status, lymph node involvement, and P27 and P53 expression was constructed to estimate survival probabilities in Rictor-positive patients. In parallel, a Lasso regression model was built using key clinicopathological variables—age, pT stage, lymph node status, neural invasion, and maximum tumor diameter—to create a clinically applicable nomogram. While both methods have been used in survival modeling, our study is the first to compare their performance in Rictor-positive gastric cancer directly. The Lasso-based model demonstrated significantly higher predictive accuracy than the AJCC 8th edition TNM staging system in both training and validation cohorts. In contrast, the Cox model showed consistent but less discriminative performance across cohorts, a difference potentially attributable to sample size limitations in the validation set. These results position the Lasso regression approach as a superior tool for prognostication in this patient population [32, 33].

Through rigorous internal and external validation, we established a robust predictive model that demonstrated consistent performance across diverse clinical settings. The Lasso regression-based nomogram exhibited superior discriminative ability compared to the Cox model, with higher C-index and AUC values for both 3- and 5-year survival predictions in all validation cohorts. This enhanced prognostic accuracy enables refined risk stratification for Rictor-positive gastric cancer patients. The resulting nomogram provides clinicians with a practical tool for individualized treatment planning and improved clinical decision-making. By integrating complex tumor biology with predictive analytics, our approach offers a precise framework for personalizing gastric cancer management and optimizing patient outcomes.

We developed a prognostic scoring system that effectively stratifies Rictor-positive patients into distinct risk categories. This tool enables personalized treatment planning by clearly differentiating between low-risk patients, who may avoid overtreatment, and high-risk patients, who may benefit from more aggressive therapeutic strategies. The visually intuitive scoring system provides clinicians with a practical decision-support tool for managing gastric cancer patients.

However, several limitations of our study must be acknowledged. Our data were derived from three medical centers, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. External validation across diverse healthcare settings is required to confirm the robustness of our model. Additionally, the model included patients across all stages of gastric carcinoma without stratification, which may affect its prognostic accuracy within specific disease subgroups. Furthermore, the assessment of tumor biomarkers—including AE1/AE3 and Rictor—relied on qualitative rather than quantitative methods. Future studies should incorporate disease-stage-specific analyses and quantitative biomarker profiling to enhance the model’s precision and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the enhanced predictive accuracy of Lasso regression over the traditional Cox regression model for identifying high-risk gastric adenocarcinoma patients with Rictor (+) status post-gastrectomy. This novel analytical approach may represent a pivotal advance in the stratification of patients at elevated risk, potentially guiding more precise preventive and therapeutic strategies.
