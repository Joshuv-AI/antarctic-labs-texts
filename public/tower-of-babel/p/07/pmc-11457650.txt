Key informant perspectives on overcoming HPV vaccination barriers in low-immunization NY counties
Authors: Samantha E Hanley, Karina Ohri, Telisa Stewart, Maribel Vargas, Arielle Hanley, Elena C Shaw, Nicholas Allis, Michael Seserman, Jana Shaw
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) infection is an important public health concern, with nearly 2,900 cases of HPV-related cancers reported in New York State (NYS) each year. Despite concerted efforts to enhance vaccine uptake in NYS, HPV vaccination rates among 13–15-year-olds failed to meet the 2020 healthy People target, and continue to lag behind the 2030 goal, of 80%. In counties with low immunization levels, understanding factors influencing decision-making among unvaccinated adolescents is crucial. This study aimed to identify barriers, facilitators, and potential interventions to improve HPV vaccine uptake. Qualitative semi-structured interviews were conducted with key informants in 15 NYS counties within the lowest quartile of HPV vaccine coverage among 13-year-old adolescents. Public health representatives, including Immunization Quality Improvement for Providers (IQIP) consultants, vaccine coordinators, medical directors, and primary care providers, were identified through purposive and snowball sampling. Interviews explored vaccination coverage knowledge, barriers and facilitating factors, and recommended strategies for improvement. All conversations were audio-recorded, transcribed, and analyzed using ATLAS.ti. Forty-four interviews were conducted, revealing barriers to HPV vaccination such as limited vaccine knowledge, vaccine misinformation, and accessibility. Key informants asserted barriers could be primarily addressed through education efforts, such increased public awareness, improved parent–doctor conversations, additional provider education on vaccine benefits, and supplementary education in patient spaces. Targeted education efforts and improved provider communication strategies have the potential to bolster HPV vaccination rates in NYS. These findings offer valuable insights for guiding future initiatives in communities facing significant barriers to vaccination.KEYWORDS: Human papillomavirus, vaccine, key informant, barriers, education, qualitative interview

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a serious public health issue and is increasingly recognized as a cause of preventable harm.1,2 Identified as the most common sexually transmitted infection in the United States, 4 out of 5 people develop the infection at some point in their lives.3 The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimate that currently 42 million people live with HPV in the United States, with about 13 million new infections occurring each year. While most cases are asymptomatic and resolve over time, persistent oncogenic strains of HPV may lead to cervical, anal, oropharyngeal, penile, vaginal, and vulvar cancers.4 The HPV vaccine is considered the most effective way to prevent infection and associated sequalae.5

Since its introduction in 2006, the HPV vaccine has shown efficacy in protecting against infection and high-risk oncogenic strains of the virus.5 According to the CDC, immunization has the potential to prevent more than 90% of HPV-associated cancers; it is recommended for both boys and girls at age 11–12 years. The vaccine works best when administered prior to HPV exposure and can be administered as early as age 9 years.6 Despite the demonstrated success of the vaccine, national data indicate that only 62.6% of adolescents aged 13–17 years were up to date with HPV vaccination in 2022, with vaccination coverage levels remaining similar to 2021 levels.1 In NYS, vaccination completion rates among adolescents aged 13–17 years also remain low at 70.7%.7 Wide variation in vaccine coverage across counties is particularly observed, with completion rates of 13-year-old adolescents ranging from 12.5% to 67.6% (with an average of 38.8%) in 2022.8 These rates remain below the 2030 healthy People target coverage goal of 80%.9

While trends in vaccine coverage among adolescents in NYS suggest HPV vaccination uptake is increasing, rates are still low compared to other vaccines. Vaccine hesitancy is considered a significant factor contributing to delays or refusal of the HPV vaccine, influenced by concerns about vaccine safety and side effects, perceptions of the vaccine’s necessity, and a lack of comprehensive information about the efficacy and benefits of vaccination.10 To address these challenges, the CDC advocates for a multifaceted strategy to improve vaccine uptake. This approach includes incorporating evidence-based practices such as targeted educational campaigns to address specific misconceptions, strong provider recommendations that leverage clinical interactions to reinforce the vaccine’s importance, and school-based vaccination programs that integrate the vaccine into routine adolescent health services. Additionally, enhancing access through low-cost options at convenient locations, implementing quality improvement (QI) projects within healthcare settings, and engaging community-based interventions and streamlined administration processes have all been shown to bolster vaccination rates and ensure broader acceptance and uptake.11–14

In order to achieve target HPV vaccination goals in NYS, the New York State Department of Health (NYSDOH) and the American Cancer Society (ACS) have collaborated with other immunization and cancer prevention partners including the New York State HPV Coalition.15 The CDC has also implemented a Vaccines for Children (VFC) provider level-immunization quality improvement program to improve timely pediatric vaccination.16 Furthermore, the Immunization Quality Improvement for Providers (IQIP) is currently working to leverage immunization information system (IIS) functionality to strengthen immunization practice, regularly schedule patient vaccination appointments, and strongly recommend eligible patients complete the vaccination series.

Notwithstanding ongoing efforts to enhance vaccination rates statewide, there remains limited understanding of the specific barriers and facilitators in counties where low vaccine immunization levels continue to persist. In order to support current initiatives to increase HPV vaccine completion rates in NYS, we carried out a semi-qualitiative study to identify barriers, facilitators, and preferred vaccine strategies in areas with the lowest levels of HPV immunization. We sought to gain a nuanced understanding from healthcare providers, public health representatives, and vaccine coordinators across targeted counties, as their direct involvement in vaccine delivery, as well as their ability to identify both individual and system-level challenges, make them unique and essential informants.

Methods

Theory

This project adapted a community-based participatory research approach (A-CBPR), which offered a framework for equitably involving experts and stakeholders throughout the research process. This approach recognizes the importance of engaging key informants in the development of the survey, sampling strategies, and interpretation of the analysis.17–19

Design

We conducted semi-qualitative, semi-structured interviews with key informants to identify barriers, facilitators, and appropriate strategies to improve HPV vaccine uptake. We interviewed forty-four key informants in counties within the lowest quartile of complete HPV vaccine series among 13-year-old adolescents using 2021 data, as defined as “high concern” by the NYSDOH. This was inclusive of the following 15 counties, ranging in coverage between 11.9% and 25.3%: Lewis, Jefferson, Fulton, Ulster, Essex, Putnam, Franklin, Hamilton, Rockland, St. Lawrence, Yates, Tioga, Richmond, Dutchess, and Sullivan.

Participants and setting

In collaboration with the New York State Association of County Health Officials (NYSACHO), the New York State HPV Coalition, and the NYSDOH Bureau of Immunization, we identified and interviewed a public health representative within each county during the summer of 2023. Each representative was equipped with specialized knowledge in HPV vaccination. At least two additional community experts were identified via purposive and snowball sampling. Informants included IQIP consultants, vaccine coordinators, medical directors, and primary care providers.

Data collection, management, and analysis

Interviewers were required to attend a 3-hour training session, and pilot test the interview process with 1) a clinician scientist with expertise in HPV vaccination, infectious disease, and public health and 2) two other team members. All interviews were guided with an 18-question interview script and prompt guide developed using previously validated survey questions, both of which were pilot-tested and revised before use for key informant interviews. Key informants consented to audio recording, and interviews were transcribed via Webex automatic transcription. All interviews were completely voluntary and lasted approximately 30 minutes. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at SUNY Upstate Medical University.

Qualitative data were analyzed using an inductive approach. Data immersion and open coding were used to develop a data codebook. All codes were examined for congruency and for potential overlap with other codes by code definition. Redundant codes were combined, and code definitions were confirmed. To facilitate coding and validation, all transcripts were loaded into ATLAS.ti. Four coders were trained on the codebook. Coder A and Coder B were each randomly assigned to 22 transcripts. Each coder independently reviewed assigned transcripts, selected quotation excerpts, and coded the excerpts using the codebook. The codes were then stripped from the transcripts. Coder C (assigned Coder A’s transcripts) and Coder D (assigned Coders B’s transcripts) re-coded the quotations independently. An inter-reliability agreement of 99% was achieved using a simple percent agreement. Three other team members then jointly reviewed the codebook and discussed patterns of code saturation; emerging themes were identified.

Sociodemographic and role characteristics, and quantitative data were summarized by standard descriptives via IBM SPSS Statistics, v25. The SUNY Upstate Institutional Review Board determined the project was exempt from IRB review, project #2044892.

Barriers and facilitators

In discussing perceived commonalities among the unvaccinated population (Table 3, question 5), themes identified included general vaccine hesitancy (n = 14, 31.8%), vaccine mistrust among the public (n = 10, 22.7%), religious community beliefs (n = 10, 22.7%), and low socioeconomic status (SES) (n = 9, 20.5%). Referenced barriers to vaccination (Table 3, Question 7) varied in range, but limited vaccine knowledge (n = 13, 29.5%), vaccine misinformation (n = 12, 27.3%), and accessibility (n = 11, 25.0%) emerged as the top-3 barriers faced in their respective county. Most respondents suggested that barriers slowing vaccine uptake in their county (Table 3, Question 8) could be addressed through education efforts; themes identified included increased public awareness (n = 18, 40.9%), improved parent-doctor conversations (n = 18, 40.9%), additional provider education on HPV vaccine benefits (n = 17, 38.6%), and supplementary education in patient spaces (n = 15, 34.1%).

Role specific questions

Key informants were asked role-specific questions to better understand nuanced perspectives on vaccination rates (Table 4). In discussing emphasis on HPV vaccination, most IQIP consultants (n = 7, 63.6%), 47.1% (n = 8) vaccine coordinators, and 50.0% (n = 4) of medical directors reported focusing on HPV vaccination “to a great extent,” compared to other childhood vaccines. More than half (n = 9, 52.9%) of vaccine coordinators, 50.0% (n = 4) medical directors, and 27.3% (n = 3) IQIP consultants disclosed that they “somewhat” focus on HPV vaccination rates, compared to other childhood vaccines. Vaccine coordinators with county immunization coalitions were more likely to report that their coalition “somewhat” focused on increasing HPV vaccination rates compared to other childhood vaccines (n = 5, 45.5%) while suggesting that their coalition may be improved through increased education efforts and community engagement.

In discussing how the IQIP program can be improved amongst IQIP consultants at the county and state level to facilitate HPV vaccination, themes identified included improvement of communication (n = 3, 27.3%) and of education (n = 2, 18.2%) amongst providers, parents, and children. Vaccine coordinators suggested that specifically education among providers (n = 4, 23.5%) would improve the Vaccine for Children (VFC) Program. Among primary care providers, more than half (n = 14, 66.7%) reported currently having quality improvement (QI) strategies in place to improve HPV vaccination rates, and 47.6% (n = 10) stated that they currently have a HPV vaccine champion at their practice.

When asked about the reasons why some providers may be hesitant to offer HPV vaccination to patients, PCPs cited motivations specific to the practice or personal beliefs (n = 13, 61.9%), discomfort in discussing sex with parents and children (n = 7, 33.3%), cultural beliefs and/or lack of trust (n = 6, 28.6%), and fear of parental rejection (n = 4, 19.0%). In discussing provider practice, almost all providers (n = 20, 95.2%) reported they always review immunizations or vaccination status with their patients during annual well visits. PCPs also reported strongly recommending HPV vaccination for both boys and girls similarly across age groups, with 42.9% (n = 9) stating they strongly recommend HPV vaccination for all age groups.

Recommendations to improve HPV vaccination in NYS

All key informants were interested in discussing recommendations for the vaccine strategy within their county. Key informants were asked, “What strategies are you aware of that have been used to improve vaccination rates in general?,” (Table 5, Question 15) and several themes were identified. Emerging themes identified included patient/provider education (n = 26, 59.1%), scheduling vaccination appointments (n = 15, 34.1%), strong provider recommendations (n = 14, 31.8%), and improved patient-provider conversations (n = 14, 31.8%). Further discussion revealed that informants believe educational campaigns, through public service announcements and social media, for example, may be important in improving current strategies. When asked, “What strategy or combination of strategies do you believe would be most effective for improving HPV vaccination rates in your county?,” (Table 5, question 17) themes identified included increased public awareness through media (n = 28, 63.6%), strong provider recommendation (n = 26, 59.1%), increased vaccine access (n = 13, 29.5%), additional provider education on HPV vaccine benefits (n = 11, 25.0), and school immunization requirement of the HPV vaccine (n = 9, 20.5%).

Discussion

The findings of our study shed light on the persistent challenges surrounding HPV vaccination rate improvement across 15 counties in NYS from the perspective of healthcare providers, public health representatives, and vaccine coordinators. Despite ongoing efforts through public health initiatives, these counties continue to grapple with suboptimal vaccination coverage among 13-year-old adolescents, ranging in completion rates between 12.5% and 67.6% in 2022. Our results underscore the urgent need for innovative strategies, with a focus on addressing several key barriers to HPV vaccination. The top three barriers identified include limited vaccine knowledge among patients and providers, misinformation about the vaccine, and accessibility. While we recognize that these identified barriers are not novel and have been reported in previous studies, these issues continue to persist, suggesting that 1) there may be a lack of adequate resources; 2) there may be insufficient local support to implement best practices effectively, or systemic issues within healthcare settings, such as lack of training or time constraints; and 3) public health campaigns may not be effectively reaching or resonating with the targeted populations, indicating a need for more tailored, culturally sensitive messaging in areas with low vaccination rates. According to key informants, addressing these barriers may be primarily achieved through targeted educational efforts, such as enhancing public awareness of vaccine information, facilitating improved parent-doctor conversations, providing additional education to healthcare providers on HPV vaccination through policy consideration for a school entry vaccination requirement, and engaging scheduling and appropriate follow-up strategies.

One of the primary implications of our findings is the critical need for targeted interventions to address barriers and to enhance vaccine uptake within these communities. Consistent with prior research, education emerged as a central strategy for overcoming vaccine hesitancy and increasing awareness of the benefits of vaccination.20 Education efforts to generate public awareness of HPV and associated sequalae among patients, dissipating any misinformation that exists and underscoring the preventative role of vaccination among vaccine hesitant families, may be key in NYS. Prior research suggests that individualized risk communication and education interventions that highlight vaccine benefits work to increase vaccination intention or uptake.21,22 For example, offering reliable information to parents and adolescents through various media outlets may encourage subsequent conversation with healthcare providers during patient appointments. Patients also actively engage with health information in clinic waiting rooms and patient spaces, with audiovisual channels garnering the most attention.23 Use of media, print, and other interactive modes in clinics may represent an opportunity to engage patients with important health information, and encourage ongoing conversation.

Our findings also highlight the pivotal role of healthcare providers in promoting HPV vaccination uptake. Enhancing provider education and communication skills, particularly in discussing the importance of HPV vaccination with parents and adolescents, is critical in increasing vaccine acceptance and uptake.24 Similar to findings in prior research, our study identifies a need for equipping healthcare providers with accurate information and effective communication strategies to address vaccine hesitancy.13 High-quality recommendations from providers have been shown to increase HPV vaccine series initiation by up to three times, and vaccine series completion by up to nine times.13,22 However, existing research shows that some providers lack adequate knowledge to offer strong vaccine recommendation to their patients.13 Therefore, targeted provider education about benefits and safety of the HPV vaccine may be important in increasing HPV vaccination recommendation among adolescents and their families.

In developing strong communication strategies, it is recommended that providers are also educated about the importance of engaging a presumptive approach when recommending the HPV vaccine. Research suggests that an approach that assumes parents will immunize their child is more effective than discussing the option to vaccinate.25 Adopting this approach may be critical in encouraging vaccine acceptance among vaccine hesitant parents. Previous studies reveal that healthcare providers often underestimate the significance of HPV vaccinations to parents and overestimate parental hesitancy.26 Providers who experience high rates of refusal or deferral are also more likely to change the strength of HPV recommendation and communication style in anticipation of vaccine hesitancy.25 Therefore, provider education should focus on the delivery of consistent provider communication strategies to patients free of cognitive bias.26,27 Support from state and local health departments, as well as clinic providers, is key in the adoption of this methodology.

Key informants also emphasized the importance of enhancing public awareness with regard to HPV vaccination. As such, fostering community engagement may serve as a key opportunity to increase awareness of HPV vaccine benefits and safety. Public endorsements from community leaders and healthcare workers can work to amplify existing vaccination messaging and engender trust within the community. Community leaders often possess deep-rooted connections within their communities and are uniquely positioned to disseminate accurate information, address misconceptions, and facilitate access to vaccination services.28,29 Previous research shows that aligning vaccination advocacy with trusted figures, such as faith-based leaders, significantly impacts vaccination acceptance and uptake. Moreover, tapping into the resources and networks of respected community members grants an opportunity to reach hesitant populations and improve vaccine access.28 By leveraging these trusted sources, vaccination campaigns can resonate with audiences of diverse demographics, driving increased awareness and vaccine acceptance.

Notably, one in five informants noted a lack of a school vaccination requirement as a barrier to HPV vaccination in NYS. As of March 2024, only five U.S. jurisdictions (Hawaii, Rhode Island, Virginia, Puerto Rico, and the District of Columbia) require the HPV vaccine for school entry.30,31 Current research suggests that HPV school vaccination requirements are associated with increased rates of vaccine initiation and represent a feasible and efficient means to improve vaccine coverage.11,12 Vaccination mandates have also been associated with improved provider recommendation, and greater vaccine uptake among diverse socioeconomic groups.32 Requiring HPV vaccination for school entry may facilitate even distribution of vaccine knowledge and promote equity in vaccine access. Given immunization requirements for school attendance have also been successful in improving vaccination rates for other vaccines, the potential impact on HPV vaccination uptake warrants further exploration.33 However, it is important to acknowledge that immunization laws are established at the state level, not the county level, which may limit the direct applicability of such recommendations to the specific counties targeted in this study. Therefore, while the discussion of state-level policy provides valuable context, policy recommendations should also address the unique needs and circumstances of counties with lower vaccination rates.

Lastly, implementation of follow-up strategies to track vaccination rates and encourage completion of vaccine series, may be key. Previous research suggests proactive follow-up measures, such as reminder messaging, significantly increase the likelihood of initation and completion of vaccination among adolescents.34 Reminder systems also represent a practical and cost-effective strategy to promote HPV vaccination education.35,36 By leveraging digital platforms, such as text messaging and e-mail reminders, healthcare providers not only engage with patients and caregivers to schedule vaccination appointments but are also able to regularly share important information about the risks associated with HPV infection and the benefits of vaccination. Even more, personalized reminders tailored to individual vaccination schedules have been shown to be effective in addressing vaccine hesitancy among patients and caregivers.35 By regularly monitoring vaccination data, healthcare providers, public health officials, and policymakers can identify areas of improvement and maximize vaccination efforts.

Our study has several limitations that should be addressed. A focus on the 15 counties with the lowest vaccination coverage in NYS may restrict the transferability of our findings to broader populations. Additionally, the inclusion of key informants primarily from public health and healthcare sectors may have biased our findings toward perspectives within these domains. The possibility of social desirability bias among key informants may have also influenced the data provided. Lastly, while we identified barriers to HPV vaccination, our study does not assess the efficacy of interventions and strategies aimed at addressing these barriers. Future studies should include a more diverse range of stakeholders, including community members and adolescents themselves, and incorporate more quantitative methods to obtain a greater comprehensive understanding of the barriers to HPV vaccination and inform tailored interventions. It is also important to note that vaccine hesitancy can vary widely across different demographic groups, influenced by factors such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and cultural beliefs. For instance, younger populations may be concerned about vaccine necessity, while individuals from lower-income backgrounds might encounter access barriers that heighten their hesitancy. To address these varied concerns, health education interventions should be tailored for each demographic group. For example, campaigns aimed at adolescents may emphasize vaccine benefits, whereas those targeting lower socioeconomic groups may advertise accessible vaccine sites. Future research should seek to understand these demographic variations in vaccine hesitancy to develop more effective, targeted strategies for increasing HPV vaccination rates.

In conclusion, our study highlights the importance of addressing ongoing barriers to HPV vaccination in counties with low immunization coverage in NYS. Targeted comprehensive interventions, including education efforts, exploration of school vaccination requirements, and engagement of appropriate scheduling and follow-up strategies, are essential to improving HPV vaccine uptake and reducing the burden of HPV-related disease. Our findings provide valuable insights for policymakers, public health officials, and healthcare providers to develop and implement strategies aimed at improving HPV vaccination rates, and to ultimately advance public health goals.
