Innovative 3D structured light technology for robotic-assisted surgery in selected spontaneous medium-volume basal ganglia hematomas: enhancing precision, surgical nuance, and clinical outcomes
Authors: FuMing Yang, ChengHao Jiang, Wei Wang, ChangYi Zhao, XinRong Chen, Huan Liao, HaiTao Zhang, GuangTing Qiu, JiaoTian Xu, LaiXing Wang, GuoSheng Han, ZhiWen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA structured-light robotic system enables noninvasive surface-based facial registration for patient-to-image alignment; however, it has not been widely adopted for basal ganglia hematoma treatment. This study evaluates its safety, accuracy, and efficiency in patients with basal ganglia hematomas.MethodsRetrospective consecutive patients with spontaneous basal ganglia hematomas (10–30 mL) admitted from January 2022 to January 2024 were grouped as surgical or non-surgical. Outcomes were analyzed, focusing on registration accuracy and surgical efficiency.ResultsBaseline preoperative hematoma volume, GCS, NIHSS, and coagulation disorders were comparable between groups (p > 0.05). Mean registration time was 120 ± 56 s and surgery time 102.22 ± 6.33 min; hematoma clearance was 72% initially and 93% at 7 days. Entry-point discrepancy was 2.2 ± 0.7 mm; catheter tip alignment in the upper/middle/lower hematoma segments was 4%/80%/16%, with a maximum 5.7-mm deviation from the midline axis. The surgery group showed faster recovery with higher 24 h-GCS (p < 0.001) and better NIHSS/ADL/mRS than non-surgery (p < 0.001 at all time points; p = 0.003 and p = 0.026 at 30 and 90 days; p = 0.041). At 90 days, facial palsy, sensory loss, and ulcers were similar (p > 0.05), whereas pneumonia (p = 0.034) and UTIs (p = 0.002) were lower in the surgery group. No mortality, bleeding, or reoperations occurred.ConclusionsSurface registration using the 3D structured light technique is a fast and precise alternative treatment for selected patients with spontaneous medium-volume basal ganglia hematomas, which can improve clinical efficiency while maintaining sufficient accuracy and safety to meet clinical requirements.Trial registrationretrospectively registered with approval by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Fourth people’s hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine (No. 2022327-001).Keywords: Three-dimensional structured-light technique, robot-assisted stereotactic hematoma evacuation, medium-volume basal ganglia hemorrhage, surgical technique

Introduction

Over the past decade, robot-assisted neuronavigation has enhanced precision in complex neurosurgeries [1–3]. The 3D structured light for surface-based registration (SBR) since 2020 allows marker-free alignment with preoperative images [4–7]. It’s been shown to be more precise and less invasive than traditional registration methods [8, 9]. Jie Chen et al. [10] and Du Cai et al. [11] have demonstrated the technology’s accuracy and safety in stereotactic procedures using the SR1-3D robot, with mean errors within 1.5–3.2 mm. These studies support that 3D structured light technology in robot-assisted surgery meet the precision demands of neurosurgical procedures. This enables the creation of personalized plans that can minimize damage to critical brain functions and shorten surgery times, compared to traditional stereotactic methods.

Basal ganglia hemorrhage can cause severe neurological deficits, including hemiplegia and hemianopia, and requires prompt neurosurgical evaluation [12]. Larger hematomas (> 30 mL) are generally managed surgically using approaches such as craniotomy or stereotactic puncture.13, 14 In contrast, management strategies for small-to-moderate hematomas remain less well defined [15, 16]. Recent randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that minimally invasive surgery (MIS) is associated with improved outcomes in patients with larger intracerebral hematomas, particularly when postoperative residual volume is minimal [17–19]. However, evidence supporting surgical intervention for small-to-moderate basal ganglia hemorrhage remains limited, [20] and recommendations for hematomas < 30 mL are inconsistent. Reports on robot-assisted drainage in this medium-volume subgroup are scarce. This study aims to evaluate 3D structured-light robot–assisted stereotactic catheter drainage for spontaneous basal ganglia hemorrhage (10–30 mL), with emphasis on safety, registration accuracy, surgical efficiency, and functional outcomes.

Methods

IRB approval

This research was approval by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Fourth people’s hospital, Tongji University School of Medicine (No. 2022327-001) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013).

Clinical data collection

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who consecutively received 3D structured light robotic-assisted stereotactic hematoma Puncture and Catheterization (HPDC) Surgery from January 2022 to January 2024. The demographics data are shown in Table 1. Consent for both the procedure and the publication of images was obtained from all participating patients. Inclusion criteria were: (1) adherence to the 2022 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association Guideline for the Management of Patients with Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage; [15] (2) CT-confirmed hemorrhage within the basal ganglia with a volume of 10-30 ml that had not extended into the ventricles; (3) a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score ≥ 9; (4) availability of complete imaging, surgical, and 90-day follow-up data; (5) no administration of anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy within two weeks prior to admission. Retrospectively, 50 cases met the inclusion criteria: 25 cases in the surgical group and 25 cases in the non-surgical group. The choice of treatment was determined by the patient and their family. Patients who did not opt for surgery received standard medical management according to AHA/ASA guidelines (non-surgical group), whereas those who chose surgery were treated with the proposed robotic-assisted procedure (surgical group).

Image preprocessing and surgical planning

In the pre-operational stage, all patients underwent axial volumetric computed tomography (CT; SIEMENS SOMATOM Definition AS+;1-mm slice thickness), contrast enhanced CT angiography (CTA; SIEMENS SOMATOM Force; spiral mode 0.25-second slices, 512-row collimation at 0.6 mm, pitch 1.2, slice thickness 1.0 mm, reconstruction interval 1.0 mm, and acquisition parameters 80 kVp/425 mA), diffusion-tensor imaging (DTI; SIEMENS Prisma; TR 4900 ms, TE 79 ms, averages 1, slices 45, slice thickness 2.5 mm, FOV 220 mm, matrix 88 × 88, spatial resolution 2.5 mm × 2.5 mm).

SinoPlan software (version 2.0.1.2; Sinovation, Beijing, China) was used for surgical planning. Two experienced neurosurgeons identified the target and devised optimal trajectories, and determine the entry (EP, cranial bone) and target point (TP, located at the lower one-third along the long axis of the hematoma) use CT. The total length of the rigid channel is set to a predetermined fixed value, typically 19 cm, while the path length is automatically calculated by the software. The same volumetric measurement method was applied across both the surgical and non-surgical groups. Specifically, hematoma volumes were measured on both baseline and follow-up CT scans using Analyze software (AnalyzeDirect, Overland Park, Kansas, USA). Admission CT was used as the baseline imaging reference for all patients, and follow-up CT scans were obtained at predefined time points along a unified admission-based timeline. Specifically, the first follow-up CT was obtained at approximately 2 h postoperatively (equivalent to 12 h after admission), followed by additional imaging at 72 h, 5 days, and 7 days after admission in both groups.

3D structured light registration

Figure 1 demonstrated the surgical workflow. Preoperative imaging is imported into the computer workstation, where we integrated CTA and DTI through multimodal fusion to facilitate the surgical plan (Fig. 1,A1A2). Patients are positioned supine (Fig. 1. B1) and are under general anesthesia, with their heads stabilized in a Mayfield head clamp. The SR1-3D robot (Sinovation, Beijing, China), with a 3D-structured-light scanner (Fig. 1.B2, B3), executes several registration processes. The registration begins with a coarse correspondence established based on several landmarks (nose root, outer canthus, nose tip) automatically extracted from preoperative thin-slice CT images (Fig. 1. B4, B5). Then the robot projects a series of striped light patterns across the patient’s face at different angles (Fig. 1. B1) tracking their deformation to construct a 3D model of the head using point cloud data. Next, the system then aligns this model with the point cloud data extracted from the preoperative images. Successful registration, indicated by an error margin of less than 1 mm, is verified (Fig. 1.B6, B7). Should any movement of the patient’s head occur, the registration is meticulously redone to ensure accuracy. In the event of any head movement by the patient, the registration must be meticulously repeated to ensure the accuracy of the alignment.

Robot-assisted stereotactic Hematoma Puncture Drainage and Catheterization (HPDC) surgery

Following registration, the structured light device is interfaced with a cannula guide adapter. The robotic arm assists in marking the skull entry point on the skin (Fig. 1.C1,C2), preceding a standard frontal or fronto-parietal surgical approach. The area undergoes routine disinfection and preparation. A 15 mm diameter burr hole is created, and the dura mater is incised with a sharp knife (5 mm diameter, Fig. 1.C3). The robotic system autonomously aligns to the pre-set surgical coordinates, enables the precise placement of a 14-French cannula into the hematoma, centered along its longitudinal axis and at the midline of its width, providing convenient access for aspiration of the clot from all surrounding areas. Initial aspiration is performed using a 10 ml syringe, followed by manual suction until initial resistance is encountered, indicating a potential field change or hematoma boundary, which calls for a cautious advancement. After hematoma aspiration, a soft ventriculostomy catheter is inserted through the existing cannula. The rigid cannula is then removed, leaving the soft catheter precisely seated at the base of the residual hematoma. The drainage tube and scalp incision are secured. Postoperatively, the cavity is treated with 0.3 mg rtPA every 8 h, adhering to the MISTIE protocol [17]. A thin-lice CT scan is conducted within 2 h post-surgery to evaluate hematoma volume, with the drainage tube removed after 3 days.

Surgical accuracy assessment

Post-operative skull CT scans are registered with pre-operative images, enabling the accurate measurement of critical distances. The entry point (EP) is identified by the actual burr hole location post-surgery, which is marked by the hole’s center or a screw on the skull. Identifying the target point (TP) in post-operative CT scans for hematoma drainage can be difficult due to the evacuated space collapse at the target site, which can affect the reconstruction of the trajectory axis. To address this, the endpoint of the drainage tube is recorded in the initial post-operative CT. We measure two metrics: ‘location,’ which determines the positioning of the catheter tip within the superior, central, or inferior third of the hematoma, and ‘vertical displacement,’ which quantifies the vertical distance between the actual catheter tip position and the longitudinal axis of the hematoma center.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, we evaluated normal distribution by Kolmogorov Smirnov test. Normal distribution data were analyzed by two tailed Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test was conducted to compare non-parametric data between groups. The results of continuous variables are reported in the form of Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). For categorical variables, we applied χ2 test (chi square test) to analyze the distribution differences between groups. In all statistical tests, P-value less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Table 1 presents the patient baseline characteristics. The study enrolled 25 patients who underwent HPDC (surgical group), and 25 patients without surgery (conservative group). The patient comprised 22 females and 28 males (1:1.27). The average age was 61.52 years (50–80). Preoperatively, no significant differences (p > 0.05) were observed across the mean hematoma volume (18.89 ± 1.65mL) (range: 9.01–31.62 ml), hypertension history (52%), motor function score (6.40 ± 0.29), GCS score (11.97 ± 0.33), ventilation requirement (16%), coagulation disorders (40%), headache (48%), and vomiting (4%).

Comparative surgical efficacy

As illustrated in Fig. 1, The workflow of SR1-3D neurosurgical navigation positioning system shows an innovative robotic-assisted device that incorporates a 3D structured light scanning technology. Table 2 summarized the extent of hematoma removal. The average registration time was 120 ± 56 s, mean operative time was 102.22 ± 6.33 min. Patients in the surgical group demonstrated a markedly reduced residual hematoma volume and enhanced clearance rates compared to the non-surgical group at corresponding time intervals post-administration. Specifically, at the 24-hour, the surgical group achieved a 72% (15.85 ± 3.28 ml) clearance rate (P < 0.05). This rate improved to 91% (18.48 ± 1.66 ml) at the 3 day, 92% (18.93 ± 1.59 ml) at 7 days, and peaked at 93% (19.64 ± 1.85 ml) by the 12–14 day, with respective non-surgical group clearance rates of 0%, 1%,12%, and 22% (Fig. 2).

Treatment outcome and complications

Table 4 details the functional outcomes and complications. The surgical group show significantly improved GCS scores immediately postoperatively (p < 0.001) and superior neurological recovery as assessed by the NIHSS, ADL and mRS scales. Specifically, the surgical group had lower NIHSS motor scores for both upper and lower limbs (p < 0.001), higher ADL scores (p = 0.026), and lower mRS scores (p = 0.041). There were no significant differences in the incidence of facial palsy (12% vs. 8%), sensory loss (20% vs. 28%), or stress ulcer (4% vs. 8%) between the groups (P > 0.05). The surgical group also had a significantly lower incidence of pneumonia (8% vs. 32%, p = 0.034) and urinary tract infections (UTI) (0% vs. 32%, p = 0.002). No mortality was recorded in either group within 90 days post-onset, and no complications related to the procedure or fibrinolytic agents were observed.

Discussion

Structured light technology has promoted neurosurgery’s convenience in emergency cases by simplifying surgical procedures and eliminating the need for traditional stereotactic methods that required rigid frames or markers, such as skin patches or bony landmarks [21, 22]. Specifically, the advanced image processing algorithms enable the direct extraction of alignment data from the patient’s anatomical structures, simplifying the preoperative registration process and significantly reducing the invasiveness of patient alignment techniques. Moreover, the robotic surgical planning system allows for a personalized, multimodal approach to hematoma identification and target setting, adeptly avoiding critical neural and vascular structures as shown in Fig. 1. Thereby shortening preoperative preparation time and reducing surgical risks through more precise and patient-specific planning.

3D structured light registration accuracy, surgical safety and efficiency

In this study, all cases utilized transfrontal or transfronto-parietal approaches for surgical access. According to literature reports, these approaches are advantageous for 3D structured light SBR, which provided greater precision in the forehead, facial, and parietal regions compared to the temporal and occipital regions [23]. In this study, the registration accuracy was less than 1 mm, with evaluated average entry point error of 2.2 ± 0.7 mm. All target points were located within the hematoma cavity. This data confirmed the reliability of the registration process for surgical procedures. The primary source of error in aligning the skull entry point was attributed to the drilling direction not being perfectly perpendicular to the skull, which resulted in calculating errors; for target catheter tip displacement, it was mainly due to hematoma collapse and brain shifts. To further enhance the precision of stereotactic catheter insertion, the following refined approaches should be considered: (1) Select a drainage tube that is compatible with the adapter’s pore size; (2) Utilize a soft drainage tube with a hard core during the puncture process, as this can improve the accuracy and stability of the procedure;16 (3) For patients with significant skin surface deformations, use a hybrid registration method—such as placing 1 or 2 skin markers at the bony landmarks on the head—which take the combined strengths of both technologies to achieve admitted targeting precision.

Neurological functional recovery evaluation in our cohort

The outcomes for patients with cerebral hemorrhage are influenced by a combination of internal factors, such as hematoma expansion, edema, ischemia, inflammation, and toxins, along with external factors like intensive care [17, 24]. Early surgical intervention is associated with improved patient recovery, as evidenced by enhanced GCS, NIHSS, and ADL scores in our study, potentially due to the rapid reduction of hematoma. Researchers have identified poor outcome predictors may be related to continuous lobar hemorrhage and delayed treatment, [25] however, the positive 90-day prognosis in our study may relate to the absence of these predictors. Determinants of ADL scores include muscle strength and cerebrovascular history, which were less prevalent in our patient cohort [26]. Furthermore, the predictive value of preoperative NIHSS scores is modest and less predictive with increasing hematoma size [27, 28]. However, there was no difference in preoperative NIHSS scores among this group of patients, so we did not rely on the NIHSS scale for prognostic prediction. Patients with markedly reduced GCS scores frequently suggest the necessity for surgical intervention [29]. However, for patients with higher GCS scores and smaller hematomas but significant motor deficits, the decision between surgical and conservative approaches can be complicated. Interestingly, in our study, GCS and motor scores did not significantly sway surgical decisions (GCS: p = 0.08, motor scores: p = 0.58), since surgical choices for these patients were largely influenced by patient preference, Nonetheless, the preoperative GCS scores were well-matched between the two groups. Further investigation is essential to elucidate the predictive significance of GCS and NIHSS scores concerning functional outcomes and surgical planning.

Large randomized trials in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) have generally failed to show consistent improvement in functional outcomes. A possible explanation is that ICH prognosis is highly dependent on hemorrhage location, in addition to hematoma volume [17]. In a cohort study involving 533 patients with ICH, Teo et al. found location-specific hematoma volume thresholds independently predicted unfavorable outcomes: 41 mL for putaminal/external capsule, 6 mL for internal capsule/globus pallidus, and 9.5 mL for thalamic hemorrhages [12]. These results suggest hematoma volume alone may be inadequate for neurosurgical decisions, and anatomical involvement should be considered in patient selection and outcome interpretation.

Consistent with these observations, we found that medium-volume basal ganglia hematomas were associated with significant preoperative neurological deficits. However, our study’s limited sample size prevented reliable subgroup analysis. Similarly, Wang et al. reported in an 84-patient robot-assisted ICH cohort that small thalamic hematomas with CT type II morphology (extending into the internal capsule) led to more severe motor and language impairment, with surgical patients showing better recovery [20]. This was linked to internal capsule involvement, where disruption of motor pathways can cause contralateral motor dysfunction. Further investigation is needed to clarify the role of hemispheric dominance, particularly in language-related outcomes.

Surgical complications and patient follow-up

In our study, 80% of catheters were accurately positioned at the hematoma’s long-axis center, which optimized evacuation and enhanced interaction with rt-PA. [17, 27] We found lower rates of postoperative pneumonia (8%, p = 0.034) and UTI (0%, p = 0.002) in the surgical group. This improvement is likely attributed to effective management of cerebral edema and hypertension, [30] as well as the maintenance of spontaneous breathing and coughing among all patients, which may have further mitigated respiratory complications. Despite no significant differences in facial paralysis (16.7%), sensory loss (27.8%), or stress ulcers (0%), the surgical group experienced shorter hospital stays, indicating improved recovery outcomes.

In this study, no rebleeding was observed in either patient group, a result that could be attributed to the exclusion of patients with coagulopathy or a history of anticoagulant/antiplatelet use. Additionally, the utilization of precise puncture paths guided by multimodal imaging fusion, which meticulously avoids blood vessels, likely played a role in reducing the risk of rebleeding. However, in actual clinical practice, a significant proportion of emergency patients have a history of anticoagulant use within two weeks, a factor that, while controlled for in our study to mitigate the risk of intraoperative bleeding or hematoma expansion, may have led to the exclusion of a substantial subset of patients commonly encountered in clinical settings. Future research should focus on studying the puncture in patients with coagulation disorders, which would be meaningful for guiding emergency physicians in their practice.

Learning curve

While experienced neurosurgeons can adeptly perform deep intracerebral hematoma removal, this proficiency is typically acquired through extensive training periods. In underdeveloped regions, which often have a higher incidence of cerebral hemorrhage, there is a notable scarcity of training opportunities, particularly for advanced techniques such as endoscopic and microsurgical procedures. At our facility, four doctors with little experience in deep brain lesion surgeries completed 200 robotic-assisted surgeries over two years after training on 30 cases. The use of robotic-assisted needle puncture, which followed a set path for accuracy, was key to these operations, greatly improving the precision and safety of the procedures. Based on our experience, effective surgical planning demands that junior doctors have a comprehensive understanding of pyramidal tract topography, vascular anatomy, and the localization of functional brain zones.

Limitations

This study was designed to evaluate the safety and feasibility of three-dimensional structured light robot–assisted frameless stereotactic treatment for spontaneous medium-volume basal ganglia hematomas, rather than to establish definitive superiority over other treatment strategies. Accordingly, the observed improvements in hematoma clearance and neurological recovery should be interpreted with caution. Firstly, this study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size. As treatment allocation was driven by patient and family preference, selection bias and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Secondly, DTI reconstruction was selectively applied in specific clinical scenarios rather than routinely, which may have influenced surgical planning and outcomes. Finally, prospective randomized studies, including comparisons between frame-based stereotactic techniques, robot-assisted approaches, and non-operative management, are needed to determine whether this strategy provides clinically meaningful benefits.

Conclusions

Three-dimensional structured light robot–assisted frameless stereotactic technology enables fast and precise non-contact surface registration based on million-scale point cloud data, providing reliable spatial localization and trajectory guidance in emergency settings. Given the functional sensitivity of the basal ganglia, spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage in this region may cause significant neurological impairment even at moderate volumes, making timely and precise intervention a critical clinical consideration. Early and 90-day neurological and functional changes were observed in this study, but remain preliminary and associative.
