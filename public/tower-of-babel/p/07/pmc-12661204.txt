Selective modification of glycoprotein substrates by GnT-V in mouse kidney
Authors: Reina F Osuka, Masamichi Nagae, Miyako Nakano, Shunsuke Tanigawa, Kazuya Ono, Yudai Tsuji, Yoshimasa Ito, Kazuo Takahashi, Ryuichi Nishinakamura, Yasuhiko Kizuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryN-Glycans are modified by various glycosyltransferases in a protein-selective manner and critically control protein functions. Here, we investigated the mechanisms for the protein substrate selectivity of N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase-V (GnT-V or MGAT5) in the mouse kidney. Through lectin-assisted proteomics, two metalloproteases on the apical surface of tubules, alanyl aminopeptidase (ANPEP) and meprin α (MEP1A), were identified as the major GnT-V substrates. We further determined the GnT-V-modified glycosites and revealed that the modified sites are highly accessible and clustered within the C-terminal domains, suggesting some structural requirements for GnT-V action. Single-cell transcriptomics revealed partial overlap of Mgat5 and Anpep or Mep1a, excluding the possibility that Mgat5 is limited in the same cell type as the major substrates. Furthermore, upon polarization of epithelial cells, the GnT-V products accumulate to the apical side, suggesting that polarized subcellular trafficking is involved in selective modification. Our findings provide insights into the mechanisms of protein-selective glycosylation in vivo.Subject areas: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Proteomics

Introduction

Glycosylation of proteins is one of the most common posttranslational modifications and is broadly classified into N-glycosylation and O-glycosylation. N-glycosylation, in particular, plays crucial roles in regulating protein functions through various mechanisms.1 In the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), N-glycans are involved in the quality control of nascent proteins by acting as markers of protein folding states. Furthermore, mature N-glycans synthesized in the Golgi apparatus regulate protein stability, activity, and localization in many biological contexts.1 Mature N-glycans have diverse structures with variable numbers of GlcNAc branches and a wide array of terminal epitopes. Furthermore, N-glycan structures attached to each glycoprotein differ, and such protein-selective glycan patterns are a molecular basis for the functional diversity of mammalian proteins. However, protein-selective glycosylation mechanisms remain unknown, except for glycans exclusively attached to specific proteins, such as polysialic acid on neural cell adhesion molecule2 and matriglycan on α-dystroglycan.3

Here, we focused on the glycosyltransferases that biosynthesize each branch structure in N-glycans. Previous studies using knockout (KO) mice for these enzymes or clinical samples revealed that each branch of N-glycan is individually associated with diseases, such as cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, diabetes, and emphysema.4,5,6 Among the N-glycan branches, the β1,6-GlcNAc branch, whose biosynthesis is catalyzed by N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase-V (GnT-V encoded by MGAT5 gene)7,8 (Figure 1A), is aberrantly upregulated in many cancers, leading to a poor prognosis.9,10 Mechanistically, the cancer pathology promoted by GnT-V may be mediated by functional glycosylation of specific target glycoproteins; the inhibition of cell-cell adhesion via E-cadherin,11 and the upregulation of signaling pathways via epidermal growth factor receptors.12 Moreover, depletion of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch in Mgat5 KO mice results in autoimmune disease-like phenotypes, such as nephritis and inflammatory bowel disease.13,14,15 This hyperactivation of the immune system in Mgat5 KO mice is likely because of the lingering residence of T cell receptors on the cell surfaces, which carry β1,6-GlcNAc branches on their N-glycans and are tethered by galectin lattices. The prolonged residence of T cell receptors promotes their clustering and sensitizes T cells.13,16 These studies suggest that the functions of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch depend on its specific carrier proteins. Therefore, revealing how GnT-V selectively modifies glycoproteins is crucial for understanding the molecular mechanisms by which GnT-V is involved in disease pathology.

The catalytic mechanism by which GnT-V recognizes the oligosaccharide moiety of glycoprotein substrates has already been revealed by the co-crystallization of GnT-V with its oligosaccharide substrates.17,18 It was also suggested that amino acid residues outside the catalytic pocket of GnT-V are involved in recognizing the chitobiose core or neighboring polypeptide region of the substrate.19,20 Furthermore, the non-catalytic N domain of GnT-V turned out to be essential for the reaction toward glycoprotein substrates but not toward oligosaccharide substrates.21 These prior studies suggested that GnT-V recognizes not only the glycan part but also the protein part of glycoprotein substrates. However, how GnT-V selects glycoprotein substrates from the various protein candidates in vivo has remained unclear. Moreover, factors other than substrate recognition, such as intra-Golgi localization,22 also likely influence the action of glycosyltransferases in vivo. To clarify the mechanisms for protein-selective glycosylation, it is essential to identify the major protein substrates of glycosyltransferases. Previously, using lectin enrichment and comparative proteomics between wild-type (WT) and glycosyltransferase-KO cells or animals, the substrate proteins for several glycosyltransferases, including N-acetylgalactosaminyltransferases (GALNTs),23,24,25,26 N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase-III (GnT-III, MGAT3),27 and fucosyltransferase-9 (FUT9),28 were successfully identified. These findings indicate that glycosyltransferases select their own substrate glycoproteins, but the mechanisms of the protein-selective glycosylation for most glycosyltransferases have remained essentially unclarified. Therefore, we sought to elucidate the selectivity of GnT-V toward glycoprotein substrates in vivo and identify the underlying mechanism by first identifying the substrates using lectin-assisted proteomics and Mgat5 KO mice.

In the present study, we identified two metalloproteases as the major physiological substrates of GnT-V in the mouse kidney. Moreover, both proteins are localized primarily at the apical surface of proximal tubules and have glycosites modified by GnT-V clustered at the C-terminal region. These observations, combined with single-cell transcriptomics, suggest that the physiological substrates of GnT-V are selected by the compound mechanism of cell-type-specific expression, subcellular trafficking in polarized cells, and structural hindrance or recognition. Our findings contribute to a greater understanding of the cellular biosynthetic mechanisms on complex N-glycan structures.

Results

ANPEP and meprin A subunit α are the major substrates of GnT-V in mouse kidneys

To clarify the mechanisms by which GnT-V selectively modifies its substrates in vivo, as the first step, identification of its glycoprotein substrates under physiological conditions is essential. To focus on tissues with high GnT-V activity, we first measured the GnT-V activity in the membrane fractions of 5 tissues (brain, lung, spleen, liver, and kidney) from WT mice using an established method with a fluorescence-labeled N-glycan substrate (Figure S1).17 Among these tissues, the kidney presented the highest GnT-V activity (Figure 1B). We also detected the β1,6-GlcNAc branch of N-glycans in the tissues by leukoagglutinating-phytohemagglutinin (L4-PHA) lectin,29 which preferentially recognizes the β1,6-GlcNAc branch (GnT-V product) and showed dramatically decreased reactivity in Mgat5 KO tissues (Figure 1C). Two distinct L4-PHA-reactive bands with high signal intensity were observed in the kidney (Figure 1C, arrowheads). On the basis of these findings, hereafter we focused on kidney in the present study.

Next, to identify the physiological substrates of GnT-V, glycoproteins bearing β1,6-GlcNAc branches in the kidney membrane fraction were purified using L4-PHA-conjugated agarose beads (Figures 1D and 1E). The bands obtained by affinity purification were excised and identified by proteomics. After the exclusion of non-N-glycosylated proteins that do not enter the secretory pathway, ANPEP, glutamyl aminopeptidase (ENPEP), meprin A subunit α (meprin α or MEP1A), and β (meprin β or MEP1B) were identified as GnT-V substrate candidates (Figure 1F and Table 1). To determine which glycoproteins are the major GnT-V substrates, each protein candidate was immunoprecipitated. We found that immunoprecipitated ANPEP and meprin α were recognized by L4-PHA and had the same mobility as the two major L4-PHA-reactive bands of kidney membrane lysates (Figure 1G, leftmost panel). By contrast, immunoprecipitated ENPEP and meprin β did not match the mobility of the major bands of kidney membrane lysates detected by L4-PHA. These findings demonstrate that ANPEP and meprin α are the major substrates of GnT-V in mouse kidneys (Figure 1G).

The major GnT-V substrates are localized at the apical surface of renal tubules

We next examined the protein levels and the localization of ANPEP and meprin α in WT and Mgat5 KO mouse kidneys. Western blotting revealed increased mobility of these proteins from Mgat5 KO mice, probably due to the loss of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch, but the levels of these two proteins were comparable between WT and Mgat5 KO mice (Figures 2A and 2B), indicating that the β1,6-GlcNAc branch does not affect the protein stability or turnover of these substrates.

Next, we investigated the localization of these substrates by immunostaining kidney sections. Both ANPEP and meprin α are localized at the apical side of the renal proximal tubules,30,31,32,33 and ANPEP and meprin α immunoreactivity was observed exclusively on the apical side in both WT and Mgat5 KO mice (Figure 2C). L4-PHA reactivity was also detected mainly on the apical side of renal tubules in WT mouse kidneys and almost completely absent in Mgat5 KO mice. These results are consistent with the biochemical data showing that GnT-V selectively modifies two apical proteins in the proximal tubules, ANPEP and meprin α, in mouse kidneys. The nearly unaltered distribution patterns of both glycoproteins between WT and Mgat5 KO mice suggest that the β1,6-GlcNAc branch had little effect on the localization of these two glycoprotein substrates (Figure 2C). To further examine whether GnT-V mainly modifies apical proteins in the kidney, laminin-1, which is known to be localized at the basal side,34,35 was analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting. The mobility of laminin-1 in the gel revealed no apparent difference between WT and Mgat5 KO mice (Figure 2D). The histochemical immunoreactivity of laminin-1 on the basal side of renal tubules barely overlapped with L4-PHA reactivity in the WT mouse kidney (Figure 2E). Our finding that the β1,6-GlcNAc branch was observed mainly on the apical side of the renal tubules raised the possibility that apical trafficking of glycoproteins in polarized cells could contribute to the selective modification by GnT-V.

Functional analysis of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch on glycoprotein substrates

Next, we investigated the effects of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch on the functions of the identified substrates. ANPEP is a metalloprotease that participates in angiotensin metabolism, and mice lacking ANPEP are defective in angiogenesis under pathological conditions.32,36 Meprin α is also a metalloprotease involved in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and inflammation.37,38 The protease activity of ANPEP in vitro was first measured using a fluorescence-labeled peptide substrate. Using cell lysates as the enzyme source, we confirmed that the ANPEP activity was elevated in the ANPEP transfectants and decreased in the presence of the ANPEP-specific inhibitor (Figure 3A), confirming the validity of this assay system. Using this system, we found that the enzyme activity of ANPEP in Mgat5 KO mice was comparable to that in WT mice (Figure 3B). Next, the in vitro activity of meprin α was also evaluated based on the degradation rate of its substrate, tropoelastin.39 Using immunoprecipitated meprin α, we confirmed that tropoelastin was digested by incubating it with meprin α in an incubation time-dependent manner (Figure S2). The enzyme activity of meprin α from WT or Mgat5 KO mice was assessed in vitro using this assay, and we found that the activity from Mgat5 KO mice was comparable to that from WT mice (Figures 3C and 3D). These findings indicate that the presence or absence of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch on these glycoproteins barely affects their enzymatic activity.

Because ANPEP and meprin α function in renal tubules, the role of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch on glycoprotein substrates upon the induction of renal tubule failure was investigated. WT and Mgat5 KO mice were injected intraperitoneally with cisplatin, which specifically damages the renal tubules.40 Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining of kidney sections revealed cisplatin damage to renal tubules in both WT and Mgat5 KO mouse kidneys (Figure 3E). However, the degree of tubule damage was almost indistinguishable between WT and KO mice. Protein levels in urine induced by cisplatin administration were measured to assess the kidney dysfunction further. However, the extent of cisplatin-induced proteinuria was not significantly different between WT and Mgat5 KO mice (Figure 3F), although we surprisingly found that the basal level of urinary protein was markedly lower in Mgat5 KO mice than in WT, which should be pursued further, as discussed further.

GnT-V-preferring glycosites are located in the C-terminal region of glycoprotein substrates

A possible factor for the selective modification by GnT-V is the structural requirement of the substrate glycoproteins or their specific glycosites, such as direct recognition by GnT-V or steric hindrance with GnT-V. To explore this possibility, we sought to determine the glycosites modified by GnT-V in ANPEP and meprin α and map these preferred sites in their 3D structures. To this end, ANPEP and meprin α were purified from WT and Mgat5 KO mouse kidneys by immunoprecipitation (Figure 4A), and the N-glycan structures on individual glycosites were analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC-MS) glycoproteomic analysis. Mouse ANPEP (UniProt ID: P97449) and mouse meprin α (UniProt ID: P28825) have 13 and 9 consensus sequences for N-glycosylation, respectively (Figure 4B). Among these sites, we successfully detected glycopeptides and assigned their glycan structures at 8 sites for ANPEP and at 6 sites for meprin α (Figures 4C–4F, S3, and S4). In total, 76 N-glycans were detected on these two glycoproteins (Tables S1 and S2). Among the glycosites, N106 of ANPEP and N257 of meprin α predominantly had oligomannose-type glycans for unknown reasons (Figures 4G, 4H, S3, and S4). The glycosites where the number of HexNAc was clearly lower in Mgat5 KO than in WT mice were likely the sites modified by GnT-V, namely, N734, N784, and N817 of ANPEP and N317, N413, and N439 of meprin α (Figures 4G, 4H, S3, and S4). In the N114 glycans in ANPEP and the N221 glycans in meprin α, the number of HexNAc was slightly reduced in the KO mice (Figures 4G, 4H, S3, and S4), implying that these sites were partially modified by GnT-V. By contrast, N128, N573, and N606 of ANPEP and N139 of meprin α did not show a decrease in the number of HexNAc in the KO mice (Figures 4G, 4H, S3, and S4), suggesting that these sites were unmodified by GnT-V. Notably, the preferred sites for GnT-V are located in the C-terminal regions, forming the oligomerization interface,39,41 in both glycoprotein substrates. Mgat5 deficiency reduced the number of fucose (Fuc) residues in ANPEP and sialic acid (Sia) residues in meprin α in the terminal structures of the glycans but not the number of Fuc residues in meprin α or Sia residues in ANPEP (Figures S5–S7). These findings imply that the loss of β1,6-GlcNAc branch did not simply result in the loss of Fuc and Sia residues in the β1,6-GlcNAc branch but had more complicated effects on N-glycan biosynthesis. Ultimately, we showed that six glycosites located in the C-terminal region of ANPEP and meprin α were mainly modified by GnT-V.

Glycosites modified by GnT-V in glycoprotein substrates exhibited no steric hindrance with GnT-V

Next, we mapped the identified preferred sites in the modeled 3D structures. As both ANPEP and meprin α form homodimers,39,41 we generated biantennary N-glycans in the structural models of ANPEP and meprin α homodimers (Figures 5A and 5B). In the ANPEP structure, the glycans at N734, N784, and N817 (preferred sites) are highly accessible. By contrast, the glycans at N106, N128, N573, and N606 (unmodified sites) and N114 (partially modified site) are located close to the polypeptide and would easily cause steric hindrance (Figure 5C). Similarly, in meprin α, the glycans at the preferred sites, N317, N413, and N439, were highly exposed, whereas those at the unmodified site, N139, and the partially modified site, N221, are sufficiently close to the polypeptide to cause steric hindrance with the polypeptide moiety of meprin α (Figure 5D). Furthermore, glycans at N114 and N606 in ANPEP and at N257 and N221 in meprin α likely caused steric hindrance in the recognition of these sites by GnT-V (Figures 5E and 5F). In brief, the sites modified by GnT-V are clustered around the specific domains that are necessary for glycoproteins to interact with each other and are free of steric hindrance to recognition by GnT-V.

If the intrinsic structures of ANPEP and meprin α are dominant determinants for modification by GnT-V, these proteins would be preferentially modified even in cultured cells. To test this possibility, a preferred (ANPEP) and a poor substrate of GnT-V in vivo were overexpressed in HEK293 cells, and the efficiency of modification with the β1,6-GlcNAc branch was compared. As a poor substrate, we focused on LAMP1, which was unidentified in the proteomic data despite its high abundance and multiple glycosylation sites in the proximal tubule cells (Tables S3 and S4, discussed further). Myc-tagged ANPEP and LAMP1 were expressed in HEK293 WT and MGAT5 KO cells, and the levels of modification by GnT-V were evaluated by blotting with L4-PHA. We found that the L4-PHA signal of LAMP1 was comparable to that of ANPEP (Figure 5G) and that these L4-PHA signals disappeared in MGAT5 KO cells, demonstrating that LAMP1 is modified by GnT-V as efficiently as ANPEP in cultured cells. This contrasts with the preferred modification of ANPEP to LAMP1 in the kidney, implying that the protein structure of ANPEP is not the primary factor for selective modification by GnT-V in vivo.

Distinct mRNA expression pattern of Mgat5 from the major glycoprotein substrates in kidney

We next hypothesized that the localization of the glycoprotein substrates would be a key for modification by GnT-V in vivo. The identified substrates are exclusively expressed at a specific membrane segment (apical side) in a specific cell type (proximal tubule cell). This expression raised the possibility that modification by GnT-V in the mouse kidney is regulated by the localization of the substrates at either the cellular or subcellular level. To test this possibility, we first analyzed the mRNA expression of GnT-V and the two major substrates by single-cell (sc) RNA-seq during mouse development and then specified the cell types expressing GnT-V and the major substrates. We found that the expression of Anpep and Mep1a is highly cell type-specific in the mouse kidney, and is highest in proximal tubule (PT) cells (Figure 6A), which is consistent with previous reports.42,43,44,45 By contrast, Mgat5 is expressed in various cell types, including podocytes (PD), proximal tubules (PT), and endothelial cells (EC). This analysis revealed that the mRNA expression of Mgat5 and the major glycoprotein substrates partially overlap, allowing for GnT-V-mediated glycosylation of ANPEP and MEP1A in the proximal tubule cells. However, the broader expression pattern of Mgat5 demonstrates that the protein-selective modification by GnT-V in mouse kidneys cannot be explained simply by shared cell type-specific expression of the enzyme and its substrates. Instead, other factors, such as polarized transport or abundance of the substrate proteins, are likely the main contributors to the selective modification. Because there are other abundant glycoproteins, such as CD63, LAMP1, and LAMP2, in the same cell type as Anpep and Mep1a (Table S4), only the abundance of ANPEP and meprin α cannot explain the selective modification by GnT-V.

We finally examined the relationship between polarized trafficking and GnT-V-mediated glycosylation in a cultured cell model. To mimic polarized renal tubule cells, MDCK cells were cultured with or without polarization (Figure 6B). Actin and podocalyxin46,47,48,49 were stained and used as markers to visualize the cell shape and the apical side of MDCK cells, respectively, to confirm whether the cells were polarized. The L4-PHA signal was detected on the peripheral surface of non-polarized MDCK cells, whereas the signal was observed mainly on the apical side of polarized MDCK cells (Figure 6B). These findings suggest that GnT-V-mediated glycosylation is associated with the polarized transport of substrates.

Discussion

Here, we identified two major physiological substrates of GnT-V in the mouse kidney and reported that the GnT-V-modified glycans of these substrates are located in the C-terminal region. In addition, these glycoprotein substrates were limitedly expressed in proximal renal tubules, whereas GnT-V was more widely expressed in the mouse kidney. Thus, the selectivity of GnT-V action in vivo is likely caused by a combination of contributions from polar trafficking, the expression level, and the structural requirements of the substrate. Our findings provide insights into how protein-specific glycan structures are synthesized in cells.

ANPEP and meprin α were exclusively expressed in proximal tubules and detected specifically at the apical side of the tubule cells. One of the possible reasons for the selective modification of these two proteins by GnT-V could be their high abundance in tubule cells. To explore this possibility, we comprehensively analyzed the mRNA levels of all detected N-glycosylated proteins in proximal tubule cells (clusters 12 and 24) using scRNA-seq data and listed these glycoproteins in the order of mRNA abundance (Tables S3 and S4). The mRNA levels of Anpep and Mep1a are ranked 19th and 34th of the most abundant glycoproteins in proximal tubule cells. Intriguingly, several other abundant glycoproteins have not been identified as major GnT-V substrates, including LAMP1, LAMP2, DPP4, and Sema3C, even though they have more than 5 N-glycosylation sequons (Table S4). LAMP1 and LAMP2, well-established lysosome markers,50,51 are also targeted to basolateral membranes in polarized cells.52,53 In addition, integrin α1, which is ranked 49th among the tubule glycoproteins, is heavily modified with N-glycans (23 sites) but was not included in the proteins identified in the present study. These findings exclude the possibility that highly abundant glycoproteins are simply modified by GnT-V as its major substrates. Rather, our results suggest that other factors, such as the subcellular trafficking of glycoproteins, have a more critical impact on protein-selective modification by GnT-V.

The relationships between the polarized sorting of glycoproteins and their glycosylation states have been studied. For example, the reactivity of various lectins differs between the apical and basal membranes of polarized cells.54,55,56 In addition, glycosaminoglycan sulfation differs between the apical and basolateral sides of MDCK cells.57,58 Furthermore, several glycans potentially play important roles in apical targeting. Apical trafficking of sialomucin endolyn is modulated specifically by N-glycan sialylation and binding to galectin-9 in polarized MDCK cells. Knockdown of sialyltransferases dramatically changed the distribution pattern of endolyn in polarized cells.59 These findings suggest that the N-glycan structure depends on the polarized trafficking of glycoproteins and vice versa. For meprin α, it was reported that the disruption of N-glycosylation has subtle effects on the apical targeting,60 whereas our results suggest that the apical trafficking of ANPEP and meprin α could contribute to their selective modification by GnT-V.

The GnT-V-preferred sites in ANPEP and meprin α were clustered at the C-terminal region, and 5 out of 6 GnT-V-preferred glycosites were also conserved in human (Figures S8 and S9). Our previous activity assay using several soluble glycoproteins and purified soluble GnT-V in vitro revealed that the GnT-V catalytic domain intrinsically has preferences for some glycoproteins.21 Furthermore, the action of GnT-V toward glycoproteins requires its N domain.21 These findings indicate that recognition of the substrate protein or steric hindrance impacts GnT-V action. Additionally, GnT-V is present in cells as a single-pass membrane protein, and the possible distance from the catalytic site to the Golgi membrane is limited. ANPEP and meprin α are also anchored to the membrane, indicating that individual N-glycans on these proteins likely have different distances from the membrane. Considering such spatial restrictions, a suitable distance from the membrane could also contribute to the GnT-V action toward specific glycosites.

In our glycoproteomic analysis, a decrease in one HexNAc was presumed simply to be loss of modification by GnT-V, but it is possible that even the same number of HexNAc in the Mgat5 KO mouse kidney sample could be a result of the loss of β1,6-GlcNAc and gain of β1,4-GlcNAc or tandem LacNAc. However, at least GnT-IVa and -IVb, which produce β1,4-GlcNAc branches on N-glycans, are known to have higher activity against the GnT-V product triantennary glycan than against the biantennary glycan.61 Thus, GnT-IVa and GnT-IVb are less likely to be more active in Mgat5 KO mice than in WT mice. In addition, even for the GnT-V-preferred sites in terminal modifications, the number of Fuc and Sia residues did not always decrease upon loss of the β1,6-GlcNAc branch and was differentially affected among glycosites (Figures S6 and S7). These site-specific changes in glycan structures induced by loss of GnT-V suggest that the actions of glycosyltransferases that modify N-glycan termini are not constant toward glycosites but can be differentially affected by various factors, including prior action of GnT-V.

Our assay revealed the comparable enzyme activity of ANPEP and meprin α in vitro between WT and Mgat5 KO mice. A previous study also revealed that an ANPEP glycoform with oligomannosidic N-glycans has the similar activity to that with complex N-glycans.62 This finding suggests that the β1,6-GlcNAc branch on these glycoproteins subtly impacts their enzymatic activity under physiological conditions. However, under conditions of acute kidney injury, meprin α was mislocalized and dispersed into the cytoplasm.63 In addition, ANPEP is involved in hypertension64 and angiogenesis in tumors,32 and meprin α plays a crucial role in modulating inflammation38 and ECM degradation37 in acute renal failure. These results indicate that specific glycans on these molecules may be involved in these pathological processes. Moreover, deglycosylation of specific glycosylation sites on meprin α had an effect on homo-oligomerization,65 and thus it is still possible that β1,6-GlcNAc branch on these glycoproteins may change the efficiency of multimer formation. Especially, under conditions of cancer, the glycosylation patterns of both ANPEP and meprin α differ from physiological conditions, which lead to the alterations in their enzyme activity.66,67,68 Elucidating the structures and functions of the N-glycans of ANPEP and meprin α under pathological conditions is an intriguing question. Moreover, considering the broad expression of Mgat5 in various cell types in the kidney, the functions of glycoproteins expressed in cells other than proximal tubules, such as podocytes, could be regulated by β1,6-GlcNAc branches. To gain a deeper understanding of the roles of GnT-V in the kidney, identification and functional analysis of minor GnT-V substrates could be effective in the future.

Surprisingly, we detected a lower basal level of urinary protein in Mgat5 KO mice than in WT mice (Figure 3F). This could be caused by either impaired production of urinary proteins in Mgat5 KO mice or altered filtration or reabsorption of urinary proteins in the Mgat5 KO kidney. To explore this possibility, the levels of major urinary protein (MUP), which is synthesized in the liver and excreted into the urine,69,70,71 were investigated in the urine and liver of Mgat5 KO mice. Consistent with the level of total proteins (Figure 3F), the level of MUP in the urine tended to be lower in Mgat5 KO mice than in WT mice (Figures S10A and S10B). Moreover, the basal level of MUP in liver tissue was also noticeably lower in Mgat5 KO mice than WT mice (Figures S10C and S10D). These findings suggest that the decreased level of proteins in Mgat5 KO urine is likely caused by liver abnormality. However, these results are preliminary, and further investigations are needed to gain more insights into the mechanisms of production and metabolism of urinary proteins in Mgat5 KO mice, such as liver histology or evaluation of liver function markers.

In conclusion, the present study identified two major glycoprotein substrates of GnT-V in the mouse kidney and suggested that these substrates are selected mainly by apical trafficking and structural requirements. Given that GnT-V is related to cancer and autoimmune diseases, clarifying how GnT-V selects glycoprotein substrates would provide clues for treating these diseases by developing new therapeutics that target glycans in a protein-selective manner.

Limitations of the study

This study contains some limitations. First, we rely on the L4-PHA lectin-conjugated beads for the identification of GnT-V glycoprotein substrates. It is possible that the reactivity of L4-PHA is affected by terminal structures of glycans, and thus our lectin-pulldown method might overlook glycoproteins that are modified by GnT-V but are weakly recognized by L4-PHA. Second, we unexpectedly found that the level of urinary proteins was lower in GnT-V KO mice than in WT mice, and the detailed mechanism for this finding should be explored in the future. Third, we used the polarized MDCK cell system to examine the relationship between cell polarity and GnT-V modification, and other cells and tissues should be analyzed to see whether GnT-V modification generally depends on cell polarity.

Method details

Reagents

The following commercial reagents were used in this study: anti-mouse aminopeptidase N (goat, R&D systems; catalog no.: AF2335-SP; RRID: AB_2227288), anti-AP-A (ENPEP) (goat, abcam; catalog no.: ab36122; RRID: AB_725759), anti-MUP (mouse, Santa Cruz; catalog no.: sc-166429; RRID: AB_2017298), anti-meprin alpha (rabbit, abcam; catalog no.: ab232892), anti-meprin beta (goat, R&D systems; catalog no.: AF2895; RRID: AB_2143457), anti-Myc tag (mouse, clone 4A6; Millipore; catalog no.: 05–724; RRID: AB_11211891), anti-Laminin-1 (rabbit, Sigma-Aldrich; catalog no.: L9393; RRID: AB_477163), anti-podocalyxin (GP135) (mouse, clone 3F2:D8; Sigma-Aldrich; catalog no.: MABS1327; RRID: AB_3102002), anti-VDAC1/Porin + VDAC3 (mouse, 20B12AF2; abcam; catalog no.: ab14734; RRID: AB_443084), horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (GE Healthcare; catalog no.: NA931V; RRID: AB_772210), HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (GE Healthcare; catalog no.: NA934V; RRID: AB_772206), HRP-conjugated anti-goat IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories; catalog no.: 705-035-147; RRID: AB_2313587), unconjugated-L4-PHA (J-Chemical; catalog no.: J112), tetramethylrhodamine-labeled phalloidin peptide (Sigma-Aldrich; catalog no.: P1951; RRID: AB_2315148), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated Streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no.: S11223), Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no.: A10036; RRID: AB_11180613), Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no.: A10040; RRID: AB_2534016), Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated anti-goat IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no.: A11056; RRID: AB_2534103), and biotinylated L4-PHA (J-Chemical; catalog no.: J212). L4-PHA was labeled with HRP using a Peroxidase Labeling Kit-NH2 (Dojindo; catalog no.; LK11) as described previously.19

Sample preparation from mice tissues

The tissues dissected from mice were homogenized with 8 volumes of homogenization buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, supplemented with a Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set V [EDTA-free]). The homogenates were centrifuged at 1,000 ×g for 10 min to remove the nuclei and then ultracentrifuged at 105,000 ×g for 30 min. The supernatants were used as soluble fractions, and the pellets were used as membrane fractions.

GnT-V activity assay using HPLC

The GnT-V activity assay was conducted as previously described.17 Membrane fractions of tissues were lysed with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set V [EDTA-free]) and sonicated, followed by incubation for 30 min on ice. The protein concentrations of lysates were measured with a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The lysates were incubated with the acceptor substrate, GnGnbi-PA (pyridylamine),82 in reaction buffer (125 mM MES [pH 6.3], 10 mM EDTA, 20 mM UDP-GlcNAc, 200 mM GlcNAc, 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100, and 1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA)) for 16 h at 37°C, followed by incubation for 2 min at 99°C to terminate the reaction. As a positive control, we used the purified his-tagged soluble form of human GnT-V (Thr121-L741) as described previously.19

After the reaction, the samples were diluted with water and centrifuged at 21,000 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was analyzed by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with an ODS column (Inertsil ODS-3, ID 4.6 mm × 250 mm) in isocratic mode using buffer A (20 mM ammonium acetate buffer [pH 4.0]) and buffer B (1% 1-butanol in buffer A), which were mixed at 4:1.

SDS-PAGE, Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) staining, western blotting and lectin blotting

The protein concentrations of samples were measured using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, and the samples were mixed with 5× Laemmli sample buffer. The buffered samples were subsequently heated for 5 min at 95°C and loaded into lanes on the gel. After the proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, the proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBS-T (Tris-buffered saline (TBS) including 0.1% Tween 20) for 30 min for western blotting or with 1% BSA in TBS-T overnight at 4°C for lectin blotting. After blocking, the membranes for western blotting were incubated with primary antibodies diluted with 5% skim milk in TBS-T overnight at 4°C. The membranes were subsequently washed three times with TBS-T and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted with 5% skim milk in TBS-T for an hour at room temperature. For lectin blotting, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated lectins diluted with 1% BSA in TBS-T for 30 min at room temperature. Before signal detection, the membranes were washed three times with TBS-T, followed by washing once with TBS. Signals were detected with a Western Lightning Plus-ECL (Revvity) or SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a Fusion-Solo 7s Edge imaging system (Vilber Lourmat). For CBB staining, the SDS-PAGE gel was washed once with water, and the proteins were stained with GelCode Blue Safe Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The stained proteins were photographed using a Fusion-Solo 7s Edge imaging system. The signal intensities were quantified using ImageJ.73

Pulldown with lectin-conjugated beads and immunoprecipitation

Membrane fractions from WT and Mgat5 KO mice were sonicated with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set V [EDTA-free]) followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. The lysates were ultracentrifuged at 105,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C. A portion of the supernatants was collected as the input samples for SDS-PAGE. For lectin pulldown, the supernatants were incubated with L4-PHA-conjugated agarose beads (Vector Laboratories, AL-1113) and rotated for 4 h at 4°C. For immunoprecipitation, the supernatants were first preincubated with Dynabeads Protein G for 30 min at 4°C to exclude nonspecifically bound proteins. After removing the beads, the lysates were incubated with antibodies and Dynabeads Protein G and gently rotated for 24 or 48 h at 4°C. After the incubation, the beads were collected, and the supernatants were collected as flowthrough fractions. The collected beads were washed with the lysis buffer twice and eluted by incubation with Laemmli sample buffer at 95°C for 5 min. The supernatants were collected as eluate fractions and used for SDS-PAGE.

Proteomics

Proteins in the eluate fraction obtained as described in “Pulldown with lectin-conjugated beads and immunoprecipitation” were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with GelCode Blue Safe Protein Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific) followed by excision of the target bands. Proteins in the gel pieces were reduced with 100 mM DTT and alkylated with 100 mM iodoacetamide. After washing, the gel pieces were incubated with trypsin overnight at 30°C. The recovered peptides were desalted using ZipTip C18 resin tips (Millipore). The samples were analyzed using nanoLC/MS/MS systems (DiNa HPLC system Kya Tech Corporation/QSTAR XL Applied Biosystems). The mass data acquired were piloted with Mascot software. NanoLC-MS/MS analyses were performed by Japan Proteomics.

Immunofluorescence staining of mouse kidney sections

To prepare kidney sections, the mice were first anesthetized and then perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) followed by 10% Formalin Neutral Buffer Solution (Wako), and the perfused kidneys were stored overnight at 4°C. Kidneys were submerged in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4°C for three days with daily medium renewal. Kidneys were sectioned at 30 μm thickness with a microtome (REM-710, Yamato Khoki). Kidney sections were permeabilized with blocking buffer (3% BSA, 0.1% NP-40 diluted with PBS) for 20 min at room temperature. Then, the kidney sections were incubated with 10 μg/mL of anti-ANPEP, anti-meprin α, or anti-laminin-1 primary antibodies with biotinylated L4-PHA diluted with blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. Before staining with anti-meprin α, kidney sections were incubated with 0.1% proteinase K in PBS for 6 min at room temperature to retrieve antigens, followed by washing with PBS three times. After overnight incubation with primary antibodies, kidney sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies and fluorescence-conjugated streptavidin for 30 min at room temperature in the dark.

ANPEP activity assay

The enzyme activity of ANPEP was measured using an aminopeptidase N (APN/CD13) Activity Assay Kit (Fluorometric) (ab273292, abcam) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, mouse kidneys or transfected cells were homogenized in ANPEP assay buffer, which was supplied in the kit, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. The same amount of supernatant and the fluorescence substrate were mixed for the reaction with or without a selective aminopeptidase N inhibitor (t-BPAPP), which was supplied in the kit. By subtracting the fluorescence signal of homogenates with the inhibitor from that of cells without the inhibitor, the activity of ANPEP was calculated.

Meprin α activity assay

Meprin α was immunoprecipitated from the membrane fractions from WT and Mgat5 KO mouse kidneys to determine its enzymatic activity. The membrane fraction was sonicated with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set V [EDTA-free]) followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. Then, the lysates were ultracentrifuged at 105,000 ×g for 15 min at 4°C, and the collected supernatants were preincubated with Protein L Magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 4°C. After removing the beads, the supernatants were incubated with anti-meprin α antibody and Dynabeads Protein G overnight with gentle shaking at 4°C. After incubation, the beads were washed three times with wash buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set V [EDTA-free]), and the bound proteins were eluted by incubation with 50 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.8) for 1 min, followed by immediate neutralization with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). The eluates were mixed with human tropoelastin (T0706-1 MG, Sigma-Aldrich) (10 μM at final concentration in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4)), followed by incubation for 12 h at 37°C. As a negative control, the same amount of vehicle (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4) was used instead of the eluate. After the reaction, the samples were incubated with 5× Laemmli sample buffer followed by heating at 95°C for 5 min, and the signal intensity of undigested tropoelastin was quantified after staining with GelCode Blue Safe Protein Stain.

Cisplatin-induced model of acute kidney injury

Adult (18–32 weeks old) WT and Mgat5 KO male mice were injected intraperitoneally with 20 mg/kg of cisplatin (p4394 cis-diammineplatinum (II), Sigma-Aldrich). The conditions of the mice were checked each day, and the urine of the treated mice was collected after three days of treatment. For the measurement of protein amount, urines were diluted with water by 200-fold. After collecting the urine, the mice were anesthetized, and then perfused with PBS and 10% Formalin Neutral Buffer Solution. Fixed kidneys were excised, embedded in the paraffin, and were sectioned. The sections were deparaffinized with xylene, hydrated using a graded series of ethanol concentrations, and stained using a PAS method. PAS-stained kidney sections were converted to virtual slides using TOCO24 (Claro). Two experienced nephrologists double-checked the tubular lesions.

Glycoproteomics

To analyze the glycan structures on ANPEP and meprin α, these proteins were immunoprecipitated from WT and Mgat5 KO mouse kidney membrane fractions as described above. The purified proteins were eluted from the beads with 50 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.8), neutralized with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and then lyophilized. The glycopeptide sample preparation, analytical conditions for LC-ESI MS, and data analyses were similar to those described previously.83 In brief, proteins (approximately 20 μg each) were reduced with dithiothreitol and alkylated with iodoacetamide. The reaction mixture was desalted through a Nap-5 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with 10 mM ammonium bicarbonate. The desalted solutions containing S-carbamidomethylated proteins were evaporated to dryness. The dried residues were dissolved in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 1 μg trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then incubated at 37°C for 16 h. After boiling to inactivate trypsin, the tryptic peptide samples were evaporated to dryness. The tryptic peptides were dissolved in 20 μL of 0.08% (v/v) formic acid and separated using an ODS column (Develosil 300ODS-HG-5; 150 mm × 1.0 mm ID; Nomura Chemicals) under specific gradient conditions. The mobile phases were: (A) 0.08% formic acid and (B) 0.15% formic acid/80% acetonitrile. The column was eluted with solvent A for 5 min, at which point the concentration of solvent B was increased to 40% over 55 min at a flow rate of 50 μL/min using a Vanquish HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The eluate was introduced continuously into an ESI source, and the glycopeptides were analyzed by LTQ Orbitrap XL (hybrid linear ion trap-orbitrap mass spectrometer; Thermo Fisher Scientific). In the MS setting, the voltage of the capillary source was set at 4.5 kV, and the temperature of the transfer capillary was maintained at 300°C. The capillary voltage and tube lens voltage were set at 15 and 50 V, respectively. MS data were obtained in positive ion mode over the mass range m/z 300 to m/z 3000 (resolution: 60000, mass accuracy: 5 ppm). MS/MS data were obtained by ion trap in LTQ Orbitrap XL (data-dependent top 3, CID). Xcalibur software ver. 2.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to show the BPC, extracted ion chromatogram (EIC) and averaged mass spectra (Ave. MS) to analyze MS and MS/MS data. For the relative quantitation of glycoforms on each glycosite, the intensity of the charge with the highest observed peptide ion (miscleavage = 0) was used. At first, the ion peak of the corresponding monoisotopic glycoform in the Ave. MS of each glycosite was identified using Xcalibur software, version 2.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), by matching the observed monoisotopic mass to the theoretical monoisotopic mass (GlycoMod tool, http://web.expasy.org/glycomod/, mass tolerance; +/0.01 Da) and also by confirming it with MS/MS data. Subsequently, EICs were generated for all glycopeptides detected and identified at each glycosite based on the Ave. MS data. Finally, the relative abundances (%) of each glycan structures on each glycosites, were calculated by setting the total peak intensities of all detected glycan structures in each EIC as 100%.

Representation of protein structure

The 3D structures of N-glycosylated mouse ANPEP and the meprin A dimer were generated with AlphaFold 3.80 The 3D structures of the proteins were depicted using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 1.2r3pre, Schrödinger).84 Structural superpositions of GnT-V luminal domains (PDB: 5ZIB, 5ZIC) were performed with program SUPERPOSE and LsqKab in the CCP4 software suite.79

Transfection

Transfection was conducted when the cells were grown at 60%–80% confluence on a 6- or 10-cm dish. The plasmids (4 μg or 5 μg for 6-cm dish, and 12.5 μg for 10-cm dish) were introduced into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were collected by scraping after 48 h of transfection. For preparing cell lysates for SDS-PAGE, transfected cells were sonicated with lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Set V [EDTA-free]), followed by incubation on ice for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 21,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatants were used.

Plasmids

The oligonucleotides used to construct the plasmids are listed in the key resources table. cDNAs encoding mouse ANPEP (mANPEP; UniProt ID: P97449) and mouse LAMP1 (mLAMP1; UniProt ID: P11438) were cloned from a cDNA library of the mouse kidneys. To construct pcDNA6 mycHisA/mANPEP, the amplified fragment of Anpep cDNA was inserted into pcDNA6 mycHisA, which had been digested with EcoRI and XhoI, by Gibson assembly. To construct the expression plasmid for ANPEP-myc tag, a fragment of the Anpep cDNA skipping stop codon was amplified by PCR using pcDNA6 mycHisA/mANPEP as a template and ligated to the EcoRI and XhoI sites of pcDNA6 mycHisA by Gibson assembly. To construct the expression plasmid for LAMP1-myc, the cDNA fragment of Lamp1 amplified by PCR was ligated into pcDNA6 mycHisA, which had been digested with EcoRI and XhoI, by Gibson assembly.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

Single cell (sc) RNA-seq data deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE149134)85 were used in this study. scRNA-seq datasets of E15.5, E17.5, and P0 kidneys and the dissociation of embryonic kidneys were described previously.85 Aliquots containing 5000 dissociated cells from each sample were applied to a Chromium Controller (10x Genomics). A Chromium Single Cell 3′ Library & Gel Beads Kit v2 or v3 (10x Genomics) was used to generate cDNA libraries, which were then sequenced by an Illumina HiSeq X. The raw sequence data were processed using the Cell Ranger count command in Cell Ranger version 3.0.2 (10x Genomics) to generate count tables of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs). All these individually generated datasets were integrated using the Cell Ranger aggr command (10x Genomics). All subsequent analyses were performed in the R statistical programming language, version 4.3.1.86 The Seurat package (version 3.1.1) was used for analyses, including quality control, data normalization, data scaling, and visualization.75,76 For quality control, cells that expressed <200 genes, >8000 genes (possibly representing doublets), or >20% of mitochondrial genes were filtered out. Principal component analysis wfas used for dimension reduction with a dimension value of 95 determined by the JackStrawPlot function.77 Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection for Dimension Reduction (UMAP) plots were generated using the uwot package.78 The obtained UMAP coordinates, Seurat cluster coordinates, and cluster-specific markers were exported as csv files for confirmation analysis using Loupe Cell Browser software (10x Genomics).

Differential expression and identification of segment-specific genes

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat (version 3.1.1), comparing each target cluster against all other clusters. Genes with a nominal p-value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched in the target cluster, as described previously.85 To identify segment-specific genes at the P0 developmental stage, pairwise comparisons were carried out between the target cluster and all remaining clusters. Genes were selected based on two criteria: (1) statistical significance (p < 0.05) and (2) low expression prevalence in non-target clusters, defined as pct.2 < 0.11. Genes overlapping between the general differential expression analysis and the P0-specific analysis were considered candidate segment-specific genes. These genes were further assessed through visualization on UMAP plots to verify cluster-restricted expression. The final gene set was defined as lineage-specific and stage-dependent. The average gene expression levels in the proximal tubule across all cells within the specified cluster were analyzed using Loupe Browser v8.0.0 (10x Genomics). To create Table S4 from scRNA-seq data (Table S3), all Ensemble IDs were first linked to Uniprot ID. Proteins registered in Uniprot as carrying at least one N-glycan were extracted and sorted in Table S4 in the order of mRNA abundance (average gene expression level) in the cluster of proximal renal tubules.

Confocal imaging of conditioned MDCK cells

Polarization of MDCK cells was induced as described in previous studies.87,88 Briefly, MDCK cells were seeded on a glass-bottom dish at the concentration of 2.5 × 104 cells/ml for polarization and cultured for 5 days. The culture medium was renewed every 48 h. As non-polarized cells, MDCK cells were seeded on a glass-bottom dish at the concentration of 6.25 × 104 cells/ml and cultured for 24 h.

The cells were washed with ice-cold PBS three times and fixed with 4% PFA with PBS for 15 min. Then, the cells were washed with ice-cold PBS three times and permeabilized with blocking buffer (3% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100 diluted with PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were incubated with primary antibody or a fluorescently-labeled phalloidin and biotinylated L4-PHA overnight at 4°C. The labeled cells were subsequently washed with ice-cold PBS three times and incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody and fluorescence-conjugated streptavidin for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Stained cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and mounted. Microscopic observation was carried out on confocal microscopy (Olympus, FV1000) installed with an oil immersion lens (Olympus, UPlanFLN 60x NA 0.90). Z-stacks were composed of sliced images obtained at 200 nm thickness and constructed using Fiji.74

Comparison of amino acid sequences

All amino acid sequences were obtained from NCBI database. Comparison of amino acid sequence was conducted at NCBI BLASTp (Needleman-Wunsch alignment of two protein sequences). Multiple alignment of glycoprotein substrates was performed with Clustal Omega server.81

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantification methods are described above. No individual data points were excluded from the reported analyses. In addition to the scRNA-seq datasets analyzed using R statistical software, statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software).
