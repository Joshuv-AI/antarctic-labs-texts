On-screen framing of the Amhara protests in 2016 by journalists in Ethiopia
Authors: Biset Ayalew Nigatu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main purpose of this research was to investigate the ways Amhara Television (ATV) framed the Amhara Protest and the challenges of protest framing. More specifically, the study is geared toward examining the ways the ATV journalists implemented media framing during the 2016 Amhara protest. The study was designed to a) identify the key frames utilized by ATV while covering public protests across the region, and b) spot the main internal and external obstacles to practising public protest framing in ATV. To attain these goals, a qualitative research design was employed. Media texts, individual in-depth interviews, and focus group discussions (FGD) were used to gather the data, and the data were analysed thematically. The findings indicate that ATV used the cause of the protest, the consequence of the protest, the government's response to the protest, and the solution to the protest as key frames. The station framed that a lack of good governance and development were the main factors that caused the protest, which is different from the quest of street demonstrators [6]. ATV portrayed the protest in a negative light, emphasizing the significant economic damage and human casualties it caused. Declaring a state of emergency, deep government renewal, and setting up command posts are portrayed as measures taken by the government. Furthermore, the approach to ending the public protest is portrayed as avoiding violent measures and peacefully presenting protestors' questions. To frame solutions, ATV soug ht the help of government officials, religious leaders, and Amhara region residents who support the perspective of the government and oppose the continuance of public demonstrations. Finally, Amhara's protest framing by ATV was heavily hampered by professional constraints, a lack of self-confidence, inconsistencies in institutional training, media ownership, political influence, emotional reactions from the public against ATV journalists, and a lack of collaboration from political leaders.

Introduction

The historical context of Amhara Television (ATV) goes back to the new emergence of media industry development after the downfall of the Provisional Military Government of Socialist Ethiopia (Derg). The Amhara Television Program was launched on April 27, 1999, under the umbrella of the Amhara Mass Media Agency (AMMA), which was renamed Amhara Broadcast Corporation (AMC) in 2020. The television program began with a 30-min weekly regional television program that aired on Ethiopia Television (ETV). It was the first regional program across the nation to be broadcast in Amharic. In addition, Amhara TV, which is situated in Bahir Dar, broadcasts its programs from its studio and transmitter using a satellite for the first time on October 26, 2012. Furthermore, the station launched an 18-h-per-day transmission on January 8, 2017, in Amharic, English, Afan Oromo, Awigna, and Himtagna languages.

Amhara TV, a regional government-run station, had 133 working journalists in 2016/17, the year the Amhara protest peaked. In general, 286 journalists were working for the Amhara Mass Media Agency (AMMA), of which Amhara TV is a member. Currently, the station uses the Nile satellite to transmit for 126 h per week.

Amhara TV also has in-house news and program guidelines (directives) that guide journalists to cover different issues or events. These guidelines include the AMMA News and Program Manual (2012), the AMMA Editorial Policy (2012), whose core values and principles are serving the public and providing objective reporting, the AMMA Editorial Meeting Communication Plan Guidelines (2015), and the AMMA Language Policy (2013). The directives and guidelines mentioned above are similar to Ref. [1] internal factors that affect issue framing in the newsroom. Similarly [2,3], assume the role of the state in putting direct and indirect pressures on the functioning of news and program production, one of which is enforcing and endorsing media laws, regulations, and guidelines.

Amhara TV was chosen as the study's topic because it is thought to keep an eye on regional events, such as protests in the streets, by offering viewers unbiased and impartial news. Additionally, it is the media that is most closely linked to the Amhara ethnic group, and it is the one that is intended to serve the Amhara public under Ethiopia's ethnically oriented federal system, which is comparable to Ref. [3] public service orientation criteria.

Statement of the problem

Generally, from 2015 to 2018, until Dr Ahmed became the Prime Minister of Ethiopia, consistent mass protests took place across the country, mainly in the Amhara and Oromia National Regional States. Consequently, the phenomena attracted the attention of the local and international media. Not enough research has been conducted so far related to local media framing of Amhara protest. Thus, the primary objective of this study was to look into the practices and challenges that Amhara TV experienced in framing the 2016 Amhara protest.

The research was conducted to answer the following research questions.

Limitation of the study

This study used a qualitative research design and the samples taken for this study are not chosen randomly so that the conclusions lack external validity. However, the findings of the study shows what is going on the ground.

Literature

Media history in Ethiopia

Ethiopia is one of the world's oldest and most independent countries and is well known as the "land of origins." It also has its alphabet, calendar, and a diversified population that speaks a wide range of languages, but until the nineteenth century, when the newspaper came, the country was too shy to adapt to modern mass media [4]. With the coming of modern mass media in Ethiopia, the incumbents used the media for various ends in the history of Ethiopia. For instance, Ethiopian unity was promoted through the media under Emperor Haile Selassie's reign [5]. Despite considerable improvements in the size of the audience, the government has used the media to promote the ideological interests of the monarchy and frame its interests, particularly in radio transmission.

However, the subsequent regime compounded the problem, although Ethiopia's media appeared promising at first. For example [6], highlight how there were aspirations for journalistic independence during the first two or three years of the Derg regime. Dialogues between competing political groups were visible in the print and electronic media, and journalists were candid and critical of the administration. Relevant national issues such as democracy, land ownership, and the appropriate type of administration for the country, among others, were argued publicly in the public print media. Nevertheless, the period that was referred to by many Ethiopian journalists as the "golden days of Ethiopian journalism" was short. In a similar spirit [4], stated that the fall of Haile Selassie in 1974 was greeted with considerable optimism by many Ethiopians. The expected hope, however, did not take place as the Derg administration quickly became another round of media domination.

The Derg regime severely restricted media freedom during that time [8,7]. For instance Ref. [8], stated that the Derg seized and assumed control of all news outlets. The media were forced to broadcast the socialist revolution's most successful stories. As a result, newspapers and broadcast media were preachers of socialist philosophy. Professional media management was utterly unimaginable at that time. The main frame strategy of the government was to nationalize privately held enterprises as well as machinery to construct a Socialist Democratic Ethiopia.

In addition, the media focused on the enemies and friends of the socialist revolution, and according to Ref. [9], the history of the press during the military government was dominated by political partisanship. Furthermore [10], indicated that serious agendas and issue frames were framed by the political technicians of the Derg regime.

The Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) seemed to reform the media by liberalizing the industry through free speech [11]. Ethiopia's 1991 regime change resulted in reforms to the constitutional and legislative framework that allowed for the establishment of an independent and democratic media environment. In addition to ratifying the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), which contains Article 19 that guarantees freedom of expression and information, the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) government also abolished censorship in 1992 and granted citizens the right to establish an independent press. Later, media proclamations like the 1999 law, the 2008 mass media and information freedom law, and the 2009 anti-terrorism law, however, returned media governance to the previous regime of media control and manipulation, which can limit the journalist's ability to practice their profession independently [12]. As a result, they suggested that "professional journalism would have better prospects with the repeal of the repressive articles of the 2009 anti-terrorism law and of the restrictive provisions in the 2008 Proclamation on Mass Media and Information Freedom that limit access to information and mandate the registration of journalists and licensing of independent print media" [12].

Ethiopia's media landscape during the EPRDF rule was characterized by a volatile media climate, a lack of professionalization, low media independence, and extreme polarization. The revolutionary democratic ideals that strive to monopolise vanguardism and hegemonic power by silencing competing viewpoints appear to have formed a perilous media environment and consider critical media outlets as threats to their existence [4]. According to Ref. [8], individuals who hold opposing views on government agendas are labelled as anti-peace, anti-democracy, terrorists, and anti-development during the EPRDF-led government and this continues to apply to anybody who disagrees with government policies even after PM Ahmed's election as prime minister of Ethiopia in 2018.

In general [13], found that the media environment during the EPRDF was similarly uncomfortable. The private media sector was handicapped by a lack of access to information from government officials as well as government harassment and oppression, forcing it to rely on international media sources for internal matters. For him, this reporting also appears to correspond to the private sector's tendency to criticize government acts while the government media primarily supports and promotes them.

Public protest in Ethiopia

Public protest is a common phenomenon, particularly in Africa. In Tunisia and Egypt, for example, large protests forced President Ben Ali to resign [14] and President Hosni Mubarak to quit in February 2011 [15]. Ethiopia, like many other African countries, has historically had several public demonstrations against different governments. For instance, the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF) has seen massive public protests since it took power by toppling the Derg regime in 1991. Following the national election results in 2005, widespread protests occurred across Ethiopia, particularly in the capital, Addis Abeba. The opposition parties, particularly the Coalition for Unity and Democracy (CUD), clashed with EPRDF, the ruling party, over the formation of the next parliament. According to Ref. [16], the EPRDF and opposition parties clashed for more than six months following the 2005 election. The discussions were halted when 131 opposition party leaders and civil society activists were arrested on suspicion of treason and violence against the country's constitutional order.

In 2016, the EPRDF government again encountered a protest in the Amhara Regional State, whose immediate cause for the Amhara protest 2016 was the attempt to arrest Colonel Zewudu [55], the chairman of the Wolkaite Amhara Identity representative. In the town of Gondar, in the Amhara Regional Government, federal security forces went in search of Colonel Zewudu on July 31, 2016, but they were all killed, and the representative was captured after a negotiated surrender.

The Wolkaite Amhara Identity is a movement that struggled to be annexed to the Amhara region, and Wolkait is an administrative district that had been part of the Amhara Territory until being relocated to the Tigray Region by the governing Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front (EPRDF); as a result, it is a contentious place between the Tigray and Amhara ethnic groups [55]. Using the Welkait case as a litmus test [17], stated that previous (EPRDF) political institutions failed to accommodate democratic procedures, resulting in the loss of democratic and peaceful players while aggressive ethnic entrepreneurs profited.

The Amhara Protests, which started in Gondar in 2016, and quickly extended to other places in the region, made a significant contribution to the national political changes in Ethiopia [18]. For them, the major reasons behind the Amhara protests were the hostile relationship between Amhara elites and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), the presence of opposition political party structures in the region, divisions within the ruling coalition (EPRDF), the diaspora community, and demonstrations in Oromia Regional State. The study also revealed that social networks and social media outlets have a crucial role in the expansion of anti-government protests in the region [18].

Media and public protest framing

The pro-government media commonly use the negative framing strategy of social movements and political protests, and it is particularly biased in how it interprets and presents the allegations made by the demonstrators, according to research done in Hungary by Ref. [19]. Additionally, they suggested that the media's dismissive and patronizing attitude would reduce public support for demonstrators and have an impact on their acceptability. Similarly [20], show that protests and protesters who challenge the status quo are frequently negatively portrayed or framed in the media. This unfavourable portrayal of "social movements" is influenced by several structural, organizational, and institutional aspects of media production as well as social unrest [8]. Media system in Ethiopia

The press market, political parallelism, journalistic professionalism, and the state's role were the original dimensions of the media system as formulated by Ref. [3]. The press market [3], also termed the press market's inclusiveness by Ref. [2] and describes how far the press reaches out to a larger audience. Political parallelism also describes the relationship between political parties and media organizations. The journalistic profession, furthermore, refers to the degree of professional autonomy among journalists, which can be restricted by political or economic actors. Autonomy can be judged in terms of the level of adherence to distinct professional norms such as common ethical principles and journalists' commitment to serving the interests of the public instead of serving the interests of the economic and political elites [3]. The level and type of governmental involvement in media operations are referred to as the role of the state. The state's involvement can take many different forms, including funding national and local public media organizations; assisting commercial media companies through direct and indirect press subsidies; limiting media content, and enforcing laws against libel [21].

Taking these dimensions into account [22], investigated to what extent [3] Comparing Media System Model explains Ethiopia's media system. He found that

Globally renowned media system models are inadequate to explain the historical development of media and politics in Ethiopia. Even though some features of the Ethiopian media, such as a weak media market, low circulation of newspapers, dominance of electronic media [by the incumbent], low professionalization, elite-oriented press, and absence of commercialization resemble elements of the polarized pluralist model, issues such as circulation of newspapers and the relationship between media and politics differ markedly from the model (2017: i).

The study also discovered that the legacy of previous regimes, the political history and philosophy of the current ruling party, and international influences are all seen to have an impact on the nature of media and politics in Ethiopia. These factors resulted in superficial liberalization, underdeveloped media markets, and political parallelism institutions. He said that the Ethiopian media system can be viewed as a media sector in need of development and an institutional setting that is still impacted by the ideologies of both the former and current regimes while being responsive to shifts in the international system [22].

[4], citing Bach (2011) claimed that the TPLF-dominated EPRDF Party changed its policies at various points to gain legitimacy and foreign support. It followed socialist ideals before switching to a liberal perspective. However, it adopted a developmental state model by the early 2000s [23]. The change in the party's policies and ideologies, in turn, mirrors the philosophy of media management in the country. Similarly, since 2008, "development reporting" has been the official reporting style for Ethiopian state media. This requires the media and journalists to actively engage in the nation's development objectives [24]. In other words, developmental journalism was the dominant media philosophy with which all media outlets had to comply if they were to operate effectively.

Public sphere

Using Habermas' public sphere theory [8], looked into ''The Challenges of Hosting Televised Deliberations in Ethiopian Media: ETV's Medrek, Fana Broadcasting Corporation's Zuria Meles, and LTV's Sefiw Mihdar''. The authors emphasized that, with PM Ahmed's ascent to power in 2018, these programs offer new formats for Ethiopian media. In other words, they said that such program formats for shows about public deliberation were previously unavailable in Ethiopian media. However, they demonstrated that the programs were ineffectual at entertaining the many viewpoints expressed by the media, guests, and government officials. They went on to say that the lack of government authorities, the guests' fear of expressing their opinions honestly and freely, the hosts' lack of professionalism and knowledge of the issues under deliberation, and the lack of courage on their part were all contributing factors. The authors also contend that the absence of an inclusive public sphere at the time contributed to Ethiopia's shaky political climate [8]. said that it is impossible to hold public debate forums in Ethiopia due to the country's tightly regulated media environment. They added that political interference and financial barriers prevent the media from realizing the public sphere in Ethiopia [8]. However, they found that the shows in EBC, FBC, and LTV focused on the incumbents' achievement stories, accusing the past administration than revealing the wrongdoings.

Media polarization

[25] consider the evidence for and implications of political polarization, distinguishing between ideological, affective, and false polarization. They reviewed three key societal-level factors contributing to these polarization phenomena: the role of political elites; partisan media; and social media dynamics. They argue that institutional polarization processes (elites, media, and social media) contribute to people's misperceptions of division among the electorate in America, which in turn can contribute to a self-perpetuating cycle fuelling animosity (affective polarization) and actual ideological polarization over time.

Similarly [5], discovered that after PM Ahmed came to power in April 2018, Ethiopia's media tended to reinforce hostility and dissociation among Ethiopians, and the media are more polarized than ever. Two primary frames—annihilation and othering—which denote the propensity of journalists to identify with their ethnic background—were identified as significant frames of reference in the Ethiopian media discourse [5].

The polarization is fuelled by ethno-nationalist media of different origins and ownership, as opposed to pan-Ethiopianist channels. Even journalists are beginning to form alliances along regional and ethnic faultlines, as illustrated by the launch of the Amhara Journalists Association, Oromia Journalists Association, Tigray Journalists Association, and so forth.

According to the report, journalists are also extremely likely to use sources that support their ideological viewpoints and refrain from mentioning sources from other ethnicities that would help to balance the story. They stated that political and ethnic agendas run the danger of conflicting with media professionalism.

Similarly, research also indicated the existence of polarization by social media users in the Ethiopian political arena. Identity-based hate speech and reform-related events have choked off Ethiopian social media [26]. That is to say, hate speech was quite common on social media and had strong ethnic, political, and religious overtones—some comments even advocated genocide. The researchers claimed that the major reasons for hate speech in Ethiopia are differences in historical memory, linking ethnicity with religion, cultural issues, and linguistic limitations. Additionally, they claimed that the nation's offline ethnic conflicts that erupted during the "political" change are reflected in the online commentators.

Media framing

Framing has been conceptualized by several scholars in a remarkably similar fashion. According to Ref. [27], "framing is the process of selecting a few elements of perceived reality and creating a narrative that highlights connections among them to promote a certain interpretation." In addition [28], outlined some roles of media frames: According to the author's description, "frames identify situations, diagnose causes, make moral choices, and suggest remedies." This means that particular media frames may influence public perceptions. Frames reveal aspects of reality while hiding others behind them. Framing is one step ahead of agenda setting (which deals with the importance of topics) in that it is concerned with how issues are presented [1].

A study by Ref. [29] confirms that theoretical frames have the possible power to influence public opinion and behaviour, and repeated exposure to a frame makes it more readily available in memory. In other words, provide and show information and images to shape attitudes, perceptions, and behaviour of the audience by promoting a certain version of reality.

According to Ref. [1], framing is a procedure in which "the method involved in frame building and frame placing is different." To the author, frame construction refers to the external elements that influence how journalists and news organizations frame a problem, while the framing process refers to the internal elements that influence how journalists and news organizations frame a problem. For [1], framing is a process that involves both frame-building (the ways frames develop) and frame-setting (the interaction of media frames and audience dispositions) as shown below in Fig. 1.

an integrated process model of framing [1].It illustrates the interplay of internal and external factors in the framing of news to alter information processing, attitudinal, and behavioural effects. It is about the relationship between frame building and frame setting.

In their content analysis [30], classified news items into parts containing facts. When it comes to the use of story frames, the relationship between key elements and frames has proven to be successful. According to Ref. [31], a typical framing of political news and programs involves parties of ideological groups battling it out over topics of debate and frames. The news media can inform consumers about topics they are unaware of. In the structure of the stories, the news framework consists of tacit theories on what is, what happens, and what matters [32].

To recap, this research project aims to investigate how the public protest framing was made by ATV. It has implications for how public protests, such as those in the Amhara region, are covered [33]. observed, "the news framing process of public protest is a result of reporters' professional conventions and hence the social ideological structure of society."

Methods

A qualitative research design was used to investigate the practices and challenges of Amhara Television (ATV) journalists' news and program framings during the 2016 public protest in the Amhara region. Amhara TV was chosen as the study's topic for it is the media most closely linked to the Amhara ethnic group and intended to serve the Amhara people with the motto we strive for social change under Ethiopia's ethnically oriented federal system, which is comparable to Ref. [34] public service orientation criteria. ATV news texts broadcast during the 2016 Amhara protest were sources of the data. In addition, news reporters, journalists, newscast and program editors, deputy news and program editors, and the Amhara TV station's editor-in-chief were part of the population.

Thirteen news excerpts were chosen purposively as long as they overtly showed the 2016 Amhara protest to investigate the station's practice of public protest framing. In addition, the two editors-in-chief, three deputy editors-in-chief, five editors, and nine reporters, whom the Amhara Mass Media Agency assigned to cover the 2016 public protest during the command post, were purposively selected based on their knowledge and experience of the subject matter under study, are capable of reflecting on the subject matter, can articulate the issue, and have time and are willing to take part in the research. The information gathered from the news article, interview, and FGD was thematically analysed. The FGD and in-depth interview transcripts, as well as the news texts, were read and reread for analysis. Initial codes were made and themes were searched for.

Data gathering instruments and analysis techniques

Text analysis, individual in-depth interviews, and FGD were used as data-gathering tools. Consequently, text analysis was used to investigate the ways ATV framed the 2016 Amhara protest. The study examined selected media texts broadcast in 2016—the year the Amhara protest erupted. The texts were originally produced in Amharic but were later translated into English for the study.

The researchers also conducted an in-depth interview with television reporters, news and programme editors, deputy editors-in-chief, and editor-in-chief of the station for their personal reflections and individual reactions. These participants were chosen from the news and program departments based on their experience and willingness to participate in the study. In addition, the researcher also conducted three FGD sessions comprising five journalists each to investigate their shared views and experiences of public protest framings at ATV. Individual in-depth interviews and FGD were employed to investigate the challenges they faced in covering the 2016 Amhara protest.

Generally, a total of 30 data sources altogether took part in the FGD and in-depth interview. The research specifically included two editors-in-chief, three deputy editors-in-chief, seven editors, and eighteen reporters. The participants' ages ranged from 28 to 44. All have first degrees in their respective fields of study, except one MA in Media and Communication; eleven in Journalism and/or Communication; nine in the English Language; six in the Amharic Language; two in Literature; and one in Law. They also have between three and sixteen years of work experience on ATV. However, the findings of this research are not generalizable as the sample was chosen deliberately.

Results and discussion

News framing practices in ATV

To examine how ATV framed the 2016 Amhara protest, four themes emerged from the media texts (such as the causes of the protest, the consequences of the protest, the government response to the protest, and the solution to the protest).

Causes of the protest

The root causes of the public protest in the Amhara region, as framed by ATV, are the lack of good governance and development. The station framed that addressing them would restore the peace and stability of the region, as can be seen in the following story:

Members of the Amhara National Democratic Movement's (ANDM) central council realized that addressing the people's development and governance concerns is critical to reducing the instability that has arisen in various sections of the region.

Members also voiced confidence in the organization's ability to apply previous experiences to the current problem (Sep 7, 2016).

ATV expressed the importance of ANDM's experience in dealing with obstacles in instances like these. Although the station framed the lack of good governance and development as the fundamental causes of political protest in the Amhara region, [55] thesis highlighted structural, institutional, and ideological flaws and inadequacies as the underlying causes of protest in Ethiopia in 2015–2018, and the core causes brought about marginalization, economic arrogance, a restricted political culture, strategic segregation, political dominance, and unstable institutional structures. According to Ref. [35], this resulted in politicized discontent and, in the end, political violence.

Furthermore, the attempted arrest of the disputed territory's representatives (Welkaite Amhara Identity Representatives), according to the Centre on Conflict and Development at Texas A&M University, triggered the Amhara protest in 2016. Besides, Ethiopia's ethnic politics, in which the country's political structure under the TPLF-dominated EPRDF was mentioned by the centre as a major factor (https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00TH11.pdf).

As illustrated below, social media was presented as an amplifier of fake news and crisis causes.

Residents of Debre Berhan city have stated that the fake news spread on social media will not stop them from continuing on their peaceful path.

Residents also expressed their grief over the loss of life and property caused by the riots that erupted in various areas (Sept.7, 2016).

Concerning the framing of ATV, social media intensified the disruption by disseminating false information, and individuals expressed their regret at the loss of human life and property as a result of the protest.

They [former EPRDF combatants] went on to say that the problems that have arisen in the meantime are due to administrative inefficiencies (Sep 7, 2016).

ATV also blamed administrative inefficiency for the protests that erupted in several places in the Amhara region. During the street demonstrations, however, the youth carried out varied slogans in different cities in the Amhara region, which differed from the framings of ATV. Stopping Amhara mass killings; restoring Ethiopia's international border with Sudan; Welkait is Amhara; The RiverTekeze is the natural boundary between Tigray and Gondar, and more earth-shattering slogans were among them. Stopping the TPLF's dominance, respect for Amhara, and Amharaness were among the rally's placards and calls for a change of the regime [36].

Regarding the cause of the protest, the findings show the discrepancy between the demands of the protesters on the street and ATV's portrayal of the demonstration. The discrepancy may emanate from the media's support of the views of the regional and federal governments, which, according to Refs. [2,3] shows political parallelism—the relationship between the media and political parties, and this was highly manifested in the news text of Amhara TV. This also shows the role of the state in putting direct and indirect pressures on the news and program production of ATV by enforcing laws, regulations, and guidelines that restrict journalists' autonomy. The findings are also similar to Ref. [34] power, knowledge, and discourse approach. In this case, ATV is the discourse producer (because it has the power to do so) with a government-friendly report, which is its version of reality (knowledge) to its discourse consumers (target audience).

The consequence of the protest

As can be seen in the following excerpts, ATV also depicted the outcome of the protest:

Youths in Showarobit city condemned the act of damaging public and government property through chaos and violence.

It is not fair to cause turmoil and violence that harm the public and government properties. As a result, it must be condemned (Sep 8, 2016).

Residents of Womberma, a community in west Gojjam, believe that the on-going violence and anarchy would damage new-born developments.

They claimed that despite the constitution's guarantee of peaceful protest, citizens should be wary of present activities that endanger society's lives and property (Sep 7, 2016).

ATV focused on the economic damage brought by the public protest, including the damage to infrastructure and property of individuals and the general public as a result of the protest. It demonstrated the negative consequences of the protest and, by that, it discouraged the public protests taking place in the region. Likewise, in Denmark and the Netherlands, economic consequence news frames appeared to be more relevant [1].

ATV also depicted the protests as restricting government employees' capacity to carry out their duties.

The recent unrest in Bahir Dar city has put a lot of pressure on government employees. According to the servants, the situation is impacting the people's ability to receive prompt services (Aug 10, 2016).

To summarize, the destruction of infrastructure, damage to individual and public properties, and loss of lives were regarded as protest consequence frames.

Government response to the protest

In the following news item, ATV attempted to emphasize an old EPRDF combatant's commitment to delivering timely solutions to public queries.

The former EPRDF combatant assured the public that the government is dedicated to providing a prompt response to public requests.

The combatants also expressed their sorrow at the death of the people during the conflict.

Ato Abay Tsehaye, Ato Abadula Gemeda, Ato Bereket Semon, and Dr Kasu Elala all stated that the administration will work hard to put an end to the abuse of public power for personal gain (Aug 8, 2016).

Thus, ATV employed key party officials as its main news source during the 2016 Amhara protest. In this regard [5], underlined Ethiopia's media approach to sources who favour their political agenda. The station portrayed the government as working hard at all levels to respond to the public's cause. The station also claimed that abuse of power for personal gain should end up there. It went on to say that the administration was working hard to stop abuses of power. In general, ATV emphasized the need to avoid abuse of power for personal gain, which resulted in instability throughout the Amhara region.

Similarly, the following text reflects the framing of ATV by citing Ethiopia's then-prime minister.

Prime Minister Hailemariam Deselagn stated that the administration will continue to respond to public demands democratically and inclusively.

Recognizing the possibilities of peaceful protest in the country, the prime minister emphasized that any activity that does not meet the necessary criteria for peaceful protest is illegal (Aug 8, 2016).

Similarly, ATV cited the then-Prime Minister, Hailemariam Desalegn, as a news source and emphasized the government's commitment to solving the problems democratically and inclusively. It also included the prime minister's remark that unlawful protests or any action that does not fulfil the required conditions were not permitted.

The Amhara National Democratic Movement (ANDM) stated that the deep rejuvenation is going as planned.

Apart from the accomplishments, the group's chairperson, Ato Demeke Mekonenn, stated that the organization will take responsibility for the problems that have arisen (Oct 17, 2016).

ATV framed the story by asserting that local government officials were accountable for the region's crises, which [37] describe as an attribution of responsibility framing, and they discovered that the responsibility frame was the most frequently used in the news in their study.

Leaders at all levels should be sincere and responsible in their responses to society's most pressing issues.

The comprehensive renewal review, which began in the central committee of the Amhara National Democratic Movement (ANDM), is now being carried out at the zonal and district levels. Senior authorities in South Shewa are likewise undergoing a comprehensive rejuvenation (Nov 13, 2017).

ATV further stressed the need for party leaders at all levels to respond honestly and ethically to public enquiries and keep their public pledges by doing their duties effectively. Furthermore, as seen in the above data, ATV portrayed the administration as being in a state of great rejuvenation.

Elders and religious leaders from Hulet EjuEnesie woreda in east Gojjam convened a meeting.

Ato Yiresaw Tamire, the spokesman for the Amhara region, said the government has started responding to some of the people's questions and assured them that issues that require more time will be addressed ahead of schedule.

The panellists expressed their condolences for the loss of life and advised society to raise its concerns in a peaceful way (Aug 14, 2016).

In its coverage, ATV depicted the government's response to public queries in the Amhara region, regardless of protestors' questions. It also demonstrated that the administration arranged public forums to address public concerns democratically and inclusively.

The Council of Ministers declared a state of emergency to end the anti-peace and anti-development forces threatening the country's constitutional order (Oct 23, 2016).

As previously noted, ATV framed the declaration of a state of emergency as the government's response to political protests to prevent anti-peace and anti-development protestors from jeopardizing the country's constitutional order. According to Ref. [8], the EPRDF administration saw those who seemed to resist official goals as anti-peace, anti-democracy, terrorists, and anti-development, while [38] found that public demonstrators are presented by the media as aggressive. Furthermore [39], pointed out that, even when public moments succeed in attracting media attention, media reports frame protests in ways that may dehumanize social movement goals. Similarly, ATV's framing of the public protest looked to favour the government's point of view over the public. In other words, the media landscape has mostly served those in positions of political power. For example [40], found that the Ethiopian Broadcasting Corporation (EBC), a government-owned organization, favoured the activities of the government and attacked anything that contradicted the government.

The solution to the protest

ATV also came up with stories that suggest solutions for the problem.

Residents of Bahir Dar said that the youth should peacefully express their concerns on good governance and development. They also urged the government to respond as soon as possible to the public's questions (Sep 7, 2016).

As indicated in the preceding excerpts, ATV portrayed the need for protesters to present their concerns about good governance and development issues peacefully and attempted to demonstrate that the government was taking steps and working hard to respond to demonstrators' pressing issues democratically and inclusively. A calm presentation of their issues was suggested as a solution. In addition, the report, however, presumed the protestors' complaints were a lack of good governance and development.

Residents of Chagni claim that declaring a state of emergency has solved the current crisis.

On his side, Chagni Mayor Ato Asemie Berle stated that the newly established command post is restoring human and democratic rights (Dec 2, 2016).

The declaration of a state of emergency on October 9th, 2016 was framed by ATV as a solution to the current crisis and that the established command post is protecting human and political rights in the region. On the other hand, the declaration of a state of emergency escalated the power struggle between EPRDF hardliners and soft-liners and gave protesters time to reorganize and plan their strategies [41]. Amnesty International, on the other hand, complained about Ethiopia's declaration of a state of emergency, fearing the infringement on political and human rights in the Amhara region.

The measures and restrictions in the State of Emergency Declaration violate, directly or indirectly, non-derogable rights such as freedom from torture and other ill-treatment and freedom from the non-retroactive application of criminal laws. The geographic expanse of the state of emergency, covering, as it does, the whole country, violates the requirement that restrictions under a state of emergency shall be necessary and proportionate to the exigencies that required the state of emergency [42].

The other story framed by ATV is portraying the commitment of religious leaders to play their roles in the restoration of peace and stability in their towns, as shown below:

Thus, ATV framed the unacceptability of violent means and the potential harm they pose to the country by enlisting the help of prominent community members, such as religious leaders, to bring the crisis to a close.

Challenges of public protest framing

The research respondents explained the internal and external bottlenecks that challenge the framing of the Amhara protest as follows.

Internal media framing impediments in ATV

Poor journalistic training

Lack of professional journalistic training has been identified as one of the key internal factors in the framing of public protest. An editor who is 40 and worked for ATV for 11 years claims that the media industry is hindered by a lack of proper capacity-building training given to journalists about issue framing as detailed below.

The training of external stakeholders does not seem to be in line with the national media industry's present rate of expansion. As a result, having a common understanding of how to frame public protest is essential for national media strategy. Because there aren't enough skilled journalists (reporters, editors, and program producers), media texts are presented in a boring way (Editor C, May 3, 2017).

Deputy Editor-in-Chief 2, who is in her late 30s and has worked for the station for 15 years, also mentioned a lack of journalistic knowledge and skills in issue framing as follows:

Journalists and media managers at the station do not appear to have well-versed knowledge in issue framing particularly public protest. As a result, institutional integrity and confidence are rapidly eroding. The general public's mistrust is also evident in many parts of society (Deputy Editor in Chief 2, 03 May 2017).

As can be seen in the above findings, journalists who were working for the station lacked issue framing in general and public protest in particular, and it compromised the integrity of the station. A lack of journalistic training seemed to create the problem and erode the trust of the public in the station. Lack of journalistic training by trainers and poor knowledge and skills of practitioners' issue framing led the practice to an ugly presentation of media stories. This is not unique to Ethiopia, but in some severe circumstances, Kenya too, another country in sub-Saharan Africa lacked expertise in journalism theory and practice [43]. The counter public developed by Ref. [35] is also manifested somehow as the public develops a sense of mistrust in ATV due to the boring style of issue framing. This led the public to feel shy about viewing ATV programs. Thus, ATV journalists should frame issues on a professional level, as "media practitioners are expected to conduct their news and program framings based on the public instead of entertaining simple political statements" [44].

Lack of self-confidence

ATV journalists are held accountable by an editor for their lack of confidence in handling issue framing. Editor E, an adult who worked in the media for 15 years disclosed that lack of self-confidence hinders journalists' capacity to exercise fully as indicated below:

The main issue among ATV journalists is a lack of self-confidence. A number of them exaggerate the issue to project a favourable image, while others downplay it to avoid being criticized by certain sections of society. As a result, the journalists' lack of confidence impacted the structuring of news and programs during the public protest reporting (Editor E, May 02, 2017).

In addition, an editor in her mid-30s who worked for 14 years underlined that.

Some journalists go through a whole wave of position. They suffer from whether we should be subservient to our profession or obedient to our superiors. This is considered a lack of self-confidence for me. Therefore, when they make news or programs, they deliberately self-censor themselves. In order not to get into conflict with their superiors, they prioritize their daily life over their journalistic career. Especially in 2016, when the Amhara resistance was high a state of emergency was announced, it was a time when self-confidence was highly in trouble as the command post controlled most of the activities (Editor B May 05, 2017).

The findings appear to be consistent with [45] findings. "Framing in the Ethiopian media industry is predominantly focused on the significance of the subject matter rather than the technical and scientific applications," the author noted. Journalists' media framing strategies in everyday news routines are ineffective in production. The journalists did not pay much attention to how the challenges are expressed scientifically. Furthermore [46], explored the usage of substantial self-censorship on the part of journalists who strive to comply with the anticipated reporting style of the official media. Related to self-censorship [34], argued that individuals changed the way they acted and even thought without ever being punished because of the pervasive dread of being seen by the state or assessed by societal standards. People had evolved into "self-regulating" beings who, without real force, "voluntarily" adjusted who they were to conform to social and political expectations [34].

Inconsistent in-house training

The Amhara Mass Media Agency, of which Amhara TV is a part, has its research directorate that gives training to the staff of the agency. But the findings of the FGD proved that the directorate is incapable of providing consistent training programs to the staff members. The training, which was developed by the department, couldn't improve the skills of the staff. The previous training primarily focused on the basics of journalism, but the agency was more ambitious and expected advanced-level reporting techniques from media practitioners to produce news and program products (Language Co-coordinator, Editor in Chief, May 14, 2016). Moreover, journalists share the inconsistency of in-house training. For instance, Reporter IV, in his late 30s who worked for 13 years for the station, narrated the irregularities of the training as follows.

The inconsistencies of the training program make the journalists incompetent in addressing different issues. Institutional training has already stopped starting in 2015. New employees who joined the agency in the last two years started their daily routines by themselves. They lack the necessary skills and knowledge for framing public protests and other fundamental elements of journalism (Reporter IV, May 7, 2017).

In addition, Editor 2, in his mid-30s who worked for 9 years in the organization, suggested a strong follow-up of the training to equip journalists with the necessary skills and knowledge:

Continuous training and capacity building program is extremely fundamental to equip journalists with different skills. The training schedule needs strong follow-ups in support of modern media industry development (Editor 2, May 7, 2017).

The findings show that journalists must receive professional training to create responsible and qualified staff, which is necessary for the media to function efficiently.

Media ownership problems

Media ownership is incredibly fundamental in addressing issues related to protest and conflict framing. Since the ATV is financed by the government, it is challenging to frame news professionally in times of protest, in which [2,3] assume the role of the state in putting direct and indirect pressures on the functioning of news and program production.

Amhara TV is fully under the control of the regional government. An annual budget is allocated to the Agency by the government. So, it’s strong ownership in controlling the contents through the assigned politicians (Editor C, May 3, 2017).

According to Ref. [47], journalists' habits of softening news stories may have been influenced by political communication issues to reduce the risks posed by various points of view, to this [4], noted that EPRDF strives for hegemonic power by silencing competing viewpoints.

ATV's deputy editors indicated that a few reporters were punished and warned for disobeying orders to report on the public protest. A reporter, for example, was docked half a month's salary for refusing to cover the 2016 Amhara protest [46]. reported about journalists in Ethiopia who have suffered no explicit penalty for transgressing editorial or ethical rules.

In addition, deputy Editor 3 also witnessed that political technicians of the ruling party have a strong interest in concentrating on the positive news about their organizations. The primary challenges in media framing are the domination of media ownership or the government. The ownership maintains a monopoly on the media sector. Instead [5], observed how the EPRDF's Revolutionary Democracy shaped the media, ensuring that the state media remained firmly under party control, and their coverage habitually focused on development initiatives such as road construction or agriculture, as well as information about other government programs. Furthermore, the state owns the media, the news agency, and the media regulator [4].

In summary [48], contend that media practitioners impose a variety of impediments and restrictions on political agenda setting, including public protest issues. According to most experts, the main impediment to adopting conflict framing is a lack of clarity regarding internal media politics. Similarly [1], refers to the framing process as the internal variables that impact how journalists and news organizations frame an issue.

External factors of media framing in ATV

Among the external media framing considerations [1], mentioned external factors that can impact how journalists and news organizations frame stories.

Political influence

External interventions, according to Ref. [49], have a range of effects on the media's overall production processes, and their activities are sometimes purposeful, as they seek to frame events in their favour. Reporter-VII additionally reinforces the participation of external actors in the following way.

Some political leaders are more concerned with dealing with their institutions' positive values. Misdeeds perpetrated by organizations do not appear to be appropriate for inclusion in the story. A lot of political leaders restrict access to information, making it difficult for the media to frame issues properly (Reporter-VII, May 11, 2017).

A reporter who is in his late 20 worked for 6 years mentioned the interference of the government as follows:

In the selection of media agenda and angles, it was required that they should be politically oriented in a way that would benefit the ruling party. Thus, reporters who were assigned to cover the public protest were provided orientation on how to report (Reporter-VI, May 8, 2017).

The findings appear to be consistent with Forbes (2005), as cited by Ref. [46], who reported on the political influence of African journalism. Finally, the media are influenced by the political, economic, and social constraints that exist in the wider systems within which they function [50].

The emotional reaction of the public

The emotional reactions of the general public seem to indicate the attitude of the audience towards ATV. The study participants mentioned a variety of challenges to covering the 2016 Amhara protest. Reporter-VIII was assigned to Gondar to carry out news coverage of the general public protests in Gondar and the surrounding woreda. He described the emotional reactions of the public towards ATV journalists as follows:

The people did not seem to watch all the local media or give interviews to them, including Amhara TV. The journalists were highly intimidated by the public. Looting of media property was common in many parts of the western Amhara. Journalists were susceptible to verbal and physical attacks by the protesters, and the general public shifted to foreign media services to get urgent and reliable information. That was the most critical and therefore the most challenging time for us (Reporter-8, May 11, 2017).

Similarly, reporter VII also explained the annoyance of the people by the government media. Journalists were forced to go with soldiers while they travelled in search of public protest news in numerous parts of the region.

I was assigned to Koladiba and Dangla, both in the Amhara region, to cover the public protests of 2016. I used to be forced to be monitored and kept by the security forces … The stereotyping of public media was a huge challenge. Society wasn't interested in local media outputs. Public media practitioners were considered spies of the government (EPRDF) in search of political gossip (Reporter-VII, May 11, 2017).

Although the journalist considered him or her a media practitioner, the public perceived him as a spy for the government; so he faced problems getting information sources. In general, the findings corroborate [51], which discovered that "Ethiopian journalists are proud of their job, but, unfortunately, they shy away from overtly identifying themselves as journalists out of embarrassment or fear of public views toward them." The public's response appears to be consistent with [35]"counter public," which caused people to violently retaliate against ATV journalists.

Political leaders' lack of cooperation

The willingness of political leaders who are assigned to different levels was not satisfactory in giving responses to questions raised by the general public. They preferred to convey news conferences via radio and newspapers only. They didn't want to be viewed or watched on the TV screen. Reporters from ATV explained the case in the following way:

I was in Bahir Dar during the peak hours of the general public protest. The political leaders in several hierarchies were not readily delivering information to ATV about the public protest of 2016. They failed to respond to any telephone calls. So, the station was highly challenged … Every politician refused to be interviewed by ATV for two reasons. The first reason was fear of public pressure. The second reason is assuming the power of the government has been deteriorating and wants another political shelter. So, the station faced challenges covering the news hours effectively during the primary week of the general public protest (Reporter-IX, May 11, 2017).

Another reporter had similar complaints about the weak cooperation of political leaders and journalists. Political leaders were not cooperative in giving interviews to ATV journalists.

Except for the highest levels of the regional political leaders, the rest weren't interested to be interviewed by ATV. The main reason was fear of public pressure. Even the questions which were raised by the protesters (demonstrators) were tough and challenging to provide an immediate response to the general public. (Reporter-III, 11 May 2017).

Similarly, journalists working for state media complain about difficulties in gaining access to people in power. For instance Ref. [52], said an interview might take months to be granted.

Summary

The purpose of this study was to look into how ATV (the screen) showed the 2016 Amhara protest (street demonstration), as well as the challenges media practitioners encountered. It found that ATV constructed news articles on public protests in the Amhara region that favoured the government's perspective. Birhane (2002), as cited in Ref. [53], supports this conclusion by stating that the ATV has been used to disseminate the government's narrative. To put it another way, the media has been utilized to satisfy the needs and whims of those in power. As key frames, ATV used the cause of the protest, its consequences, the government's response to it, and potential solutions to the protest. The solution ATV focused on was persuading protesters to cease protesting and present their demands in a peaceful and nonviolent way.

In terms of the protest's cause, ATV cited a lack of good governance and development as the key issues, which contradicts the demonstrators' placards, one of which reads "Stop Amhara Mass Killing." The protest, on the other hand, resulted in economic destruction and the loss of lives, on which ATV was strongly focused in its presentation. To prevent the crisis, ATV highlighted government activities such as declaring a state of emergency, establishing command posts, and pursuing deep government renewal at different levels. In response, ATV depicted the government's responses to the demonstration as ones that would bring about solutions to the ongoing situation. It asserted that the administration was being rejuvenated and that it was ready to answer demonstrators' queries.

In terms of information sources, ATV emphasized governmental authorities, religious leaders, and residents of several Amhara towns who provided solutions but were opposed to the ongoing public protests. Throughout the 2009 European Parliamentary election campaign, official sources dominated media coverage. Local government authorities were cited far less frequently than foreign government officials or EU spokespersons [54].

Amhara's protest framing by ATV was heavily hampered by both internal and external factors. These include professional constraints, a lack of self-confidence, inconsistencies in institutional training, media ownership, political influence, emotional reactions from the public against ATV journalists, and a lack of collaboration from political leaders. These elements caused ATV news reports to be supportive of the government's view.

Conclusion

The contrast between what the protesters on the street demand and how the screen (ATV) shows the demonstration is different. This discrepancy may emanate from Ethiopia's lapdog media tradition. Finally, the study recommends that ATV journalists should receive on-the-job training on how to frame news stories during times of instability or violence. In addition, the research calls for future researchers to investigate the framing of public protest and conflict by ATV after PM Ahmed came to power.
