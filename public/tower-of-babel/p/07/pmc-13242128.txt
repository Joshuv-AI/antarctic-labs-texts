Discriminative and predictive power of radiomics features from preoperative MRI studies in patients with supraspinatus lesions
Authors: Umile Giuseppe Longo, Paolo Giaccone, Luca Bacco, Federico D’Antoni, Alberto Lalli, Benedetta Bandini, Calogero di Naro, Carlo Casciaro, Ara Nazarian, Mario Merone, Emiliano Schena, Rocco Papalia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Relying solely on MRI scans may prove insufficient for obtaining a complete view of a patient’s supraspinatus health status comprising both objective and subjective disability level. On the other hand, while Patient Reported Outcome Measures (PROM) capture patients’ personal experiences, they may not provide objective data.This study assessed the correlation level between preoperative MRI radiomics features and pre-and post-operative PROM scores in patients diagnosed with supraspinatus lesions. 44 patients who underwent arthroscopic repair for full-thickness Rotator Cuff Tears (RCT) of any grade between January 2019 and January 2022 in a single surgical centre were enrolled in the study. All coronal T2-weighted MRI scans were evaluated independently by two orthopedic surgeons blinded to the patients’ clinical information, and the radiomics features were extracted. The strength of linear and non-linear relationships were assessed respectively through Fisher’s statistical test applied to linear regression models, namely F-statistics, and through a model-free AI-based Mutual Information estimator. Together, these methods provided a comprehensive feature selection framework, with overlapping results reinforcing feature relevance and differences revealing complementary patterns in the radiomics–PROM relationship. Besides, they allowed us to construct an integrated ranking of radiomic features based on their strength of association with clinical outcomes, from which we selected those ranking in the top 10 most important of at least three PROMs.The radiomics features most frequently related to the patient’s health condition were Maxi- mum2DDiameterColumn, Flatness, LeastAxisLength, and features gathered from the Gray Level Co-occurrences Matrix (GLCM), like Correlation, Maximum Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and Informational Measure of Correlation 2 (Imc2).Identifying crucial visual features pertaining to the patient’s health status highlighted the potential for radiomics-guided personalized medicine in rotator cuff surgery. Incorporating both linear and non-linear analyses adds depth to the findings, establishing a strong groundwork for future advancements in the field.

Introduction

Rotator cuff disease is the most common cause of shoulder pain, accounting for up to 70% of all shoulder-related medical consultations [1, 2]. In particular, Rotator Cuff Tears (RCTs) of the supraspinatus muscle are the most common in people over 60, with a prevalence of 61.9% in men and 38.1% in women [3, 4].

The risk factors are identified by older age, greater BMI and smoking [5], but also hypertension, dyslipidemia and diabetes [6].

To select the appropriate treatment plan, physicians are especially interested in determining the status of the tendons involved, the dimensions and expansion of the rupture, and the evidence of muscular atrophy in imaging reports [7–9]. This information is commonly retrieved by visual inspection of Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans. Recent studies have evaluated the association between factors identified on preoperative MRI and the reparability of RCTs, with infraspinatus fatty infiltration being the most effective in predicting reparability [10]. Also, they reported a high diagnostic accuracy of MRIs, with sensitivity and specificity rates exceeding 90% for full thickness tears [11].

An accurate analysis can contribute to more precise management decisions and whether surgical rotator cuff repair should be performed [1]. However, surgical intervention may be negatively influenced by factors such as fatty infiltration, multiple tendon involvement, lower preoperative muscle strength, diabetes, and older age [12].

To assess the preoperative level of disability as well as the success of the rotator cuff repair, the most commonly applied clinical Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) are the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI), the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES), the Constant Murley Score (CMS), the Simple Shoulder Test (SST) and the Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS), which provide objective functional outcome measures. On the other hand, other questionnaires such as the 36-Item Short Form health survey (SF-36) or the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) focus more on the perception of the condition from the patient’s perspective, providing an insight into their psychological well-being [13–18].

Relying solely on parameters provided by MRI may prove insufficient for obtaining a complete view of a patient’s supraspinatus health status. On the contrary, questionnaires, while capturing the subjective experiences of patients, lack the utilization of quantifiable parameters in patient analysis [19]. Therefore, it is imperative to establish a correlation between the data extracted from MRI and the actual health status of the patient, as indicated by questionnaire responses. To reconcile the discrepancy between subjective PROMs and objective imaging data, we hypothesized that radiomics features from MRI scans could offer a more objective and measurable indicator of dysfunction, aligning closely with well-established questionnaire scores. Radiomics analysis has emerged in medical imaging, introducing novel pathways for objective evaluations. Radiomics involves extracting an extensive array of quantitative numerical features from medical images, providing a data-centric method for understanding and assessing medical conditions. Recent studies [20] have highlighted the capability of MRI-based radiomics in diagnosing and classifying musculoskeletal injuries. These advancements in imaging analysis offer a chance to improve the objectivity in evaluating supraspinatus lesions.

Identifying radiomics features correlating with the subjective scores from questionnaires could offer a comprehensive and objective assessment of a patient’s overall health status, which could be instrumental for clinicians in managing RCTs. Thus, this study aimed to assess the statistical relations of preoperative MRI radiomics features with both pre- and postoperative outcome scores in patients diagnosed with supraspinatus lesions of any grade. To this end, we applied two complementary methods, F-statistics and Mutual Information, to examine the relationship between radiomics features from MRI scans and the aforementioned PROMs.

This work explores the association between objective visual features and subjective patient-reported outcomes, aiming to provide valuable tools to support treatment planning in the context of rotator cuff surgery.

Materials and methods

Demographics

A retrospective study was conducted among patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair for full-thickness rotator cuff tears of any grade between January 2019 and January 2022. in a single surgical center. Duration of symptoms was at least 3 months. Each patient was given full verbal and written information about the study, and the operating surgeon obtained written informed consent for the procedure. Preoperative evaluations were performed the day before surgery, and follow-up evaluations were conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. Each patient was evaluated for arm dominance, trauma history, duration and type of preoperative symptoms, as well as lesion type.

RCTs were classified according to the classification by DeOrio [21] and Gerber [22].

DeOrio and Cofield used the length of the greatest diameter of the tear to categorize the tear into 1 of 4 groups: small, medium, large, or massive [23]. In contrast, Gerber assessed tear severity by the number of tendons involved, defining a massive tear as one affecting more than two tendons, whereas Cofield defined it as a tear larger than 5 cm [22].

This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee. The study was developed following Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines.

Eligibility criteria

Patients were included in the study if they had an RCT diagnosed upon clinical examination, no episodes of shoulder instability, no radiographic signs of fracture of the glenoid or the greater or lesser tuberosity, MRI evidence of RCT, symptoms duration of at least three months and inadequate response to nonoperative management (including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, and rest). Exclusion criteria comprised a history of prior surgery on the affected shoulder, autoimmune or systemic inflammatory arthritis, or inability to complete questionnaires due to language barrier or cognitive disorder.

Arthroscopic procedure

A diagnostic arthroscopy was performed to evaluate the extent of the RCT, any lesions of the biceps tendon, and other associated lesions. The single-row anchor repair technique was employed in all tears. One row of suture anchors double-loaded with No. 2 Fiberwire (Q-FIX, Smith & Nephew, London, United Kingdom) was employed. The anchors were placed inside the lateral aspect of the footprint, and both sutures were passed through the tendon in a mattress fashion. The number of suture anchors employed varied on a case-by-case basis. On average, 1.9 anchors were used (range, 1–2 anchors). Additionally, for L-shaped and U-shaped tears, a side-to-side suture was initially used to repair the tears, facilitating the convergence of the two edges of the cuff before securing the cuff to the bone. All biceps tenodesis procedures were performed using an established technique [24, 25].

Postoperative management

Postoperative management was standardized for all patients. The arm was supported using a sling with an abduction pillow for six weeks. At six weeks, the sling was removed, and overhead stretching with a rope and pulley was started. Stronger iso-inertial strengthening and rehabilitation of the rotator cuff, deltoid, and scapular stabilizers were initiated 10 or 12 weeks after the operation. Rehabilitation was continued for six months. Heavy manual work and overhead activities were allowed after a good restoration of shoulder strength, defined as the absence of significant pain during resisted shoulder movements, particularly in overhead positions, and typically achieved 6 to 10 months after surgery.

Clinical outcomes scales

Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) - The 13-item SPADI score quantifies shoulder pain and disability in patients. The original SPADI score ranged from 0 (best condition) to 100 (worst condition). In this study, the reverse scale was utilized for comparison with other PROMs [26].

Simple Shoulder Test (SST) – The 12-item SST represents the shoulder’s state in functional terms. The overall score ranges from 0 (worst condition) to 12 (best condition) [27].

American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) - The 11-item ASES score involves 11 questions covering pain (1 item) and function (10 items). The score ranges from 0% (worst condition) to 100% (best condition) [28].

Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS) - The 12-item OSS questionnaire was created to evaluate pain and disability [29]. The individual scores are summed up to a total possible score of 48 (best condition) [30].

Constant Murley Score (CMS) - The 100-item CMS evaluates pain, activities of daily living, range of motion, and strength in the affected shoulder. The scores range from 0 (worst condition) to 100 points (best condition) [31].

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) - The 14-item HADS measures the subjective levels of anxiety and depression. It ranges from 0 (best condition) to 42 (worst condition) [32] and, similar to SPADI, in this study we reversed the scale for comparison with other PROMs.

36-Item Short-Form Health Survey Questionnaire (SF-36) - The 36-item SF-36 evaluates the physical component and the mental component ranging from 0 (worst condition) to 100 (best condition) [33].

Images acquisition and preprocessing

Non-arthrographic MRI studies were performed on all patients preoperatively. Coronal, T2-weighted spin-echo MRI scans (repetition time, 3200 milliseconds; echo time, 85 milliseconds) were obtained in all patients. T2-weighted, Gradient-Echo (GE) Spectral Pre-saturation Inversion Recovery (SPIR) sequences in true axial scans were acquired, as well as T1-weighted, GE SPIR sequences in oblique coronal planes that were parallel to the course of the supraspinatus muscle, and oblique sagittal planes parallel to the glenoid fossa.

Patients underwent MRI at various locations, but all scans were standardized in terms of protocols and magnet strengths. Two orthopedic surgeons blinded to the patients’ clinical information and surgical history evaluated all scans independently.

Despite the multiple sequences acquired, we employed the coronal T2-weighted scans only due to better visibility of the tendon tears and a more proper alignment with several previous studies [4, 34]. Furthermore, to focus the subsequent visual feature extraction on the specific regions of interest, we had the supraspinatus muscles slice-wise manually contoured on ITK-SNAP [35] by an expert physician and further revised by a shoulder surgeon with more than ten years of experience. All MRI scans were computationally corrected for intensity inhomogeneities by the N4ITK algorithm [36], and the relative muscle manual masks underwent morphological binary dilation by a square connectivity structuring element to allow for the subsequent processing to focus on the supraspinatus and the adjacent tissues.

Features extraction

From each 3D MRI, we extracted established radiomics features using an Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI)-compliant open-source software, namely PyRadiomics (https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html) [37], contributing to the broader goal of standardizing radiomics analyses in medical imaging. The 107 extracted features can be broadly categorized into 18 intensity-based features, 14 shape-based features, and five texture matrices from which a total of 75 texture-based features were extracted. Each feature class provides a unique set of data points that contribute to the comprehensive MRI scan analysis. A detailed list of all extracted features is provided in the supplementary materials.

Intensity-based Features - These features describe the distribution of voxel intensities within the image Region Of Interest (ROI) defined by the mask without considering any spatial pattern. They offer basic statistical metrics to depict the histogram of gray-level intensities within the ROI, comprising, for instance, the mean, the median, the standard deviation, and other statistical moments, such as skewness and kurtosis.

Shape-based Features - These features describe the ROI’s two- and three-dimensional geometry. They are independent of the gray-scale intensity distribution, providing insights into the selected image area’s physical dimensions and shape characteristics. Some descriptors refer to the ROI volume, sphericity, elongation, or flatness.

Texture-based features - These higher-order statistics capture information about the spatial arrangement and patterns of gray-scale intensities within the image ROI, offering a more detailed view of the tissue characteristics. Such features are typically computed as numerical descriptors of different texture matrices, considering different aspects of the spatial relationships between voxel intensities and providing an overall quantitative representation of texture. The matrices that served as the basis for the texture description were the Gray Level Co-occurrences Matrix (GLCM), the Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (GLSZM), the Gray Level Run Length Matrix (GLRLM), the Gray Level Dependence Matrix (GLDM) and the Neighboring Gray Tone Difference Matrix (NGTDM). Texture features derived from these matrices included entropy, contrast, energy, etc.

Analysis methods

We employed two complementary approaches to investigate statistical dependence between MRI radiomics features and clinical PROMs: first, we evaluated the strength of linear relationships through Fisher’s statistical test applied to linear regression models, namely F-statistics; then, we analyzed non-linear interactions through a model-free AI-based Mutual Information estimator. These methods were chosen for their ability to capture different relationship types between random variables, allowing the identification of those radiomics features that exhibit higher associations with each clinical PROM, regardless of linearity or non-linearity. In particular, the inclusion of the AI-based MI estimation was motivated by the need to account for the non-linear and potentially intricate interactions between high-dimensional radiomics features and patient-reported outcomes. These interactions may reflect underlying biological complexity that is not adequately represented through linear models alone. Furthermore, conventional methods, such as correlation coefficients, Principal Component Analysis, or embedded feature selection techniques, were deemed inadequate due to inability to capture complex and non-linear relationships, the disregard for outcome relevance, or the lack of interpretability for clinical application. Our chosen approach addresses these limitations more effectively.

F-statistics - To effectively identify the radiomics features exhibiting a straightforward, linear influence on the PROMs, for each given outcome measure, we fitted a linear regression model on each feature, further computing the F-statistics to assess the overall significance of such regression. The F-statistics is calculated by comparing the variance explained by the regression model to the variance that cannot be explained by the model (i.e., the residual variance), thus assessing whether the linear regression model explains a significant portion of the variance of the dependent variable. One metric relies on fundamental statistical analyses, providing a rigorous foundation, while the other leverages artificial intelligence, offering a more sophisticated measure for uncovering intricate patterns within the data. Both methodologies were employed to assess the discriminative and predictive relevance of radiomics feature groups regarding pre-operative and post-operative PROMs respectively. Specifically, the analysis of pre-operative PROMs aimed to identify quantitative and objective imaging features that correspond to patients’ subjective perceptions of supraspinatus-related functional impairment and pain. Conversely, the evaluation of post-operative PROMs focused on uncovering radiomic features with the greatest prognostic value, that is those most sensitive to predicting symptomatic recovery one year after undergoing the same standardized arthroscopic procedure. Subsequently, we constructed a comprehensive ranking of individual visual features based on the strength of linear and/or non-linear correlation with clinical outcomes, selecting the most important ones ranking in the top 10 of at least three PROMs. This process facilitated the identification of the most relevant features for each PROM based on each respective method and the determination of the most significant features when considering both analytical perspectives.

Finally, 44 patients (20 females, 24 males, mean age 58.9 ± 7.8) met the selection criteria and were enrolled in the study. Overall, there were two small tears, 27 medium-sized tears, eight large tears, and seven massive RCTs. The patient demographics is summarized in Table 1.

Preoperative analysis

The discriminative potential of radiomics features was assessed through their statistical relevance with the reported PROMs at admission time. An F-statistics and a mutual information value were estimated for each feature and each clinical PROM at admission time. In contrast, the average values for each feature group were normalized between 0 and 1 to be depicted proportionately within the same frame and reported in Fig. 1. Radar plots showed a predominance of the shape-based features as the most relevant group across the clinical outcomes according to the Mutual Information score. The F-statistics scores were in agreement with the non-linear analysis in the top three PROMs of Fig. 1 (i.e., SF36, SST, and OSS) while indicating different groups as the most linearly dependent on the other clinical outcomes. In particular, the intensity features were found to be the most relevant for both ASES and CMS, while HADS and SPADI were more correlated to texture features.

Then, we moved on to a more fine-grained analysis involving the most relevant individual features. Starting from the whole rankings, we selected the top 10 most important radiomics features for each PROM according to each of the two analytical assessments. Then, we merged the obtained lists of best features, focusing on the most frequent ones in the top 10 of at least three preoperative PROMs based on Mutual Information, F-statistics, or both scores. The features selected by the analysis are reported in Table 2, sorted by frequency, and grouped by classes. As noted, the shape-based features comprised almost half of the selected best visual properties, confirming the previous finding about the average predominance of this class. Furthermore, considering the total incidence of the number of extracted features, 7 out of 14 shape-based descriptors (i.e., 50.0%) were remarked as critical visual features concerning the preoperative PROMs; on the other hand, only two descriptors out of 18 (i.e., 11.1%) and 6 out of 75 (i.e., 8.0%) were labeled as crucial intensity and texture features respectively. In particular, it is noteworthy that, among the five employed texture matrices, almost all selected texture descriptors were derived from the GLCM, suggesting a greater suitability of these visual features in relation to the clinical outcomes. A complete ranking of all the extracted features can be found in the supplementary materials.

Postoperative analysis

The predictive potentials of radiomics features were assessed using the reported PROMs at one-year follow-up. We focused on the difference between the PROMs reported after one year and those reported at admission time, thus investigating how informative the preoperative visual features could be on the expected variation of the perceived clinical conditions. Similar to the previous analysis, we estimated the F-statistics and the mutual information values for every visual feature from the preoperative MRI scan and each reported PROM variation. Then, we computed the average predictive relevance of each feature group according to both analytical scores and reported the normalized results as radar plots for each clinical outcome. As shown in Fig. 2, although OSS and ASES exhibited a certain level of concordance in selecting shape-based features again, none of the groups emerged as predominant; on the contrary, the analysis indicated an average balance among the three feature groups. In particular, the MI shaded areas results were more spread out over the radar plots; however, in the preoperative analysis they were more in line with the shape class (Fig. 1). This highlights that Mutual Information and F-statistics serve as complementary analytical tools, enhancing the robustness of findings when their assessments align (e.g., in the case of SST, OSS, and ASES) and broadening the analytical perspective when they diverge (e.g., for SF36, CMS, HADS, and SPADI).

Discussion

This study investigated the potential of radiomics analysis, attempting to select the most influential features in detecting the current patient health status and predicting its postoperative variation.

The major findings of the present study were, on the one hand, the predominance of the shape-based feature class, as well as a remarkable relevance of the GLCM characteristics when assessing the severity of the preoperative clinical condition; on the other hand, we found that the evolution of the overall clinical picture was driven by a more heterogeneous set of visual features, with particular emphasis on a wider ensemble of texture descriptors. Specifically, our analyses pointed out a limited collection of radiomics features as the most frequently related to the patient’s health condition, namely Maximum2DDiameterColumn, Flatness, LeastAxisLength, GLCM-Correlation, GLCM-MCC, and GLCM-Imc2. These features were particularly noteworthy as they showed a high significance through both analytical methods, indicating a consistent influence on various clinical outcomes. Furthermore, their presence, in both preoperative and postoperative contexts, suggested that these features could be of fundamental importance in aiding the physicians in selecting appropriate treatment plan, according to the patients’ preoperative MRI features.

These descriptors are primarily geometric and extracted in a data-driven manner, but their relevance may be interpreted through the lens of known anatomical and pathological changes associated with rotator cuff tears. For example, Maximum2DDiameterColumn represents the maximum length of the region of interest (ROI) in the coronal plane, which likely corresponds to the longest extent of supraspinatus muscle or tendon involvement. A greater diameter could reflect tear size, retraction, or muscle elongation, which are known to influence surgical complexity and recovery, and thus impact patient-perceived function and pain. Flatness quantifies how much the ROI deviates from a perfectly spherical shape, in this case, how “flattened” the supraspinatus appears in the coronal plane. This could relate to muscle atrophy or architectural distortion, both of which are common in chronic tears and may correlate with functional impairment reported in PROMs. Finally, LeastAxisLength measures the shortest axis of the ROI’s bounding ellipsoid, potentially reflecting localized thinning or muscle wasting, which again may influence strength and range of motion, which are dimensions often captured by scores like CMS or ASES.

According to the investigation, shape-based variables appear to have the strongest correlation with clinical outcomes. Features like LeastAxisLength, Flatness, and Maximum2DDiameterColumn could be employed preoperatively in clinical practice to evaluate tear size and shape, which would help with surgical approach selection and reparability assessment. A greater tear diameter with a lower flatness, for example, would necessitate the use of extra anchors or a double-row repair, enabling more individualized and efficient surgical planning.

While these interpretations remain hypothesis-generating, the repeatable, observer-independent quantification enabled by radiomics is itself a major strength. In contrast to qualitative visual assessments, these metrics allow for consistent, scalable characterization of structural changes that might otherwise go unnoticed. Future work involving correlation with intraoperative findings or histological muscle quality could further validate the clinical meaning of these features.

This study describes the discriminative and predictive power of radiomics MRI features in preoperative and postoperative questionnaire scores of patients with supraspinatus lesions, attempting to uncover objective imaging features linked to patient-reported outcomes with either diagnostic or prognostic value for post-surgical recovery.

In general, the use of radiomics features in clinical research to predict patient outcomes has proliferated in recent years, as confirmed by several related publications, also within the orthopedics field. For instance, a recent study [20] aimed to develop and evaluate MRI-based radiomics models for detecting supraspinatus tendon injuries in shoulder joints. Radiomics characteristics computed from T2-weighted coronal images demonstrated relatively high accuracy for recognizing tears and differentiating tear severity.

Moreover, concerning the efficacy of hip preservation surgery with fibula allograft, another study [39] presented a polynomial prediction model that integrated clinical features and CT radiomics. The model demonstrated improved predictive performance over models based solely on clinical data, indicating the relevance of radiomics features in predicting the outcomes of orthopedic surgeries. Notably, the authors reported two radiomics characteristics as the most closely associated with the endpoint of the surgical treatment in focus: the GLCM Idmn and the MinorAxisLength, a texture- and shape-based feature which was remarkable in our study as well (Table 2), despite differences in the considered anatomical regions.

Another study [40], focusing on lumbar disc herniation, explored the combination of radiomics feature analysis and clinical features through artificial intelligence to predict postoperative objective outcomes, such as hospital Length of Stay (LOS), operation time and complications onset. The study found that including radiomics features in several Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models resulted in a minimal but detectable improvement in accuracy, suggesting the value of multimodal data inputs for future predictive modeling.

On the contrary, it has also been shown that standard knee-related MRI features, including tear pattern, tear length, tear distance from the meniscocapsular junction, tear thickness, and integrity of any inner meniscal fragment, tend to be poor predictors of reparability as assessed by arthroscopic criteria, even when using higher resolution 3T scanners [41]. The definition of a standardized set of radiomics features remains imperative to obtain a protocol applicable to the everyday clinical practice.

Hence, these research studies demonstrated how clinicians can obtain a more holistic view of patient health by integrating radiomics features in standard clinical practice, which is essential in choosing the best treatment and predicting long-term outcomes.

Another key aspect in this area is developing, training, and validating suitable AI models to support clinical decision making. Concerning RCT management, many studies investigated the predictive potential of AI diagnostic and prognostic purposes, focusing most on preoperative MRI scans entirely processed through Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [42]. Using a deep CNN, a screening model was created to detect RCTs in routine shoulder MRI. The axial plane performed the best between the individual MRI planes, followed by sagittal and coronal planes, suggesting that deep-learning-based automatic detection can effectively identify torn areas in various regions of the rotator cuff in all three imaging planes [43]. However, a previous study [34] found the coronal plane to be more appropriate for detecting tendon ruptures in the shoulder when using visual descriptors such as the mean intensity value of the supraspinatus tendon. As this plane offers enhanced sensitivity and specificity for identifying tendon ruptures, further confirmed by other studies [44], our study focused on MRI scans of the coronal plane.

Additional work [45] has also demonstrated that DL is feasible to detecting supraspinatus tears on coronal MRI. This study developed a 3-stage DL pipeline and tested it on 200 shoulder MRI studies. The results showed the classifier achieved relatively high sensitivity and specificity.

Despite the promising predictive results, there is still a notable for paucity of DL studies for shoulder MRI evaluations [46], and in particular literature, is still lacking in studies investigating the impact of radiomics on RCTs management. Hence, the main strength of this article is in its novelty, since the predictive ability of radiomics for supraspinatus tears has not been previously explored.

Another key point consists of employing two complementary approaches to investigate the statistical dependence between MRI radiomics features and clinical outcomes. F-statistics was primarily utilized to identify linear correlations between features and outcomes. It is adept at pinpointing features that directly and proportionally influence the PROMs. However, its limitation is in recognizing only linear dependencies, hence to address this, we employed Mutual Information, through a model-free AI-based estimator. Mutual Information is suitable for detecting any form of dependency between variables, including those beyond linear relationships, which might be missed by conventional linear models. Therefore, by integrating these two approaches, our study strived to offer a more comprehensive understanding of how MRI-derived radiomics features correlate with subjective PROMs typically used in clinical assessments of supraspinatus injuries.

Furthermore, another strength was the balance between specificity and generality in the patient population enrolled. In fact, while this study analyzed a homogeneous pathology cohort specified by precise excluding criteria, the authors recognized the importance of broader applicability. Thus, to enhance diversity, a varied population in terms of age and BMI was included. This intentional shift allowed for a more comprehensive explanation within a specific pathology.

Limitations

However, a limitation of this study is that the findings may be subject to bias due to the limited size of the patient cohort. A larger sample size would enhance the study’s statistical power; however, it would also imply a more heterogeneous cohort, which may pose challenges for obtaining precise and reliable results. Future studies with larger and more diverse patient cohorts would contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the method’s applicability and reliability in clinical settings.

Furthermore, a larger patient cohort will allow the application of more sophisticated ML models.

Besides, despite one of the key advantages of radiomics is its ability to extract quantitative features automatically and reproducibly from medical images, several limitations exist, particularly when comparing these automated methods to manual estimation. First of all, even though radiomics provides objective quantification, the quality of shape-based features is highly dependent on the accuracy of the segmentation mask. In our study, regions of interest were manually segmented by experts using ITK-SNAP and refined by a senior orthopedic surgeon to ensure consistency. Nonetheless, manual segmentation itself introduces potential variability and is a limitation for widespread clinical adoption unless automated segmentation tools are integrated. Secondly, many shape descriptors used in radiomics, while mathematically well-defined, may not correspond directly to familiar anatomical measurements used in routine clinical practice, which can make them harder to interpret or trust from a clinician’s perspective. Moreover, shape descriptors can be sensitive to image acquisition parameters, voxel size, and resampling methods. Despite protocol standardization efforts in our study, this remains a broader challenge for radiomics studies aiming for multi-center applicability.

This research lays the foundation for future more clinically-focused studies, employing selected radiomics features for an AI-guided assessment of the patient’s health status and prediction of postoperative outcome scores. In particular, the validation of our findings will involve developing predictive models using the most informative radiomics features identified in this study and training them on larger, multicenter datasets with external validation cohorts to ensure generalizability. This will enable an automatic support to the clinical condition assessment, such as tear severity and overall tendon status, and to precise eligibility criteria by predicting surgical treatment effectiveness.

For clinical implementation, several key factors must be considered. First, reproducibility and standardization are essential: radiomics features, particularly shape and texture descriptors, must be reliably extracted across different scanners, imaging protocols, and institutions. This requires standardized acquisition parameters, preprocessing pipelines, and ideally automated segmentation tools to reduce inter-operator variability. Second, successful implementation will require integration of radiomics features with conventional clinical data such as patient demographics, tear size, and comorbidities to enhance predictive accuracy and relevance. Finally, prospective validation in real-world clinical workflows is essential. Models must not only demonstrate statistical performance but also show that they can improve clinical decision-making, for example, by identifying patients less likely to benefit from surgery or more likely to experience poor functional recovery, and ultimately contribute to better patient outcomes.

Conclusions

The study effectively linked patient-reported measures with imaging data by thoroughly examining both preoperative and postoperative outcomes. Identifying crucial visual features pertaining to the health status of the patient highlighted the potential for radiomics-guided personalized medicine in rotator cuff surgery. The incorporation of F-statistics and Mutual Information in the analyses adds depth to the findings, establishing a strong groundwork for future advancements in the field. In particular, the increased specificity in treatment selection, guided by the MRI features identified in this study, is expected to pave the way for more tailored and effective interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes in cases of supraspinatus injury.

Future research should prioritize validating and extending our findings in larger, multicentre cohorts to enhance generalizability beyond the present single-centre sample of 44 patients, eventually including more objective biomechanical metrics (e.g., via motion analysis or image-derived kinematics) to reduce reliance on subjective assessments. With an expanded cohort, it will be possible to stratify patients by key clinical covariates (e.g., age, comorbidities, tear size) to estimate the independent predictive value of radiomic features on PROMs while adjusting for confounders. Moreover, by extending follow-up beyond the short- to medium-term, we can assess whether the observed correlations between MRI-derived radiomic features and functional recovery or failure persist over the long term. Although overall effect sizes may attenuate with time, we hypothesize that the most robust pre- and post-operative features identified here will continue to predict long-term outcomes after rotator cuff repair. In parallel, future works could explore a truly multimodal model by integrating MRI-derived radiomics signatures with complementary biomarkers, such as genetic polymorphisms, markers of systemic or local inflammation, and serum proteomic profiles. Leveraging advanced machine-learning algorithms on this rich, multimodal dataset holds the promise of boosting predictive accuracy for tear reparability and postoperative outcomes, optimizing treatment selection and improving long-term patient outcomes in supraspinatus injury.
