One decade of school environment monitoring and its influence on providing adequate opportunities for engaging in physical activity from the Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance Global Matrices 1.0 to 4.0
Authors: Vedrana Sember, Gregor Jurak, Gregor Starc, Marjeta Kovač, Farid Bardid, Shawnda A. Morrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Global Matrix is an international initiative that monitors 10 indicators of health-related behaviours and sources of influence on child physical activity (PA) including within the school environment. This study examined global secular trends for the School indicator across Global Matrices 1.0 to 4.0, including its underlying benchmark evidence and socioeconomic indicators across the diverse global regions of participating jurisdictions.MethodsSchool indicator and benchmarking data for each country were compiled for every Global Matrix edition from peer-reviewed and online resources. Benchmark scoring distributions, secular trends and socioeconomic indicators were synthesised to determine the variety and utility of each benchmark by region for report cards published from 2014 to 2024. Bivariate correlations were conducted within-edition between socioeconomic data and School indicator final scores.ResultsTo date, >68 countries/jurisdictions have participated in at least one Global Matrix. Disparity in School indicator grades were apparent (range: A to F), irrespective of regional gross domestic product. The most common benchmark cited was “percent of schools where the majority (≥80 %) are offered the mandated amount of physical education (PE)”. Final grades were significantly associated to a country's GINI score (a measure of income inequality distribution; τ values = 0.274 to 0.572, p values= <0.001 to 0.030).ConclusionThe School indicator is widely used across regions, and despite differences in countries’ socioeconomic development, its grading has remained balanced through its consistent use of key benchmarks, especially those reflecting a required amount of PE, and access to facilities and equipment. Countries with greater income equality tended to be graded higher on the School indicator than those exhibiting less income equality.

Introduction

School programmes are recognised as one of the eight investments that comprise an effective strategy to increasing overall physical activity (PA) in children.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that schools implement systems to encourage daily PA and healthy habits in children,2 and school-based interventions promoting moderate-to-vigorous PA have been shown to reverse childhood obesity trends, particularly when sustained over adequate durations to affect meaningful change.3, 4, 5 Indeed, just as global health guidelines continue to emphasise the positive health impacts associated with performing sufficient PA, (e.g. reducing non-communicable disease state, improved mental health and sleep outcomes),6 a comprehensive review involving 387,437 participants from 23 countries found only ∼7 % of children adhere to 24-h movement behaviour global guidelines.7 Thus, prioritising the development and implementation of effective PA strategies in accessible environments like schools is essential to improving child PA and health outcomes across the lifespan.8

The Active Healthy Kids Global Alliance (AHKGA) is a non-profit organisation that advocates for increasing PA in children and adolescents globally. The organisation launched its flagship Global Matrix (GM) initiative in 2014,9 wherein 15 countries from five continents released country-specific Report Cards on the state of PA in their child populations.10 A summary of the GM initiative history and guiding instructions are available elsewhere.11,12 Given its critical impact on overall PA in children, the school environment remains a pivotal indicator within the GM initiative. Indeed, schools are the primary environment where children spend a significant proportion of their waking hours, making them opportune spaces for promoting and accruing valuable PA minutes.13 Schools can affect a wide range of children originating from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, and school's legislation tends to be robust since they (generally) establish and maintain clear standards, often have mandated physical education (PE) hours, maintain facilities, and hire qualified staff to develop curricula,14 making it a crucial public health asset to act as a consistent PA monitoring hub.15, 16, 17

The most recent GM 4.0 edition confirmed there was wide global variation in School indicator grades.18 An earlier paper by Coppinger et al. (2020)19 first broached the topic of how school and community environments might affect child PA, although their analysis was limited to one GM edition, published half a decade ago, and limited to the European region only. No review has yet examined the global distribution of School indicator benchmarks, nor identified benchmarks that may be underutilized. Therefore, the aims of this study were: (i) present one decade of global secular trends for the GM School indicator, (ii) compile a detailed examination of the rationale used to evaluate the School indicator by country, (iii) determine whether any systematic changes in benchmark scoring patterns occur between GM editions, and (iv) suggest how to improve School benchmarking criteria for future GM editions.

Methods

A total of 68 countriesd have participated in at least one of GM editions to date, constituting a decade of engagement (2014–2024). The initiative began with open invitations for interested countries to join in 2012 (GM1.0),10 2014 (for GM2.0),20 2017 (for GM3.0)21 and 2019 for GM4.0.22 Key data regarding the harmonised Report Card development and process are available from flagship publications listed above. In general, countries were required to gather and synthesize the most robust data available in their region on nine core indicators of child PA, representing health behaviours (Overall PA, Organised Sport Participation, Active Play, Active Transportation, Sedentary Behaviour) and sources of influence (Family and Peers, School, Community and Environment, and Government Strategies and Investments).20 From GM3.0 onward, a new indicator, Physical Fitness, was added to this list of compulsory indicators.21 Based on multiple sources, and following a harmonised approach, a country assigns a letter grade to each indicator, ranging from “A” (i.e., we are succeeding with a large majority of children and youth) to “F” (i.e., we are succeeding with very few children and youth), or incomplete “INC” for sources which do not have enough data to be validly assessed (see: Table 1, grading rubric). Data collection and extraction

School indicator and benchmark data were compiled by two researchers in two epochs, [July 2023 (VS) and October 2024 (FB)], gathered from at least two sources: 1) country-specific peer-reviewed publications, and/or 2) the freely-available, published data archived on the AHKGA web portal (https://www.activehealthykids.org/). Extracted data were then validated by the senior author (SAM) for accuracy, and later confirmed by the remainder of the authorship team. For each School indicator by country, supplementary information was extracted, including: the GM round, data source, and notes on the PE system within that country, among others (see Appendix files: Table A1). Socioeconomic data (i.e. Gross Domestic Product, GDP; Gross National Income, GNI; index of income distribution, GINI) were collected for each of the corresponding Report Card launch years, or the year immediately prior, when data were being assembled by country teams, from open sources (https://data.worldbank.org/), accessed April 01–19th, 2025 and confirmed May 08, 2025.24

Evidence analysis and synthesis

All letter grades were converted from categorical to interval data using methods described elsewhere.22 When tallying individual benchmarks, data were coded in one of three ways, according to whether the authors: (a) simply mentioned that particular benchmark as a justification for their School indicator grade using in-text description only, (b) provided concrete statistical evidence of a given benchmark criteria, but did not cite statistics within-text, or (c) benchmark criteria were mentioned clearly in-text and authors provided overt supporting evidence for that benchmark. Benchmark data are therefore presented as a “total” frequency value, which includes any mention of using that benchmark in a country's final grade, or “evidence only” when authors cite exact statistics to ‘back up’ their benchmark claims. Two-way bivariate correlation statistics were conducted within-edition, (SPSS v.21, IBM, Chicago, IL) to determine whether socioeconomic data (GDP, GNI, GINI) shared variance to School indicator final scores. Because of the anticipated (relatively) small sample sizes (i.e. between 10 and 50 countries with full datasets) and a potential for tied ranks, in part due the ordinal nature of the grading variable, Kendall's tau correlations were conducted, and bias corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95 % confidence intervals were reported. Significance level was set at p < 0.05.

School Indicator secular trends

School indicator change scores are reported in Table 2. Within countries participating in GM 1.0 and 2.0, several reported changes to their School indicator grades. For example, Colombia's School indicator improved from F in 2014 to D in 2016. Mozambique ranked their School indicator grade lower between editions, moving from a C in 2014 to D in 2016. Countries reporting the highest School indicator grade for GM3.0 were Finland, Portugal, Slovenia, (each A) and Denmark (A-). In the latest GM4.0, 57 countries were able to grade their Schools. The highest grades were reported for Hungary, Portugal, Slovenia, and Viet Nam (A), with Hungary's ranking their School Indicator the highest grade of A+. Viet Nam and Finland moving from A to B grades, and Australia (from B+ to C+) reported the greatest decrease in grades from 2018 to 2022. In total, thirty-seven countries were able to grade their schools before the COVID-19 lockdown took place, whereas only four countries were able to grade their schools after (Canada, China, Slovenia, Thailand). Of the total 18 INC school scores, 14 were in the inaugural year of a country joining the GM initiative; these countries cited not being able to rank the School indicator because of a) insufficient data, or b) information were not available, or c) there was not enough time to collect the information. India and Venezuela reported INC twice in a row, for example, whereas Scotland was the only country to grade an INC three times consecutively. All three countries reported no available data sources for Schools.25, 26, 27

Bivariate correlations

Significant relationships were apparent between country GDP (in $USD), or GNI (in $USD) and School indicator grade for GM4.0 only (GDP: τ −0.342, p = 0.007, 95 % BCa CI: 0.535 to −0.115; GNI: τ −0.330, p = 0.009, 95 % BCa CI: 0.525 to −0.102). However, upon further inspection of the data, there appeared a skewing of the relationship due to two outlier countries (USA, China). When the two countries were removed from the analysis, the relationship between GDP, GNI and grade score did not reach significance (p > 0.05). Notably, GINI scores were consistently correlated to School grade scores for every GM edition (Fig. 1). Countries reporting greater income equality distributions also reported higher final School indicator grades for GM1.0 (τ = -0.572, p = 0.017, 95 % BCa CI: 0.815 to −0.160); GM2.0 (τ = -0.444, p = 0.003, 95 % BCa CI: 0.641 to −0.192); GM3.0 (τ = -0.544, p < 0.001, 95 % BCa CI: 0.700 to −0.354); and GM4.0 (τ = -0.274, p = 0.030, 95 % BCa CI: 0.479 to −0.040).Fig. 1Bivariate correlations between GINI score (x-axis) and School indicator grade score (y-axis) for (A) GM edition 1.0, (B) GM 2.0, (C) GM 3.0 and (D) GM 4.0.Fig. 1

Discussion

To address the key aims of this study, we found: (i) 10-year secular trends for the School indicator within and between countries indicate the School indicator gradings are fluid, sometimes changing upwards of 3-grade positions between editions (ii) countries use a variety of benchmarks to evaluate their School indicator grades, (iii) benchmark scoring patterns indicate Benchmark 5 is not well-utilised by any country, and (iv) School indicator benchmarking may benefit from omitting Benchmark 5, or placing it in an alternative PA indicator. Finally, School grades were not correlated with indices of economic status (GDP, GNI), but did share significant variance with income distribution inequality (GINI).

Notable School Indicator secular trends between editions

School indicator grades are dictated by available evidence, which varies considerably within and between countries. These are not only informed by raw data but also policy/curriculum changes, and other types of information, often available only at a local level, or grey literature sources, available in that country's native language. Some reasons countries cited for increasing their School indicator grade included: improvement in school-based PA strategies/policy,28 guaranteed access to PE facilities and equipment in public schools supported by law, and high proportions of teachers teaching PE who are specialists in the field,29 or positive government support for the promotion of PA among school-age children and youth.30,31 Conversely, decreases in School indicator rankings were cited when changes in national school policies occurred within a country.32 Grade improvements were often attributed when countries were able to collect more comprehensive data on the PA taking place in schools, and the policies and practices that supported it, allowing for a more exacting school environment assessments.33 More specifically, Ethiopia, South Korea, UAE and Uruguay recorded the greatest net positive changes between GM editions. Guernsey, the Channel Islands, and Wales changed their grades from INC in 2018 to B- in 2022. Slovenia and Portugal were the only countries who were able to rate the School indicator both before and after COVID-19, likely thanks to their robust child national fitness surveillance programmes which were able to gather data even during socially challenging times.34

School Indicator high performers

The highest overall school grades were reported by England (A-) in GM1.0; Slovenia in GM 2.0 (A); Denmark (A-), Finland (A-), Portugal (A), and Slovenia (A) in GM3.0; Hungary, Malaysia, Montenegro, Portugal, and Slovenia in GM4.0 (all A).22 These countries report high volumes of compulsory PE lesson durations (e.g. 225 min per week, Hungary), touting highly specialised expertise, where PE classes are taught by specialist graduate PE teachers.18,31,35,36 In Viet Nam, the country attributed their high School indicator grade to significant efforts put forth to promote PA in schools, and that their government had implemented additional policies to encourage additional PA (e.g. walking and cycling), and creating more public spaces for PA to take place.36

Hallmarks of the high performers

Each highly-graded country reported that most of their schools had ‘above average’ sport and school infrastructure, with enough indoor and outdoor facilities to comfortably implement their national PE curricula. Except for Malaysia and Viet Nam, most of these countries are part of the European Union. There, PE is compulsory in primary and lower secondary levels for most countries. 18,31,35,36 PE teachers tend to have a bachelor's degree at the primary level, and/or a master's degree at the lower secondary level. These countries use both formative and summative assessments 18,31,35,36; for example, Slovenia and England have created progression scales for assessing performance in PE at the national level.18,37 Portugal and Finland noted their countries had increased the minimum teaching time allocated to PE, and by extension, increasing net PA minutes for their children. Portugal, Slovenia and Hungary all systematically monitor physical fitness in their children and youth.16,38,39 These countries tend to promote a cross-curricular approach to PE and support the inclusion of PA in other subjects. Continuing professional development opportunities are available for both PE teachers and teachers of other subjects to enhance their training in physically active learning.40 Numerous countries report school-based curricula that specify a minimum number of PE hours for children in primary schools. However, in many other countries, there are no clear guidelines or recommendations regarding a required number of PE hours. To promote best practices and support PE worldwide, systematic, cross-cutting comparisons for government, PA, and school policies are essential, and have been recently updated with the GoPE initiative.41 It is important to reiterate that the WHO specifically highlights the promotion of PA in schools as a key public health strategy,2 noting that policy and environmental factors are important correlates of PA.42 These factors also help explain why the Schools Indicator remains one of the most consistently graded indicators across all editions of the Global Matrix.

Most common benchmarks used for assessment

The most common factor for assigning Schools indicator grades was the percentage of schools in which the majority (≥80 %) of students are offered the required amount of PE or are taught by a PE specialist. When countries are initially unable to assign a grade, it is difficult to determine whether it was because of significant policy changes, or curriculum changes within the country, or for some other reason. Furthermore, some countries sometimes mentioned they reported grade changes between editions due to alterations in their benchmark assessments, or lack of reliable new data available between sampling periods, or specific policy changes at local or national levels. For example, when reviewing the audited data from France, their country cited a reform of their national PE curriculum which mandated added 3 h of PE per week, automatically improving their grade for that epoch. Thus, it is crucial for countries to clearly specify which school benchmark criteria were used to assess their School indicator grades, and which were responsible for grade changes year-on-year, to better ensure accurate and comparable data between countries. Although the need to harmonize the grading system has been noted previously,19 the present study confirms that, to date, it has not been applied consistently across countries.

Recommendations to improve School Indicator benchmarking and study considerations

Countries generally used multiple benchmarks in their assessment School indicator except for Benchmark 5 (i.e., “percent of parents who report their children and youth have access to PA opportunities at school in addition to PE classes”) which is almost never used across all GM editions. This benchmark pertains to parental views on the school environment for PA, information which tends to not be collected in national surveys/monitoring. We therefore suggest removing Benchmark 5 from subsequent editions of GM or moving it to a more useful indicator theme (e.g. the Family and Peers indicator). Across the board, it is apparent that countries should work to improve their own national database on the ‘state of play’ regarding the quality of PE available in the school settings of their region. It is also important to acknowledge that some observed grade improvements likely reflect the availability of new or alternative data sources rather than genuine changes in school policies or practices, which introduces a potential bias, and underscores the need for more consistent and comprehensive surveillance across countries. Risk of bias assessment for each country's source material was not within the scope of this study but may constitute a future avenue of investigation. Moreover, we recognize that Global Matrix participation historically has tended to skew toward high-income countries (HICs), which might have influenced pooled estimates of total School indicator results. To mitigate against this possible bias, we attempted to correlate country GDP or GNI to their grade scores; however, there were no significant correlations found, indicating that the findings of this study did not depend solely on a country's wealth status, but on the income distribution within a given jurisdiction. The authors would like to underscore the need to expand participation from low- and middle-income countries in future editions. Finally, it is notable that countries which report more income distribution equity also report higher School indicator grades. Thus, while a country's total income is not directly associated with its final School indicator grades, the distribution of that wealth does influence the indicator—and, by extension, the potential of the school system to support physical activity and public health within the population.

Conclusion

For over a decade, the School indicator has been widely and consistently utilised across diverse geographical regions. Schools represent a crucial learning environment for children and an opportune place where PA interventions may influence child public health. Income distribution inequality is related to a country's School indicator score, an important consideration when generalising and contextualising results of past or future GM editions.
