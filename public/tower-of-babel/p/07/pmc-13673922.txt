Prevalence and predictors of percent body fat-defined obesity among federal contingency force commandos in India: implications for military obesity standards
Authors: Devika Bhat, Rakshit Shah, Mihir Rupani, Nitishkumar Tank, Ankit Sheth, Ankit Viramgami, Sanjay Kotadiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is increasingly affecting military personnel, yet its accurate assessment remains challenging because body mass index (BMI) does not distinguish between fat and lean mass; percent body fat (PBF) offers a more physiologically meaningful alternative. However, data on PBF-defined obesity prevalence and its predictors among elite Indian military personnel, such as Indian federal contingency force (IFCF) commandos, remain scarce.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among 116 IFCF personnel stationed in Gujarat, western India. PBF was measured using direct segmental multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 770) and served as the reference standard, with PBF-defined obesity considered as PBF ≥ 25%. BMI-based obesity was classified using both Asian (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) and WHO (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) criteria, with diagnostic accuracy of BMI evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Data on demographics, service-related factors, physical activity (Global Physical Activity Questionnaire), and eating behavior (Adult Eating Behavior Questionnaire) were additionally collected, and predictors of obesity identified using multivariable Firth-penalized logistic regression.ResultsPBF-defined obesity prevalence was 16% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 10–23%). The Asian BMI criterion overestimated obesity at 28% (95% CI: 20–36%), while the WHO criterion identified no obese individuals. BMI demonstrated good discriminative ability (AUC, 0.85; 95% CI: 0.75–0.95). The Asian BMI criterion showed sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 82%. The empirically derived optimal cut-off (25.4 kg/m²), closely approximated the Asian threshold. Each additional year of age increased obesity odds by 15% (95% CI: 1.03–1.31), those recruited from central armed police forces had 75% lower odds compared to army (95% CI: 0.06–0.83), and every additional 1000 MET-minutes/week of physical activity reduced obesity odds by 31% (95% CI: 0.45–0.98).ConclusionApproximately one in six IFCF commandos were obese by PBF-based criterion. The WHO BMI criterion substantially underestimated obesity, while the Asian BMI criterion closely aligned with the PBF-derived cut-off. These findings support the Asian BMI criterion for obesity screening, while supplementary PBF assessment may be considered where feasible. Obesity prevention efforts should prioritize individuals at greater risk, such as older personnel and those in service roles involving relatively lower physical activity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28667-5.Keywords: Body composition, Body mass index, Exercise, Feeding behavior, India, Military personnel, Obesity, Sensitivity and specificity

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a critical global public health challenge in recent decades, primarily driven by widespread lifestyle shifts, such as changes in food supply and consumption patterns, as well as evolving transportation modes [1, 2]. The Global Burden of Disease 2021 study reported that the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity more than doubled between 1990 and 2021, with an estimated 2.11 billion adults worldwide having a high body mass index (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) in 2021 [3]. Furthermore, approximately 3.7 million global deaths (5.4% of total global mortality) and 128 million disability-adjusted life years (4.5% of global burden) in 2021 were attributed to high BMI [3, 4]. Contrary to common perceptions about military fitness, armed forces worldwide are being increasingly impacted by obesity. Obesity prevalence among active-duty military personnel ranges from 12% to 18% across several European armed forces, with even higher rates reported in some Asian countries, for instance, around 29% in Saudi Arabia and as high as 44.2% in Thailand [5–8]. In the United States (U.S.), obesity among active-duty service members rose from 5% in 1995 to 12.7% in 2008, reaching 21.6% by 2022 [9, 10]. Considering the Indian context, reported obesity prevalence among military personnel varies markedly depending on the BMI criterion applied, ranging from near-zero estimates using the WHO BMI criterion to 29.8% using the Asian BMI criterion [11, 12].

Obesity within the military carries serious implications. Operationally, it affects physical performance and endurance, thereby compromising combat readiness [13]. At an individual level, obese personnel face greater risk of musculoskeletal injuries, increasing the likelihood of disability-related discharges from service [14]. In the long term, obesity is linked to chronic diseases such as diabetes, cardiovascular conditions, chronic kidney disease, certain cancers, and musculoskeletal disorders [15]. It also affects mental well-being, with higher rates of serious psychological distress, depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, reported among obese personnel [16]. Collectively, these conditions impose substantial long-term medical and financial burdens on military systems worldwide. Accurate assessment and surveillance of obesity are thus of utmost importance in military settings.

Obesity in military populations is still predominantly assessed using BMI-based classification systems worldwide, including in India [11, 17–19]. BMI is popular due to its affordability, simplicity, and ease of application, but it does not differentiate between fat and lean mass [18, 20]. This limitation often results in obesity misclassification, especially among physically active populations such as the military. While some studies, such as those from China, suggest that BMI may overestimate obesity among military personnel, contrasting evidence has been reported among U.S. military populations indicating that BMI often underestimates obesity compared to percent body fat (PBF) measures [13, 21]. These discrepancies underscore the population-specific variability of BMI in classifying obesity across diverse military settings. PBF, a direct measure of body composition, appears more physiologically meaningful, and has demonstrated stronger correlations with metabolic health outcomes and fitness performance than BMI [22–24]. Techniques such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) are commonly used to measure PBF [25]. While DXA is considered the clinical gold standard, its utility is constrained by high cost, immobility and radiation exposure [25]. In contrast, BIA offers a portable, radiation-free and cost-effective alternative [25]. Notably, newer multi-frequency BIA devices have demonstrated strong correlations with DXA for PBF measurements (r = 0.90, p < 0.001), and appear suitable for large-scale field use, especially in resource-constrained military settings [26].

Despite these advances, most existing Indian military studies continue to rely solely on BMI-based cut-offs, with PBF-based assessments remaining the exception [11, 12, 27]. In addition, data from elite specialized units such as the Indian federal contingency force, comprising commandos trained in unconventional warfare and counterterrorism, are conspicuously absent from the published literature [28]. Their stringent recruitment standards and sustained high-intensity operational training likely produce a body composition environment distinct from that of the broader Indian military and civilian population, making independent characterization necessary. Furthermore, physical activity and eating-related behaviors are recognized as important determinants of percent body fat, yet their concurrent evaluation alongside direct body composition measurement remains largely unexamined in this population [29, 30]. To address these gaps, we conducted this cross-sectional study among an accessible group of Indian federal contingency force personnel to estimate obesity prevalence using PBF-based criterion, evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of BMI criteria against the PBF standard, and explore key demographic, service-related and lifestyle predictors of PBF-defined obesity.

Methods

Study design, setting and duration

We conducted this cross-sectional study among personnel of the Indian federal contingency force, an elite commando unit stationed at one of their unit locations in Gujarat, a state in western India. The federal contingency force commandos in India, are specialized military units, trained in unconventional warfare such as counterterrorism operations, to address the nation’s internal security concerns [28]. Volunteers from the Indian army and central armed police forces (CAPF) undergo a rigorous selection process, and only a small proportion of candidates with exceptional levels of physical fitness ultimately qualify for deputation to the federal contingency force [28]. This study was conducted after obtaining written permission from the supervisory Directorate of the selected unit, with data collection carried out on-site over a two-month period in March and April 2024.

Study population

Active-duty personnel at the selected unit location, who were willing to participate by providing written informed consent and were present at the site during the data collection period (i.e., not deployed elsewhere on operational duties), were considered eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were set mainly to minimize inaccuracies in body composition analysis and comprised individuals with visible swelling/lump on the body, or extensive skin lesions [31].

Sample size and participant recruitment

The required sample size was calculated using EpiInfo software version 7.2.6.0 [32], based on the reported obesity prevalence of 18%, defined by the PBF ≥ 25% criterion, from a prior study among 121 male Indian naval personnel [27]. Applying a 95% confidence level with 5% absolute precision, the calculated sample size was 227 participants. However, as the total personnel strength of the selected unit was only 184, we adopted a census-based sampling approach, attempting to recruit all eligible personnel stationed at the unit during the study period. Initially, a single, 30-minute group counseling session was organized to thoroughly brief personnel regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, and the voluntary nature of participation. Subsequently, participants were screened for eligibility. Eligible participants were scheduled for assessments, with dates allotted based on their convenience. Participants were instructed to maintain adequate hydration on the day prior to the assessment, abstain from caffeine intake (tea/coffee) for at least 12 h (i.e., after 8 pm the previous evening), and avoid alcohol consumption for 24 h (i.e., after 8 am the previous morning) [33]. On the day of testing, participants arrived in a fasting state (without breakfast), with water consumption permitted up to 1 h before the assessment [33]. All the assessments were done in the morning hours (i.e., before 12 pm) to avoid diurnal variations in body composition analysis.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed through the digital KoboToolbox software [34]. The questionnaire included an initial section, capturing demographic information, service-related details and behavioral characteristics, followed by specific sections incorporating standard questionnaire tools for assessment of eating behavior patterns and physical activity levels, and the last section capturing anthropometric and body composition measurements.

Eating behavior pattern

We utilized the Adult Eating Behavior Questionnaire (AEBQ), a validated 35-item tool for assessing eight appetitive traits, including four ‘food approach’ traits (Enjoyment of Food, Emotional Over-Eating, Food Responsiveness, Hunger) and four ‘food avoidant’ traits (Emotional Under-Eating, Satiety Responsiveness, Slowness in Eating, Food Fussiness) among the study participants [35]. On a five-point Likert scale, participants rated 31 positively worded items (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), and four negatively worded items (reverse scored; 1 = strongly agree, 5 = strongly disagree). AEBQ sub-scale scores for each appetitive trait were calculated by taking the mean of the responses to the items corresponding to that particular trait. Higher sub-scale scores indicated stronger tendencies toward the associated appetitive behavior [35].

Physical activity level

The Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) was used to evaluate physical activity levels across three domains: work-related, transportation, and recreational activities [36, 37]. As per World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, vigorous-intensity activities (e.g., running drills, heavy lifting, gym workouts) were assigned a metabolic equivalent of task (MET) value of 8, while both moderate-intensity activities (e.g., weapon cleaning, parade, yoga) and active transport (walking/bicycling) were assigned a MET value of 4. MET refers to the ratio of an individual’s working metabolic rate to the resting metabolic rate, with 1 MET defined as the energy expended at rest, being approximately 1 kcal/kg/hour [36]. Total weekly physical activity for each participant was calculated by summing the MET-minutes/week for each of the three domains.

Anthropometry and body composition assessment

Anthropometric measurements were performed in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the WHO STEPwise approach to chronic disease risk factor surveillance (STEPS) manual [36]. To ensure measurement uniformity, all assessments were conducted by a single trained technician. Height was measured using a portable stadiometer (Seca 213, Seca GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), while waist and hip circumferences were measured using a flexible, non-stretchable tape, with all readings recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Body weight and PBF measurements were obtained using a direct segmental multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (DSMF-BIA) (InBody 770, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea) [33]. The device works on the principle of determining impedance and reactance across five body segments (right arm, left arm, trunk, right leg, left leg), by transmitting imperceptible currents at six frequencies (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, and 1000 kHz) through eight tactile electrodes (two per footplate and two per handgrip) [33]. Participants initially stood upright and barefoot on the footplates to measure their body weight, then grasped the hand electrodes with arms extended away from the trunk for about 60 s to complete the PBF assessment. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²).

Obesity classification criteria

PBF-based criterion

This served as the reference standard for determining the true prevalence of obesity and evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of BMI in identifying PBF-defined obesity. Study participants (all males) with PBF ≥ 25% were classified as obese, in accordance with the Endocrine Society of India (ESI) guidelines [38].

BMI-based criteria

Two BMI-based obesity thresholds were applied and compared against the PBF reference standard. According to the Asian BMI criterion recommended for the Indian population by the ESI and the Indian Consensus Group for obesity diagnosis, participants with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² were classified as obese [38–40]. According to the WHO BMI criterion, participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² were classified as obese [39]. The WHO criterion was additionally applied, as it remains the most widely used threshold in existing Indian military research studies, enabling contextual comparison with the published literature.

Statistical analysis

Data collected using the digital software were exported into Excel, cleaned, and checked for completeness. The final dataset had no missing values for the variables included in the analyses. Subsequently, the data were coded and analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 and R software version 4.4.3. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, with categorical variables presented as counts (percentages) and continuous variables expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To evaluate BMI’s ability to identify PBF-defined obesity (PBF ≥ 25%), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as a summary measure of overall discriminative ability. The optimal BMI cut-off for predicting PBF-defined obesity was identified by evaluating all possible combinations of sensitivity and specificity across the ROC curve, and selecting the threshold that maximized Youden’s J statistic, defined as sensitivity + specificity − 1 [41]. Diagnostic accuracy of the Asian BMI criterion (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) and the obtained optimal BMI cut-off was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR⁺, LR⁻), and the Cohen’s κ statistic, each reported with their 95% confidence intervals (CI). Differences between obese and non-obese groups (defined by PBF-based criterion), in terms of the eight appetitive traits (assessed via the AEBQ sub-scale scores) and physical activity levels, were evaluated using the Student’s t-test. For determining the predictors of PBF-defined obesity, Firth-penalized logistic regression was used in order to address the low events-per-variable ratio and potential quasi-separation issues with our small sample size [42]. This method improves estimation accuracy and provides finite, stable coefficient estimates by incorporating a bias-reduction penalty term into the likelihood function, making it more robust than standard maximum likelihood estimation in small-sample settings [42]. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in univariable analyses were selected as candidates for the multivariable model, in line with standard variable selection practice [43]. Multi-collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF values > 4 indicating collinearity. Multivariable analysis identified independent predictors of obesity, reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with their 95% CI. Statistical significance in the multivariable model was determined at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. Model performance was internally validated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples to yield optimism-corrected estimates of discrimination and calibration, accounting for potential overfitting in our small-sample setting [44]. Lastly, to mitigate the risk of Type I errors arising from multiple comparisons, a supplementary analysis was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to identify predictors in the multivariable model that remained statistically significant after controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) at 10% [45].

Results

A total of 116 Indian federal contingency force personnel were successfully enrolled in this study. The remaining individuals (n = 68) were unavailable due to temporary deployment on operational duties and therefore could not be included. None of the available personnel declined participation, and none were excluded based on the pre-defined exclusion criteria. This corresponded to a coverage of 63% of the unit’s total personnel strength and a response rate of 100% among personnel screened for eligibility. Prevalence of obesity

Using the reference PBF-based criterion (PBF ≥ 25%), the actual prevalence of obesity in our study was 16% (95% CI: 10–23%) (Fig. 2). The Asian BMI criterion (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) overestimated obesity prevalence at 28% (95% CI: 20–36%), whereas the WHO BMI criterion (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) substantially underestimated it, classifying none of the participants as obese (95% CI: 0–3%).

Agreement between PBF- and BMI-based criteria

As no participants were classified as obese using the WHO BMI criterion, measures of agreement could not be meaningfully estimated for this definition; therefore, agreement analyses were restricted to the Asian BMI criterion. Using this criterion, 32 participants were identified as obese (Table 2). Among these, only 14 were truly obese (true positives) as per the PBF-based criterion, while 18 were falsely classified as obese (false positives). Further, of the 84 participants classified as non-obese by the Asian BMI criterion, 80 were correctly identified (true negatives), while 4 were actually obese (false negatives).

The ROC curve analysis demonstrated that BMI had good discriminative ability in detecting PBF-defined obesity, with an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.75–0.95) (Fig. 3). The optimal BMI cut-off determined using Youden’s index (J = 0.61) was 25.4 kg/m², which was only marginally higher than the Asian BMI cut-off (25 kg/m²). Compared with the PBF standard, the Asian BMI criterion demonstrated a sensitivity of 78% (95% CI: 52–95%) and a specificity of 82% (95% CI: 73–89%) (Table 3). The positive predictive value was modest at 44% (95% CI: 26–62%). In contrast, the negative predictive value was high at 95% (95% CI: 88–99%). Using the optimal BMI cut-off modestly improved specificity (89%; 95% CI: 81–94%) but reduced sensitivity (72%; 95% CI: 49–89%). Predictors of obesity status

Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the univariable regression and without evidence of multi-collinearity (see Table S1 in Additional File) were subsequently entered into multivariable analysis as candidate predictors (Table 5). These variables included age; service-related factors such as current rank, and basic service; appetitive traits such as food responsiveness, food fussiness, and slowness in eating; and weekly physical activity levels. In the adjusted model, age, basic service, and physical activity remained significantly associated with PBF-defined obesity after controlling for all other included variables. Each one-year increase in age was associated with 15% higher odds of obesity (adjusted OR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.03–1.31; p = 0.014). Personnel recruited to the current service from the CAPF had 75% lower odds of obesity (adjusted OR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.06–0.83; p = 0.023) compared to those from the army. Weekly physical activity levels were inversely associated, with every 1000 MET-minutes/week increase reducing the odds of obesity by 31% (adjusted OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.45–0.98; p = 0.034). Model robustness was assessed using internal validation with 1,000 bootstrap resamples (see Tables S2 and S3 in Additional File). The mean optimism was 0.07, yielding an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.67–0.86), compared with an apparent AUC of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.74–0.93). This indicated acceptable model discrimination after accounting for overfitting. Calibration was adequate (intercept: 0.03; slope: 1.16), and bootstrap estimates of regression coefficients were consistent with the original model. Further, all the three predictors retained their statistical significance after controlling for multiple testing at a 10% false discovery rate (FDR) (see Table S4 in Additional File).

Discussion

Summary of main findings

In this cross-sectional study among Indian federal contingency force commandos, approximately one in six personnel (16%) were classified as obese using the PBF-based criterion (PBF ≥ 25%) as the reference standard. In comparison, the Asian BMI criterion (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) overestimated obesity prevalence at 28%, whereas the conventional WHO criterion (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) identified no participants as obese. BMI demonstrated good discriminative ability for detecting PBF-defined obesity (AUC = 0.85), with the Asian BMI criterion showing a sensitivity of 78% and specificity of 82%. Older age and recruitment to the current service from the army (vs. CAPF) were associated with higher obesity odds, whereas greater physical activity was protective.

Obesity prevalence – a comparison with existing literature

Observed obesity prevalence varied considerably depending on the classification criterion applied, underscoring the importance of specifying obesity definitions when comparing military studies. Using PBF ≥ 25% criterion, the 16% obesity prevalence observed in our study is comparable to the 18% prevalence reported in a study among Indian naval personnel, although PBF in that study was estimated using skinfold measurements rather than BIA [27]. Internationally, similar prevalence has been reported among Polish military aviation personnel (15.5%) and a Brazilian firefighter-military cohort (16.8%) [46, 47]. In contrast, substantially higher estimates have been reported among U.S. military personnel (30.1%) and Czech artillery soldiers (41.2%), likely reflecting actual differences in fitness practices and occupational demands across settings [13, 48]. Few studies from the Indian general population have assessed obesity using PBF-based criteria, limiting direct civilian comparison. A study from Pune employing multistage stratified sampling reported a PBF-defined obesity prevalence of 57.4%, while a convenience sampled cohort, recruited during routine health checkups from Delhi reported 84.7% prevalence [49, 50]. Both estimates are considerably higher than that observed in our study, a pattern consistent with the physically demanding occupational environment of the federal contingency force.

None of the participants in our study met the WHO obesity criterion (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²), consistent with previous Indian military studies reporting very low prevalence estimates ranging from 0 to 3.3% [11, 12, 19, 27]. In contrast, substantially higher WHO BMI-based obesity prevalence has been reported among military personnel from the United Kingdom (13.5%), France (12%), Germany (18%), the U.S. (21.4%), and Saudi Arabia (29%) [5–7, 13, 51]. The difference could somewhat be explained by the recognized tendency of the WHO BMI threshold to underestimate adiposity in South Asian populations, where excess body fat and metabolic risk can accumulate at lower BMI values [40]. Accordingly, the near-zero prevalence observed using the WHO threshold is unlikely to accurately reflect the true burden of excess adiposity in our study setting. Notably, the NFHS-5 survey reported obesity prevalence of 4.2% among adult Indian men using the WHO BMI criterion, which is substantially higher than that observed in our cohort [52].

Applying the Asian BMI criterion (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²), obesity prevalence in our cohort was 28%, comparable to estimates reported among Indian armed forces personnel by Dutt et al. (29.8%) and among Indian Air Force personnel by Varte et al. (28.8%) [12, 53]. Similar Asian BMI-based prevalence estimates have also been reported among military personnel in Japan (28%), whereas higher estimates have been observed in South Korea (32.9%) and Thailand (44.2%) [8, 54, 55]. In the Indian general population, the NFHS-5 and ICMR-INDIAB surveys reported Asian BMI-defined obesity prevalence of 22.9% and 25.4%, respectively, among adult males [52, 56]. The fact that the Asian BMI-based estimate in our physically active military cohort was higher than both national civilian figures may seem counterintuitive. However, this finding likely reflects differences in body composition between Indian military and civilian populations. In our study, the Asian BMI criterion overestimated obesity relative to the PBF criterion, whereas Indian community studies have generally reported the opposite pattern, with PBF-defined prevalence exceeding Asian BMI based estimates [50]. This divergence suggests that participants in our study carried a proportionally greater fat-free mass relative to the general Indian population.

Diagnostic accuracy of BMI for detecting PBF-defined obesity

The diagnostic accuracy analysis indicated that BMI reasonably distinguished individuals with and without PBF defined obesity (AUC = 0.85). Using the Asian BMI criterion, sensitivity and specificity were 78% and 82%, respectively. Further, the NPV was high (95%), indicating that individuals classified as non-obese by BMI were unlikely to have excess adiposity by PBF criteria, supporting the utility of BMI as a practical first-line screening tool, particularly for ruling out obesity. By contrast, the PPV was modest (44%), indicating that many individuals classified as obese by Asian BMI criterion were not obese according to PBF measurement. This limitation may partly reflect the relatively low prevalence of PBF-defined obesity in our cohort (16%), which can reduce PPV despite acceptable sensitivity and specificity [57]. The moderate agreement between Asian BMI and PBF classifications (κ = 0.45) is consistent with findings reported among Brazilian military firefighters (κ = 0.43) and Czech military personnel (κ = 0.35) [58, 59].

The optimal BMI cut-off identified in our study was 25.4 kg/m², closely approximating the Asian BMI threshold of 25 kg/m², whereas the WHO threshold of 30 kg/m² failed to identify any cases of PBF-defined obesity. These findings support the Asian BMI criterion as a more appropriate screening threshold for this population. A similar optimal cut-off (24.4 kg/m²) was previously reported among Indian naval personnel, suggesting broadly consistent findings across Indian military cohorts [27].

Predictors of PBF-defined obesity

Our study’s finding that each additional year of age is associated with 15% higher odds of obesity (aOR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.03–1.31), aligns with evidence from both Indian and international military populations. Among Indian armed forces personnel, Dutt et al. reported a progressive increase in mean BMI across increasing age groups, while Bhalwar et al. found a 7% increase in the odds of metabolic syndrome per additional year of age [12, 60]. Among U.S. active-duty personnel, individuals aged ≥ 40 years had approximately three times higher obesity odds than those aged < 20 years [61]. The association might be attributed mainly to the transition from active operational roles to more sedentary, administrative positions as individuals gain seniority [61, 62]. Further, aging itself is a non-modifiable risk factor linked to physiological changes such as increase in fat mass, due to reductions in resting metabolic and fatty acid oxidation rates [63].

Personnel recruited from the CAPF had 75% lower odds of PBF-defined obesity compared to those from the army (aOR: 0.25; 95% CI: 0.06–0.83). Since direct data on inter-group differences in job responsibilities were not collected, a definitive explanation for this finding cannot be offered. However, one plausible interpretation is that the distribution of combat versus non-combat roles differed between army and CAPF recruits at the study site, with CAPF personnel potentially occupying roles involving higher habitual physical activity. Evidence from Indian military studies support the notion that role type influences obesity. Varma et al. reported lower obesity prevalence among personnel in Arms (combat) roles compared to Service (support) roles (13% vs. 18.7%), and Bhalwar et al. observed a similar pattern for metabolic syndrome (8.1% vs. 12.4%) [60, 64]. Comparable findings have also been reported among German armed forces personnel, where obesity odds were lower in combat units than in non-combat support units (aOR: 0.89) [5]. Systematic data on role distribution and training load across service backgrounds within federal contingency force are needed to adequately explore this association.

Weekly physical activity was inversely associated with PBF-defined obesity, with each additional 1,000 MET-minutes/week associated with 31% lower odds (aOR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.45–0.98). This is consistent with findings from Indian and international military studies. Bhalwar et al. reported 35% lower odds of metabolic syndrome among Indian army personnel expending greater than 2,800 kcal/week in physical exercise, and studies among French, Polish, Thai, and U.S. military personnel have similarly documented an inverse relationship between physical activity levels and the presence of overweight or obesity [8, 51, 60, 62, 65]. Regular physical activity is known to increase energy expenditure and prevent excess fat accumulation, enhance insulin sensitivity, and preserve lean muscle mass [66]. However, these findings should be interpreted with consideration of certain limitations of GPAQ in military settings. Military activities such as combat drills and tactical exercises may be difficult to classify accurately within the GPAQ framework, and self-reported tools can overestimate physical activity compared with objective measures [67]. Accordingly, the observed association should be interpreted as reflecting a general protective relationship between physical activity and obesity rather than a prescriptive activity threshold.

None of the appetitive traits measured using the AEBQ significantly predicted PBF-defined obesity in our study. This contrasts with previous research among Polish and U.S. military recruits that have found behavioral traits such as emotional eating, having a faster eating rate and ignoring of internal satiety cues, to be associated with higher BMI [30, 62, 68]. One possible explanation is that the AEBQ may have limited applicability in our study population. Federal contingency force personnel undergo rigorous training regimens that place special emphasis on mental resilience along with physical endurance [28]. This may reduce the expression or reporting of emotional and impulsive eating behaviors captured by the AEBQ. However, the possibility of limited statistical power should also be considered. Further validation of the AEBQ in military settings is warranted before its broader application in such populations.

Practical implications

The findings of this study support the use of the Asian BMI criterion (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) over the conventional WHO threshold for obesity screening in Indian federal contingency force. However, the modest agreement between BMI- and PBF-based classifications suggests that elevated BMI values in physically conditioned military personnel may not always reflect excess adiposity. Where operationally feasible, supplementary assessment using portable BIA-based methods may help improve obesity classification accuracy, reduce unnecessary placement of personnel in lower medical categories, and minimize the need for unwarranted metabolic investigations. Future studies should also explore whether combining BMI with simple anthropometric measures, such as waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio, improves agreement with PBF-based obesity assessment.

Obesity prevention efforts should prioritize individuals at greater risk, such as older personnel and those in service roles involving relatively lower physical activity. Understanding the specific occupational demands and physical activity patterns of army versus CAPF personnel within the federal contingency force may further help in designing targeted preventive strategies. Future research should also evaluate other determinants not assessed in this study, such as gender, ethnicity, family history of obesity, socio-economic status, education level, specific job role, dietary intake, occupational stress, and sleep behavior. Understanding the influence of these factors may inform more comprehensive obesity control measures.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first to estimate obesity prevalence, examine its predictors, and evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of BMI against PBF-based reference standards among Indian federal contingency force personnel. We implemented stringent standard operating procedures for the anthropometric measurements, as well as PBF assessments via DSMF-BIA. Adherence to the STROBE and STARD reporting standards further strengthens the methodological rigor, transparency, and reproducibility of the study findings.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. First, the relatively small sample size reduced statistical power, as reflected by wide confidence intervals for prevalence estimates, diagnostic performance measures, and odds ratios, thereby limiting generalizability to the broader federal contingency force population. Second, only male personnel could be included in this study, as there were no female personnel in the selected federal contingency force unit. Third, although PBF was used as the reference standard, it does not account for differences in body fat distribution, such as visceral versus subcutaneous adiposity, which may vary by age, sex and ethnicity and may be more strongly associated with cardiometabolic risk than total body fat [69]. Fourth, DSMF-BIA has demonstrated a modest systematic underestimation of PBF relative to DXA in military populations, which could have resulted in some misclassification of obesity status [26, 33]. Fifth, sedentary behavior, a recognized independent risk factor for obesity, was not assessed separately, as the GPAQ does not incorporate sedentary time into MET-minute calculations. Its assessment might have provided additional insights into behavioral determinants of obesity. Sixth, individual dietary intake was not assessed, as we assumed minimal variability due to the common mess facility. However, differences in food choices within the mess and external food consumption may have influenced obesity outcomes and could not be captured in this study. Lastly, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference between the identified predictors and obesity status. Despite these limitations, the study provides important initial evidence on obesity assessment and its correlates among Indian federal contingency force personnel.

Conclusion

We found that approximately one in six (16%) Indian federal contingency force personnel were obese according to the PBF-based criterion, indicating a meaningful burden of excess adiposity in this physically conditioned population. The conventional WHO BMI criterion substantially underestimated obesity in this population, whereas the Asian BMI criterion showed better agreement with PBF-defined obesity and closely approximated the empirically derived optimal BMI cut-off. These findings support the potential utility of the Asian BMI criterion over the conventional WHO threshold for obesity screening in Indian federal contingency force personnel and suggest that supplementary PBF assessment may improve obesity classification in physically conditioned populations. Older age and army service background (vs. CAPF) were associated with higher obesity odds, whereas higher physical activity levels were protective. Further large-scale, multi-center studies are warranted to validate these findings across diverse Indian military populations.
