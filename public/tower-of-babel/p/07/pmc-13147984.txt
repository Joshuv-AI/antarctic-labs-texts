GeothermalKits for modeling geothermal energy: A scenario-based and physical model-free simulation platform
Authors: Yanlong Kong, Xiaoming Tian, Yonghui Huang, Wanlong Cai, Miao Yu, Boyan Meng, Chaofan Chen, Changwei Liu, Zhongpeng Wang, Yulie Gong, Haibing Shao, Zhonghe Pang, Jiyang Wang
Published: 2026

Main text

The large-scale development of geothermal energy relies heavily on numerical simulation technologies. However, existing simulators often face challenges such as high modeling complexity, low computational efficiency, and ambiguously defined simulation scenarios. Here, we have developed a completely novel research approach: a physical model-free, scenario-based modeling framework. This approach, aiming to solve interdisciplinary problems in geothermal energy research and engineering, enables users to obtain engineering-ready outputs and actionable solutions directly, thereby reducing the need for extensive post-processing. In addition, physical model-free modules are designed to further improve operational simplicity, particularly in cases where geological heterogeneity and uncertainty exert limited influence on computational results. Based on this approach, we have developed the software platform “GeothermalKits,” which is available in two versions: a basic edition (https://xiaomingtian-1.github.io/) designed for enterprise users with an emphasis on operational simplicity and a professional edition (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17231957), enhanced for academic and research applications requiring professional knowledge and advanced modeling skills. Unlike conventional numerical simulators that primarily perform single forward simulations, GeothermalKits emphasizes usability, professionalism, and comprehensive end-to-end integration, contributing to the broad recognition and adoption of simulation across both industrial and academic geothermal communities.

Scenario-based modeling

From the perspective of geothermal engineering applications, most projects require accounting for uncertainties associated with various engineering parameters by performing uncertainty quantification analyses or parameter optimization. Both processes typically involve a large number of forward simulations, followed by quantitative evaluation or optimization of key parameters. These two types of analyses represent the most common modeling scenarios encountered in practical geothermal projects.

In conventional numerical modeling workflows, users are required to manually adjust model parameters and restart each new forward simulation, necessitating human intervention throughout uncertainty quantification and model optimization processes. This manual procedure results in low automation efficiency and significantly increases computational time. To address this limitation, GeothermalKits proposed a scenario-based modeling paradigm, which differs from conventional numerical simulation approaches by enabling automatic parameter variation between consecutive forward simulations. Users only need to specify the total number of sampling iterations or scenario cases, while the system autonomously updates parameter sets through either auto-sampling or file-based parameter loading, thereby eliminating repetitive manual operations and enhancing workflow automation.

In addition to scenarios for uncertainty quantification and optimization analyses, GeothermalKits also integrates a carbon-related scenario via a computational module for evaluating equivalent carbon emission reductions achieved through geothermal energy utilization. Currently, few existing simulators link geothermal performance simulations with the corresponding carbon savings related to fossil fuel combustion. However, such carbon accounting is becoming increasingly important as governments worldwide implement carbon tax policies and emission reduction frameworks. GeothermalKits provides a specialized and practical scenario for quantifying the carbon mitigation benefits of geothermal energy systems, offering a valuable tool for enterprises engaged in sustainability assessment and carbon management.

Physical model-free

Each forward simulation inherently involves the construction of a geological model, mesh generation, and initialization of petrophysical parameters. However, in many practical engineering applications, sufficient geological or production data are often unavailable during the early stages of project development to support the construction of a detailed geological model, even though the need for uncertainty quantification still exists. Another common situation arises when the required engineering accuracy is moderate, in which highly detailed geological models become unnecessary, and time-consuming simulations offer limited practical benefits.

To accommodate such cases, GeothermalKits introduces a simplified modeling strategy in specific modules by omitting the manual geological modeling steps, a design referred to as the physical model-free strategy. It is important to note that “physical model-free” in this context does not imply the use of a proxy model. Instead, the software incorporates predefined yet sufficiently accurate geological templates while providing user interfaces that expose only the most influential input parameters. This approach allows users to focus on parameters that exert the greatest impact on engineering outcomes while ensuring computational efficiency.

For instance, in a typical well spacing optimization problem, the geological structure can often be adequately represented by a homogeneous reservoir model, where well spacing is the key factor influencing thermal breakthrough, production rate, and well temperature. Accordingly, this module is designed as a physical model-free module with a built-in two-dimensional homogeneous geological model. Users can perform simulations by specifying only rock properties, initial conditions, well controls, and economic indicators, without needing to manually construct a geological model.

A similar design principle has also been applied to modules involving borehole heat exchanger (BHE) simulations, including shallow BHE, deep coaxial BHE, and deep U-type BHE configurations, thereby improving usability while maintaining modeling reliability.

Modules

The architecture of GeothermalKits is demonstrated in Figure 1F. The basic edition of GeothermalKits integrates a suite of commonly used geothermal simulation modules. All modules are developed based on the physical model-free design philosophy and cover a total of eight computational modules, namely, well spacing optimization, resource assessment, shallow BHEs, deep coaxial BHE, deep U-type BHE, static formation temperature prediction, carbon emission reduction calculation, and Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) geothermal power generation. Together, these modules comprehensively cover a wide range of geothermal applications—from hydrothermal systems to closed-loop BHE systems, as shown in Figure 1A, and from subsurface geothermal production to surface geothermal power generation and carbon reduction assessments. Notably, the well spacing optimization module is designed according to the scenario-based modeling concept.

To enable more in-depth studies of hydrothermal geothermal systems, the professional edition does not adopt the physical model-free approach, as geological heterogeneity and uncertainty play critical roles in determining the performance of geothermal resource development. Therefore, the professional edition incorporates functions for detailed geological modeling based on Delft Advanced Research Terra Simulator (DARTS) kernels, including local geological modeling (Figure 1P), Petrel model import (Figure 1N), discrete fracture network modeling (Figure 1O), and Gmsh mesh import. The governing equations for energy and mass conservation of the hydrothermal system are presented in Figures 1D and 1E, respectively.

Water properties are determined as functions of the thermodynamic state, as defined by the phase diagram of water (Figures 1B and 1C). Furthermore, following the scenario-based modeling concept, the professional edition includes additional modules such as dynamic resource assessment and static volumetric resource assessment, thereby enhancing its capability to support research-oriented and high-fidelity geothermal simulations.

Applications

Figures 1J–1M present the simulation results of the scenario-based modeling modules. Most of these scenarios involve multiple forward simulations, making them computationally intensive numerical processes. Figure 1J illustrates the results of the well spacing optimization model, using the Xiongxian geothermal field as an example. As the well spacing increases from 100 to 1,000 m, the water head drop and the temperature drop of the production well exhibit opposite trends—the water head drop becomes more pronounced, while the temperature drop becomes less significant. This is because larger well spacing slows down the thermal breakthrough process; however, the production well receives delayed recharge from the injection well, resulting in a noticeable decrease in water head. Considering the economic costs associated with both water head drop and temperature drop, the optimal well spacing is determined to be 400 m. Figures 1L and 1M show the results of dynamic2 and static geothermal resource assessments, respectively, both conducted using the scenario-based modeling module in the professional edition. The software then applies statistical methods to derive key indicators, such as mean values and confidence intervals. Throughout this process, users are not required to manually reset parameters for each scenario, as the software automatically executes all simulations and compiles the complete set of statistical results. In addition, a specialized scenario-based modeling module considers the context of global carbon neutrality goals. Based on the methodologies of China Certified Emission Reduction (CCER) and GB/T 11615-2012, this module evaluates the annual CO2 emission reductions achieved by substituting conventional coal-fired boiler heating with geothermal energy utilization, thereby providing a quantitative framework for assessing the carbon mitigation potential of geothermal systems.

The physical model-free modules are primarily designed to simplify the geological modeling process for BHE systems. Figure 1H presents a comparison between the simulation results from the shallow BHE module and the analytical solution reported by Bayer et al.3 The simulated temperature distribution along the observation line (Figure 1G) agrees well with the analytical results. In the central region of the target area, a temperature drawdown cone forms as a result of geothermal energy extraction. At specific borehole locations along the observation line, local temperature depressions are observed, reflecting the influence of BHE operation on the surrounding temperature field. The deep coaxial BHE module was validated against the analytical solution of Beier.4 Figure 1I shows the variation in temperature along the borehole depth after 2,880 h (120 days) of operation for both the annulus and the inner pipe. As depth increases, the overall wellbore temperature rises, but a distinct temperature difference is observed between the water temperature of the annulus and the inner pipe. The relative error between the simulation results and Beier’s analytical solution is 2.2%, confirming the accuracy and reliability of the module. For the deep U-type BHE module, the validation was performed using measured field data from a real engineering project reported by Cai et al.5 After 1,400 h (ca. 60 days) of operation, the average deviation between the simulated and measured outlet temperatures of the deep U-type BHE did not exceed 0.27°C (a relative error of 1.02%). The corresponding temperature variation curves are shown in Figure 1K, further demonstrating the module’s capability to reproduce realistic geothermal system behavior with high accuracy.
