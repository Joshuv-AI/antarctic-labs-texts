Defining Haplosufficiency in Autosomal Recessive Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy Using Molecular Markers in Disease Carriers
Authors: Alison Gaynor, Dove Enicks, Katherine Karam, Lesly Turcios-Hernandez, Marina Provenzano, Christopher R. Heier, Melissa A. Hale, Nicholas E. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autosomal recessive forms of limb-girdle muscular dystrophy are caused by biallelic loss of function variants. Haplosufficient carriers are hypothesized to have sufficient protein expression to maintain normal physical function. This study aims to determine relative protein expression in haplosufficient carriers of 3 subtypes of autosomal recessive LGMD: LGMDR1 (CAPN3, calpain-3), LGMDR9 (FKRP, FKRP), and LGMDR3 (SGCA, α-sarcoglycan). In addition, this study clarifies fukutin-related protein (FKRP) enzymatic functionality in LGMDR9 carriers. Individuals affected with LGMDR1, R9, and R3 and corresponding carriers provided a muscle biopsy following a physical examination. RNA-seq analysis was performed to determine global gene-expression patterns. Gene expression of CAPN3, FKRP, and SGCA were determined using RNA-seq normalized counts, and relative quantification of disease-associated proteins was determined by western blotting. Carriers of LGMDR1 were found to have approximately 25% total calpain-3 expression compared with noncarrier controls, while LGMDR9 carriers had nearly 50% functionality of FKRP. LGMDR3 carriers exhibited α-sarcoglycan and β-sarcoglycan protein levels comparable with controls. Carriers of several autosomal-recessive LGMDs have reduced expression of disease-related proteins compared with noncarrier controls with associated reduced protein functionality. Since these haplosufficient carriers have normal physical strength, this degree of protein expression may represent the threshold to restore haplosufficient status after gene replacement therapy.

Introduction

The limb-girdle muscular dystrophies (LGMDs) are a set of 32 different muscular dystrophies clinically characterized by progressive weakness of the proximal muscles.1 These rare genetic disorders affect approximately 1:14,500-1:23,000, although the carrier frequency is projected to be as high as of 1:150–1:211.2 Currently there are no approved LGMD gene therapies available to patients. Most LGMDs are due to loss of function of a protein in the dystrophin associated protein complex. These proteins provide structural support and/or appropriate linkage to either the sarcomere or the laminin-basement membrane of the skeletal muscle.3 While a small proportion of LGMD cases are autosomal dominant, the vast majority (∼90%) follow an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance.1 In this study, we examine 3 autosomal recessive LGMD subtypes: LGMDR1 (formerly 2A), LGMDR9 (formerly 2I), and LGMDR3 (formerly 2D).

The most common subtype of LGMD, LGMDR1, represents 30% of individuals living with LGMD worldwide.4 It is caused by loss-of-function, recessively inherited variants in the gene CAPN3, which encodes the muscle-specific enzyme calpain-3. Onset of LGMDR1 varies widely, occurring anywhere from childhood to about 40 years.5 Calpain-3 is a calcium-dependent protease that has a variety of cell processes, including restoration and modulation of the sarcomere.6 There are numerous current research efforts to develop a gene-based therapy for LGMDR1. This includes CRISPR-mediated correction and transplantation of patient satellite cells, as well as adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene replacement.7

LGMDR9 is caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the gene FKRP. FKRP encodes the glycosylation enzyme fukitin-related protein, or FKRP.8 Reduced functionality of FKRP can result in disrupted linkage of the sarcolemma to the extracellular matrix, leading to breakdown of the muscle tissue.9 There are currently 2 active LGMDR9 gene therapy clinical trials registered through the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), both of which are AAV-mediated gene replacement treatments.10,11

LGMDR3 is linked to biallelic pathogenic variants in the gene SGCA, which encodes the protein α-sarcoglycan (SGCA). α-sarcoglycan associates with 3 additional sarcoglycan proteins: β, γ, and δ, forming the sarcoglycan complex.12 The sarcoglycan complex provides linkage to both dystroglycan as well as dystrophin, further supporting anchorage to both the sarcomere and the basement membrane.13 Absence of SGCA protein deteriorates that linkage, leading to muscle wasting. At this time, there are 2 completed LGDMR3 clinical trials evaluating the same gene replacement AAV vector.14

Current AAV-based gene replacement allows for a gene transfer of a product up to 4.7 kb long.15 There are current preclinical/clinical trials occurring for 6 subtypes; however, more are eligible for development.16 Success for these therapies may be considered replacement of the gene with sufficient protein expression to achieve normal sarcolemmal function. However, it has been difficult to proxy sufficient protein expression in these clinical trials due to lack of clear functional endpoints in preclinical models. This may be assisted by quantifying protein expression in heterozygote LGMD carriers. LGMD carriers, as defined as individuals that have 1 pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in an LGMD gene associated with recessive inheritance, do not develop symptoms of LGMD and sustain unaffected skeletal muscle function.

Here, we used haplosufficent carriers of LGMD to define sufficient protein expression to maintain normal sarcolemmal function. These carriers were compared with matched LGMD individuals in 3 autosomal recessive LGMD subtypes. We hypothesize that these levels may be considered a therapeutic efficacy threshold whereby an AAV gene replacement therapy could restore the full functionality of the affected gene and subsequent muscle function. Furthermore, these data could support the notion of protein restoration levels as surrogate endpoints for accelerated approval of gene or protein replacement therapies.

Methods

Cohort Enrollment

Individuals diagnosed with LGMDR1 (study ID NCT03981289, NCT05618080), LGMDR3 (NCT03981289), or LGMDR9 (NCT04202627) were enrolled in ongoing natural history studies. Enrolled patients had biallelic pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in CAPN3, FKRP, or SGCA. All participants had a neurologic examination including MRC (Medical Research Council) scores that confirmed the presence of a limb girdle pattern of weakness (patients) or absence thereof (carriers). Muscle biopsies of the tibialis anterior from participants were collected using a 14-gauge argon supercore muscle biopsy of the tibialis anterior. Muscle biopsies and blood/saliva samples of haplosufficient LGMD carriers (n = 15) and adult controls (n = 17) were collected through an in-house biorepository study (CIMR Neuromuscular Research Biobank). Muscle tissue was immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

All studies were approved by WIRB Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board (IRB 20200147, 20224719, 20234245, 20204038). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study procedures or biospecimen collection.

Genotyping Carriers/LGMD Affected Individuals

LGMD affected and carrier gDNA was extracted from either whole blood (ACD tube) using phenol/chloroform phase separation followed by isopropanol precipitation, or saliva (Oragene) using the GenoTek prepIT L2P kit. gDNA was cleaned of impurities using column-based elution (New England Biolabs [NEB] Monarch DNA cleanup kit). A custom Illumina Ampliseq panel targeting known pathogenic variants in CAPN3, FKRP, and SGCA was used to determine genotype/haplotype of affected/carrier individuals. Libraries were prepped with the AmpliSeq Library PLUS for Illumina kit with 10 ng of high-quality genomic DNA input. Two primer pools were used for this panel. Prepped libraries were sequenced on an Illumina iSeq, and Sanger sequencing was performed to confirm pathogenic variants.

RNA Extraction/RNA-seq

RNA was extracted from muscle biopsies using a TRIzol phase separation followed by ethanol precipitation and column-based DNAse treatment/elution (Zymo Labs), as previously described.17

Total RNA-sequencing of muscle RNA (100–150 ng) was completed with NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB), as previously described.17 Paired-end, stranded sequencing was completed with the NextSeq500 or NextSeq 2000 (Illumina) with a target of 150 million reads per sample. Resulting fastq files were aligned to GRCh38 with STAR 2.7.11 with 2-pass alignment.18 RNA-seq data sets for several samples have been deposited in National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under bioproject PRJNA1079722 and PRJNA1079724 (eTable 1). Principal component analysis (PCA), differential gene expression comparing LGMD carriers to affected individuals (normalized to unaffected controls), and quantification of normalized counts was performed using DESeq2.19 Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with log2FoldChange >1 and p-adjusted value <0.05. Allele-specific expression (ASE) for the mutant haplotypes corresponding to LGMDR9 and LGMDR3 carriers were quantified with genome analysis toolkit (GATK) ASEReadCounter.20 Because the majority of LGMDR1 carriers harbored frameshift mutations, haplotype phasing was required to determine ASE. We used the package phASER for both haplotype phasing and quantification of ASE for LGMDR1 carriers.21

Western Blotting

Approximately 2 mg of muscle tissue was homogenized using 3-mm zirconium beads in a BeadBug Homogenizer (Thomas Scientific). Protein was lysed in either RIPA buffer (ThermoFisher) supplemented with cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Millipore Sigma) (LGMDR1, LGMDR3) or tris-buffered saline (pH 7.6) complete with 1% Triton-X 100 and HALT protease inhibitor cocktail (ThermoFisher) (LGMDR9). After lysis, samples were spun at 14,000 x g for 15 minutes. The resulting supernatant was removed, and protein concentration was quantified using the Pierce bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) protein assay.

Protein samples were supplemented with Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad), 10% 2-mercaptoethanol and were denatured at 95°C for 3–5 minutes. For LGMDR1 and LGMDR3 analyses, 8 mg of protein was loaded. For LGMDR9, between 40-80 mg of protein was loaded into gel. Samples were loaded into 4–20% TGX Stain-Free gels (Bio-Rad) and run at 100 V for approximately 1.5 hours. Gels were transferred to LF-PVDF membranes using the TransBlot Turbo System and blocked using EveryBlot buffer (Bio-Rad) for 1 hour. Stain-free protein blot images were procured using the ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad) before antibody addition. Primary antibodies diluted in EveryBlot buffer were added for overnight incubation. The following antibodies and dilutions were used: Calpain-3 (1:500, Proteintech 10415-1-AP), alpha-sarcoglycan (1:1,000, Thermo Scientific MA5-32672), beta-sarcoglycan (1:1,000, Novus Biologicals H00006443-M02), FKRP (1:500, Thermo Scientific PA5-63857), dystroglycan (Novus Biologicals AF6868), IIH6 (Millipore Sigma 05–593), laminin (Thermo Scientific, PA1-16730), and vinculin (1:1,000, Thermo Scientific, 14-9777-82). The following day, blots were washed in tris-buffered saline supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 (tris-buffered saline with tween-20 [TBST]). Secondary antibodies including StarBright fluorescent (Bio-Rad) and Alexa Fluor fluorescent (Thermo Scientific) were diluted in EveryBlot buffer in a ratio of 1:1,000-2,500. Blots were submerged in secondary antibody solution and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature before imaging using the ChemiDoc MP.

Relative protein quantification from blot was determined using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad). Expression levels of the protein of interest were normalized to stain-free protein expression before comparison to healthy controls. Stain-free protein images were cropped before normalization to remove the 12 kDa hemoglobin beta band observed on immunoblots caused from carry over of blood in protein extraction (eFigure 2).

Three immunoblot technical replicates were performed for LGMDR1 samples and 4 for LGMDR3 samples. Two separate protein extractions (stemming from the same muscle biopsy) were performed for both subtypes in an effort to validate reproducibility. Note we did not perform technical replicates for LGMDR9 analysis due to limited sample tissue.

Laminin-Overlay Assay

This assay was completed with LGMDR9 affected and carrier samples in addition to healthy controls. 40 mg of protein per sample were run in a Western blot as detailed above. The resulting blot was incubated in laminin-binding buffer (10 mM trienthanolamine, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 5% non–fat milk, pH = 7.6) for 1 hour. The blot was then incubated overnight in laminin-binding buffer complemented with 7.5 nM Engelbreth-Holm-Swarm laminin (Millipore Sigma) and 3% bovine serum-albumin.22 The following day, the blot was washed in TBST followed by an additional overnight incubation in laminin primary antibody. The blot was washed again in TBST and incubated for 1 hour in secondary antibody at room temperature before imaging.

Statistical Analysis

Graphpad Prism version 10 was used to perform necessary statistical analysis. For LGMDR1 and R3 analysis, normalized protein expression for each sample was averaged across technical replicates, then graphed under the corresponding classification (control, carrier, affected). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to determine statistical significance of various groups.

Data Availability

RNA-seq FASTQ files have been deposited in Sequence Read Archive (NHLBI) as listed in eTable 1.

Total Calpain-3 Is Significantly Reduced in Carriers of LGMDR1

CAPN3 protein in its full-length form is inactive, but undergoes autolysis to reveal an active proteolytic domain composed of 2 noncovalently-bound fragments.25 These autolysis products further devolve into 3 fragments sized at 60, 58, and 55 kDa.26,27 Total calpain-3 is determined by adding expression of the full-length (94 kDa) band to the 55–60 kDa autolysis products.

We performed 3 immunoblot technical replicates detecting calpain-3. One replicate did not include 2 carrier samples (lack of timely availability). Average CAPN3 protein expression in LGMDR1 carriers across replicates was approximately 25% relative to controls (range 16.5%–35%) (Figure 1E). This is significantly reduced from the 50% expected protein expression given the haplotypes of the heterozygous carriers. Despite the severe reduction, carrier protein expression was significantly greater than LGMDR1 affected samples. Affected samples reported an average of 7.2% total calpain-3 expression relative to controls (range 3–14.2%). These minimal levels of total calpain-3 expression are in alignment with previous studies analyzing samples from affected patients homozygous for null (frameshift, nonsense) variants.28

There was a persistent residual band detected in LGMDR1 affected samples that measured ∼50 kDa (Figure 1D). ∼50 kDa bands have been observed with similar polyclonal antibodies, and have been denoted as nonspecific detection.6,29 Previous studies have demonstrated zero calpain-3 detection in subjects with biallelic null variants, which is the case for 4/5 of the affected subjects in this study.29 It is therefore plausible this band does not represent a calpain-3 product, but rather nonspecific cross-reactivity with other calpains. For instance, the N-terminal region of calpain-2, which is highly homologous with calpain-3, has demonstrated the ability to “rescue” calpain-3 functionality through intermolecular complementation with the calpain-3 58 kDa autolytic product. The prominent band could represent the remaining calpain-2 C-terminal fragment, which is ∼53 kDa.30

Regardless of the affected samples, this severe reduction in total calpain-3 amongst LGMDR1 carriers implies that haplosufficiency can be maintained with only 25% calpain-3 expression.

LGMDR9 global gene expression displays the observed, albeit modest, gradient pattern of differential gene expression as visualized by heatmap, while gene expression PCA analysis reveals LGMDR9 carriers cluster closely with controls (Figure 2B, eFigure 1B). LGMDR9 Carrier Samples Have Reduced Glycosylated α-Dystroglycan

Given the variable distribution of FKRP protein levels between LGMDR9 carriers and affected participants, we next examined functionality of FKRP. FKRP directly glycosylates the transmembrane protein α-dystroglycan, which functions to provide linkage of the sarcolemma to the laminin epitopes of the basement membrane. The glycan that links α-dystroglycan to the basement membrane (extracellular matrix) is referred to as matriglycan.31

To determine extent of sarcolemma maintenance in carriers of LGMDR9, we used a core-dystroglycan antibody (AF6868) to quantify α-dystroglycan levels. The 150 kDa α-DG band represents the higher molecular weight protein bound to matriglycan, while the lower 100 kDa band is unbound. Carriers averaged 54% expression of 150 kDa α-DG compared with controls, while affected individuals had close to 0% expression (Figure 3B). Although insignificant due to variability in expression among individual carriers, this trends toward ∼50% expression of glycosylated α-DG. Affected individuals displayed prominent 100 kDa banding, signifying α-DG composition is largely unglycosylated. Carriers also demonstrated reduced, albeit insignificant, total α-DG expression (100 and 150 kDa products), at 56% compared with controls, while affected samples had roughly 80% total α-DG (Figure 3C). It is curious that carriers had reduced total α-DG expression compared with affected samples, which could imply loss of α-DG and declined sarcolemmal maintenance. Surprisingly LGMDR9 affected samples reported largely similar expression of core α-DG compared with controls (heavily expressed 100 kDa product), which differs from previous studies which have found reduced α-DG levels in LGMDR9. β-DG expression differed amongst carriers and affected samples, at 46% and 73% of controls, respectively (Figure 3D). β-DG expression has previously been reported as normal in LGMDR9 affected samples, differing from our findings.32,33

LGMDR9 Carriers Have ∼ 50% FKRP Enzymatic Functionality

We hypothesized that carriers would have about 50% FKRP protein functionality in accordance with their haplotype. To test this hypothesis, we determined FKRP glycosylation ability by quantifying relative matriglycan expression with the IIH6 antibody. The IIH6 antibody recognizes a specific functional epitope of the matriglycan chain of glycosylated α-DG, providing a more direct measure of FKRP glycosylation ability. Although the IIH6 measures do not achieve statistical significance (Discussion), the carriers trended toward having ∼50% matriglycan expression (Figure 3, E and F). As a complementary approach we performed the laminin-overlay assay to ascertain laminin-binding ability of present matriglycan. Indeed, we found that carrier samples had impaired (57.5%) laminin-binding ability (Figure 3, G and H). Individuals with LGMDR9 had minimal matriglycan and virtually zero laminin expression, thus displaying little to no ability to anchor to the basement membrane (Figure 3, G and H). These 2 assays indicate that carriers have approximately half the enzymatic functionality of FKRP when compared with controls. In total, this combination of assays indicate that haplosufficiency is reached at approximately 50% FKRP functionality.

LGMDR3 Carriers Have Equivalent α-Sarcoglycan Expression Compared with Controls

Muscle-biopsy derived SGCA protein levels were consistent in carriers and controls. We completed 4 immunoblot technical replicates. Across all replicates, carriers averaged 89% α-sarcoglycan expression relative to controls (range 77%–103%). Affected samples averaged 9% relative α-sarcoglycan expression (range, 5.4%–15.1%) (Figure 4, D and E).

As disruption of 1 member of the sarcoglycan complex had been previously shown to impact levels of other members of the functional core, we also assessed relative levels of SGCB (β-sarcoglycan).12 Across 3 replicates (SGCB protein could not be measured from 1 blot), LGMDR3 carriers possessed an average of 103% SGCB protein expression relative to controls (range 82.5%–125%). LGMDR3 affected individuals averaged 18.6% expression (range 16.7%–21.5%) (Figure 4F). These measures suggest that SGCB protein expression does indeed correspond with SGCA expression. In total, these results demonstrate that LGMDR3 carriers have SGCA protein expression consistent with controls.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that while haplosufficient carriers of autosomal recessive LGMDs maintain a phenotype comparable with unaffected controls, carriers often differ in protein expression and functionality. Carriers of LGMDR1 have approximately 25% expression of calpain-3 protein, while LGMDR3 carriers maintain α-sarcoglycan levels comparable with controls. LGMDR9 carriers have similar levels of FKRP protein compared with controls, but demonstrate approximately 50% enzymatic function. In the case of LGMDR9, reduction in functionality is dose-dependent, hovering between controls and affected samples. These findings suggest that for some LGMD subtypes, an AAV gene replacement therapy achieving 50% protein expression or less would lead to haplosufficiency.

Currently, there are 5 active LGMD gene-replacement clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov.14 Molecular outcome measures for these trials include measures of protein restoration both through western blotting and immunofluorescence at various timepoints post vector administration. These analyses quantify relative restoration of protein expression; however, a sufficient threshold of acceptable restoration has not been established. Publications related to completed trials quantify protein expression in relation to healthy controls.35 However, the use of noncarrier controls as a restoration standard may lead to an overestimation of required therapeutic levels when treating these recessive, loss-of-function LGMD subtypes. This is made apparent by the findings of this analysis, which reveals decreased recovery of protein in heterozygous carriers of several LGMD subtypes, despite all maintaining a healthy phenotype. This analysis provides a range of relative protein expression and functionality for haplosufficient individuals that suggests a lowered standard of wild-type protein restoration for specific subtypes may be a sufficient benchmark to indicate therapeutic success.

The first subtype detailed in this analysis is LGMDR1 (CAPN3). Considering all carriers included in this analysis were heterozygous for null CAPN3 variants (3 of which had the Slavic founder mutation c.550del), we hypothesized that these individuals would have 50% reduction of calpain-3. Surprisingly, LGMDR1 carriers were found to have about 25% relative expression of total calpain-3 (defined by full-length calpain-3 in addition to 55–60 kDa autolysis products). This severe reduction of haplosufficient calpain-3 levels below 50% could be explained by the assertion that calpain-3 has the ability to form a catalytic homotrimer.36 We hypothesize that the enhanced enzymatic activity that could be garnered by dimerizing reduces the amount of calpain-3 required for muscular maintenance. Furthermore, total RNA-seq demonstrated that the reduced levels of calpain-3 did not alter global gene expression patterns. In total, these analyses demonstrating the diminished levels of projected calpain-3 required to reach haplosufficiency may substantially reduce the barrier for gene-replacement therapeutic efficacy.

The second subtype we analyzed was LGMDR9 (FKRP). 3 of the 4 carriers were heterozygous for missense variants, while one had a frameshift variant. The predominance of missense variants in this cohort suggests that the quality (functionality) of FKRP may be affected, rather than the quantity. Our observations of FKRP protein expression and functionality differ from previous studies. While to the authors' knowledge there has not been a published study assessing FKRP levels in LGMDR9 carriers, there have been numerous with LGMDR9 mouse models. One publication reported zero FKRP protein expression in both Leu276Ile knock-in homozygous (affected) or heterozygous (carrier) mice.37 This is curious, considering our cohort also predominately harbored the Leu276Ile variant, but we observed normal FKRP protein expression in LGMDR9 carriers and drastically increased FKRP protein expression in LGMDR9 affected samples. The apparent elevated levels of FKRP protein in affected individuals could be due to a compensatory mechanism in response to lack of FKRP activity. This phenomenon has not been reported before and could be the subject of future study.

Another study modeling Leu276Ile knock-in mice found that heterozygous (carrier) mice actually had increased IIH6 (glycosylated α-DG) compared with wildtype mice, insinuating that heterozygous mice actually had elevated FKRP enzymatic functionality.38 This differs from our observations, which report ∼50% reduction in FKRP functionality in LGMDR9 carriers. These discrepancies between our study and the literature are perhaps explained by the differences in model organism (human vs mouse) and the variability introduced when imposing a human variant in an animal model.

Our study reported a slight reduction of α-DG expression in LGMDR9 carriers, suggesting deterioration of the sarcolemma. Carriers also exhibited roughly 50% glycosylated α-DG and laminin-binding ability. We additionally blotted for matriglycan with the IIH6 antibody, which trended toward 50% detection in carriers. The banding on the IIH6 blot was faint due to large amount of protein required for strong antibody recognition (>50 μg). Owing to limited biopsy muscle mass, we were forced to load slightly under threshold. Despite lack of significance, the IIH6 results corroborate the findings from the laminin-overlay assay. What remains unclear is if the reduction of observed matriglycan/laminin-binding in carriers is attributed toward diminished FKRP functionality, decreased α-DG presence, or a combination of both. Regardless, the intermediate levels of α-DG and matriglycan in R9 carriers are outcomes that define haplosufficiency and can better inform outcome measures for LGMDR9 gene-replacement therapies.

The final subtype examined was LGMDR3 (SGCA). LGMDR3 carrier SGCA protein levels were roughly equivalent to controls and significantly greater than affected samples. Expression of SGCB protein mirrored SGCA, insinuating that SGCB expression was dependent on functionality of SGCA.

Given roughly 50% of SGCA transcripts originated from the pathogenic allele (Table 4), it is possible that up to half of the detected α-sarcoglycan on the immunoblots may be misfolded and nonfunctional. However, this is unlikely due to endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation (ERAD) of misfolded sarcoglycans. This mechanism is proven to degrade the vast majority of mutant sarcoglycan proteins to prevent localization to the sarcolemma. Although some may escape ERAD and reach the sarcolemma, their inability to assemble into a complex with neighboring sarcoglycans would lead to degradation at the membrane.39 Therefore, it is reasonable that the protein extracted from LGMDR3 carrier samples is functional α-sarcoglycan, and that any mutant product has been degraded. This insinuates that LGMDR3 carriers are not haplosufficient, but rather maintain levels of functional α-sarcoglycan consistent with controls.

This analysis is the first to examine protein expression in carriers of LGMD. However, preceding studies have quantified protein expression in affected individuals with LGMD, which can provide comparison and context for the results of this study.

Previous analysis with LGMDR1 samples revealed variable expression of calpain-3, which appeared to be dependent on genotype. For instance, patients homozygous for frameshift mutants c.550delA or c.328C > T had a complete loss of calpain-3 expression, while some compound heterozygotes with 1 null and 1 missense variant had normal expression.28,40 This aligns well with what we observed in our analysis; all individuals—either carrier or affected—that housed at least 1 null allele had reduction of calpain-3.

We did not observe the same convergence with the literature in our LGMDR9 analysis. Previous laminin-overlay analysis revealed some LGMDR9 affected individuals (about 10%) had about equal matriglycan expression to a control.41 This conflicts with the close to 0% laminin-overlay detection seen across all LGMDR9 affected samples in our study. Furthermore, this ∼100% laminin detection far exceeds the ∼50% haplosufficiency benchmark established in our LGMDR9 carrier samples. The discrepancy observed between matriglycan expression in the LGMDR9 individuals in our analysis and the literature could be due to experimental parameters, such as the choice of healthy controls or differences in experimental reagents, or the minority of LGMDR9 individuals from that study with total matriglycan expression could have environmental or pharmaceutical influence confounding measurements.

Previous LGMDR3 studies have modeled LGMDR3 carrier status in SGCA-null mouse models. One study found that heterozygous SGCA-null mice (1 wild-type and 1 null allele) had α-sarcoglycan expression consistent with wild-type mice.42 This is concordant with our study, which also found normal protein levels in LGMDR3 carriers.

This study contains several limitations. Carriers in this analysis had either frameshift, nonsense, or missense genotypes, resulting in a wide range of impacts on protein levels and function. It is possible a greater distribution of pathogenic variants may provide a wider range of functional expression in carriers. We also were limited in sample size for each subgroup. In some instances, only 4 carriers could be included in the analysis. Having additional samples with diversified genotypes would have led to more robust measures of protein abundance and haplosufficiency. In addition, we were restricted in our ability to adequately perform certain molecular functional assays because of limited mass of patient muscle biopsies.

The urgency of approving LGMD gene-therapies is recognized by the FDA entity Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER), which is considering LGMD gene-therapy applications under accelerated approval. Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research has indicated that protein quantification would be a valid clinical trial biomarker.43 The question remains, however, what standard of protein quantification would be acceptable. The findings of this study suggest haplosufficient protein thresholds could supply that standard of protein restoration, allowing for expedited approval of these biologics. Scaling down dosing of gene therapy for some LGMD subtypes to match a haplosufficient carrier could allow for lower doses of gene therapy deliverables, resulting in reduced monetary costs to all stakeholders. Furthermore, delivering lower doses of the viral therapeutic vector could imply reduced risks of adverse immune responses and toxicity in patients.

Reducing the bar of restoration to that of a haplosufficient carrier provides an attainable threshold of restoration, thereby supporting approval of these life-changing treatments in the foreseeable future.
