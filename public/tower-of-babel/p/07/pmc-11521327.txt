Microphase iron particle growth promoted by solar wind implantation in lunar soils
Authors: Xuejin Lu, Jian Chen, Haijun Cao, Xiaohui Fu, Xiaojia Zeng, Le Qiao, Chengbao Liu, Guang Zhang, Peng Zhang, Zongcheng Ling
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLunar soils record the history and spectral changes resulting from the space-weathering process. The solar wind and micrometeoroids are the main space-weathering agents leading to darkening (decreasing albedo) and reddening (increasing reflectance with longer wavelength) of visible and near-infrared spectra. Nevertheless, their relative contributions are not well constrained and understood. In this study, we examine the near-infrared spectral variation as a function of lunar latitude and chemical composition using remote spectroscopic analysis of mare basalts and swirl regions. The results indicate that the reflectance of lunar mature soils darkens and the spectral slope flattens (reddening effect saturation) in areas of enhanced solar wind flux. We propose a previously unrecognized stage of space weathering (the post-mature stage), in which solar wind implantation may contribute to the growth and coarsening of metallic iron particles into larger microphase iron. This space-weathering mechanism is dominated by the solar wind and has important implications for understanding the alteration processes of airless bodies across our solar system.

Significance Statement

The production and accumulation of metallic iron particles during space-weathering processes lead to alterations in the spectral properties of airless bodies. However, the origin and distribution of metallic iron with various particle sizes are not yet well understood. In this study, we provide evidence via remote sensing spectroscopic observations and laboratory simulations that metallic iron undergoes growth and coarsening to form microphase iron with larger particle sizes, promoted by solar wind implantation. These findings carry significant implications for understanding the space-weathering mechanisms and resulting optical alterations of materials on the Moon as well as other airless bodies within the solar system.

Introduction

Space weathering is a pervasive process that occurs on the surfaces of airless celestial bodies due to exposure to the harsh space environments. Solar wind particles and micrometeoroids are the primary space-weathering agents. These agents alter the optical properties of surface materials, resulting in darkening and reddening effects on their reflectance spectra and ultimately suppressing mineral absorption features (1). On the Moon, newly formed crater materials show more obvious absorption features and higher albedo compared with lunar mature soils. These alterations in the spectral properties are predominantly attributed to the production of submicrometer-sized metallic iron particles (SMFe0) (2, 3). Specifically, the larger-sized iron particles (>∼40 nm, known as microphase iron or Britt–Pieters particles (4)) decrease the albedo in the visible and near-infrared spectral regions, while the smaller-sized iron particles (<∼10 nm) redden spectra in the visible wavelengths and cause the near-infrared spectra to darken and slightly redden. The intermediate-sized iron particles (nanophase iron) induce both darkening and reddening effects in the visible and near-infrared regions (5). Consequently, several spectroscopic parameters, such as the spectral slope and optical maturity (OMAT) (6), have been developed to evaluate the degree of space weathering and exposure duration of lunar soils. These spectral parameters offer valuable insights into the magnitude of space weathering experienced by the lunar soil (3, 7, 8).

Previous studies have suggested that the effects of lunar space weathering display both latitudinal and compositional variations (9–15). Multispectral images from the Clementine mission have revealed a latitudinal trend in the Moon's spectral reflectance (9). This trend revealed that low-latitude mare regions are darker than high-latitude soils, likely due to the enhanced solar wind flux (9–11). The spectral continuum slopes of high-latitude regions (≥75°) are much lower than those of low-latitude regions, as shown by data from the Kaguya Spectral Profiler (12). This latitudinal trend also depends on the composition of lunar soils (13). The polarization spectra indicate that the particles in lunar soils at high latitudes are coarser than those at low latitudes (14, 15). These studies suggest that the influence of space weathering gradually diminishes when moving toward higher latitudes (9–15). However, the mechanism behind the variations in spectral effects and the size evolution of SMFe0 remains poorly understood.

Given that the degree of space weathering decreases with increasing lunar latitude, this study utilized the spectral properties of lunar global mare and magnetic anomaly regions to evaluate space-weathering processes on the Moon. This analysis highlights the significant role of the solar wind in lunar space-weathering mechanisms. These results enable a comprehensive analysis of the latitudinal variation of space-weathering effects, offering valuable insights into the distribution and intensity of geological processes, such as solar wind implantation and micrometeorite bombardment on lunar surface.

Results

Spectral variation with lunar latitudes

Spectral reflectance at 750 nm (R750) and 1,550 nm (R1,550) of mare basalts in lunar nearside from Kaguya multiband imager (MI) observations (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) was used to investigate the latitudinal variations in lunar space weathering. Lunar mare units were classified based on their FeO contents derived from Lunar Prospector (LP) gamma-ray spectrometer data ranging from 14 to 23 wt.% (16) with a step size of 1 wt.% (Fig. 1A). Similar to previous results derived from Clementine data (9, 11), the variation in R750 with latitude was also observed in MI data (see the solid lines in Fig. 1B): R750 values decrease with decreasing latitudes. Additionally, similar variations were observed in the near-infrared bands (R1,550). For soils with FeO content of 14–15 wt.%, R750 decreases from 0.08 to 0.06 with a decrement of 0.02 as the latitude decreases from 60° to 0° (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). This trend is more pronounced in the near-infrared wavelengths. The R1,550 of lunar soils with a similar composition decreases from 0.15 to 0.11 with a decrement of 0.04 as the latitude decreases to 0° (dashed lines in Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

The extent of reflectance decrease with decreasing latitude also depends on the composition. Latitudinal variation becomes less pronounced with increasing FeO content in lunar soils (Fig. 1C). In the most FeO-rich soils (22–23 wt.%), the decrease in reflectance with decreasing latitude is minimal. Thus, the latitudinal effect almost disappears in FeO-rich soils. It is noteworthy that this latitudinal effect is not found in the low-FeO (<14 wt.%) highland regions (11), which suggests that a narrow range of FeO content maximizes the effect of space weathering. Furthermore, reflectance at the lunar equator (R0) is linearly dependent on FeO content. As lunar soils reach their darkest state, R0 can serve as an initial value for increasing latitudes (SI Appendix, Table S1). The spectral parameter diagram (R750 versus R1,550/R750) used to characterize space weathering shows a trend opposite to the reddening effect observed in conventional spectral evolution: the spectral slope decreases with decreasing latitude and increasing space weathering (Fig. 2A). The saturated trend of the spectral slope is composition-dependent and more pronounced in FeO-rich soils. This trend seems to contradict previous conclusions and has previously been overlooked. This trend represents a late stage in the lunar soil maturation process and the evolution of its spectral characteristics.

Laboratory simulation of spectral characteristics

The spectral reflectance of silica gel samples was reported by Noble et al. (5). The R750 and R1,550/R750 values for four groups of simulated samples containing different sizes of SMFe0 are plotted in Fig. 2B. Similar to the latitudinal trend of spectral reflectance, the variation in spectral slopes shows a saturated trend in samples with more and larger-sized SMFe0: the 35-nm-sized group reaches saturation at an SMFe0 abundance exceeding 0.348 wt.%, while the 40-nm-sized group reaches saturation at an abundance exceeding 0.498 wt.% (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S2). In contrast, samples containing smaller SMFe0 particles (8 and 15 nm) did not show a saturated trend in spectral slopes even when the SMFe0 abundance exceeded 1.6–2.0 wt.% (SI Appendix, Table S2). This suggests that spectral slope saturation is mainly controlled by the particle size of SMFe0, rather than being solely dependent on the SMFe0 content as previously believed.

Latitudinal variation of SMFe0 content

We also examined the latitudinal and compositional trends of nanophase iron and microphase iron content, based on the findings of Trang and Lucey (2019) (8) (Fig. 3). The abundance of nanophase iron particles remains approximately constant (∼0.55 wt.%) across the 0°–45° latitudinal range, while the content of microphase iron particles decreases monotonically (∼1.1–0.8 wt.%) with increasing latitude (Fig. 3A and C). These trends indicate that microphase iron content is higher at lower latitudes, where the soils are subjected to more intense space weathering. Within the same latitude, the nanophase iron and microphase iron content exhibit variations with the composition of lunar soils. The variation in nanophase iron content with FeO content is subtle, ranging from 0.47 to 0.65 wt.% (Fig. 3B). Except for the lunar equatorial region, a significant increase is observed in microphase iron content (from ∼0.90 to ∼1.05 wt.%) when FeO content exceeds 17 wt.% (Fig. 3D).

Spectral variation at lunar swirls

Two swirls with different FeO content (Reiner Gamma, FeO: 18–22 wt.%; Mare Marginis, FeO: 10–14 wt.%, SI Appendix, Fig. S4) were selected to investigate the composition-dependent spectral variation in lunar magnetic anomaly regions (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Bright swirl portions and low-albedo dark lanes form a typical lunar swirl (Fig. 4A, C, and D). Hyperspectra from the M3 instrument of a fresh crater to the surrounding mature surface depict a typical spectral pattern altered by space weathering (Fig. 4B and E). Compared with the spectral pattern of fresh craters, the swirl portion is brighter than the surrounding mare regions (Fig. 4F), while the dark lanes show lower reflectance and moderate spectral slopes (Fig. 4G).

Regarding the spectral parameters (Fig. 5), the dark lanes of Reiner Gamma demonstrate a distinct spectral variation trend compared with fresh craters and bright portions of Reiner Gamma. From fresh craters and bright swirls to the surrounding mature soils, the R750 continues to decrease while the ratio of R1,550/R750 (spectral slope) increases. From bright swirls to dark lanes, the R750 decreases further, while the ratio of R1,550/R750 increases and then decreases. This suggests that the spectral reddening effects of space weathering are saturated while darkening effects remain active in these dark lanes of Reiner Gamma. This spectral evolution is similar to the latitudinal trend of mature mare regions. However, this trend is not evident in the dark lanes of the low-FeO swirl (e.g. Mare Marginis, SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Discussion

Spectral variation in latitude effects and lunar swirls

Previous studies suggest that the content of nanophase iron particles is lower at bright swirls compared with nonswirl background surfaces, as indicated by the spectral evidence (8, 17). However, limited attention has been given to the spectral differences between the dark lanes of swirls and the surrounding mature lunar soils. Our results indicate that spectral variations with latitude in the lunar mature mare region (0°–60°) are similar to the spectral variation trends in dark lanes (Figs. 2 and 4). Based on the spectral properties, we infer that more microphase iron particles were formed in the dark lanes compared with the bright swirls and nonswirl mature soils. These larger-sized SMFe0 particles can reduce reflectance at all wavelengths and attenuate the reddening effect on spectral slopes (Figs. 2 and 4), similar to the latitudinal spectral trend observed from higher latitudes (±60°) to the equator. However, the spectral slope saturation observed in the latitudinal trend is not evident in all dark lanes of lunar swirls. It usually occurs at the swirl portions where magnetic anomalies are relatively weak in Fe-rich mare context, such as in the southwest portion of Reiner Gamma [FeO > 18 wt.%, surface magnetic field intensity <50 nT (18)]. In the core region of the Reiner Gamma, where the magnetic anomaly is the strongest, the dark lanes exhibit no saturated trend in the spectral slope. This pattern does not occur in the low-FeO swirls [e.g. Mare Marginis, FeO < 14 wt.%, surface magnetic field intensity <20 nT (16)], even though the magnetic field intensities of some regions are very weak. Thus, the spectral evolution in dark lanes depends on the composition and solar wind flux, consistent with the latitudinal trend affected by the FeO content of mare units.

Based on the statistical results of the latitude effect using data from Trang and Lucey (8) (Fig. 3C), this increase in microphase iron content is about ∼0.2 to ∼0.3 wt.%. However, the microphase iron content in swirl regions does no show significant variation. We note that the accuracy of microphase iron prediction is reported as 0.6 wt.% (8). Thus, this apparent lack of significant variation in swirl regions is partly due to the variations falling within the predictive accuracy range (i.e. <0.6 wt.%). The latitudinal effect becomes evident due to the extensive dataset used for averaging. In contrast, the limited number of data pixels available for the swirl regions may not reveal these differences.

Solar wind implantation is recognized as one of the main factors affecting the spectra of lunar surface (9, 19) and produces smaller-sized iron particles during the space-weathering process (17). Spectral variations following the latitudinal trend, controlled by the solar wind flux, which decreases as a cosine function of latitude, were identified by Hemingway et al. (9). The average solar flux decreases from 63.6% at the equator to ∼30% at ±60° latitude (11). The similarity of the spectral variation trend in dark lanes to the lunar latitudinal trend supports the hypothesis that the formation of the swirls is due to the shielding effect of magnetic anomalies on the solar wind (20). Under a strong magnetic shield, the solar wind flux is greatly reduced at lunar swirls, similar to the reduction observed from the lunar equator to polar regions. This leads to attenuated space weathering at the bright swirls as well as in the lunar polar regions (60°–90°). In contrast, the solar wind flux is not significantly weakened in the dark lanes formed among bright swirls. Hence, the solar wind flux plays a significant role in the spectral evolution and space-weathering processes of lunar surface.

Formation mechanism of the larger-sized SMFe0

Based on the above results, we suggest that both solar wind flux and FeO content control the formation rate of larger-sized SMFe0 particles in lunar soils. Previously reported mechanisms for the formation of larger-sized microphase iron particles include dense clustering in agglutinated glass and dissociation of Fe-bearing materials associated with high-velocity micrometeorite impacts (21, 22). However, the spectral slope saturation trends indicate the formation of more microphase iron in regions of enhanced solar wind flux, although no significant differences exist in impact fluxes and velocities between the bright swirls and inter-swirl dark lanes (23).

Thus, we propose an evolutionary mechanism for the coarsening of SMFe0 particle sizes related to solar wind implantation and lunar soil composition. The solar wind implants hydrogen ions, reducing ferrous iron ions to nanometer-sized metallic iron nuclei by breaking Fe–O bonds or reducing ferrous iron (24, 25). When nanophase iron particles reach the saturation limit, the ferrous Fe ions available for reduction in the surface layer of soil particles are insufficiently dense to form additional nanophase iron particles (8). Instead of forming isolated nanophase iron particles with reddening and darkening effects, these nanometer-sized iron nuclei redeposit onto preexisting SMFe0 spheres generated by micrometeoroid bombardment, increasing particle size without significantly increasing the number density of SMFe0 spheres. In addition, micrometeorite impacts result in vapor deposition. This vapor deposition is enriched with iron atoms in areas of high solar wind flux. The deposition of these Fe-rich vapors onto the surface of mineral grains enhances the size of preexisting metallic Fe particles. This coarsening process was demonstrated by laboratory analogs, where the spectral slope no longer increased after successive laser irradiation, while the content of metallic iron persisted in increasing (26). Some of the nanophase iron particles grow larger, moderating the spectral reddening effect and transforming from nanophase to microphase iron particles (26). The FeO-rich lunar soils have more FeO available for reduction (27) and develop larger-sized SMFe0 particles exhibiting significantly accelerated formation and coarsening rates.

These nanometer-sized iron particles in mature soils dissolve and redeposit onto the surface of larger SMFe0 particles, a process known as Ostwald ripening (28, 29). Previous research suggested that Ostwald ripening cannot occur on the Moon because the maximum temperature of the lunar sunlit surface does not exceed 150°C (30). However, spectroscopic evidence indicates that SMFe0 growth indeed exists in the nearside mare regions, resulting in darker reflectance and a moderate slope at the equator, while exhibiting brighter and redder spectra at higher latitudes (<60°). A similar spectral variation trend is also observed in the dark lanes of swirls within FeO-rich mare contexts, but not in low-FeO swirls and highland regions. This suggests that the occurrence of Ostwald ripening on the Moon is influenced by the composition of lunar soils. We suggest that FeO-rich materials might reduce the temperature threshold necessary for Ostwald ripening (31). In addition, micrometeoroid bombardments can generate localized high-temperature environments. The heating processes promote the growth of SMFe0 as well as the formation of amorphous rims on soil grains (32). Another possibility is that the Moon does not reach the requisite temperatures for Ostwald ripening. However, considering that vapor deposits from micrometeorite impacts in low-latitude regions contain more iron atoms, this may also facilitate the formation of larger metallic iron particles.

Based on the evolutionary mechanism proposed above, we hypothesize that the solar wind flux dominates SMFe0 abundance, while the particle size of SMFe0 is mainly dependent on FeO content of parent materials. The proportion of larger-sized SMFe0 particles is expected to be higher in mature and FeO-rich lunar soils. The spectral evidence from both the latitudinal trend and lunar swirls supports these hypotheses.

In addition, laboratory simulation studies of space weathering conducted with various samples indicate that the effects of space weathering are composition-dependent (33–36). For example, Xu et al. (36) suggest that more FeO-rich olivine powder yield larger-sized SMFe0 particles than low-FeO olivine under the same conditions. These experimental findings align with our observations of latitudinal trends and lunar swirls. Moreover, recent spectroscopic analyses of the Chang’e-5 landing site, which is characterized by high FeO content, further substantiates this mechanism for the formation of larger-sized SMFe0 particles (37).

Post-mature stage of space weathering

Our results indicate that spectral slope saturation occurs in FeO-rich and mature mare soils. We therefore propose a three-stage space-weathering process on the Moon. (i) In the first stage (rapid weathering), the small-sized nanophase iron particles [<10 nm (5)] form and primarily influence absorption in the short-wavelength region, resulting in a reddening of the spectral slope in the visible wavelength range (1, 37, 38). Spectral characteristics of freshly exposed materials gradually alter over time, following the spectral variation trend described above. This rapid weathering stage usually lasts 104–106 years and is primarily influenced by solar wind activity (3, 18, 39). (ii) In the second stage (fresh-to-mature), the effects from micrometeorite bombardments gradually accumulate and begin to dominate the space-weathering processes, resulting in the production of 15–25-nm-sized SMFe0 particles. This causes a pronounced reddening of the spectral slope across both visible and near-infrared wavelength ranges. This fresh-to-mature stage spans 108–109 years and is primarily driven by micrometeorite bombardments (40). The lunar surface regions from 60° latitude to the poles and the bright regions of lunar swirls are characterized by this fresh-to-mature stage. (iii) In the third stage (post-mature), some nanophase iron particles coarsen through Ostwald ripening and transform into microphase iron particles. This results in absorption across the full wavelength region and saturation of the spectral slopes. Large-sized SMFe0 (>40 nm) may form in agglutinitic glass and glass-coated mineral grains. The mature soils on lunar surface from the equator to 60° latitude, as well as the dark lanes of lunar swirls, are characterized by the post-mature stage.

In addition, we suggest that the particle size distribution of SMFe0 serves as a critical indicator of the degree of space weathering. Hemingway et al. (9) proposed that the fast space-weathering process induced by the solar wind occurs in the initial space-weathering stage. In this study, we suggest that solar wind promotes an increase in SMFe0 particle size during the post-mature weathering stage. This means that solar wind implantation has consistently played a significant role throughout the various processes of space weathering on the Moon.

Solar wind implantation not only rapidly produces metallic iron during the rapid weathering stages but also contributes to the increase in metallic iron grain size during later, prolonged periods (the post-mature stage). The discovery of this spectral saturation effect is crucial for understanding the evolutionary path and spectral behavior of SMFe0 after reaching saturation during the space weathering of the Moon. These diverse stages and degrees offer vital references for investigating space-weathering mechanisms throughout the solar system. This specific evolution mechanism of SMFe0 in mature and FeO-rich lunar soils has important implications for understanding the space-weathering processes of airless bodies across different locations with varying solar distances and composition within the solar system.

Materials and methods

Study regions

Mare regions at latitudes of 30° S–60° N and longitudes of 90° W–90° E were analyzed in this study. MI pixels with slope > 1.0° and OMAT > 0.25 were excluded to avoid contamination by fresh materials from impact craters and steep hills (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The OMAT was calculated using parameters from Clementine reflectance at 750 and 950 nm (6) and corrected MI datasets which matched the Clementine reflectance (19). Slope values for the mare regions were obtained from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter aboard the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Thus, the statistical regions were confined to flat mare plains devoid of large craters and steep slopes.

Reiner Gamma swirls (7.5°N, 60°W) are located on the southern portion of Oceanus Procellarum. Mare Marginis swirls (16°N, 84°E) are located on the eastern limb of lunar nearside and near the Goddard C crater. The distribution and extent of these swirls were mapped by Denevi et al. (41) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

MI spectral analysis for latitudinal trend

We adopted a similar approach to spectral statistics to allow comparison with previous studies (9, 12). Data from the Kaguya MI were used to evaluate the properties of visible and near-infrared spectra within the of 0°–60°latitudinal range, which has been corrected for shading effects due to topography (4). The MI instrument collected spectra of lunar surface materials at five visible bands (415, 750, 900, 950, and 1,001 nm) and four near-infrared bands (1,000, 1,050, 1,250, and 1,550 nm). Reflectance values at 750 nm (MI Band 2) and 1,550 nm (MI Band 9) which exhibit minimal mineral absorption features were selected to assess the spectral properties of lunar surface. The FeO content derived from the LP gamma-ray spectrometer was used to classify lunar surface materials (15). MI images were resampled to 0.5°/pixel to match the resolution of the FeO abundance map (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Regions of lunar surface with slopes >1.0° and/or OMAT values exceeding 0.25 were excluded from the statistical results (6, 19).

M3 spectral analysis of lunar swirls

A hyperspectral dataset with a space resolution of 140–280 m/pixel from the M3 instrument was utilized for a detailed spectral analysis of lunar swirls (42). The reflectance image frame M3G20090210T051732_V01_RFL from optical period OP1b was employed to analyze the Reiner Gamma swirls, while the frame M3G20090602T074711_V01_RFL from optical period OP2c was used for Mare Marginis swirls. In contrast to previous studies on lunar swirls, this investigation focuses on the low-reflectance dark lanes, which are believed to experience enhanced solar wind flux (43). Over 15 M3 spectra were selected from each dark lane of these lunar swirls. The spectra are normalized to the reflectance value at 750 nm to facilitate comparison of the spectral slope across different portions of lunar swirls (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S5).
