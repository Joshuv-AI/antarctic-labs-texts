Quantum Computing Calculations of Protein–Ligand Binding Energies Using Decomposition Methods on Simulated and Real Quantum Hardware
Authors: Hironobu Kitajima, Carlos Bistafa, Takao Kobayashi, Shu Kanno, Okimasa Okada, Kouta Murasaki, Jumpei Koyama, Ryuta Saito, Qi Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We analyzed the performance of quantum algorithms for studying protein–ligand systems, using both simulator and real hardware. To achieve this, we selected thrombin and 5 ligands as a test case and employed a decomposition strategy using density matrix embedded theory, dividing the ligand systems into three fragments each. First, the energy of one of the fragments was calculated using the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) using a state vector simulator, while the energy of the remaining fragments was calculated using Couple Cluster Singles and Doubles (CCSD), with the protein treated as point charges located at the respective atomic sites. Through these noiseless simulations, we evaluated the results under ideal conditions using the state-of-the-art Unitary CCSD ansatz to validate the efficacy of the strategy in a controlled setting without quantum noise. Subsequently, we evaluated the impact of approximations in the ansatz, optimizer, and active space size, which are necessary to decrease the computational cost, in order to target real hardware during the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum era. Finally, we performed the VQE calculations using a superconducting quantum computer developed by the RIKEN RQC–Fujitsu Collaboration Center and also analyzed the noise effect through simulations. The results demonstrated that the use of quantum algorithms can enhance the binding energy correlation value, offering potential applications in the workflow of computer-aided drug design.

Introduction

With the increase in computational power over the last few decades, in silico approaches have become powerful and necessary tools for studies involving chemical and biological systems. Computer-aided drug design (CADD) is an integral part of the primary pipeline for discovering new medications, assisting in screening chemical compound candidates, and reducing costs associated with experiments as well as the need to handle toxic substances. Molecular dynamics simulations and quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) simulations , can be applied to understand the conformation of ligands and the conformational change that a biological target, such as a protein, undergoes when interacting with them. Quantum chemistry methods based on wave functions or density functional theory (DFT) can be used to accurately describe small molecules and provide parameters for the simulations.

Nonetheless, quantum chemical calculations frequently constitute a major bottleneck in any workflow of computational chemistry-based approaches due to the exponentially increasing computational cost associated with the growth in system size when utilizing very accurate wave function-based post-Hartree–Fock methods. Strategies like the fragment molecular orbital (FMO) method are frequently used to reduce the calculation cost of the protein system. In this sense, quantum computing may represent a better alternative because of the possibility of using qubits to represent spin orbitals, which could enable a polynomial computational cost to solve the electronic structure problem exactly via the quantum phase estimation (QPE) algorithm, consequently providing a quantum advantage. A fault-tolerant quantum computer capable of executing such an algorithm could revolutionize the study of large molecular systems, significantly impacting molecular modeling and drug discovery.

At the moment, however, we are still limited to noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices. These devices have a few qubits and a considerable level of noise, making QPE calculations infeasible. To enable molecular calculations on NISQ devices, hybrid algorithms such as the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) have been developed. In VQE, only the expectation value is calculated using NISQ devices, while the optimization of the parameters that describe the trial wave function is performed on a classical workstation.

Aiming at the study of large molecular systems, a method combining VQE and QM/MM simulations has already been developed. However, since current NISQ devices hinder the possibility of conducting full-scale QM/MM simulations, within the CADD domain, efforts have been concentrated on developing strategies to perform calculations for large systems and evaluate the possibility of improving protein–ligand binding energy correlation through quantum algorithms. , It is also worthy to mention that in this context, the term binding energy refers mainly to the binding electronic energy, i.e., it does not include translational, rotational, or vibrational energy. However, it is worth mentioning that rotational and translational contributions are almost zero, since they would be 3/2RT before and after binding. As for vibrational contributions, very few vibrational modes (∼200 cm–1) are excited at room temperature, and if there is a significant contribution, it would be from zero-point vibrational energy. However, since vibrational modes formed by noncovalent van der Waals bonds are also considered low vibrational modes, the change in zero-point vibrational energy before and after binding is also expected to be small.

The first study to attempt a benchmark of a quantum algorithm in a system of biological relevance was conducted by Malone et al., who combined VQE calculations and symmetry-adapted perturbation theory (SAPT) , to study the iron-containing center of lysine-specific demethylase 5 (KDM5A), a protein relevant to human cancer. They achieved good accuracy in the electrostatic and exchange components of the energy, but limited their study to simulations.

Next, Kirsopp et al. explored the use of a decomposition technique based on density matrix embedding theory (DMET) in order to perform an evaluation of real hardware usage for large molecular systems. DMET is an approach used to study strongly correlated systems, representing the system as a fragment coupled to an entangled quantum bath. It allows for precise modeling of local electron correlations on the fragment using advanced post-Hartree–Fock techniques and has been applied to Hubbard models and chemical systems. Kirsopp et al. then combined it with VQE to study a series of BACE1 inhibitors complexed with the active site of the BACE1 enzyme, a well-known system with available benchmark data. They partitioned the system into fragments, calculating only one fragment with VQE on NISQ devices based on superconducting and trapped-ion technologies, while the remaining fragments were treated by using classical computing quantum chemistry methods. Their results, despite not reproducing experimental measurements for the ranking of ligands, were comparable to those of state-of-the-art DFT calculations and MD simulations.

Considering the limitations faced by the aforementioned previous studies, in the present NISQ era, DMET can be an effective technique to reduce computational cost for both noiseless simulations running in simulators made of classical workstations and calculations performed on real hardware. Particularly, noiseless simulations based on state-vector simulators running on supercomputers are a powerful way to access the accuracy of a quantum algorithm in a controlled setting free of quantum noise. However, the simulation cost increases exponentially with the number of qubits, especially because of the complexity involved in representing quantum states through complex numbers and the memory allocation needed for them. While a typical workstation is sufficient for simulations involving up to 30 qubits, a single 40-qubit simulation would require the simultaneous parallel execution of all 1024 nodes available on the Fujitsu Quantum Simulator. Thus, the usage of DMET to divide the system into smaller fragments, combined with active space, is a promising strategy to perform calculations of large systems under the constraints of NISQ devices.

In the previous study on binding energy calculations using quantum computing and DMET, the authors assumed a linear correlation between the calculated binding energy values and experimentally determined pIC50 values, which could somewhat lead to inaccuracies. Therefore, in the present work, we further investigated the applicability of the DMET technique to quantum computing studies of protein–ligand systems through a more proper comparison between the calculated binding energies obtained using both the noiseless simulator and NISQ devices and the experimental enthalpy values. ,

For selecting the molecular system to be used for quantum computing calculations, we paid due consideration to aspects neglected by previous studies. Particularly, accurate calculations depend on reliable information about the X-ray crystallographic structures of the system. Therefore, we selected as targets for the present study systems where enthalpy measurements by ITC and X-ray crystallographic data are available. In this sense, thrombin, a key enzyme in the coagulation cascade, responsible for converting fibrinogen to fibrin, which forms the structural basis of blood clots, is a natural candidate. Thrombin inhibition is a critical aspect of anticoagulant therapy used to prevent and treat thromboembolic disorders such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and atrial fibrillation. Inhibiting thrombin can effectively reduce the level of formation of clots and manage conditions associated with excessive clotting. Due to its significant relevance, there is accurate data available on thrombin structure interacting with a few ligands, including X-ray crystallographic structure and values for enthalpy. ,

We first studied the accuracy improvements in the binding energy correlation achieved by applying state-of-the-art quantum algorithms and ansätze using noiseless simulations, as these algorithms are too resource-intensive for current quantum hardware. Subsequently, we discussed the necessary approximations to make the calculations feasible on NISQ devices and how they may impact the results. Finally, we analyzed the results from calculations performed using an actual quantum computer and discussed strategies for error mitigation, closing with a discussion on noise-induced shift energy values based on noisy simulation results.

Calculation Methods

System

The initial molecular structures of thrombin interacting with respective ligands were obtained from two works by Baum et al., , whose data was deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Using these initial structures, we prepared structures in which the number of residues aligned and matched the structure that presented fewer residues, making them consistent and suitable for energy comparisons. An initial automatic protonation of the structure was first carried out using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE). The structure was then corrected to ensure a neutral system, with the ligand carrying a +1 charge and the protein a −1 charge. All the water atoms were then removed.

Next, a force field relaxation of the structures was performed. For this, we used AMBER14ETH force field implemented in MOE. This force field combines parameters from AMBER14 with extended Hückel theory (EHT) parameters. Essentially, AMBER’s bonded and nonbonded parameters are used for amino acids, nucleic acids, and analogous chemical species, while an AMBER nonbonded parameters and EHT bonded parameters are used for other small molecules. AM1-BCC charges were employed. For energy stabilization calculations (structural relaxation), the coordinates of the residues of the protein within 10 Å from the ligand were fixed, and energy optimization of the side chains of the protein beyond 10 Å was performed using MM calculations.

Finally, using the structures obtained for the protein, we created an electrostatic embedding in which the protein atomic charges were fitted using ESP , at the Hartree–Fock (HF) level of calculation with the minimal basis set STO–3G. AM1-BCC charges and Mulliken charges were also tested, but the ESP charges showed the best correlation with actual measurements (Figure S1). The electrostatic embedding, composed of 4023 point chargesequal to the number of protein atomsarranged at the respective atomic positions of the protein to reproduce the Coulomb field, was used to represent the protein in quantum calculations (Figure , gray). The image was constructed using the VMD software. This is a good first approach, including electrostatic and polarization contributions, but it does not include dispersion or temperature effects that can become very relevant, but would demand additional computational effort outside of the scope of the present study. ,

DMET Calculations and Noiseless Simulations

DMET is a method based on Schmidt decomposition and is used to decompose a molecular system into small fragments in order to reduce the computational burden commonly observed when employing highly accurate quantum chemistry methods.

Initially, the system is calculated with an inexpensive method, usually HF, to generate the initial molecular orbitals. As molecular orbitals are typically delocalized over two or more atoms, a localization process is necessary before dividing the system into fragments. Several methods exist for localization, ranging from well-known schemes such as Boys and Pipek-Mezey, to more modern approaches like Intrinsic Atomic Orbitals (IAO) and Meta-Löwdin.

After obtaining localized molecular orbitals, the system can be divided into fragments, which will be utilized in more accurate quantum chemistry calculations, such as CCSD, or even quantum algorithm calculations like VQE. To perform these calculations, each fragment is treated as an open quantum system entangled with its environment, also commonly referred as “quantum bath”. In other words, the calculation is performed on a system composed of the molecular orbitals localized on the atoms of the fragment, along with the molecular orbitals from the surrounding atoms of other fragments. As a consequence of the Schmidt decomposition, the number of necessary bath orbitals for a given fragment is, at most, the same as the number of localized orbitals on the fragment, greatly reducing the calculation cost. ,

In DMET, self-consistency is achieved through an iterative update of the correlation potential and, when needed, the global chemical potential. Starting from an initial guess, a low-level mean-field Hamiltonian is solved to obtain a reference wave function and its one-particle density matrix. This mean-field state is then used to construct, for each fragment, the corresponding fragment + bath embedding problem. The embedded Hamiltonians are solved with a correlated many-body solver to obtain fragment density matrices and energies. The global chemical potential is adjusted to recover the correct total particle number, while the correlation potential is optimized so that the low-level and high-level local one-particle density matrices agree as closely as possible. These steps are repeated until the relevant quantities converge according to the predefined criteria. Particularly, the number of electrons calculated for each fragment, the sum of which should coincide with the number of electrons known for the total system, within a given precision that can usually be controlled by the chemical potential. Details of the DMET loop are discussed at length in a recent review.

To perform DMET calculations, we used the Tangelo 0.4.0 package, an open-source Python package as the code base, and implemented the necessary additional features and tools ourselves. First, for molecular orbital localization, several methods based on PySCF implementation are available, with the default being meta-Löwdin, which we used in our study.

In Tangelo 0.4.0, Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) is the only optimization method available for DMET. For optimization processes using SLSQP, the gradient of the evaluation function was required. However, in the case of using actual quantum devices, it is known that derivatives, which are more susceptible to noise compared to function values, can lead to a decrease in accuracy. Therefore, for the purpose of actual quantum hardware experiments, we implemented the COBYLA (constrained optimization BY linear approximation) algorithm, which is an optimization method that does not rely on gradients, into the Tangelo VQE solver.

The COBYLA algorithm is a type of simplex method for constrained nonlinear optimization. In the simplex method, the value of the objective function is calculated on a simplex defined within the space of optimization parameters, and the determination of the minimum point and the update of the simplex are repeated sequentially, reducing the volume of the simplex while leaving promising vertices as the optimal points. There are several variations of the simplex update method, but in COBYLA, its distinctive feature is that the interpolation formula for the simplex vertices before the update is solved by linear programming to determine the vertices after the update. Our implementation uses the COBYLA program from the SciPy package, with modifications to integrate it with the Tangelo optimization interface.

Also, in order to add the electrostatic embedding contribution from the thrombin protein into the Hamiltonian, the QM/MM functionality of PySCF was adopted because of its compatibility with Tangelo. Consequently, the point charges representing the protein were inserted into Tangelo’s molecule object, and HF calculations were performed.

The binding energy of a complex system such as protein–ligand, E binding, is defined as the energy difference of the complex and its constituents, i.e., 1 Ebinding=Eprotein+ligand−Eligand−Eprotein Similar to ref , for computational feasibility, we adopted the rigid approximation, assuming that structural changes of the protein are negligible when interacting with the ligand. In such a case, the last term on the right-hand side of eq cancels out the protein–protein terms within the first one, reducing to 2 Ebinding=Eligand‐in‐protein−Eligand‐in‐vacuum In other words, for each ligand and method herein, two calculations were performed: one for the ligand in vacuum and the other for the ligand in the presence of the protein, represented by the point charges.

The rigid approximation constitutes a simplification of the actual protein–ligand binding process, since in biomolecular systems, ligand binding may induce conformational rearrangements in both the ligand and the protein environment. These induced rearrangements can contribute to the binding energy and are not explicitly captured within the rigid approximation. Nevertheless, the rigid approximation is widely employed in computational studies of protein–ligand interactions because it significantly reduces the computational complexity while still retaining the dominant electrostatic and short-range interaction contributions. The approximation is generally considered reasonable when the binding process does not involve large conformational changes. As it will be clear from our results, in the present system, the meaningful correlation observed between the calculated and experimental tendencies suggests that the essential interaction features are adequately captured despite these simplifications, and at least qualitative discussions are possible. In recent years, deep-learning-based approaches to treat flexible proteins have seen great developments. , It is worth mentioning, however, that due to the computational cost involved when using a quantum algorithm, whether using a simulator or real hardware, the rigid approximation is still the standard procedure, as it can also be seen in previous studies. Moreover, as we are discussing mainly the electronic binding energy, the limitations due to the rigid approximation are expected to have a less significant impact when compared to the approximations used for the quantum calculations regarding basis set and active space.

For calculating the binding energy, the following methods were considered. As a reference for traditional classical computing quantum chemistry, we performed HF/STO–3G calculations for the whole ligand. To evaluate the effect of using the minimal basis set, we also performed HF calculations using several Pople’s basis sets. To have an estimation of how the lack of electron correlation impacts the HF result, we also performed calculations using the CCSD/STO–3G level of theory. A calculation using DMET, where the energy of each of the three fragments was calculated using CCSD (labeled DMET-CCSD), was also performed to attest to the accuracy of the DMET treatment. Finally, we have the aforementioned DMET-CC:VQE:CC, where only the energy of fragment 2 was calculated using VQE, while the energy of the other two fragments was calculated using CCSD.

For the DMET-CC:VQE:CC calculations, even using the minimal basis set STO–3G, fragment 2 presents 48 molecular orbitals (24 molecular orbitals localized on the atoms of the fragment plus 24 molecular orbitals localized on atoms at the quantum bath, Figure S2), requiring a total of 96 qubits to represent them, which is computationally unfeasible for current quantum devices or even simulators. Therefore, to reduce the size of the system to be calculated, we employed an active space for the VQE calculation involving fragment 2.

For the active space selection of fragment 2, in addition to the computational cost of the noiseless simulations, we also had to consider real hardware availability constraints. For the present study, only 4 qubits were available for a limited amount of time. Therefore, with the aim of conducting experiments on real hardware, we focused on analyzing two active space cases. First, a minimal active space of (2e, 2o) is used, since each qubit can be used to represent a spin–orbital, consequently 4 qubits can be used to represent 2 molecular orbitals. Second, an active space of (4e, 4o) would be more interesting if additional resources were available. Tests with a larger active space were also performed. Figure presents the orbitals included in the active spaces (2e, 2o) and (4e, 4o) for fragment 2 (for larger active spaces, see Figure S3a).

Molecular orbitals used for the active space of Fragment 2 for (2e, 2o) and (4e, 4o). The localized orbitals are doubly occupied, while the bath orbitals are empty. Orbitals used for other fragments and for (8e, 6o) case are available in the SI (Figure S3a,b).

Accordingly, an active space was also used for fragments 1 and 3, using two molecular orbitals far from the valence region (lowest occupied orbital and highest unoccupied, Figure S3b), which were verified to have a minimal impact on the results (Table S1). In principle, for CCSD, no active space would be necessary, but the Tangelo 0.4.0 implementation of DMET imposes a restriction such that, when applying different solvers to different fragments, if one fragment is calculated using an active space strategy, all other fragments must also have an active space defined. The active spaces do not need to be the same size, however. Therefore, we selected the two molecular orbitals expected to make the smallest contribution to the energy, thereby making the resulting wave function as close as possible to the intended CCSD wave function. Similar to what was observed by Kirsopp et al., the use of an active space in the CCSD solver may alter the energy of the individual ligands, but the overall trends and coefficients are preserved.

It is worth noting that since DMET uses a set of localized orbitals on the fragment plus bath orbitals on nearby atoms on other fragments, the chemical reasoning used for multireference calculations is not directly applicable here. The active space is composed of doubly occupied orbitals on the fragment with empty orbitals from the bath to allow interaction between the fragment and bath. This is an approximation employed to enable calculations using the VQE algorithm, while robust DMET results, in principle, demand correlation from all molecular orbitals present in the set (localized on the fragment plus the quantum bath). A method to combine DMET with CASSCF to better explore active space usage and reduce computation cost exists, but it is beyond the scope of this study. We have, however, performed DMET-CASCI calculations, using the FCI solver available in Tangelo, considering the same active spaces employed for DMET-CC-VQE-CC cases to verify the robustness of the results in a scenario free of the common problems observed in quantum variational algorithms, such as VQE.

The usage of a small active space combined with a minimal basis set brings a limitation for accuracy and potential comparison with experimental or state-of-the-art classical-computing quantum chemistry results. We verified the impact of these limitations by performing noiseless simulations for larger basis set and active spaces, and the results are briefly discussed and available in the Supporting Information (SI).

As for the ansatz, two cases were considered: The first was the ideal case, where the physics-chemistry-inspired UCCSD ansatz was utilized. UCCSD is recognized as a very accurate ansatz, providing results that may surpass classical computing quantum chemistry results obtained with the CCSD method on which it is based. Therefore, as a benchmark, we performed a DMET-CC:VQE:CC calculation, where UCCSD was used as the ansatz for the VQE calculation. This calculation aimed to verify the potential improvement provided by quantum computing under ideal conditions.

As the number of quantum gates involved in UCCSD is prohibitive for current quantum devices, even when using a small active space, we analyzed the approximations needed to perform calculations using real hardware. For this, we performed noiseless simulations using the Hardware Efficient Ansatz (HEA). HEA was designed to fit within the limitations of the current NISQ devices. HEA achieves this by employing a single layer of 1-qubit gates followed by a layer of entangled 2-qubit gates. The entire circuit can be repeated (the so-called “repetition layers”, here denoted by L), increasing the expressibility of the ansatz (and potential accuracy of the results), but also resulting in a deeper (and computationally demanding) circuit to be executed.

For the present study, we employed the HEA circuit available in Tangelo 0.4.0, illustrated in Figure S4, considering a 4-qubit example. Each qubit is equipped with arbitrary-angle rotation gates around the Z, X, and Y axes, followed by CNOT gates that entangle the qubits. While HEA is NISQ-friendly, the low number of quantum gates in the ansatz comes at the expense of not considering the physical properties. For instance, there are no guarantees that basic quantities, such as the particle number, will be conserved. For initial parameters, we employed the common strategy of using random numbers. Tests with different seeds showed that the results would be resilient to stochastic errors (Table S2), as a VQE calculation is performed every DMET step, and the system is influenced by the other components calculated with classical computing approaches. In recent years, more robust initialization techniques have been proposed, such as machine-learning-based initial parameters or the use of oo-pCCD. The use of more sophisticated initialization strategies can help to improve VQE performance, enhancing convergence or contributing to avoiding energy landscape issues or the barren-plateau problem, − but this discussion is beyond the scope of the present work.

Therefore, we carefully evaluated the impact caused by changes from the theoretical ideal condition (UCCSD ansatz, SLSQP optimizer, (4e, 4o) active space) to the actual hardware experiment (HEA, COBYLA optimizer, minimal active space) as well as the influence and cost of L, establishing an accurate estimation for the results before conducting the experiments.

Our analysis revolves mainly around the coefficient of determination, R 2. This coefficient is commonly employed as a statistical measure for assessing the predictive accuracy of computational models of protein–ligand binding energy against experimental results. The R 2 coefficient quantifies the proportion of variance in experimental data that can be explained by the model, thus, offering a metric for model performance. In other words, the coefficient is obtained from a linear fitting of a plot of calculated binding energies versus experimental data, which, in our case, are enthalpy values. An R 2 coefficient closer to 1 indicates a better prediction alignment, whereas a value closer to zero suggests a poor predictive capability.

Experiments with Real Quantum Hardware and Simulations Including Noise Models

We performed single-point energy calculations using an actual quantum computer. Specifically, we used a 64-qubit superconducting quantum computer developed by RIKEN RQC–Fujitsu Collaboration Center. This system operates at an ultralow temperature (around 20 millikelvin) and utilizes microwave signals in the 8 GHz range to control the qubits.

Considering the limitations of 4 qubits and the machine time available, our estimations indicated that full calculations starting from a trial wave function calculated with classical-computing quantum chemistry methods and having VQE converge interactively to the optimized wave function parameters for all 5 ligands, both in vacuum and in the presence of the protein, were not feasible. Therefore, we opted to use real hardware to perform single-point calculations only, similar to the approach used in ref for the case of a trapped-ion quantum computer. In other words, we first performed the intended shot-based VQE calculations in a simulator and, after reaching a converged result, we repeated the final iteration of the shot-based VQE calculation using the real hardware solely to recalculate the energy contribution of fragment 2. To accomplish this, we used an in-house-developed transpiler to convert the quantum-circuit-related operations from Tangelo 0.4.0 to Qulacs and perform shot-based VQE calculations on the simulator and real hardware. In all cases, a total of 104 shots was performed.

To reduce possible effects caused by the quantum noise, two commonly used error mitigation strategies were employed: zero noisy extrapolation (ZNE) − and readout error mitigation (ROEM). ZNE is a technique where a part of or the whole quantum circuit is extended to scale the noise level. Specifically, we employed the commonly used approach of a two-point extrapolation, by performing the calculation on the intended quantum circuit (HEA) and on a modified version of it with 3-fold the number of CNOT gates. This modification is assumed to cause a proportional increase in quantum noise, allowing for an estimation of a theoretical zero-noise condition by using linear regression. This procedure was repeated 10 times, and the average values were used to calculate the estimates. ZNE was performed for all ligands, both in a vacuum and in a protein.

For ROEM, we used a functionality available in the Fujitsu Quantum Platform that analyzes and corrects the error based on a filter matrix and pseudoinversion. This was applied to all ligands except for 3dhk. This was the largest ligand, and as will be clear from our results, it showed significant sensitivity to method change, so the data were acquired first, at a time before the implementation of ROEM in our platform. Without additional computational time, we could not acquire a new data set for it.

The combination of the two techniques provided a robust data set, comparable to state vector simulations, with the highest difference in the calculated energy of fragment 2 being no larger than 0.01%.

To understand how noise would affect the results if more shots were performed or larger active spaces were employed, we used noisy simulations. For this, we employed a wrapper available in Tangelo 0.4.0 that allows the utilization of noisy gates implemented in Qulacs. Due to the computational cost involved, we opted for the same strategy used for calculations performed on quantum hardware: after having converged DMET results for the ligand in vacuum and in protein, we used the last VQE state obtained in the final iteration of DMET and recalculated the energy using noise gates available in the Qulacs library. This approach allows us to discuss the noise effect on the energy of fragment 2, and consequently, the shift that the total energy of the system and the binding energy would undergo.

The noise model was based on depolarization, with the probability parameter set as 0.005 for 1-qubit gates. As 2-qubit gates are known to be more susceptible to the noise, we used a parameter 4 times larger, that is, 0.02. A test of the parameter dependence was conducted for the smallest ligand and active space, showing that for a given fixed number of shots, there is an increase in the shift caused by the noise that is proportional to the parameter values (Table S3). For all of the ligands, two cases were considered. First, for the smallest active spaces, (2e, 2o) and (4e, 4o), where the noisy simulations presented a reasonable computational cost, five runs of shot-based VQE with a total of 100 000 shots each were performed, providing good accuracy and error estimation. For the two sets of (8e, 6o) active spaces, the estimation based on a small number of shots was that each ligand would require approximately two months of calculation if the same setup was used; therefore, we performed only one run with 20 000 shots to obtain a rough estimation of the noise in these cases.

Results and Discussion

Classical Computing Results and Ideal Quantum Computing Case

We begin by discussing the results of classical computing quantum chemistry calculations. Figure shows the relation between energy values (kcal/mol) calculated with different methods and experimental data for the enthalpy change ΔH (kcal/mol) , of the five ligands and the respective coefficient of determination R 2.

Binding energy correlation obtained using classical-computing quantum chemistry methods: (a) HF/STO–3G, (b) CCSD/STO–3G, (c) DMET-CCSD/STO–3G.

First, the HF method yields a value of R 2 = 0.724 (Figure A). Given that HF is the fundamental method for solving electronic structure problems using wave functions, but it completely lacks electronic correlation due to its mean-field nature and single-determinant approach, the results are within the expected range.

To estimate the basis set effect, we performed HF calculations using several Pople basis sets, including diffuse and polarization functions. As expected, the energy values for the ligands in vacuum or interacting with the protein decrease. However, since the binding energy is an energy difference, this decrease largely cancels out, with values changing by 1 to 2 kcal/mol from STO–3G to 6–31G and less than 1 kcal/mol from 6–31G to 6–311G* (Table S4). Adding diffuse functions to heavy atoms (6–31+G, 6–311+G*) or to all atoms (6–311++G*) also presents a small effect, changing the binding energy by 0.5 kcal/mol or less (Table S5) compared to their counterparts without diffuse functions. Therefore, the energy trend among the ligands is mostly preserved, with only 2zgb and 3dhk, which have very close binding energies, reversing their positions. This significantly increases R 2, as was also expected by using more accurate basis sets (Figure S5). Hence, as we are dealing with energy differences, it is possible to have a semiquantitative discussion, even with a minimal basis set, while keeping in mind that the basis set effect is around 3 kcal/mol or less and that the R 2 could be ∼10% larger.

Next, by introducing dynamic electronic correlation using the CCSD method, we obtained R 2 = 0.737 (Figure b). It is worth noting that this is a standard CCSD calculation performed without the use of any active space. As expected, introducing electronic correlation improved the results. However, in this particular case, the improvement is marginal considering the increasing computational cost involved. As for the basis set effect, the trend is similar to that observed in the HF case (Table S6).

Next, we applied DMET, dividing the ligands into 3 fragments and using CCSD for each of them (DMET-CCSD), and obtained a promising result of R 2 = 0.761 (Figure c). In this case, as well, no active space was used. The result is very close to the one obtained using the same quantum chemistry method for the entire system, but the fragmentation strategy can be naturally employed to improve the computational performance. The fact that both CCSD and DMET-CCSD results are similar also indicates that the chosen fragmentation is adequate and that boundary effects, if any, are negligible or, at least, should not have a negative impact on the results.

It is noteworthy that the binding energy of the 3dhk ligand presents the largest changes among all ligands, indicating a clear dependence on the calculation method employed. The 3dhk ligand is also the largest ligand considered herein, with fragment 3 being composed of two phenyl groups. This change in the energy value has a considerable impact on the binding energy coefficient values, and as we will see in the following, it is even more significant when using the VQE algorithm.

Having verified the suitability of the fragmentation and of the DMET results, we proceeded to quantum computing calculations using DMET-CC:VQE:CC. We first obtained a benchmark for the desired calculation using quantum computing in the ideal case and then moved to approximations aimed at decreasing the computational cost. The results are summarized as a heatmap shown in Figure . For the ideal case, we performed a DMET-CC:VQE:CC calculation where the VQE algorithm with the UCCSD ansatz and SLSQP optimizer was used to calculate the energy of Fragment 2 with a minimal active space (labeled “DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[UCCSD, SLSQP]”) and a (4e, 4o) active space (“DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[UCCSD, SLSQP]”).

A minimal active space in principle seems not to be enough to obtain a satisfactory coefficient, as the value of 0.639 is even smaller than the value obtained with HF. The 3dhk ligand is particularly problematic in this case, as its binding energy obtained (∼−82 kcal/mol) is considerably higher than the HF value (∼−85 kcal/mol), but all ligands present binding energy values that exceed HF. It is worth mentioning that, as one would expect, the energy values of the ligand both in vacuum and when interacting with the protein are smaller than the respective HF counterpart. However, since the binding energy is calculated as a difference, slight differences in the correlation energy obtained with DMET-CC:VQE:CC in each case (vacuum and protein) may lead to higher binding energy values than those obtained with HF.

Increasing the active space to (4e, 4o) alleviated the issue and greatly improved the results, with a value of R 2 = 0.762, a coefficient slightly larger than the DMET-CCSD result, showing that the quantum algorithm can reproduce or even improve the classical computing result.

For an active space composed of (8e, 6o), two cases were tested. In one, we used the additional orbitals localized in the hydrogen atoms (Figure S3a, left), while in the other, we selected p-like orbitals localized on oxygen and carbonyl (Figure S3a, right). For the first case, the coefficient is considerably worse, with 4 ligands presenting energy values higher than the corresponding HF values, and 2zgb presenting an excessively low energy value­(∼−101 kcal/mol). In the second case, the coefficient value is close to the CCSD result. This highlights the limitations when combining active space with DMET due to the necessity of using localized and bath orbitals instead of canonical ones. If the use of canonical orbitals were possible, systematic strategies to select orbitals that contribute most to dynamic correlation, as presented in ref , would be possible. Based on chemical reasoning only, a suitable and relatively small active space to describe Fragment 2 would be composed of the π and π* orbitals of the carbonyl group, the oxygen lone pair, and the σ* of the NH3 + group. When using DMET, however, such strategies are not feasible, as we have a mix of occupied localized orbitals and delocalized bath orbitals, and become less intuitive to reasonably orient the choice only on chemical reasoning.

To verify how the different active spaces impact the results without the usual limitations observed in calculations performed using variational quantum algorithms such as VQE, we performed calculations using the DMET-CC-CASCI-CC approach, that is, replacing the VQE solver with the FCI solver available in Tangelo. As one can observe in Figure , DMET-CC-CASCI-CC and DMET-CC-VQE-CC/UCCSD results agree very well for the minimal active space (2e, 2o) and for the (8e, 6o)-case 2, and also reasonably well for (4e, 4o). The only significant divergence is the problematic (8e, 6o)-case 1, which is therefore a poor choice for the active space. Neglecting this last case, we can see that the values for R 2 consistently increase as the active space increases, from 0.638 to 0.730, and finally to 0.737.

In the end, very accurate DMET results would demand the entire set shown in Figure S2, which is unfeasible because of the VQE cost. Therefore, we moved with the DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[UCCSD, SLSQP] case as potentially the best cost-benefit for our study, within the limitations imposed by the basis set and active space employed.

Approximations Aiming at Calculations in Real Hardware

After benchmarking the binding energy results with the quantum algorithm in a controlled setting provided by noiseless simulations for an ideal scenario, we analyzed necessary approximations to enable calculations on real hardware, considering the resources (time and number of qubits) available. We considered two aspects: (i) the ansatz used for the quantum circuit and (ii) the optimizer used to find the parameters of the VQE wave function. We also analyzed the influence of the active space, as we were limited to 4 qubits, meaning that only 2 molecular orbitals would be included in the active space, and as discussed above, at least for the UCCSD ansatz, such a minimal active space was not enough to correctly describe the binding energy correlation. For the sake of conciseness, we restricted our discussion to repetition layers up to L = 3, but results for L = 4 and 5, as well as individual plots for all cases, are presented in the SI.

First, we analyzed the change of the ansatz, from UCCSD to HEA. Figure shows the DMET-CC:VQE:CC results obtained using HEA for both active spaces considered. Interestingly, the results for DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 2, SLSQP] and DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 3, SLSQP] showed an improvement over the results obtained using the DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[UCCSD, SLSQP], suggesting that the UCCSD results with the minimal active space were affected by the barren-plateau problem or were trapped in a point of the energy landscape. Meanwhile, DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 2, SLSQP] and DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 3, SLSQP] yielded better binding energy values for all the ligands and R 2 values of 0.729 and 0.718, respectively, which are more in line with values obtained with classical-computing quantum chemistry methods. Excessive flexibility of the ansatz, with more degrees of freedom than the wave function of the system actually possesses, does not contribute to improving the energy results for the ground state of a molecular system. Particularly, simply increasing the L in the HEA may lead to excessive entanglement of the system, resulting in a state that is highly complex and difficult to optimize. This can be observed when L is increased in calculations involving HEA (Figure S7).

By increasing the active space size, the result obtained using DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[HEA, L = 2, SLSQP] is now less accurate than the result obtained with DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[UCCSD, SLSQP], as one would expect. Increasing L, the results obtained with DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[HEA, L = 3, SLSQP] seem to improve R 2, but this is just because the binding energy of ligand 3dhk presents an excessively low value of ∼−104 kcal/mol (Figure ). As aforementioned, the ligand 3dhk has the largest fragment 3 (two phenyl groups), and its presence as a quantum bath when the HEA had more flexibility seems to have led to an excessive correlation between the fragments that greatly affected the binding energy, a trend confirmed by increasing L (Figure S8).

From these results, we can draw two major conclusions: First, for the small active spaces here considered, L > 2 is not beneficial. Second, unlike the UCCSD case, by employing HEA, the results for the minimal active space (2e, 2o) are reasonable and close to the value obtained with (4e, 4o), with both being close to the HF results. Therefore, the use of a minimal active space for DMET-CC:VQE:CC, despite not being ideal, could be regarded as reasonable, although results are expected to be just marginally better than HF values.

The second point to be addressed is the choice of the optimizer. SLSQP, like most optimizers, relies on gradients to search for the minimum. However, in NISQ devices, due to the quantum noise, this would be unsuitable as the derivative of a noisy function can amplify the effects of the noise. Therefore, optimizers that use limited or no information from gradients would be more appropriate so as not to increase the quantum noise in the results. In this regard, we opted for the COBYLA optimizer, which was implemented in Tangelo 0.4.0.

Figure shows the results obtained using the COBYLA optimizer and the minimal active space, with HEA and L = 1, 2, and 3, and (4e, 4o) with L = 2. First, for DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 1, COBYLA], the results are essentially the same as HF values (differences of 10–4 kcal/mol), consequently, the resulting R 2 coefficient is also the same as obtained with HF. Increasing L, DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 2, COBYLA] yielded a R 2 coefficient larger than DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 2, SLSQP], as a consequence of 2zc9 presenting an unusually lower binding energy value (lower than CCSD and DMET-CCSD results).

Using a larger active space, DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[HEA, L = 2, COBYLA] yielded consistent results, with the binding energy values for 4 ligands being slightly lower than those obtained with HF, while only 3dux yielded a value slightly larger than that of HF (by 0.4 kcal/mol). Because of this, the R 2 coefficient considerably improved, with a value closer to the DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[UCCSD, SLSQP] result. Therefore, if 8 qubits were available, the preferable setup for calculations on real hardware would be DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[HEA, L = 2, COBYLA].

For a 4-qubit scenario, L = 2 presented the highest coefficient, but closer inspection of the heatmap in Figure reveals that this is a consequence of 2zc9 presenting an unusually lower binding value (in fact, lower than CCSD and DMET), while 2zfp and 2zgb presented values higher than HF. Therefore, the good coefficient is just a consequence of an error cancellation. In addition, even being a modest circuit, our performance tests indicated that using 2 layers would increase calculation time in 41% compared to 1 layer, so we opted to perform calculations using DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 1, COBYLA], which are consistently close to HF. Even if the significance is limited in terms of potential improvements over HF results, the calculation works as a benchmark of the hardware in development and allows us to discuss potential limitations when using real machines. It also enabled us to verify the correct functioning of processes necessary for the proper calculation such as the execution of a transpiler from Tangelo to Qulacs and the efficiency of implemented error mitigation strategies.

It is reasonable to suppose that if ROEM had been applied, the value would be closer to the noiseless simulation results.

Among the ligands studied, 3dhk consistently exhibited the largest sensitivity to the choice of the computational method. In classical calculations, its binding energy varies significantly depending on the level of theory. In noiseless DMET-CC:VQE:CC simulations, it was particularly affected by the choice of ansatz and active space, with HEA introducing excessive correlation when a larger L was employed. Finally, in the real hardware experiments, 3dhk showed relatively small fluctuations, although the absence of ROEM may have limited the accuracy of the result. These findings indicate that 3dhk serves as a stringent test case for evaluating methodological robustness and highlight the importance of carefully balancing approximations and error mitigation strategies in NISQ era quantum simulations.

Overall, considering the approximations made in order to perform the calculations within resource constraints, the results are very promising. First, the values, despite deviating from the noiseless simulations due to the limited number of shots used for VQE, are still consistent. The quantum noise effect was also satisfactorily mitigated.

Second, from the simulations, we observed that the noise-induced shift presents similar values for the ligand in a vacuum and in a protein, but they are not identical. As expected, in all cases, the noise contributes to increasing the energy of fragment 2, meaning that the noise-shifted DMET energy is less negative than that of the ideal case calculated with state vector simulations. The effect on the binding energy, however, depends on whether the noise is larger for the ligand in vacuum or for the ligand in protein. Similar to the results obtained using real hardware, for an active space of (2e, 2o), all five ligands presented a slightly larger noise-induced shift in vacuum than in protein, which consequently resulted in a more negative binding energy.

Interestingly, for the (4e, 4o) active space, the noise is larger in vacuum for only 3 of the ligands. In addition, as expected, the noise-induced shift increases as the active space increases, since the number of qubits becomes larger, consequently increasing the number of quantum gates. Finally, with 5 runs of 100 000 shots, the noise-induced shift in the binding energy is reasonable for the minimal active space, with all values being below 1 kcal/mol, except for the 3dux case. This indicates that for an active space of (4e, 4o), the number of runs or shots might be insufficient. For (8e, 6o), where the simulation cost is exponentially higher, a test performed using a single run of just 20 000 shots results in a very large noise-induced shift (Table S7).

Nonetheless, in the future, with (early) fault-tolerant quantum hardware, which will present negligible levels of noise, a DMET-based approach could be used to obtain the energy of more complex systems, for instance, a ligand interacting with an active site involving a metal, which typically demands large active spaces that are computationally intensive for classical hardware.

Conclusions

In this study, we analyzed the performance and potential applicability of quantum algorithms for studying protein–ligand systems. For this, we calculated the binding energy correlation of 5 ligands with thrombin as a test case.

To overcome this, we employed the decomposition method DMET combined with the VQE hybrid quantum algorithm. In order to perform the calculations, the ligand systems were divided into three fragments. The energy of one of the fragments was calculated using VQE, while the energy of the remaining fragments was calculated using CCSD with the protein treated as point charges located at the respective atomic sites. By performing state-vector-based noiseless simulations, we evaluated the results under ideal conditions using the state-of-the-art UCCSD ansatz and the SLSQP optimizer and confirmed that for such an ideal condition, the strategy based on the quantum algorithm can potentially provide results as good as or even better than classical-computing calculations.

After, we analyzed the approximations necessary for performing the calculation on real hardware, considering resource constraints (time and number of qubits). We confirmed through noiseless simulations that HEA, with just 1 or 2 repetition layers, would be sufficient to describe the main aspects of our problem and that the use of COBYLA as the optimizer was suitable. Ideally, we found that DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(4e, 4o)/[HEA, L = 2, COBYLA] would be the most appropriate choice for real hardware calculations, meaning an 8-qubit calculation. However, as we had only 4 qubits available, we opted instead to perform DMET-CC:VQE:CC­(2e, 2o)/[HEA, L = 1, COBYLA], as the simulations showed that such a setup would be able to at least slightly improve HF results.

By performing single-point calculations and applying error mitigation strategies based on ZNE and ROEM, we found that within the employed approximations, consistent results were obtained, and the quantum noise was effectively mitigated. Noisy simulations showed that, for small active spaces and reasonable fidelity, the noise-induced shift, despite being considerable for both the in-vacuum and in-protein cases, presented similar values, so the effect on the binding energy was relatively smaller.

With additional resources, calculations for system sizes not achievable with classical hardware are expected, making quantum computing a promising technology to be added to the CADD pipeline once quantum computers with more qubits and better fidelity are developed.
