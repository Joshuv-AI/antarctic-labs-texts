Leveraging the Rural-Urban Commuting Area Tool to Address Geographic Disparities in Cancer Care: A Dual-Application Framework for Institutional and National Initiatives
Authors: Meredith C.B. Adams, Cody L. Hudson, Matthew L. Perkins, Robert W. Hurley, Umit Topaloglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEWe developed and validated a dual-purpose, open-access Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) tool to standardize geographic coding for cancer disparities research, addressing National Institutes of Health (NIH) Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) Initiative Common Data Element requirements while supporting institutional catchment area analyses.METHODSThis web-based tool16 integrates US Department of Agriculture RUCA codes with census tract data and electronic health record systems, meeting NIH HEAL Initiative Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) data ecosystem requirements. We implemented the tool using Wake Forest Cancer Center's 2023 registry data (n = 21,219) and conducted systematic comparison with county-level Rural-Urban Continuum Code (RUCC) classifications using 18,714 cancer cases across 336 ZIP codes, focusing on breast, colon, and lung cancers to demonstrate enhanced geographic granularity.RESULTSAmong 21,219 patients with cancer, 19.51% (n = 4,140) resided in rural areas, with 4.81% (n = 1,022) in the most rural census tracts (RUCA codes 7-10). Comparative analysis revealed 9.4% disagreement between RUCA and RUCC classifications, affecting 1,765 patients. Twenty-eight ZIP codes classified as rural by RUCA were located within metropolitan counties according to RUCC, encompassing 109 patients with cancer who would be misclassified using county-level measures. As a separate use case, integration with NIH HEAL Initiative standardized rurality data collection across 15 research studies.CONCLUSIONThe RUCA tool addresses critical gaps in geographic data standardization by providing census tract-level precision that county-level classifications miss. This dual-application framework aligns institutional catchment analyses with national standardization efforts, identifying 109 patients with cancer who would be misclassified as urban residents using traditional county-level approaches, thereby enhancing targeted interventions for rural cancer care access.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality in the United States, with outcomes significantly influenced by geographic factors affecting access to screening, timely diagnosis, and quality treatment.1-3 Rural populations face disproportionate barriers to specialized cancer care, resulting in later-stage diagnoses, lower participation in clinical trials, and poorer survival outcomes.4 These disparities represent a critical challenge to achieving the National Institutes of Health (NIH) National Cancer Institute's (NCI) goal of ensuring equitable cancer care for all Americans.1,5,6

The NIH has increasingly prioritized geospatial approaches to quantify and address access gaps, developing resources such as the GIS Portal and State Cancer Profiles.7,8 Concurrently, the NIH Helping to End Addiction Long-term (HEAL) Initiative has established Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) codes as a required Common Data Element (CDE) to standardize rurality assessment in pain management and substance use disorder research.9-11 Despite these advances, significant challenges remain in operationalizing geospatial tools for frontline cancer research and clinical applications.6,12,13

Two critical unmet needs persist in this domain. First, cancer centers face institutional challenges in defining their catchment areas and developing targeted interventions to address geographic disparities within these regions.14 Second, accessible, interoperable tools that facilitate RUCA integration into cancer research workflows and clinical trial management are limited.15 These gaps hamper efforts to systematically assess and address rural cancer disparities at both institutional and national levels.

We present a novel RUCA tool16 developed for dual application: supporting Wake Forest Cancer Center's catchment area analysis and equity interventions and enabling NIH HEAL Initiative clinical trials to comply with CDE requirements while facilitating cross-study analyses of geographic factors affecting health outcomes. We describe the tool's development, validation through systematic comparison with county-level classifications, and implementation across institutional and national contexts.

METHODS

Tool Development

The RUCA tool was developed as a web-based application using a modern software architecture that prioritizes accessibility, interoperability, and data security. Data sources include United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) RUCA codes (version 3.10), US Census Bureau tract boundaries, and geocoding capabilities integrated with electronic health record systems. The tool integrates USDA RUCA codes (version 3.10) with US Census Bureau tract boundaries and geocoding capabilities.

Data Privacy and Regulatory Compliance

Data collection and analysis were conducted under Wake Forest University School of Medicine IRB protocol IRB00131029. The system geocodes zip codes and retains only census tract identifiers, maintaining compliance with 45 CFR §164.514. For institutions requiring enhanced privacy protections, code availability to create a local deployment option eliminates external data transmission while maintaining full functionality.

Public Access and Implementation

The RUCA tool is freely available16 with open-source code repositories.17,18 Researchers can access the tool through a web-based interface for individual queries or API integration for database connectivity.

Dual Application

Institutional Implementation

The Wake Forest Cancer Registry systematically collects data for all patients diagnosed or treated at Advocate Health sites, following American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer and North Carolina Central Cancer Registry standards. The registry encompasses over 50,000 annual cases across a six-state region, providing comprehensive data on patient demographics, tumor characteristics, staging, treatment, and outcomes.

We applied the RUCA tool to map Wake Forest Cancer Center's catchment area using 2023 patient-level data, quantifying geographic distribution and identifying clusters with limited access to specialized cancer services. Analysis focused on breast, colon, and lung cancers as common cancer types that facilitated data deidentification.

National Implementation

For NIH HEAL Initiative trials, the tool was disseminated through the HEAL Data Ecosystem within the IMPOWR and MIRHIQL networks and broadly through the NIH HEAL CDE program.10 The tool's API supports automated RUCA code assignment through REDCap databases.

Validation

We conducted systematic validation using Wake Forest Cancer Center's 2023 registry data (n = 21,219) and comparative analysis with county-level Rural-Urban Continuum Code (RUCC) classifications. Patient addresses were geocoded and assigned RUCA codes, with results stratified by rural-urban classification following Federal Office of Rural Health Policy recommendations.

RUCA Versus RUCC Comparative Analysis

To demonstrate enhanced granularity over traditional county-level measures, we conducted systematic comparison between RUCA and RUCC classifications using cancer registry data encompassing 18,714 cancer cases across 336 ZIP codes in our catchment area, focusing on breast, colon, and lung cancers as representative common cancer types. RUCA codes 7-10 represent increasingly rural areas with substantial health care access challenges, following Federal Office of Rural Health Policy definitions.

Comparative analysis with county-level RUCC classifications revealed important methodological differences (Table 3). Among 18,714 cancer cases across 336 ZIP codes, RUCA and RUCC agreed in 90.6% of cases but disagreed for 1,765 patients (9.4%). Disagreement rates varied by cancer type (Table 4), with lung cancer showing 11.87% disagreement, colon cancer showing 9.94% disagreement, and breast cancer showing 7.44% disagreement. Most significantly, 28 ZIP codes were classified as rural by RUCA but located within metropolitan counties according to RUCC, affecting 109 patients with cancer who would be misclassified using county-level measures (Table 5).

The detailed RUCA code distribution demonstrates significant heterogeneity within broad urban classifications (Table 2). While 68.46% of patients reside in metropolitan area cores (RUCA code 1) with minimal geographic barriers, those in micropolitan areas (codes 4-6, totaling 14.69%) face moderate access challenges despite urban classification. Patients in RUCA codes 7-10 encounter substantial barriers including greater travel distances, limited local health care infrastructure, potential treatment delays, and challenges with follow-up care coordination.

NIH HEAL Initiative Integration

The tool was implemented across 15 NIH HEAL Initiative research studies for standardized rurality data collection.

DISCUSSION

The RUCA tool supports NCI's geographic equity goals by providing standardized methods to quantify access barriers. Our analysis identified 109 patients with cancer who would be misclassified using county-level measures, demonstrating the tool's potential value for targeting interventions to underserved communities. The tool's integration capabilities could support improved rural recruitment in clinical trials.

The dual-application framework shows alignment between institutional and national priorities through shared infrastructure. Cancer centers can leverage the same tool for catchment area reporting while contributing to broader research initiatives requiring standardized geographic data and creating opportunities for multilevel interventions addressing rural cancer disparities.

Tables 1 and 2 show that while 80.49% of patients reside in areas classified as urban (RUCA 1-6), this aggregate masks important gradients in access to care. Nearly 15% of patients live in micropolitan areas (RUCA 4-6), which, despite an urban designation, often face moderate barriers such as longer travel distances to radiation oncology, fewer local subspecialists, and reduced access to clinical trials.

The 4.81% of patients in the most rural categories (RUCA 7-10) likely experience compounded challenges: geographic isolation, transportation limitations, and constrained local health care infrastructure. RUCA codes 7-10 specifically capture small town cores, small towns with high commuting patterns, and completely rural areas—populations most vulnerable to treatment delays, care fragmentation, and poor follow-up adherence.

It is important to emphasize that the lower proportion of rural patients in our registry does not indicate a lower cancer incidence in rural communities. Rather, it likely reflects access barriers, travel time, financial burden, and referral limitations, which reduce the likelihood of rural patients receiving diagnosis or treatment at comprehensive cancer centers. This under-representation underscores the need for targeted outreach and innovative care delivery models.

Our comparative analysis highlights the limitations of county-level RUCC in identifying rural patients with cancer (Tables 3-5). Although the overall agreement between RUCA and RUCC was 90.6% at the case level, 1,765 patients (9.4%) received discordant classifications. Notably, 28 ZIP codes classified as rural by RUCA were embedded within metropolitan counties according to RUCC, encompassing 109 patients with cancer who would be misclassified as urban in county-level analyses.

Disagreement rates varied by cancer type, with lung cancer showing the highest divergence (11.87%), followed by colon (9.94%) and breast cancer (7.44%). These differences suggest that geographic access barriers may differentially affect diagnostic and treatment patterns across cancer types, with implications for disease-specific outreach strategies.

Such findings demonstrate that census tract-level RUCA classification is methodologically superior to county-level approaches for identifying at-risk populations, especially rural pockets within otherwise urban-classified counties.

The tool's census tract-level approach provides enhanced granularity over county-level classifications. By serving both institutional catchment analyses and national standardization needs, the tool supports more precise rural-urban classification than binary approaches.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the tool relies on census tract boundaries, which may not always reflect patient-perceived barriers or community boundaries. While RUCA codes offer more granularity than county-level measures, they still represent administrative rather than experiential geography. Second, the current implementation has limited international applicability as it relies on US-specific census and USDA data structures. Finally, the tool captures geographic barriers but does not directly incorporate other social determinants of health that may interact with rurality to influence cancer outcomes.
