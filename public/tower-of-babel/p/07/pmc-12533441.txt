Targeting SHP-1 to alleviate testicular inflammation and apoptosis in a Poly(I:C)-induced orchitis model
Authors: Shimin Wang, Jianing Tong, Bicheng Jin, Qiong Lei, Qingliang Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrchitis, an inflammation of the testes primarily caused by viral infections such as mumps, presents a significant threat to male reproductive health. This study explores the role of Src homology 2 (SH2)-containing tyrosine phosphatase-1 (SHP-1), a known tumor suppressor, in mitigating inflammation and apoptosis in testicular cells within a model of viral-induced orchitis. To simulate the immune response associated with viral orchitis, we utilized Poly (I:C), a synthetic analog of double-stranded RNA, which mimics the molecular patterns of viral RNA. This model provides a relevant framework for investigating immune responses in the testes triggered by viral-like stimuli. Our research aimed to elucidate the impact of SHP-1 expression on the inflammatory and apoptotic pathways activated during testicular inflammation. We found that Poly (I:C) induced significant inflammation and apoptosis in Leydig and Sertoli cells, characterized by reduced SHP-1 expression and elevated phosphorylated-STAT3 levels. Enhancing SHP-1 expression attenuated these inflammatory and apoptotic responses, whereas reactivating STAT3 with colivelin reversed the suppression of cytokine production and cell death. Moreover, inhibiting SHP-1 with TPI-1 treatment post-Poly (I:C) administration significantly exacerbated testicular inflammation and apoptosis, underscoring SHP-1’s critical protective role. Introduction

The role of Src homology 2 (SH2)-containing tyrosine phosphatases, particularly SHP-1 and SHP-2, in regulating immune responses is well-established in oncology [1–3], but less understood in testicular disorders such as orchitis. Orchitis, an inflammation of the testes triggered by infections, trauma, or autoimmune reactions, significantly impacts male fertility by disrupting testicular immune privilege [4, 5], which is crucial for sperm protection by creating an immunologically tolerant environment that shields Germ cells from systemic immune attacks [6]. SHP-1 and SHP-2 play key roles in modulating inflammatory signaling within testicular immune responses [7]. SHP-1 is predominantly expressed in hematopoietic and epithelial cells. It acts as a negative regulator by dephosphorylating components of the JAK/STAT pathway, thereby attenuating immune activation [8, 9].

This is particularly relevant in orchitis, where SHP-1 expression inversely correlates with disease severity. SHP-2, though less directly linked to inflammation, regulates developmental processes that might influence testicular health [10, 11]. Understanding the breakdown of testicular immune privilege is critical, as this underlies orchitis pathology [12]. The testes typically create a barrier that isolates sperm antigens from the immune system, preventing autoimmune reactions [13, 14]. However, infections and trauma can compromise this barrier, triggering immune attacks on sperm cells. These attacks are characterized by elevated inflammatory cytokines and T-cell infiltration, which in turn lead to reduced fertility or infertility [15–17].

Our research uses a polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid (Poly(I:C))-induced orchitis model, simulating viral infection to explore SHP-1 and STAT3’s regulatory effects on testicular inflammation and injury [18, 19]. Poly(I:C) mimics immunological stress from viral infections known to precipitate orchitis [20]. We study SHP-1 and phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) expression across testicular cell types, including non-induced and Poly(I:C)-stimulated Leydig, Sertoli, and Germ cells [21, 22], to understand how changes in SHP-1 and STAT3 function influence orchitis pathophysiology.

This analysis aims to provide insights into the interactions between SHP-1 and STAT3 in the testicular immune environment, identifying potential therapeutic targets for preserving male fertility amid autoimmune and inflammatory challenges [23–25]. By elucidating how these phosphatases regulate testicular inflammation and apoptosis, our findings may lead to novel interventions that restore immune privilege and protect against orchitis-induced fertility issues.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from The Experimental Animal Center (Beijing, China). They were housed in a controlled environment with regulated humidity, temperature, and a 12:12 h light–dark cycle. Food and water were available ad libitum. All animal experiments followed the guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocol was approved by the Canadian Council on Animal Care (CCAC) of Guizhou Electric Power Workers Hospital.

Isolation and preparation of the different testicular cells

Leydig and Sertoli cells were isolated from 3-week-old C57BL/6 male mice, and Germ cells from 5 weeks, following established protocols [26, 27]. The testes of three mice were decapsulated and subsequently exposed with 0.5 mg/mL type I collagenase (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)-F12 (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) for 15 min. To isolate Leydig cells within the spermatogenic tubule, the suspension was filtered through an 80-um copper mesh. DMEM-F12 was used to culture these Leydig cells, which included 100 mg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 10% fetal calf serum (Life Technologies), for a duration of 24 h and incubated in a humidified atmosphere composed of 95% air and 5% CO2 at 32 °C. After 24 h of incubation, spermatogenic cells and cell debris were removed by washing twice with PBS. Leydig cells that adhere to culture dishes were used in this study. Following this, although the Leydig cells were isolated in 0.125% trypsin for 5 min, despite staining the cells with 3-b-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-HSD), a marker for Leydig cells, the testicular macrophages did not separate. it was revealed that the purity of the Leydig cells exceeded 92% [28].

According to the reference, Immunostaining with F4/80, a macrophage marker, revealed that the Leydig cell preparation had a macrophage ratio of less than 5%. It is possible that there were a few other cell contaminants, such as vascular and fibroblasts endothelial cells [25]. At room temperature, type I collagenase eliminated the peritubular myoid cells from the seminiferous tubules. Following slicing into 1-mm pieces, the microtubules were then exposed to 0.5 mg/mL hyaluronidase (Sigma) for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, using gentle pipetting, the Sertoli cells were separated from the Germ cell. It was then retrieved from DMEM-F12 suspension and germ cells were isolated by collecting non-adherent cells after 6 h of incubation, during which Sertoli cells rapidly adhered to the culture dish. After collection, Germ cells were further cultured for 24 h in serum-free medium to confirm the absence of residual adherent cell contamination (e.g., Sertoli cells or fibroblasts). Germ cell purity was assessed by nuclear morphology: cells with round nuclei (characteristic of Germ cells) and Sertoli cells with irregular triangular nuclei were distinguished by DAPI staining. Based on their unique morphological characteristics, less Sertoli cells were detected in the Germ cell preparation throughout the culture period [28]. After the Sertoli cells were allowed to incubate for another 24 h, they were then stimulated in a hypotonic solution (pH 7.4, 20 mM Tris-buffered saline (TBS)) for 1 min to remove any non-adhered Germ cell. Afterwards, when immunostaining for WT1 was performed, a marker of Sertoli cells, it was revealed that the Sertoli cells had a purity of over 95% [29].

In vitro testicular inflammation model establishment

The male C57BL/6 mice (5–8 weeks) were acclimatized under standard conditions for 3–7 days, including a 12-h light/dark cycle, controlled temperature, and free access to food and water. Anesthesia was induced with 50 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium before surgically exposing the testes. Following euthanasia, testes were aseptically collected, and Sertoli, Leydig, and Germ cells were isolated and cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic. In the first step for transfection, cells were cultured in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) for 24 h to stimulate cells with 0.3 μg Poly(I:C) and 1 μL Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo) were added to each well, while controls received Lipofectamine 3000 and PBS. As a second step, the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and kept in serum-free DMEM-F12. Samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h post-Poly(I:C) treatment to analyze protein and mRNA levels. QRT-PCR, Western blot, or ELISA were used to quantify orchitis-related factors TNF-α, IL-1β, MCP-1, and IFN-β [25, 30].

In vivo modeling of orchitis in C57BL/6 mice

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were acclimatized for 3–7 days and randomly divided into four groups (n = 3–4/group): (1) PBS control (bilateral intratesticular PBS), (2) orchitis model (0.5 mg/kg poly(I:C) [31] + PBS, Absin #abs812972), (3) inhibitor control (3 mg/kg SHP-1 inhibitor TPI-1 + PBS, Absin #abs812922) [32, 33], and (4) treatment group (poly(I:C) + TPI-1). Orchitis was induced in Groups 2 and 4 via bilateral intratesticular injection of poly(I:C) (0.5 mg/kg in PBS), a synthetic double-stranded RNA mimicking viral infection, on day 0. Starting on day 6, Groups 3 and 4 received daily bilateral intratesticular TPI-1 (3 mg/kg in PBS) for four consecutive days, while controls received equivalent PBS volumes. Eight hours after the final injection, mice were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.) for testicular tissue collection. Tissues were analyzed by H&E staining, TUNEL assay, and Western blotting to evaluate STAT3, p-STAT3, SHP-1, poly(I:C), IL-1β, and IL-10 levels.

Plasmid construction and transfection

Sertoli cells, Leydig cells, and Germ cells were cultured in 6-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) for 24 h and then transfected with either pcDNA3-empty vector or 100 nM pcDNA3-SHP-1 vector(Invitrogen) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo, cat. L3000015). The SHP-1 overexpression vector was constructed using cDNAs obtained from RT-PCR of mouse RNA [30]. Promoter fragments of the mSHP gene (−720 to + 15 bp) were amplified by PCR, ligated into the pGL3-basic plasmid, and cloned into the EcoRI site of pcDNA3. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into One Shot TOP10 competent E. coli cells, propagated, and extracted using the Plasmid Plus Midi Kit (Promega), with insert integrity confirmed by sequencing. After 24 h, the cells were starved in serum-free DMEM-F12 for 2 h for Poly(I:C) stimulation, and then transfected with 2 μg/ml poly(I:C)(double-stranded RNA) using 2 μl Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following this treatment, the cells were incubated with 10 nM colivelin (Tocris, Bristol, U K) for another 24 h.

RNA extraction from tissues or cells

We extracted total RNA from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent, adhering to the protocol supplied by the manufacturer. To eliminate DNA contaminants, the RNA samples were treated with DNase I (Invitrogen, RNase-free). Prior to reverse transcription, we amplified the glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene through 30 cycles of polymerase chain reaction (PCR).Using 2 μg of RNA, 2.5 μM Random Hexamers, 2 μM dNTPs, and 200 U M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase in a 20-μL reaction, the cDNA was successfully synthesized. PCR was performed using the ABI PRISM 7300 Real-Time cycler and the Power SYBR Green Master Mix Kit.In this study, we determined the relative mRNA levels of each target gene as outlined in the No. 2 User Bulletin of Applied Biosystem (P/N 4303859).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

To perform quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis, complementary DNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using a reverse transcription mixture containing 25 hexamers, 2 μM dNTPs, and 200 μM Moloney murine RNA (Promega), followed by PCR amplification on an ABI PRISM 7300 cycler (Applied Biosystems) with 0.2 μL cDNA, 0.5 μL each of forward/reverse primers, and 10 μL Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2 − ΔΔCT method and normalized to GAPDH, with results presented as fold change relative to the control group to enhance data clarity. Genomic DNA contamination was monitored through parallel reactions with GAPDH primers omitting reverse transcriptase and blank controls. A gene-specific standard curve generated from purified PCR products of positive controls was employed for absolute quantification, with standard concentrations verified by A260/A280 spectrophotometry. Primer sequences were derived from previously published works, as indicated in Table 1 [20, 34–36].

Histologic analysis

Testes were fixed in 4% (W/V) paraformaldehyde (Sigma) for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 3-mm slices using a rotary microtome. The process involved reagents such as Xylene (33535, Xilong Science Co., LTD.), anhydrous ethanol (32061, Xilong Science), 95% ethanol (32061, Xilong Science), hematoxylin dye solution (ZLI-9610, Zhongxian Jinqiao), eosin dye solution (G1100, Solarbio), and hematoxylin blue return solution (G1040, Servicebio). Tissue processing utilized a tissue dehydrator (KD-TS3S1, Zhejiang Jinhua Kedi), paraffin embedment machine (Leica), slicer (Leica), and microscope (CX43, OLYMPUS). The sections underwent slicing, baking, dewaxing, hydration, staining, and microscopic examination. Histologic analysis was performed using hematoxylin and eosin staining, as previously described [20].

Western blot analysis

For Western blot, testes or cells were lysed, and protein concentration was measured using BCA kits (Pierce Biotechnology). Proteins (15 μg/sample) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with TBS (pH 7.4) containing 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Bax (Abcam, ab32503), anti-Caspase9 (Abcam, ab202068), anti-GAPDH (Abcam, ab9485), anti-c-Caspase9 (CST, cat.#9509), anti-SHP-1 (Abcam, ab124942), anti-STAT3 (Abcam, ab119352), and anti-p-STAT3 (Abcam, ab76315). Mouse anti-GAPDH (TransGen Biotech, HC301) at 1:2000 served as the loading control, with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (Servicebio, GB23301) as the secondary antibody at 1:2000. Rabbit Anti-IL-1β (Immunoway, YT5201) and Rabbit Anti-IL-10 (Immunoway, YT5138) were used at 1:1000, with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (Servicebio, GB23303) at 1:2000. After rinsing with TBS-Tween, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Antigen–antibody complexes were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence kits (Zhongshan Biotechnology, China). Image J software (version 1.53t, National Institutes of Health, USA) was used for quantitative analysis of the grayscale of Western blot bands.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Cultured cells were stimulated with Poly(I:C) for 12 h to measure IL-1β (Abcam, ab197742), Monocyte Mhemoattractant Protein-1 (MCP-1, Abcam,ab208978), Tumor Necrosis Factor-a (TNF-α, Abcam,ab208348), and Interferon-b (IFN-β, Abcam,ab252362) levels in the supernatant fluid, using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Elisa) from R&D Systems(Minneapolis, MN, USA). The absorbance values at 405 nm were measured with a microplate reader from Bio-Rad Laboratories (Hercules, CA, USA), and as described previously, protein concentrations were determined using standard curves.

TUNEL staining and nuclear localization

Tissue slides were prepared by removing PBS with absorbent paper. TUNEL reaction mixture (Beyotime, C1090) was applied and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in a dark, humidified chamber. After washing with PBS, DAPI (KeyGEN BioTECH, Cat. No: KGA215-50) was added for nuclear staining (2 min in darkness). Slides were dried and sealed with antifade mounting medium. Fluorescence microscopy (DAPI: excitation 330–380 nm/emission 420 nm; CY3 (Biolegend, excitation 510–560 nm/emission 590 nm) was performed to visualize nuclei (blue) and TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells (red). For quantification, apoptotic cells were counted in 10 randomly selected fields per slide using ImageJ software. Both interstitial spaces and seminiferous tubules were analyzed, and the number of TUNEL-positive cells (DAPI-stained) nuclei was calculated.

Flow cytometry

We used Annexin V fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) as apoptosis detection kits (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA) to quantify the number of dead cells. Briefly, harvested cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/sample) were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 100 μL binding buffer. In summary, after mixing each cell suspension with 10 µL of Annexin-V FITC, 20 µL of binding buffer and 5 µL of PI were added and in dark at room temperature for 15 min. Before analysis, 400 μL binding buffer was added to adjust sample volume, and then analyzed using BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer with standard configuration: Annexin V-FITC detected in FL1 channel (530/30 nm) and PI in FL2 channel (585/42 nm). The flow cytometer was then used to count the number of dead cells.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Student’s t test was used to determine the significance between different cell treatments. Statistical analysis of differences among multiple groups was performed using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post-hoc comparisons. The calculations were performed using SPSS version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Significance levels were denoted as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), and p < 0.001 (***). Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software.

Results

Poly(I:C) induces pro-inflammatory responses in mouse testes and testicular cells

To verify the involvement of Poly(I:C) in triggering an inflammatory response in the testes, we examined the levels of pro-inflammatory markers Il-1β, Tnf-α, Mcp-1, and Inf- β in the testes of mice in vitro treated with Poly(I:C), as well as in isolated Sertoli, Leydig, and Germ cells. Significant increases in the mRNA levels of Tnf-α, Il-1β, Mcp-1, and Ifn-β were observed in the Poly(I:C)-treated testes in a time-dependent manner at 2, 4, 8, and 12 h post-injection, compared to untreated testes (Fig. 1A–D). Notably, the mRNA levels of these pro-inflammatory factors peaked at 8 h after Poly(I:C) injection, suggesting successful induction of inflammation in the testes of treated mice. Furthermore, Sertoli, Leydig, and Germ cells were separately transfected with Poly(I:C) to stimulate inflammation. The mRNA expression of the four pro-inflammatory factors increased significantly in Sertoli and Leydig cells at 8 h post-transfection, as shown by qRT-PCR data, while less inflammation were observed in Germ cells (Fig. 2A–C). Elisa data supported these findings (Fig. 2D–F).

Poly(I:C) induces apoptosis in germ cells but not in Sertoli and Leydig cells

Given the link between orchitis and testicular injury, we employed pro-apoptotic markers and flow cytometric analysis to quantify cell death in Sertoli, Leydig, and Germ cells after 8 h of Poly(I:C) stimulation. There was a significant increase in Germ cell death following transfection with Poly(I:C), compared to cells isolated from control mice, while no significant increase in cell death was observed in Sertoli and Leydig cells (Fig. 3A–C). In addition, qRT-PCR results showed that the mRNA expression of the pro-apoptotic marker Bax was significantly higher in Poly(I:C)-treated Germ cells than in untreated cells, whereas it remained unchanged in Sertoli and Leydig cells (Fig. 3D–F). Western blotting revealed significantly increased levels of Bax and cleaved caspase-9 protein expression in Poly(I:C)-treated Germ cells, but not in Sertoli and Leydig cells (Fig. 3G–I, Supplementary Fig. 3A–C). These findings indicate that Poly(I:C) transfection induces an inflammatory response mainly in Sertoli and Leydig cells, while it triggers cell death in Germ cells.

SHP-1 overexpression inhibits poly(I:C)-induced STAT3 phosphorylation in Sertoli, Leydig, and germ cells

To investigate the roles of SHP-1 and STAT3 in cytokine expression and cell injury, we analyzed SHP-1 and STAT3 expression and phosphorylation levels in Sertoli, Leydig, and Germ cells after Poly(I:C) transfection. Eight hours post-transfection, qRT-PCR and Western blot revealed a significant decrease in SHP-1 mRNA and protein levels across all three cell types (Fig. 4A–C), while total STAT3 protein levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4D–F). However, STAT3 phosphorylation (pSTAT3) significantly increased (Fig. 4D–F, Supplementary Fig. 3D–F), suggesting that Poly(I:C) transfection activates SHP-1–STAT3 signaling by downregulating SHP-1 and enhancing STAT3 phosphorylation.

Further exploration by overexpressing SHP-1 showed that SHP-1 upregulation increased SHP-1 mRNA and protein levels in all cell types (Fig. 5A–C), leading to reduced STAT3 phosphorylation in the context of Poly(I:C)-induced inflammation, while total STAT3 levels remained stable (Fig. 5D–F). This suggests SHP-1 upregulation effectively inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation induced by Poly(I:C). To confirm the reversibility, SHP-1 overexpressing cells were treated with 10 nM colivelin for 24 h, which restored STAT3 phosphorylation in Germ and Leydig cells but had no significant effect on Sertoli cells (Fig. 5D–F, Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). These results confirm that SHP-1 serves as a negative regulator of STAT3 phosphorylation, particularly in Germ cells.

SHP-1 overexpression repressed inflammation in the testicular cells as well as their apoptosis

In this study, we employed Germ cells, Sertoli cells, and Leydig cells derived from C57BL/6 mice to assess the impact of SHP-1 overexpression and colivelin treatment on the inflammation induced by Poly(I:C). Our findings indicate that overexpression of SHP-1 in Sertoli and Leydig cells significantly decreased the mRNA levels of Il-1β, Tnf-α, Mcp-1, and Ifn-β, demonstrating the inhibitory effect of SHP-1 on the expression of pro-inflammatory factors in these cells (Fig. 6B–C). Conversely, no significant changes were observed in the mRNA levels of these inflammatory markers in Germ cells (Fig. 6A), suggesting a minimal impact of SHP-1 on these cells. Further evaluation of colivelin treatment, particularly its role in activating the STAT3 signaling pathway, revealed a substantial reduction in the protein levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, and IFN-β in both Sertoli and Leydig cells (Fig. 6D–F). These results highlight the effectiveness of colivelin in suppressing inflammation in specific testicular cells, emphasizing the critical role of STAT3 activation in maintaining cellular stability. These findings elucidate distinct mechanisms by which SHP-1 and colivelin mitigate inflammation triggered by Poly(I:C), with SHP-1 primarily reducing the expression of inflammatory mediators, while colivelin enhances anti-inflammatory capabilities through STAT3 activation. This research provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of testicular inflammation and may offer new strategies for treating-related diseases.

SHP-1 suppresses poly(I:C)-induced apoptosis in testicular cells, while colivelin reverses this effect

We examined the impact of SHP-1 overexpression and colivelin treatment on apoptosis in Sertoli, Leydig, and Germ cells stimulated with Poly(I:C). Flow cytometry revealed a significant decrease in apoptosis among SHP-1 overexpressing Germ cells, as shown by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining, which was reversed by colivelin treatment (Fig. 7A). This effect was not seen in Sertoli and Leydig cells (Fig. 7B, C and Supplementary Fig. 1), where apoptosis rates remained consistent. Bax mRNA levels decreased significantly in SHP-1 overexpressing Germ cells (Fig. 7D) and were restored after colivelin treatment, while no significant changes occurred in Sertoli (Fig. 7E) and Leydig cells (Fig. 7F). Western blot confirmed that SHP-1 overexpression reduced Bax protein and cCaspase-9 levels (Fig. 7G), consistent with qRT-PCR findings, and colivelin restored these levels (Fig. 7H, I and Supplementary Fig. 4D–F). These results underscore the involvement of the SHP-1–STAT3 pathway in regulating Poly(I:C)-induced apoptosis in Germ cells, highlighting its potential as a target for therapeutic intervention in testicular inflammation and apoptosis.

The effect of in vivo SHP-1 inhibition on poly(I:C)-induced orchitis

In the control group, testicular architecture was well-preserved, as shown by HE staining (Fig. 8A). In contrast, the Poly(I:C) group (0.5 mg/kg) exhibited significant tissue damage and inflammatory cell infiltration, which intensified in the Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 group (with TPI-1 administered starting from day 6 post-treatment), indicating that the SHP-1 inhibitor TPI-1 exacerbates testicular inflammation post-Poly(I:C) treatment. The TPI-1 alone group showed no significant tissue damage, highlighting its specific effect in Poly(I:C)-induced inflammation. TUNEL staining (Fig. 8B) revealed minimal apoptosis in the PBS group, while the Poly(I:C) group showed increased apoptosis, further enhanced by TPI-1, as confirmed statistically (Fig. 8C). These apoptotic findings align with the HE staining, suggesting SHP-1 inhibition exacerbates cell death in Poly(I:C)-induced orchitis. Western blot (Fig. 8D) showed that TPI-1 reduced SHP-1 expression (Fig. 8E) and decreased IL-10 in both TPI-1 and Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 groups, indicating increased inflammation (Fig. 8F). While total STAT3 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 8G), phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) levels decreased in the Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 group compared to TPI-1 alone, with no significant difference between Poly(I:C) and Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 groups (Fig. 8H). This suggests SHP-1 inhibition enhances inflammatory signaling via p-STAT3. In addition, IL-1β levels were lower in the Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 group compared to TPI-1 alone, though not significantly different from the Poly(I:C) group (Fig. 8I). These results demonstrate that SHP-1 inhibition by TPI-1 exacerbates Poly(I:C)-induced testicular inflammation and apoptosis. Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of Src homology 2 (SH2)-containing tyrosine phosphatase-1 (SHP-1) in modulating inflammation and apoptosis in a Poly(I:C)-induced orchitis model. While SHP-1’s function in tumor suppression is well-known [8], its role in testicular inflammation remains underexplored. Our findings highlight SHP-1’s significance in mitigating inflammation and apoptosis, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target in orchitis.

Poly(I:C), a synthetic double-stranded RNA, effectively mimics viral infection, inducing significant inflammation and apoptosis in Leydig and Sertoli cells—changes characterized by reduced SHP-1 expression and increased phosphorylated STAT3 levels. Concomitantly, elevated pro-inflammatory markers including IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, and IFN-β reach their peak at 8 h post-injection. Notably, SHP-1 overexpression exerts a regulatory effect: it significantly reduces these pro-inflammatory markers in Sertoli and Leydig cells, decreases Germ cell apoptosis, and acts as a negative regulator of STAT3 phosphorylation [9]. The reduction in p-STAT3 levels upon SHP-1 overexpression supports SHP-1’s role in maintaining cellular stability by mitigating excessive inflammatory responses. Colivelin, a STAT3 activator, restored STAT3 phosphorylation levels, counteracting SHP-1’s suppressive effects [37].

TPI-1, a SHP-1 inhibitor, exacerbated inflammation and apoptosis following Poly(I:C) treatment, as shown by HE staining and TUNEL assays. The Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 group exhibited pronounced tissue damage and increased apoptosis, underscoring SHP-1’s protective role. Western blot analysis revealed that TPI-1 decreased SHP-1 expression and increased phosphorylated STAT3 levels, correlating with heightened pro-inflammatory cytokines and apoptotic markers [38, 39]. SHP-1’s dual role in regulating cellular functions and controlling inflammation is crucial for maintaining testicular immune privilege, protecting Germ cells, and preserving testicular function [4, 6].

This study exhibits both correlation and expansiveness with previous analogous research. In investigations into viral infection-related orchitis, Wu et al. [40] demonstrated that mouse testicular cells activate downstream signaling pathways upon recognizing mumps virus nucleic acids through specific receptors, leading to the activation of interferon regulatory factor IRF3 and NF-κB. This triggers interstitial and Sertoli cells to produce abundant type I interferons, which suppress viral replication, combat infection, and promote adaptive immune responses by upregulating ISG15, OAS1, and MX1. Kalichman et al. [41] focused on human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and revealed associations between viral loads in plasma/semen and inflammatory responses. Another study [42] showed that Zika virus (ZIKV) infection causes degenerative necrosis of Germ cells and interstitial cells, along with seminiferous tubule rupture; in the late infection stage, Sertoli cells initiate antiviral responses involving IRF3, IFN-α, IFN-β, etc., to modulate the immune microenvironment for persistent ZIKV infection. Collectively, these studies from diverse viral types and signaling pathways illuminate the mechanisms by which viral infections induce testicular inflammation.

In SHP-1-related immune regulation research, Kurara Takagane et al. [43] found that SKAP2 prevents excessive inflammation by inhibiting the TLR4-NF-κB pathway and regulates the TLR4–IFNβ pathway via SHP-1/SHP-2 activation to suppress inflammation-mediated tumorigenesis. QinLei Gu et al. [44, 45] confirmed that SHP-1 acts as a key intracellular mediator in regulatory T cells (Tregs), fine-tuning the balance between Treg-mediated suppression and conventional T cell (Tcon) activation/resistance; genetic deletion of SHP-1 in Tregs impairs in vivo inflammation control. These findings corroborate the negative regulatory role of SHP-1 in this study, demonstrating its conserved anti-inflammatory mechanism across different tissues and disease models.

In addition, research on therapeutic strategies for orchitis provides contextual support. Current studies focus on pathways (e.g., p38MAPK, NF-κB, and PI3K/Akt) and inflammatory factors (e.g., NOS system, TNF-α, and IL-6) closely linked to orchitis and Germ cell function. For instance, AMPK activation promotes macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [46], and Deng et al. [47] showed that melatonin alleviates LPS-induced inflammation and oxidative stress in sheep orchitis by inhibiting the p38MAPK pathway. These findings suggest that comprehensive intervention in inflammatory and apoptotic pathways represents a promising therapeutic strategy for orchitis, with the dual regulatory role of SHP-1 identified here offering a novel target for such interventions.

Notably, while in vitro experiments have clearly demonstrated that SHP-1 influences inflammation and apoptosis by regulating STAT3 phosphorylation, the in vivo outcomes following TPI-1 administration are more complex. For instance, p-STAT3 levels in the TPI-1 monotherapy group did not increase significantly, and IL-1β expression in the Poly(I:C) plus TPI-1 group failed to rise as anticipated. This suggests that the in vivo regulatory mechanism may involve molecular compensation or pathway crosstalk distinct from those observed in the in vitro model. Overall, SHP-1 overexpression inhibits STAT3 phosphorylation, reducing inflammation and apoptosis, while its inhibition by TPI-1 highlights the therapeutic implications of modulating this pathway.

The clinical translational potential of targeting SHP-1 is underexplored. Despite murine studies linking it to inflammation/cell survival via STAT3, key gaps limit extrapolation to humans. First, species differences—such as divergent SHP-1 activity or Poly(I:C) responsiveness in human vs. murine testicular cells—hinder applicability. Moreover, in vivo network complexity risks off-target effects unobserved in vitro. Notably, clinical use would need cell-specific delivery to avoid systemic immunosuppression, given SHP-1’s broad hematopoietic roles. Longitudinal human studies are also needed to validate its correlation with viral orchitis or infertility severity, establishing therapeutic or biomarker potential. While mechanistically relevant, bridging preclinical–clinical gaps is critical to unlock its value.

In conclusion, our findings underscore the critical roles of SHP-1 and STAT3 in regulating inflammation and apoptosis during Poly(I:C)-induced orchitis. Targeting the SHP-1–STAT3 axis presents therapeutic potential for managing testicular inflammatory conditions. Future research should further explore SHP-1 modulation in various diseases to harness its clinical applications.
