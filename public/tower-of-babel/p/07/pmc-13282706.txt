Autonomous clinical Artificial Intelligence and the validation gap: structural risks and regulatory priorities for European health systems
Authors: Răzvan Cherecheș
Published: 2026

At HIMSS 2026 in Las Vegas, the companies that build the digital infrastructure of clinical care converged on a single message: autonomous Artificial Intelligence (AI) agents are ready for the clinic. Oracle unveiled a clinical AI agent spanning 30 medical specialties. Amazon launched an agentic assistant offering personalised triage through health information exchanges. Google presented conversational AI that, according to the company, now handles 80% of inbound patient calls in some deployments. Epic debuted agents for documentation, billing, and scheduling alongside a platform for building custom clinical agents [1, 2]. Any European health policymaker paying attention should be unsettled–not by the technology itself, but by the gap between what these systems do and what anyone has done to validate them.

These are not the narrow, single-task AI tools that have populated medical imaging suites for a decade. They are systems designed to reason across clinical workflows, decompose objectives into multi-step tasks, and act on those plans with varying degrees of human oversight. The >1000 tools the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has authorised since the mid-1990s are predominantly pattern-recognition instruments: they accept an input, produce an output, and a clinician decides what to do with it [3]. Agentic AI operates differently–it coordinates actions across electronic health records and scheduling systems, adapting its behaviour based on outcomes. We are witnessing a qualitative shift from tools that inform clinical decisions to systems positioned to execute them.

The validation gap

This shift arrives against a backdrop of already inadequate validation for far simpler technologies. A 2025 cross-sectional study in JAMA Network Open examining 903 FDA-authorised AI medical devices found that clinical performance studies had been reported for only approximately half, while one-quarter explicitly stated that no clinical evaluation had been conducted [4]. A separate analysis of 692 FDA-approved devices found that only 3.6% of authorization summaries reported race or ethnicity data, 81.6% omitted study subjects’age, and a mere 1.6% included evidence from randomized controlled trials [5].

These figures describe static devices. Agentic AI introduces categories of risk that current validation frameworks were not designed to address. An agent that autonomously triages patients, suggests next clinical steps, and coordinates care across specialty functions, in effect, acts as a clinical process–not a device. Its behaviour may change with context, improve or degrade through use, and produce unpredictable effects when multiple agents operate within the same health system. The FDA’s existing 510(k) pathway, through which the vast majority of AI devices have been cleared by demonstrating “substantial equivalence” to a predicate device [3], offers no mechanism to evaluate these properties.

A transatlantic regulatory divergence

The United States and the European Union are pursuing fundamentally different strategies. The US approach remains guidance-based: the FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled device lifecycle management is advisory, not binding [6]. The current US administration has moved to reduce regulatory constraints on AI adoption, creating a federal vacuum that individual states are attempting to fill with divergent requirements [7]. The result is regulatory fragmentation that offers no coherent framework for evaluating agentic systems.

Europe’s trajectory is different. The EU AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024, classifies most medical AI systems as high-risk, triggering requirements for conformity assessment, risk management documentation, data governance, transparency, and human oversight [8, 9]. These obligations layer on top of the existing Medical Device Regulation (MDR), creating a dual-certification framework that is more demanding than anything the FDA currently requires. The new EU Product Liability Directive, to be implemented by December 2026, explicitly includes software and AI as products subject to strict liability [10].

The practical timeline creates a specific vulnerability. While high-risk AI system obligations under the AI Act’s Annex III apply from August 2026, the rules for AI systems embedded in regulated medical devices have been extended to August 2027, with further delays anticipated through the proposed Digital Omnibus package [8]. This creates a window during which US-developed clinical AI agents may seek entry into European markets where the conformity assessment infrastructure is still being assembled.

Implications for European health systems

This regulatory gap creates a concrete risk: as US-developed agents become embedded in major electronic health record platforms used globally, European hospitals may deploy agentic AI through routine software updates rather than through deliberate procurement decisions, importing the US validation deficit along with the technology. The MDR requires that significant changes to medical device software trigger new conformity assessments, but it remains unclear whether adding an AI agent via a platform update would be classified as a significant change.

Yet the opportunity is equally concrete. The EU AI Act’s conformity assessment framework provides a structured basis for evaluating what agentic AI systems actually do in clinical settings. European researchers are well-positioned to define the validation science that does not yet exist: methodologies for evaluating autonomous clinical agents in real-world settings and frameworks for monitoring performance drift in self-improving systems.

A call for proactive European engagement

Three actions are needed. First, European regulators should accelerate the development of harmonized standards for agentic AI validation under the AI Act, using the regulatory sandboxes mandated by the Act to test approaches before they become binding. Second, the research community should treat the validation of autonomous clinical agents as an urgent methodological priority–we need prospective studies comparing agentic AI performance against current clinical processes before these systems become so embedded that rigorous evaluation becomes methodologically compromised. Third, health professional education must incorporate AI agent governance as a core competency, integrated into specialist training frameworks rather than relegated to elective modules.

HIMSS 2026 was not a technology showcase. It was a declaration: the companies that build the infrastructure of clinical care have decided that AI agents now belong in that infrastructure. The question for European health systems is not whether agentic AI will arrive–it is whether it will arrive validated.
