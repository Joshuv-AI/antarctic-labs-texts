Clinicopathological characteristics and renal outcomes of IgA nephropathy in systemic lupus erythematosus: a case report and systematic literature review
Authors: XiXi Zhao, ZhenZhen Li, GuangYan Cai, FengKun Chen, Jian Zhang, XuanLi Tang, Xu Zhang, ZhiYu Duan, Yan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether IgA nephropathy (IgAN) in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) represents a coincidental comorbidity or a distinct clinico-pathological entity remains unclear. This study aimed to characterise the demographic, clinical, pathological, and prognostic features of this rare association.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of the PubMed and Embase databases to 31 May 2025 were conducted to identify all reported cases of biopsy-proven IgAN in patients with SLE, excluding cases with concomitant thrombotic microangiopathy, negative Gd-IgA1 immunostaining, prior IgA vasculitis, or ANA-negative disease. Study quality was assessed using the JBI checklist. Individual patient data were pooled; descriptive statistics summarized clinical features, and group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U and Fisher’s exact tests. Individual patient data were extracted and analyzed as a single cohort. Renal outcomes were defined as either a ≥ 40% decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) from baseline or progression to end-stage renal disease.ResultsThirty-one patients (25.8% male; mean age 40.8 ± 15.8 years; 67.7% Asian) were included. Median proteinuria was 1.10 g/24 h, and 12.9% presented with nephrotic syndrome. Microscopic haematuria was observed in 93.5% of patients. Mean eGFR at biopsy was 84.0 ± 53.4 mL/min/1.73 m², with 12.9% exhibiting acute kidney injury (AKI). All patients were antinuclear antibody-positive, 64.5% were anti-dsDNA-positive, and 55.2% had hypocomplementaemia. Renal biopsy revealed dominant mesangial IgA and complement C3 deposition, with electron-dense deposits confined to the mesangium in all but one case. Immunosuppression (glucocorticoids 87.1%; cyclophosphamide 22.6%) yielded complete remission in 54.8% of patients. Over a median follow-up of 16.5 months, 27.8% of patients reached the renal endpoint, including three of four patients with AKI.ConclusionsLimited by retrospective case report methodology, this largest cohort to date suggests IgAN complicating SLE manifests a distinctive phenotype that bridges features of primary IgAN and lupus nephritis. Although immunosuppression is effective, long-term renal risk remains non-negligible. Early recognition and targeted therapy may improve outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05092-9.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease with a global prevalence of 43.7 per 100,000 population [1]. The highest reported prevalence rates are observed in the United Arab Emirates, Barbados, and Brazil [2]. Renal involvement, manifesting as lupus nephritis (LN), occurs in 20%–65% of patients depending on the population studied [3–5].

LN typically exhibits a “full-house” immunofluorescence pattern characterised by concomitant mesangial and capillary wall deposition of IgG, IgA, IgM, complement C3, C1q, and fibrin-related antigens. On light microscopy, LN typically shows the following: mesangial hypercellularity; endocapillary- or extracapillary proliferation; subendothe-lial (“wire-loop”) and/or subepithelial deposits (with “spikes” or “humps”); fibrinoid ne-crosis; karyorrhexis; haematoxylin bodies; and well-formed circumferential or segmen-tal cellular crescents. Electron microscopy reveals large, electron-dense deposits dis-tributed not only in the mesangium but also along the glomerular basement membrane (subendothelial, subepithelial and intramembranous), often accompanied by extensive foot-process effacement and microtubular structures. Class II LN (also termed mesangial proliferative LN), as defined by the International Society of Nephrology/ Renal Pathology Society 2003 classification, features diffuse mesangial hypercellularity and/or mesangial matrix expansion on light microscopy, with mesangial immune deposits (full-house pattern) on immunofluorescence and/or electron microscopy, and no significant endocapillary- or extracapillary proliferation, necrosis, or subendothelial/subepithelial deposits [6]. Consequently, a few authors consider class II lupus nephritis with dominant IgA deposits to represent a secondary form of IgA nephropathy rather than typical lupus nephritis. However, rare instances of biopsy-proven IgAN coexisting with SLE have been reported [7]. In 1995, the first such cases were described in Modern Pathology, including three SLE patients with IgAN, two of whom experienced significant estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline and one of whom died from infection [8]. Since then, sporadic case reports have emerged, but all were single-centre series with no more than five patients each [9]. As a result, the demographic profile, clinical presentation, treatment response, and long-term prognosis of SLE patients with IgAN remain poorly defined.

Here, we report an additional case of biopsy-verified IgAN in a patient with SLE and perform a systematic review of all published cases. By analysing this pooled cohort, we aim to clarify the clinical characteristics, therapeutic responses, and renal outcomes of this rare overlap syndrome.

Materials and methods

Literature-search strategy

We conducted a systematic search of PubMed and Embase from inception to May 31, 2025 using the following strategy:

(“Systemic Lupus Erythematosus” [MeSH] OR “SLE” [tiab]) AND (“Immunoglobulin A Nephropathy” [MeSH] OR “IgA nephropathy” [tiab] OR “IgAN” [tiab] OR “IgA nephritis” [tiab] OR “IgA glomerulonephritis” [tiab] OR “Berger* disease” [tiab]).

No language restrictions were applied. Reference lists of all retrieved articles were manually-searched for additional cases.

Study selection and case eligibility

After removing duplicates, titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (XXZ and ZZL), with disagreements resolved by a third senior reviewer (ZYD). Reviews, case–control, cohort and case-report studies containing individual patient data were retained. Two cases were subsequently excluded: one case with concomitant thrombotic microangiopathy [10] and one with negative galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) immunostaining with the monoclonal antibody KM55 [11].

Two patients who were antinuclear antibody (ANA)-negative and did not fulfil current SLE classification criteria [12, 13], along with three patients with documented prior IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura) [14, 15], were excluded. In total, 31 biopsy-proven SLE–IgAN cases extracted from 23 publications [7–9, 16–35], plus one new case from our centre, were analysed (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers (XXZ, ZZL) assessed the methodological quality of each included case report using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports [36]. This 8-item checklist assesses clarity of patient demographics, disease history, diagnostic criteria, intervention description, clinical outcomes, adverse events, lessons learned, and potential sources of bias. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. For descriptive purposes, studies were categorized as high quality (meeting ≥ 7 of 8 JBI criteria), moderate quality (6/8 criteria), or low quality (≤ 5/8 criteria). This threshold was chosen to ensure that studies classified as high quality reported nearly all essential domains of a case report.

When only spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR; g/g) was provided, we converted it to 24-h proteinuria using the validated 1:1 approximation (1 g/g ≈ 1 g/d) [38, 39]; otherwise the originally reported 24-h value was retained.

All 31 patients fulfilled current SLE classification criteria and showed mesangial-proliferative glomerular lesions with dominant IgA and complement C3 deposits on immunofluorescence. Electron-dense deposits were confined to the mesangium in all but one patient, who also exhibited scattered subepithelial deposits [30]. Two patients, including the index case, demonstrated positive staining for KM55 [28].

Double-immunofluorescence staining of Gd-IgA1

We used the KM55 kit (Immuno-Biological Laboratories, Japan) to detect Gd-IgA1 in renal tissue [40]. Briefly, paraffin-embedded Sect. (3 µm thickness) were deparaffinised, subjected to antigen-retrieval using proteinase K digestion (10 min, 37°C), blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin, and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-KM55 primary antibody (1:100 dilution). After washing, slides were exposed to Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rat IgG (1:100; Life Technologies) for 1 h at room temperature, counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and abserved using a BX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Treatment definitions and renal endpoints

We documented each treatment component, including renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, glucocorticoids (oral or intravenous), and additional immunosuppressive agents, such as cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, calcineurin inhibitors, and rituximab. Complete remission (CR) was defined as a reduction of proteinuria to < 0.3 g/24 h (or a spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio < 300 mg/g [< 30 mg/mmol]) with an eGFR decline ≤ 10% from baseline, indicating (stable renal function). Partial remission (PR) was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in proteinuria and an eGFR decline ≤ 10% from baseline. Progression of chronic kidney disease was captured by a composite renal endpoint: sustained ≥ 40% decline in eGFR from the renal biopsy value or progression to ESRD (eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m², initiation of chronic dialysis or kidney transplantation).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median [inter-quartile range, IQR] based on Shapiro–Wilk normality tests. Group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. All P-values were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Given the observational nature of case reports, formal meta-regression was not performed. Exploratory subgroup analyses comparing patients with versus without AKI were performed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical outcomes.

Results

Case report

A 62-year-old Han Chinese man was referred with a 2-month history of fatigue, chest tightness, and anorexia, followed by 2 weeks of gross haematuria and a 1-week rise in Scr. He had 10-years history of hypertension and reported photosensitive dermatitis triggered by ultraviolet exposure. In January 2019, he was admitted to a local hospital for pneumonia and cerebral infarction, with echocardiography revealing moderate pericardial effusion and Scr ranging from 51 to 64 µmol/L.

On 17 April 2019, laboratory tests at the local hospital showed serum albumin 35.6 g/L, Scr 523 µmol/L, haemoglobin 88 g/L, dipstick protein 1+, and occult blood 3+. Renal ultrasound demonstrated bilateral enlargement with increased cortical echogenicity. Six days later, his Scr rose to 606.7 µmol/L, and he was transferred to our unit on 24 April.

At admission, his blood pressure was 140/80 mmHg, with a normal body temperature, and a body mass index of 23.88 kg/m². Urine output ranged from 600 to 1,000 mL/day. He was pale, with scattered erythematous lesions on both forearms and the back, but no apparent leg oedema. Chest ultrasound revealed bilateral pleural effusion.

Laboratory findings were as follows: white blood cell count 3.2 × 10⁹/L (lymphocytes 0.6 × 10⁹/L), haemoglobin 84 g/L, platelets count 84 × 10⁹/L, albumin 33.7 g/L, lactate dehydrogenase 271 U/L, and Scr 724 µmol/L. the complement C3 level was 0.25 g/L and C4 was 0.04 g/L. ANA were positive at 1:160, anti-dsDNA weakly positive, and direct Coombs test was negative. Urinalysis showed trace protein, occult blood 3+, and 1,294 erythrocytes/µL, with 24-h protein excretion of 0.49 g/day. The following serologic autoantibody tests were negative: rheumatoid factor (RF), anti-Smith (anti-Sm), anti-ribosomal P protein (anti-Rib-P), anticardiolipin (aCL), anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) and tumour markers. Serologies for hepatitis B and C viruses, HIV, and syphilis were also negative. The patient fulfilled the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for SLE with a weighted score of 19 points (ANA positivity as entry criterion; leukopenia 3 points, thrombocytopenia 4 points, pericarditis 3 points, hypocomplementemia 4 points, anti-dsDNA 5 points, cutaneous involvement 2 points), confirming SLE diagnosis despite the atypical presentation in an elderly male without arthritis. Catheterisation of right internal-jugular vein was performed on 26 April and haemodialysis initiated. Percutaneous renal biopsy was undertaken on 28 April. Light microscopy of 14 glomeruli showed one global sclerotic glomerulus, which included three cellular, one cellular-fibrous and two small cellular crescents (Fig. 2). The remaining glomeruli exhibited mild, diffuse mesangial hypercellularity and matrix expansion with scant fuchsin-positive deposits (Fig. 2). Tubules showed vacuolar and granular degeneration, brush-border loss, and numerous red-cell casts. The interstitium was oedematous with focal lymphomononuclear infiltrates and mild fibrosis, small arteries had thickened walls without luminal narrowing. Immunofluorescence revealed granular mesangial deposits: IgA 3+, IgM 1+, C3 2+; IgG and C1q were negative. Electron microscopy confirmed mild mesangial proliferation with electron-dense deposits confined to mesangial and paramesangial regions, Basement membranes were unremarkable, and most podocyte foot processes were effaced. The final pathological diagnosis was IgAN with acute tubular injury secondary to obstruction of red-cell cast.

Treatment comprised intravenous methylprednisolone 40 mg daily, oral cyclophosphamide 50 mg daily, and hydroxychloroquine 400 mg daily. Haemodialysis was discontinued on 10 May. At discharge, the patient’s Scr had fallen to 456 µmol/L; white blood-cell count was 8.6 × 10⁹/L, haemoglobin 85 g/L, platelet count 128 × 10⁹/L, complement C3 0.32 g/L, complement C4 0.04 g/L, and 24-h protein excretion was 0.29 g/day. At telephone follow-up in April 2020, serum creatinine had decreased to 304 µmol/L and 24-hour urinary protein excretion was 0.12 g/day (Supplementary Table 2).

Study selection and quality assessment

A total of 2915 records were identified through database searching (PubMed n = 294, Embase n = 2621). After removing non-relevant, 30 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 7 were excluded (reasons: ANA negative, KM55 staining negative, IgAN combined with thrombotic microangiopathy, and IgA vasculitis). 23 articles met the inclusion criteria, comprising 31 individual patients (Fig. 1). Of the 23 included publications, 20 (87.0%) were classified as high quality (meeting ≥ 7/8 JBI criteria), 2 (8.7%) as moderate quality (6/8 criteria), and 1 (4.3%) as low quality (≤ 5/8 criteria, Supplementary Table 1). While the overall reporting completeness was high, two items were frequently unmet or only partially met: post-treatment clinical outcomes (item 6) and identification of adverse events (item 7). Notably, several conference abstracts achieved high total scores despite limited follow-up data, reflecting that the checklist scores primarily represent reporting completeness rather than clinical evidence reliability. Consequently, the certainty of evidence derived from these case reports remains inherently low.

Clinical features of the pooled cohort

Having described the index patient in detail, we next present the demographic and laboratory characteristics of the entire pooled cohort (n = 31), which was assembled by systematic review of all published cases plus the present report. The pooled cohort comprised 31 patients with biopsy-proven IgAN and SLE, Assembled through systematic review of published cases and inclusion of the present report. Renal disease was the first manifestation of systemic autoimmunity in 22 patients (71.0%), whereas 9 patients (29.0%) already carried a pre-existing SLE diagnosis before developing renal involvement. Of the 31 patients, 8 (25.8%) were already on immunosuppressive therapy when renal manifestations were first detected. Two-thirds of the patients were Asian (67.7%, n = 21): 11 from mainland China (35.5%), 4 from Turkey (12.9%) and 3 from Japan (9.7%). Three patients were from Italy (9.7%) and seven were from the Americas (22.6%), of whom four were from the United States (12.9%). Men constituted 25.8% (8/31), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1:3. Mean age at biopsy was 40.9 ± 15.7 years (range: 15–74 years), with 16.1% of patients (5/31) ≥ 60 years of age (Table 1). Hypertension was present in 45.2% (14/31). Median Scr was 80.4 (range: 64.8–192.0) µmol/L and mean eGFR was 84.0 ± 53.4 mL/min/1.73 m²; Four patients (12.9%) presented with acute kidney injury (AKI) [17, 21, 30]. Median 24-h protein excretion was 1.10 (range: 0.63–2.39) g/day and 12.9% (4/31) had nephrotic syndrome. Haematuria was almost universal (93.5%, 29/31), with gross haematuria in 19.4% (6/31); only two patients had no red cells on urine microscopy. All patients were ANA-positive (Table 2). Anti-ds-DNA antibodies were detected in 64.5% (20/31), anti-Sm and anti-U1-RNP in 16.1% each (5/31), and antiphospholipid antibodies in 25.8% (8/31). Hypocomplementaemia was recorded in 55.2% (16/29). The mean C3 level was 69.6 ± 30.8 mg/dL, with 51.7% (15/29) exhibiting low C3; the median C4 level was 13.1 (9.0–16.3) mg/dL, with low C4 in 41.4% (12/29).

Renal biopsy findings

Immunofluorescence results were available for 27 patients and showed mesangial IgA deposits in every case (100%). C3 was co-dominant in 66.7% (18/27), while IgG, IgM, and C1q were detected in 29.6% (8/27), 40.7% (11/27), and 7.4% (2/27), respectively. All biopsies were negative for C4. Electron microscopy was performed in 11 patients [8, 16, 17, 21, 23, 27, 29, 30]; one biopsy contained no glomerular profiles [8], leaving 10 evaluable specimens. Electron-dense deposits were confined to the mesangium in nine patients, while one additional patient also exhibited scattered subepithelial deposits [30]. KM55 staining for Gd-IgA1 was positive in two patients [28]. Renal biopsy was repeated in two patients who had received an initial diagnosis of LN within 1 year of SLE onset (Class II [22] and Class IV [31]), achieved complete remission, and underwent subsequent re-biopsy years later for recurrent proteinuria or persistent haematuria, demonstrating pure IgAN without full-house features.

Treatment and prognosis

Glucocorticoids were prescribed in 87.1% (27/31) of patients, of whom 22.6% (7/31) also received cyclophosphamide and 9.7% (3/31) mycophenolate mofetil. One patient was maintained on azathioprine and one underwent five courses of plasma exchange. Overall response was favourable: CR was achieved in 54.8% (17/31) and PR in 12.9% (4/31). Follow-up data sufficient to assess the renal endpoint were available for 18 individuals (median follow-up 16.5 months), of whom five (27.8%) met the composite criterion (≥ 40% decline in eGFR or progression to ESRD). Three of four AKI cases (75%) reached the composite renal endpoint, compared with only two of 14 non-AKI cases (14.3%; Fisher exact test, p = 0.044). The index patient was able to discontinue dialysis; however, despite a substaintial fall in Scr, discharge eGFR remained < 15 mL/min/1.73 m², indicating irreversible loss of renal function.

Discussion

This study presents the largest cohort to date of patients with co-existing SLE and biopsy-proven IgAN. By integrating all published cases since the first description of SLE-IgAN by Hong Kong investigators in 1995 [8], along with one new case from our centre, we characterised the demographic, clinicopathological, and prognostic features of 31 individuals.

The geographic distribution of SLE–IgAN mirrored that of primary IgAN: two-thirds of patients were Asian, with China and Japan together accounting for nearly half of the entire cohort [41]. This pattern contrasts with the epidemiology of SLE alone, which shows the highest recorded incidences in Poland, the United States, and Barbados, with China ranking fourth [2]. The over-representation of Asian subjects suggests that the predisposition to IgAN—presumably driven by genetic factors such as the CFHR1-3 deletion or HLA-DQ risk alleles—may also govern the emergence and recognition of this dual pathology. The male-to-female sex ratio of 1:3 was consistent with the female predominance typical of lupus, yet the proportion of men was clearly higher than that in unselected SLE or LN cohorts. Mean age at biopsy was 40.9 years, intermediate between that of primary IgAN (range: 35–41 years) [42, 43] and classic LN (range: 28–31 years) [44, 45]. Notably, 16% of our patients were aged 60 years or older—an age group that accounts for < 2% of most primary-IgAN series [46, 47]. This over-representation of elderly individuals may reflect, at least in part, the observation that one-third of patients were diagnosed IgAN more than 1 year after SLE onset, implying that IgAN may emerge as a late complication of long-standing lupus rather than as a coincidental primary disease.

At presentation, 12.9% of the SLE–IgAN patients had AKI—similar to the 8.5% reported in primary IgAN [48], but lower than the 17.7% observed in LN, where class III/IV disease reaches 21.1% [49]. Median Scr (80.4 µmol/L) and mean eGFR (84.0 mL/min/1.73 m²) values were squarely within the 55–90 mL/min/1.73 m² range typically seen in newly diagnosed IgAN [42, 43] and are appreciably higher than the 65–67 µmol/L creatinine levels recorded in most LN cohorts [50]. Likewise, median proteinuria was 1.10 g/24 h, and only 12.9% of patients met criteria for nephrotic syndrome—figures that mirror population-based IgAN series [42, 43] but are substaintially lower than the 1.8–2.0 g/day and ~ 50% nephrotic-range proteinuria reported in class III/IV LN [50, 51]. To further contextualize the baseline risk profile, we compared our cohort with the international IgA nephropathy prediction tool (IIGANPT) derivation and validation cohorts. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the SLE-IgAN cohort exhibited a comparable median proteinuria and eGFR but a markedly lower male predominance (25.8% vs. ~50–58%) and a higher proportion of Asian patients, consistent with the epidemiology of both SLE and IgAN in East Asia. Microscopic haematuria was almost universal (93.5%), with gross haematuria in 19.4%—a prevalence closer to that of primary IgAN than to the 63%–80% haematuria rate described in LN [51, 52].

Complement profiles also occupied an intermediate position. Hypocomplementaemia typically occurs in 50%–89% of unselected SLE patients [50, 53] ; in the literature cohort the prevalence was 55.2%. Isolated low C3 was present in 51.7%—lower than the 64% reported for SLE overall [53] but almost twice the 28% observed in primary IgAN [54]. Low C4 was found in 41.4%, again below the 62% typical of SLE [53], yet substaintially higher than the ~ 10% described in IgAN cohorts without autoimmune disease [55]. Taken together, these data indicate that SLE–IgAN carries a discernible lupus-like complement signature superimposed on an IgAN phenotype.

When an SLE patient in serological remission develops new-onset proteinuria the first diagnostic question is whether LN has flared or a second glomerular disease has emerged. IgAN superimposed on SLE is exceptionally rare, with epidemiological data suggesting that some SLE-risk alleles may even confer protection against IgAN [56]. Histology remains the most direct diagnostic clue. Pure IgAN is defined by dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgA, usually accompanied by C3 and only scant IgG/C1q, whereas LN classically shows a “full-house” pattern. In this systematic cohort, every biopsy displayed the IgA-dominant, C3-positive, C1q-negative profile characteristic of primary IgAN, supporting the notion of a true dual pathology rather than an atypical LN variant.

Serum Gd-IgA1, the key pathogenic marker in IgAN, is readily detected using lectin-based or monoclonal antibody assays. However, elevated plasma Gd-IgA1 is not entirely specific to IgAN: it is also found in a minority of healthy individuals and in patients with other immune-mediated diseases [57]. In contrast, renal deposition of Gd-IgA1 appears far more discriminatory. Using the KM-55 monoclonal antibody targeting the glycosylation defect in the hinge region of the IgA1 heavy chain, strong mesangial positivity is consistently observed in primary IgAN and Henoch–Schönlein purpura nephritis. Strong IgA staining is also observed in a subset of secondary IgAN cases associated with chronic liver disease, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, or staphylococcal infection. By contrast, LN typically exhibits only faint or negative KM-55 reactivity [58, 59]. The positive KM55 staining in our index case reinforced the diagnosis of SLE combined with IgAN.

Treatment response in the pooled cohort was encouraging: 67.7% achieved CR or PR. This exceeds the 17% CR rate seen at 12 months in the STOP-IgAN immunosuppression arm [60], and is numerically higher than the 52.2% combined remission rate observed in the TESTING methylprednisolone group [61]. By contrast, retrospective studies have reported 70%–100% remission rates in patients with class II LN receiving similar immunosuppressive therapy [62, 63]. However, these cross-cohort comparisons are unadjusted and should be viewed only as hypothesis-generating. Thus, SLE–IgAN may appear to occupy an intermediate therapeutic position between primary IgAN and pure LN. This numerically higher remission rate than in primary IgAN cohorts coincides with the frequent use of hydroxychloroquine in the literature series. Observational data suggest that hydroxychloroquine may reduce proteinuria and preserve renal function in IgAN [64, 65]. however, its independent benefit in SLE-IgAN overlap remains to be proven in prospective, controlled studies.

In the literature cohort, 27.8% of patients reached the renal composite endpoint, a figure that falls between the 3.7% ESRD incidence reported for class II LN [66] and the 17.7%–50% long-term risk of renal function decline or ESRD observed in primary IgAN [42, 43]. This intermediate prognosis reinforces the concept that SLE–IgAN is a distinct clinicopathological subgroup rather than a simple overlap of two diseases.

Our analysis has limitations. First, given the retrospective nature of this study and its reliance on published case reports and small case series, our findings are subject to publication bias and should be considered exploratory. The absence of a control group further limits the strength of comparative conclusions. Moreover, because the SLE-IgAN cohort was compiled from globally dispersed case reports, assembling a matched control group from a single centre or even multicentre from a single country would not satisfy the requirements for valid comparison. Instead, we have contextualized our findings by comparing them with outcomes from large, multinational cohort studies of primary IgA nephropathy and lupus nephritis class II. Prospective, multicentre registries are needed to validate these preliminary observations and to better define the clinical significance of IgAN in SLE. Second, treatment regimens and observation periods were heterogeneous, precluding formal subgroup comparisons or survival modelling. Third, because only 18 of 31 patients had adequate follow-up data and the median observation period was 16.5 months, our study has limited power to detect slower, late renal deterioration that may manifest over years to decades. Consequently, the reported event rate (27.8% reaching the composite renal endpoint) should be viewed as a preliminary estimate, and the long-term renal risk in SLE-IgAN may well be higher than these short-term data suggest. Prospective, multicentre registries with extended follow-up are required to establish the true natural history of this rare overlap syndrome. Fourth, histological data were confined to the descriptions given in the original case reports; Oxford MEST-C scores and NIH activity/chronicity indices were not available. This precludes alignment with the current standard for IgAN risk stratification and may attenuate the precision of our survival estimates. Nevertheless, the key clinical covariates required by the validated clinical-only IIGANPT equation (age, sex, eGFR, proteinuria, mean arterial pressure, albumin) were present in ≥ 90% of patients, allowing exploratory risk profiling consistent with that model’s 0.78 C-statistic. Future prospective registries should incorporate centralized MEST-C reading and standardized LN indices to refine prognostication in this rare overlap population. While included case reports scored highly on the JBI checklist (median 7/8), they uniformly exhibited methodological limitations—short follow-up, incomplete post-treatment data (item 6), and absent adverse event reporting (item 7)—rendering these scores indicative of report quality rather than clinical evidence robustness. As case reports provide low-certainty evidence by design, this review offers a descriptive summary of clinical characteristics without inferring causality or therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusions

This systematic review of 31 patients with coexisting SLE and IgAN represents the largest cohort reported to date. The clinical phenotype—characterised by prominent haematuria, moderate proteinuria, favourable response to immunosuppression, and intermediate renal prognosis—suggests that SLE-IgAN may constitute a distinct clinicopathological subgroup; however, its formal classification as a separate entity awaits mechanistic and prospective validation. Clinicians should consider concurrent IgAN in SLE patients presenting with atypical renal features. Early recognition and targeted therapy may improve outcomes. Prospective, multicentre studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify underlying mechanisms.
