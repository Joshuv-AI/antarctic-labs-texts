Incidence and risk factors of postoperative delirium in patients undergoing elective lung cancer surgery : a retrospective study using the national inpatient sample database
Authors: Xueqin Qi, Chang Liu, Bizhen Liao, Xinmiao Wang, Yadan Wu, Yichu Liao, Lifang Lin, Xue Bai, Zhonglian Wang, Hong Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer is the world’s deadliest cancer, and surgery offers cure for over 75% of patients. However, postoperative delirium (POD) impairs recovery, accelerates cognitive decline, and increases mortality. Despite evidence that multicomponent prevention can avert 30–40% of POD, its incidence and predictors in lung cancer surgery remain under-studied.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults (≥ 18 years) undergoing elective lung cancer surgery resection from 2010 to 2019 using the U.S. National Inpatient Sample. Emergency admissions, rib fractures, and preexisting delirium or dementia were excluded. POD was identified by ICD-9/10 codes. We extracted demographics, comorbidities, perioperative complications, length of stay, and costs. Wilcoxon rank-sum and χ² tests compared groups, and univariable and multivariable logistic regression—with stepwise selection and Bonferroni correction—identified independent POD predictors. Analyses were performed in R, with P < 0.05.ResultsAmong 30,813 resections, 1,499 patients (4.86%) developed POD. Age ≥ 71 years (OR 2.10; 95% CI 1.75–2.51) and 61–70 years (OR 1.59; 1.35–1.88), female sex (OR 1.12; 1.01–1.24), teaching-hospital status (OR 1.38; 1.21–1.57), and more extensive resections (lobectomy OR 1.43; 1.28–1.60; pneumonectomy OR 2.58; 2.06–3.23) increased POD risk, whereas urban hospitals were protective (OR 0.71; 0.55–0.91). Preoperative neurological disorders (OR 5.62; 5.02–6.29), psychoses (OR 1.62; 1.35–1.95), fluid–electrolyte disorders (OR 1.27; 1.14–1.42), and hypertension (OR 1.21; 1.08–1.36) were independent predictors. Postoperative arrhythmia, deep-vein thrombosis, stroke, shock, urinary infection, and hypoxemia further elevated risk; chronic pulmonary disease and respiratory failure were protective.ConclusionsPOD complicates nearly 5% of lung cancer resections. Key demographic, clinical, and institutional factors identify high-risk patients who may benefit from targeted, multicomponent prevention strategies in thoracic surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03263-3.Keywords: Postoperative delirium, Elective lung cancer surgery, Risk factors, Perioperative complications, National inpatient sample

Background

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for a substantial proportion of global cancer diagnoses [1]. Surgical intervention remains the primary treatment for more than three-quarters of lung cancer patients [2]. Surgical resection is the cornerstone of curative treatment for over 75% of patients, yet postoperative complications occur in 10–50% of cases and can substantially impair recovery and long-term survival [3]. Among these complications, POD is particularly concerning due to its detrimental effects on cognitive function and quality of life, especially in elderly patients [4]. POD is a common geriatric syndrome that typically occurs after anesthesia and surgery. It is characterized by sudden and fluctuating disturbances in mental status, including confusion, impaired attention, and altered levels of consciousness, reflecting acute brain dysfunction [5, 6, 6]. Despite extensive research, the underlying mechanisms of POD remain incompletely understood. Numerous studies suggest that its pathogenesis may involve neurodegenerative changes, neuroinflammation, sleep disturbances, neurotransmitter imbalances, β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition and excessive tau protein phosphorylation []. The clinical and economic burdens of POD are considerable: it accelerates cognitive decline, increases institutionalization and mortality, and is estimated to incur $26–42 billion in U.S. healthcare costs annually [8, 9].

Despite its clinical importance, few large-scale studies have specifically investigated the incidence and risk factors of POD in patients undergoing lung surgery. To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective analysis using the United States National Inpatient Sample (NIS) to examine the incidence and predictors of POD following elective lung surgery.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data extracted from the United States National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database covering the years 2010 through 2019. The NIS, maintained by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), represents approximately 20% of all inpatient hospitalizations in the US annually, providing a stratified sample from over 1,000 hospitals [10]. This publicly accessible database contains patient demographics, hospital attributes, total hospitalization charges, length of stay (LOS), and diagnostic and procedural codes based on the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM).

Data collection

Data were retrieved from the NIS database for the years 2010 to 2019. We queried the NIS for 2010–2019 hospitalizations of adults (≥ 18 years) with a primary lung cancer diagnosis who underwent elective segmentectomy, lobectomy, or pneumonectomy [11]. Among patients screened for lung surgery, those diagnosed with delirium were identified using ICD-9-CM codes for transient mental disorders, acute and subacute delirium (2930, 2931, 29381, 29382, 29383, 29384, 29389, 2939, 29281, 78097) and ICD-10-CM codes (F05, R418, R4182, R4183, R41840, R41842, R41843, R41844, R4189, F060, F062) [10, 12].

The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: 1)Patients<18 years old; 2)patients with rib fractures or emergency admissions; and 3) preoperative evaluation for delirium or dementia [5]. The recruited participants were divided into two groups based on the incidence of POD, including the delirium group and the non-delirium group. This study extracted and summarised demographic data for each group(Table 1), including gender, race, and age. Outcome indicators such as LOS and total hospitalization costs were also analyzed. Perioperative complications prior to discharge were identified from the database using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes, covering conditions including arrhythmia, acute myocardial infarction, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, respiratory failure, prolonged mechanical ventilation, pneumonia, stroke, sepsis, postoperative shock, urinary tract infection, hypoxemia, postoperative pneumothorax, and other pulmonary complications. Preoperative comorbidities that may be potentially related to the incidence of delirium were also considered, including acquired immune deficiency syndrome, weight loss, nutritional anemia, obesity, rheumatoid arthritis/collagen vascular diseases, other neurological disorders, chronic blood loss anemia, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, coagulopathy, depression, uncomplicated diabetes, diabetes with chronic complications, drug abuse, hypertension, hypothyroidism, liver disease, lymphoma, fluid and electrolyte disorders, metastatic cancer, paralysis, peripheral vascular disease, psychosis, pulmonary circulation disorders, renal failure, non-metastatic solid tumor, peptic ulcer disease, and relationships between bleeding and POD, as well as between valvular disease and POD.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.5.3). For continuous variables, group comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while Chi-square tests were applied for categorical variables to evaluate differences between patients with and without postoperative delirium (POD). To identify factors associated with POD, we constructed both univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression models. All variables provided by the NIS were considered in the initial model. For the multivariable analysis, we employed a stepwise regression strategy to systematically retain significant predictors and reduce the risk of model overfitting. To control for Type I error due to multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction where appropriate. While recognizing the potential for inflated significance in large datasets, we defined statistical significance as P < 0.05 and supplemented all regression outputs with odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to reflect both statistical and clinical relevance [5, 13].

Results

Incidence of POD in lung cancer surgery patients

A total of 30,813 patients who underwent lung cancer surgery were identified from the NIS database between 2010 and 2019. Among them, 1,499 cases of postoperative delirium (POD) were recorded, yielding an overall incidence rate of 4.86% (Table 2). During the 10-year study period, the annual incidence of POD demonstrated a fluctuating trend. The incidence peaked in 2012 (6.02%) and again in 2014 (5.79%), followed by a progressive decline beginning in 2015, reaching the lowest point in 2017 (3.03%). In the final three years of the study (2017–2019), the incidence rate stabilized, fluctuating modestly between 3.03% and 3.39% (Fig. 1).

Demographic characteristics of lung cancer patients with POD

A significant difference in the incidence of POD was observed between genders (P = 0.010), with women accounting for 41.1% of the non-delirium cohort. Moreover, patients who developed POD were significantly older: the ≥ 71-year stratum exhibited a higher delirium rate than the 61–70-year stratum (P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant difference in the incidence of POD was detected across racial groups (P = 0.087). Furthermore, the type of surgery was significantly associated with delirium incidence (P < 0.001): segmentectomy was more frequent in the delirium group (70.4% vs. 60.8%), whereas lobectomy and pneumonectomy were less common.

Risk factors for POD in lung cancer patients

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several independent risk factors for POD (Table 3). Increasing age was strongly associated with higher odds: compared with patients ≤ 50 years (reference), those aged 51–60 years had an OR of 1.18 (95% CI 1.00–1.38; P = 0.050), 61–70 years an OR of 1.59 (95% CI 1.35–1.88; P < 0.001), and ≥ 71 years an OR of 2.10 (95% CI 1.75–2.51; P < 0.001). Female sex was also a modest risk factor (OR 1.12; 95% CI 1.01–1.24; P = 0.036). Insurance status (P = 0.025), teaching hospital status (OR 1.38; 95% CI 1.21–1.57; P < 0.001), urban hospital setting (OR 0.71; 95% CI 0.55–0.91; P = 0.008) and hospital region (P = 0.001) were additionally significant, whereas race and hospital bed size showed no independent effect. Lobectomy and pneumonectomy conferred higher odds than segmentectomy, with ORs of 1.43 (95% CI 1.28–1.60; P < 0.001) and 2.58 (95% CI 2.06–3.23; P < 0.001), respectively.

Relationship between POD and perioperative complications in lung cancer surgery

The association between POD and postoperative complications is summarized in Table 5. In univariate analysis, arrhythmia, deep vein thrombosis, stroke, postoperative shock, urinary tract infection, hypoxemia, and other pulmonary complications were more frequent among patients with delirium (all P < 0.001). Multivariate logistic regression identified arrhythmia (OR 1.95; 95% CI 1.35–2.82; P = 0.001), deep vein thrombosis (OR 1.31; 95% CI 1.08–1.59; P = 0.006), stroke (OR 1.62; 95% CI 1.36–1.92; P < 0.001), postoperative shock (OR 2.14; 95% CI 1.02–4.49; P = 0.044), urinary tract infection (OR 1.42; 95% CI 1.25–1.62; P < 0.001) and hypoxemia (OR 1.48; 95% CI 1.10–1.99; P = 0.009) as independent predictors of delirium. Conversely, respiratory failure was inversely associated (OR 0.71; 95% CI 0.50–0.99; P = 0.043), while acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, continuous ventilatory support, pneumonia, wound infection, postoperative pneumothorax and other pulmonary complications showed no independent effect.

Discussion

Over the 10-year study period, we observed a fluctuating but overall declining trend in the recorded incidence of POD following lung cancer surgery. Notably, the incidence peaked in 2012 (6.02%) and 2014 (5.79%), followed by a progressive decline from 2015 onward, reaching a low of 3.03% in 2017. In the final three years of the study, the incidence remained relatively stable, between 3.03% and 3.39%. This downward trend likely reflects both true reductions in delirium and improvements in perioperative care. Previous epidemiological studies report POD rates of 5–16% following lung resections [14], while broader surgical cohorts—such as a 30-hospital study of over 20,000 older adults—have documented overall delirium rates of approximately 12%, with incidences up to 57% in cardiac surgery patients [15]. Recognized risk factors include advanced age, lower educational attainment, low BMI, and more severe postoperative complication [14, 16]. This temporal pattern may reflect not only changes in the actual occurrence of POD but also evolving clinical practices and documentation behaviors. In light of its significant clinical burden, both the European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care and the American Geriatrics Society recommend a comprehensive, multi-component strategy for the prevention and management of POD-including preoperative risk stratification, minimization of high-risk medications, maintenance of sleep–wake cycles, early mobilization, and cognitive reorientation which may reduce POD incidence by 30–40% [17–19].

Building on the unmet need to stratify delirium risk in lung cancer surgery, our multicenter cohort confirms that both immutable characteristics and intervenable conditions jointly drive POD [20]. Age and sex remain foundational axes of vulnerability, while baseline neuropsychiatric disorders, fluid–electrolyte imbalance and hypertension emerge as pivotal preoperative contributors. Equally, the occurrence of arrhythmias, thromboembolic and cerebrovascular events, infectious complication, and hypoxemic episodes in the postoperative period further amplify delirium risk. Together, these insights advocate for a bundled care approach that marries frailty assessment and psychiatric optimization with rigorous monitoring and early management of cardiothrombotic, metabolic and infective insults to reduce delirium in this high-risk population [17].

Patients who developed delirium following lung surgery had an average hospital stay that was two days longer than those without delirium. This extended hospitalization not only delays postoperative recovery but also imposes a significant financial burden on patients and their families [21]. A study by Inouye et al. recently reported that delirium was associated with an 8.8-fold higher odds of a new dementia diagnosis [4]. This extended burden underscores the necessity for mechanistic studies to delineate the pathophysiology of POD and for interventional trials aimed at reducing its incidence, shortening length of stay and improving overall outcomes [22, 23].

Patients who had POD were notably older than those who did not. The results was consistent with prior studies, which have consistently identified advanced age as an independent risk factor for POD [24, 25]. The mechanisms underlying age-related neurocognitive dysfunction are complex and not fully understood. Existing research suggests that postoperative inflammatory changes, particularly those influencing cerebral blood flow, may contribute to neurocognitive dysfunction in elderly patients [12]. Also, the female sex was identified as a key risk factor for POD, consist with previous study [26]. Estrogen plays a pivotal neuroprotective role, attenuating inflammatory cascades, scavenging reactive oxygen specie and regulating neurotrophic factors within the central nervous system [27]. After menopause, the decline in circulating estrogen may weaken these defenses, rendering elderly women especially susceptible to perioperative neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier disruption [28]. Insurance coverage emerged as an independent determinant of POD risk (P = 0.025). Using Medicare as the reference category, Medicaid beneficiaries exhibited a 24% higher odds of POD, while patients with private insurance or self-pay status showed no significant difference. Notably, those classified as “no charge” had substantially lower odds, suggesting that social support structures or institutional funding mechanisms may mitigate delirium risk. These findings imply that disparities in socioeconomic resources, access to preoperative optimization and postoperative care coordination—particularly for Medicaid recipients—should be addressed through targeted perioperative pathways to equalize outcomes [29, 30].

This study also revealed a significant relationship between the incidence of POD following lung surgery and the presence of multiple preoperative comorbidities. Among these, preoperative neurological disorders were identified as the most prominent risk factor, with the highest odds ratio (OR = 5.62). Additionally, psychiatric disorders were strongly associated with an elevated risk of delirium after lung surgery (OR = 1.62). Other complications fluid and electrolyte imbalances (OR = 1.27), and hypertension (OR = 1.21), were also identified as significant risk factors for POD. Studies suggest that psychiatric disorders are closely linked to hippocampal dysfunction in the brain [26, 31]. Acute traumatic stress, such as that experienced during major surgery, may lead to excessive and prolonged secretion of cortisol. Elevated levels of circulating glucocorticoids can adversely affect hippocampal activity, resulting in attention deficits and cognitive impairment, ultimately triggering POD [32, 33]. Further research is needed to explore the relationship between psychiatric disorders and POD to provide more evidence-based support. Moreover, prolonged hypertension has been linked to altered cerebral blood flow and brain atrophy, resulting in neuronal damage and other pathological changes that contribute to cognitive impairment [34]. Effective management of hypertension through antihypertensive medications has been shown to prevent, delay, or even reverse the progression of POD [35].

Among patients who had postoperative complications, the proportion experiencing delirium was higher compared to those without complications. Specific complications associated with delirium included arrhythmia, deep vein thrombosis, respiratory failure, stroke, sepsis, postoperative shock, urinary tract infection, and hypoxemia. Postoperative shock in lung surgery patients can lead to insufficient cerebral perfusion and impaired oxygenation [36]. Additionally, excessive fluid administration during shock treatment can promote brain edema formation and exacerbate hypoxia and ischemia [37]. Therefore, both mechanisms may contribute to brain injury and trigger delirium [38–40]. Some studies have linked delirium to hypoxemia, identifying it as a risk factor for short-term cognitive impairment, although this association has not been universally confirmed [41, 42].

For patients who experience stroke as a postoperative complication, cerebral hypoperfusion and poor blood circulation can result in hypoxia, which increases the risk of delirium. Schussler et al. reported a pneumonia incidence rate of 2–30% following lung surgery [43]. Inflammatory stimuli may cross the blood-brain barrier and directly damage neurons, increasing biomarkers of neuronal injury and thus the incidence of delirium [44]. Regarding the relationship between urinary tract infections and delirium, one possible explanation may involve the production of cytokines associated with the infection. Cytokines trigger a cascade of reactions, such as the synthesis of interleukins, which may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of delirium [45].

Frailty influences postoperative delirium through various physiological mechanisms. Frail patients face heightened stress during surgery and recovery, potentially disrupting brain function and triggering delirium [46]. Additionally, frailty increases the risk of complications, such as infections and cardiovascular events, which can affect cognitive function. Compared to surgery type, frailty has a more significant impact, potentially serving as a key determinant of postoperative delirium risk [47].

In summary, multiple factors (demographics, underlying diseases, socioeconomics, and postoperative complications) work together to form a high-risk state for POD. Developing intervention measures for different risk types will help reduce the incidence of POD, shorten hospital stays, and improve prognosis. In the future, mechanistic studies should be conducted to reveal the pathological pathways, and intervention trials for high-risk patients should be designed to improve the overall postoperative recovery efficiency of lung surgery patients.

Our study has several notable limitations. Because it is a retrospective analysis of the U.S. National Inpatient Sample, we cannot draw causal inferences and our findings rest entirely on administrative data rather than prospectively collected clinical observations. The NIS provides broad, national coverage but lacks detailed perioperative parameters such as anesthetic depth, intraoperative hemodynamics, surgical duration, blood loss, pain management strategies, and preoperative cognitive or frailty assessments; without these data, unmeasured confounding may influence our results. Delirium identification in the NIS relies on ICD coding, which varies in sensitivity and specificity across institutions and over time; although we harmonized ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes and adjusted for admission year to mitigate temporal inconsistencies, residual misclassification likely remains. Moreover, our ten-year study window (2010–2019) spans evolving diagnostic criteria, clinical practices, and coding systems—particularly during the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10—and despite standardized cross-version mapping, cohort effects may have affected both exposure definitions and outcome ascertainment. By including only elective lung cancer surgery patients discharged alive, we may have underrepresented individuals with severe complications or early mortality, potentially underestimating the true incidence of POD. We conducted an additional sensitivity analysis by stratifying the cohort into two groups based on the coding era: ICD-9 and ICD-10. The results demonstrated that the incidence of postoperative delirium and its associated risk factors were largely consistent across both coding periods, indicating that our primary findings are unlikely to be affected by variations in the coding system.Preventive strategies for postoperative delirium (POD) vary significantly between hospital types, influenced by resources, healthcare professionals’ capabilities, patient demographics, and socioeconomic factors. Teaching hospitals, with more specialized resources and higher care levels, can implement comprehensive, individualized preventive measures. In contrast, non-teaching hospitals, constrained by resources and expertise, may offer simpler strategies, resulting in higher POD incidence. To address these disparities, future efforts should focus on standardizing prevention guidelines, fostering cross-hospital collaboration, and optimizing resource allocation to improve outcomes across all settings.Finally, because the NIS overrepresents larger urban and academic hospitals, our findings may not generalize to smaller, rural, or non-U.S. healthcare settings where perioperative protocols, patient demographics, and resource availability differ substantially. Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating standardized, bedside delirium assessments and detailed intraoperative data are warranted to validate and extend our observations.

Conclusions

In this national cohort of 30,809 elective lung cancer resections, POD affected 4.86% of patients and exhibited a peak incidence early in our study period before stabilizing around 3% from 2017 onward. Advanced age emerged as the strongest predictor, with patients ≥ 71 years more than twice as likely to develop POD. Female sex, teaching-hospital status, and more extensive resections were also associated with higher risk, while urban hospitals showed a protective effect. Preoperative neurologic disorders, psychoses, fluid–electrolyte imbalances, and hypertension increased POD odds, whereas chronic pulmonary disease was protective. Key perioperative complications—including arrhythmia, deep-vein thrombosis, stroke, shock, urinary infection, and hypoxemia—further heightened risk. These findings identify high-risk subgroups and reinforce the need for targeted, multicomponent prevention strategies in thoracic surgery patients.
