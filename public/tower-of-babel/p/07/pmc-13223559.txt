Microplanning for entomological surveys to sustain the onchocerciasis elimination effort in West Africa
Authors: Ngayo Sy, Lassane Koala, Alioune Seck, Adoum Mahamat Oumar, Olabanji Surakat, Kareen Atekem, Franklin Ayisi, Dziedzom K de Souza, Anne Heggen, Jamie Tallant, Charles D Mackenzie, Daniel A Boakye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFormer Onchocerciasis Control Programme countries like Senegal have made important progress towards eliminating onchocerciasis. Senegal has engaged in post-mass drug administration (MDA) activities, mainly entomological surveillance, but the country initially struggled to collect the recommended number of blackflies in all the country’s transmission zones. Here, we implemented a simple approach in the entomological surveillance protocol to increase the number of Simulium damnosum collected.MethodsThe study was conducted over two consecutive years, 2022 and 2023, in the main S. damnosum capture points located along the Gambie and Falémé river basins. We reviewed environmental data, analysed and interpreted previous blackflies capture data and processed field visits to identify optimal periods of blackfly capture in each capture point.ResultsAfter the implementation of our approach, the number of blackflies captured increased by approximately 42% in 2023 compared with 2022 captures. The numbers of blackflies captured increased from 71 999 and 23 666, respectively, in Gambia and Falémé in 2022, to 101 276 and 34 780 in 2023.ConclusionOur results reveal that simple and effective microplanning of blackfly capture periods can support onchocerciasis post-MDA surveillance activities by increasing the number of blackflies captured.

Introduction

Onchocerciasis is a neglected tropical disease (NTD) caused by Onchocerca volvulus (Nematoda: Filarioidea), a filariasis transmitted to humans through the bites of Simulium (Diptera: Simuliidae) blackflies.1 Onchocerciasis is currently endemic to 31 countries in sub-Saharan Africa, in Yemen, and now only two countries (Venezuela and Brazil) in the Americas.2 After several decades of vector control in 11 countries in West Africa (Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Togo, Benin, Mali, Burkina Faso, Guinée, Gambia, Sierra Leone, Niger and Senegal), the Onchocerciasis Control Programme (OCP) succeeded in eliminating this disease as a public health problem.1 Currently, the WHO has targeted the interruption of onchocerciasis transmission to be achieved in at least 12 endemic countries by 2030.3 The main strategy recommended for onchocerciasis control and elimination is the mass drug administration (MDA) of ivermectin. By using this strategy, most of the countries formerly under the OCP have made important progress towards the target of eliminating transmission. Indeed, Niger was recently the first country in Africa verified as having reached elimination of transmission of onchocerciasis,4 and Senegal has stopped ivermectin distribution, as it has obtained evidence that transmission has been interrupted. Senegal is currently engaged in post-MDA activities based upon surveillance of the parasite infection in the vector Simulium damnosum s.l. populations, as recommended by the WHO.1 To accomplish this, the National Program for the Elimination of Lymphatic Filariasis and Onchocerciasis (PNEFO: Programme National d'Elimination de la Filariose lymphatique et de l'Onchocercose) conducts regular entomological surveillance along the two endemic basins of the country, Gambie and Falémé. The regions bordering the two rivers’ basins are considered the onchocerciasis transmission zones of Senegal.

The WHO recommends the collection and analysis of a minimum of 6000 flies per transmission zone.1 Unfortunately, during the first year of surveillance, the PNEFO struggled to collect sufficient flies at all capture areas, which is important to have a reliable record of the scale of onchocerciasis transmission in the transmission zones. Unforeseen and variable factors, such as the irregularity of rainfall and the consequent productivity of larval breeding sites, influence the abundance of blackfly adults.5 Additionally, other manageable factors, such as the selection of capture sites and the timing of capture periods, when inadequately planned, can negatively impact blackfly catches. A common example of inadequate planning occurs when the primary driver for NTD programme planning is financial resource availability. As a result, activities may start after the peak abundance of S. damnosum or end prematurely before it reaches its peak, leading to an insufficient number of flies being collected for a proper assessment.

The issue of capturing enough flies is particularly important for assessing elimination of transmission, especially as programmes move closer toward national elimination goals and subsequent surveillance activities.1,5 The Senegal PNEFO planned entomological surveillance at fixed time periods and set capture points. However, during a programme review, the team identified this approach as a challenge for effective fly capture. This article argues that, with improved planning, including the effective interpretation of previous entomological surveillance data and recent ground prospection, it is possible to estimate the productivity of S. damnosum breeding sites within weeks. This approach can significantly enhance entomological surveillance and support achievement of the surveillance goals.

Methods

Study sites

Senegal is endemic to five NTDs that require preventive chemotherapy, including onchocerciasis. Onchocerciasis is endemic along the river basins of Gambia, Falémé and their tributaries, which cover an area of ​​approximately 36 000 km2 or 18% of the national territory. Eight health districts are endemic for onchocerciasis, four in the Tambacounda region (Tambacounda, Dianké Makha, Goudiry and Kidira), three in the Kédougou region (Kédougou, Saraya, Salémata) and one in the region of Kolda (Vélingara). Senegal has provided evidence of the interruption of local transmission of onchocerciasis and has started post-treatment surveillance in the country's two transmission zones, namely the Falémé and Gambia river basins. The study area includes 21 capture points, 14 of which are located along the Gambia basin and seven along the Falémé basin. The capture points were located near former endemic villages. In addition, according to the WHO criteria, the capture points were in a shaded place, sheltered from the wind, not a place with crowds of people, and were accessible all year round.5 Previous studies conducted in the regions indicated that the savanna group, Simulium damnosum sensu stricto and Simulium sirbanum, are the main vectors of onchocerciasis in Senegal’s endemic regions.6,7 The two species are onchocerciasis competent vectors with the ability to carry out long-range migration.5,7

Study design

The study was conducted over two consecutive years, 2022 and 2023. In the first year of the study, the programme conducted fly captures by choosing the same capture period for all capture points, disregarding previous capture data or the particularity of each capture point. In the second year, the team utilised fly capture data from the first year to inform and refine microplanning efforts. In 2022, the PNEFO carried out captures of S. damnosum in 21 capture points from September to November. This survey resulted in the collection of 95 665 blackflies, including 71 999 from the Gambia basin and 23 666 from the Falémé basin. The results of this collection are summarised in Table 1.

Microplanning activities focused on key factors to enhance fly capture, including whether collection started early enough, lasted sufficiently long and aligned with peak blackfly activity periods throughout the day. Examination of the programme results shows that many capture points had collected >1000 blackflies in the first month of collection, indicating that blackfly densities were already high several weeks before the month of September, the originally planned starting collection date. In addition, captures were stopped in December while most sites continued to collect significant numbers of blackflies; indicating sites were still productive well after the month of December, the ending month of fly collection. In addition, the PNEFO adhered to the WHO-OCP capture protocol, carrying out captures from 07:00 h to 18:00 h.5 However, in this part of Senegal, sunset occurs after 19:00 h, and blackfly activity is known to increase in the hours leading up to sunset. Consequently, stopping captures at 18:00 h likely played a significant role in the collectors’ inability to capture adequate numbers of blackflies.

Capture of adult blackflies

Blackfly captures were carried out from September to December 2022 and from July 2023 to January 2024 in all sites. Blackfly captures were carried out every day from 07:00 h to 18:00 h using the standard human landing catches (HLC) process.5,8,9 The catchers settle in the capture point and bare their legs to expose them to the blackflies. Each female blackfly that lands on the leg of the captor is immediately covered and enclosed in a capture tube before she begins her biting process. The catchers take turns capturing the blackflies, for 1 h each. The captured blackflies are kept alive, and wrapped in damp cotton wool, for dissection. Blackfly captures were carried out 2–10 d per month in each capture point during the study period. Blackflies intended for molecular analyses were captured and preserved in alcohol for subsequent analyses. At the end of the day, the capturers record on a capture sheet the number of females captured, as well as information relating to the weather conditions (rain, wind) that prevailed during the capture.

Morphological identification of collected blackflies

The morphological identification of blackflies was carried out in stages according to the criteria set by the WHO.5 First, S. damnosum s.l. was distinguished from other blackfly species based on the black coloration of the legs cuticle, except for the posterior basitarsus having a broad creamy white band, the broadened and flattened shape of the anterior basitarsus and the presence of silvery lateral scales on the abdomen.5 Next, the different groups (savannah or forest) of the S. damnosum complex were distinguished using characterisations based on the observation of the colour of the tufts of bristles located at the base of the wings, the relative colour of the antennae, of the procoxa, prosternum and mesonotum.5

Ethical considerations

Senegal’s Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health approved all PNEFO activities related to onchocerciasis surveillance and elimination activities. The study was deemed to be research not involving human subjects because the activities were part of routine surveillance for an infectious disease by the Senegal public health authorities. The study was clearly explained in French and the local language to all the study participants. All those involved in the blackflies collection with the HLC were volunteers from endemic communities. The volunteers for HLC signed consent forms after being informed of the project’s activities. The consent forms were in French and their local languages. To minimise the risk of fly bites, the selected volunteers were shown how to select a position for blackfly capture, how to use the HLC method to effectively capture blackflies without being bitten and how to report the daily collections in the capture sheets. Once the demonstrations were over, the collectors were equipped with capture tubes and spent the entire day capturing blackflies. These captures were made under the supervision of the PNEFO team, who ensured that they had fully understood the technique and instructions and that they were capturing blackflies and no other similar insects. To further reduce risk, all local study participants received ivermectin treatment as part of the national programme’s mass distribution campaigns.

The initial capture programme was maintained for the Wassadou depot and Inake capture points. The start of captures was brought back to July in 16 capture points and the end of captures was extended to December for 17 points, and until January for four capture points.

Blackflies abundance

In 2022, as in 2023, the peak of blackfly densities was observed in the month of October. All captured blackflies were morphologically identified as belonging to the savannah group, Simulium damnosum sensu stricto and Simulium sirbanum. A total of 136 056 blackflies were captured in 2023/2024 compared with 95 665 blackflies in 2022, a difference of 40 391 blackflies (42.22% increase in catches). From September to December 2022, 95 611 blackflies were captured compared with 89 504 during the same period in 2023. Extending activities to July, August and January, months which had been excluded from the collection period in 2022, made it possible to collect 46 552 blackflies, or 34% of the total collection for 2023. Discussion

The implementation of our approach allowed the Senegal PNEFO to collect significantly more blackflies in 2023 compared with 2022, with collections increasing by approximately 42% in 2023. We found that the period of high productivity for blackflies starts from June–July and extends until January–February. The WHO recommends capturing blackflies throughout the period of high transmission, that is, the period of high blackfly densities, for entomological surveillance of onchocerciasis.1 This time frame would allow to have a representative transmission situation as a function of time and space. This period of high blackfly productivity is not specific to Senegal. Simulium damnosum are more abundant in the same periods for other West African countries such as Ghana or Burkina Faso.9,10

Assessment of the timing of collections by basin revealed that most collections in the Gambia and Falémé basins were carried out in September and October. However, more than one-half of the capture points in the Gambia basin remained productive until January, while blackfly densities began to decline from November in the Falémé basin. This highlights significant differences between individual capture points. For example, certain capture points in the Gambia, such as Itato, Bantacocouta and Diaguiry Point, reached their peak densities in December, at the end of the rainy season. Scheduling captures in these points from September to December, as was done in 2022, resulted in missing these peak densities. Seven capture points, consisting of five on the Gambia and two on Falémé, yielded relatively fewer blackflies in 2023 compared with 2022. This is potentially due to the variation in the densities of blackflies. The adult densities vary as a function of larvae; changes in larvae populations directly influence adult densities.11

The larvae population was influenced by the timing and strength of rainfall, which affects the level of various watercourses, thus creating either early or late water flow necessary for the establishment of S. damnosum at a breeding site. Certain anthropogenic activities, such as artisanal gold panning in the border areas of Senegal with Guinea or Mali, can also influence the establishment of blackflies in a breeding. These observations, based on data from 2 y of activity, demonstrate that better planning and knowledge of factors, such as climate, anthropogenic activities and the diligence of vector collectors, can improve the number of flies caught. It also emphasises the importance of collecting baseline entomological data before surveys are planned and implemented. In the current context of post-MDA and postelimination surveillance activities, our approach can assist onchocerciasis programmes in endemic countries in improving their entomological surveillance efforts.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that effective microplanning of blackfly capture activities based on the local context of each country can enhance entomological monitoring by significantly increasing the number of blackflies captured and avoiding wasted resources. This approach can be easily adapted to the local context of other countries endemic to onchocerciasis to support their entomological surveillance activities and progress to their goal of elimination of transmission.
