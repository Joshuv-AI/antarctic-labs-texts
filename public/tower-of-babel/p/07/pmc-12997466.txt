Natural history of latent brucellosis and diagnostic performance of B-SPOT: a 24-month prospective cohort study based on large-scale screening in northern China
Authors: Hongyu Wang, Yan O. Wang, Guanmin Yuan, Dan Li, Yutong Chen, Yan A. Wang, Quanlin Xue, Jianpeng Cai, Yuhan Sun, Jingjing Zhao, Yulei Sun, Binhuang Sun, Wei Guo, Xiaokun Liu, Hongyan Liu, Jingwen Ai, Wenhong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhether latent brucellosis exists prior to symptom onset and predisposes individuals to active disease remains to be determined. We established a novel ELISpot method for brucellosis (B-SPOT) based on host-specific immune. Diagnostic efficacy was evaluated in a testing cohort comprising brucellosis and non-brucellosis participants. A large-scale screening, using serum agglutination test (SAT) and B-SPOT for latent brucellosis, was later performed in Kangping, a brucellosis-endemic area in northern China, followed by a 24-month prospective cohort study to observe the natural development of latent brucellosis without any intervention. Among 41 brucellosis and 57 non-brucellosis participants, the sensitivity of B-SPOT for brucellosis was 95.1%, and the specificity was 98.2%, compared to the combination of blood culture and SAT. 2459 residents were screened, and the prevalence of latent brucellosis was 3.3% (81/2459). Among 63 latent brucellosis cases successfully followed, 33.3% (21/63) progressed to acute brucellosis within 2 years, while none in the control group progressed(p < 0.001). 16/21 (76.2%) progressed within the first six months, and 14/21 (66.7%) presented with joint pain as their initial symptom. B-SPOT (10.94[95%CI 2.48–48.33, p = 0.002]) outperformed SAT (3.04[95%CI 1.14–8.01, p = 0.026]) in predicting the progression of latent brucellosis. Latent brucellosis accounts for 3.3% in the endemic area, among whom 33.3% progressed to acute brucellosis within two years. The first six months are a critical window, and joint pain is the primary initial symptom. B-SPOT demonstrates high diagnostic efficacy for brucellosis disease activity and can better predict the progression of latent brucellosis.

Introduction

Brucellosis is a zoonotic disease caused by pathogenic Brucella spp., including Brucella melitensis, Brucella abortus, Brucella suit and Brucella canis. Recent research indicates that the global incidence of human brucellosis is higher than previously estimated, with 1.6–2.1 million new cases annually [1,2]. The disease is prevalent in regions including the Mediterranean, the Middle East, Central Asia, parts of Africa, and South America. Countries with the highest incidence rates include Iran, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan [3]. Brucellosis has a variety of clinical presentations, including undulant fever, fatigue, joint and muscle pain, and sometimes involve the central nervous system, urogenital system and heart valves. Chronic brucellosis would even cause persistent damage to multiple organs. Earlier diagnosis and intervention are of great significance for further optimizing the prevention and control of this disease.

Isolating Brucella spp. from clinical samples is the "gold standard" for diagnosing human brucellosis [4,5]. However, positivity for blood culture and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is extremely low [6]. Immunological tests hold significant diagnostic value in brucellosis. Currently, the serum agglutination test (SAT) is the most common laboratory test in China, which detects all agglutinating antibodies (including immunoglobulin M, immunoglobulin A, immunoglobulin G) against Brucella S-lipopolysaccharide antigen [7]. However, SAT could remain positive after effective treatment, which was not in accordance with disease status [5,8,9]. Developing new immunological methods will be significant for diagnosis and treatment.

Since 1938, latent brucellosis has been suggested to describe someone who never had an attack of brucellosis-related symptoms but was present with a positive SAT result [10–12], with the reported prevalence ranging from 1% to 16% [13–17]. However, all the studies above were collected from hospitals, household members or high-risk populations (veterinarians, cattle farmers, vaccination personnel, etc.). The existence of latent brucellosis remains controversial, and the natural prevalence remains to be determined. Researches have shown that latent brucellosis may have the potential for disease activity. A meta-analysis including 13 previous studies with 107 asymptomatic cases demonstrated that 15.4% latent cases would develop symptoms during a follow-up period of 0.5–18 months [18]. Jing Hu et al. screened 595 family members of shepherds in Jilin Province, China, and conducted an 18-month follow-up on 15 asymptomatic seropositive subjects, finding that 7 of these individuals (7/15) developed brucellosis within 18 months [19]. Currently, there is still a lack of studies that follow a sufficiently large population with latent brucellosis over a longer period.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) quantitatively measured the cytokine secretion from antigen-specific immune cells [20]. T-Spot ®.TB test was the Mycobacterium tuberculosis-specific ELISpot Assay. After incubation with early secreted antigenic target 6 (ESAT6) and culture filtrate protein 10 (CFP 10) antigens, specific T cells were stimulated and secreted interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), which will be captured by anti-human IFN-γ antibodies and stained [21], which has been widely used in diagnosing active and latent tuberculosis infection worldwide [22–24]. As Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Brucella spp. were facultatively intracellular pathogens and mainly infect macrophages [25], some studies suggest that ELISpot could also be used to assess the Brucella infection among animals by detecting T-cell responses to specific antigens in peripheral blood [26]. However, related research reports are limited, and none have been reported in human beings.

This study aimed to figure out whether latent brucellosis exists prior to symptom onset and whether it predisposes individuals to active disease. First, we established a novel ELISpot assay for brucellosis named B-SPOT, which detected IFN-γ secretion from antigen-specific T cells after incubation with Brucella outer membrane protein 25/31 (Omp25/31) peptide. Second, a test cohort, including brucellosis and non-brucellosis participants, was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of B-SPOT. Subsequently, a large-scale screening, using SAT and B-SPOT, was conducted among residents in a brucellosis-endemic area, followed by a 24-month follow-up, to characterize the natural history and evaluate the B-SPOT efficacy in latent brucellosis (Figure 1(A)). Methods

The test cohort

Our study included a test cohort and a screening cohort. The test cohort was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of B-SPOT. Patients diagnosed with brucellosis (brucellosis group) or excluded from brucellosis (the non-brucellosis group) were recruited from Huashan Hospital affiliated to Fudan University and the Sixth People's Hospital of Shenyang from August 2022 to August 2023. The gold standard was the combination of blood culture and SAT, based on Chinese brucellosis consensus as previously described: patients, who had brucellosis-related symptoms (undulant fever, arthritis, etc.) and epidemiologic history, and meet at least one of the following diagnostic result: (1) positive culture of Brucella spp. in blood or other sample; (2) Positive SAT with titers of >1:100 [8,27]. The epidemiologic history, clinical manifestation and peripheral blood samples were collected. All patients accepted the SAT and B-SPOT assays. The infection pathogens of the non-brucellosis group are listed in Supplement Table S1.

The screening cohort

We later performed a large-scale cross-sectional screening followed by a screening cohort study to investigate the incidence and natural history of latent brucellosis from 16 villages of Kangping County, Shenyang, one of the brucellosis-endemic areas in northern China. From July to August 2022, we recruited residents from 16 villages through voluntary registration for free physical examinations. Participants underwent questionnaires, basic physical check-ups, and blood tests.

The inclusion criteria were (1) Age: 18–80 years, no gender restriction; (2) Currently residing in brucellosis endemic areas. The exclusion criteria were those who were previously diagnosed with brucellosis or received related antimicrobial treatment.

Epidemiologic history, brucellosis-related clinical manifestations, and medical history were collected on the screening through a questionnaire and peripheral blood samples were drawn. All participants were tested for the rose Bengal plate test (RBPT), a serial screening test for human brucellosis, on enrollment. All RBPT-positive cases and an equal number of negative cases were further tested for B-SPOT and SAT.

We followed up all subjects by telephone at periods of 6, 12 and 24 months after enrollment, while peripheral blood was additionally collected from subjects, whose SAT or B-SPOT was positive. The primary outcome was brucellosis progression, and the secondary outcome was the emergence of self-reported brucellosis-related symptoms and seroconversion.

Ethical statement

Informed consent was obtained from all participants and guardians, and the study protocol was approved by local institutional review boards (KY2022-1011, 2022-07-001-01).

Sample size evaluation

For the test cohort to evaluate the efficacy of the diagnosis for B-spot, we used PASS software (version 15) to calculate the sample size. We set a two-sided interval, with the confidence interval of 0.95 (α = 0.05) and confidence interval width of 0.20 (β). The estimated sensitivity was 90%, and the specificity was 90%. At least 44 human brucellosis and 44 non-brucellosis participants should be recruited.

For the screening cohort to screen possible latent brucellosis, we take the formula to calculate the sample size: n = [DEFF * Np(1 − p)]/[(d2/Z21−α/2) * (N − 1) + p * (1 − p)] (DEFF = 1) [28]. We set a finite population (N = 1,000,000), a two-sided interval type (Z1−α/2 = 1.96) and set the prevalence of latent brucellosis among residents as 5% (p), as previously reported [15,16]. The confidence limit was 1% (d), and the confidence interval was 95%. At least 1822 residents should be recruited.

Brucellosis-related definition

In this study, we defined each phase of human brucellosis according to immune response, symptom and treatment course (Supplement Figure 1). Acute brucellosis was defined as participants who had brucellosis-related symptoms (fever, fatigue, muscle pain, arthritis, central nervous system infection, etc.), at least 1 positive laboratory result (blood culture, PCR, next-generation sequencing or SAT), and treatment duration of less than 6 months. Chronic brucellosis was defined as participants, who were still present with brucellosis-related symptoms and at least 1 positive laboratory result after standard antibiotic treatment for more than 6 months.

Treatment-naïve brucellosis was defined as acute brucellosis cases, who have not yet started standard antibiotic therapy before. Treatment-experienced brucellosis was defined as those who have started standard antibiotic therapy, with symptoms either relieved or not relieved within the past 6 months.

Latent brucellosis was defined as participants, who had never been diagnosed with brucellosis previously, nor present with brucellosis-related symptoms, but with positive SAT or B-SPOT test. Brucellosis progression was defined as latent brucellosis present with both newly emergent brucellosis-related symptoms and positive laboratory results during the follow-up.

Omp25- and Omp31-derived peptide library

We selected the predominant proteins Omp25 and Omp31 from the Brucella outer membrane protein (OMP) family as target antigens. Species specificity was verified using NCBI BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). Protein structure was evaluated using DNASTAR Lasergene software (version 15). Each protein was split into short peptides consisting of 10–15 amino acids, which were individually assessed for MHC I immunogenicity using the iedb tool (http://tools.iedb.org/immunogenicity/). Following a comprehensive evaluation, 46 Omp31 peptides and 40 Omp25 peptides were chemically synthesized and dissolved in a final concentration of 1 mg/ml per peptide in storage (Supplement Table S2). Peptide solutions were stored long-term at −80°C. Working solutions were prepared by diluting peptide storage solutions to 10 ug/ml and were temporarily stored at −20°C.

B-SPOT assays

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from peripheral blood samples by density gradient centrifugation and adjusted to a concentration of 2.5 × 106 cells/mL in AIMV cell culture medium. Every sample was distributed into 3 cell culture wells, with 50 µL mixed Brucella antigen work solution, RPMI 1640 (negative control), and phytohemagglutinin (PHA, positive control) added separately. Subsequently, 100 µL prepared cell suspension was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 16–20 h.

After incubation, the plates were washed four times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Then, 50 µL alkaline phosphatase-labelled mouse anti-human interferon-γ antibody was added to each well, followed by incubation at 4°C for 1 h. After four washes with PBS, 50 µL NBT/BCIP substrate solution was added to each well. The plates were incubated in the dark for 7 min to develop colour. The reaction was stopped with distilled water, and after drying, the number of spot-forming cells (SFCs) specific to Brucella antigens will be stained blue and counted.

Rose Bengal Plate Test (RBPT)

Thirty microliters (30 µL) of serum sample and 30 μL of antigen (Sinoreca Biotechnology (Beijing) Co., Ltd) were taken on the slide and mixed thoroughly. The appearance of definite clumping/agglutination within 3 min was considered a positive reaction.

Serum Agglutination Test (SAT)

SAT (Sinoreca Biotechnology (Beijing) Co., Ltd) was carried out in a tube employing a double-dilution method starting with an initial 1/25 dilution and utilizing fixed Brucella antigen. After an incubation period of 20–22 h at 37°C, the SAT reactions were examined. The serum dilution, showing more than 50% agglutination, was considered the highest agglutinating titer. A positive result was defined as an agglutinating titer of 1:50 or higher.

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 µL of whole blood using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany, Cat. No. 69506), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration and purity of DNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and all samples had an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0. Brucella in whole blood samples was detected using a one-tube nested quantitative real-time PCR assay targeting the Omp31 gene, following the manufacturer's instructions (Brilliant Science, Yunnan, China) [29].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the R software 4.2.2, SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0. Normal distribution and homoscedasticity of the continuous values were assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Levene test. The χ2 test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variations. The Wilcox test was used to compare SAT titers. Student's t-test was used to compare SFCs and other continuous variations. The diagnostic performance of B-SPOT is evaluated using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). The AUC value ranges from >0.5 to 1, with values closer to 1, indicating better diagnostic values. The prediction efficacy of B-SPOT and SAT for latent brucellosis progression was plotted by the Kaplan-Meier curve and compared using Cox regression. All tests were two-tailed, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Result

Diagnostic efficacy evaluation of B-SPOT in brucellosis among the test cohorts

We established a Brucella-specific peptide library targeting the outer membrane protein 25/31 family of Brucella. Using the ELISpot method, we determined the optimal stimulation concentration for peripheral blood mononuclear cells and established the B-SPOT experimental method (Figure 1(B)).

From August 2022 to August 2023, we enrolled 41 brucellosis patients and 57 non-brucellosis participants (51 non-brucellosis clinical patients + 6 healthy controls, infection pathogens were listed in Supplement Table S1) in Shanghai, China, as the B-SPOT testing cohort. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells and plasma samples were collected, and all underwent SAT and B-SPOT testing. The baseline characteristics of these 98 participants are shown in Supplement Table S3. Among the 41 brucellosis patients, 26 (63.4%) were male, with a median (Interquartile range, IQR) age of 53.0 years (43.0-58.0 years). Eight patients (19.5%) had treatment-naïve brucellosis, 16 patients (39.0%) had treatment-experienced brucellosis, and 17 patients (41.5%) had chronic brucellosis. Among the 57 non-brucellosis participants, 51 were infected with other pathogens, and 6 were healthy controls. Thirty-four (59.6%) were male, with a median (IQR) age of 61 years (39.5–69.5 years). Compared to the non-brucellosis group, the brucellosis group was more likely to have a history of livestock contact (p < 0.001) and joint pain (p < 0.001), but a lower prevalence of hypertension (p = 0.010).

The median (IQR) SFCs of B-SPOT were 52 (25–78) in the brucellosis group, and 0 (0–2) in the non-brucellosis group. Taking the combination of blood culture and SAT as the gold standard (at least one of the two was positive), we plotted the ROC curve for B-SPOT (Figure 1(C)), with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.986. Using a cutoff of 6 as the optimal threshold, the sensitivity of B-SPOT was 95.1%, and the specificity was 98.3%. Further a Spearman rank correlation test showed a high consistency between B-SPOT and SAT (rho = 0.87, p < 0.001). The only false positive case in B-SPOT was infection with Talaromyces marneffei, which tested negative in SAT. Another false positive case in SAT was infected with Nocardia, which tested negative in B-SPOT. Besides, all 41 brucellosis samples went through PCR for Brucella Omp31, and 2 samples were reported as positive.

Next, we evaluated the effectiveness of B-SPOT in assessing brucellosis at different stages (Figure 1(E,F)). The SFCs of treatment-naïve brucellosis (87 [55.5–99]) were significantly higher than those of chronic brucellosis (p < 0.001). The SFCs of treatment-experienced brucellosis were relatively lower than treatment-naïve cases, but higher than chronic cases (p > 0.05). Using a cutoff of 60.5, the sensitivity of B-SPOT in distinguishing between treatment-naïve and chronic brucellosis was 76.5%, with a specificity of 75.0% and an AUC of 0.768 (Figure 1(F)).

Large-scale cross-sectional screening for latent brucellosis in northern China followed by a 24-month prospective cohort study

Next, we conducted a large-scale epidemiological investigation in 16 epidemic villages in Shenyang, Liaoning Province, followed by a 24-month prospective cohort study (Figure 2(A)). A total of 2663 residents were enrolled. Fifty-nine cases without blood samples, 45 cases with a history of brucellosis, and 100 cases that did not complete the questionnaire were excluded, and 2459 cases were finally analysed. Among these 2459 cases, 1191 (48.4%) were male, with a median age of 57 years (IQR: 50–65), and 1394 (56.7%) had a history of close contact with livestock. 81 were tested positive with RBPT, and all of them were SAT positive, with the ratio of 3.3% (81/2459). To compare the diagnostic consistency between SAT and B-SPOT among residents in epidemic areas, we further conducted B-SPOT testing on 73 SAT-positive and 84 SAT-negative samples according to their age, gender and contact history, while excluding 8 SAT-positive samples that didn't have enough blood for further testing. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, sex, and exposure history (Supplement Table S4). Using SAT as the gold standard, B-SPOT demonstrated a high consistency with SAT with a sensitivity of 86.3% and a specificity of 100.0% in diagnosing latent brucellosis (Supplement Table S4).

We conducted a 24-month follow-up since enrollment, collecting data on the incidence of acute brucellosis through questionnaires at 6, 12 and 24 months, and assessing immunological changes through blood samples (Figure 2(B), Table 1). Among the 81 cases of latent brucellosis, 63 individuals were successfully followed, of whom 21/63 (33.3%) developed brucellosis within one year, with 14/21 (66.7%) presenting joint pain as their initial symptom. In contrast, none of the 402 immunologically negative cases developed acute brucellosis over the past years, indicating a higher risk for brucellosis progression among latent brucellosis (p < 0.001) (Table 1). Among the 21 new cases, 16/21 (76.2%) progressed to acute brucellosis within the first 6 months, suggesting that the first six months could be a critical window for brucellosis progression (Figure 2(B)). The predictive value of B-SPOT in brucellosis progression

We next compared the SFCs between latent brucellosis in the prospective cohort and treatment-naïve brucellosis in the test cohort. The results indicated no significant difference in SFCs between these 2 groups (Figure 3(A)). The cutoff value was 63, with an AUC of 61%, a sensitivity of 75%, and a specificity of 65.3% (Supplement Figure 2). In contrast, treatment-naïve brucellosis cases exhibited relatively higher SAT titers (Figure 3(B)) (p < 0.001), indicating that SAT performs better in differentiating latent brucellosis from acute infection. Further survival analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive efficacy of SAT and B-SPOT for brucellosis progression among residents in the epidemic area (Figure 3(C–E)). The hazard ratio (HR) for SAT positivity in predicting brucellosis progression within one year was 3.04 (95% CI 1.14–8.01, p = 0.026), while the HR for B-SPOT positivity or both SAT and B-SPOT positivity in predicting brucellosis progression was 10.94 (2.48–48.33, p = 0.002), indicating a significant advantage of using both tests over SAT alone in predicting the progression of latent brucellosis (Figure 3).

Discussion

B-SPOT demonstrated a sensitivity of 95.1% and a specificity of 98.2% compared to the combination of blood culture and SAT. The sensitivity for distinguishing between treatment-naïve and chronic cases was 76.5%, with a specificity of 75.0%. Screening among 2663 residents in the endemic area revealed a prevalence of 3.3% for latent brucellosis. During a 24-month follow-up, 33.3% of the latent brucellosis patients progressed to brucellosis within one year, while none in the control group developed the disease. The first six months following the diagnosis of latent brucellosis were identified as a critical window for brucellosis progression, with joint pain being the main initial symptom. Although B-SPOT could not distinguish between latent and acute brucellosis, it outperformed SAT in predicting brucellosis progression.

In our study, B-spot demonstrated a satisfying diagnostic accuracy compared to the combination of blood culture and SAT, which was the most common adapted laboratory diagnosis pathway in China. We took a combination method for the gold standard because the blood culture had an extremely low positivity, while the SAT could cause false positivity. The poor diagnostic performance of PCR in this study may be attributed to prolonged sample storage. A single gold standard could cause a wrong evaluation (high false positivity or false negativity) for any new laboratory method in brucellosis. This novel test could provide additional evidence for diagnosing human brucellosis in the future. There were also other ELISpot methods reported for brucellosis [26,30]. However, all these methods previously reported were tested in stocks; none of them had been validated among human beings. Another difference was that they used Brucella purified protein derivative (Br-PPD), which was a mixture of all the proteins with a wide range of sensitivity and specificity. The purification from pathogenic strains could cause additional safety concerns. We used chemically synthesized OMP family proteins, which demonstrated high species specificity and only crossed with some non-pathogenic strains, including Ochrobactrum spp., Falsochrobactrum spp., Phyllobacterium leguminum, etc., according to the blast results. In our clinical practice, the only false positivity for B-SPOT was the Talaromyces marneffei. The cross-reaction could possibly be because we split the complete protein into short peptides of 15 amino acids in length. In the future, the composition of this short peptide library needs to be optimized.

Although B-SPOT effectively distinguishes brucellosis from non-brucellosis cases, it fails to clearly differentiate between latent, treatment-naïve, and chronic brucellosis. The small sample size could be one limitation and should be improved in future research. However, T-Spot®.TB assay demonstrated similar problems. ELISpot detected host adaptive immune response to infection, instead of infection itself, which could be “memorized” by the host for a long time. A combination of ELISpot and other clinical examinations can enhance its discriminative power. Luo Y et al. suggested that blood routine examination plus T-Spot®.TB assay could help improve the efficacy for distinguishing active and latent tuberculosis infection [31]. Artificial intelligence, like a convolutional neural network, could also serve as a new tool to read the SFCs image [32], which could be used for B-SPOT in the future to improve the efficacy in evaluating disease status.

The prevalence of latent brucellosis in the endemic rural area is 3.3% in our study. This ratio is slightly lower than previously reported [15,16]. One explanation is that we visited all 16 villages and recruited all the participants in the real rural area, instead of collecting samples from the hospital. Another reason is that we excluded residents who had diagnosed with brucellosis or suffered from suspected symptoms. Thus, we believed our study provided a more objective estimate of the prevalence of latent brucellosis. The long-term follow-up suggested that these participants had a higher risk of developing brucellosis. Its primary public health significance lies in providing a deeper understanding of the disease burden, while also offering a theoretical basis for the surveillance of high-risk populations and the advancement of the treatment window.

Whether to intervene in latent brucellosis requires more in-depth exploration in the future. For latent tuberculosis infection at high risk for progressing to active tuberculosis, it is recommended to use medication for effective prevention and control [33]. It is anticipated that the risk factors for the progression of latent tuberculosis and latent brucellosis may differ; therefore, identifying the risk factors for the progression of latent brucellosis to active infection is key in deciding whether to implement treatment measures. We found that the B-SPOT score is a risk factor for the progression of latent brucellosis to active brucellosis. Future research is needed to further explore the application of B-SPOT in latent brucellosis intervention.

This study has several limitations. In the diagnostic cohort, we only included a small subset of the population, and the study was conducted based on a priori cohorts with already established diagnoses. Intracellular pathogens, including Listeria spp., mycobacterium tuberculosis, Salmonella spp., should be additionally evaluated. In the future, a prospective cohort is needed to evaluate its diagnostic efficacy. Other pathogen detection method including PCR or antigen detection, should also be considered. In the screening cohort, latent brucellosis was screened through SAT, B-SPOT and medical history inquiry. Blood culture or other pathogen detection method was not conducted, which might overestimate the real prevalence. Follow-up on latent brucellosis was conducted for only 2 years, and the long-term prognosis of latent brucellosis still requires further follow-up. Besides, due to time and cost constraints, not all 2663 participants in this study underwent B-SPOT testing; thus, the prevalence of latent brucellosis based on B-SPOT needs to be confirmed through further prospective studies. Another limitation was that this study observed the natural history of latent brucellosis instead of an intervention. Whether to use antibiotic intervention and which antibiotic regimen to adopt needs to be explored in future cohorts.
