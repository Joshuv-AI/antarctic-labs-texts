Intergenerational neuroimaging’s present and future: Considering sex as a biological variable to enhance knowledge of brain development through parent–offspring similarity
Authors: Izumi Matsudaira, Ryo Yamaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intergenerational neuroimaging, which is used to investigate brain similarities between parent–offspring dyads, promises to elucidate the neural substrates of intergenerational transmission. However, merely identifying similar brain regions or networks between parents and offspring is not sufficient to reveal the mechanisms underlying this transmission. To understand these mechanisms, it is necessary to consider the potential contributions of shared genetic and environmental factors to the development of brain features that are similar between parents and offspring. Sex as a biological variable (SABV), a key factor in intergenerational neuroimaging, provides crucial insights into brain development. Although sex-based differences in brain developmental trajectories have been investigated, the role of SABV in parent–offspring brain similarity has been overlooked. In this narrative review, we summarize the key findings of previous intergenerational neuroimaging studies, grouping them into three categories based on study design: studies of mother–offspring dyads, studies combining fathers and mothers, and studies distinguishing between father–offspring and mother–offspring dyads. Furthermore, we highlight the genetic and environmental factors that may underlie sex-specific parent–offspring brain similarities. Finally, we propose further considerations to clarify the significance of parent–offspring brain similarity in human brain development. Advancements in intergenerational neuroimaging may shed light on mechanisms by which mental health risk is transmitted across generations, potentially providing opportunities for more effective prevention, stratification, and treatment.

Introduction

It is well established that parents and their offspring are often similar in complex traits, such as psychopathologies, personality, cognitive function, and parenting behavior (Duarte et al., 2020, Jester et al., 2009, Niu et al., 2018, Stern et al., 2024, Uher et al., 2023). This similarity arises from intergenerational transmission, whereby parents’ traits influence the next generation through genetic and environmental pathways (Branje et al., 2020). Understanding the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission may facilitate the development of interventions aimed at breaking the repetition of maladaptive experiences, such as psychopathology and abuse, or promoting the acquisition of adaptive traits, such as resilience and positive parenting behavior.

Elucidating the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission requires research that integrates genetic and environmental factors. However, directly linking molecular-level phenomena with complex behavioral traits remains challenging (Flint et al., 2014). Moreover, given that environmental factors range from conditions within the family to broader sociocultural contexts, the biological mechanisms that link environmental factors to complex behavioral traits are difficult to explain. To address these challenges, intergenerational neuroimaging, which involves analyzing brain images of parent–offspring dyads, has attracted increasing attention (Constant-Varlet et al., 2024, Ho et al., 2016). Information on brain structure and function obtained through neuroimaging is regarded as an endophenotype of psychiatric disorders (Flint et al., 2014, Liu and Gershon, 2024). Intergenerational neuroimaging has started to identify the neural substrates of intergenerational transmission of psychopathology (Foland-Ross et al., 2016, Ho et al., 2016, Yamagata et al., 2016). Researchers have analyzed parent–offspring similarity in structural and functional characteristics across various brain regions and networks (Constant-Varlet et al., 2024).

Nevertheless, simply identifying the similar brain regions or networks between parents and offspring is not sufficient to explain the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission. Parent–offspring brain similarity is likely influenced by shared factors in the developmental processes of parents’ brains and those of their offspring, such as genetic influences and shared environmental exposures. To elucidate the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission via intergenerational neuroimaging, it is crucial to identify these shared factors and how they specifically influence the development of individual brain regions and networks. A key factor that must be considered is sex as a biological variable (SABV). The developmental trajectories of the structural and functional features of the brain show sex-based differences (Kaczkurkin et al., 2019, Lawrence et al., 2023). Moreover, genetic mechanisms that exhibit sex-specific inheritance patterns are believed to play a role in brain development (Gregg et al., 2010, Lawson et al., 2013, Perez et al., 2016). However, most intergenerational neuroimaging studies to date have focused predominantly on mother–offspring dyads, with insufficient consideration of SABV.

In this review, we highlight how explicitly incorporating SABV into intergenerational neuroimaging research can advance our understanding of human brain development. It can also guide future research to advance this field and decode the biological mechanisms of intergenerational transmission.

Definition of intergenerational neuroimaging

In this review, we define intergenerational neuroimaging as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies that assess structural or functional brain similarity between parents and their offspring (Fig. 2), and we select prior research within this scope. Specifically, we include studies in which brain images were obtained from parents and offspring using the same protocol and in which brain similarity was assessed on the basis of correlation coefficients, cosine similarity, or related indices derived from structural and functional metrics extracted from the respective images. The number of intergenerational neuroimaging studies has gradually increased since the publication of the review by Ho et al. (2016) (Ho et al., 2016). Accordingly, we searched PubMed in April 2025 for studies published since 2016 that included the following search terms: (("intergeneration"[All Fields] OR "intergenerational"[All Fields] OR "parent-child"[Title/Abstract] OR "parent offspring"[Title/Abstract] OR "mother-child"[Title/Abstract] OR "father-child"[Title/Abstract]) AND ("brain"[Title/Abstract] OR "neural"[Title/Abstract]) AND ("similarity"[Title/Abstract] OR "resemblance"[Title/Abstract] OR "concordance"[Title/Abstract] OR "correlation"[Title/Abstract])). From the search results, we selected and screened studies that matched the aforementioned scope. If studies that we knew to be key papers or that were listed in the recent review by Constant-Varlet et al. (2024) (Constant-Varlet et al., 2024) were not captured in the search results because of differences in terminology, we manually added them. We did not restrict the types of brain features (e.g., gray matter volume [GMV], cortical thickness [CT], surface area [SA], local gyrification index [LGI], fractional anisotropy [FA], or resting-state functional connectivity [rs-FC]) assessed in each study. Studies that meet these criteria generally fall into two categories: those aiming to identify the neural basis of (1) intergenerational transmission (Ahtam et al., 2021, Colich et al., 2017, Dimanova et al., 2023, Fehlbaum et al., 2022, Foland-Ross et al., 2016, Kepinska et al., 2024, Kim-Spoon et al., 2023, Matsudaira et al., 2025, Minami et al., 2022, Okada et al., 2020, Su et al., 2024, Takagi et al., 2021, Vandermosten et al., 2020, Yamagata et al., 2016) or (2) parent–child biobehavioral synchrony (Abraham et al., 2020, Clinchard et al., 2024, Kim-Spoon et al., 2023, Lee et al., 2017a, Lee et al., 2017b, Zhou et al., 2023). Although the first category focuses primarily on the transmission of psychiatric risk or cognitive function and the second category focuses on parent–child interactions, both treat similarity in brain characteristics between parents and offspring as an intermediate phenotype. Therefore, this review does not distinguish between the two.

Although some studies have acquired brain images from parents and offspring, certain research falls outside the scope of intergenerational neuroimaging as defined in this review; such research was excluded. Specifically, we excluded studies that did not assess the relationship between the same intermediate phenotype in parents and offspring, for example, studies that examined the association of brain activity while parents and children performed different cognitive tasks (Lee et al., 2018), those that used simultaneous fMRI scanning to investigate how one participant’s performance influenced the other’s brain activity during collaboration (Cosgrove et al., 2019, Kerr et al., 2020), and those that used hyperscanning to record parent and child brain activity simultaneously during social interaction (Ratliff et al., 2021). Additionally, studies using modalities other than MRI, e.g., electroencephalography (Hill et al., 2020), were excluded because their spatial resolution and the nature of the measured brain signals differ from those of MRI, complicating direct comparisons with the MRI-based studies we reviewed.

In the following section, we summarize the findings from prior studies selected according to the criteria outlined above, stratified by study design, and we discuss the importance of incorporating SABV in intergenerational neuroimaging.

Intergenerational neuroimaging and SABV

In intergenerational neuroimaging, SABV can be addressed by analyzing parent–offspring similarity separately by sex or by testing the significance of sex effects on similarity. The feasibility of such analyses depends on the sample composition of each study. In this section, we use the previously described criteria to classify the selected studies into three categories: studies that (1) examined similarity in mother–offspring dyads only (n = 7), (2) examined parent–offspring similarity by combining fathers and mothers (n = 8), and (3) examined similarity separately in father–offspring and mother–offspring dyads (n = 5). We then summarize the main findings of each study. For the methodological details of each study, readers are referred to the original articles.

Before the literature is reviewed, two methodological concerns should be considered. First, the sample sizes of the existing studies are generally insufficient. Second, it is not always confirmed that brain similarities are specific to parent–offspring dyads. The details of these concerns are explained in Section 5. Studies of mother–offspring dyads

Early studies on intergenerational neuroimaging focused exclusively on mother–daughter dyads, on the basis of previous findings suggesting that maternal depression is more likely to be transmitted to daughters. For example, maternal depression was more strongly associated with offspring’s emotional dysfunction than paternal depression was (Connell and Goodman, 2002). Additionally, the influence of maternal depressive symptoms on offspring’s internalizing problems appears to be specific to daughters (Fergusson et al., 1995, Piche et al., 2011). There are seven studies of mother–offspring dyads that are consistent with the scope of this review.

As a pioneering work, Foland-Ross et al. (2016) reported that the CT of the fusiform gyrus in the mother significantly predicted that of her daughter when the mother had a history of recurrent major depressive disorder (MDD). This relationship was absent in mother–daughter dyads where the mother did not have a history of depression, suggesting that CT in the fusiform gyrus may play a key role in the intergenerational transmission of depressive symptoms (Foland-Ross et al., 2016). Colich et al. (2017) also studied mothers with a history of MDD and their daughters alongside healthy mother–daughter dyads. They reported that putamen activation during the anticipation of reward loss was significantly associated between mothers and daughters, but this association was observed regardless of the mother’s history of MDD (Colich et al., 2017).

Two studies have also investigated the neural basis of the intergenerational transmission of behavioral traits other than depression. These studies examined the similarity between the brains of mothers and their offspring, including their sons. Fehlbaum et al. (2022) examined mother–offspring similarity in brain structure in areas associated with reading ability. They reported that the GMV, SA, and LGI were significantly more strongly correlated between mothers and offspring than between unrelated pairs, whereas CT and the shape pattern of the occipitotemporal sulcus showed no such correlation (Fehlbaum et al., 2022). Likewise, Okada et al. (2020) investigated the mother–offspring similarity of neural metabolism in the region related to prosociality. They reported that the GABA+ /Glx ratio in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) was significantly and positively correlated between mothers and offspring (Okada et al., 2020).

Three studies have investigated whether an offspring’s sex influences mother–offspring brain similarity. However, it was difficult to find significant sex-based differences. Dimanova et al. (2023) demonstrated that the GMV in the corticolimbic circuitry (CLC), including the ACC and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), showed significantly stronger correlations between mother–offspring dyads than between unrelated pairs. However, there were no significant differences in the correlation coefficients between mother–son and mother–daughter dyads (Dimanova et al., 2023). Similarly, Ahtam et al. (2021) reported that the sulcal depth and area in the right frontal lobe, as well as the graph sulcal pattern in the right parietal lobe, were similar between mothers and their offspring but not between offspring and unrelated adult women. Again, no significant differences in similarity were found between mother–son and mother–daughter dyads (Ahtam et al., 2021). Kim et al. (2021) confirmed similarity in rs-FC within the networks that mature earlier, including the visual, auditory, and sensorimotor networks, between mothers and their infants. This similarity increased with the infants’ age; however, no significant interaction between age and sex was observed, suggesting that there was no apparent sex-based difference in similarity (Kim et al., 2021).

Because each of these studies focused on different brain regions and feature types, it is difficult to draw a unified conclusion. The fact that differences in similarity between mother–son and mother–daughter dyads have not been observed indicates that brain similarity specific to mother–daughter dyads may not be robust. However, as will be discussed later in Section 5.1, the lack of sufficient statistical power may have affected the results. Thus, it cannot be concluded that there are no sex differences in mother–offspring brain similarity.

Studies of parent–offspring dyads

Although mother–offspring dyads comprise a larger proportion of the overall samples, studies including father–offspring have also been conducted. Eight studies analyzed parent–offspring brain similarity by combining data obtained from fathers and mothers.

Su et al. (2024) reported that the time series of vmPFC and dorsomedial PFC activity as participants watched naturalistic videos was more strongly correlated between parents and their offspring than between unrelated pairs (Su et al., 2024). Similarly, Takagi et al. (2021) reported that the rs-FC in the medial PFC and frontoparietal network and the GMV in the default-mode network (DMN), subcortical areas, cerebellum, and visual network were sufficiently similar between parents and offspring to allow discrimination from unrelated pairs. Notably, boys and girls were similar to their parents across almost all networks, but the similarity was stronger in girls (Takagi et al., 2021). Thus, sex differences that were not observed in studies of mothers and offspring alone have been identified in studies that included fathers. However, only 3 out of 84 parents in this study were fathers; therefore, their level of contribution to the observed sex differences is unclear.

Six studies aiming to elucidate the neural basis of parent–offspring biobehavioral synchrony included mothers and fathers in their designs and explored how parent–offspring brain similarity relates to other variables. The goals of these studies are to identify predictors of similarity and to understand how such similarity is linked to developmental outcomes in offspring. For example, Lee et al. (2017a) reported that greater similarity in rs-FC patterns between parents and offspring was associated with greater synchrony in daily mood fluctuations (Lee et al., 2017a). Furthermore, higher synchrony was associated with greater emotional competence in offspring. In another study, Lee et al. (2017b) linked higher similarity in connectivity patterns between the DMN and other networks to more closely matched sleep durations between parents and offspring and better sleep quality in offspring (Lee et al., 2017b). Lee and colleagues published similar findings. Zhou et al. (2023) reported that greater parent–offspring similarity in the connectivity patterns of the affective network was associated with lower negative affect and anxiety and higher ego resilience in offspring; however, this association was observed only in families with high cohesion (Zhou et al., 2023). Similarly, Clinchard et al. (2024) reported that higher household socioeconomic status (SES) was linked to greater parent–offspring similarity in rs-FC (Clinchard et al., 2024). They also reported that greater similarity in rs-FC was associated with stronger perceptions of parental monitoring by offspring, which, in turn, predicted lower substance use one year later. Kim-Spoon et al. (2023) reported no association between household chaos and child substance use when parent–offspring similarity in rs-FC was high (Kim-Spoon et al., 2023). Conversely, when similarity was low, household chaos was linked to child substance use through the variable of parental monitoring. Additionally, another research group examined the neural basis of biobehavioral synchrony based on structural brain connectivity. Abraham et al. (2020) reported that structural connectivity within the cortico-basal ganglia circuit was not similar between parents and offspring when parents had a history of depression (Abraham et al., 2020). However, when parental care was high, similarity was observed even in the presence of a history of parental depression.

These studies associate parent–offspring brain similarity with various aspects of the home environment. Although the home environment reflects the characteristics of fathers and mothers (Kong et al., 2018), whether these variables operate in the same way for fathers and mothers remains unclear. Given the limited number of fathers included in the existing samples, SABV cannot be properly considered. Moving forward, studies should enable the systematic investigation of these relationships separately for fathers and mothers.

Studies distinguishing between father–offspring and mother–offspring dyads

Five studies recruited fathers and mothers in roughly equal numbers to examine the patterns of similarity within father–offspring and mother–offspring dyads separately.

Vandermosten et al. (2020) investigated white matter tissue properties associated with reading ability and reported that the FA of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus showed comparable similarity in father–offspring and mother–offspring dyads (Vandermosten et al., 2020). Similarly, Kepinska et al. (2024) examined parent–offspring similarity in the structure of the transverse temporal gyrus (TTG). The TTG is the central auditory region and can be subdivided into multiple gyri, with the first typically referred to as Heschl’s gyrus (HG) (Kepinska et al., 2024). In mother–offspring dyads, similarity was observed in the GMV, CT, and SA of the right HG as well as in the shape complexity of the left HG. Moreover, an interaction between maternal GMV and offspring sex was found for offspring GMV in the right HG, suggesting a stronger association in mother–daughter dyads. However, given that the association in mother–son dyads followed a similar trend, the authors noted that this does not conclusively indicate similarity specific to mother–daughter dyads. Additionally, the laterality of the TTG’s SA (i.e., the direction of the difference between the left and right SA) was similar between the fathers and their offspring. No significant differences in father–offspring similarity based on the offspring’s sex were reported (Kepinska et al., 2024).

Two studies have examined similarity separately for each parent–offspring sex combination—father–son, father–daughter, mother–son, and mother–daughter—and have reported patterns of similarity specific to mother–daughter dyads. For example, Yamagata et al. (2016) revealed stronger positive correlations than other dyad types in the GMV of the corticolimbic system, including the amygdala, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, ACC, and vmPFC, in mother–daughter dyads (Yamagata et al., 2016). Similarly, Minami et al. (2022) investigated whether similarity in brain structure between parents with a history of depression and their offspring would show female specificity; they reported that the β-values of independent components (ICs) identified by source-based morphometry—reflecting the weighted sum of GMV across regions in each IC—were specifically similar in mother–daughter dyads (Minami et al., 2022). The GMV of the right caudal middle frontal gyrus also showed similarity specific to mother–daughter dyads.

These studies included groups that participated as mother–offspring pairs only, father–offspring pairs only, and full trios consisting of parents and offspring. Therefore, none directly compared the similarity of fathers and mothers to the same offspring. Moreover, while studies reporting specific mother–daughter similarity compared correlation coefficients between mother–daughter dyads and other combinations (Minami et al., 2022, Yamagata et al., 2016), they did not clarify how similarity differed among the other combinations—father–son, father–daughter, and mother–son dyads.

In our projects (Matsudaira et al., 2023), we investigated differences in brain structural similarity between parents and offspring in varying sex-based combinations using the data from biological parent–offspring trios (Matsudaira et al., 2025). Specifically, we analyzed similarity in CT, SA, and LGI across 360 cortical regions (Glasser et al., 2016) and subcortical volume (SV) separately for father–son, father–daughter, mother–son, and mother–daughter dyads. We found that the pattern of parent–offspring brain similarity varied according to the sex combination. Regarding CT, daughters had regions that resembled only the father, only the mother, and both parents, whereas sons exhibited a predominance of regions that were similar only to the mother. Regarding SA, sons showed no significant similarity to either parent in any region. Regarding LGI, many regions resembled those of both parents regardless of the offspring’s sex. Regarding SV, region-specific patterns were observed; for example, the hippocampal volume resembled that of the mother in daughters and that of the father in sons.

Furthermore, we conducted pairwise comparisons of the correlation coefficients between the four parent–offspring sex combinations in the same study. The SA of the left orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) was similar in the father–daughter and mother–daughter dyads but not in the father–son and mother–son dyads. Previous research had suggested that the structure of the OFC is specifically similar in mother–daughter pairs (Minami et al., 2022, Yamagata et al., 2016); however, our findings indicate that father–daughter similarity also exists, suggesting that the pattern may be specific to parent–daughter dyads but not exclusive to mother–daughter dyads. A finding that shows similarity between both parents and daughters, but not between parents and sons, suggests that genetic or environmental factors shared specifically between parents and daughters may influence the development of the OFC. Reportedly, daughters and sons develop different types of relationships with their parents (Fernandes et al., 2018), and these differences may be reflected in sex-specific patterns of similarity. Conversely, the CT of the left supplementary motor area (SMA) showed significant similarity only in mother–son dyads. This finding suggests that factors transmitted from mothers to sons contribute to the development of the SMA in males. Few factors are exclusively shared between mothers and sons; nevertheless, certain factors, while not unique to them, may show functional characteristics specific to mother–son dyads. As we describe in the following section, examples of such factors include the X chromosome and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Why is SABV necessary in intergenerational neuroimaging?

This section lists potential factors that may contribute to sex differences in parent–offspring brain similarity and discusses the necessity of incorporating SABV in intergenerational neuroimaging research.

Sex differences in the developmental trajectory of the brain

Studies have reported sex-specific patterns in the age-related changes in various brain metrics. For detailed descriptions of the developmental trajectories of each brain metric, readers are referred to other relevant empirical studies and review articles (Brain Development Cooperative Group, 2012, Cao et al., 2017, Lawrence et al., 2023, Lebel and Deoni, 2018, Raznahan et al., 2011, Ritchie et al., 2018, Wierenga et al., 2014a, Wierenga et al., 2014b). In brief, it is generally reported that males have larger mean values than females do, although the overall distribution patterns are similar (Kaczkurkin et al., 2019). The age at which brain size peaks differs between sexes, and this difference is thought to vary across brain regions (Kaczkurkin et al., 2019). Such sex differences in developmental trajectories may influence the similarity of brain metrics between parents and their offspring.

Sex differences in the intergenerational transmission of psychopathology

As described above, one of the key objectives of intergenerational neuroimaging is to clarify the mechanisms underlying the intergenerational transmission of psychiatric disorders (Ho et al., 2016). Meta-analytic evidence has confirmed that the offspring of a parent who has a psychiatric disorder have a substantially increased risk of developing not only the same disorder but also other psychiatric conditions (Uher et al., 2023). Growing evidence suggests that the intergenerational transmission of psychiatric disorders may depend on the specific sex combination of the parent and offspring. Several studies have shown same-sex transmission patterns. For example, suicide risk in boys is significantly associated with paternal but not maternal suicide history, whereas in girls, suicide risk is significantly associated with maternal but not paternal suicide history (Cheng et al., 2014). Similarly, anxiety disorders in parents are significantly associated with an increased incidence of anxiety disorders in their children of the same sex but not those of the opposite sex (Pavlova et al., 2022). In contrast, some studies have suggested that the intergenerational transmission of psychopathologies may be more pronounced in opposite-sex dyads. It has been reported that paternal mental illness, including schizophrenia, is associated with a higher prevalence of psychotic symptoms in daughters, whereas maternal mental illness is linked to a higher prevalence of psychotic symptoms in sons (Aylott et al., 2019, Goldstein et al., 2011). The same pattern is also observed in MDD (Nomura et al., 2001). These findings suggest that there are various mechanisms of intergenerational transmission of psychopathology among parent–offspring sex combinations. Therefore, SABV should be included in intergenerational neuroimaging research to better infer the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission.

Parent-of-origin effects

Genetic factors are considered to contribute to parent–offspring brain similarity. In particular, parent-of-origin effects—genetic mechanisms in which inheritance patterns differ on the basis of sex (Guilmatre and Sharp, 2012)—may lead to sex-specific brain similarities between parents and offspring (Constant-Varlet et al., 2024, Ho et al., 2016) owing to genomic imprinting and the transmission of mtDNA and sex chromosomes. The following section outlines each of these mechanisms and, to the extent currently known, their relevance to brain development.

Genomic imprinting

Genomic imprinting refers to the phenomenon in which one allele is silenced on the basis of its parental origin, resulting in the expression of a single copy of the gene. Although imprinted genes account for < 1 % of the human genome, they are thought to have a disproportionately large impact on development (Lawson et al., 2013). Because many imprinted genes regulate brain development, imprinting is believed to influence behavior, cognition, and personality (Badcock and Crespi, 2008). The “imprinted brain hypothesis” proposes that offspring are more likely to exhibit autistic traits when paternally derived gene expression is dominant (a strong paternal bias), whereas schizotypal traits are more likely to emerge when maternally derived gene expression is dominant (a strong maternal bias) (Badcock and Crespi, 2008). However, critics have argued that it is difficult to describe psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders solely on the basis of imprinting status because these are highly heterogeneous phenotypes (Davies and Isles, 2008, Isles, 2022). Moreover, because common variants are not generally associated with imprinted genes (Escott-Price et al., 2015), the relationship between imprinting and brain development in nonclinical populations is unlikely to be straightforward.

Three theories have persisted as explanations for the evolutionary significance of genomic imprinting (Patten et al., 2014): the kinship theory (Haig, 2004), the sexual antagonism theory (Day and Bonduriansky, 2004), and the maternal–offspring coadaptation theory (Wolf and Hager, 2006). In the sexual antagonism theory and the maternal–offspring coadaptation theory, genomic imprinting is viewed as a mechanism that modulates offspring similarity to their parents (Patten et al., 2014). The sexual antagonism theory is based on selection pressure (Day and Bonduriansky, 2004) and proposes that genomic imprinting has evolved to promote the expression of the allele that is more advantageous, depending on the offspring’s sex and the parent from whom the allele is inherited. The maternal–offspring coadaptation theory posits that genomic imprinting has evolved to enhance the offspring’s fitness through phenotypic coordination between the mother and the offspring (Wolf and Hager, 2006). According to this theory, if mother–offspring dissimilarity increases the offspring’s fitness, the maternal alleles will be silenced, and the paternal alleles will be expressed (Patten et al., 2014). Although none of these theories appears to provide a complete and comprehensive explanation for the evolutionary significance of genomic imprinting (Perez et al., 2016), all three of them support the idea that imprinting may also serve as a molecular genetic basis for parent–offspring neural similarity.

mtDNA

mtDNA is the DNA of the cellular organelle responsible for energy production and is inherited exclusively from the mother. Paternal mitochondria are tagged with ubiquitin prior to fertilization and subsequently degraded in the early embryo, meaning that only maternally derived mitochondria and their mtDNA are passed on to the next generation (Sutovsky et al., 1999). The brain relies more heavily than other organs on mitochondrial energy production, using it for processes such as synapse formation (Pei and Wallace, 2018). Mutations in mtDNA have been identified in Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease (Shoffner et al., 1993), and more recently, mtDNA variants have been implicated in the pathophysiology of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (Chang et al., 2024). In cohort samples not limited to patients with specific disorders, some studies have reported associations between mtDNA and psychiatric traits (Lambertini et al., 2015), whereas others have reported no such link (Howe et al., 2017). Moreover, experimental studies in mice have shown that subclinical mitochondrial mutations can introduce variability into stress response capacity (Picard et al., 2015), suggesting that subtle individual differences in energy metabolism influence human cognition and behavior (Pei and Wallace, 2018).

Sex chromosomes

It has been suggested that genes encoded on the X chromosome or chromosomal modifications may underlie the observed pattern of psychosis transmission across generations, particularly between opposite-sex parent–child dyads (Aylott et al., 2019, Goldstein et al., 2011). In support of this hypothesis, accumulating evidence suggests that the X chromosome plays a role in brain development. Genes on the X chromosome are predominantly expressed in the brain (Nguyen and Disteche, 2006). A genome-wide association study has identified four loci on the X chromosome associated with brain structure (Smith et al., 2021). Neuroimaging studies on individuals with sex chromosome aneuploidies have reported that total brain volume decreases as the number of X chromosomes increases (Raznahan et al., 2016). This finding indicates that X chromosome copy number may contribute to the well-documented sex difference in total brain size between typical XX females and XY males (Raznahan and Disteche, 2021).

In addition to these findings, two lines of evidence support the role of the X chromosome as a key basis for sex differences in the brain. The first is X chromosome inactivation (XCI). Daughters inherit two X chromosomes, one from each parent, whereas sons exclusively inherit their single X chromosome from their mothers. Consequently, females would be expected to have higher expression levels of X-linked genes than males. XCI is a dosage compensation (DC) mechanism that equalizes expression levels between the sexes (Plath et al., 2002). Through this process, female cells become a mosaic, wherein one of the two X chromosomes is inactivated in each cell. The choice of which chromosome is inactivated varies across tissues (Cotton et al., 2015, Tukiainen et al., 2017). However, approximately 15 % of X-linked genes escape inactivation (Carrel and Willard, 2005); these escaping genes have been proposed as a molecular basis for sex differences (Deng et al., 2014). Indeed, recent studies have reported an association between DC and brain features in humans (Jiang et al., 2025, Mallard et al., 2021).

The second factor is the parent of origin of the X chromosome. Previous studies have investigated the association between the origin of the X chromosome and neural or behavioral phenotypes in Klinefelter syndrome and Turner syndrome; however, the findings have been inconsistent. Several studies have reported differences between paternal and maternal origins (Bruining et al., 2010, Cutter et al., 2006, Kesler et al., 2003, Larsen et al., 2024, Lepage et al., 2013, Stemkens et al., 2006), whereas others have reported no significant differences (Boks et al., 2007, Brown et al., 2002, Good et al., 2003, Loesch et al., 2005, Ross et al., 2008, Skakkebaek et al., 2014, Zeger et al., 2008). More recently, a study reported that skewed XCI toward the paternal or maternal X chromosome is associated with cognitive function (Abdulai-Saiku et al., 2025). Skewed XCI commonly occurs in the general population (Shvetsova et al., 2019). Notably, the preferential activation of the maternal X chromosome has been associated with cognitive decline in female mice (Abdulai-Saiku et al., 2025). Although the implications of this finding for human brain development remain unclear, the parent of origin of the X chromosome possibly contributes to the neural differences observed between men and women, as well as among women (Snell and Turner, 2025).

At present, there is limited evidence indicating that the Y chromosome contributes to parent–offspring brain similarity; however, it is associated with brain development. The Y chromosome is exclusively passed from father to son. While the X chromosome encodes > 800 genes, the Y chromosome contains 60–100 genes, some of which are expressed in the brain. Recent transcriptomic analyses of the human fetal brain have confirmed high expression of PCDH11Y and NLGN4Y, both of which encode cell adhesion molecules (Buonocore et al., 2025). Notably, PCDH11Y, which encodes a protocadherin 11 Y-linked protein, is a human-specific gene (Wilson et al., 2006) and may be one of the factors contributing to sex-based differences in early brain development.

Summary of parent-of-origin effects and parent–offspring brain similarity

The fact that genomic imprinting, mtDNA, and sex chromosomes are shared between parents and offspring in a sex-specific manner implies that the brain developmental characteristics influenced by these mechanisms are likely to present in a similar manner within parent–offspring dyads. In other words, these molecular foundations could contribute to parent–offspring similarities in brain structure and function. Analyses based on different parent–offspring sex combinations can help explore these possibilities. By explicitly incorporating SABV into similarity analyses, the influence of sex-specific genetic mechanisms on brain development could be indirectly examined. This approach would lay the groundwork for further research, such as molecular genetic studies using animal models or investigations involving nonbiological parent–offspring dyads.

Sex-based differences in the parent–child relationship

The physiological responses and emotions of parents and children synchronize during their interactions (Feldman, 2017, Feldman, 2007), and this biobehavioral synchrony is reflected at the neural level (Lee et al., 2017a). Various physiological and emotional factors shared within parent–offspring interactions may contribute to similarities in their brain characteristics through neuroplasticity.

The ways in which parents interact with their child may vary depending on the child’s sex. A recent meta-analysis reported that parents often favor daughters over sons; however, this bias is typically not overt enough for children to consciously perceive (Jensen and Jorgensen-Wells, 2025). Although no clear pattern has been observed for mothers (Rigo et al., 2019), fathers have been shown to engage differently with sons and daughters in their manner of speaking and style of play (Mascaro et al., 2017). Moreover, fathers demonstrate differential neural responses: their medial OFC is activated in response to happy facial expressions from daughters and to neutral facial expressions from sons (Mascaro et al., 2017). These findings suggest that sex-based differences are inherent in parent–child interactions. Considering that the physiological and emotional factors shared within these interactions may also vary on the basis of the offspring’s sex, it logically follows that parent–offspring brain similarity is shaped by genetic factors and by behavioral dynamics. Therefore, even from this perspective, the importance of incorporating SABV into similarity analyses becomes especially evident.

Important factors other than SABV

To gain insights into brain development through intergenerational neuroimaging, additional factors should be considered alongside SABV. Here, we outline the methodological challenges in intergenerational neuroimaging and in elucidating the developmental significance of parent–offspring brain similarity.

Methodological points

The sample sizes of previous intergenerational neuroimaging studies have generally been insufficient to detect sex-based differences. Dimanova et al. (2023) did not detect any differences in the mother–offspring similarity of GMV between sons and daughters (Dimanova et al., 2023). They performed a post hoc power analysis and reported that their sample size (23 sons and 16 daughters) was underpowered to detect significant sex-based differences. In our study, we conducted pairwise comparisons of correlation coefficients across the father–son (58 dyads), father–daughter (87 dyads), mother–son (60 dyads), and mother–daughter (90 dyads) groups and identified differences between sex combinations in specific regional brain metrics (Matsudaira et al., 2025). However, these differences were observed in only a small subset of the brain regions where overall parent–offspring similarity was confirmed. In other words, although differences in correlation coefficients were observed between different parent–offspring sex combinations for most regional features, these differences were not statistically significant. This outcome suggests that larger sample sizes are needed to robustly investigate differences in parent–offspring similarity across sex combinations. Notably, some intergenerational neuroimaging studies have included siblings as offspring participants. Such a design can serve as a strategy to increase the sample size. In such cases, the same parental data are included multiple times in the analysis, compromising data independence and increasing the risk of false-positive findings. It is possible to adjust for the effects of nonindependence by refining the statistical model (Kepinska et al., 2024). However, assembling data that exclude sibling participants or avoid using the same parent’s data multiple times is essential for ensuring an appropriate assessment of parent–child similarity. Because acquiring brain imaging data from parents and children is extremely time and cost intensive, future studies should prioritize multisite collaborations and harmonization to substantially increase sample sizes.

As emphasized by a prior review article on intergenerational neuroimaging (Constant-Varlet et al., 2024), comparisons with unrelated individuals should be included when evaluating parent–offspring similarity. Brain characteristics can sometimes resemble each other even between individuals who are not genetically related. For example, friends have been reported to show similar GMV in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) (D’Onofrio et al., 2022). Therefore, even if a strong correlation is observed between parents and offspring in a particular brain region, this correlation must be significantly stronger than that of unrelated pairs to establish that the similarity is truly specific to parent–offspring dyads. Recent intergenerational neuroimaging studies have incorporated such comparisons (Ahtam et al., 2021, Dimanova et al., 2023, Fehlbaum et al., 2022, Kepinska et al., 2024, Matsudaira et al., 2025, Su et al., 2024, Takagi et al., 2021). Moving forward, this procedure should be mandated in the field.

Challenges in assigning meaning to parent–offspring brain similarity

This review has argued that parent–offspring brain similarity is a valuable tool for advancing our understanding of human brain development. However, the developmental significance of the phenomenon itself—namely, the fact that the parents’ and offspring’s brains are similar—remains unclear at present. One possible reason is that it remains unclear what constitutes typical versus atypical similarity during development. It has been reported that functional brain similarities between parents and offspring predict positive outcomes in offspring, such as emotional adjustment and sleep quality (Lee et al., 2017a, Lee et al., 2017b, Zhou et al., 2023). Conversely, parent–offspring brain similarity may also signal the intergenerational transmission of risk factors. Kim et al. (2021) reported that lower maternal educational attainment was associated with greater similarity in rs-FC between mothers and their offspring in infancy (Kim et al., 2021). Low SES was interpreted as a risk factor for psychiatric disorders, and this similarity may indicate accelerated maturation during infancy. Moreover, Foland-Ross et al. (2016) reported that mothers with a history of depression and their daughters exhibited brain structural similarity that was not present in the control group, suggesting that brain similarity may contribute to the intergenerational transmission of psychiatric disorders (Foland-Ross et al., 2016). In contrast, Abraham et al. (2020) reported that no parent–offspring similarity was observed in the structural connectivity of the cortico-basal ganglia circuit when a parent had a history of MDD, and they suggested that disrupted similarity indicates the risk of intergenerational continuity of psychopathology (Abraham et al., 2020). Several challenges remain in understanding the typicality or atypicality of parent–offspring brain similarity.

First, it is unclear whether determining similarity using a single index is a sound approach. Calculation of similarity indices, such as cosine similarity (Zhou et al., 2023), Euclidean distance (ED) (Matsudaira et al., 2025), and the “accuracy” metric proposed by Takagi et al. (the winning rate of parent–child dyads when the correlation coefficients of brain metrics were compared with those of stranger–child pairs (Takagi et al., 2021)), is useful for examining the association between parent–offspring brain similarity and behavioral or physiological phenotypes. However, these indices focus solely on how closely parents’ and offspring’s numerical values align, without determining whether similarity occurs at the higher or lower side of the scale. For example, our previous study revealed that the ED of parents and offspring in the SA of language- and auditory-related regions was associated with the ED of intelligence. This suggests that similarity in the SA of these regions may reflect comparable levels of intelligence between parents and offspring (Matsudaira et al., 2025). However, for other brain regions, it remains unclear how parent–offspring similarity is related to behavior. Therefore, it cannot be assumed that there is always a linear relationship in which greater brain similarity corresponds to greater behavioral similarity. It has also been reported that the correlation between parent–offspring similarity of rs-FC patterns and offspring emotional competence is less than moderate (Lee et al., 2017a). Analyses that account for differences in the nature of similarity may detect the relationship between brain similarity and offspring behavioral outcomes with higher sensitivity. Therefore, to better investigate the relationship between brain similarity and behavior, analytical methods that can distinguish different patterns of similarity should be implemented. For example, response surface analysis can help differentiate between cases where similarity occurs on the higher side of the value range and those where it occurs on the lower side (Barranti et al., 2017).

Second, many studies have focused on only specific structural or functional metrics, making it difficult to comprehensively characterize parent–offspring brain similarity. Because each brain metric has distinct genetic underpinnings and developmental trajectories (Geschwind and Rakic, 2013, Paus et al., 2008, Rakic, 1988, Raznahan et al., 2011; L. Wierenga et al., 2014), even within the same brain region, the presence or absence of parent–offspring similarity may vary depending on which metric is assessed. For example, CT is thought to be susceptible to experience-dependent plasticity and tends to change after birth (Lövdén et al., 2013), whereas the LGI is considered to be strongly influenced by genetics and remains relatively stable postnatally (Zilles et al., 2013). Fehlbaum et al. (2022) demonstrated that LGI values are similar between parents and offspring in the reading network, whereas CT does not show such similarity (Fehlbaum et al., 2022). Concordantly, our study found that the LGI exhibits parent–offspring similarity across a wide range of regions, whereas CT is similar only in a limited number of regions (Matsudaira et al., 2025). Multidimensional analyses of parent–offspring similarity should be conducted across multiple metrics within each brain region to examine their associations with outcomes in offspring or parents. This approach will help identify whether parent–offspring similarity in each brain region is typical or atypical.

Third, most intergenerational neuroimaging studies have had a cross-sectional design and have focused on offspring during puberty. The youngest offspring participants were reported by Kim et al. (2021), who studied newborns (Kim et al., 2021), whereas the oldest were studied by Minami et al. (2022), with an average age of 27 years (Minami et al., 2022). However, because these studies differed in the age range they included and in the measures they assessed, it remains unclear when parent–offspring brain similarity first emerges and how long it persists. Ideally, brain similarity should be assessed at the same age in parents and their children; however, this would require a large-scale birth cohort and would prove challenging to implement. Thus, researchers should aim to clarify the relationship between parent–offspring brain similarity and age using appropriate study designs that are currently available. Specifically, cross-sectional studies should be conducted with sufficient sample sizes within each child age group to compare parent–offspring similarity across groups, whereas longitudinal studies should be conducted using parent–offspring brain images acquired over multiple years.

Concluding remarks

Intergenerational neuroimaging has provided valuable insights into parent–offspring brain similarity, offering a unique perspective to deepen our understanding of human brain development. To date, several studies suggest that parent–offspring brain similarity patterns may differ depending on the sex of parents and offspring. These differences likely reflect the roles of genetic or environmental factors shared in a sex-specific manner between parents and offspring. To advance this field, intergenerational neuroimaging studies should explicitly incorporate SABV by obtaining data from both parents and by implementing analytical frameworks that examine parent–offspring sex combinations in detail. Methodological rigor will be vital for clarifying the developmental significance of brain similarity and its relationship to behavioral outcomes. By addressing these challenges, future research can better delineate the mechanisms of intergenerational transmission and ultimately inform strategies for promoting adaptive development across generations.
