Deep learning model using planar whole-body bone scintigraphy for diagnosis of skull base invasion in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Xingyu Mu, Zhao Ge, Denglu Lu, Ting Li, Lijuan Liu, Cheng Chen, Shulin Song, Wei Fu, Guanqiao Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study assesses the reliability of deep learning models based on planar whole-body bone scintigraphy for diagnosing Skull base invasion (SBI) in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) patients.MethodsIn this multicenter study, a deep learning model was developed using data from one center with a 7:3 allocation to training and internal test sets, to diagnose SBI in patients newly diagnosed with NPC using planar whole-body bone scintigraphy. Patients were diagnosed based on a composite reference standard incorporating radiologic and follow-up data. Ten different convolutional neural network (CNN) models were applied to both whole-image and partial-image input modes to determine the optimal model for each analysis. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration, decision curve analysis (DCA), and compared with expert assessments by two nuclear medicine physicians.ResultsThe best-performing model using partial-body input achieved AUCs of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.73, 0.86) in the internal test set, 0.84 (95% CI: 0.77, 0.91) in the external cohort, and 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73, 0.83) in the treatment test cohort. Calibration curves and DCA confirmed the models’ excellent discrimination, calibration, and potential clinical utility across internal and external datasets. The AUCs of both nuclear medicine physicians were lower than those of the best-performing deep learning model in external test set (AUC: 0.75 vs. 0.77 vs. 0.84).ConclusionDeep learning models utilizing partial-body input from planar whole-body bone scintigraphy demonstrate high discriminatory power for diagnosing SBI in NPC patients, surpassing experienced nuclear medicine physicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-024-05969-y.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), primarily prevalent in Southeast Asia, ranks as the most common form of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, with approximately 129,000 new cases and 73,000 deaths reported worldwide annually (Bray et al. 2024). The invasion of cancer cells into the skull base, termed skull base invasion (SBI), significantly exacerbates prognosis. Studies reveal that SBI escalates the likelihood of bone metastasis in patients with advanced NPC stages (Wu et al. 2022). Moreover, from the 5th to the current 8th edition, the American Joint Committee on Cancer consistently includes SBI within the T3 classification (Li et al. 2022a, b). Discriminating between minor and major SBI aids in refining patient prognostication accuracy and guiding treatment choices (Li et al. 2022a, b). The ability to grade SBI based on MRI findings is pivotal, as extensive involvement correlates with poorer patient prognoses compared to limited involvement (Feng et al. 2019). Precise identification of SBI is crucial for formulating effective treatment strategies and predicting patient outcomes, which in turn impact salvage therapy selection and survival rates (Gorolay et al. 2022). In essence, precise identification of SBI is crucial in formulating effective treatment strategies and predicting patient outcomes in NPC, impacting salvage therapy selection and survival rates.

In the clinical realm, conventional computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serve as the primary modalities for evaluating tumor spread and skull base invasion in NPC. CT excels in anatomically delineating sclerotic and lytic lesions; however, it may overlook early bone marrow invasions lacking cortical bone destruction (Yi et al. 2016; Hiyama et al. 2019). Conversely, MRI is renowned for its sensitivity in detecting early tumor infiltration into bone marrow. Nonetheless, peritumoral inflammation can mimic bone invasion in MRI images, frequently leading to false positives (Zhan et al. 2023).

99mTc-MDP planar whole-body bone scintigraphy plays a pivotal role in pre-treatment tumor evaluation, detecting metastases across various cancers (Vicentini et al. 2023). Its widespread adoption is attributed to its high sensitivity in identifying osteoblastic skeletal metastases, facilitating precise cancer staging, treatment planning, monitoring, and survival prognosis, especially in NPC patients (Yen et al. 2022). However, its efficacy in detecting SBI is limited due to tracer distribution in the nasopharynx and low spatial resolution, reducing specificity. While studies demonstrate that SPECT/CT significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy, surpassing SPECT and approaching MRI (Zhang et al. 2014; Li et al. 2017), it entails additional acquisition time and exposes patients to higher radiation doses.

Previous research showcases the efficacy of deep learning methods in augmenting 99mTc-MDP planar whole-body bone scintigraphy (Hajianfar et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2023). Wang et al. employed a deep learning model to automate bone metastasis diagnosis from 99mTc-MDP planar whole-body bone scintigraphy, notably advancing diagnostic workflows (Wang et al. 2024). Another study underscored the efficiency of deep learning in identifying metastatic bone diseases, achieving remarkable classification speed (2.5 min for 134 images) and an area under the receiver operating curve (AUC) of 0.897 (Ibrahim et al. 2023). Additionally, research by Li et al. (2022a, b) demonstrates that regional bone scans can confirm metastases from lung cancer and differentiate between different pathological lung cancer types (Li et al. 2022a, b). This capability highlights a significant advantage of deep learning algorithms: their capacity to identify imaging patterns imperceptible to radiologists and enhance the diagnostic performance of planar whole-body bone scintigraphy.

In this study (Fig. 1), we assessed the dependability of deep learning models in discerning SBI from non-SBI in planar whole-body bone scintigraphy, thereby augmenting their applicability in NPC pre-treatment evaluation. The research encompassed the comparison of diverse deep learning models utilizing different input modalities and architectural frameworks. These models underwent validation using an independent external test set. Furthermore, their diagnostic efficacy was benchmarked against that of seasoned physicians to gauge their effectiveness and precision in clinical contexts.

Materials and methods

Approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University (Approval No. 2023QTLL-16) and Liuzhou Workers’ Hospital (Approval No. XJS2018094), with informed consent requirements waived. To ensure best practice in application of AI in medical imaging, CLAIM (Checklist for AI in Medical Imaging) (Tejani AS et al. 2024) was rigorously applied (Supplementary Table 1).

Study design and patient eligibility

Three distinct patient cohorts were constructed for this study. Initially, a primary cohort was retrospectively established, comprising both training and internal test sets, facilitating model comparison and hyperparameter optimization. This cohort consisted of 806 patients newly diagnosed with nasopharyngeal cancer at the Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University between January 2014 and July 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) histopathologically confirmed nasopharyngeal cancer, (b) availability of pretreatment whole-body bone scintigraphy (WBS) and MRI scans within 1 week before therapy initiation, and (c) absence of distant bone metastasis. Exclusion criteria included: (a) concurrent malignant neoplasms or prior anticancer therapy, (b) missing MRI data or inadequate image quality, and (c) incomplete clinical follow-up data.

Subsequently, an external cohort was compiled for the final model evaluation. This dataset, obtained from Liuzhou Workers’ Hospital, comprised 114 patients newly diagnosed with nasopharyngeal cancer between January 2019 and July 2023. Inclusion and exclusion criteria aligned with those of the primary cohort.

Finally, a treatment test cohort of 316 patients diagnosed with NPC who underwent treatment at the Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University between January 2014 and July 2023 formed a distinct internal test cohort. Inclusion and exclusion criteria mirrored those of the primary cohort, with the additional requirement that these patients had undergone therapy for nasopharyngeal cancer. The purpose of this cohort was to validate the deep learning model’s performance in patients with a history of nasopharyngeal cancer treatment.

Image acquisition and evaluation

Whole-body bone scintigraphy was conducted 2–4 h post-injection subsequent to intravenous administration of 555 to 925 MBq of 99mTc-MDP. A dual-head gamma camera (Siemens Symbia T16 for the internal set and Symbia E for the external set) was utilized for image acquisition. The cameras were equipped with low-energy parallel-hole high-resolution collimators, and patients were positioned supine with arms down. The energy acquisition window was centered on 140 KeV with a 20% window, employing a continuous scan velocity of 12–15 cm/min and a matrix size of 1024 × 256.

Given the challenge of pathologically confirming skull base invasion, the reference standard for assessing this aspect (Zhang et al. 2014; Zhan et al. 2023) relied on a combination of imaging features from multiple modalities, including simulated SPECT, CT, and MRI. Diagnostic criteria included: (1) absence of fat signal in the skull base on all pre-contrast T1-weighted images (T1WI); (2) contrast enhancement on post-contrast T1WI with fat suppression; (3) evidence of bone destruction with decreased density concentration; and (4) observation of osteosclerosis with increased density concentration on simulated CT images. Additionally, confirmation of skull base invasion in nasopharyngeal carcinoma relied on 6-month follow-up imaging, with persistent or expanding invasions in subsequent images confirming bone invasion. Ultimately, the definitive diagnosis was achieved through consensus between the two nuclear medicine physicians who independently assessed the images.

Deep learning procedures and best model selection

In the primary cohort, eligible patients were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 564) and an internal test set (n = 242) at a ratio of 7:3. Two strategies were implemented: utilizing whole-body input and partial body input, wherein the skull of each patient was segmented using Labelme software by a nuclear medicine physician (Russell et al. 2008). Across both input strategies, we evaluated ten deep learning architectures: DenseNet121, DenseNet169, GoogLeNet, Inception_v3, MobileNet_v3_small, ResNet101, ResNet152, SimpleViT, VGG16, and VGG19. The detail of partial body segmentation and hyperparameter optimization process was demonstrated in Supplementary Materials. The optimal model for each architecture was selected based on the checkpoint exhibiting the highest AUC on the validation set, with the model demonstrating the highest overall AUC chosen for subsequent analyses. To elucidate the significance of image regions for diagnosing skull base invasion, we utilized gradient-weighted class activation mapping and attention maps to generate heatmaps (Selvaraju et al. 2017). Furthermore, for comparison purposes, whole-body bone images from the external test set were independently assessed by two nuclear medicine physicians (X.M. and Z.L., each with 6 and 10 years of experience, respectively) from the internal center. Readers were provided access solely to whole-body bone images, maintaining blindness to other imaging modalities, clinical data, and histopathologic findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation), by X.M. and Z.G. Platform Onekey AI (http://www.medai.icu/) was used for building and evaluating the deep learning models. The models’ performance was evaluated through AUC analysis, employing bootstrapping to calculate 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, various metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), precision, recall, and F1 score, were computed to assess model performance. Calibration analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA) were conducted for further evaluation. Patient characteristics were compared using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. Inter-reader reproducibility was determined from the external test set using linearly weighted Cohen κ values. McNemar’s test was used to compare the diagnostic performance of the physicians and the deep learning model. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Data set characteristic

The patient selection process is depicted in Fig. 2. After excluding cases with poor image quality (n = 92), a total of 806 patients were included in the primary cohort, with a mean age of 51 years ± 11 (SD), comprising 565 females and 241 males. According to the composite reference standard, 421 patients were classified as SBI, while 385 were classified as non-SBI. External cohort consisted of 114 patients, with a mean age of 50 years ± 11, including 90 females and 24 males. This set comprised 78 cases of SBI and 36 cases of non-SBI. Treatment test cohort comprised 316 patients, with a mean age of 50 years ± 10, consisting of 238 females and 78 males. Among these patients, 136 were identified as having SBI, while 180 were identified as not having SBI. Patient characteristics, including age and sex, did not exhibit significant differences across the datasets (Table 1).

Comparison of the developed machine learning models

The outcomes of the various deep learning models are outlined in Supplementary Table 2 (for whole-body image input) and Table 3 (for partial body image input). Notably, employing partial body images during training significantly improved model performance, resulting in higher AUC, sensitivity, and specificity values in test sets compared to using whole-body images. Specifically, the DenseNet121 model utilizing partial body images achieved the highest AUC of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.73, 0.86) with 72.6% sensitivity and 71.0% specificity in the internal test set. In external cohort, it attained an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI: 0.77, 0.91) with 84.6% sensitivity and 63.8% specificity. Moreover, in treatment test cohort, it achieved an AUC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73, 0.83) with 78.0% sensitivity and 69.7% specificity (Table 2). Furthermore, calibration curves and DCA affirmed the models’ exceptional discrimination, calibration ability, and potential clinical utility across both internal and external datasets (Fig. 3). Comparison with nuclear medicine physicians

To evaluate the performance of the top-performing deep learning model against human observers in detecting base of skull invasion, 114 patients from external cohort were assessed by two nuclear medicine physicians from the primary center. Figure 6 illustrated the diagnostic results of each physician. The results, comparing the radiologic readings with the best-performing deep learning model in external cohort, are presented in Table 3. Among the two physicians, the nuclear medicine physician with 10 years of experience achieved a higher AUC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.66, 0.84) with 61.5% sensitivity and 91.7% specificity. The nuclear medicine physician with 6 years of experience achieved an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.66, 0.84) with 61.5% sensitivity and 88.9% specificity. Notably, the AUCs, sensitivity, and specificity values attained by the nuclear medicine physicians with 6 and 10 years of clinical experience were comparable. Interobserver agreement for diagnostic accuracy between the two readers was deemed good (κ = 0.84). However, it is noteworthy that the AUCs of both nuclear medicine physicians were lower than those of the best-performing deep learning model in external test set (AUC: 0.75 vs. 0.77 vs. 0.84).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated deep learning models using CNN to diagnose SBI in NPC based on planar whole-body bone scintigraphy. Our results revealed that the deep learning models achieved a remarkable discriminatory power, with an AUC of 0.80 in the internal test set, 0.78 in the treatment test cohort and 0.84 in the external cohort. Notably, utilizing partial body images significantly enhanced model performance, outperforming whole-body images. The deep learning model demonstrated superior performance compared to experienced nuclear medicine physicians, with higher sensitivity and specificity. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis confirmed the reliability and clinical utility of the model, underscoring its potential as a decision-support tool for nuclear medicine physicians.

While whole-body bone scintigraphy remains a suitable imaging modality for clinical diagnosis of malignant bone diseases during the early stages, the procedure inherently bears a number of challenges (Gustafsson et al. 2022). Our findings align with previous studies that have utilized deep learning in whole-body bone scintigraphy for diagnostic tasks. Previous studies focused on automating classification of normal/abnormal scans and distinguishing malignant from non-neoplastic bone diseases, for example, Ghasem Hajianfar et al. showed promising results in automating classification of normal/abnormal scans and distinguishing malignant from non-neoplastic bone diseases on whole-body bone scintigraphy based on deep learning (Hajianfar et al. 2023). Our study specifically addressed the diagnosis of skull base invasion in NPC. In particular, the use of deep learning models in our study demonstrated superior performance when utilizing partial body images, showcasing the importance of regional features in diagnosing specific pathologies. Similarly, Cao et al. demonstrated the utility of deep learning in classifying thoracic bone images for diagnosing bone metastasis (Cao et al. 2022).

While MRI remains the diagnostic modality of choice for NPC (Wu et al. 2023; Maher et al. 2023), its limitations in detecting skull base invasion necessitate complementary imaging techniques. As such, the introduction of deep learning models for diagnosing SBI in NPC has significant clinical implications. For example, Nakagawa et al. (2024) demonstrated that CNN techniques are highly effective for CT-based diagnosis of skull base invasion by nasopharyngeal malignancies. Their model achieved high accuracy (AUC: 0.973), outperforming two radiologists (senior radiologist [SR]: 0.838; junior radiologist [JR]: 0.595). Our deep learning model offers a non-invasive and potentially more accessible alternative for diagnosing SBI, potentially in some cases where access to MRI may be limited. Moreover, previous study indicated that 99mTc-MDP SPECT/CT have excellent specificity for the diagnosis of skull base invasion in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (Zhang et al. 2014) and combing with MRI can significantly enhance the diagnostic performance (Li et al. 2017). It highlighted that SPECT/CT has important impact on clinical management decision for NPC. Nevertheless, considering the generalizability, extra radiation dose and time consuming of SPECT/CT, the use of SPECT/CT in diagnosis of skull base invasion in nasopharyngeal carcinoma may be limited. As such, the deep learning model can serve as a decision-support tool for nuclear medicine physicians by providing additional diagnostic information based on planar whole-body bone scintigraphy. By analyzing regional features indicative of skull base invasion, the model offers valuable insights to physicians, aiding in more informed decision-making. Additionally, the model’s high sensitivity and specificity reduce the likelihood of false positives and false negatives, improving diagnostic confidence and patient management strategies. Overall, integrating the model into clinical practice can streamline diagnostic workflows and enhance patient care in NPC management.

In our study, we compared the performance of the deep learning model with that of experienced nuclear medicine physicians. Previous studies have compared the deep learning model to radiologist or nuclear medicine physicians in several circumstances including identifying metastatic bone foci on whole-body bone scan (Ibrahim et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2022), differentiating malignant to benign bone fracture on multidetector CT (Foreman et al. 2024), that demonstrated the diagnostic performance of deep learning model is superior to physicians with lower experience with a shorter time of diagnosis. In line with these studies, the model outperformed both physicians in terms of sensitivity and specificity, demonstrating superior diagnostic accuracy in detecting SBI in NPC. While interobserver agreement between the physicians was good, the model exhibited higher discriminatory power, indicating its potential as a valuable diagnostic tool in clinical practice. Integrating the model into clinical settings can complement human expertise, providing physicians with additional support and enhancing diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, the model’s consistent performance across different test sets underscores its robustness and generalizability, further supporting its integration into routine clinical practice. However, further validation and refinement of the model are necessary to address any limitations and ensure its optimal performance across diverse patient populations and clinical settings.

Several limitations must be acknowledged in our study. First, the retrospective nature of the dataset may introduce inherent biases and limitations in data collection and analysis. Moreover, the geographical limitations of the data may affect the external validity of the study, necessitating further validation in diverse patient populations and clinical settings. Moreover, integrating additional clinical and imaging data, such as patient demographics and histopathological findings, can enrich the model’s diagnostic capabilities and improve its performance in real-world settings. The implementation of a fully automatic segmentation method could potentially offer improved repeatability and a more convenient workflow for future enhancements. Furthermore, a long-term follow-up is needed to evaluate whether SBI at whole-body bone scan have potential relationship with prognosis in NPC.

Conclusion

Our study developed and validated deep learning models for diagnosing skull base invasion in NPC based on planar whole-body bone scintigraphy. The models demonstrated high discriminatory power and outperformed experienced nuclear medicine physicians, particularly when utilizing partial body images. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis confirmed the reliability and clinical utility of the models, highlighting their potential as decision-support tools in clinical practice.
