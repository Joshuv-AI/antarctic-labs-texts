Effect analysis of transosseous wire fixation for patella fractures: therapeutic efficacy
Authors: Huilong Zeng, Hualin Zeng, Zhangsheng Dai, Kaibin Fang, Xiaocong Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOpen reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) represents a standard surgical technique for the management of patellar fractures, with tension band fixation serving as the commonly employed method. To enhance fixation strength, wires are frequently threaded through the medullary canal of the bone. This study aims to evaluate the potential benefits of utilizing transosseous wire fixation in the treatment of patellar fractures and its impact on patient outcomes.MethodIn this study, 223 patients with patellar fractures who had undergone open reduction and tension band surgery at our hospital were included, and a retrospective case-control study was conducted. The patients were divided into two groups: fixation using pin-tail bolt locking intramedullary Kirschner wires was received by one (observation group), while the standard treatment protocol was received by the other (control group). Both groups were followed regularly for one year, and postoperative complications were recorded. A comparative analysis was performed to evaluate differences in postoperative functional recovery and knee joint range of motion between the two groups. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS (version 26.0).ResultsA total of 223 patients were included in the study; 72 of these were assigned to the observation group and received transosseous wire placement, while the remaining patients were assigned to the control group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of gender, age, or fracture type. No statistically significant difference was found in the number of intraoperative X-ray fluoroscopies (P = 0.11). However, the operative time was significantly longer in the observation group compared with the control group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were detected in the incidence of nonunion (P = 0.56), refracture (P = 0.58), internal fixator fracture (P = 0.32), or wound infection (P = 0.96) between the two groups. In contrast, a statistically significant difference was observed in the incidence of internal fixation loosening (P < 0.05). Among patients with internal fixation loosening, no significant difference was noted in Bostman scores between the control and observation groups (P = 0.60).ConclusionIt is indicated by findings that although postoperative complication rates are not increased by the use of transosseous wire fixation in patellar fractures, no significant benefits are provided by this technique in terms of functional scores, knee range of motion, or the rate of fracture healing.

Introduction

Patellar fractures are represented as a common type of injury, and a significant proportion of all skeletal fractures is accounted for by them [1]. Surgical intervention is often indicated for displaced patellar fractures to ensure optimal recovery [2]. A widely adopted method for stabilizing such fractures involves the application of a tension band construct with Kirschner wires and steel wire [3]. Bone tunnels are drilled by this method to pass wires, which are then used to stabilize and maintain the anatomical reduction of the patellar fracture.

Nevertheless, significant traction has been gained by the use of titanium cable as a favored alternative to traditional steel wire, due to its comparable biomechanical performance [4]. The transosseous wire placement technique is widely regarded as the “gold standard” treatment and is extensively used in clinical practice. Bone tunnels are drilled by this method to pass fixation wires, which are then buried beneath the patellar surface. A key advantage of this technique is the reduction in prominent hardware, as routing the wire through bone tunnels minimizes metal exposure on the surface of the patella. It is suggested by some studies that compared with the traditional superficial wiring method, this approach may decrease soft tissue irritation and improve patient comfort [5]. Consequently, irritation to the peri-patellar soft tissues is reduced, and mechanical compression on the quadriceps tendon and parapatellar retinacula is alleviated. More stable fracture fixation and improved postoperative recovery may be contributed to by these advantages [6].

Despite these potential benefits, uncertainties remain concerning the transosseous wire placement technique. A major point of debate is whether the risk of postoperative refracture is increased by this method—specifically, whether bone integrity might be compromised by the tunnels created for wire insertion and whether higher refracture rates might be led to. Consequently, the overall risk-benefit profile of this technique for patients has yet to be fully established. To address this question, a retrospective case-control study was conducted to evaluate clinical outcomes in patients treated with transosseous wiring for patellar fractures. The clarification of the safety and efficacy of the technique is expected to be provided by the results of this investigation, thereby providing valuable guidance for its clinical application.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective case-control study was conducted that included 335 patients aged 18 years or older with patellar fractures, treated between January 2013 and March 2020. Patients who underwent surgical treatment for patellar fractures at a regional medical center within this period were comprised by the inclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria were as follows: loss to follow-up, presence of concomitant fractures, open fractures, previous ipsilateral lower extremity surgery, underlying conditions such as poliomyelitis that could influence lower limb strength, associated meniscal or ligament injuries, and incomplete medical data.

A total of 23 patients were lost to follow-up, and an additional 89 were excluded according to the following criteria: combined other fractures; open fracture; previous ipsilateral lower extremity surgery; concomitant diseases that may affect lower limb strength; meniscal or ligament injuries; incomplete medical records; and irregular follow-up attendance.

This retrospective analysis comprised 223 patients who received definitive surgical management, namely open reduction and tension band internal fixation, for patellar fractures at our hospital. Fracture classification

The initial radiographs of all fractures were retrospectively assessed and were classified according to the AO fracture classification system into type A (extra-articular fractures), type B (longitudinal fractures involving a portion of the articular surface), and type C (transverse fractures involving the entire articular surface), along with their respective subtypes. The surgical technique was selected by the attending physician. Approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024 − 250).

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by attending physicians with over 20 years of clinical experience.

Open reduction and tension band fixation was received by every patient; tension bands were conventionally constructed with Kirschner wires and steel wires. Notably, in some cases—often patients with greater financial means—a perforated wire and titanium cable were utilized for the tension band construct instead.

A longitudinal anterior incision, typically measuring 7–10 cm in length, is made over the patella to provide sufficient surgical exposure. Prior to fracture reduction, meticulous debridement is performed so that hematomas and soft tissue debris interposed within the fracture site are removed.

Following complete fracture reduction, two Kirschner wires (K-wires)—either standard or those with a tail-hole design—are inserted along the long axis of the patella in a parallel orientation. Precise fracture alignment is confirmed intraoperatively using fluoroscopy.

In the control group, conventional cerclage wiring is performed using either stainless steel or titanium wire. Typically, two wires are applied: one is placed in a figure-of-eight configuration across the anterior patellar surface, and another is placed circumferentially around the patellar margin. In both techniques, the wire is tightened adjacent to the ends of the K-wires so that a stable tension band construct is established.

In the observation group, transosseous wire placement is performed. A K-wire is used to drill transversely through the proximal patella from one side to the other, and care is taken to avoid penetration into the articular cartilage.

Prior to the placement of the pin-tail bolt locking intramedullary Kirschner wire, a tunnel is pre-established with a larger-diameter Kirschner wire. A conventional lumbar puncture needle is used as a guide so that the wire can be threaded through the bone canal. The same cerclage wiring procedure as used in the observation group is undergone by the control group. The titanium cables used in the surgical procedures, with a diameter of 1.0 mm, are supplied by Xiamen Dabo Co., Ltd. (China). A dedicated tensioner, specifically designed for these 1.0 mm cables and produced by the same manufacturer, is employed during surgery.

Postoperative management

Postoperative care included routine antibiotic prophylaxis and analgesic management. Operative time was systematically recorded; however, blood loss was not accurately quantified because a tourniquet was routinely applied in most procedures. Upon patient admission and during the postoperative period, the CAPRINI score is applied by surgeons as standard protocol at our hospital to assess the risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the lower limbs [7]. The CAPRINI score for deep vein thrombosis is a clinical tool used to assess patients’ risk of developing deep vein thrombosis. Targeted preventive measures are developed by physicians with the help of this scoring system through comprehensive analysis of individual risk factors. Its core content includes risk factor assessment, risk stratification, and prevention recommendations. Multiple risk factors are covered by the Caprini score, including age, surgical type, cancer history, and history of venous thrombosis. Patients are divided into four risk levels based on scores: low-risk, moderate-risk, high-risk, and very high-risk. For low-risk patients (1–2 points), basic preventive measures are recommended; for moderate-risk patients (3–4 points), combined physical prevention is required; for high-risk patients (5–8 points), combined pharmacological prevention is required; for very high-risk patients (>8 points), intensified anticoagulation therapy is required. Stepwise intervention strategies are facilitated by this stratified management. For patients with a CAPRINI score exceeding 4 (indicating high risk), prophylactic anticoagulation is routinely administered. Low molecular weight heparin or rivaroxaban is typically used as part of the standard preventive regimen [8]. On the first postoperative day, quadriceps contraction and ankle mobilization exercises are commenced by patients under medical supervision. Partial weight-bearing with crutches is initiated at four weeks post-surgery, followed by progression to full weight-bearing ambulation at eight weeks. Monthly radiographic evaluations are conducted until radiographic evidence of fracture healing is observed.

Outcome assessment

All complications were documented, including wound infection, delayed union, nonunion, refracture, fracture displacement, and internal fixation loosening. Delayed union was defined as the failure to achieve radiographic union within three months after surgery, while nonunion was defined as the absence of healing after nine months. Functional outcomes were assessed using the Bostman scoring criteria [9]. The Bostman score is the most widely used method for assessing functional outcomes following surgical fixation of patellar fractures. Patients are evaluated by this scoring system based on postoperative knee range of motion, pain levels, daily activities, and muscle strength recovery. Patients were divided into two groups according to the fixation technique employed: transosseous wire placement was received by the observation group, while conventional wire/cable cerclage was undergone by the control group. A comparative analysis of demographic characteristics and clinical outcomes was conducted between the groups. Outcome measures included complication rates, fracture healing status, knee range of motion, and Bostman scores assessed at three months postoperatively.

To ensure objectivity in measuring knee range of motion, standard goniometers were used by two independent physicians while remaining blinded to each other’s results. Functional evaluation was performed using the Bostman criteria, which comprise eight items: range of motion, pain, work capacity, muscle atrophy, use of walking aids, knee effusion, giving-way episodes, and stair-climbing ability. Outcomes were categorized as excellent (28–30 points), acceptable (20–27 points), or poor (< 20 points).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The chi-square test was used for the analysis of categorical data, and the t-test was employed for the comparison of continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

No significant differences were found between the observation group and the control group in terms of gender, age, or fracture type. No statistically significant difference was detected in the number of intraoperative X-ray fluoroscopy exposures between the two patient groups (P = 0.11). A longer operative time was recorded in the observation group compared with the control group, and this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was observed in the nonunion incidence between the two groups (P = 0.56). No statistically significant difference was noted in the refracture incidence between the two groups (P = 0.58). No statistically significant difference was found in the internal fixator fracture incidence between the two groups (P = 0.32). No statistically significant difference was detected in the wound infection incidence between the two groups (P = 0.96). A statistically significant difference was identified in the internal fixation loosening incidence between the two groups (P < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was revealed in the Bostman scores of patients with internal fixation loosening in the control group versus the observation group (P = 0.60). Discussion

It was indicated by our study that patients with patellar fractures can be effectively treated by both the transosseous wire placement technique and traditional techniques. Fracture healing was achieved in more than 90% of the patients included in this study. A lower probability of postoperative internal fixation loosening is offered by the transosseous wire placement technique, whereas a 7% probability of experiencing internal fixation loosening after surgery was observed in patients treated with traditional techniques. Nevertheless, no functional impairment was experienced by patients who developed internal fixation loosening as a result. Kirschner wires combined with steel wire in a tension band configuration are widely utilized for fracture fixation and are recognized as a well-established surgical technique for the management of patellar fractures [10]. Nevertheless, significant potential for refinement is offered by this surgical technique. One key aspect for improvement is found in the implant materials, particularly the steel wire components. The wire tension band fixation technique is especially indicated for midshaft transverse fractures—including those with comminution and displacement—as it enables the restoration of an anatomically reduced and stable articular surface following repair [11]. However, owing to the dynamic nature of the knee joint, conventional wires are insufficient to counteract the bending stresses exerted on the patella, and loosening of the construct is often resulted in. Consequently, prolonged postoperative immobilization is required, which impedes early functional exercise and increases the risk of late complications such as joint adhesion, stiffness, muscle atrophy, and post-traumatic arthritis. Moreover, limited restraint against anterior displacement during knee flexion and extension is provided by these wires, resulting in suboptimal stability. The tightening of the steel wire relies on subjective manual force, making consistent and appropriate tension difficult to be achieved. Wire breakage may be led to by excessive tightening, whereas compromised fracture reduction can be resulted from insufficient tension. Additionally, gradual tension loss over time is prone to occur in steel wires. In contrast, superior mechanical properties are offered by titanium, including higher tensile strength and fatigue resistance, making it regarded as a more advantageous implant material compared to steel wire [12].

Superior tensile strength, durability, and wear resistance are demonstrated by titanium cable, and material creep is generally avoided. Even if the locking clamp is deformed, the integrity of the titanium cable is maintained under tension without detachment. In terms of hardness, a close match to that of cortical bone is achieved by titanium cable, offering sufficient flexibility for repetitive motion within the knee joint to be endured, while abrasion is minimized and the risk of breakage is reduced. Tensioning can be precisely controlled through the use of a dedicated tensioning device, allowing accurate and consistent force to be applied by the surgeon. Additionally, Kirschner wires with a tail hole can be utilized as a practical alternative to conventional Kirschner wires. Issues such as wire migration and dislodgement are addressed by this integrated approach, thereby supporting the earlier initiation of functional rehabilitation.

The primary goal of tension band fixation is to stabilize the fractured patella and to facilitate healing. However, localized soft tissue irritation over the anterior knee is often experienced by patients due to the presence of metallic implants [13]– [14]. To mitigate these complications, the transosseous suturing technique has been introduced into clinical practice [15]. It is suggested by Hu et al. that favorable clinical outcomes are demonstrated by a hybrid technique in which peripatellar cerclage wiring is integrated with transosseous tunnel fixation [16]. Enhanced implant fixation has been sought by several researchers through the introduction of steel wires or titanium cables into the bone tunnel [17]. Theoretically, metal exposure on the patellar surface can be reduced by routing the cable through a bone tunnel, whereby irritation to the peri-patellar soft tissues is minimized. Compression on the quadriceps tendon and parapatellar retinaculum is also reduced by this approach, while more reliable transosseous fixation is enabled [18]. However, further investigation is required regarding whether a higher risk of complications is associated with the transosseous tunnel fixation technique.

In this study, it was observed that the transosseous fixation technique was not associated with a higher incidence of complications, despite a longer operative time being required. Although bone tunnels were created within the patella by the transosseous technique—a process in which a potential risk of fracture is carried—it was demonstrated by the results that no increased refracture rate was caused among patients. The importance of meticulous attention to technical details during the bone tunnel procedure is highlighted by our experience. Specifically, drilling should be performed at a safe distance from the fracture site, orientation toward the central area of the patella should be maintained, and careful direction away from the articular surface should be ensured.

A significantly lower rate of internal fixation loosening is offered by the transosseous technique [19]– [20]. This advantage is supported by our findings, as internal fixation loosening is considered among the most frequent complications that are encountered after patellar fracture surgery and, in some cases, is accompanied by implant fracture [21]. In our study, internal fixation loosening was observed postoperatively in nearly 10% (11/151) of patients. Notably, most instances were recorded after fracture healing had been achieved, often when normal activity had been resumed by the patients. In the majority of cases, displacement of Kirschner wires was involved. No significant functional impairment associated with implant loosening was revealed by functional assessment of these patients. Compared with steel wire, titanium is recognized to possess superior fatigue resistance under cyclic loading [22]. Precise tensioning is required by steel wire, yet the tension level cannot be quantified and suboptimal tension is often produced due to factors such as soft tissue impingement, thereby increasing the risk of micromotion [23]. Additionally, a correlation with patellofemoral arthritis is demonstrated by alterations in patellar height. The occurrence of patellofemoral arthritis after patellar fracture surgery also needs to be critically monitored [24]. In this study, internal fixation fractures were encountered in two cases, neither of which had been treated by the transosseous technique; however, statistical significance was not reached by this result. It is plausible that secondary trauma or strenuous physical activity may contribute to the loosening or fracture of internal fixation devices.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, as a retrospective non-randomized investigation, selection bias is inherently introduced. Confounding factors that affect the interpretation of outcomes may be introduced by the absence of randomization and the use of customized rehabilitation protocols. Second, although differences in complication rates were observed among the groups, the statistical power of these findings is constrained by the relatively small sample size. Therefore, these results need to be validated by future studies with larger cohorts. Third, clinical outcomes and complication profiles may have been influenced by variations in surgical techniques over different time periods.

Conclusion

It was indicated by the findings that although transosseous wire fixation was used in patellar fractures, postoperative complication rates were not increased; however, no significant benefits were provided by this technique in terms of functional scores, knee range of motion, or the rate of fracture healing
