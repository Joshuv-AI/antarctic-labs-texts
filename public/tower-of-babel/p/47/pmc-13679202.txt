Asymmetric cement distribution in the coronal plane increases the risk of prosthetic loosening following total knee arthroplasty: an in-silico study
Authors: Jun Han, Zhong Zhou, Wenqiang Xu, Han Wang, Lijunpeng Jia, Yong Ma, Jingchi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal knee arthroplasty (TKA) is the standard treatment for end-stage knee osteoarthritis (KOA). Aseptic loosening of the tibial component is a common complication following TKA. Previous studies have indicated that reduced cement volume and thinner cement mantles can lead to stress concentration in the cancellous bone beneath the tibial plateau, which serves as an etiological basis for prosthetic loosening. Our preliminary study has shown that variations in cement distribution can alter the local biomechanical environment. Asymmetric cement distribution is a frequently observed clinical phenomenon; however, no study has yet investigated its impact on the risk of prosthetic loosening.MethodsA three-dimensional model of the tibial side following total knee arthroplasty was constructed. The model included the tibial cortex, cancellous bone, polyethylene insert, tibial tray, and bone cement. Three model groups were created based on the coronal plane distribution of the cement: symmetric cement distribution, 75% of cement on the tibial side (medial), and 75% of cement on the fibular side (lateral). The model with symmetrical cement distribution achieved cement thickness exceeding 2 mm on both sides. In contrast, in models with asymmetrical cement distribution, the cement thickness on the thinner side fell below this threshold. All models were subjected to a 2100 N compressive load at flexion angles of 0°, 30°, and 60°, under both varus and valgus loading conditions. The differences in compressive stress distribution within the cancellous bone beneath the tibial plateau were analyzed across the models to predict the risk of prosthetic loosening.ResultsIn models with asymmetric cement distribution, a pronounced stress concentration trend was observed in the cancellous bone on the thinner cement side. Under varus and valgus loading conditions, the load borne by the cancellous bone on the thicker cement side decreased compared to the symmetric distribution model, with the extent of this reduction being smaller than the increase in stress on the thinner cement side. Under axial loading conditions, the peak stress in the asymmetric cement distribution model was consistently higher than that in the symmetric model. A more substantial increase in peak stress was observed when the lateral cement layer was thinner.ConclusionAsymmetric cement distribution leads to an aggravated trend of compressive stress concentration in the cancellous bone on the side with a thinner cement mantle, where the cement thickness is less than 2 mm, the threshold for prosthesis loosening reported by published studies. This serves as a risk factor for increased aseptic loosening of the prosthesis.Keywords: Asymmetric cement distribution, Total knee arthroplasty, Prosthetic loosening, Biomechanicak deterioration, Knee osteoarthritis

Introduction

In the context of a globally aging population, the incidence of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) has risen significantly [1]. Although various joint-preserving treatments are widely employed, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains the only definitively effective intervention for patients with end-stage KOA [2]. With increasingly stringent aseptic techniques in surgical practice, the incidence of periprosthetic joint infection and subsequent revision due to septic loosening has declined. Consequently, aseptic loosening has emerged as the leading cause of revision surgery, underscoring the need to address its underlying mechanisms [3].

Aseptic loosening, a specific form of implant fixation failure, is primarily driven by abnormal interfacial stress concentrations and bone compression [4]. These biomechanical factors initiate a biological response that leads to osteolysis and eventual prosthetic instability [5]. A comprehensive understanding of the etiology and pathogenesis from a biomechanical perspective is therefore essential for optimizing surgical strategies and improving long-term implant survivorship [6]. Bone cement plays a critical role as an interfacial reinforcement medium, significantly enhancing initial implant stability [4]. Evidence indicates that the thickness of the cement mantle profoundly influences the local biomechanical environment [7]. Reduced cement volume and thinner cement mantles are recognized risk factors for mechanical loosening [6].

Furthermore, the spatial distribution of cement, which is often asymmetric in clinical practice, also markedly affects peri-implant stress patterns [8]. Our preliminary studies suggest that cement distribution alters local stress distributions [9]; however, the impact of asymmetric distribution with a constant total cement volume on the postoperative mechanical environment and the risk of prosthetic loosening remains unexplored. Especially when the thinner side of the asymmetric cement distribution is below the “safety threshold (e.g., 2mm) [10]”, we hypothesize that even if the total amount of cement is sufficient, the risk of prosthesis loosening remains high. The main purpose of this study was to validate this hypothesis, and which may provide theoretical guidance for the optimization of TKA operation.

Methods

Construction of TKA models with different cement distribution

The modeling strategy employed in this study is consistent with the approach used in our previous research on postoperative complications of spinal and femoral implants [11]. The relevant modeling work was completed in prior studies and is briefly described below: A healthy 25-year-old adult volunteer was selected and underwent thin-slice CT scanning of the knee joint after providing informed consent [12]. The scan covered a range of 30 cm above and below the knee joint, with a slice thickness of 1 mm [13]. After scanning, the images were exported in DICOM format and used to reconstruct the bony structure contours [14]. Only the tibial bony structure model was utilized in this study. After completing the reverse contour construction, the model is imported into 3D CAD software, where engineering-based modeling strategies are applied to replace the irregular surfaces of the reverse-engineered model with smooth parametric surfaces [15]. Our preliminary study has confirmed that this method effectively improves computational convergence efficiency, reduces error rates, and enhances analysis accuracy [16].

The prosthesis model was developed based on the Smith & Nephew cemented short-stem prosthesis. Reverse engineering was performed within the same 3D-CAD software using the prosthesis design drawings, achieving a modeling error of less than 0.1 mm. Similarly, only the tibial metal tray and polyethylene insert were retained for this study [17]. After modeling, the postoperative assembly was constructed following the surgical protocol of a standard TKA procedure. Non-bony structures, including the tibial plateau cartilage and menisci, were removed. An osteotomy was then performed 8 mm below the plateau [18], with the osteotomy plane parallel to the tibial plateau to avoid introducing varus or valgus malalignment in this procedure [19]. It is well-established that an aberrant osteotomy plane and improper prosthesis positioning are risk factors for localized stress concentration and prosthetic loosening. This study controlled for this potential confounding factor arising from surgical technique to isolate the specific effect of cement distribution on the local stress environment.

In the assembly of TKA models, three distinct models were constructed by adjusting the distribution of bone cement in the coronal plane. The amount of cement used in TKA procedures is approximately 7.5 ml. Since variations in cement volume themselves represent a risk factor for prosthesis loosening, this variable was controlled in the present study to eliminate confounding effects (Fig. 1). The cement fully filled and encapsulated the tibial component beneath it. In the control model, the cement distribution was symmetric at 50% medially and laterally. In the two models with asymmetric cement distribution, configurations with 75% (i.e., 5 ml) cement on the tibial and fibular sides were constructed, respectively (Fig. 1). In the asymmetric cement distribution models, the cement distribution remained consistent in the sagittal plane (i.e., whether on the thicker or thinner cement side, the cement thickness was uniform anteriorly and posteriorly). By employing this model construction strategy and cement dosage, the model with symmetrical cement distribution achieved cement thickness exceeding 2 mm on both sides. In contrast, in models with asymmetrical cement distribution, the cement thickness on the thinner side fell below this threshold.

Mesh convergency test, and boundary and loading conditions

The material properties remained consistent across all models. In accordance with similar studies, material parameters were assigned using properties well-established in the literature [20]. Given that osteoporosis is an independent risk factor for implant loosening [21], the material properties of the bony structures were defined based on those previously characterized for osteoporotic bone. All interfaces—specifically, those between the polyethylene insert and the titanium alloy prosthesis, the titanium alloy prosthesis and the bone cement, and the bone cement and cancellous bone—were set as “bonded” contacts [22]. Then, a mesh generation strategy consistent with our previous research was adopted: the models were filled using tetrahedral elements of varying sizes [23], with finer meshes employed in regions of stress concentration (such as the bone cement and cancellous bone surfaces in contact with the cement) to avoid errors (Fig. 2) [24]. In the current models, the average mesh quality exceeded 0.8, effectively ensuring the reliability of the analysis [25].

Numerical mechanical simulation method: A. Element construction strategy; B. Boundary and loading conditions

Subsequently, a mesh convergence study was conducted on the control model (the model with symmetric cement distribution) [26]. Given that the key region for stress values computation is the cancellous bone interface beneath the cement layer, surface refinement was performed on this area [27]. The mesh size was incrementally reduced with a step size of 0.2 mm, and the analysis was solved under an axial load of 2100 N to compute the peak interfacial compressive stress [28]. The process of reducing the mesh size was terminated when further decreases resulted in a change in stress of less than 3% (at which point the influence of mesh size on the analysis results was considered negligible, and the current mesh size was deemed suitable for analysis) [29]. All models employed an identical mesh generation strategy. The inferior surface of the model was fully constrained in all degrees of freedom. Subsequently, under knee flexion conditions of 0°, 30°, and 60°, an axial load of 2100 N was applied, followed by the application varus-valgus moment (Fig. 2).

Results

The peak compressive stress at the cancellous bone interface beneath the cement layer was measured to assess the risk of prosthetic loosening. An overall identical variation tendency was observed across different models. Specifically, compared to the model with symmetrical cement distribution, a pronounced stress concentration was found on the thinner cement side within the cancellous bone. Under varus and valgus loading conditions, the load borne by the cancellous bone on the thicker cement side decreased relative to the symmetric model, though the magnitude of this reduction was smaller than the stress increase on the thinner side. In the model with a thinner medial cement layer, the maximum stress under varus loading increased by 81.47% compared to the control, but decreased by only 24.2% under valgus loading. Similarly, in the model with a thinner lateral cement layer, the maximum stress under valgus loading increased by 72.31%, while decreasing by only 19.82% under varus loading (Fig. 3).

Computed stress value on the cement-cancellous bone interface, and corresponding variation tendency between different models

Under axial loading conditions, the peak stress in asymmetric cement distribution models was consistently higher than that in the symmetric model. A more substantial increase in peak stress occurred when the lateral cement layer was thinner. Compared to the symmetric control model, the lateral thinner cement model exhibited stress increases of 71.59%, 75.16%, and 40.67% under 0°, 30°, and 60° compressive loads, respectively. In contrast, the medial thinner cement model showed increases of only 5.97%, 19.75%, and 21.77% under the same loading conditions (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

Fixation failure is a common complication among osteoporotic patients [30]. To address this issue, we have conducted extensive preliminary research. Primarily, the reduction in bone strength increases the risk of micro-fractures in cancellous bone under identical loading conditions [21]. This can lead to the formation of a cavity around the implant, resulting in micromotion and loss of stability. Furthermore, the progression of osteoporosis reduces the elastic modulus of bone tissue. The consequent increase in the mismatch of elastic moduli at the implant-bone interface elevates interfacial stress, which is also a significant contributing factor to cancellous bone micro-fractures and resulting fixation failure [31].

Like KOA, osteoporosis is a prevalent condition among elderly patients [32]. With the advancing trend of an aging population, a growing number of older adults with osteoporosis are undergoing TKA operation. The widespread adoption of TKA operation and the extensive use of cement augmented prosthesis have contributed to a large patient population at risk of the complication [33]. Furthermore, revision surgery for prosthetic loosening faces a series of challenges, including advanced patient age and bone loss, underscoring the continued practical significance of research into risk factors for implant loosening and the optimization of surgical techniques [34].

Previous studies have extensively discussed the potential etiology of prosthetic loosening. Among these, smaller cement volumes and thinner cement mantles at the prosthesis-bone gap have been identified as significant contributing factors [35]. As a semi-fluid material, bone cement exhibits extensive diffusion upon application, and this diffusion is considered a key factor in the change of the local biomechanical environment [36]. Based on the fundamental principles of biomechanics, within models with identical material properties, stress concentration tendencies can effectively reflect the potential risk of trabecular bone microfracture, and resulting risk of fixation failure [37]. Therefore, this study measured the peak compressive stress at the cement-bone interface beneath the bone cement as a metric for assessing prosthesis loosening (Figs. 4 and 5).

A typical case illustrating the main topic of this study: a patient with asymmetric cement distribution in the coronal plane, where the cement thickness on the thinner side was less than 2 mm (red arrow), experienced prosthetic loosening (blue arrow) following TKA

Due to the overall consistent trend observed across various conditions: Asymmetric cement distribution leads to a significant increase in compressive stress on the thinner cement side when the cement thickness on that side is less than the widely accepted threshold (2 mm), this cement distribution pattern may elevates the risk of prosthesis loosening, which is consistent with the phenomena observed in typical clinical cases. Furthermore, thinning of the cement on the medial side leads to a less pronounced increase in stress compared to that on the lateral side. Consequently, under conditions of similarly asymmetric cement distribution, reduced cement coverage on the lateral side poses a greater risk and therefore warrants greater caution.

Admittedly, following limitations still existed in this study. Specifically, a comprehensive simplification strategy was employed in this study. Firstly, this study employed an approximate linear elastic material property to simulate bone cement, and the contact type between cement and bony structures was set as bonded. However, in the real biomechanical environment, bone cement exhibits viscoelastic characteristics, and its porosity is related to multiple factors such as the mixing ratio of the cement and the degree of compaction. More importantly, as the number of loading cycles increases [38], the interface between bone cement and cancellous bone may loosen, and cracks may occur within the cement due to fatigue failure [39]. These variables have not been considered in the existing models. Therefore, it must be acknowledged that although the modeling strategies adopt commonly used methods in similar studies (including contact types and material properties), these deficiencies limit the existing models to simulating only the immediate postoperative mechanical environment.

Meanwhile, this modeling strategy based on a single volunteer’s morphological structure neglects the influence of local morphology, contour changes (e.g., knee varus or valgus) [40], and changes in the matching condition between bone tissue and prosthesis (which we assumed to be well matched) on the local mechanical environment and the risk of prosthesis loosening [41]. Moreover, this modeling, based on a single plane symmetry feature, although it eliminates confounding of experimental results caused by the irregular dispersion of cement, also ignores the impact of cement distribution in other planes on the local mechanical environment.

Finally, although the currently selected cement dosage can provide biomechanical explanation of the typical case reflected scenario: In the model with symmetrical cement distribution, the cement thickness on both sides exceeded 2 mm [10]. In contrast, in models with asymmetrical cement distribution, the cement thickness on the thinner side fell below this threshold. We still acknowledge that even in patients whose cement thickness is larger than 2 mm, changes in asymmetrical cement distribution may still affect the local biomechanical environment.

Therefore, this study reflects the situation where the risk of prosthesis loosening increases when the thinner side of the asymmetric cement distribution falls below the cement thickness safety threshold (2 mm) verified by previous studies in an idealized scenario. Thus, further large sample clinical studies and mechanical tests are needed to investigate the impact of the aforementioned confounding variables on the existing conclusions.

Conclusion

Asymmetric cement distribution leads to an aggravated trend of compressive stress concentration in the cancellous bone on the side with a thinner cement mantle, where the cement thickness is less than 2 mm, the threshold for prosthesis loosening reported by published studies. This serves as a risk factor for increased aseptic loosening of the prosthesis. Mechanical tests should be conducted to determine whether the currently computed immediate postoperative biomechanical changes can persist into the postoperative period after cyclic loading. Moreover, clinical studies should also be conducted to evaluate potential interactions between co-variables and cement distribution changes.
