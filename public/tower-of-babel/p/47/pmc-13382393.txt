Associations between early changes in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and outcomes across randomized trials: Insights from the ctMoniTR project

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCirculating tumor DNA (ctDNA) dynamics have emerged as a promising biomarker of treatment response in oncology drug development. Early decreases in ctDNA levels after treatment initiation are associated with improved long-term outcomes in patients with advanced cancer. To be considered for regulatory decision-making, patient-level meta-analyses evaluating individual-(I-) and trial-(T-) associations between early ctDNA changes and clinical outcomes using randomized controlled trial (RCT) datasets are necessary.MethodsWhile not fit-for-purpose, the Friends of Cancer Research ctMoniTR Project patient-level dataset included 10 RCTs in advanced cancer that were aggregated. Cox proportional hazards models assessed I-associations between molecular response (MR) and overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS) using either 90% decrease (MR90) or clearance (MR100) as MR cutoffs. T-associations compared treatment effects on MR with treatment effects on OS or PFS.ResultsIn pooled analyses across all trials, MR90 and MR100 were significantly associated with improved OS (MR90 adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] = 0.51, 95% CI 0.44–0.59; MR100 aHR = 0.45, 95% CI 0.39–0.53; both p < 0.0001) and improved PFS (MR90 aHR = 0.62, 95% CI 0.54–0.71; MR100 aHR = 0.56, 95% CI 0.48–0.64; both p < 0.0001). Associations were generally consistent across cancer types and treatment modalities. Trial-level associations between MR and OS were weak (R2 ~ 0.08–0.13), whereas associations between MR and PFS were stronger, particularly in aNSCLC (R2 up to 0.74).ConclusionsEarly decreases in ctDNA were consistently associated with improved clinical outcomes at the individual-level across advanced cancers. Although trial-level associations were modest, stronger relationships with PFS support continued prospective evaluation of ctDNA dynamics as potential early endpoints in oncology drug development. Additional work using datasets that prospectively included plasma collection for ctDNA analyses is warranted.

Introduction

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a promising biomarker for monitoring tumor dynamics and treatment responses in patients with cancer. Many studies have demonstrated that decreases in ctDNA levels following treatment initiation are associated with improved long-term outcomes, including overall survival (OS), in patients with advanced disease [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. These observations suggest that decreases in ctDNA may reflect early treatment effects and could be used as an early endpoint that is reasonably likely to predict long-term outcomes in clinical trials. Given this potential, there is growing interest in evaluating existing data to support use of ctDNA as an early endpoint for regulatory decision-making in oncology drug development.

Establishing robust evidence to support drug approval is critical for any novel early endpoint, including ctDNA [10]. To be considered as an early endpoint, data demonstrating the endpoint is a surrogate is often assessed. Generally, assessing surrogate endpoints includes a two-level meta-analytical approach that considers both 1) individual-(I-) associations and 2) trial-(T-) associations [11,12]. The I-association assesses whether the biomarker can categorize individuals of a clinical trial population into subgroups with differentiated levels of clinical response based on their early endpoint response, while the T-association evaluates whether treatment effects on the biomarker reliably predict treatment effects on the clinical outcome in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Because demonstrating full surrogacy through predicting survival is often unattainable, FDA may instead consider an early endpoint that is reasonably likely to predict the long-term endpoint for drug approval [11].

Friends of Cancer Research established the ctMoniTR project to assess how changes in ctDNA levels are associated with long-term outcomes (i.e., OS and progression-free survival; PFS). The clinical trials included in these analyses represented distinct clinical settings, leading to three I-association aggregate analyses combining patient-level data in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (aNSCLC) in different treatment settings (i.e., tyrosine kinase inhibitor; TKI, immunotherapy; IO, and chemotherapy) [1,3,13]. The ctMoniTR project demonstrated that on-treatment decreases in ctDNA were consistently associated with improved outcomes in patients with aNSCLC across treatment modalities and a variety of assays.

Data from the ctMoniTR Project offer a valuable opportunity to explore associations between ctDNA dynamics and clinical outcomes in the advanced disease setting. In this meta-analysis, we leverage patient-level data from the RCTs that were part of the ctMoniTR Project to assess I- and T-associations between on-treatment decreases in ctDNA and clinical outcomes and to evaluate analytical approaches that can inform future analyses. Though the included trials were not originally collected or structured to support formal regulatory qualification of ctDNA as an early endpoint, these analyses provide important insights into association patterns and methodology relevant to that future goal.

Materials and methods

Clinical trial data

Each industry sponsor reviewed the informed consent forms that were approved by the local institutional review board to ensure that the data were suitable for secondary use beyond their original intent. Patient-level clinical and ctDNA data were cleaned, formatted, and de-identified and anonymized by the sponsor prior to submission to the ctMoniTR Project. Sponsors uploaded patient-level clinical trial data to a third-party data aggregator (Cancer Research And Biostatistics; CRAB, Seattle, WA). To be considered for inclusion in these analyses, RCT datasets included patient-level data with ctDNA data from at least two plasma collection timepoints (i.e., one at “baseline” up to 14 days before randomization and at least one on-treatment), clinical covariates to select patients with advanced cancer (i.e., stage and prior lines of therapy), and OS and PFS data. A total of 5657 patients were submitted for all modules of the ctMoniTR Project; after removing single-arm trials (n = 897) and trials lacking OS data (n = 795), 3965 patients remained for patient-level filtering (Supplementary Fig. 1).

ctDNA data

Sponsors employed different ctDNA assays, which used different approaches to remove non-tumor related variants, such as germline and Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential (CHIP) variants, with one of the following methods: a) sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to identify CHIP variants for removal, b) baseline sequencing of a tumor biopsy sample to identify and track only tumor-derived variants, or c) bioinformatic removal of non-tumor variants according to assay-specific quality control procedures. Variant Allele Frequency (VAF) or alternate metrics (such as circulating tumor fraction estimates; ctFE) for all filtered variants were included in derived ctDNA metrics.

Similar to our previous ctMoniTR analyses [1,3,13], we defined index as the date of randomization, baseline (T0) as 0–14 days before index, and the on-treatment (T1) measure as 17–70 days after index to maximize the inclusion of patients given different ctDNA sampling timing across sponsors. For each ctDNA timepoint, we identified the Maximum (Max) VAF (or ctFE) over all filtered variants. If multiple on-treatment ctDNA samples were available in the 17–70 day window, we used the ctDNA sample with the lowest (“best response”) Max VAF. To account for sampling variability across studies, we designated 17 days post randomization as the earliest timepoint for ctDNA collection (original target was 21 days).

The percent change in the Max VAF (or ctFE) from baseline was calculated using the following equation:Percent Change = (Max VAFT1 – Max VAFT0) / Max VAFT0

Patients were categorized as having a molecular response (MR) if they achieved a 90% or greater decrease (MR90) or a 100% decrease (MR100, “Clearance”) in the Max VAF from T0 to T1. These cutoffs were selected based on past ctMoniTR analyses as the most appropriate to capture ctDNA responses across all treatment modalities. Patients who did not achieve this decrease were categorized as not having a molecular response (nMR).

Statistical analyses

We performed I-association and T-association analyses among all 10 trials, and then among the seven aNSCLC trials. For I-association models, we performed patient-level Cox Proportional Hazards models using the pooled dataset, with “small VAF at baseline” (i.e., VAF ≤0.5, yes/no) included as a covariate and stratification by cohort, where each trial arm was considered a unique cohort. To account for post-baseline ctDNA measurements in I-association analyses, survival outcomes were landmarked at 70 days post-baseline [14,15]. We conducted subgroup analyses to compare MR versus nMR survival within patients who received the same treatment class: IO-containing, TKI-containing, or Chemotherapy only.

We used a two-stage meta-analytic approach for T-association analyses [16]. First, we estimated treatment effects separately within each clinical trial using univariate Cox Proportional Hazards Models (Survival-Treatment for OS and PFS) and Logistic Regression Models (MR90-Treatment; MR100-Treatment) to estimate the treatment effect on clinical endpoints (OS, PFS) and on the early endpoints (MR90, MR100). We then plotted the associations derived from the first stage for each RCT on a scatter plot, with MR-Treatment association on the x-axis, and Survival-Treatment association on the y-axis. We calculated an R2 value using a weighted regression, with the weights set to the inverse variance of the Survival (or PFS)-Treatment hazard ratio (HR) [17]. We estimated confidence intervals (CIs) for the R2 value using bootstrapping with 100,000 iterations (percentile method). We conducted analyses using the SAS statistical software package (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) or R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Dataset

In total, 10 RCTs were included in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Table 1). To address potential heterogeneity across cancer types, we conducted two analyses: one including all 10 RCTs and another restricted to the 7 aNSCLC studies, to assess the impact of heterogeneity on the observed associations. Three RCTs from the ctMoniTR Project assessed cancers other than aNSCLC and had not previously been analyzed. The aNSCLC only data (n = 7 clinical trials) included treatment with TKI, IO, and/or chemotherapy. We excluded patients who lacked a T0 ctDNA sample (n = 1115), had a ctDNA value at T0 reported as non-detected (ND; n = 421), lacked a T1 ctDNA sample (n = 352), or if we could not confirm the patient had advanced stage disease (n = 127). After all patient-level filtering criteria were applied, 1950 patients remained.

I-associations

We performed I-associations aggregating data from all 10 RCTs and using two MR definitions: MR90 and MR100. We landmarked analyses at 70 days, reducing the sample size to n = 1899 patients for OS analyses and n = 1589 patients for PFS analyses. Using either definition, patients with MR were associated with improved OS compared to those with nMR (Fig. 1; adjusted HR [aHR] for MR90 [95% CI] = 0.51 [0.44-0.59], p < 0.0001; aHR for MR100 [95% CI] = 0.45 [0.39-0.53], p < 0.0001). Patient-level associations between on-treatment decreases in ctDNA and improved OS were consistent across cancer types and treatments.Fig. 1I-associations between MR and OS for all RCTs (n = 10) using (A) a 90% decrease in ctDNA (MR90) and (B) a 100% decrease in ctDNA (MR100) as definitions for MR.

I-associations between MR and OS for all RCTs (n = 10) using (A) a 90% decrease in ctDNA (MR90) and (B) a 100% decrease in ctDNA (MR100) as definitions for MR.

These patterns were similar when focusing on only the patients with aNSCLC (Supplementary Fig. 3; aHR for MR90 [95% CI] = 0.53 [0.45-0.63], p < 0.0001; aHR for MR100 [95% CI] = 0.47 [0.38-0.57], p < 0.0001). To determine whether this was driven by treatment modality, we assessed associations by treatment type in patients with aNSCLC. For both MR90 and MR100, MR was consistently associated with improved OS across treatment categories, though the strength of association varied by treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4).

We then performed I-associations between ctDNA decreases and PFS and saw similar patterns for all trials (Fig. 2; aHR for MR90 [95% CI] = 0.62 [0.54-0.71], p < 0.0001; aHR for MR100 [95% CI] = 0.56 [0.48-0.64], p < 0.0001). Additionally, MR was associated with improved PFS for all comparisons except for the TKI MR90 vs. nMR90 comparison where the observed effect size was dampened (Supplementary Fig. 6; aHR for TKI MR90 [95% CI] = 0.70 [0.46-1.08], p = 0.1504).Fig. 2I-associations between MR and PFS for all RCTs (n = 10) using (A) a 90% decrease in ctDNA (MR90) and (B) a 100% decrease in ctDNA (MR100) as definitions for MR.

T-associations

We performed T-associations for outcomes-treatment vs. MR-Treatment for all trials (n = 10), and then for only the trials in aNSCLC (n = 7). Two trials included three arms, and we performed comparisons between each treatment arm and the control arm, leading to a total of 12 comparisons. One non-aNSCLC trial was not included in the comparisons with MR100 because no patients achieved MR100, thus we could not calculate an odds ratio for the trial.

When comparing OS-Treatment associations vs. MR-Treatment associations in all trials, T-associations were low (Fig. 3; for OS-Treatment vs. MR90-Treatment, R2 = 0.086 [0.003-0.446] and for OS-Treatment vs. MR100-Treatment, R2 = 0.085 [0.000-0.476]). This same pattern occurred when focusing only on studies that assessed aNSCLC (Supplementary Fig. 7; for OS-Treatment vs. MR90-Treatment, R2 = 0.129 [0.006-0.636] and for OS-Treatment vs. MR100-Treatment, R2 = 0.077 [0.000-0.669]).Fig. 3T-associations for all RCTs for (A) OS-Treatment vs. MR90-Treatment and (B) OS-Treatment vs. MR100-Treatment.

We then assessed associations between PFS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment. Discussion

We combined RCTs in advanced cancer from the ctMoniTR Project, including seven aNSCLC trials and three additional trials in different cancer types, to evaluate associations between early changes in ctDNA and long-term clinical outcomes. I-associations showed that decreases in ctDNA levels from baseline to early on-treatment timepoints were associated with improved OS. These findings are consistent with prior ctMoniTR analyses focused on aNSCLC and reinforce the observation that early reductions in ctDNA reflect treatment response and favorable clinical outcomes [1,3,13]. T-associations were stronger for PFS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment than for OS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment, for both MR90 and MR100. Although the trial-level associations in this meta-analysis were modest, our findings provide practical and early statistical insights that may inform the design of future meta-analyses.

The I-association analyses were generally consistent across datasets. However, the number of trials included in these analyses was limited, and the dataset was heavily weighted toward aNSCLC studies. Additionally, different effect sizes were seen by therapy class, suggesting application of these results across cancer types and treatment modalities should be done cautiously in future studies. Despite this, combining studies with different treatment modalities, including IO, TKI, and chemotherapy, did not meaningfully alter the interpretation of the individual-level findings, suggesting that the relationship between early ctDNA changes and survival outcomes should be further investigated across therapeutic classes. While using clearance to define MR (MR100) provides a clean and easily defined cutoff, ensuring consistency across assays remains a challenge. Differences in tumor biology, natural history of the disease, and patterns of ctDNA shedding may influence both baseline ctDNA levels and the magnitude of change observed during treatment. Larger datasets incorporating additional patients, therapies, and tumor types will help to better understand the generalizability of these observations.

Interpreting T-associations in this analysis raises important considerations for future meta-analyses to validate ctDNA as an early endpoint. The appropriate R2 threshold to demonstrate a biomarker endpoint is a valid surrogate for clinical benefit is unclear [18,19]. While some suggest an R2 value greater than 0.7 and with a 95% CI that excludes 0.6 as the evidentiary threshold for surrogacy, there are no universally accepted criteria [18,20]. While OS is widely considered the most definitive measure of clinical benefit in oncology, associations between OS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment were relatively weak in our analysis. By contrast, associations between PFS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment were much stronger. Importantly, T-associations between PFS, the most widely used surrogate endpoint, and OS were also weak. The selection of appropriate long-term endpoints for meta-analyses to qualify ctDNA as an early endpoint in advanced cancer requires careful consideration.

Several sources of variability likely contributed to the observed trial-level results. The analyses combined trials with heterogeneous clinical trial designs, treatment classes, and cancer types. There were relatively few studies with limited replication, limiting a robust estimate of treatment-level correlations. The discrepancy between OS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment and PFS-Treatment vs. MR-Treatment may be due to treatment effects, confounding, or cross-over [11]. Additionally, each trial used a different assay and, in some cases, relied on algorithmic approaches to remove germline and CHIP variants, which have known challenges. Comparison of assays was limited by the lack of standardized reporting or analytical requirements across ctDNA platforms, as well as differences in assay methodologies (ddPCR vs. NGS, and varying panel sizes).

The approach we used to define changes in ctDNA levels as an early endpoint has known limitations. First, assessing on-treatment ctDNA at a single timepoint may not fully capture ctDNA kinetics over the course of treatment that may ultimately provide better T-associations. Additionally, our assessments focused on an early timepoint at least 3 weeks after treatment initiation; however, it is possible that evaluating ctDNA at later timepoints would yield stronger associations. Patients with non-detected ctDNA at baseline were excluded to avoid misclassification and to be consistent with previous ctMoniTR inclusion/exclusion requirements, so the full trial-eligible population could not be assessed. Furthermore, some trials did not assess ctDNA in all randomized patients. Future meta-analyses should ensure that included RCTs prospectively incorporate plasma collection so the entire intent-to-treat population has ctDNA data to minimize selection bias and enable a more complete assessment.

Together, these factors highlight the challenges of evaluating surrogate relationships using heterogeneous retrospective datasets and underscore the importance of carefully curated, prospective research projects that are specifically designed for future ctDNA-focused meta-analyses intended to support regulatory decision-making. The methodological lessons learned here, including the importance of standardized ctDNA collection and prospective plasma sampling, provide a foundation for future meta-analyses aimed at regulatory advancement of ctDNA as an early endpoint. The next steps for regulatory qualification should include trials with prospective plasma collection to reduce ctDNA missingness, include more RCTs for additional statistical power and generalizability, include analyses limited to a single therapy class and/or cancer type, and consider statistical methodologies that could account for cross-over.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of this study further support the association between early decreases in ctDNA and improved clinical outcomes in advanced cancer. However, important questions remain regarding the optimal definition of MR and the most appropriate setting in which ctDNA dynamics can serve as reliable early endpoints across disease settings. Continued efforts to expand datasets, standardize analytical approaches, and prospectively collect complete ctDNA data will be critical for determining whether ctDNA-based measures can ultimately support regulatory decision-making in oncology drug development.
