Vaccine hesitancy in context of COVID-19 in East Africa: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Melsew Setegn Alie, Gossa Fetene Abebe, Yilkal Negesse, Amanuel Adugna, Desalegn Girma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe outbreak of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic has had a significant impact on human lives, and the development of effective vaccines has been a promising solution to bring an end to the pandemic. However, the success of a vaccination program heavily relies on a significant portion of the population being vaccinated. Recent studies have indicated a rise in vaccine hesitancy over time and inconsistent factors affecting it. This study aimed to synthesis of the pooled prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and associated factors among various communities in East Africa.MethodsThe review encompassed relevant descriptive and observational studies conducted between January 1, 2020, and December 26, 2023. We browsed various databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, African online Journal, cross-references, and Web of Science. After extracted and exported to R the data analysis was performed using R version 4.2. Meta-package were used to estimate the pooled prevalence and factors of vaccine hesitancy. Publication bias was assessed through funnel plots, Egger's test, and trim-and-fill methods.ResultsAfter carefully screening an initial pool of 53,984 studies, a total of 79 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The overall pooled prevalence of vaccine hesitancy was 40.40% (95% CI: 35.89%; 45.47%, I2: 99.5%). Identified factors influencing vaccine hesitance were female sex, under 40 years old, inadequate prevention practices, relying on web/internet as a source of information, having a negative attitude towards the vaccine, uncertainty about vaccine safety, fear of adverse effects, uncertainty about contracting COVID-19, and belief in conspiracy myths.ConclusionsApproximately four out of ten individuals in this region express hesitancy towards vaccination. A tailored approach that considers the socio-demographic context could significantly reduce this hesitancy. To achieve high vaccination coverage, a comprehensive strategy is essential, necessitating substantial social, scientific, and health efforts. The success of vaccination campaigns within this population relies on the widespread and consistent implementation of effective interventions.RegistrationRegistered in PROSPERO with ID: CRD42024501415.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20324-z.Keywords: COVID-91, SARS-COV-19, Vaccine, Hesitancy, East Africa

Background

COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, has profoundly impacted the world since its emergence in Wuhan, China. The World Health Organization (WHO) declared it a global health emergency and later a pandemic. Over 5 million confirmed cases and around 100,000 deaths have been reported in Africa alone [1–3]. The rise of new, more dangerous variants underscores the importance of vaccines, which are the most effective way to combat the virus. A successful vaccination program requires at least 70% of the population to be vaccinated, and achieving herd immunity may necessitate even higher rates [4–10]. Various vaccines have been developed, including mRNA vaccines and vector vaccines like AstraZeneca and Johnson and Johnson [11]. As of May 8, 2023, over 13 billion vaccine doses have been administered globally, with COVAX facilitating nearly 2 billion doses to Africa [12, 13]. On May 5, 2023, the WHO announced that COVID-19 is no longer a public health emergency of international concern, marking a significant milestone in the global response to the pandemic [14].

Vaccine hesitancy refers to the reluctance or refusal to accept vaccinations, even when they are readily available [15]. This issue has gained significant attention, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, as many individuals remain hesitant to get vaccinated despite easy access to vaccines. In 2019, the WHO identified vaccine hesitancy as one of the top global health threats [5, 10, 16, 17]. The reluctance to vaccinate can be understood through three primary factors, commonly known as the "3Cs": confidence, complacency, and convenience. According to the WHO's Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (WHO-SAGE), vaccine hesitancy is characterized by a delay in accepting vaccines or outright refusal, even when vaccination services are accessible [18, 19]. The initial hesitation towards a specific vaccine or vaccination program is a complex psychological issue that varies across different cultures and regions [20, 21]. In Africa, vaccine hesitancy occurs against a backdrop of historical inequities in global health research, social and cultural complexities, insufficient community involvement, and widespread public distrust [22].

The global prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance has varied from region to region (36% in Africa to 83% Oceania) [23]. The prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy is between 23.6% and 97% globally [24, 25]. COVID-19 vaccine hesitance prevalence has varied, 42.2% in Uganda [26], 53.9% in Ethiopia [27], 48% in 16 countries across the continent [28], 49.8% in Nigeria [29],37.6% in Arab [30], 19.1% in Canada [31], 20% in Mexico and India [28], 33% in Italy [32], 21.4% in other studies of India [33], 25.5% in Bangladeshi [34], and the prevalence of vaccine hesitance varies from geography to geography [35]. Vaccine hesitancy is more pronounced among certain groups, particularly women, individuals with lower levels of education, low-income populations, Black adults, those with comorbidities, and people residing in rural or semi-urban areas [24, 34, 36–41].

The global hesitancy towards COVID-19 vaccines is a significant concern [24, 42, 43]. Previously identified factors associated with vaccine hesitancy across different domains include socioeconomic and demographic characteristics such as age, sex, residence, income, occupation, and marital status [43–47]. Other factors include vaccine-related knowledge [43, 48–50], attitudes towards COVID-19 vaccination [43, 46, 50], conspiracy beliefs [43, 50–52], trust and confidence [53], Information sufficiency and side effect [21], COVID-19 preventive behaviors [54–56], and perceived safety and side effects of the vaccines [57–60].

Global efforts to mitigate the effects of the pandemic and reduce its health and socio-economic impacts heavily rely on preventive measures [61, 62]. As a result, substantial resources from the scientific community and the pharmaceutical industry, bolstered by government initiatives, have been dedicated to developing effective and safe vaccines for SARS-CoV-2 [63]. Despite significant efforts to develop effective COVID-19 vaccines, vaccine hesitancy remains a major challenge. Factors influencing hesitancy can differ across contexts, but building confidence and trust in vaccines is essential for improving acceptance [53, 64]. Additionally, vaccine shortages complicate the global response to the pandemic [19].

Data on vaccine hesitancy in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) including East Africa, has been sparse [65]. The issue of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy adds another layer of concern [65, 66]. As LMICs begin to receive larger quantities of COVID-19 vaccines [67], it's crucial to understand the factors influencing vaccine acceptance. This knowledge is essential for developing strategies to enhance vaccine coverage and effectively combat the pandemic [44]. To promote vaccine uptake, we must explore whether individuals are willing to get vaccinated, the reasons behind their willingness or reluctance, and identify the most trusted sources of information that influence their decisions [65].

To date, most systematic reviews and meta-analyses on COVID-19 vaccination have primarily focused on vaccine acceptance and rejection rates [21, 24, 68, 69], with few studies summarizing the factors influencing vaccine hesitancy [21, 70] in out of region East Africa. This gap has resulted in a lack of consolidated data on this important issue in the region, highlighting the need for robust regional research and evidence. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address this empirical gap by determining the pooled prevalence of vaccine hesitancy in Eastern Africa. Additionally, it seeks to fill a methodological gaps, as there are currently no systematic reviews or meta-analyses specifically targeting this area. Furthermore, this systematic review and meta-analysis will present strong evidence regarding vaccine hesitancy in Eastern Africa. To tackle this issue and design effective regional interventions, we conducted a comprehensive study across multiple East African countries to evaluate the overall prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy and the associated factors. Our research aims to synthesize evidence on COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in the region and identify its predictors.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) to ensure a rigorous selection and reporting of studies.

Protocol and registration

This review has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews. This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in PROSPERO and the ID of this systematic review and meta-analysis: CRD42024501415.

Data sources and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analyses were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline [71]. We conducted a thorough literature search using various electronic bibliographic databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, African online Journal, cross-references, Web of Science, and Science Direct. Our goal was to obtain accurate results, so we utilized a combination of keywords and relevant thesauri focused on COVID-19 vaccination hesitancy. Our search terms encompassed a wide range of keywords related to our topic “((Vaccine refusal) AND (COVID-19)), or ((hesitancy) AND (COVID-19)) AND (Vaccine) or ((Refusal) AND (vaccine)) AND (COVID-19), ((COVID-19) AND (Vaccine)) AND (acceptance), (((COVID-19) AND (Vaccine)) AND (refusal)) AND (Africa), (((((Vaccine) and (Acceptance) and (hesitancy) and (factors)))), (((Determinant) AND (COVID-19)) AND (Vaccine)) AND (hesitancy), (((factors) AND (COVID-19)) AND (Vaccine)) AND (hesitancy)”. We used Boolean operators (AND/OR) to combine these search terms effectively. To ensure the comprehensiveness of our search, we also included materials from institutional repositories and examined the reference lists of similar articles. By employing this approach, we were able to gather a wide range of relevant studies and review articles related to our topic of interest.

Inclusion criteria

The systematic review employed two approaches, namely CoCoPop (condition, context, and population) and PICOS (population, exposure of interest, and outcome or response, Study type), to determine which studies to include. Any papers that did not meet these criteria were classified as irrelevant. The included studies had all study design and focused on the hesitancy of the COVID-19 vaccine and its determinants in the general population. Only papers published in English until December 26, 2023, were considered for this review and meta-analysis. The quality of each article was assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale, and all articles successfully passed the quality assessment.

Exclusion criteria

We have excluded studies related to COVID-19 that focus on animal experimentation, reviews, commentaries, letters to the editors, studies where raw data cannot be analyzed, and protocols specifically conducted in East Africa.

Study data management

After conducting a thorough search and gathering various articles, we proceeded to remove any duplicate files. This screening process was carried out in two stages: first, by evaluating the titles and abstracts, and then by conducting a full-text screening were done by two authors. To ensure accuracy, two independent reviewers utilized the EndNote software to assess the potential relevance of each article for further review. The assessment was based on a predetermined set of inclusion and exclusion criteria. In cases where there were discrepancies between the reviewers' assessments, they were resolved through discussion and by consulting a third reviewer. For audit purposes, electronic records were maintained for both the included and excluded studies, with clear explanations provided for any exclusions made.

Quality assessment for included papers

The study's risk of bias was assessed using a validated quality assessment checklist for prevalence studies developed by Hoy and colleagues[72]. This checklist comprises nine items that contribute to a total score of 9. These items encompass crucial aspects such as the target population, sampling frame, sampling method, response rate, data collection procedures, study case definition, study instruments, and parameters for the numerator and denominator. Based on the obtained scores, the studies were classified as low-risk, moderate-risk, or high-risk in terms of bias. Data extraction

The information was obtained by utilizing a Microsoft Excel template for data extraction. Two authors (MSA and DG) independently extracted the data using a standardized data extraction form. The form underwent iterative testing and revision as necessary. It encompassed various key details, such as the first name of the primary author, publication year, country, study design, survey duration, participants, sample size, response rate, prevalence (reported hesitancy), and factors influencing hesitancy.

Measurements

Vaccine hesitancy

World Health Organization (WHO) declared vaccine hesitancy as ‘the reluctance or refusal to vaccinate despite the availability of vaccines’ [73].

Attitude towards COVID-19 vaccine

The study assessed participants' attitudes toward the COVID-19 vaccine using ten attitude-related questions, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The scale assigned values as follows: 1 for "strongly disagree," 2 for "disagree," 3 for "neutral," 4 for "agree," and 5 for "strongly agree." Participants who answered 80% or more of the attitude questions were classified as having a "positive attitude," while those who did not meet this threshold were categorized as having a "negative attitude" [74].

Prevention practice

Based on summative score above the mean considered as adequate preventive practice and below the mean score is not adequate prevention practices [75].

Risk of publication bias

When analyzing research studies, it is important to consider the possibility of publication bias, which can skew the results. Two common methods for evaluating publication bias are funnel plots and Egger's test. A p-value of less than 0.05 is typically used as a threshold for determining statistical significance in detecting publication bias. To evaluate the variability in reported prevalence among studies, researchers use statistical tests such as I2. A value of less than 50% is considered to indicate low heterogeneity, while values between 50 and 75% indicate moderate heterogeneity, and values greater than 75% indicate high heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analyses

A meticulous sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall estimation of prevalence. Each study was systematically removed, and the subsequent impact on the estimate was closely observed. The exclusion of any single study did not yield a significant effect on the pooled prevalence estimate. Moreover, none of the studies deviated beyond the lower and upper boundaries of the confidence interval. This suggests that the collective findings of the studies remained robust and consistent, reinforcing the reliability of the overall prevalence estimate.

Data synthesis and analysis

The data extracted from individual articles was processed using Microsoft Excel 2013 and then exported to R software version 4.2 for further analysis. The analysis focused on 79 individual studies to determine the pooled prevalence of COVID-19 vaccination hesitancy among general population in East Africa. Due to high heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 99.5%, p = 0.001), a meta-analysis was conducted using the random effects model to estimate the pooled prevalence with a 95% confidence interval. The results were presented using a forest plot and tables. The Cochran’s Q test was used to test heterogeneity, with I2 statistics indicating low (25%), moderate (25%–50%), and high (> 50%) heterogeneity [76]. Pooled odds ratios for factors associated with vaccine hesitancy were also estimated, with statistical significance determined at P < 0.05 of the I2. An explanatory variable was included if data was available from at least two of the studies. Publication bias was assessed using visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry and a weighted Egger’s regression test with P = 0.05 as a cutoff point to declare the presence of publication bias. Subgroup analysis was performed based on potential sources of heterogeneity. Leave one out sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the influence of single studies on the overall effect and presented in tables.

Result

Study identification and selection

This systematic review and meta-analyses were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline [71] and we use it appropriately. The initial electronic search identified a total of 53,984 articles. After removing duplicate articles and excluding 13,102 records that were deemed irrelevant, 26,472 articles remained. These remaining articles underwent further screening based on their abstracts and titles, resulting in 399 articles for full-text screening. Out of these, 262 articles were excluded for to unrelated finding, qualitative finding and non-English published articles, while 137 articles were assessed for further and only 79 articles were included in the analysis. The reasons for exclusion included incomplete reporting of the desired outcome, qualitative study design, a study protocol, qualitative study, and unavailability of the full text. Characteristics of the studies

A total of 79 studies were included in this analysis. Out of these, 37 studies were conducted in Ethiopia [27, 74, 75, 77–110], 9 in Kenya [111–119], 3 in Somalia [120–122], 5 in Sudan [123–127], 4 in Malawi [128–130], 6 in Uganda [131–136], 2 in Mozambique [137, 138], 7 in Tanzania [139–145], 4 in Zambia [146–149], and 2 in Zimbabwe [150, 151]. The sample sizes in these studies varied, ranging from 191 to 17,682 participants, resulting in a total sample size of 90,834 participants. The participants included in these studies were from diverse backgrounds, including the general population, students, healthcare providers, eye health workers, and medical students. The first survey was conducted in 2020, and the most recent survey was conducted in 2023. All of the studies were cross-sectional and were published between 2020 and 2023. Meta-analysis of the COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy rates

The study analyzed the pooled prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitance in all population groups from 79 publications in east Africa. Finally, 79 studies were selected for systematic review and meta-analysis. The current systematic review reports studies from the following different geographical areas: Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, Somali, Sudan, Uganda, Malawi, Mozambique, Zambia, Zimbabwe. The findings showed that the pooled prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitance in east Africa was estimated at 40.40%, (95% CI: 35.89%; 45.47, I2; 99.5%). The forest plot of this systematic review and meta-analysis presented in Fig. 2. To determine the source of heterogeneity, meta-regression was conducted using sample size and year of publication as covariates. Surprisingly, the results revealed that neither sample size nor year of publication had an effect on the heterogeneity between studies. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was performed by removing studies one by one to assess the impact of a single study on the overall effect estimate. However, the removal of any single study did not significantly affect the pooled prevalence. Additionally, none of the studies fell outside the lower and upper limits of the confidence interval.

Sub-group analysis

This study investigated research conducted in East Africa regarding COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, analyzing 79 records from various countries in the region. The findings revealed that the pooled prevalence of vaccine hesitancy in Ethiopia was estimated at 46.79% (95% CI: 41.69%–52.51%), with a p-value of less than 0.001. This indicates that, on average, approximately 46.79% of the population in Ethiopia expresses hesitancy towards the COVID-19 vaccine.

Similarly, in Tanzania, the pooled prevalence of vaccine hesitancy was found to be 51.73% (95% CI: 37.57%–71.24%), with a p-value of less than 0.01. This suggests that around 51.73% of the population experienced hesitancy regarding the COVID-19 vaccine when it became available.

These findings highlight significant disparities in vaccine hesitancy across different countries. The study also assessed hesitancy among various populations. Among healthcare workers, the pooled prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy was recorded at 30.99% (95% CI: 23.09%–41.60%), with a high heterogeneity (I2 = 99%) and a p-value of less than 0.01. In contrast, the pooled prevalence among the general population was 45.89% (95% CI: 40.24%–52.35%), with a heterogeneity (I2 = 96%) and a p-value of less than 0.01. Notably, the second highest levels of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy were observed among students, with a pooled prevalence of 48.53% (95% CI: 37.60%–62.64%)(Table 2).

Meta-analysis of the factors associated with COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined seventeen studies to explore factors associated with vaccine hesitancy in East Africa. The findings highlighted several key factors linked to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, while others—such as rural residence, age over 49, higher educational levels, and individual perceived benefits—were not significantly associated during the meta-analysis. The factors that showed a significant association included being under 40 years of age, female gender, inadequate preventive practices, reliance on the web/internet for information, negative attitudes toward the vaccine, uncertainty about vaccine safety, fear of adverse effects, doubts about contracting COVID-19, and beliefs in conspiracy myths.

In the analysis of individuals under 40 years, five studies were pooled, revealing a low statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 24%). The meta-analysis indicated that being under 40 years of age is significantly associated with vaccine hesitancy, with a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 2.97 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.02; 4.37), and I2 = 24%, P = 0.26. Furthermore, four primary studies were pooled to investigate the relationship between female gender and vaccine hesitancy. The results indicated that women were 79% more likely to express hesitancy toward receiving the vaccine compared to men (pooled odds ratio [POR] = 1.79; 95% CI: 1.40 to 2.29; I2 = 42%).

Additionally, the analysis of four studies showed that individuals with inadequate preventive practices were more likely to hesitate in getting vaccinated compared to those with adequate practices. The pooled results indicated that individuals with inadequate preventive practices were 2.26 times more likely to hesitate about vaccination (POR = 2.26; 95% CI: 1.33; 3.84; I2 = 89%).

Moreover, data from four studies revealed that individuals with a negative attitude toward vaccines were significantly more likely to exhibit hesitancy compared to those with a positive attitude. Specifically, individuals with negative attitudes were 3.14 times more likely to hesitate regarding COVID-19 vaccination (POR = 3.14; 95% CI: 1.23; 8.02; I2 = 95%). A meta-analysis of two studies found that individuals unsure about the safety of the COVID-19 vaccine exhibited significantly higher levels of hesitancy. Those uncertain about vaccine safety were 3.58 times more likely to hesitate in getting vaccinated compared to their counterparts (POR = 3.58; 95% CI: 1.34; 9.60)(Table 4).

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive review and meta-analysis conducted to explore the extent and factors influencing COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among the general population in East Africa. The findings of this research hold significant value for various stakeholders, including researchers, healthcare policymakers and implementers, government officials, communities, and healthcare professionals. According to the systematic review and meta-analysis, the estimated pooled prevalence of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy among the general population in East Africa is 40.40% (95% CI: 35.89%; 45.47, I2: 99.5%). This means that a considerable portion of the population in this region exhibits reluctance towards receiving the COVID-19 vaccine. Furthermore, the study identified several key determinants that significantly contribute to the level of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. The factors statistically identified in meta-analysis and systematic review was age < 40 years, being female sex, not adequate prevention practices, web/internet as source of information, poor attitude to the vaccine, unsure about vaccine safety, fear of adverse effect of vaccine, unsure about contracting COVI-19 and myths of conspiracy. These factors demonstrate a strong association with individuals' hesitancy to receive the COVID-19 vaccine. Overall, this systematic review and meta-analysis provide valuable insights into the prevalence and determinants of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in East Africa. The findings can inform evidence-based decision-making, policy formulation, and targeted interventions aimed at addressing vaccine hesitancy and promoting vaccine acceptance within the region.

This systematic review and meta-analysis finding was comparable with the systematic review conducted in Canada with prevalence of hesitancy was 42.3% [152],and systematic conducted Turkey which found that vaccine hesitancy was 39.8% [153]. Our systematic review and meta-analysis finding is higher than the systematic review conducted by Pekcan et al.(2022) which showed level of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy was 21% [154]. This study was lower than the finding from global prevalence study which were conducted by Prabani et al.(2022) which showed the pooled prevalence of hesitancy was 53% [155]. This finding give insight that hesitance to COVID-19 vaccine is a challenge in all region of world and all segments of the populations. The possible justification could be there is common factors contribute for hesitance like complacency, convenience and confidence which influence the vaccine acceptance level of the individuals [156].

This systematic review and meta-analysis identify different factors affecting COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. In this study, we found an association between certain socio-demographic variables, such as being under the age of 40 and being female, with vaccine hesitancy. This finding aligns with the findings of previous studies conducted by Prabani et al. (2022) in Canada, the United States, and Italy, which also indicated that females had a higher rate of vaccine hesitancy [152, 155, 157, 158]. Moreover, studies conducted on the general population have consistently shown that females tend to exhibit higher levels of hesitancy towards COVID-19 vaccines [44, 159]. Additionally, being younger was found to be associated with both low and uncertain vaccine acceptability [160]. This pattern of behavior may be attributed to optimism bias, which refers to the tendency to be more optimistic about the health risks faced by others compared to oneself. Interestingly, optimism bias has been reported to be higher among younger individuals compared to older individuals [161, 162].

In this study female sex were associated with higher COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. Females were more likely hesitate to vaccination as compared with male. This finding is aligns with the study conducted by Zintel et al. [159], Kafadar et al. [163], and Bradfield et al. [164] which indicated that being female increase the likelihood of vaccine hesitancy as compared with counterpart male sex. The possible justification for this could be due to the women are more concerned about vaccine safety, particularly regarding potential long-term effects of COVID-19 vaccinations for themselves and their children [165]. Another possible explanation is that women may higher perceive risk than benefit associated with the vaccine compared to men [166].

In this study we identified the significant correlation between a negative attitude and vaccine hesitancy, which showed that individuals who possess a negative attitude being 3.14 times more likely to hesitate as compared with individuals with positive attitude. This finding supports the results of a previous systematic review conducted in the United States, which demonstrated that negative attitudes can increase an individual's level of vaccine hesitancy [158]. Negative attitudes towards vaccines may arise from a variety of factors, including conspiracy beliefs related to COVID-19 or a lack of trust in the medical community. A study conducted among Black participants found that nearly all participants (97%) endorsed at least one general COVID-19 mistrust belief, which suggests that mistrust may be further amplified by the sociopolitical climate in certain countries [167, 168]. These findings underscore the importance of building trust in vaccines as a means of promoting vaccine acceptance and reducing vaccine hesitancy.

In this study individuals who had never perceived themselves as being at risk of contracting COVID-19 were found to be 2.54 times more likely to hesitate when it came to getting vaccinated. This finding aligns with a similar study conducted in the United States [158], which found that individuals who were not or only slightly concerned about COVID-19 were less likely to get vaccinated once the vaccine became available [169]. On the other hand, those who believed they were at risk of being infected with COVID-19 or perceived the severity of the infection were more likely to get vaccinated [170]. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant association between vaccine hesitancy and uncertainty about vaccine safety. Individuals who were unsure about the safety of the COVID-19 vaccine were 3.58 times more likely to hesitate when it came to getting vaccinated. The perception of potential harms associated with the vaccine also acted as a barrier to vaccine uptake. Skepticism about vaccine safety was a common reason for individuals not intending to pursue vaccination against COVID-19. In a study by Ruiz, J et al.(2021) and Reiter et al.(2020) found that for each unit increase in perceived potential harms of the COVID-19 vaccine, there was a 5% reduction in vaccine acceptance [48, 171].

In this study the myth of conspiracy was identified as a significant factor in vaccine hesitancy. Individuals who believed in conspiracy theories were found to be 1.94 times more likely to hesitate in getting vaccinated against COVID-19. This finding was consistent with similar studies conducted in the US [158]. In addition, individuals who feared adverse effects of the vaccine were 2.25 times more likely to hesitate. Systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that fear of adverse effect had significant effect on vaccine hesitancy in the pooled population. Several studies [78, 83, 135] have shown that concerns about adverse effects increase vaccine hesitancy. The review also found that individuals who did not practice adequate prevention behaviors were 2.26 times more likely to hesitate in getting vaccinated. This could be individuals with lower expectation of benefit and high expectation of adverse effect of the vaccine increases the level of vaccine hesitancy [172]. In this study, the education level of the individual did not seem to have a significant impact on vaccine hesitancy. However, previous studies have shown that individuals with higher education levels are more likely to accept vaccines in the general population. This is supported by research that suggests higher education levels are associated with greater health information needs [173].

Limitation

This systematic review and meta-analysis faced several limitations. Firstly, it included only studies published in English, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, certain variables related to COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy were excluded from the analysis because they were reported in only one primary article. Additionally, the study relied solely on cross-sectional data, limiting the ability to establish cause-and-effect relationships. To address these limitations, future researchers are encouraged to incorporate longitudinal studies and qualitative research into their investigations.

Clinical and public health implication

The findings of this study carry significant clinical and public health implications for addressing the high rates of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. The relevance of this research suggests that targeted public health interventions aimed at the factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy could greatly enhance vaccine uptake among the general population. Additionally, these findings are crucial for managing vaccine wastage and optimizing resource use in the region, as they help identify barriers to vaccination prior to its distribution.

Moreover, this systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the need for designing regional interventions and programs tailored to the identified factors. Furthermore, the study's results are vital for researchers, healthcare leaders, policymakers, program developers, and healthcare providers, as they collaboratively work to devise strategies to prevent and mitigate the consequences of vaccine hesitancy in East Africa.

Conclusion

Vaccine hesitancy towards anti-SARS-CoV-2 vaccines is a prevailing issue in East Africa. Approximately four out of ten individuals in this region express hesitancy towards vaccination. Several significant factors have been identified through systematic analysis and meta-analysis, including being female and under 40 years old, inadequate prevention practices, relying on web/internet as a source of information, having a negative attitude towards the vaccine, uncertainty about vaccine safety, fear of adverse effects, uncertainty about contracting COVID-19, and belief in conspiracy myths. To achieve high vaccination coverage, a comprehensive approach is needed, requiring significant social, scientific, and health efforts. The success of vaccination campaigns in this population depends on widespread and consistent implementation of interventions. Furthermore, strategies developed to address vaccine hesitancy related to COVID-19 can be adapted to combat hesitancy towards vaccinations in general. Public health institutions should take into account the findings of this meta-analysis and the recommendations put forth by various authors focusing on this topic. By doing so, they can effectively manage vaccine hesitancy and develop optimal strategies for education and communication campaigns. These efforts aim to enhance population confidence, ensure vaccine safety, and foster trust in vaccinations.
