Transmission dynamics of ST346 carbapenem-resistant Providencia huaxiensis carrying blaNDM-1 and/or blaKPC-2 during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Jiayao Yao, Jintao He, Jingyi Guo, Haotian Xu, Jie Sheng, Pengpeng Min, Xinyan Tang, Chengjin Wu, Tingjuan Zhang, Yunsong Yu, Hua Zhou, Xi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCarbapenem-resistant Providencia spp. (CRPs) isolates are increasing, posing severe therapeutic challenges. Here, we analyzed 46 CRP isolates carrying blaNDM-1 and/or blaKPC-2 from patients (n = 40) with 60% demonstrating clinical infections and clinical environment samples (n = 6). 33 Providencia huaxiensis strains were identified, with ST346 (93.9%, 31/33) being the predominant sequence type. Transmission dynamics analysis identified that Cluster I (n = 12, SNPs≤36) linking to non-transferable pCTX-M-NDM-repM plasmids, replaced Cluster III (n = 13, SNPs<25), which harbored both IncT_NDM-repM (conjugation frequency: 5.04E-2; a fitness cost of 17.31%–19.41% with p < 0.0001) and pCTX-M plasmids. Notably, we identified 9 (29.0%, 9/31) ST346 isolates co-producing KPC-2 and NDM-1 and tracked the evolution of this phenomenon. Bayesian Skyline analysis indicated a 120% expansion of the ST346 clone during 2020–2022, in contrast to the decline in the cross-host ST4 clone (80% reduction). Our study tracks and alerts to the emergence of a hospital-acquired ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis clone with multidrug resistance, hypervirulent phenotype, and elevated transmission risk.

Introduction

Providencia spp., classified within the family Morganellaceae (order Enterobacterales), are ubiquitously distributed in natural and anthropogenic ecosystems, including soils, water systems, and animal reservoirs.1 These opportunistic pathogens can cause a range of severe human infections, including urinary tract infections, pneumonia, bacteremia, and meningitis.2 Currently, the following five species are clinically prevalent: Providencia stuartii, Providencia rettgeri, Providencia hangzhouensis, Providencia huashanensis, and Providencia huaxiensis.3,4 Among these, P. stuartii and P. rettgeri are widely prevalent worldwide, whereas P. hangzhouensis, P. huashanensis, and P. huaxiensis have been identified in China and associated with human infections in recent years.3

According to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI), P. stuartii and P. rettgeri are known to exhibit intrinsic resistance to various antimicrobials, including ampicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, cefazolin, cephalothin, tetracyclines, tigecycline, polymyxin B, colistin, and nitrofurantoin.5 Carbapenems are generally considered the last option in the treatment of patients with severe bacterial infections, especially those caused by Providencia species. Since the carbapenem-resistant Providencia spp. (CRPs) has been reported in Japan,6 and the global prevalence of CRP strains, particularly those harboring blaNDM, blaIMP, blaVIM, and blaOXA-48, has been increasing, posing a serious threat to global public health.7,8,9,10 However, most studies on CRPs focus only on individual cases and lack in-depth systematic research. Global systematic epidemiological analysis of CRPs remains insufficient.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) represents an escalating and complex global challenge, particularly when viewed through the “One Health” lens encompassing the tripartite interactions among human, animal, and environmental dimensions.11,12 In the context of healthcare-associated infection (HAI) prevention and control, the critical elements primarily involve the human-environment interface, where antimicrobial selective pressures and cross-contamination pathways converge to drive the evolution of resistance.13 The unique ecological niche of healthcare settings facilitates long-term bacterial survival through adaptive mechanisms, such as the carriage of disinfectant resistance genes (e.g., qacEΔ1-sul1) and biofilm formation.14,15,16,17 Sustained exposure within this ecological niche markedly enhances the adaptive fitness of the pathogens. For instance, P. stuartii has been demonstrated to colonize urinary catheters, leading to recurrent exposure among susceptible patient populations.18,19 Of particular concern is how ICU sinks, toilets, and medical wastewater—termed "humidity-rich zones"—have evolved into interspecies genetic exchange platforms for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) determinants.20,21,22,23 In addition, antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) mediated by plasmids and antimicrobial residues have been shown to disseminate across the human-environment interface into increasingly complex networks of natural ecosystems (e.g., rivers, insects, soils, and plants) through the “One Health” transmission continuum.12,13 Therefore, multi-dimensional surveillance of nosocomial clonal dissemination, plasmid dynamics, and ARGs transmission is imperative.

In this study, we conducted a decade-long prospective surveillance of CRPs in a tertiary hospital and performed phenotypic and genotypic characterization of CRP isolates harboring blaNDM-1 and/or blaKPC-2 to elucidate epidemiological drivers of resistance gene dissemination. Furthermore, we employed a comprehensive dataset for in-depth genomic epidemiology analyses to reveal potential factors underpinning clonal transmission dynamics and nosocomial outbreaks of CRPs linked to clinical environments, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. More importantly, we conducted a comparative analysis between globally disseminated and regionally confined epidemic clones.

Results

Clinical characteristics of carbapenem-resistant Providencia spp. isolates

A total of 151 CRPs isolates producing carbapenemases were collected from hospitalized patients across various departments between January 2017 to December 2024. Through WGS and ANI analysis, 40 CRP strains were classified as known species, including P. huaxiensis (n = 27), P. huangzhouensis (n = 8), and P. rettgeri (n = 5). These isolates were obtained from 38 patients (32 males and 6 females), aged from 29 to 97 years and were primarily collected from urine (70.0%, 28/40), with 53.5% (15/28) showing clinical evidence of infection, followed by sputum (20.0%, 8/40), of which 62.6% (5/8) demonstrated clinical infection (Table S1). Notably, 92.5% (37/40) of sampling sites had a history of invasive catheter intervention.

In addition, 4 carbapenem-resistant P. huaxiensis (CR-P. huaxiensis) carrying blaNDM-1 were isolated from clinical settings (ward sinks [n = 3], toilet [n = 1]) and 2 blaNDM-1-positive CR-P. huaxiensis were isolated from medical wastewater. Thus, a total of 46 CRPs were included in further investigations.

Antimicrobial susceptibility

The 46 CRPs strains showed MDR to clinically important antimicrobials (Figure S1; Table S2). All isolates demonstrated 100% resistance to meropenem, imipenem, ertapenem, cefepime, ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime, and ceftazidime-avibactam. The resistance incidences for amikacin, chloramphenicol, and aztreonam were 93.48%, 93.48%, and 17.39%, respectively. Notably, cefiderocol (MIC90 ≤ 2 mg/L) and aztreonam-avibactam (MIC100 ≤ 0.06 mg/L) demonstrated potent activity. In addition, all isolates exhibited disinfectant resistance, evidenced by MIC75 ≥ 64 mg/L for the disinfectant chlorhexidine.

Genetic characteristics of dual carbapenemase-producing ST346 isolates

Nine dual carbapenemase-producing (DCP) ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis co-harbored IncT_NDM-repM plasmids and other four blaKPC-2-bearing plasmid-types: pZ26CR2253_KPC-like (n = 4), pZ21CR2119_KPC-like (n = 2), pZ38CR2433_KPC-like (n = 2), and IncP (n = 1) (Figure S3). BLAST analysis demonstrated that these plasmids spanned at least six bacterial genera across multiple taxonomic families: Citrobacter, Enterobacter, and Klebsiella (family Enterobacteriaceae); P. mirabilis (family Morganellaceae); and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (family Pseudomonadaceae). In addition, four Tn6296-derived variants (ΔTn6296-1 to ΔTn6296-4) were identified in the genetic contexts of blaKPC-2, characterized by insertions and deletions (Figure 3B). Notably, ΔTn6296-4 existed in pZ21CR2119_KPC-like plasmids and displayed five copies of ΔtnpR-ISKpn27-blaKPC-2 flanked by IS26.

Plasmid transfer capacity and fitness cost

Among the four plasmids carrying the blaNDM-1, only the IncT_NDM-repM type plasmids (85.7%, 18/21) demonstrated conjugative transfer capability to the recipient strain E. coli J53 (Table S4), with a high conjugation efficiency of 5.04E-2 (IQR: 2.50E-2, 9.17E-2) (Table S5) and long-term stability (>95% retention after 70 generations) (Table S6). Furthermore, the transconjugants harboring IncT_NDM-repM exhibited significantly impaired growth compared to the recipient strain E. coli J53, including reduced OD600 (F = 12.78, p < 0.0001), AUC (F = 184.1, p < 0.0001), and relative growth rate (decreased by approximately 17.31%–19.41%, p < 0.0001) (Figure S4A). In addition, excepting IncP plasmid, the other three plasmids (pZ21CR2119-KPC_like, pZ26CR2253_KPC_like, and pZ38CR2433_KPC_like) could be transferred into E. coli J53 (Table S4), albeit with conjugation efficiency of 2.32E-2, 6.39E-5, and 6.89E-3, respectively (Table S7). For another, two types of blaKPC-2-carrying plasmids also exhibited fitness costs in transconjugants (Figure S4B).

Phylogenetic analysis of carbapenem-resistant Providencia spp. isolates in this study

To delineate the evolutionary relationships of CRPs, we analyzed the SNP differences based on core genomes of 46 strains (including three species) using Snippy (Figure 4A). Notably, 30 ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis isolates exhibited SNPs ≤135, except for three isolates (Z5CR1824, WW-Pro-1, and WW-Pro-2) (Figure 4B). These strains clustered into three distinct clusters (Cluster I–III): Cluster I (n = 12), uniformly harboring the pCTX-M_NDM-repM plasmid, displayed SNPs ranging from 1 to 36. Within this cluster, four environmental isolates (WJS-Pro-1 to WJS-Pro-4) and three patient-derived isolates (Z25CR2199, Z19CR2107, Z23CR2179) demonstrated <25 SNPs, implying evidence of patient-environment clonal transmission. The remaining two clones, both carrying IncT_NDM-repM plasmids, comprised Cluster II (n = 3, SNPs <20) and Cluster III (n = 13, SNPs <25). In contrast, CR-P. hangzhouensis and CR-P. rettgeri did not exhibit clonal dissemination in this study (Figures 4C and 4D). The virulence of ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis isolates

We evaluated the virulence levels of ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis isolates using the G. mellonella larval infection model (Figure S5). Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis demonstrated that both ST346-Cluster I and ST346-Cluster III exhibited significant virulence in the G. mellonella model. Notably, ST346-Cluster I showed significantly higher virulence compared to the hypervirulent K. pneumoniae strain NTUH-2044 (p < 0.0001, HR = 1.949), while ST346-Cluster III displayed comparable virulence to NTUH-2044 (p = 0.6427). Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in virulence was observed between ST44 and ST346-Cluster III (p = 0.6427). In contrast, ST32 showed no significant difference in virulence compared to the less-virulent K. pneumoniae strain XH1508 (p = 0.7162).

Global epidemiological profiling and phylogenetic analysis of P. huaxiensis

A total of 1,697 non-redundant whole-genome sequences of Providencia spp. strains were retrieved from the NCBI database, with geographic metadata analysis revealing predominant strain origins in China (41.9%, n = 712) and the United States (30.0%, n = 510) (Figure 5). 91.4% isolates (1551/1697) were classified into 14 known species using FastANI (ANI > 95%), with the majority being P. stuartii (22.9%, 388/1697), followed by P. hangzhouensis (21.7%), P. rettgeri (17.1%), P. huashanensis (14.6%), Providencia manganoxydans (4.2%), P. huaxiensis (3.3%), and Providencia alcalifaciens (2.7%) (Table S8). Notably, a paradoxical discrepancy was observed between the low global prevalence rate of P. huaxiensis and its clinical predominance in our hospital; conversely, the globally prevalent species P. stuartii remained undetected in our decade-long epidemiological survey of CRPs. Furthermore, P. hangzhouensis was predominantly found in China (65.3%, 241/369), while P. huashanensis was more prevalent in the United States (55.4%, 137/247) and China (24.6%, 61/247). Origins and migration dynamics of ST4 and ST346 clone strains

A Bayesian Skyline analysis of 15 ST4 isolates using an uncorrelated relaxed clock (500 million iterations) estimated a divergence age of around 1929.79 (ESS > 2000), with 3.08E-3 evolutionary rate (ESS > 1000), 1.86E-2 regional migration rates (ESS > 2000), and 1.02E-2 host-associated migration rates (ESS > 2000) (Table S10). For the ST346 clone isolates, 33 isolates using a strict clock (200 million iterations) estimated a divergence age of 2008.37 (ESS = 736), with 1.72E-2 evolutionary rate (ESS = 534) (Table S11). Furthermore, the dynamic evolution of the clonal groups was analyzed using Bayesian Skyline to estimate the effective population size (Ne). For ST4 clone isolates, The Ne of ST4 clone isolates remained below 1,000 throughout the study period, while this lineage underwent an approximately 80% contraction post-2021 (Figure 7A). In contrast, the ST346 clone isolates exhibited a relatively smaller Ne value (<100), suggesting a potential hospital-level prevalence (Figure 7B). Notably, a rapid expansion occurred in this lineage, with the median Ne nearly doubling (approximately 120% increase) during 2019–2021. The joint analysis of Bayesian temporal divergence and phylogeographic modeling (SpreadD3), estimated a heterogeneous spatial transmission network of ST4 P. huaxiensis clone isolates in China from 1942 to 2024 (Figure 7C). Key transmission hubs included Sichuan (posterior probability peak: 0.21), Guangdong (peak: 0.12), Beijing (peak: 0.17), and Anhui (peak: 0.16). The posterior probability of the host estimated that Homo sapiens (peak: 0.75) and the fly (peak: 0.25) were the key for transmission. Sichuan, as the origin of ST4 clonal isolates, sequentially disseminated the strains to Anhui (1942–1960) and Beijing (1958–1972) through human-mediated transmission. Subsequently, Anhui further propagated the clones to Guangdong (1962–1991) via insect vectors (flies). Notably, Guangdong emerged as the primary dissemination hub, spreading the clonal strains to Zhejiang (1992–2020), Henan (1992–2020), and Shaanxi (2008–2024) through diverse transmission pathways.

Discussion

Carbapenem resistance observed in Providencia spp., predominantly mediated by blaNDM-1,24 has escalated into a global threat with outbreaks spanning China,10 Romania,25 and Italy.26 As shown in our study, 46 CRP strains producing NDM-1and/or KPC-2 enzymes were isolated from both patients and clinical environment samples, with ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis as the dominant species. Our analysis revealed that 60% (24/40) of patients exhibited infections at sampling sites with a history of invasive catheter intervention. Among these, 50% (13/26) of ST346 clone carriers developed active infections. These findings suggest that the ST346 clone exhibits heightened pathogenicity, particularly in immunocompromised patients, leading to increased rates of exogenous catheter-mediated infections.

Notably, 29% (9/31) of ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis isolates carried both blaNDM-1 and blaKPC-2 on distinct plasmids were identified, demonstrating an emerging trend of DCP in Enterobacterales.27,28 This trend will further limit therapeutic options, potentially driving the emergence of pandrug-resistant bacteria. Fortunately, AST results showed that cefiderocol and aztreonam-avibactam exhibited potent activity against CRPs, with only 4.35% cefiderocol non-susceptibility (CFDC-NS). Particularly, aztreonam-avibactam demonstrated superior in vitro efficacy against DCP strains (MIC100 ≤ 0.06 mg/L), suggesting that combination therapies could be a viable option in clinical scenarios where monotherapy fails.29 Among the currently available antimicrobials, cefiderocol is a last resort option, especially for carbapenem-resistant (CR) Acinetobacter baumannii and metallo-β-lactamase (MBL)-producing Pseudomonas aeruginosa, because newer β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitors are inactive against these pathogens.30 A variety of mechanisms have been reported to confer resistance to cefiderocol: β-lactamases (particularly NDM, KPC, and AmpC variants conferring resistance to ceftazidime/avibactam, OXA-427, and PER- and SHV-type ESBLs), porin mutations, and mutations affecting siderophore receptors, efflux pumps, and target (PBP-3) modifications, as well as heteroresistance.31,32 The coexpression of multiple β-lactamases, often in combination with permeability defects, appears to be the main mechanism of resistance.31 Additionally, a meta-analysis revealed that while the overall prevalence of CFDC-NS was low, it was significantly higher in carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (12.4%).30 However, this study did not include Providencia spp., warranting further clinical surveillance.

During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), Cluster I (n = 12, SNPs ≤ 36) carrying exclusively pCTX-M-NDM-repM plasmids replaced Cluster III (n = 13, SNPs < 25) (2017–2018), which harbored both IncT_NDM-repM and pCTX-M plasmids (Figures 4B and 4E). This shift likely occurred because Cluster I evolved from the Cluster III lineage by integrating the 36-kbp NDM-repM fragment from IncT into pCTX-M, followed by loss of the IncT plasmid (Figure 2). This genomic streamlining may have reduced fitness costs, thereby enhancing its capacity to drive nosocomial transmission (Figure S4). Notably, the minimal SNP divergence (<10 SNPs) between the Z25CR2199 isolate and four clinical environment isolates elucidates the "clinical environment-to-patients" transmission chain. This environmental microbial screening demonstrated that sink basins and toilet seats serve as significant reservoirs for pathogen dissemination in a hospital, providing actionable insights for optimizing clinical infection control protocols. Furthermore, virulence assessment using the G. mellonella infection model revealed that ST346 CR-P. huaxiensis isolates exhibited strong virulence, suggesting their potential for significant clinical pathogenicity. However, virulence-associated genes in Providencia spp. remain largely uncharacterized, warranting further in-depth investigation. In summary, the ST346 clones demonstrate both high transmissibility and pathogenicity, highlighting their critical importance for enhanced clinical infection prevention and control measures.

Both IncT_NDM-repM and pCTX-M_NDM-repM plasmids harbored a seamless insertion of identical approximately 36-kbp insertion sequence carrying blaNDM-1 flanked by a Tn3-family and Tn5073-like mercury resistance module (merRTPFADE_urf2) on the hp and traI, which is an untypical recombination mechanism characterized by the absence of TSDs.33,34 Tn3-family transposons are widespread and notorious in the dissemination of ARGs, particularly the prevalence of carbapenem resistance.35 Additionally, IncT_NDM-repM plasmids exhibited high efficiency of transferability (5.04E-2, IQR: 2.5E-2, 9.17E-2) and long-term stability (>95% retention after 70 generations), facilitating plasmid-mediated inter-species transmission of ARGs. Furthermore, combined evidence of vertical clonal dissemination (SNPs ≤ 36) and horizontal spread of diverse transferable KPC-plasmids across various species (Figure S3) supports a proposed evolutionary trajectory for DCP strains: ancestral clones inherently harboring IncT_NDM-repM with blaNDM-1 subsequently acquired blaKPC-2 through horizontal plasmid transfer during polymicrobial colonization.

In addition to plasmids, transposons (Tn) and insertion sequences (IS) are also critical mechanisms in ARGs spread within the “One Health” framework.12 Core genetic context analysis of blaNDM-1 and blaKPC-2 revealed that the initial dissemination of resistance genes was mediated by transposons (ΔTn125 and ΔTn6296), followed by multiple copies through more flexible elements (IS26 and ISCR1 element). Previous studies have concluded that IS26-mediated gene insertion and amplification, combined with ISCR1-driven rolling-circle replication, significantly increased the copy number of resistance genes.36,37,38 For instance, five copies of the ΔtnpR-ISKpn27-blaKPC-2 flanked by IS26, a compact and mobile structure, were identified in this study. Furthermore, ISCR1-ΔtrpF-bleMBL-blaNDM-1-ΔISAba125-ΔcatB-arr-qacEΔ1-sul1 structure posing three copies of the blaNDM-1 gene in the chromosome, through circular intermediation flanked by ISCR1.

The NCBI database revealed a paradoxical epidemiological pattern: P. huaxiensis exhibited low global prevalence but high clinical endemicity within our hospital, while the typically dominant P. stuartii was absent among local Providencia species. This discrepancy may reflect underrecognized epidemiological traits (e.g., host adaptability) and/or antimicrobial overuse-driven regional transmission networks that facilitate niche dominance by P. huaxiensis and cryptic species such as P. hangzhouensis.39 Notably, traditional MALDI-TOF MS misidentified three distinct Providencia species as P. rettgeri in this study, exposing inherent limitations in spectral database-dependent identification and potential biases in prior epidemiological assessments relying solely on MS-based methods.39 These findings underscore the critical importance of advancing the widespread adoption of WGS to improve the accuracy of hospital infection surveillance and track emerging pathogens.

Through Bayesian Skyline analysis, we observed an interesting phenomenon in the ST4 and ST346 P. huaxiensis clones during the COVID-19 pandemic. The ST4 clone, which had been in a slow decline, saw a sharp drop (about 80%) in population size during the pandemic. In contrast, the ST346 clone had a short-term outbreak from 2019 to 2021 (a rise of around 120%) and then a quick contraction from 2022 to 2024. We analyzed potential drivers underlying the observed phenomena as follows. Firstly, extensive use of disinfectants during the pandemic likely intensified the contraction of ST4 (MICCHG = 16), which exhibited a weaker resistance profile compared to ST346 (Medium of MICCHG = 64). Second, the ST4 clone, which primarily relies on community and multi-host transmission pathways (Figure 7C), may have experienced dissemination disruption due to the social distancing and infection control measures implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic. In contrast, the ST346 clone harbored multiple resistance determinants, including a universal carbapenem resistance rate of 100% and the qacEΔ1-sul1 disinfectant resistance cassette (95.7% prevalence), enabling short-term clonal expansion under selective pressures within confined ecological niches. Growth curve assays indicate a moderate fitness cost associated with plasmid carriage, which may be one of the reasons why the ST346 clone experiences adaptive instability and population decline in the later stages.

Conclusion

In summary, this one-decade surveillance study delineates the dissemination dynamics of blaNDM-1 and blaKPC-2 among CRPs through a comprehensive phenotypic-genotypic analysis, demonstrating that clinical settings are significantly linked to clonal transmission and nosocomial outbreaks with the “One Health” framework. Notably, we report the DCP strains in ST346 P. huaxiensis and track their evolutionary acquisition of NDM- and KPC-producing capabilities. Transmission dynamics analysis identified that Cluster III repleased Cluster I to reduce fitness costs, thereby enhancing its capacity to drive nosocomial transmission during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022). In addition, IS26-mediated insertion and amplification of blaNDM-1 and blaKPC-2 in plasmids, as well as ISCR1-driven rolling-circle replication, posing three copies of blaNDM-1 in the chromosome, were identified. Furthermore, our findings highlight divergent evolutionary trajectories between globally disseminated clones and regionally restricted strains. Finally, Bayesian Skyline analysis revealed that ST346 and ST4 clonal groups exhibited opposing patterns of population expansion during the COVID-19 pandemic. Consequently, multi-dimensional surveillance of nosocomial clonal dissemination is imperative, particularly when coinciding with severe infections.

Limitations of the study

Some assays were performed in monoculture settings and did not adequately simulate ecological competition in polymicrobial environments such as the gut, which is worth further investigation in the future. Moreover, the main limitation of this study lies in the lack of multicenter data support. Further research is needed to investigate the subsequent evolutionary dynamics of these two dominant clones in China and to conduct clinical pathogenicity cohort studies.

Experimental model and subject details

Bacterial strains and reagents

All bacterial strains used in this study have been included in the "Bacterial and virus strains" section of the key resources table. Additionally, detailed clinical information and antimicrobial resistance profiles of the 46 Providencia spp. isolates (the primary study subjects) are comprehensively described in Tables S1 and S2, respectively.

Strain culturing

The strains were grown overnight on MH agar plates or in LB broth with shaking at 250 rpm at 37°C.

Method details

Collection and identification of clinical isolates

From January 2017 to November 2024, CRPs isolates in patients were collected from a tertiary hospital located in Zhejiang, China. Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Sysmex-bioMérieux, France) was used for preliminary species identification. The carbapenem gene was detected by colloidal gold immunochromatography (NG-Test® CARBA 5, FOSUM DIAGNOSTICS) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR).55,56 Essential anonymized metadata were collected for analysis, including patient gender and age, collection dates, sources, clinical diagnosis, co-isolated pathogenic bacteria, invasive devices, and determination of infection versus colonization at sampling sites.

Environmental screening for CRPs isolates

Referencing previous studies, environmental sampling and bacterial screening studies were conducted in wards and medical wastewater.57,58 In May 2024, two rounds of medical wastewater sampling were conducted. Subsequently in June 2024, a sampling was conducted on a per-bed basis, with swab samples collected from the surrounding environment and equipment surfaces (including bed rails, bedside tables, ward operating tables, ward sinks, and toilet) within patient rooms based on the screening results of CRPs in patients. Concurrently, samples were obtained from equipment surfaces in communal areas outside patient rooms (including mobile trolleys and nursing stations). A total of 347 samples (including 30 from sinks and 26 from toilets) in 95 per-beds were enriched in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) containing 8 mg/L vancomycin (Van) and 20 mg/L colistin (CST) at 200 rpm overnight. Subsequently, screening was performed using antibiotics-containing MH agar plates (8 mg/L Van, 20 mg/L CST, and 32/4 mg/L ceftazidime-avibactam) for CRPs producing NDM, followed by identification using MS and PCR.

Antimicrobial susceptibilities testing

Antimicrobial susceptibilities testing (AST) was conducted following the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute standards (CLSI, 2024). Broth microdilution method was used to measure minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of twelve antimicrobials: meropenem, imipenem, ertapenem, ciprofloxacin, cefepime, ceftazidime, amikacin, chloramphenicol, aztreonam, ceftazidime-avibactam, aztreonam-avibactam and cefiderocol. MIC results were interpreted following CLSI guidelines, except for aztreonam-avibactam,59 for which breakpoints were referenced to aztreonam and ceftazidime-avibactam (R≥16/4 mg/L). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as a quality control strain. Besides, the MIC value of disinfectant chlorhexidine was also determined using the aforementioned method.

Conjugation transfer assay, fitness cost analysis, and plasmid stability testing

To confirm the transferability of carbapenem resistance gene, conjugative assay was performed using recipient E. coli J53 (azideR), following previous study.60 Overnight LB cultures of donor and recipient strains were adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 in 0.9% NaCl, mixed 1:1, and 50 μL aliquots were spotted on 0.22-μm filters (37°C, 6 hr). Filters were transferred to 1 mL of 0.9% NaCl, vortexed and subjected to a 1/10 serial dilution up to 10-7. 20 μL Aliquots from each dilution gradient (10-1 to 10-7) were spotted on single-antibiotic plates (100 mg/L ampicillin) and double-antibiotic plates (100 mg/L ampicillin or aztreonam, with 300 mg/L azide). Conjugation frequency was calculated from transconjugant/donor counts, with transconjugants validated by MALDI-TOF MS and PCR. Subsequently, growth rate measurements and serial passage experiments with a 1:100 dilution ratio were conducted to assess plasmid fitness cost and plasmid stability, as described previously.60

Galleria mellonella larvae infection model

G. mellonella infection model was established as described previously.61 Briefly, bacterial cultures in the logarithmic growth phase (37°C, 200 rpm, 4 h) were washed with sterile PBS and adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0/mL. The bacterial suspension was further diluted to 1×108 CFUs/mL. Each group of 10 larvae was injected with 10 μL of the working bacterial suspension (with three biological replicates) and then incubated in the dark at 37°C. The less-virulent K. pneumoniae strain XH1508 and the hypervirulent K. pneumoniae strain NTUH-2044 were selected as quality controls. The number of dead larvae was recorded every 12 hours within three days after injection. The log-rank test was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.

Whole-genome sequencing and genomic analysis

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on all isolates following previously established protocols.62 The complete genome sequences were acquired using Unicycler v 0.4.863 and annotated using the RAST (http://rast.nmpdr.org/).40 ResFinder v4.6.0,41 PlasmidFinder v2.0.1,42 and MobileElementFinder (MGE) v1.0.343 from the Center of Genomic Epidemiology (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/services/) were used to identify antimicrobial resistance genes, plasmid types, and mobile genomic elements, respectively. Sequence comparisons were performed using BLAST v2.16.0,44 the results were further visualized using Easyfig v2.2.245 and Proksee v2.0 (https://proksee.ca/).46

Global epidemical analysis of Providencia spp.

To further monitor the global phylogeographic of Providencia spp., we assessed all available 1,697 genomes of Providencia spp. in GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome, accessed on December 17, 2024). Species were delineated based on average nucleotide identity (ANI) > 95% using FastANI v1.34 (https://github.com/ParBLiSS/FastANI). ARGs and plasmid-types were identified using ABRicate v1.0.1 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate).47 PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/) was used to identify the multi-locus sequence typing (MLST).48 A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) based on core genomes using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy),49 with tree inference executed in FastTree v2.1 (https://www.microbesonline.org/fasttree/),50 and further visualized by ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/).51 Heatmaps were utilized for the visualization of SNPs using TBtools-II v2.210.52 Sankey diagrams were generated using RAWGraphs v2.0 (https://app.rawgraphs.io/), an open-source data visualization platform. BEAST v1.10.4 (https://beast.community/programs) and SpreadD3 v0.9.7rc (https://github.com/phylogeography/SpreaD3) were employed to conduct time-calibrated phylogenetic analysis and phylogeographic inference.53,54

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate and all data were presented as means ± SD. Experiments included three independent biological replicates, each performed in triplicate. Unpaired t-test between two groups, or one-way ANOVA, and log-rank test were performed. Graphpad Prism 10 was used to calculate the P values (∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.0001).
