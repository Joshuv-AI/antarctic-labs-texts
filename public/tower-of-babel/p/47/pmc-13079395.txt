Timing the decline: Cellular circadian rhythms and Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Christopher S. Colwell
Published: 2026

A growing body of work links circadian disruption to aging and neurodegenerative disease, yet a central question has remained unresolved: Do altered daily rhythms merely reflect disease and lifestyle, or do they reveal intrinsic clock properties that shape vulnerability to brain aging? In PNAS, Roh et al. (1) show that circadian timing measured directly in patient-derived skin fibroblasts associates with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology, brain atrophy, cognition, and longitudinal clinical decline. By measuring intrinsic circadian period and its deviation from the canonical 24-hour day in older adults with cognitive complaints, the authors move circadian research beyond behavioral proxies and toward cellular biomarkers. Their findings position intrinsic circadian timing as a potential new dimension of biomarker discovery in neurodegeneration.

Circadian disruption is a consistent and early feature of AD. Patients exhibit fragmented sleep–wake cycles, dampened rest–activity rhythms, and behavioral changes such as sundowning (2–4). Epidemiological studies suggest that circadian disturbance predicts dementia risk and progression (3, 5, 6). Experimental models further implicate the clock in regulating protein homeostasis, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and amyloid-β dynamics. Yet causality has remained unclear. Most human studies rely on sleep or activity measures shaped by environmental and behavioral factors, making it difficult to isolate intrinsic circadian biology.

To disentangle endogenous clock properties from environmental influences, investigators have turned to cell-based assays (Fig. 1). Human fibroblasts exhibit robust, self-sustained molecular oscillations that reflect cell-autonomous clock function and correlate with chronotype and physiological circadian period in vivo (7, 8). While these approaches have revealed disease-associated circadian phenotypes in psychiatric and metabolic disorders, their application to neurodegeneration has been limited. In the present study, the authors combine fibroblast circadian phenotyping with multimodal biomarkers, neuroimaging, cognitive testing, and longitudinal follow-up in 135 participants-one of the most biomarker-rich examinations of intrinsic circadian timing in cognitively impaired older adults.

Circadian rhythms measured in patient-derived dermal fibroblasts provide two complementary biomarkers of brain aging. A longer intrinsic cellular circadian period (clock speed) associates with Alzheimer’s disease-related tau pathology, glial activation, and medial temporal atrophy. In contrast, greater deviation of the cellular circadian period from 24 h (Δ-period; clock mismatch) is associated with age, widespread brain atrophy, and cognitive impairment. Both circadian metrics independently predict faster clinical progression, highlighting cellular measures of circadian timing as a potential biomarker of neurodegeneration.

In PNAS, Roh et al. (1) show that circadian timing measured directly in patient-derived skin fibroblasts associates with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology, brain atrophy, cognition, and longitudinal clinical decline.

A central advance of the study is the demonstration that two separable circadian metrics: Clock speed (intrinsic period length) and clock mismatch (deviation from 24 h) map onto distinct biological pathways. A longer intrinsic cellular period was associated with plasma pTau-217, neurofilament light chain, glial activation, and medial temporal atrophy characteristic of AD. In contrast, greater Δ-period showed broader associations with age, widespread brain atrophy, and impairments across cognitive domains. Both measures independently predicted faster clinical decline, suggesting that circadian biology contributes to neurodegeneration through at least two dimensions.

The link between longer intrinsic period and tau pathology is particularly striking. Experimental studies show that clock gene alterations can lengthen circadian period and disrupt neuronal homeostasis, but direct evidence connecting intrinsic circadian timing to AD biomarkers in humans has been limited. These findings raise the possibility that slower-running cellular clocks increase vulnerability to tau-related pathology or that AD-related processes durably reshape molecular timekeeping. Either possibility strengthens the case for a bidirectional relationship between circadian timing and neurodegeneration.

Similarly, the association between Δ-period and aging aligns with the circadian resonance hypothesis, which posits that biological systems function optimally when intrinsic rhythms align with the 24-hour day (9, 10). Deviation from 24 h reduces longevity and impairs physiology and cognition across species (11, 12). By linking cellular Δ-period to brain aging in humans, the study extends this framework and suggests that temporal misalignment may represent a systemic vulnerability factor.

These findings prompt a reexamination of decades of research on sleep and dementia. Behavioral rhythm disruption may not simply reflect illness; it may signal stable, cell-autonomous differences in circadian timing that influence susceptibility to pathology. At the same time, neurodegenerative processes may further destabilize circadian regulation, creating a feedback loop that accelerates decline. Longitudinal studies assessing cellular circadian timing before overt pathology will be essential to disentangle these possibilities.

If altered cellular circadian timing reflects vulnerability, the implications extend beyond biomarker discovery. Circadian rhythms are uniquely amenable to intervention through light exposure, sleep timing, pharmacological chronotherapy, time-restricted feeding, and exercise (13–16). Light-based interventions can improve sleep and behavioral symptoms in dementia (17, 18), although melanopsin retinal ganglion cells that mediate circadian photoentrainment are reduced in AD (19, 20). Preclinical evidence suggests that alternative strategies, including scheduled feeding and timed exercise, may improve circadian function and neuropathology (14–16). The prospect that circadian interventions could modify disease risk or progression elevates circadian biology from a descriptive framework to a therapeutic target.

By integrating cellular circadian phenotyping with established biomarkers and clinical outcomes, Kim et al. (1) introduce circadian timing as a new dimension of precision medicine for brain aging. The minimally invasive nature of skin biopsy further enhances translational potential. As the global burden of dementia rises, incorporating circadian biology into research and clinical practice may open new opportunities to identify risk, monitor progression, and design targeted interventions. Understanding how the body keeps time may ultimately prove essential to understanding how the brain loses it.
