Geotrichum candidum as an Opportunistic Human Pathogen in Cancer Patients and Benign Conditions
Authors: MARKUS MAULHARDT, ALEXANDER C. ANGLEITNER, REHSAN AKDAS, WILKEN HARMS, PATRICIA SCHARFENBERG, JUDITH BÜNTZEL
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Geotrichum candidum (G. candidum)is a yeast-like saprophytic fungus of low virulence, and common part of the human microbiome (1). It can be detected on the skin, in the sputum or feces of healthy subjects (2). Yet, G. candidum can turn into a rare, tissue invasive opportunistic pathogen which predominantly affects immunocompromised hosts, including patients with cancer or stem cell and solid organ transplant recipients in particular (3-6). G. candidum is the causative agent of local or disseminated disease, referred to as geotrichosis, that may lead to a devastating course of disease in these immunosuppressed individuals (7, 8).

Oral geotrichosis clinically resembles candidiasis and is commonly associated with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection or diabetes mellitus (9-11). G. candidum has been reported to be associated with invasive lung infections in chronic pulmonary disease (2, 12) and is said to cause renal abscesses in leukemia and terminally ill patients with cancer (2, 3). Patients with chemotherapy-induced neutropenia might be particularly susceptible to the development of disseminated G. candidum infection (Still, patients with benign conditions like diabetes mellitus, endocarditis, burn injuries, post-surgery, HIV or autoimmune diseases have been reported to become infected by this fungal strain).

Unfortunately, data on treatment options and risk factors for lethal outcome of invasive G. candidum infection is sparse. However, several smaller case reports indicate that G. candidum is susceptible to a variety of different antifungal compounds, especially amphotericin B. Despite the fact that the incidence of invasive infection and fungemia caused by G. candidum and other Geotrichum species is low, it should be considered in patients with hematological malignancies like acute leukemia and persistent febrile neutropenia, especially under broad-spectrum anti-infective therapy without clinical improvement (2, 3, 14).

Diagnostic tools to detect G. candidum infection or fungemia include culturing the specimens taken from patients, e.g. blood cultures, sputum, tissue or stool samples, on dextrose agar growth medium and characterize the colonial and microscopic morphologic features. Next-generation sequencing makes it possible to detect this fungal strain with certainty (24-26). The exact confirmation of G. candidum infection might allow for adjustment of antifungal treatment and decrease potentially lethal complications like Geotrichum sepsis, length of hospital stay and mortality. However, diagnosis can be complicated by the fact that there is often a co-infection with bacterial, viral or other fungal pathogens that may lead the clinical course in the first place. Additionally, Geotrichum infections often involve sites that are difficult to access for diagnostic sampling (e.g., via bronchoscopy or endoscopy), and blood cultures may remain negative despite clinical evidence of fungemia (27, 28).

Close collaboration of the treating clinicians with microbiologists and fungal experts is crucial. To further elucidate this opportunistic pathogen and its relevance in human disease, we analyzed publicly available data and discuss potential therapeutic strategies for this fungal species. In addition, we report the case of a 56-year-old male patient who underwent allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) for acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and developed fatal G. candidum invasive pneumonia in relapse and eventually died from this rarely reported fungal infection.

Patients and Methods

A systematic online search was conducted in Medline on 28 April 2024 using the following search string: “(“geotrichum”[MeSH Terms] OR “geotrichum”[All Fields]) AND ((casereports[Filter]))”. Settings were set to “case reports”, as we intended to extract individual data on the course of the disease as well as outcome parameters and treatment of choice. Our aim was to use these cases in addition to our institution’s case to build a retrospective historic cohort for further analysis. Case reports were retrieved and supplemented by further scientific literature on G. candidum. We eventually selected data on 22 cases published from 1971 through 2022 for final analysis according to the PRISMA guidelines (Figure 1) (29). All case reports included are listed in Table I. A case of G. candidum mycosis from our academic center was added to the data pool, enabling us to include a total of 23 cases of G. candidum infection to further analysis. The data were collected and organized using Microsoft Excel (version 2016; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). To summarize the demographic and clinical variables, descriptive statistical analyses were applied. Leukopenia was defined as <1,000 leukocytes/µl. For all statistical computations, GraphPad Prism version 9.3.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) was employed. Fisher’s exact test was used and a two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics. Localized or disseminated infection with G. candidum is a rare event. Patients susceptible to infection are either immunosuppressed (hematological neoplasm, concurrent infectious diseases, organ transplant) or showed risk factors for being immunocompromised (metabolic disorder, major trauma, post-surgery;Table I and Table II). G. candidum mycosis was found across all age groups, the median age at infection was 42 years (range=4-80 years) and sex distribution was even (Table I and Table II). Among the 23 clinical cases reviewed, eleven patients (47.8%) were reported to suffer from malignant disease as underlying condition, with hematological disorders representing the bulk of cancer cases (8/11; 72.7%; Table II), while 12/23 patients (52.2%) had another underlying disease. Sites of infection or detection of G. candidum specimens included blood (9), respiratory tract (7), skin (4) and intestinal tissue (3). Even the cultured vitreous fluid of one patient with indolent endophthalmitis after cataract surgery grew G. candidum. Seven patients showed more than one site of infection (Table I).

Antifungal treatment and reported outcomes. Overall, 10/23 (43.5%) of patients with documented G. candidum infection died (Table III). Geotrichosis from G. candidum is frequently deadly for patients with cancer. However, it is not always clear whether the underlying condition or the fungal infection itself was the primary cause of death. Leukopenia was observed in 7/15 patients (Table III). The following antifungal drugs were administered: amphotericin B/liposomal amphotericin B (13/20, 65.0%), micafungin, itraconazole, ketoconazole, voriconazole, (oral) nystatin plus caspofungin, isavuconazonium and anidulafungin in the case from our center. Cancer itself as well as hematological malignancies are risk factors for fatal outcome [patients with cancer (11 cases) vs. others (12 cases) p<0.01, blood cancer (8 cases) vs. others (15 cases) p<0.05)].

Leukopenia, which was significantly more common in patients with cancer (p<0.01), probably contributes to higher mortality rates. Differences in the choice of drugs for treatment (e.g., amphotericin B/liposomal amphotericin B vs. others) or whether monotherapy or sequential therapy was administered does not seem to have an impact on patients’ prognosis in our explorative retrospective analysis.

Case report from our institution. A 56-year old male patient (case 23, Table IV) was diagnosed with AML with KMT2A partial tandem duplication and two FLT-3 mutations in our institution in 2022. Bone marrow biopsy showed 70.0% blast infiltration. The subject underwent induction chemotherapy with daunorubicin/cytarabine and the oral protein kinase inhibitor midostaurin (Table IV). Consolidation therapy included high-dose cytarabine and again midostaurin. Conditioning with the FBM-ATG protocol was followed by allo-SCT from a matched unrelated donor as consolidation in late 2022. The post-transplant period was uneventful, apart from an Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) reactivation that responded well to rituximab treatment. Complete molecular remission was achieved, no signs of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) detected and midostaurin maintenance commenced. Unfortunately, molecular AML relapse eventually occurred 13 months post-allo-SCT. Our patient was started on the small molecule gilteritinib and underwent three donor lymphocyte infusions (DLI; Table IV). During that time, he developed recurrent fever, a cough and recurrent dyspnea.

Repetitive blood cultures turned out sterile. A repeat computed tomography (CT) scan revealed persistent bipulmonary consolidations in the presence of suspected fungal pneumonia (Figure 2). There were also signs of endobronchial spread of the pathogen in a tree-in-bud pattern. Antigen tests for Candida and Aspergillus in the bloodstream were negative. We performed a bronchoscopy to secure material for microbial work-up from bronchoalveolar lavage. Enterococcus faecium and a fungus that was subsequently identified as G. candidum, as well as Candida glabrata grew in agar growth medium. These strains were deemed responsible for the morphologic aspect of fungal pneumonia in the CT scans. Apart from different broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem, vancomycin, ceftazidime, moxifloxacin, linezolid), our patient was given sequential antifungal treatment, even before the detection of G. candidum in the respiratory tract. Intravenous antifungal treatment included caspofungin [70 mg/kg body weight (BW)], liposomal amphotericin B (initially 1 mg/kg BW at the time of G. candidum detection, continued with 3 mg/kg BW), isavuconazonium (200 mg daily) and anidulafungin (100 mg daily). He was put on a ventilator and died eight days later from pneumonic, fatal G. candidum sepsis. A CT scan three days prior to his death still showed persistent pneumonia with fungoid morphology.

Being the recipient of an allo-SCT and treated for relapsed acute leukemia, the reported patient was particularly vulnerable to fungal infections and succumbed to pneumonic sepsis within two months after detection of Geotrichum candidum. Discussion

G. candidum mycosis is an uncommon infection. The pathogenicity of this fungus in humans appears limited, with only 22 cases suitable for further analysis in the available medical literature covering a time span of 50 years. In patients with intact immunity, G. candidum in the blood is rapidly cleared by neutrophilic phagocytosis and thus geotrichosis is not a fatal disease (2). Although deemed a yeast of low virulence, G. candidum can turn into a deadly germ for immunocompromised hosts. Leukopenia probably aggravates the course of disease in these patients. Patients with leukemia and with chemotherapy-induced neutropenia or those who underwent allo-SCT as well as patients on immunosuppressive agents seem to be particularly vulnerable to tissue-invasive fungal infections of opportunistic fungi like G. candidum (13, 14). Especially patients with hematological malignancies show worse survival. Overall, this outcome is probably due to higher rates of leukopenia (leukopenia+blood cancer 6/8 cases) in this subgroup. Fitting into this pattern, the relapsed AML patient from our center was in a state of deeply impaired cellular immunity when he developed fatal G. candidum pneumonia that did not respond to any of the administered antifungal drugs. While (liposomal) amphotericin B is a common treatment choice (25), the particular antifungal drug chosen for an individual patient did not seem to have a specific impact on outcome in our explorative analysis. Antifungal resistance is a concerning development. Besides, the Geotrichum genus remains an uncommon but under-recognized cause of opportunistic infection in patients with severe tissue damage like burn injuries (17).

Infection rates caused by fungal pathogens like G. candidum are likely on the rise, partly due to the widening of immunosuppressive therapy for several indications, partly as adverse events of antineoplastic therapy for an increasing number of patients with cancer (2). In line with that, commonly used corticosteroids and metabolic disorders likely contribute to the risk of fungal infections. Our case report, even though with fatal outcome, illustrates the importance of thorough diagnostic measurements and microbial work-up for febrile patients with impaired host defense.

Our analysis provides a structured and systematic approach to an elusive infectious complication of acute or chronic immunosuppression. Still, this retrospective study has several limitations. Despite being a multi-center study, an infection with G. candidum is an unusual rare event. This is reflected by our small sample size which restricts the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, historical and publication bias should be considered when interpreting the presented data as unusual or fatal cases are more likely to be published and antifungal treatment has evolved over time. We suspect that G. candidum is an underreported opportunistic fungus of low virulence in immunocompetent hosts, but potentially life-threatening in immunocompromised individuals. Additionally, heterogeneity among the included cases represents another limitation: patients differed considerably regarding underlying diseases, immune status, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic strategies. We report an explorative analysis with different confounding factors (e.g., age, ICU admission, unadjusted p-value, subset overlaps). Furthermore, detailed clinical and microbiological data were missing in some reports, limiting the ability to draw robust conclusions regarding optimal antifungal treatment and risk factors for mortality. Finally, given the retrospective nature of this analysis, causal relationships between G. candidum infection, underlying comorbidities, and treatment outcomes cannot be definitively established.

Despite these limitations, our study presents the first retrospective cohort of patients affected by G. candidum infection and highlights the potentially devastating impact of this opportunistic pathogen in immunocompromised patients with cancer. The included cases underwent heterogeneous therapeutic approaches, which underlines the need for more systematic reporting and research.

Conclusion

Our findings further suggest that G. candidum is likely an underreported opportunistic fungus that may cause severe, life-threatening disease, particularly in individuals with impaired immunity or in burn injuries (17). Therefore, clinicians should remain vigilant and consider geotrichosis, possibly caused by G. candidum, as a relevant differential diagnosis in high-risk populations. Early detection and targeted antifungal therapy could be crucial and potentially life-saving in these cases.
