Emotional burden and family functioning among caregivers of individuals with eating disorders
Authors: Rosaria Di Lorenzo, Martina Rovatti, Carolina Bottone, Ilaria Rubini, Roberta Covezzi, Sergio Rovesti, Paola Ferri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe involvement of a caregiver is fundamental in the process of caring for a person with eating disorders (ED). The aim of this study is to evaluate the functioning of family unit and the emotional burden of caregivers of individuals with ED treated at an outpatient service.Materials and methodsWe contacted by telephone the caregivers of individuals in care at ED Centre of the AUSL-Modena and selected a sample of 50 caregivers of 42 individuals with ED, who provided their informed consent. The following scales were administered to caregivers: caregiver burden inventory (CBI), Beck’s depression inventory (BDI), family assessment device (FAD), depression, anxiety and stress-scale (DASS-21). The following scales were administered to the care recipients: global assessment of functioning and clinical global impression severity scale. Demographic variables relating to the individuals with ED and their caregivers were collected: sex, age, employment situation, marital status, number of family members, living condition, family role. Clinical variables of care recipients were collected: body mass index, ED diagnosis, duration of ED and treatment and care at ED centre, medical complications, psychiatric comorbidities, substance use. The data was statistically analyzed.ResultsAll caregivers were the parents of individuals with ED, in particular the mother (76%), and were employed. Caregivers reported a mild to moderate emotional burden in CBI and mild to severe depressive symptoms in BDI in 62% of cases. Family functioning reported by FAD scale was slightly altered in the areas of "communication", "roles" and "affective involvement". Most care recipients were females (98%), suffering from anorexia nervosa (85.6%) with an average age of 18.54 ± 4.74. At multiple linear regression, two statistically significant associations were underscored with CBI score (dependent variable): the age of individuals in a negative way and the psychiatric comorbidities of the individuals with ED in a positive way.ConclusionsThe parents of sons with ED represented their caregivers, who suffered from a mild emotional burden and depressive symptoms and lived in altered family functioning, especially in communications. Ensuring psychological support for the caregiver may be useful for improving both caring and family relationships.

Introduction

An eating disorder (ED) is defined as an altered eating behaviour associated with excessive concern about body weight, which subsequently compromises both physical health and psychosocial functioning. These psychiatric disorders often present high rates of morbidity and mortality [1–4].

ED occurs in both sexes and can affect any age group. However, it is more common among adolescents (59% are between 13 and 25 years of age, 6% are younger than 12 years of age) and young women (90% of patients) [5].

Global eating disorder prevalence increased from 3.5% to 7.8% between 2000 and 2018 [6] and 22% of children and adolescents worldwide show disordered eating [7] According to a recent report, one-year prevalence of anorexia nervosa is 0.16% for females and 0.09% for males whereas one-year prevalence of bulimia nervosa is 0.32% for females and 0.05% for males [6].

In Italy, approximately 8,000 individuals are being treated in centres dedicated to ED treatment and care. Of these, approximately 3,000 have been treated for more than five years. It is estimated that one in eight young people may have at least one eating disorder by the age of 20. The most common eating disorders include anorexia nervosa (42.3%), bulimia nervosa (18.2%) and binge eating (14.6%) [8].

The aetiology of eating disorders is heterogeneous and involves a variety of factors: biological, such as genetics and neurobiology, and psychological and social factors. Twin studies have shown that the likelihood of developing an eating disorder if one twin is affected is 50%. Psychological factors, such as perfectionism, obsessive-compulsiveness, and neuroticism, also play a role. Developmental factors, such as childhood sexual abuse, and sociocultural factors, such as media exposure and the value of a slim body for women in Western culture, also contribute to the aetiology of eating disorders [9].

The approach to the patient with ED is multidisciplinary, based on psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy and nutritional counselling. It is of utmost importance to start treatment as early as possible to minimize the risk of medical complications and psychiatric comorbidities [9]. The management of eating disorders requires the involvement of a multidisciplinary team, which usually includes a psychotherapist, the primary care physician, a dietitian, a psychiatrist, one or more nurses, an exercise therapist, an occupational therapist and a social worker or family therapist. European guidelines often recommend family-based therapy and individual trans-diagnostic cognitive-behavioural psychotherapy (CBT-E), which is usually administered in 20 sessions for bulimia nervosa and 40 sessions for anorexia nervosa. In addition, treatment incorporates both individual and family psychoeducation [10–12].

The significance of family relationships, specifically family functioning, has been recognized and investigated in eating disorders. In more recent years, ED‐based family therapy has become manualised and includes similar forms of evidence‐based interventions: family‐based treatment [13], the Maudsley model of family therapy [14] and systemic family therapy [15]. Multi‐family therapy (MFT) is an umbrella term encapsulating a variety of approaches bringing together several families affected by the same concern [16]. MFT is grounded in group therapy, based on the idea that families learn from one another in supporting their loved one [17]. MFT is an effective treatment for anorexia nervosa and leads to improvements in individual, as well as some caregiver and family factors [18] although not all studies agree on the improvement of family functioning, caregiving, nor patient and parental anxiety [19].

As with most complex disorders, patients with ED need to be accompanied and supported by a caregiver, who in most cases coincides with the patient's parents. Although it is essential to involve family members, prolonged assistance can compromise the psychological well-being of the caregiver, denoting a condition known as caregiver burden (CB), which in turn risks worsening the care recipient's conditions [20].

Eating disorder symptoms are pervasive in the family, and the nature of the disorder affects interpersonal relationships. In many cases, the role of caregiver is performed by the family members of the recipient of care and in cases of eating disorders, parents often take on this responsibility [21, 22]. In the early stages of the disease, patients often lack insight, and it is therefore essential that caregivers are able to recognize the early signs and facilitate access to care. The perception of the role of the caregiver and its management are influenced by several factors, including the disease itself, social attitudes towards the disease and factors related to care such as costs and interactions with services. It is therefore essential to provide support to caregivers to reduce the negative effects of high perceived burden and low care effectiveness, which are common and related to depression and anxiety [20, 23, 24].

The caregiver's practical and emotional reactions to the illness can hinder recovery if there are discrepancies and strong emotions expressed within the family unit. Caregiver distress and inadequate coping skills can increase caregiver distress, leading to a vicious cycle. A common but ineffective coping mechanism may be the tendency to overprotect the ill family member, adapting to the illness as if it were a normal condition [22, 23]. In these cases, family members may collude with the attitudes typical of the disease, organizing the family unit around the disease, which can lead to divisions between family members [25, 26]. Empirically-supported interpersonal models of ED maintenance, such as the cognitive-cognitive interpersonal ED maintenance model, indicate that family members who encounter negative caretaking effects are more prone to engage in accommodation of their loved one’s ED behaviours in efforts to reduce family conflict and stress, which, in turn, maintain core ED symptoms and further perpetuate difficulties in family functioning [27].

The risk for the caregiver is that they may be overwhelmed by emotions, which can negatively influence the course of the disease through overprotective and controlling behaviours. In addition, prolonged contact with the care recipient compromises the caregiver's ability to cope with the ED. Implementing interventions designed to meet the needs of caregivers and improve their coping skills has been shown to produce benefits in terms of reducing the burden and distress experienced by caregivers.

The New Maudsley Collaborative Care Approach was developed to assist families of patients with chronic illnesses [28]. This model provides training to caregivers in skills such as positive communication through the use of motivational interviewing, nutritional support, and management of other challenging behaviours. Additionally, caregivers can be trained to perform motivational interviewing in a self-management format through the use of DVDs and books that describe the various strategies [25, 29, 30]. Caregiver accommodation behaviour is more pronounced during the early stages of the disease and is associated with both patient and caregiver distress. The New Maudsley Collaborative Care model has been shown to reduce both accommodation and expressed emotion and improve patient outcomes [28, 31, 32]. The care provided to caregivers must take into account the stage of the disease and the presence of interpersonal behaviours (accommodation, expressed emotion, family division). Therefore, the implementation of psycho-educational interventions to support the caregiver can improve the caregiver's ability to adapt and, consequently, facilitate the caregiving process [25]. The support provided to the caregiver is fundamental in the care process, since a high perceived burden and poor effectiveness of care in ED are common and burdened by depression and anxiety. The caregiver's distress can aggravate the ED course, leading to a vicious circle: overprotection of the sick family member and ineffective adaptation to the disorder with the risk of worsening ED medical and psychological consequences [25].

Purpose of the study

A comprehensive evaluation of anxiety and depression dimensions, emotional burden, and family functioning in caregivers of individuals with ED in relation to caregiver and care recipient variables.

Materials and methods

Setting and design of the study

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the ED Centre of the AUSL-Modena to assess the emotional burden experienced by caregivers of individuals with eating disorders treated at the same centre and the functioning of their families.

The sample of caregivers and individuals with ED

We selected all caregivers of individuals with an eating disorder who were older than 12 years and were treated at the ED Centre of AUSL-Modena during the study period ranged between 1-5-2023 and 1-10-2023, who agreed to participate in the study, providing their informed consent. In case of multiple caregiving, all caregivers were included in the analysis. Concomitantly, we selected the care recipients of our caregivers, requiring their informed consent if over 18 years old, All subjects with ED treated by ED Centre from 2017 to 2023 and cared for by the caregivers of our sample, who agreed to participate in the study by providing their informed consent, were included in the study, without exceptions regarding the type of ED, sex and age. If a care recipient refused to participate in the study, his/her caregiver was also excluded. Therefore, for inclusion in this study, informed consent from both the caregiver and the recipient was required.

Variables of caregivers and care recipients

The scales administered to caregivers

Four scales, translated and validated in Italian, were administered to caregivers who agreed to participate in the study: caregiver burden inventory (CBI), Beck's depression inventory (BDI), family assessment device (FAD), and depression, anxiety and stress scale (DASS-21).

The caregiver burden inventory [33] is a multidimensional questionnaire designed to assess caregiver burden across five distinct domains: time dependence (items 1–5), developmental (items 6–10), physical (items 11–14), social (items 15–19), and emotional (items 20–24) burden. The questionnaire comprises 24 items, each of which permits a response on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (indicating that the item is "not at all relevant") to 4 (indicating that the item is "very relevant"). The total score on the questionnaire ranges from 0 to 96, with a cut-off score of ≥ 21. The questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, moderate inter-scale correlations, and high factorial loadings, rendering it a practical instrument for evaluating caregiver burden. The utilization of the Caregiver Burden Inventory to assess caregiver burden in patients with eating disorders has been demonstrated to facilitate the development of targeted monitoring strategies for caregivers, thereby enabling the implementation of evidence-based intervention programs [20].

The Beck's Depression Inventory [34] is a psychometric questionnaire used to assess the severity of depression. This 21-item questionnaire is designed to assess the cognitive, affective, somatic, and vegetative symptoms of depression, as outlined by Beck [34]. The BDI may be completed by self-administration, or orally, reporting feelings during the previous week, excluding the day of administration. Each response is assigned a score from 0 to 3, and the total score for each section is calculated by adding the scores for the individual responses. A clinically significant cut-off point is 15/16. Scores exceeding this threshold indicate the presence of depressive symptoms. When examining the scores in greater detail, depressive symptoms are unlikely to be present if the score is less than 10, mild if it falls between 10 and 19, moderate if it is between 20 and 29, and severe if the score is equal to or greater than 30 [35]. In 2014, a study conducted in patients diagnosed with binge eating and obesity showed the efficacy of the BDI as a diagnostic instrument for mood disorders and major depression in ED disorders [36].

The Family Assessment Device rating scale [37] was developed for the purpose of measuring perceived family functioning. It has been translated and validated in Italian [38]. The FAD was designed to assess the six dimensions of the McMaster model of family functioning as the overall functioning of a family, through 60 items [39, 40]. The dimensions analyzed are the following: Problem Solving (FAD-PS), Communication (FAD-C), Roles (FAD-R), Emotional Resonance (FAD-ER), Affective Involvement (FAD-AI), Behaviour Control (FAD-BC), and General Functioning (FAD-GF). Additionally, the scale provides cut-off scores that allow family members to be classified as satisfied with functioning in a domain or dissatisfied. In the latter case, scores above the cut-off score indicate an inadequate family model. The questionnaire should be completed by scoring each of the 60 proposed items on a Likert scale of 1 (very true) to 4 (completely false). The experimenter should then sum the scores obtained for the various domains, ensuring that the number (1 = 4, 2 = 3, 3 = 2, 4 = 1) in the items marked with an asterisk is converted correctly. Once the sum of the different domain scores has been obtained, the weighted number (with a minimum value of 1 and a maximum value of 4) is calculated so that it can be compared with the cut-off value [41].

The DASS-21 [42] was developed for the purpose of measuring symptoms of depression (DASS-D), anxiety (DASS-A), and stress (DASS-S). It is employed for the assessment of patients' response to treatment. Each of the three domains contains seven items, which are rated on a Likert scale from 0 to 3. The ratings are as follows: 0 = does not fit me at all; 1 = fits me sometimes; 2 = fits me a good part of the time; 3 = fits me most of the time [43]. The results are interpreted as follows: The DASS-A scale (> 19 = very severe anxiety; 19–15 = severe anxiety; 14–10 = moderate anxiety; 9–8 = mild anxiety; 7–0 = normal) and the DASS-D scale (> 27 = very severe depression; 27–21 = severe depression; 20–14 = moderate depression; 13–10 = mild depression; 9–0 = normal), DASS-S (> 33 = very severe stress; 33–26 = severe stress; 25–19 = moderate stress; 18–15 = mild stress; 14–0 = normal) [44]. A study, which was conducted in 2021 to investigate the prevalence of anxiety and depression symptoms in a sample of eating disorder patients compared with a group of healthy patients during the lockdown period in Italy, showed that patients with eating disorders exhibited higher scores than controls at the DASS-21 [45]. A validation study of the DASS-21 scale translated into Italian by Bottesi and colleagues was conducted in clinical and nonclinical samples [46].

The scales administered to individuals with ED

In order to correlate the severity of ED suffered by the care recipients, two scale were administered to individuals with an eating disorder: the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) and the Clinical Global Impression Severity Scale (CGI-S).

The Global Assessment of Functioning is a psychometric instrument designed to assess the severity of psychiatric illness. It has been recognised globally and is included in the fourth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV-TR) [47]. This rating scale comprehensively measures patients' psychological, social and occupational functioning [48]. The Global Assessment of Functioning scores range from 1 to 100, where 100 corresponds to the absence of symptoms and 1 to a severely impaired state. The various levels are divided into 10-point intervals and briefly described with the help of concrete examples [49].

The Clinical Global Impression (CGI) scale [50] provides an overall assessment of the patient's global functioning in the period before and after the administration of a drug. In particular, the CGI-Severity (CGI-S) assesses the severity of the pathology at the time the aforementioned scale is administered by the physician. It is based on a seven-point scale from 1, which corresponds to a non-pathological condition, to 7, which corresponds to the most severely ill patients: 1 is assigned to a non-pathological condition, 2 to a borderline mental illness, 3 to the mildly ill, 4 to the moderately ill, 5 to the extremely ill, and 6 to the severely ill.

Statistical analysis and ethical considerations

Means and standard deviations (m ± SD) were calculated for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Correlations between the scale scores were performed with Spearman's correlation test. The homogeneity of the scales was assessed with Cronbach's alpha. We applied the multiple linear regression (stepwise forward and backward model) between the CBI scale score, as dependent variable, and the selected variables, as independent variables. The probability significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled individuals (caregivers and individuals treated for ED). Additionally, the enrolled subject's general practitioner or free choice paediatrician was informed about the proposed study. Patient data were obtained from the informatics system of ED Centre, while caregiver data were requested from each caregiver when he/she completed the questionnaires.

The data were collected anonymously, with each selected patient assigned a progressive numerical code. Access to the study data was granted to the researchers, who were bound by an obligation of confidentiality and treatment of the data in accordance with the current Law.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess the caregiver burden, anxious and depressive symptoms, and family functioning of caregivers of individuals with ED in care at a psychiatric service.

The majority of individuals treated at ED Centre were female, with an average age of 18.54 ± 4.74 years, in line with the findings of previous studies [8, 51], confirming the greater vulnerability of the female sex in adolescence to this disorder [6, 7]. As highlighted by recent studies, sex steroids and puberty may represent risk factors for development and maintenance of EDs in females as well as neural circuitry involved in reward [52]. The most prevalent diagnosis among individuals with ED was anorexia nervosa, followed by bulimia nervosa, in line with the literature which underscores an early developmental of anorexia in especially in the Western country [5, 7, 8]. Most of our individuals with ED lived in their parental family, did not work nor were students and were single, suggesting dependence on their parental family, probably due to their young age or due to reduced autonomy capacity because of illness [51]. Among individuals with ED, only few people had psychiatric comorbidities, such as panic attacks, self-harm, depression, emotionally unstable personality disorder, generalized anxiety syndrome, and mixed anxiety-depressive syndrome, which overlapped with those identified by a previous study [9]. Although this observation differs from literature studies, which highlight that ED patients suffer from several mental health problems, the comorbid disorders of ED patients in our sample are similar to those frequently reported such as depression and anxiety [53].

The majority of caregivers in our sample was represented by the mother of the care recipient, who had an average age of 49.22 ± 6.74 years, was employed and married, in accordance with a previous study [25]. This result is consistent with the young age of the care recipients and confirms their dependence on the family due to limited autonomy [51]. We reported a mild to moderate caregiver burden according to the CBI scores, which highlight the prevalence of two components of the burden, represented by “time dependence” and “developmental” burden, suggesting that caregiver time restrictions hinder personal growth or life goals due to caregiving responsibilities.

The level of caregiver burden reported by us is lower than that reported in the literature regarding the burden of caregivers of patients with other disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease (47.54 ± 17.61) [54], or undergoing palliative care (52.20 ± 16.10) [55], investigated by the same scale. This result suggests a different burden due to less extensive care in ED compared to other serious and terminal medical conditions, which can lead to a heavier emotional and physical burden. In our sample of caregivers, emotional stress was the least represented component of caregiver burden. This could be interpreted as a greater emotional involvement associated with a positive expectation of improvement on the part of caregivers, probably because they were mostly parents of individuals with ED. Indeed, most of our caregivers were employed, indicating that their caregiving activity was compatible with a stable employment, which, in turn and paradoxically, may reduce caregiver stress by keeping them out of the family environment [56].

The analysis of depressive symptoms in the caregivers of our sample by means of the BDI, shows a mild-moderate level of depression. In particular, about half of the sample of caregivers showed mild-moderate symptoms of depression whereas only 2% exhibited severe symptoms. These results are in line with the average score of the BDI observed in a previous study on caregivers of individuals with ED [57].

In the DASS-21, the percentage of caregivers with mild to moderate depression was 32%, and 30% with severe and very severe depressive symptoms, reporting a higher number of caregivers suffering from depression in the sample in comparison with the BDI. However, both scales indicated a prevalence of depression in over 50% of caregivers. It is noteworthy that depressive symptoms are prevalent in our caregiver sample, probably because it was mainly composed of women, who have a greater vulnerability to depression associated with female sex, as documented in the literature [58].

The CBI and the scales used to detect depression, BDI and DASS-21, suggest a mild-to-moderate magnitude for both burden and anxious-depressive symptoms in caregivers of individuals with ED. Moreover, all these scales were correlated between them, suggesting that burden and anxious-depressive symptoms can influence each other in caregivers. Furthermore, statistically significant correlations were found between the BDI and the DASS-21, suggesting that depressive symptoms in the caregiver may also be associated with anxiety and stress.

Finally, the FAD scale, which assesses family functioning in several areas, allows us to classify the degree of perceived satisfaction or dissatisfaction in seven domains, including problem solving, communication, roles, emotional resonance, affective involvement, behavioural control, and general functioning. The FAD scale scores obtained indicate that the most altered areas in our sample with scores above the cut-off were those of "communication", "roles" and "affective involvement", whereas the "problem solving" domain obtained a score that indicated borderline dysfunctional and the macro-areas of "emotional resonance", "behaviour control" and "general functioning" were not compromised. This result confirms that the prevalent alteration within the family of a subject with ED is the communication between and among the members, which has been historically highlighted by the family therapy school [59, 60].

The results of our analyses indicate statistically significant correlations between the CBI and FAD, in particular FAD-Roles, FAD-Behaviour Control and FAD-Affective Involvement, suggesting that, in the presence of a mild or moderate burden in caregiver, the macro-areas of ‘roles’, ‘behaviour control’ and affective involvement at the family level are concomitantly altered. We highlighted a statistically significant association between BDI and the FAD-Communication, FAD-Roles and FAD-Affective Involvement scales, suggesting a link between depressive symptoms and alterations in family functioning with regard to 'communication', 'roles' and 'affective involvement', Our findings suggest that caregiver burden and depressive and anxious symptoms are strictly related to alterations in family functioning, mutually reinforcing in the case of an individual with ED. A final correlation was identified between the DASS-21 scale and the FAD-Roles. This suggests that there is a correlation between caregiver anxiety and depression and family conflicts related to 'roles', which are probably unclear and ambiguous in case of a subject with ED. Our results are in line with another study [61], which underscored that more severe psychosocial functioning in the FAD Roles and anxiety symptoms among family of individuals with EDs were linked with greater tolerance of unacceptable behaviours and tendency to ignore the negative consequences of a patient’s eating disorder symptoms, and maintaining inappropriate adaptive family member roles.

A statistically significant correlation was observed between the GAF scale, the CBI and the BDI, suggesting a relationship between an alteration in the global functioning of a subject with ED and an increase in burden and depressive symptoms in their caregiver. However, the GAF scale, which measures the subject's psychological, social and occupational functioning, and the CGI-S, which assesses the severity of psychopathology, were not significantly correlated in our sample. The mean score on the GAF indicated minimal or no symptoms and good functioning in all areas examined. In contrast, the mean score on the CGI-S scale indicated a moderate degree of illness. This discrepancy can be interpreted in light of the clinical characteristics of individuals with ED, who, despite the severity of the disorder, are still stenotic and performing individuals [62]. It is important to note that, in our sample, the CGI-S scale is inversely correlated with the BMI value in a statistically significant manner. This association underscores the severity of our sample of individuals with ED, in accordance with the values of the CGI-S scale.

Our multiple linear regression model highlights that two variables are statistically significantly associated with the CBI scale total score: the age of the subject with ED in a negative way and psychiatric comorbidities in a positive way, suggesting that as the age of the subject with ED increases, the burden of the caregiver decreases, whereas the presence of a psychiatric comorbidity in the subject with ED may increase the caregiver burden. This result underscores a direct relationship between caregiver burden and responsibility for a minor as well as between caregiver burden and complex clinical situations due to psychiatric comorbidities.

In light of the aforementioned results, it can be stated that the caregivers of individuals with ED, despite presenting a mild to moderate emotional burden, suffer from mild to severe depressive symptoms in more than half of cases and live in a family with impaired functioning, albeit slight, in some areas, such as in communication.

Indeed, as confirmed by the literature [20], the emotional and social burden of the caregiver is linked to role conflicts between caregiver and other family members. Consequently, a mild or moderate caregiver burden can affect family functioning, further compromising the health of the person with ED [25]. ED symptoms have a pervasive impact on family and interpersonal relationships since this disorder is intertwined with these relationships. This highlights the importance of ensuring adequate psychological support to caregivers, which can facilitate the care process for individuals with ED and improve family relationships. Authors suggest that multi-family therapy for eating disorders (MFT) across the lifespan can support and promote broader family and caregiver functioning [63].

Limitations and advantages of the study

The first limitation of the study is constituted by the limited sample size, which was secondary to the difficulty in recruiting caregivers willing to participate in this study. In fact, the recruitment phase of the study was the most problematic, both for the choice of the most suitable individuals, which took place through the help of the lists of caregivers provided by the AUSL-Modena ED Centre, and, subsequently, for the difficulty in contacting caregivers by telephone. A total of 50 caregivers agreed to participate in the study, which represents a sufficient sample size for the success of the study, although not large enough to obtain reliable results on a large scale.

Furthermore, the research setting, which is limited to the catchment area of ​​the AUSL-Modena ED Centre, represents another element that limits the generalization of the results to other realities in the national territory. Another limitation is represented by the cross-sectional design of the study, which does not allow inferences of cause and effect, but only highlights relevant and significant associations. The study's strengths include a considerable time span, as individuals in treatment at the AUSL-Modena ED Centre were recruited from 2017 to 2023. Furthermore, the high internal reliability of the scales employed, all of which have been validated in Italian, is evidenced by previous studies on the CBI [20], the BDI [35], the FAD [38] and the DASS-21 [46].

Conclusions

In conclusion, it can be stated that the majority of caregivers of individuals with ED are mothers, and that the emotional burden of the caregiver is associated with anxious-depressive symptoms, both of mild-moderate degree. The correlation between the CBI, BDI, DASS-21 and FAD scales, highlighted by our study, allows us to conclude that the mild-moderate emotional burden and the anxious-depressive symptoms of the caregiver of a subject with ED are associated with an imbalance of roles within the family environment, characterized by alterations in communication and strong emotional involvement among family members. The burden of caregivers of individuals with ED was lower than that assessed in caregivers of individuals with other chronic and terminal diseases using the same CBI scale. However, the burden in caregivers of individuals with ED, although mild-moderate, is associated with a high frequency of anxious and depressive disorders and altered family functioning. Finally, our results indicated that despite the severity of ED, as evidenced by the CGI-S score and the inverse correlation with BMI of these individuals, their global functioning was relatively preserved, suggesting an insidious course of these disorders.

In conclusion, the rating scales employed in this study have the potential to be used in a multitude of clinical settings to identify areas of functional impairment between the affected subject and his/her caregiver at an early stage. This would allow us the implementation of intervention before the emotional distress and depressive symptoms of the caregiver reach a critical point, which could further impair family functioning. This will improve family functioning and provide care to the person with ED over time, counteracting the often chronic and highly disabling course of this disorder.
