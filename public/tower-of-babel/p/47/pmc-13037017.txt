Systemic lupus erythematosus mimicking Japanese spotted fever due to Rickettsia japonica detection in an eschar: a case report
Authors: Kento Izuta, Masashi Nishikubo, Ryohei Nomoto, Shuji Sumitomo, Yoshinori Matsuoka, Ryutaro Seo, Hiroaki Nishioka, Koichi Ariyoshi, Eiichiro Sando
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) is a subset of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) characterized by neurological and psychiatric manifestations. NPSLE diagnosis requires exclusion of alternative etiologies, including infection. Rickettsial diseases exhibit features similar to those of SLE, including fever, rash, and cytopenia. In several reported cases, patients initially suspected of having rickettsial diseases were ultimately diagnosed with SLE. Recently, rickettsial DNA detection in eschars and/or blood using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has been performed to diagnose rickettsial diseases.Case presentationHerein, we report a case of NPSLE complicated by a concurrent localized Rickettsia japonica infection. The patient was a 43-year-old man residing in a Japanese spotted fever (JSF)-endemic region. He was transferred to our hospital because of respiratory failure and consciousness disturbance. Fever, cytopenia, and elevated liver enzyme levels were observed. Physical examination revealed eschars on the right thigh. The differential diagnoses were JSF and SLE. Treatment with minocycline and glucocorticoids improved the patient’s clinical status. Subsequently, R. japonica DNA was detected in eschar samples; however, convalescent antibody titers against R. japonica remained negative. The patient’s symptoms recurred after discontinuation of glucocorticoid therapy, accompanied by elevated titers of SLE-associated antibodies.ConclusionsThis case highlights a rare instance of localized rickettsial infection, which should be considered in the differential diagnosis of febrile diseases in rickettsia-endemic areas, particularly in the context of PCR-based diagnostic approaches for rickettsial diseases.

Background

Neuropsychiatric systemic lupus erythematosus (NPSLE) is a subset of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) characterized by neurological and psychiatric symptoms [1]. SLE is an autoimmune disease that can affect multiple organ systems and is diagnosed based on characteristic clinical features (e.g., fever and mucocutaneous, serosal, and musculoskeletal symptoms) and laboratory evidence (e.g., hematological/renal disorders and SLE-specific antibodies) [2]. Antinuclear antibody (ANA) positivity is identified in almost all SLE cases [2].

NPSLE is diagnosed when neuropsychiatric manifestations in a patient with SLE are attributed to the disease itself after excluding other causes, such as infections, metabolic disturbances, and drug effects. NPSLE encompasses a spectrum of manifestations, ranging from mild cognitive impairment and mood disorders to severe, potentially fatal complications (e.g., seizures, psychosis, and cerebrovascular diseases) [3] that necessitate prompt high-dose glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive treatments [4, 5]. Additionally, pleural involvement in severe SLE can cause respiratory failure, typically presenting as pleuritic chest pain with exudative pleural effusion.

Japanese spotted fever (JSF) is a tick-borne disease first described in 1984 in Tokushima Prefecture, Japan [6]. JSF, caused by Rickettsia japonica, typically presents with fever, cytopenia, and a characteristic rash that spreads from the limbs to the trunk, including the palms and soles. Sites of tick bites often develop black eschars [7]. Severe complications of JSF include multiorgan dysfunction, occasionally accompanied by respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation [8–11]. Early tetracycline administration shortens hospitalization, lowers mortality, and prevents severe outcomes [12]. The diagnostic gold standard for rickettsial diseases, including JSF, is serological confirmation by detecting convalescent antibodies using indirect immunofluorescence or immunoperoxidase assays. Nevertheless, the utility of rickettsial DNA detection in eschars and/or blood using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for rapid diagnosis has been increasingly reported [13, 14].

SLE and rickettsial diseases, such as JSF, share some common signs and symptoms, including fever, rash, and cytopenia. In severe cases, both diseases affect multiple organs, including the lungs, pleura, and central nervous system, making the differential diagnosis more complicated. SLE diagnosis, particularly in cases with neuropsychiatric disorders, requires the comprehensive assessment and exclusion of infectious diseases [15]. In some reported cases, the first tentative diagnosis of suspected rickettsial diseases was ultimately confirmed as SLE [16]. Establishing an accurate diagnosis in such complex cases can be time-consuming; however, immediate intervention, even with limited information, is often necessary in critical care settings. Herein, we report a complex case of NPSLE manifesting with severe symptoms, such as fever, respiratory failure, and altered consciousness and presenting significant diagnostic challenges. The differential diagnosis was complicated by findings suggestive of JSF, such as black eschars with positive results for R. japonica on PCR testing.

Case presentation

The patient was a 43-year-old man residing in a rural region endemic to Japanese spotted fever. Seven weeks before admission to our hospital, he presented to the referring hospital with a two-week history of right-sided chest pain, which occurred in July. Although the patient denied outdoor leisure activities prior to symptom onset, his occupation as a construction site supervisor involved routine outdoor work. He was unaware of any tick exposure at this time. Computed tomography (CT) revealed pleural effusion in the right lung, and thoracentesis confirmed exudative pleural effusion (Fig. 1A). The patient’s complete blood count was within the normal range; however, C-reactive protein level was slightly elevated at 1.23 mg/dL. Results for ANA, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies, and rheumatoid factor were negative. Surgical pleural biopsy revealed inflammation, suggesting no specific diagnosis. Bacterial and mycobacterial cultures of the pleural effusion were negative. Five weeks before admission to our hospital, the patient was temporarily discharged from the referring hospital and remained at home. Three weeks before admission (during September), he may have been exposed to tick bites at home. Two weeks prior to admission, the patient developed fever, worsening chest pain, and increased pleural effusion; antibiotic treatment with piperacillin/tazobactam did not alleviate his condition. Two days before admission, the patient manifested altered consciousness and was transferred to our hospital.

On arrival, the patient’s consciousness level was E3V2M5 on the Glasgow Coma Scale, and he presented with a temperature of 37.8 ℃, heart rate of 80 beats/min, blood pressure of 119/75 mmHg, SpO2 of 97% (on 8 L/min oxygen), and respiratory rate of 30 breaths/min. Due to severe respiratory failure with altered consciousness, the patient underwent endotracheal intubation, right thoracic drain placement, and mechanical ventilation and was admitted to the intensive care unit. Physical examination revealed two black eschars with a red halo on the lateral (Fig. 1B) and medial (Fig. 1C) right proximal thigh. Each eschar measured 3–4 mm in diameter. No malar rash, discoid rash, oral ulcers, arthritis, or significant lymphadenopathy was observed. Blood tests indicated pancytopenia, liver dysfunction, elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, and low complement levels (Table 1). CT revealed increased right pleural effusion (Fig. 1D). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed a slightly increased cell count and elevated interleukin-6 level (7.74 pg/mL; Table 1), however, the immunoglobulin G (IgG) index was within the normal range (0.48). Magnetic resonance imaging of the head suggested a thickened dura mater or subdural hematoma in the left frontal area, with no evidence of meningitis or stroke. Electroencephalographic findings obtained in the absence of sedative medications indicated generalized brain dysfunction with no obvious epileptiform discharges. NPSLE was considered in the differential diagnosis owing to the 6-week course of pleuritis, sequential development of fever, cytopenia, and low complement levels, which were accompanied by consciousness disturbance with elevated interleukin-6 level in the cerebrospinal fluid [17]. Although the subacute course of pleuritis was atypical, other clinical features were plausibly attributable to severe rickettsial diseases. Given the patient’s eschars and residence in a JSF-endemic area, JSF was also considered in the differential diagnosis. As the patient was intubated because of progressive respiratory failure and consciousness disturbance, immediate therapeutic intervention was required. Therefore, testing for SLE-specific antibodies was ordered, and the patient’s eschars and buffy coat were collected for PCR testing for R. japonica. Subsequently, methylprednisolone pulse therapy combined with intravenous minocycline was initiated.

Succeeding tests showed that ANA had turned positive 80 times (homogeneous pattern) and that anti-dsDNA antibodies were positive at 50.8 on chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (Table 1). The anti-Smith antibody was negative. For JSF, nested PCR analysis of the 17-kDa antigen gene and gltA gene of R. japonica was performed as described previously [18–20]; the results were positive for the eschar on the lateral proximal thigh but negative for the buffy coat. The PCR assay was repeated three times, and all tests yielded positive results for the eschar. Sequence analysis of both showed high homology with R. japonica strain YH (99.7% and 100% similarity, respectively), confirming the presence of R. japonica (the gltA gene sequence analysis was performed 4 years later using the preserved specimen, and each sequence was deposited in the NCBI/EMBL/DDBJ database under accession numbers LC897193 and LC897194). However, serological test results for R. japonica IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM) on days 1, 9, 16, 23, and 37 were consistently negative (Table 2).

Following the provision of interventions, respiratory failure and altered consciousness improved rapidly, leading to extubation on the third day of admission. Subsequent findings confirmed SLE diagnosis, with the patient meeting multiple diagnostic criteria. Specifically, the patient’s fever, pleuritis, neurological disorder, hematological disorder (leukocytopenia), and immunological disorder (low complement levels, anti-DNA antibody positivity, and ANA positivity) fulfilled 5 out of 11 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria (1997), met 6 out of 17 Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics criteria (2012), and yielded 23 points in the European League Against Rheumatism/ACR 2019 criteria [21, 22].

However, we were concerned about how rickettsial infection contributes to the patient’s condition. Following discussion with rheumatologists, we decided to complete the minocycline treatment for 2 weeks and taper off the glucocorticoids with close monitoring. The patient was discharged home on day 25. Glucocorticoid treatment and tapering regimen were as follows: high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1 g/day for three consecutive days, initiated on the day of admission); transition to oral prednisolone at 70 mg/day (approximately 1.0 mg/kg/day) for approximately two weeks; tapering to 30 mg/day over three weeks, then to 20 mg/day over six weeks; tapering by 2.5–5 mg every 2–4 weeks; and discontinuation after approximately 12 months. At 2 years after discharge, right-sided pleural effusion similar to that observed during disease onset, accompanied by fever, recurred. ANA positivity at 640 times (homogeneous and peripheral patterns) and elevated anti-DNA antibody levels at 23 IU/mL (measured by radioimmunoassay) were also noted. We intensified immunosuppressive therapy by administering mycophenolate mofetil and hydroxychloroquine along with glucocorticoids, which resulted in prompt clinical improvement and sustained disease control without treatment with anti-rickettsial antibiotics. He was followed up at the rheumatology clinic for 6 years post discharge. While his condition had been maintained with immunosuppressive therapy, anti-DNA antibody levels remained consistently detectable within 15–25 IU/mL. The patient experienced some minor relapses with serositis or arthritis, which were managed by immunosuppressants. These findings suggest that the sequential clinical course of this patient could be explained by SLE with pleural effusion and coincidental localized R. japonica infection.

Discussion and conclusions

In this report, we describe a case of NPSLE in which differential diagnosis was complicated by concurrent localized rickettsial infection. This case underscores that although a positive PCR result for Rickettsia in eschars is useful for the rapid diagnosis of rickettsial diseases, it does not necessarily indicate a systemic infection and that a comprehensive workup, including confirmation of convalescent antibodies, is required. This case also highlights a rare but important instance of possible localized rickettsial infection.

Rickettsial diseases, which encompass various subgroups, pose a significant global infectious threat [23]. Among rickettsial diseases, JSF is endemic to Japan and is part of the spotted fever group, exhibiting typical clinical features, such as fever, rash, and eschars. A rise in serum antibody titers between acute and convalescent phases is considered the serological gold standard for diagnosing JSF and other rickettsial diseases; however, the time required for seroconversion can limit early diagnosis. PCR testing of eschars and blood allows for a faster diagnosis, aiding early interventions in severe cases [13, 14]. Nonetheless, PCR analysis of eschar samples merely confirms the presence of R. japonica DNA within the eschar lesion and cannot substitute paired serology, which provides evidence of the immune response required to confirm a systemic infection, underscoring the need for careful interpretation in clinical context.

In the present case, PCR was performed using a conventional method involving sequence analyses targeting the 17 kDa antigen gene. Although high sequence homology confirmed the presence of R. japonica in the eschar, the negative serology for R. japonica IgG and IgM is consistent with the possibility that rickettsiae were localized at the bite site without causing systemic disease. This interpretation is further supported by the negative PCR result from a buffy coat specimen collected on day 14 after symptom relapse, when systemic infection would be more likely to yield detectable rickettsial DNA in blood. Furthermore, steroid pulse therapy and subsequent prolonged steroid use might have affected the R. japonica serology results. However, in JSF cases, seroconversion typically occurs within 2–3 weeks, even as early as 9 days after the onset of fever [6, 24]. Considering that a few cases of JSF and other similar rickettsial infections exhibited delayed seroconversion [24, 25], the possibility of delayed seroconversion in conjunction with impaired antibody production due to glucocorticoids cannot be completely ruled out. Nevertheless, the pre-treatment sample obtained 14 days after the onset of fever—amidst systemic inflammation prior to minocycline or glucocorticoid therapy—would be expected to show seroconversion or at least an early rise in antibody titers as observed in typical systemic JSF. Patients with autoimmune diseases (including SLE) who are receiving maintenance steroid therapy show impaired but detectable antibody response [26, 27]. Data on IgG and IgM dynamics in localized rickettsial infection remain insufficient, and further investigations are necessary. The present case highlights that although PCR positivity for rickettsial DNA in eschar samples facilitates rapid diagnosis, systemic rickettsial infection should be confirmed with supporting evidence such as paired serology and/or detection of rickettsial DNA in blood, and these results should be interpreted within clinical context.

The results of the 17-kDA antigen gene analysis, which revealed 99.7% identity with R. japonica, were consistent with the diagnosis of R. japonica in combination with the clinical context of the patient. However, the identity of 99.7%, not 100%, prompted us to further investigate gltA, as R. japonica is known for its extremely low genomic diversity in Japan [28]. Therefore, we additionally performed gltA gene sequence analysis but analyzed only 357 bases. This limitation was likely due to the use of a preserved specimen collected 4 years earlier and the low amount of pathogen DNA in the sample, which allowed amplification only via nested PCR analysis. It was difficult to distinguish other related Rickettsiae species, such as R. heilongjiangensis, R. raoultii, and R. conorii, from R. japonica solely based on gltA gene analysis because they also showed 100% similarity to R. japonica. However, in the 17-kDa antigen gene analysis, the identity with R. heilongjiangensis was 99.1%, which was sufficient to identify the causative pathogen of R. japonica. Fujikawa et al. reported a similar case of severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) in which PCR targeting R. japonica was positive in the eschar, despite negative blood PCR and IFA results [29]. However, because sequence data for the eschar-derived amplicon were not reported, definitive attribution to R. japonica remains uncertain. In contrast, we confirmed the amplicon sequence, which increases diagnostic confidence and supports localized R. japonica infection.

While ANA and anti-dsDNA antibodies are key criteria for classifying SLE, ANA is occasionally detected in various infectious diseases—including intracellular bacterial infections e.g., those involving Rickettsia spp.—and can persist in some cases [30]. Moreover, Rickettsia can persist in humans long after clinical recovery, which might lead to persistent positive ANA [31–35]. Therefore, the possibility of seronegative and persistent systemic Japanese spotted fever with a falsely positive ANA test result must be considered. However, this diagnosis fails to explain the preceding pleurisy, and some SLE cases are initially seronegative and seroconvert during the clinical course [36, 37]. Most cases of persistent rickettsial infections have shown elevated convalescent antibodies [34, 35], with the majority of them testing positive for Rickettsia spp. on nested PCR of blood [35]. Therefore, in the present case, the persistently negative convalescent antibody up to the 37th day along with the negative nested PCR result in a buffy coat specimen obtained on day 14 were incompatible with the hypothesis. Furthermore, the patient received glucocorticoid therapy for one year following the initial onset without recurrence of symptoms. The relapses that occurred at and after two years were treated with intensified immunosuppressive therapy without administering anti-rickettsial agents. These clinical observations are more consistent with an autoimmune etiology responsive to immunosuppression rather than a persistent rickettsial infection.

We speculate that R. japonica proliferated at the bite site, resulting in eschar formation, but it did not progress to systemic dissemination; furthermore, SLE played a central role in the patient’s systemic inflammation. However, the mechanism of localized rickettsial infection remains undetermined. Although the mechanism underlying apparent localization remains unclear, acquired tick resistance (ATR), an IgE- and basophil-dependent response after repeated tick bites, has been proposed as a host factor that may reduce tick feeding efficiency and transmission of tick-borne pathogens [38]. In this case, prior tick exposure is plausible, given the patient’s outdoor occupation; however, ATR was not evaluated. In addition, considering that ticks or mites can simultaneously transmit multiple pathogens to humans [39, 40], the possibility of co-infection with other pathogens should also be considered. In this case, if the tick that transmitted R. japonica also transmitted other pathogens, it could play some role in causing black-crusted eschars and systemic inflammation. In Japan, as a tick-borne disease that causes black-crusted eschars, differential diagnoses (in addition to JSF and tsutsugamushi disease) also include SFTS, tularemia, and bartonellosis [41–43]. We performed the serological analysis for the SFTS virus and Francisella spp., but did not test for Bartonella spp. as the patient did not exhibit lymphadenopathy [44]. However, considering ticks may transmit rickettsiae and Bartonella [40], it remains an important limitation of our study.

In conclusion, this case provides insights into the mimicry between SLE and rickettsial infections and the possible localization of rickettsiae at a bite site. Although PCR testing of eschars provides rapid results for the diagnosis of rickettsial diseases, such as JSF, performing a comprehensive assessment that includes both IgG and IgM paired serology is crucial to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.
