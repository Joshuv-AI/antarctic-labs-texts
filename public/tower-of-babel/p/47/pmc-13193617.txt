Contrast-Induced Neurotoxicity: A Rare and Poorly Understood Entity
Authors: Vivek Gupta, Navjeet Kaur, Rajeev Goel, Amit Shankar
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Iodinated contrast agents play an invaluable role in diagnostic and interventional procedures, and their use has increased significantly over the years. Although rare, contrast-induced neurotoxicity can happen with any contrast investigation.[1] Renal toxicity and allergic reactions induced by contrast are known, and guidelines exist to manage and prevent them; however, contrast-induced neurotoxicity (CIN) remains a poorly recognized entity. Risk factors seem to be hypertension and renal failure.[2] CIN is described as new neurological symptoms after endovascular procedures with contrast media, and after excluding other possible causes like bleeding or infarction.[3] CIN has an incidence that broadly ranges from 0.3% to 1% but might increase to 4% when hyperosmolar contrast is used.[4] We hereby present a case of a patient with prolonged unconsciousness and focal seizures after endovascular treatment of a giant internal carotid artery (ICA) aneurysm.

We report a 89-year-old hypertensive female who presented to the emergency department with complaints of an acute onset of severe headache and vomiting. On admission, the patient was conscious and alert without any focal neurological deficits and stable vitals. Patient had left eye ptosis with vision loss for 15 years. The computed tomography (CT) head did not reveal any acute bleed. Angio-CT with 3D reconstruction revealed a giant (>5 cm) wide-necked, partially thrombosed left cavernous ICA (anterior genu) aneurysm with significant mass effect on the surrounding structures. Diagnostic cerebral angiography was done, and the aneurysm was treated endovascularly using flow diversion and coiling [Figure 1]. The procedure was uneventful. About 150 ml of iso-osmolar non-ionic contrast (Omnipaque) was used during the procedure, which lasted for around 3 hours.

Cerebral digital subtraction angiography images (a and b) of the left internal carotid artery showing a giant aneurysm with dysplastic neck centered at the anterior genu. Post-procedure angiogram images (c and d) showing decreased flow in the sac with a flow diverter (FD) and coils in situ.

Post procedure, the patient could not be extubated for 6 hours and had a low Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 8. Cone beam CT (CBCT) head post procedure showed hyper-dense left-sided sulci [Figure 2a]. To rule out subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH), a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain was done [Figure 3]. On susceptibility weighted images, no SAH or intra-cerebral haemorrhage was seen. No acute infarct was seen on diffusion-weighted images. Since no acute lesion was noted except hyper-density in the sulcal spaces on CBCT, it was thought to be contrast leakage due to temporary disruption of the blood–brain barrier, and symptomatic treatment was initiated.

(b) Diffuse unilateral cortical thickening, hyperattenuating brain, and obliteration of the sulcal spaces (black arrow), suggesting cerebral edema with minimal mass effect seen 24 hours later. (c and d) Gradual resolution of the edema with medical management leading to a decrease in hyperattenuation and opening up of the sulcal spaces (white arrows). NCCT: Non contrast computed tomography

MRI brain done 6 hours after the procedure (a-c) did not show any subarachnoid hemorrhage, acute infarct, or cerebral edema. In view of continued low GCS, repeat MRI brain (d-f) nine days later also did not reveal any significant findings. GCS: Glasgow coma scale, MRI: Magnetic resonance imaging

The following day, CT head showed left hemisphere edema and diffuse parenchymal hyperdensity without any significant mass effect [Figure 2b]. Anti-edema measures were started. Intravenous injection of mannitol 100ml three times a day and intravenous injection of dexamethasone, 6mg, four times a day were started for 3 days. They were gradually tapered. The 48-hour CT head showed resolving left cerebral oedema [Figure 2c]. The patient remained clinically lethargic. She developed focal onset of right-hand tonic-clonic seizures 72 hours post-procedure with continued altered awareness. Anti-epileptics were started and were gradually escalated to three drugs, which caused the eventual cessation of the seizures. She continued to be in poor neurological condition despite improved CT appearance [Figure 2d] and remained intubated with a GCS score of 3. Repeat MRI brain done on day 9 post procedure did not reveal any infarct or haemorrhage, and the cerebral oedema seen on the initial CT had resolved. The patient passed away 10 days after the procedure secondary to sepsis complicating CIN.

CIN is rare and grossly under-reported. Less than 100 cases have been reported till now, in the published literature, and are mainly secondary to diagnostic or therapeutic endovascular procedures, making it an exceedingly rare adverse effect of contrast usage.[1] The close differential diagnosis in the current clinical setting is hyperperfusion syndrome, especially when giant aneurysms are treated with flow diversion techniques. This was ruled out in the current case as the immediate post-procedure CT showed hyper-attenuating parenchyma on the ipsilateral side, in contrast to the hypo-attenuation, which is generally observed in hyperperfusion syndrome [Figure 2a].

CIN can occur after various types of diagnostic or therapeutic procedures. Direct intra-arterial or intrathecal contrast administration, for example, in neuro-interventional and cardiovascular procedures, has a higher risk as compared to intravenous contrast administration and often occurs in the territory of the blood vessels where the contrast agent has been injected.[5] The pathophysiology and exact etiology of this condition are poorly understood. There is no direct evidence linking this condition to the amount of contrast used. The available literature is insufficient to provide the exact frequency of CIN related to all neurological endovascular procedures.[3]

CIN presentation is varied, but symptoms are usually self-limiting. The common symptoms in the reported literature include low GCS score, confusion,[6] cortical blindness, seizures,[7] motor/sensory deficit,[8] and aphasia.[2] The presumed pathophysiology is the transient disruption of the blood–brain barrier and endothelial dysfunction with leakage of contrast into parenchyma and subarachnoid spaces, causing direct neurotoxicity[9] and vasogenic cerebral edema. These might be accentuated in neuro-interventional procedures due to repeated injections in a single vascular territory, causing contrast accumulation and further breakdown of the blood–brain barrier.[10] The risk factors include chronic hypertension, as in our case, impaired cerebral autoregulation, cerebral ischemia, diabetes, renal impairment, large contrast volume, and previous adverse reaction to iodinated contrast media.[6]

Cross-sectional neuroimaging (CT and MRI) findings help in ruling out more frequent ischemic or hemorrhagic complications of endovascular procedures. The commonly reported CT findings include subarachnoid and cortical hyperattenuation (42.1%) secondary to contrast staining (unilateral more common than multilobar), followed by cerebral oedema or sulcal effacement (26.3%) and loss of grey–white differentiation (7.9%), and the rest showing no acute CT abnormalities (40.8%) as reported by Mariajoseph et al.[1] In the same study, the majority of the patients showed normal MRI (53.1%), followed by signal change (40.8%), unilateral (22.4%), more common than bilateral (18.4%), mostly cortical (80%), and cerebral edema (12.2%).

This entity is known to be self-limiting with transient symptoms, and most cases improve within 48–72 hours.[6] There is no definitive therapy for CIN, and only symptomatic treatment has been advocated. In our case, close neurological monitoring and supportive treatment, including steroids, vigorous hydration, anti-epileptics, and anti-edema drugs, were given. Previous studies have documented mortality in these patients, though rare, as was seen in the index case.[3]

In conclusion, CIN is an infrequent occurrence with no specific clinical signs; therefore, it is an absolute diagnostic challenge. It is essentially a diagnosis of exclusion with neuroimaging guidance and a high degree of suspicion. The awareness of this entity might therefore help in early recognition and timely supportive management to limit further neurological dysfunction.
