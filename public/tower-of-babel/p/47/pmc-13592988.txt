PI3K–Akt signaling network crosstalk in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury: Mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Wenzhe Yang, Qian Yang, Zhihui Wang, Xu Zhao, Jingxuan Deng, Xinran Xia, Jinzheng Wang, Linhui Xia, Yanfang Wang, Fen Cui, Rubin Tan, Jinxiang Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral ischemia (CI) is an acute central nervous system disorder resulting from the abrupt interruption of blood flow to brain tissue. The restoration of blood flow during treatment is frequently accompanied by cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (CI/R) injury, a secondary injury mechanism that substantially limits the overall efficacy of reperfusion therapy. The phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway, a central regulator of cell survival, proliferation, and stress response, has attracted growing interest for its role in CI and CI/R injury. During the early ischemic phase, PI3K-Akt activation in neurons and brain microvascular endothelial cells serves as a crucial endogenous protective mechanism, inhibiting apoptosis and maintaining energy metabolic homeostasis, thereby mitigating initial brain damage. As ischemia progresses, downregulation of this pathway in microglia and infiltrating macrophages can promote excessive release of proinflammatory factors, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), exacerbating local inflammation and tissue injury. In the reperfusion phase, reactivation of the PI3K–Akt pathway exerts multi-level neuroprotection: It counteracts neuronal apoptosis and oxidative stress, promotes angiogenesis and blood–brain barrier (BBB) repair in endothelial cells, and modulates neuroinflammation in glial cells. Importantly, the PI3K–Akt pathway does not function in isolation but engages in complex crosstalk with multiple signaling cascades, such as Wnt/β-catenin, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR). Moreover, it shapes the immune microenvironment by fine-tuning the functions of immune cells, including regulatory T cells (Tregs). This intricate network dynamically governs the survival of the neurovascular unit by regulating mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, inflammation, and autophagy. This review systematically explores the dynamic changes, cell-specific functions, and interactive mechanisms of the PI3K–Akt pathway during CI and CI/R injury, and discusses its potential for precision treatment strategies and clinical translation.

Introduction

Cerebral ischemia (CI) is an acute cerebrovascular disease caused by the sudden interruption of cerebral blood flow, which can rapidly induce neuronal damage and even permanent neurological deficits. It ranks among the neurological disorders with the highest rates of mortality and disability worldwide.[1] Ischemia abruptly disrupts the supply of blood and oxygen to brain tissue, rapidly triggering a cascade of pathophysiological processes—including energy metabolism dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and apoptotic cell death—ultimately leading to irreversible impairment of brain structure and function.[2,3] Although reperfusion therapies, such as intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy, have significantly improved outcomes in acute ischemic stroke, the process of restoring blood flow can itself induce a more complex injury known as cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (CI/R) injury. This secondary damage involves dynamic responses from neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and vascular endothelial cells.[4] Moreover, excessive activation of proinflammatory and pro-apoptotic pathways—including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling—markedly exacerbates oxidative injury, neuroinflammation, and cellular apoptosis.[5–7] In contrast, the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway, a well-established pro-survival cascade, has been demonstrated to effectively counteract these injury processes upon activation. Since the initial report by Hashiguchi et al[8] highlighting its key protective role in CI/R injury, mechanistic research on this pathway has gained increasing attention.[9]

The upstream PI3K–Akt signaling pathway is activated by various extracellular factors—such as growth factors, hormones, and cytokines—through receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs).[10,11] Upon activation, PI3K catalyzes the conversion of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), which facilitates the recruitment and activation of multiple downstream signaling molecules, initiating a cascade of intracellular signaling events.[12] A central downstream effector of this pathway is Akt. Activated Akt phosphorylates diverse substrates—such as tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2), Bcl-2-associated agonist of cell death (Bad), p53, and glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β)—to regulate critical biological processes including cell survival, proliferation, metabolism, and angiogenesis.[13]

The activity and function of the PI3K-Akt pathway during CI and CI/R injury are characterized by high dynamism, cell-type specificity, and extensive crosstalk with multiple signaling pathways, collectively forming an intricately orchestrated regulatory network. Accordingly, this review will systematically elaborate on the multidimensional protective mechanisms of the PI3K-Akt pathway within various cell types of the neurovascular unit (NVU) during both CI and CI/R injury. This scope is extended to its fine-tuning of immune cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs). A particular emphasis will be placed on analyzing the pathway’s crosstalk and feedback regulation with key pathways—including Wnt/β-catenin, GSK-3β, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), MAPK/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), and phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)—specifically in the contexts of mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. Finally, by integrating emerging perspectives, such as heart–brain interactions, this review will explore novel therapeutic strategies targeting the PI3K–Akt pathway and assess their clinical translation potential, aiming to provide new insights and a theoretical foundation for developing multi-target and precision therapies for CI/R injury.

Endogenous Protective Mechanisms of the PI3K–Akt Signaling Pathway in Cerebral Ischemic Injury

When CI occurs, dysfunction of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and a decline in the activity of the antioxidant system lead to the gradual accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). This results in elevated intracellular oxidative stress levels, ultimately inducing oxidative damage.[14] Furthermore, metabolic disturbances and alterations in the cellular microenvironment caused by hypoxia can trigger multiple programmed cell death mechanisms, including apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, further disrupting brain tissue structure and function. Subsequently, microglia and astrocytes become activated, and together with the release of necrotic cell debris, they collectively promote the secretion of various proinflammatory mediators. This process provokes a robust inflammation, thereby inflicting damage upon neurons and vascular endothelial cells within the ischemic region.[15] At the same time, reduced expression of tight junction proteins, damage to the blood-brain barrier (BBB) structure, and increased permeability allow harmful substances to easily enter the brain parenchyma, causing persistent chronic damage.

Activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway can alleviate the above pathological processes at multiple levels. First, this pathway can enhance cellular antioxidant defenses and reduce ROS levels, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and inhibiting related programmed cell death, such as apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis.[14] Furthermore, the PI3K–Akt pathway can suppress the overactivation of microglia and astrocytes, reducing the release of inflammatory factors. It also alleviates neuroinflammation by inhibiting the action of inflammatory mediators and curbing the excessive activation of proinflammatory signaling pathways.[16] In addition, this pathway maintains the structural stability and functional integrity of the BBB by upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins, reducing its permeability, thereby preventing the invasion of exogenous harmful molecules and alleviating chronic damage to brain tissue.[17] The activation of PI3K–Akt also promotes astrocyte function, further supporting neuronal survival, regeneration, and axonal repair, thereby contributing to multi-level neuroprotective effects.[14,16–18]

Protective effects in neurons: Against oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death

Activation of endogenous antioxidant defenses

During CI, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway exerts protective effects against oxidative stress in neurons through multiple mechanisms. In the middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model in rats, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway in neurons promoted the nuclear expression of Keap1–Nrf2 signaling, further upregulating the gene transcription of key antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant reserves and basal defense capacity. Consequently, this mechanism suppressed the oxidative stress response and counteracted the damage caused by the progressive accumulation of ROS. Compared to the group treated with a PI3K inhibitor, the experimental group showed a significant reduction in cerebral infarction volume.[19] In another study, a mouse MCAO model was established, and fibroblast growth factor 10 (FGF10) was injected into the lateral ventricles to activate the PI3K-Akt pathway, inducing the production of HO-1, which further inhibited oxidative stress and protected neurons and the BBB.[20,21]

Inhibition of proinflammatory signaling in neurons

When CI occurs, the local hypoxic environment and substances released from necrotic tissue trigger inflammation. Early activation of this response promotes the clearance of necrotic debris and maintains the microenvironment’s normal physiological state. However, excessive activation of the inflammation leads to excessive cell clearance, resulting in counterproductive tissue damage. Activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway in neurons exerts a protective effect by suppressing inflammation through modulating microglia, inhibiting inflammation-related pathways, and reducing the release of proinflammatory factors. In a rat MCAO model, intraventricular injection of miR-204-5p activated the PI3K–Akt pathway, leading to a decrease in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in neurons, while the levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 increased, thereby inhibiting local inflammation, and reducing the inhibition of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF), which are involved in neuronal damage repair. Compared with the MCAO model alone, the experimental group exhibited significantly reduced hippocampal neuronal damage. Behavioral tests further demonstrated that activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway enhanced cognitive function in mice.[22] In another MCAO rat model, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway upregulates the levels of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) subunits and erythropoietin (EPO). This confers neuroprotection through two principal mechanisms: First, by modulating downstream inflammatory mediators and immune cell infiltration to suppress the initiation and progression of neuroinflammation; and second, by improving cellular energy metabolism under hypoxic conditions. Together, these actions collectively mitigate inflammatory damage to neurons. This protective effect was reversed after the use of PI3K inhibitors.[23,24] In an oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) model of neural stem cells, naloxone activated the PI3K-Akt pathway, which in turn reduced the activation and assembly of the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. This led to a significant suppression of the inflammation and conferred protection to the neural stem cells. This finding demonstrates that the PI3K–Akt pathway primarily regulates inflammation during CI by inhibiting inflammatory initiation.[25] In addition, in microglia, the PI3K–Akt pathway can activate the downstream mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), promoting the polarization of microglia toward the M2 type (anti-inflammatory type), reducing the levels of inflammatory factors IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, thereby inhibiting the inflammation.[26]

Maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis and modulation of cell death

CI can trigger cell apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, leading to extensive brain tissue damage. The activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway exerts multifaceted neuroprotective effects: During the early ischemic phase, it induces moderate protective autophagy to clear damaged cellular components and alleviate inflammation; at later stages, it counteracts secondary injury by suppressing excessive autophagy, apoptosis, and ferroptosis. Furthermore, this pathway promotes cellular regeneration and repair through mechanisms such as the release of neurotrophic factors.[19,27,28]

In a mouse MCAO CI model, inhibition of NADPH oxidase 2 (NOX2) activates the PI3K–Akt pathway, thereby downregulating the expression of Beclin-1, a key protein in autophagosome formation. This process reduces NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and promotes angiogenesis, ultimately mitigating autophagic damage.[14] In the rat MCAO model, intranasal administration of sodium butyrate activates this pathway, thereby inhibiting apoptosis by reducing the expression of cysteine-aspartic acid protease-3 (C-Casp3) and Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax). In brain coronal sections, a decrease in the number of Fluoro-Jade C (FJC) and C-Cas3-positive neurons indicates inhibition of autophagy.[29] In another rat MCAO model, intraperitoneal administration of Rehmannioside A activated the neuronal PI3K–Akt pathway, upregulated the activity of glutathione (GSH), oxidized glutathione (GSSG), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) content, thereby effectively inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis.[30] In the OGD model constructed in HT22 cells, activation of this pathway upregulates the expression of ferritin heavy chain-1 (FTH1), Beclin1 (a key initiator of autophagosome formation), and LC3-II (an autophagosome membrane marker), thereby cooperatively inhibiting ferroptosis and activating moderate autophagy.[31] These findings demonstrate that the PI3K–Akt pathway confers broad neuroprotection by targeting distinct molecular players within different cell death programs.

Beyond regulating cell death, the PI3K–Akt pathway also exerts protective effects by maintaining mitochondrial functional integrity. Mitochondrial dysfunction represents a central component of neuronal injury following CI.[32] In the context of CI, activation of this pathway suppresses mitochondrial over-fission and fragmentation. Such fragmentation not only impairs the mitochondrial capacity to clear ROS and regulate calcium homeostasis but also leads to excessive ROS release, which subsequently activates the NLRP3 inflammasome. This process exacerbates oxidative stress and inflammatory injury, ultimately forming a vicious cycle.[33–35] Although the PI3K–Akt pathway does not directly regulate the expression of the fission protein dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), it confers neuroprotection by restoring downstream calcium homeostasis, redox balance, and energy metabolism.[36] Moreover, under physiological conditions, mitochondrial ROS also participate in maintaining cellular signaling pathways, revealing its dual role in ischemic injury.[37]

Ferroptosis represents another critical target mediated by the PI3K–Akt pathway in neuroprotection. The burst of ROS triggered by CI leads to lipid peroxidation within cell membranes, establishing the foundation for the onset of ferroptosis.[38] By activating downstream axes, such as PI3K/Akt/Nrf2, this pathway upregulates the expression of key proteins like GPX4 and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) while downregulating pro-ferroptotic factors, such as acyl-coenzyme A (acyl-CoA) synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), thereby synergistically enhancing the clearance of lipid peroxides and reducing neuronal susceptibility to ferroptosis.[30,39–41] Furthermore, studies suggest an interaction between ferroptosis and mitochondrial damage. For instance, in specific models, ferroptosis may be accompanied by elevated Drp1 levels and the activation of mitochondrial autophagy,[42,43] indicating that the two processes may form a mutually exacerbating injury network in CI. The specific regulatory mechanisms underlying this interaction warrant further investigation.

Protective effects in vascular endothelial cells: Preservation of the BBB and initiation of repair

After CI occurs, vascular endothelial cells participate in various protective mechanisms by activating the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway. On one hand, it reinforces the overall structure of the BBB by regulating BBB-related proteins, thereby preventing and delaying the increase in its permeability. On the other hand, by promoting endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and ultimately angiogenesis, it jointly enhances BBB function, reducing further damage caused by increased permeability. Meanwhile, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway also indirectly exerts neuroprotective effects by regulating macrophage polarization and neuronal function, thus coordinately alleviating cerebral ischemic injury. In the mouse MCAO model, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway is activated in cortical and hippocampal neurons, inducing endothelial progenitor cells to release exosomes,[44] which significantly reduce levels of ROS and lipid peroxidation products MDA in neurons, thereby alleviating oxidative stress damage. This effect can be reversed by PI3K inhibitors.[27] In the mouse CI model treated with Apelin-13, the PI3K–Akt-mediated BBB protective effect was also significantly weakened by PI3K inhibitors. The PI3K–Akt pathway also works synergistically with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), such as nitric oxide (NO), to promote the migration of early endothelial cells, proliferation, and lumen structure formation. This initiates the process of vascular regenerative repair in the early phase of ischemia, counteracting ischemia-hypoxia injury and protecting the BBB. At the same time, this pathway can regulate the expression of aquaporin 4 (AQP4) in astrocytes, maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the BBB.[28] In addition, studies have shown that brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) can protect astrocytes by releasing microvesicles.[45] In an OGD-induced cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (bEnd.3) endothelial cell model, the ginkgolide B derivative compound 10-O-(N, N-dimethylaminoethyl) ginkgolide B methanesulfonate (XQ-1H) activates the PI3K–Akt pathway via insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), thereby triggering the downstream cascade reaction of GSK-3β/β-catenin/VEGF, promoting β-catenin nuclear translocation and VEGF transcription expression, and increasing CD31-positive microvascular density, thereby promoting vascular proliferation and lumen formation.[46]

In human choroidal vascular endothelial cells (HCVEC), hypoxia-induced stimulation promotes the expression and secretion of colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1). This factor binds to its receptor, colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R), which activates the PI3K–Akt pathway in macrophages, promotes their migration, and M2 polarization. Additionally, it further stimulates the proliferation of HCVEC and enhances their angiogenic capacity. The PI3K inhibitor LY294002 can reverse this effect.[47] In a hypoxic model of mammary gland epithelial cells, exogenous VEGF and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) also induce the upregulation of downstream growth-related protein 43 (GAP-43), microvesicle-associated protein 2, and ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA-1) through the PI3K–Akt pathway, thereby promoting endothelial cell regeneration. In the OGD model of human umbilical vein endothelial cells, the coumarin derivative fraxetin activates the PI3K–Akt pathway, enhances the expression of CD34 and CD41, and thereby induces angiogenesis. In the control group without fraxetin, this pro-angiogenic effect was significantly weakened.[48]

Additionally, in a rat CI model, injection of Erigeron breviscapus activated the neuronal PI3K–Akt pathway, inhibited the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9), and reduced the degradation of tight junction proteins claudin-5 and zona occludens 1 (ZO-1), thereby maintaining the integrity of the BBB. Transmission electron microscopy reveals intact endothelial tight junctions, closely connected basement membranes, and astrocytic endfeet, with normal BBB permeability and preserved neuronal ultrastructure in ischemic penumbra in the experimental group. These protective effects can also be antagonized by PI3K inhibitors.[49,50]

In summary, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway in vascular endothelial cells exerts multifaceted protective roles post-ischemia. It directly facilitates vascular regeneration and indirectly supports NVU function by preserving BBB integrity and mediating intercellular communication, playing a key role in brain repair after ischemic injury.

Protective effects in astrocytes: Phenotypic modulation and support of the NVU

Following CI, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway is activated within astrocytes, playing a crucial role in regulating their phenotypic transformation, inflammation, vascular regeneration, and neuroprotection, thereby reducing cell apoptosis and safeguarding the structural and functional integrity of brain tissue.

In a rat MCAO model, the application of 2,3,5,6-tetramethylpyrazine (TMP) activates the PI3K–Akt pathway in astrocytes. Compared with the sham-operated group that did not receive TMP treatment, pathway activation induces astrocytes to transform from the neurotoxic A1 response phenotype to the neuroprotective A2 phenotype. A1-type astrocytes are often associated with brain tissue damage and dysfunction due to their release of inflammatory mediators and activation of the complement system, while A2-type astrocytes participate in tissue repair and neural regeneration.[51] In addition, PI3K–Akt pathway activation is closely related to the expression regulation of astrocyte functional proteins. In the aforementioned model, pathway activation inhibited abnormal expression of AQP4 and connexin 43 (Cx43) in astrocytes, which helps maintain BBB stability, promotes vascular regeneration, and strengthens the structural integrity of neuron–glial cell units.[51]

In a mouse CI model, injection of Apelin-13 activates the PI3K–Akt pathway within brain tissue, further upregulating the phosphorylation levels of ERK and AQP4, while inhibiting the degradation of tight junction proteins and apoptosis of astrocytes. This reduces tissue cell damage caused by the hypoxic–ischemic environment and inflammatory injury resulting from the entry of damaging factors into brain tissue due to increased BBB permeability. Consequently, it effectively protects neural function and reduces infarct volume. At the same time, Apelin-13 can induce upregulation of VEGF, AQP4, and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) expression in brain tissue, inhibit iNOS expression, and ultimately result in decreased BBB permeability and enhanced neuroprotective effects.[28] Among these, NO produced by eNOS is a key angiogenic factor that works synergistically with other factors to promote endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and vascular lumen formation, thereby accelerating the formation of new blood vessels in the early phase of ischemia. Upregulation of AQP4 also plays a key role in maintaining water–salt balance, alleviating cerebral edema, and enhancing BBB function.[28] It is worth noting that the protective effect mediated by Apelin-13 was significantly weakened after the use of PI3K inhibitors, suggesting that this mechanism depends on the complete activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway.

In summary, activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway in astrocytes not only regulates their reactive phenotype transformation and anti-inflammatory mechanisms but also participates in the protection of the BBB and angiogenesis by regulating the expression of key proteins. It is an important regulatory pathway in the neural repair process following CI.

Protective role in oligodendrocytes: Maintenance of myelin integrity

During the repair process following CI, oligodendrocytes play a key role in neuroprotection and myelin regeneration by activating the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway. The activation of this pathway mainly regulates the expression of specific proteins, promoting the maturation, migration, and axonal contact of oligodendrocytes, thereby enhancing the myelin formation capacity of neurons. This initiates the neuronal repair process early to counteract hypoxic-ischemic injury.

Studies have shown that extracellular endogenous neuregulin 1 (NRG1) can activate the PI3K–Akt pathway, thereby upregulating the expression of myelin basic protein (MBP) and non-phosphorylated neurofilament protein (SMI-32). MBP is the main component of myelin sheath, primarily secreted by mature oligodendrocytes, while SMI-32 regulates the surface state of neuronal axons, providing the necessary conditions for oligodendrocyte migration and myelin formation. The significant increase in the expression of the above proteins indicates enhanced oligodendrocyte regeneration and myelin formation, which helps reduce demyelination and structural damage to neurons. It is worth noting that this protective effect was significantly weakened after the use of PI3K inhibitors, suggesting that it depends on the activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway.[52] In addition, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway in a rat CI model can also upregulate the expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). GFAP is one of the cytoskeletal proteins of astrocytes, but its upregulation during brain injury repair is also thought to be closely related to glial cell activation and regeneration, thereby jointly participating in brain tissue reconstruction and functional recovery.[53]

Thus, following CI, the PI3K–Akt pathway in oligodendrocytes promotes myelination and axonal repair by regulating the expression of myelin-related and neuron-interacting proteins, playing a crucial neuroprotective role.

In summary, this section systematically elucidates the multidimensional protective mechanisms of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway during CI. Pathway activation not only confers protection by suppressing key injury processes—including programmed cell death (apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis), oxidative stress, and inflammation—but also exerts precise, cell-specific regulation within the NVU. Specifically, in neurons, the pathway boosts endogenous antioxidant defenses and suppresses multiple death signals; in astrocytes, it promotes conversion to the neuroprotective A2 phenotype; and in vascular endothelial cells, it upregulates tight junction proteins and stimulates VEGF-mediated angiogenesis, thereby strengthening both barrier integrity and blood supply.

More importantly, the cell-specific actions of the PI3K–Akt pathway are not isolated but, through intricate crosstalk, form an integrated defense system. For example, microglial polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype suppresses neuroinflammation. Enhanced endothelial function directly strengthens the BBB and promotes astrocyte survival through the release of microvesicles. In a reciprocal manner, astrocytes secrete growth factors that support endothelial regeneration and provide neurotrophic support. These synergistic effects, together with crosstalk between the PI3K–Akt pathway and key nodal points like GSK-3β, collectively constitute a sophisticated networked regulatory system. Consequently, the core function of the PI3K–Akt pathway is to coordinate homeostasis across the entire NVU, thereby effectively mitigating initial ischemic injury and creating favorable conditions for subsequent neurological recovery.

Active Repair Mechanisms of the PI3K-Akt Signaling Pathway in Reperfusion Injury

During ischemia-reperfusion, the rapid restoration of blood flow and oxygen leads to the explosive generation of ROS,[54] leading to severe oxidative stress,[55] which in turn causes cell membrane rupture, deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage, and programmed cell death. Meanwhile, due to the further cellular damage caused by the aforementioned processes, massive release of proinflammatory factors promotes the recruitment and infiltration of monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils, and triggers the spread of inflammation by activating inflammasomes. In addition, activated astrocytes can further induce damaging processes, such as pyroptosis, apoptosis, and mitochondrial autophagy.[56–58] The vascular system is also profoundly affected: Newly formed blood vessels are fragile and easily damaged, tight junction proteins are downregulated, and BBB permeability increases, leading to further deterioration of ischemic penumbra tissue.[59,60]

In this pathological context, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway acts as a key endogenous protective mechanism, and its activation exerts neuroprotective effects in multiple dimensions. First, this pathway enhances the cellular scavenging capacity for ROS and inhibits their de novo generation by directly suppressing NOX2 activity—achieved through upregulating the keap1–Nrf2–HO-1 axis and related antioxidant factors (such as SOD, catalase [CAT], and HO-1).[14] This suppresses lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction caused by oxidative stress. Additionally, by activating the downstream mTOR signaling pathway to inhibit excessive autophagy, these combined mechanisms collectively alleviate neuronal apoptosis and necrosis.[61]

Secondly, the PI3K–Akt pathway also plays an important role in protecting the BBB. Pathway activation can increase the expression of tight junction proteins, such as claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1, stabilize the structure of brain microvascular endothelium, inhibit the penetration of inflammatory factors and immune cells, and maintain the integrity of the BBB.[62–64] In addition, this pathway can also promote the expression of VEGF and eNOS, inducing angiogenesis to repair the microcirculatory network in the ischemic region. This provides channels for the transfer of pro-repair factors and a nutritive environment for later cellular regeneration and repair, thereby enhancing the potential for neurological functional recovery.[55] Furthermore, the PI3K–Akt pathway synergizes with the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway to stabilize endothelial cells, thereby achieving long-term repair and functional stabilization of the BBB.[65]

Reparative effects in neurons: Integrated stress response and promotion of survival

Synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects

During CI/R, neurons are the target cells most susceptible to oxidative stress and inflammatory factors. The PI3K–Akt pathway is rapidly activated at this stage, thereby regulating multiple downstream effector molecules and significantly alleviating cell damage caused by reperfusion. Its neuroprotective effects are mainly achieved by upregulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes, inhibiting the production of proinflammatory factors, and reducing the degree of cell apoptosis.

In a rat middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) model, treatment with a compound of pig heart blood and Danshen (PCB-DS) significantly activated the PI3K–Akt pathway in neurons, accompanied by a decrease in ROS levels and reduced expression of the oxidative stress marker lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), suggesting its potential protective role against oxidative damage. Moreover, expression of apoptotic markers caspase-3 and caspase-9 was downregulated, suggesting suppression of the apoptotic cascade.[64] The activation of this pathway also involves the regulation of downstream signals, such as S6 kinase (S6K) and mTOR, promoting the expression of HIF-1α and HO-1, thereby enhancing tissue tolerance to acute hypoxia-reoxygenation stimuli. The above effects were significantly reversed after the application of PI3K inhibitors, confirming that this protective effect depends on the activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway.[66]

In vitro oxygen glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) cell models were established using PC12 cells (a rat adrenal pheochromocytoma cell line) and rat brain tissue. Following treatment with 6′-O-galloylglucoside (GPF), the PI3K–Akt pathway is activated in neurons, inducing an increase in HO-1 expression. Concurrently, this promotes the nuclear translocation of the transcription factor Nrf2 and its binding to the antioxidant response element (ARE), thereby inhibiting ROS production and alleviating rapid, severe oxidative stress damage caused by the acute oxygenation environment. The application of a PI3K inhibitor significantly diminished these effects, indicating strong pathway dependence.[54,67] Additionally, in PC12 cells treated with OGD/R and MCAO/R rat models, intervention with the natural alkaloid nuciferine (lotus alkaloid) can also activate the PI3K–Akt pathway, significantly suppressing the expression levels of proinflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation and providing further protection to cells. These findings demonstrate that the primary role of the PI3K–Akt pathway in CI/R is to suppress hyperactivated inflammatory storms and promote repair.[68]

In summary, the PI3K-Akt pathway plays a key role in neuronal protection after CI/R injury. It forms a positive feedback loop that supports neuronal survival. These findings highlight the PI3K–Akt pathway as a promising therapeutic target for acute brain injury interventions.

Fine-tuned modulation of autophagy for homeostasis maintenance

During CI/R, intense metabolic and energy crises can trigger excessive activation of autophagy, thereby exacerbating neuronal apoptosis and tissue damage. Activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway mitigates reperfusion-induced neuronal damage by preserving mitochondrial function, alleviating Golgi stress, and finely tuning autophagic activity to prevent secondary injury.

PI3K-Akt signaling exerts a dual regulatory effect on autophagy. Class I PI3K suppresses autophagy through Akt/mTORC1 activation, whereas class III PI3K promotes autophagy via Beclin-1 upregulation.[69] Thus, the neuroprotective outcome depends on the balance and context of pathway activation rather than global autophagy suppression.

In CI/R models, multiple interventions have demonstrated that PI3K–Akt-dependent autophagy modulation confers neuroprotection. Apelin-13 activates PI3K-Akt signaling through the APJ receptor, leading to mTOR/S6K upregulation, increased Bcl-2 expression, and suppression of pro-apoptotic and autophagy-related proteins, including Bad, Bax, caspase-3, Beclin-1, and LC3-II. This coordinated response inhibits apoptosis while preventing excessive autophagic clearance of reparable cellular components. Notably, the protective effect can be blocked by PI3K inhibitors.[66,70] Similarly, electroacupuncture pretreatment activates PI3K–Akt–mTORC1 signaling, inhibits the autophagy initiation factor p-ULK1 and FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1)-mediated mitophagy, and reduces autophagy-driven neuronal injury during reperfusion.[71]

In addition, cell-based therapies further highlight the role of PI3K–Akt signaling in autophagy regulation. In MCAO rats, olfactory mucosa-derived mesenchymal stem cells (OM-MSCs) activate neuronal PI3K–Akt signaling via secretion of pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF), thereby alleviating Golgi stress, reducing Golgi phosphoprotein 3 (GOLPH3) upregulation and Golgi fragmentation, and suppressing autophagy-related damage. These protective effects are markedly attenuated by PI3K inhibition, confirming pathway dependence.[72]

Collectively, PI3K-Akt signaling serves as a central regulator of autophagy following CI/R by restraining excessive autophagic activation, preserving mitochondrial and Golgi integrity, and supporting cellular repair processes. This fine-tuned modulation of autophagy substantially enhances neuronal survival and represents a critical molecular target for postischemic neuroprotection.

Reparative effects in vascular endothelial cells: Reconstitution of the BBB and promotion of angiogenesis

During ischemia-reperfusion, BMECs are subjected to multifaceted insults, including oxidative stress, proinflammatory cytokines, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These stressors contribute to BBB disruption, microvascular dysfunction, and progressive deterioration of brain tissue. The PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, in endothelial cells, reduces endothelial cell damage and promotes endothelial regeneration via multiple pathways by regulating mechanisms, such as antioxidant defense, anti-apoptosis, and angiogenesis. This facilitates post-reperfusion microenvironment remodeling, provides channels for the transport of repair factors, and creates a nutritive environment for cellular repair. Meanwhile, it promotes the downregulation of BBB permeability to reduce the infiltration of damaging factors. Collectively, these actions alleviate CI/R injury and promote multifaceted repair processes after damage.

Enhancement of endothelial cell viability

In the OGD/R model of mouse bEnd.3, curcumin treatment significantly activated the PI3K–Akt pathway, leading to a decrease in MDA and ROS levels, and an increase in the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD and GSH, suggesting that this pathway exerts an endothelial protective effect through an oxidative stress relief mechanism.[73]

In a coculture model of OGD/R neurons and BMECs cocultured with OM-MSCs, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway downregulated the mitochondrial fission protein Drp1 and autophagy- and apoptosis-related proteins, such as Bax and LC3-II, while upregulating Bcl-2, thereby inhibiting mitochondrial autophagy and endothelial cell apoptosis, and alleviating reperfusion injury.[72,74]

Stimulation of angiogenesis

The PI3K–Akt signaling pathway also plays a vital role in promoting microvascular reconstruction and functional recovery by regulating the expression of various angiogenic factors. In the BMECs model of OGD/R, exogenous fibroblast growth factor 17 (FGF17) significantly activated the PI3K–Akt pathway. This activation suppressed ROS production, enhanced the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, promoted phosphorylation of GSK-3β, and reduced the proinflammatory cytokine IL-6. In addition, FGF17 inhibited ferroptosis, thereby reducing endothelial apoptosis and preserving the integrity of the BBB.[75]

In addition, in hippocampal neurons, cortical neurons, and astrocytes, the PI3K–Akt pathway can also upregulate the expression of tight junction proteins claudin-5, occludin, and ZO-1, thereby reducing BBB permeability and enhancing barrier function.[76–78] The protective effects of FGF21 were abolished upon co-treatment with a PI3K inhibitor, confirming the dependence of these effects on PI3K–Akt pathway activation.

In the brain microvascular pericytes of the OGD/R model, Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen) and Panax notoginseng (Sanqi) induce the binding of Angiotensin-I (Ang-I) to the Tie-2 receptor, activate the PI3K-Akt pathway, enhance cell migration, and inhibit c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNK)/ERK/P38 signaling, thereby promoting the release of angiogenic factors. Consequently, cerebral tissue perfusion is improved, providing a fundamental nutritive metabolic environment for tissue repair.[79] Similarly, in the human brain microvascular endothelial cell (HBMEC) OGD/R model, chlorogenic acid (CGA) treatment upregulates VEGF expression through this pathway, reduces apoptosis, and enhances angiogenic capacity.[63]

Furthermore, in rat MCAO/R models and OGD/R-treated HBMECs, overexpression of miR-22 activates the PI3K–Akt pathway and upregulates VEGF and Ang-I expression, significantly enhancing the angiogenic potential of endothelial cells. PI3K inhibitors can further regulate this effect, suggesting that the PI3K–Akt signaling axis is a key mechanism for miR-22 regulation of angiogenesis.[62]

Synergistic promotion of angiogenesis with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway

During reperfusion, the PI3K–Akt pathway and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway act synergistically to further enhance VEGF expression in endothelial cells, promoting angiogenesis. VEGF binds to its receptor and activates the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, promoting Akt nuclear translocation and phosphorylation of transcription factors, thereby upregulating VEGF transcription expression. At the same time, Wnt3a and Wnt5a bind to frizzled receptors (FZD) and activate the Wnt pathway, inhibiting GSK-3β activity, reducing β-catenin degradation, and enhancing its stability. This synergistic mechanism further promotes vascular remodeling, which is crucial for maintaining cerebral tissue perfusion and barrier function.[65]

Regulatory role in microglia: Induction of anti-inflammatory polarization for microenvironment improvement

Following CI/R injury, microglia—the resident immune cells of the central nervous system—are rapidly activated and exert dual roles in both neuronal damage and tissue repair. As an important signaling regulatory axis, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway regulates the inflammatory and oxidative stress responses of microglia via multiple mechanisms—including the regulation of immune cell phenotype switching—and plays a key role in reperfusion-related neuroprotection.

Primarily, the PI3K–Akt pathway upregulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes by promoting the binding of p62 to Keap1, thereby dissociating the Nrf2 inhibitory complex, promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation, and activating the expression of downstream antioxidant genes, such as HO-1, NAD(P)H, and Nqo1. This mechanism significantly reduces oxidative stress under OGD/R conditions compared to controls. Meanwhile, p62’s upregulating effect on mTORC1 activity further participates in regulating autophagy levels. Additionally, p62 inhibits the activity of the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the expression of inflammatory factors, such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, and ultimately alleviates excessive inflammation through multi-pathway mechanisms.[54,73]

OGD/R treatment in a rat BV2 microglial model using metformin (Met) resulted in PI3K–Akt activation of the BV2 microglial pathway, leading to downregulation of the proinflammatory factors IL-1, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, thereby inhibiting microglial inflammatory activity. Assessment using a modified neurological severity score (mNSS) demonstrated that the PI3K–Akt pathway reduced CI/R injury in rats, and this protective effect was reversed by Akt inhibitors.[80,81]

In the microglial OGD/R model and BV2 microglia induced by lipopolysaccharides (LPS), treatment with quercetin and traditional Chinese medicine preparation: Erjingpill activated the PI3K-Akt pathway, inhibited the expression of glycolysis-related proteins, and promoted the conversion of microglial polarization from M1-type (proinflammatory) to M2-type (anti-inflammatory), thereby mitigating inflammation and improving the tissue microenvironment.[82,83]

In summary, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway regulates microglia via mechanisms including antioxidant defense, anti-inflammatory responses, and metabolic reprogramming. Serving as a key factor, it terminates the vicious cycle of inflammation, reduces damage from inflammatory storms, and initiates repair programs, collectively contributing to inflammation regulation and neuroprotection following CI/R injury.

Supportive role in astrocytes: Inhibition of autophagy-associated apoptosis and promotion of regenerative repair

During CI/R, astrocytes not only participate in maintaining the homeostasis of the neural microenvironment, but also play an important role in neural repair and inflammation regulation. The activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway has been shown to alleviate autophagic and apoptotic damage caused by reperfusion in astrocytes, promote their regeneration and functional recovery, thereby maintaining the fundamental network architecture of brain tissue, and play an auxiliary supportive role in the repair processes of various cells during the reperfusion period.

Studies have demonstrated that activation of the PI3K–Akt–mTOR signaling cascade effectively suppresses excessive autophagy in astrocytes, thereby reducing cellular injury.[84] In the rat CI/R model, cornin injection can significantly activate the PI3K–Akt pathway in astrocytes. Compared with the control group, treated animals showed upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and the autophagy adaptor protein p62, while the expression of pro-apoptotic and autophagy-related proteins—Bax, caspase-3, Beclin-1, and LC3-II—was markedly downregulated. These findings indicate that both apoptosis and autophagy were effectively suppressed. Furthermore, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway also promotes the expression of GFAP, suggesting enhanced astrocyte proliferation and activation, which contributes to the repair and remodeling of neural networks in the injured area.[84] As a physical supporter of neurons and a key component of the BBB, astrocytes receive regenerative protective effects from the PI3K–Akt pathway. These effects enable the PI3K–Akt pathway to serve as a reparative bridge connecting neurons and the BBB, thereby acting as a crucial integrator and protector of the microenvironment during the reperfusion period.

In summary, within astrocytes, the PI3K–Akt pathway primarily alleviates cerebral tissue damage induced by reperfusion through mechanisms including the inhibition of autophagy and apoptosis, as well as the promotion of cellular regeneration, demonstrating significant neuroprotective potential.

Overall, during the more complex pathological stage of reperfusion, the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway evolves a more sophisticated and elaborate response system. It exerts cell-specific protective functions within the NVU: In neurons, the focus shifts to rectifying severe oxidative and inflammatory damage caused by sudden reoxygenation and to the precise regulation of autophagy for homeostasis; in endothelial cells, it not only repairs the ischemia-damaged BBB by strengthening tight junctions but also promotes angiogenesis to counter further reperfusion injury; in microglia, pathway activation promotes polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype to alleviate inflammation; and in astrocytes, it enhances barrier-support functions and increases resistance to reperfusion-induced autophagic damage and dysfunction.

These cell-specific actions operate synergistically through the cerebral microenvironment, generating a network-based protective effect. Specifically, the PI3K–Akt pathway acts systemically throughout the injury process: Upstream, it reduces ROS generation at the source to mitigate oxidative stress; at mid- and downstream levels, it regulates nodes, such as mTORC1, to maintain autophagic balance and suppress mitochondrial damage, while also enhancing brain tissue resilience by upregulating protective proteins. These core mechanisms—including Wnt/β-catenin-mediated angiogenesis and Nrf2/HO-1-driven antioxidant protection—act synergistically to constitute a comprehensive defensive network against CI/R injury. This network-based regulatory mechanism thereby ensures the proper functioning of the entire cerebral microenvironment and facilitates subsequent tissue repair. Therefore, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway establishes a continuous, multi-cellular, and integrated protective network during reperfusion, which not only mitigates early CI/R injury damage but also provides critical support for long-term neural recovery, underscoring its pivotal role as a central regulatory pathway in reperfusion injury.

Crosstalk Between the PI3K–Akt and Other Signaling Pathways in the Regulation of CI and Reperfusion Injury

Synergy between PI3K–Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling: Promotion of angiogenesis and neural repair

During the CI phase, growth factors (e.g., insulin-like growth factor [IGF] and epidermal growth factor [EGF]) activate RTKs, initiating the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway. Activated Akt phosphorylates and inhibits GSK-3β in microglia,[85] which prevents β-catenin degradation. The subsequent accumulation and nuclear translocation of β-catenin enhance the transcription of Wnt target genes, leading to improved neural repair, cell proliferation, and differentiation.[86] During reperfusion, the PI3K–Akt and Wnt/β-catenin pathways form a synergistic regulatory network that collaboratively promotes angiogenesis. VEGF binding to its receptor, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR), activates the PI3K–Akt pathway. The activated Akt then translocates to the nucleus and phosphorylates specific transcription factors and other regulatory proteins, leading to the upregulation of VEGF gene transcription. Concurrently, Wnt ligands (e.g., Wnt3a and Wnt5a) bind to FZD, initiating Wnt signaling. This activation inhibits GSK-3β, stabilizes β-catenin, and thereby indirectly enhances VEGF transcription. Notably, VEGF can also feed back into the PI3K–Akt pathway, forming a positive regulatory loop. Both pathways converge at the GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling node, synergistically amplifying VEGF expression and significantly promoting angiogenesis and tissue repair.[65]

Synergy between PI3K–Akt and GSK-3β Regulation: A central node in neuroprotection and repair

The PI3K–Akt pathway mediates multifaceted and central regulatory functions in CI/R injury via its key downstream target, GSK-3β. Acting as a signaling hub, GSK-3β integrates survival and death signals from upstream pathways, and its activity directly determines neuronal fate.

During CI/R, the inhibition of GSK-3β by the PI3K-Akt pathway is critical for initiating endogenous repair mechanisms. The PI3K–Akt pathway acts synergistically with the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, converging at the GSK-3β node to significantly promote postischemic angiogenesis, neurogenesis, and tissue repair. This collaboration forms a positive feedback loop with the PI3K–Akt signaling, further activated by VEGF.[87]

Concurrently, the critical role of the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β signaling axis in CI/R injury can be inferred from its mechanistic similarities to chronic neurodegenerative diseases. Specifically, the inactivation of GSK-3β constitutes a central mechanism by which Akt suppresses the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. Under ischemia-reperfusion stress, impaired Akt activity diminishes the inhibitory phosphorylation of GSK-3β, leading to its hyperactivation. The activated GSK-3β directly triggers mitochondrial fragmentation and cytochrome c release through mechanisms, such as promoting the activation of the mitochondrial fission protein Drp1 and the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), thereby initiating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. This mechanism closely mirrors pathological processes in chronic neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting the universal importance of the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β axis in maintaining mitochondrial function and cell survival.[88,89]

Through its negative regulation of GSK-3β, the PI3K–Akt pathway provides dual protection in reperfusion injury by simultaneously promoting repair processes and inhibiting cell death. Although the precise regulatory network of this axis in CI/R requires further elucidation, targeting GSK-3β—for example, using inhibitors, such as TDZD-8 or lithium salts—has been demonstrated to effectively mitigate neuronal apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction, showing clear translational potential and representing one of the most promising neuroprotective strategies.[90,91]

Synergy between PI3K–Akt and the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in coordinated antioxidant defense

During reperfusion, Akt-mediated phosphorylation of Keap1 relieves its inhibition of Nrf2, driving the expression of antioxidant genes, such as HO-1, to mitigate oxidative stress. The resulting ROS-scavenging environment in turn prevents ROS-mediated inhibition of Akt, thereby sustaining its activity. Furthermore, Nrf2 may upregulate growth factors and pro-survival signals, further amplifying the PI3K–Akt pathway. Collectively, these interactions establish a critical positive feedback loop for antioxidant defense that enhances cellular resistance to cerebral ischemic injury and alleviates reperfusion damage.[61]

Crosstalk between the PI3K–Akt pathway and PTEN: Fine-tuned negative feedback regulation

The PI3K-Akt and PTEN signaling pathways are functionally antagonistic, maintaining intracellular signaling homeostasis. During reperfusion, IGF binding to its receptor (IGFR) in neurons activates the PI3K–Akt pathway, catalyzing the conversion of PIP2 to PIP3. This process is antagonized by PTEN, a key phosphatase that catalyzes the dephosphorylation of PIP3 back to PIP2. Thereby, PTEN negatively regulates PI3K–Akt signaling intensity and prevents cellular dysfunction caused by its overactivation.[92]

Crosstalk between the PI3K–Akt and mTOR pathways: Coordinated repair and dynamic homeostasis

During CI/R, a complex relationship of synergy and balance exists between the PI3K–Akt pathway and its key downstream target, the mTOR complex. Upstream neurotrophic factors, including BDNF and IGF-1, bind to their receptors (tropomyosin related kinase B (TrkB) and IGF-1R), activating the PI3K–Akt pathway. This activation leads to the phosphorylation and activation of mTORC1. Moderate mTORC1 activation suppresses excessive autophagy and promotes essential protein synthesis, thereby supporting cellular repair.[93–95] However, this pathway contains a precise negative feedback mechanism: Activated mTORC1 phosphorylates insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1), leading to its degradation and consequent feedback inhibition of upstream PI3K–Akt signaling.[96] Although this mechanism prevents excessive signaling activation, overly strong or ill-timed feedback can prematurely attenuate crucial survival signals and impair the clearance of damaged cellular components by excessively inhibiting beneficial autophagic flux, ultimately exacerbating oxidative stress and promoting cell death.[97]

Crosstalk between PI3K–Akt and the NF-κB pathway in immunoregulation: Bidirectional regulation of cell fate and inflammatory outcomes

The interaction between the PI3K–Akt and NF-κB pathways in CI/R injury exhibits marked temporal and contextual dependence, collectively determining the balance between inflammation and cell survival. During the ischemic phase, energy depletion inhibits the PI3K–Akt pathway, while NF-κB is primarily activated directly by stress signals, such as ROS and calcium overload, initiating early proinflammatory cytokine expression and driving the injury process.[98] During the reperfusion phase, proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF and IL, in microglia bind to their receptors (TNFR and IL-R), recruiting adaptor proteins and activating downstream signaling events including the inhibitor of kappa B kinase (IKK) complex, thereby promoting nuclear translocation of NF-κB transcription factors and driving the expression of various inflammatory mediators. Concurrently, the co-activated PI3K–Akt pathway negatively regulates inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB activity, forming an intrinsic feedback inhibition loop.[99,100] In neurons, with the restoration of neurotrophic factor signaling, such as BDNF and IGF-1, the PI3K–Akt pathway is reactivated, exerting dual regulation on NF-κB signaling. On one hand, strongly activated Akt can directly phosphorylate and inhibit IKK activity, or indirectly attenuate NF-κB transcriptional activity by inhibiting downstream targets, such as GSK-3β. This mechanism constitutes an important negative feedback loop aimed at limiting excessive NF-κB activation triggered by ROS bursts and early inflammatory factors, thereby mitigating the storm of proinflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects. On the other hand, under moderately controlled activation conditions, Akt-guided NF-κB signaling may shift toward exerting neuroprotective functions. Akt can direct the NF-κB pathway to initiate the expression of a series of anti-apoptotic genes, including Bcl-2, cellular FLICE-like inhibitory protein (c-FLIP), and cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein (cIAP) family (cIAPs), through non-IKK-dependent mechanisms, such as direct regulation of the p65 subunit. At this stage, NF-κB activation no longer primarily drives destructive inflammation but transforms into a program promoting cell survival. Thus, the crosstalk between these two pathways constitutes a key dynamic balancing node that influences the ultimate outcome of injury and repair.[101]

Notably, the PI3K–Akt pathway also plays a crucial fine-tuned bidirectional regulatory role in the inflammatory microenvironment of reperfusion injury by precisely modulating immune cells, particularly Tregs. This regulation also exhibits cellular specificity and complexity. Within Tregs, overactivation of PI3K–Akt signaling phosphorylates and inhibits their key transcription factor FoxO1, leading to downregulation of the core identity-defining protein Foxp3, thereby impairing Treg immunosuppressive function and stability. This indicates that in this specific cell population, moderate inhibition of PI3K-Akt signaling is actually beneficial for maintaining Treg stability and function.[102–105] However, from the perspective of the overall pathological environment, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway indirectly supports Treg function by shaping a favorable immune microenvironment. For instance, in neurons and glial cells, Akt activation directly inhibits apoptosis and alleviates oxidative stress. Meanwhile, it suppresses microglia/macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype and promotes their shift to the M2 phenotype, thereby reducing the production of proinflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, at the source. This suppressed overall inflammatory environment creates favorable conditions for Treg recruitment and function. Tregs, by secreting inhibitory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β), directly act on microglia to inhibit their production of proinflammatory factors, thereby further enhancing the immunosuppressive state and collectively limiting inflammatory spread.[106] PI3K–Akt signaling in other immune cells, such as dendritic cells, has also been shown to induce a “tolerogenic” phenotype, thereby promoting Treg differentiation and expansion.[107]

Thus, the PI3K–Akt pathway influences the outcomes of injury and repair through two principal mechanisms: Directly modulating the cytokine storm via crosstalk with NF-κB, and indirectly shaping the inflammatory landscape through its fine-tuned, bidirectional regulation of immune cells, such as Tregs. Together, these two arms of regulation form a critical dynamic balancing point that decisively shapes the final outcomes of injury and repair.[107]

Crosstalk between PI3K–Akt and the MAPK/ERK pathway: From synergistic survival to antagonistic imbalance

The interplay between the PI3K–Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways in CI/R injury exhibits distinct temporal dynamics, transitioning from initial synergistic survival to late-phase antagonistic imbalance, profoundly influencing neuronal fate. During the ischemic phase, energy metabolism collapse leads to strong suppression of both pathways, depriving cells of key pro-survival signals and shifting fate determination toward more dominant pathways, such as necroptosis.

During the initial phase of reperfusion, residual neurotrophic factors or those newly synthesized by reactive astrocytes (e.g., BDNF, IGF-1, and NGF) bind to their RTKs, triggering the concurrent activation of both the PI3K–Akt and MAPK/ERK pathways. Akt phosphorylates Bad, resulting in its inactivation and cytoplasmic retention, and directly inhibits Caspase-9 and GSK-3β via phosphorylation. Simultaneously, the ERK pathway complements Akt by phosphorylating and activating the transcription factor cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) to enhance the anti-apoptotic function of Bcl-2, as well as by phosphorylating the pro-apoptotic protein Bim to promote its degradation. This complementary interaction leads to synergistic inhibition of apoptosis and consolidation of cell survival. However, during the late phase of reperfusion, under pathological conditions characterized by persistent oxidative stress and inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF-α), overactivation of both pathways triggers antagonistic crosstalk. Specifically, hyperactivated Akt phosphorylates RAF to inhibit its kinase activity, thereby negatively regulating downstream ERK signaling.[108] Furthermore, sustained ERK activation phosphorylates IRS-1, promoting its degradation and thereby exerting negative feedback inhibition on the PI3K-Akt pathway. This mutual inhibition represents a physiological mechanism to prevent excessive signaling activation. However, under pathological reperfusion conditions, such negative feedback may cause premature termination of survival signals, ultimately leading vulnerable neurons to succumb to persistent inflammatory and oxidative stress.[109]

In the crosstalk among the aforementioned signaling pathways, the presence of feedback mechanisms in the interaction between the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway and the PTEN, mTOR, and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways conversely reveals the inherent risks of excessive activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling itself. This represents a major controversy and unresolved issue in current neuroprotection research. In mature, post-mitotic neurons, strong mitogen-like signals (such as hyperactivated Akt) may inappropriately drive the re-expression of cyclins, causing neurons to abnormally re-enter the cell cycle. However, neurons lack the ability to complete cell division, and this reentry into the cell cycle has been widely recognized as a key pathway leading to neuronal apoptosis or degeneration.[110] In astrocytes, some studies have shown that overactive PI3K-Akt signaling promotes reactive gliosis, tends to drive their transformation into subtypes unfavorable for neural repair, potentially promotes the excessive formation of glial scars, and thereby physically and chemically impedes axonal regeneration and neural circuit reconstruction.[111]

The core of the networked regulatory mechanism of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway, as elucidated in this chapter, lies in its role as a central regulator of cell survival and metabolism and its ability to form a complex regulatory network in CI/R injury and neurodegenerative diseases. This network arises from extensive crosstalk with key downstream targets like GSK-3β and multiple signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin, Nrf2, and mTOR), extending into the dimension of immune regulation, such as the fine-tuning of Treg cell stability.[112] The functionality of this network is context-dependent and converges on core pathological processes, including mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis, indicating its role as an integrated system determining neuronal fate.[113] In the regulatory network of CI/R injury, the PI3K/Akt/GSK-3β axis plays a dominant role as the core hub: It directly determines the cell survival baseline and establishes a platform for cross-pathway crosstalk. Meanwhile, the Wnt/β-catenin, Nrf2/HO-1, and mTOR pathways, as primary synergistic and effector pathways, play key executive roles in repair and defense. The NF-κB and MAPK/ERK pathways act as important variable actors in inflammation and stress responses, while bidirectional regulatory pathways, such as PTEN, ensure the homeostasis and balance of the entire network. This network view underscores that future therapeutic interventions must transcend single-target approaches and focus on developing precise, multi-targeted, temporally controlled, and cell-type-specific strategies. Only by prioritizing targeting the core axis (such as GSK-3β) and synergistically regulating primary synergistic and crosstalk pathways based on the injury phase can precise and effective neuroprotection and repair be achieved.[114–116]

Therapeutic Strategies

Therapeutic approaches during the CI phase

The PI3K–Akt pathway plays a pivotal role in neuroprotection throughout the CI phase. Studies have shown that this pathway mediates brain tissue repair and angiogenesis through various mechanisms. For instance, the PI3K–Akt pathway contributes to neuroprotection by modulating oxidative and acidotic stress responses. In CI models, monomethyl lithospermate (MOL), a constituent of Shenxiong Tongmai granule, activates PI3K–Akt, facilitating the nuclear accumulation of Nrf2, a master transcription factor that regulates antioxidant enzyme expression. Activation of Nrf2 significantly decreases ROS levels, thereby providing neuroprotection.[117] Similarly, in HT22 neuronal ischemia models, intravenous injection of lyoniresinol activates PI3K–Akt signaling, increases the activity of the antioxidative enzyme SOD, reduces ROS production and MDA accumulation, effectively mitigating oxidative stress.[118] Regarding angiogenesis, the pathway promotes vascular regeneration by upregulating multiple growth factors. In MCAO rat models, administration of bone marrow mononuclear cells and Ad-VEGF-modified mesenchymal stem cells activates neuronal PI3K–Akt signaling, resulting in increased expression of VEGF, BDNF, and Ang-I. This enhances angiogenesis, reduces reactive gliosis, and supports neuronal regeneration. Notably, these reparative effects are reversed upon treatment with PI3K inhibitors.[44,119,120] However, in cases of prolonged or extensive injury, neovascularization is slow and immature, limiting the complete reversal of CI phase-induced damage.[30,119]

Recent research on CI treatment strategies increasingly focuses on the crosstalk between PI3K–Akt and other signaling pathways, exploring combination therapies to strengthen neuroprotection. For example, in rat MCAO models, resveratrol activates the JAK3/STAT2 pathway within the ischemic penumbra, which subsequently upregulates the PI3K–Akt/mTOR pathway. This cascade downregulates pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3 while upregulating anti-apoptotic Bcl-2, thereby inhibiting apoptosis, providing neuroprotection, and conferring ischemic tolerance to the brain.[121] Similarly, sodium butyrate activates the GPR41 protein-coupled receptor pathway, which provides neuroprotection by reducing neuronal apoptosis and enhancing the therapeutic effect of butyrate.[29]

In summary, the PI3K–Akt pathway is critically involved in the treatment of CI/R injury. Coordinated activation of multiple signaling pathways through combination therapies offers promising prospects for improving ischemic injury and promoting neural repair.

Therapeutic strategies during the CI/R phase

Given the pivotal regulatory role of the PI3K–Akt pathway in CI/R injury, this pathway has emerged as a critical therapeutic target in ischemic stroke research. Drug development and gene intervention strategies targeting PI3K-Akt show promising clinical potential.[122–124] During the CI/R phase, activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway plays a key neuroprotective role by promoting antioxidative responses, reducing inflammation, and supporting tissue repair. For example, in hypoxic primary cortical neurons, (-)-tetrahydroalanine activates PI3K–Akt to enhance autophagy, improve cell viability, and prevent excessive autophagy.[125] In a rat CI/R injury model, administration of human OM-MSC activates PI3K-Akt, thereby reducing oxidative stress and alleviating Golgi apparatus stress.[72] In a rat CI/R model, compounds, such as Scutellarin and Met, activate PI3K–Akt, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, decreasing ROS production, and mitigating neuroinflammation.[80,126] Furthermore, miR-199a-5p injection activates PI3K–Akt, leading to reduced oxidative stress and inflammation, which helps maintain BBB integrity.[127] In the MCAO/R rat model, Danhong injection activates PI3K–Akt, reducing neuronal apoptosis through regulation of p53 expression.[128]

The PI3K–Akt pathway also interacts with other signaling cascades, such as Nrf2 and NF-κB, to enhance neuroprotection and promote recovery. For instance, puerarin activates PI3K–Akt to facilitate Nrf2 nuclear translocation, thereby decreasing ROS and protecting neurons.[54] Quercetin modulates the PI3K/Akt/NF-κB pathway, promoting anti-inflammatory effects and preserving BBB integrity.[82]

In summary, PI3K–Akt activation provides a theoretical foundation for combination therapies targeting multiple protective mechanisms, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes in CI/R injury.

Summary

The PI3K–Akt signaling pathway is one of the few critical pathways with neuroprotective effects, significantly alleviating tissue damage caused by CI/R injury by regulating key processes, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, autophagy, and apoptosis, BBB integrity, and vascular regeneration across multiple cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, and pericytes.[129–131] However, this pathway exhibits distinctly different regulatory characteristics during the ischemic and reperfusion phases of brain injury, providing new targeted directions for precision stroke therapy.[82]

During the ischemic phase, the PI3K–Akt pathway primarily mitigates ongoing brain tissue damage by suppressing oxidative stress and inflammation in neurons and microglia, modulating moderate autophagy in astrocytes, protecting BBB function, and reducing endothelial apoptosis to maintain vascular stability. In contrast, during the reperfusion phase, the pathway more actively regulates microglial polarization, inhibits endothelial cell-mediated inflammatory cascades, stabilizes the BBB structure, and promotes neurological recovery by enhancing VEGF-mediated angiogenesis and neural progenitor cell repair capacity.[54,73,80,92,117]

Nevertheless, abnormal or excessive activation of the pathway may result in metabolic imbalances, exacerbated inflammation, and abnormal angiogenesis, leading to adverse effects.[132] Under these conditions, the PTEN pathway exerts a braking regulatory function to ensure appropriate activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway, thereby maximizing the protection and repair of brain tissue through integrated network regulatory mechanisms. Critically, the PI3K–Akt pathway interacts with key pathways—including Wnt/β-catenin,[133] Nrf2/HO-1,[134] MAPK/ERK,[108] and mTOR[97]—to form a multifaceted, multi-nodal signaling network across different pathological stages. This integration ensures timely and proportionate responses to injury signals within diverse cellular contexts. Specifically, the PI3K–Akt pathway exerts phase-dependent regulatory functions. During the ischemic phase, it initiates preliminary protection to counteract oxidative stress and promote NVU repair.[46] Upon reperfusion, the pathway amplifies its protective effects, precisely modulates cellular metabolism and inflammatory balance,[135] and deploys negative feedback mechanisms to curb the burst of ROS,[136] thereby achieving holistic protection of brain tissue. Concurrently, PTEN acts as a key regulator that fine-tunes the activation state of the PI3K–Akt pathway, preventing its overactivation and the consequent adverse effects, such as metabolic dysregulation and pathological angiogenesis.[92]

Therefore, elucidating the central role of the PI3K–Akt pathway within this complex network and the molecular specifics of its crosstalk with other pathways is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the pathophysiology of cerebral ischemic injury and for the subsequent development of novel therapeutic strategies characterized by precise timing, cell-specific targeting, and multi-pathway coordination.

Perspectives

Regulatory role of PI3K–Akt pathway in heart-brain organ crosstalk

Heart–brain crosstalk refers to the bidirectional communication between the heart and the brain through neural, hormonal, immune, and inflammatory pathways. Dysfunction or injury in one organ can directly or indirectly lead to damage in the other.[137,138] In recent years, the PI3K–Akt pathway has emerged as a crucial regulator in the crosstalk between the brain and heart.[139] It functions as a “molecular switch” within the bidirectional communication of the brain–heart axis, where its activation or inhibition state determines whether the outcome of this crosstalk is protective or detrimental, playing a key protective role in both the heart and the brain.[140]

In the brain-to-heart axis, acute brain injury—particularly involving the insular cortex—can lead to autonomic nervous system dysfunction, triggering an exaggerated sympathetic storm. This results in the release of large amounts of catecholamines (e.g., norepinephrine), which act directly on the myocardium and cause adverse outcomes, such as calcium overload, vasospasm, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis.[141] In this process, Akt phosphorylation in cardiomyocytes inhibits pro-apoptotic proteins, such as Bad and Caspase-9, protecting against catecholamine-induced apoptosis. Concurrently, Akt activation of eNOS stimulates NO production, which alleviates catecholamine-induced vasospasm and improves cardiac perfusion, thereby countering the sympathetic storm.[142,143] Furthermore, ischemic brain injury prompts endothelial cells to release microvesicles laden with inflammatory factors and mitochondrial components. These microvesicles enter the circulation, compromise the BBB,[144] and facilitate the entry of inflammatory mediators into the periphery. This cascade triggers damage in distal organs, particularly causing cardiac inflammation, fibrosis, and dysfunction.[145–147] The PI3K–Akt pathway exerts a central regulatory function in the inflammatory amplification mechanism along the brain–heart axis by attenuating cardiac inflammatory injury through suppression of proinflammatory signaling pathways, such as NF-κB.[148] Furthermore, brain injury can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in glucocorticoid excess (e.g., cortisol) that may contribute to cardiac metabolic disturbances and functional impairment. Additionally, PI3K–Akt signaling participates in regulating HPA axis activity, where moderate Akt activation helps maintain axis homeostasis and suppresses excessive stress responses.[149]

Similarly, within the heart-to-brain axis, cardiac disorders (e.g., heart failure and myocardial infarction) reduce cardiac output, which can induce brain injury through cerebral hypoperfusion, systemic hypoxia, or embolic events. Throughout this process of brain-derived cardiac injury (cerebral–cardiac syndrome), the PI3K-Akt pathway acts as a key protector on the cardiac side.[150,151] In cerebrovascular endothelial cells, Akt activation promotes vasodilation via eNOS, maintains cerebral perfusion, enhances the expression of tight junction proteins, stabilizes the BBB structure, and reduces the entry of harmful substances into the brain. In neurons, activated Akt exerts its classic anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects, directly protecting neurons from ischemic and hypoxic damage. It also promotes signal transduction of neurotrophic factors, such as BDNF, supporting neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity.[152,153] Consequently, targeted activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy for cerebral and cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury. Owing to the cell-type and context-dependent nature of this pathway’s effects, future therapeutic approaches must emphasize precision medicine, incorporating timing-specific interventions and cell-selective targeting for comprehensive multi-system protection.

Innovative therapeutic strategies targeting the PI3K–Akt pathway

Although the neuroprotective potential of the PI3K–Akt pathway in CI/R injury is increasingly recognized, clinical translation faces challenges, including precise modulation, low delivery efficiency, and potential side effects. Future studies should focus on:

Optimization of small molecule agonists/inhibitors

Developing highly selective PI3K or Akt modulators to enhance therapeutic efficacy while avoiding metabolic disturbances and tumorigenic risks.[154] The in-depth investigation of this pathway in diseases, such as cancer, provides a valuable reference for drug development (e.g., highly selective allosteric modulators) in the field of CI/R injury.[155] Currently, small-molecule drugs targeting the PI3K–Akt pathway are actively under development in fields such as oncology, but their direct application to CI/R injury faces significant obstacles. The main bottlenecks lie in: First, insufficient selectivity. PI3K–Akt pathway inhibitors that have entered clinical trials exhibit risks of systemic metabolic disorders (such as hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia) and immunosuppression, which contradicts the goal required for CI/R injury: Brief and controllable activation of the pathway in the ischemic region. Therefore, developing novel, highly selective allosteric modulators is crucial. Second, a low BBB penetration rate. Cutting-edge solutions focus on constructing brain-targeted delivery systems. For example, researchers are using nanocarriers modified with targeting peptides (e.g., transferrin receptor [TfR] or low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 [LRP1] targeting peptides) to cross the BBB via receptor-mediated endocytosis; designing prodrugs activated by brain-specific enzymes; or exploring local delivery techniques, such as intranasal administration—all aimed at increasing drug concentration in the brain while reducing systemic exposure risk.[156–158]

Genetic modulation strategies

By utilizing CRISPR-Cas9 or adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors to target and regulate core pathway regulators (e.g., PTEN and Akt1), neuroprotective effects can be enhanced, offering new insights for achieving long-acting and precise neuroprotection. The core challenges of this strategy lie in ensuring the specificity, safety, and efficient delivery of editing. Future efforts will require leveraging more advanced vector engineering and targeted technologies to mitigate off-target effects and immunogenicity risks.[159]

Multi-pathway synergistic therapy

Co-activating signaling pathways, such as PI3K–Akt, Nrf2, and Wnt/β-catenin, enhances the synergistic effects of antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue repair responses, forming a more robust endogenous protective network. Exploring the interaction mechanisms among these pathways and their optimal joint sequential timing is the key to designing efficient combination therapies.[132,133]

Future research should focus on the following three directions: First, given the distinct functional differences of the PI3K–Akt pathway in neurons, endothelial cells, and glial cells, further clarification of its cell-type-specific regulatory mechanisms is required. Second, as the pathway exhibits spatiotemporal dynamics during the ischemic and reperfusion phases, defining the optimal intervention time windows for each stage is critical for refining therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, based on its networked crosstalk with multiple pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin, Nrf2/HO-1, and mTOR, developing multi-target, temporally coordinated combination therapeutic strategies may hold greater neuroprotective potential and clinical translation prospects than single-pathway interventions.

Furthermore, integrating multi-omics technologies, such as single-cell and proteomics analysis, will deepen understanding of the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of PI3K–Akt at different CI/R injury stages. Employing human-derived brain and heart organoids alongside animal models will further enhance the clinical feasibility and translational potential of these intervention strategies.
