Implementation and evaluation of a psychological first aid program to manage post-traumatic stress injuries among Canadian police officers
Authors: Séphora Minjoz, Delphine Collin-Vézina, Christine Genest, R Nicholas Carleton, Rosemary Ricciardelli, Sandra Moll, Geneviève St-Hilaire, Steve Geoffrion
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolice officers are exposed to potentially psychological traumatic events and are at high risk of developing post-traumatic stress injuries (PTSI). Development and wide implementation of best practices for managing PTSI are needed. The psychological first aid (PFA) framework encompasses trauma-informed knowledge to guide the development of best practices. Based on the framework and on a pilot performed among Canadian police officers, we propose a PFA program including: 1) PTSI awareness e-learning; 2) PFA training provided by local trainers; and, 3) peer-to-peer PFA intervention to mitigate PTSI in police officers. The study was designed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a large-scale PFA program implementation among Canadian police officers.MethodsA multi-phase mixed-methods participatory action research study is being performed in five phases according to the PFA implementation and the dynamic sustainability frameworks. The program 1) was adapted to environmental needs; 2) is progressively deployed; and, to be evaluated for 3) feasibility; 4) effectiveness; and, 5) sustainability. Local trainers (N = 10) were trained to then train PFA providers (N = 322 police officers) who could then administer the intervention to police officers as needed (i.e., PFA recipients). The e-learning is being co-developed and will be disseminated. Program feasibility (i.e., acceptability, demand, practicality, implementation) will be assessed through interviews with trainers (n = 10), providers (n = 30), and recipients (n = 20). The program effectiveness will be assessed by tracking changes in PTSI literacy and stigma among officers who complete the e-learning (n = 5700) using pre-post e-learning questionnaires. Pre-post training questionnaires with participating providers (n = 175) will track perceived competence to provide the intervention. Pre-post intervention questionnaires with recipients (n = 64) will track changes in putative protective factors (i.e., professional quality of life, work safety, coping, sense of efficacy, sense of hope) and symptoms of PTSI (i.e., anxiety-, depressive-, and post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms).DiscussionThe PFA program is designed to help officers to recognize PTSI, promote self-care strategies and help-seeking, enhance organizational support, and expand psychological support. Background

Potentially psychological traumatic event (PPTE; i.e., a stressful event with the potential to cause trauma-related mental health conditions [1]) exposures are frequent in the police environment. Police officers routinely encounter high-risk and high-stress events (e.g., exposure to deceased bodies, killing someone in the line of duty [2, 3]), and may be exposed to extreme PPTE (e.g., terrorist attacks, plane crashes [4]). Over 90% of Canadian police officers report numerous PPTE exposures, especially sudden violent or accidental deaths, serious transportation accidents, and physical assaults [5]. Repeated PPTE exposures may compromise physical and mental health [6–8], particularly by increasing the risk of developing post-traumatic stress injuries (PTSI) [9–12], PTSI include post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), symptoms of depression-, anxiety- and trauma related disorders, substance abuse, and suicidal behaviors (i.e., ideation, planning, attempts, [5, 13, 14]). A recent meta-analysis involving police officers (n = 272 463) from 24 countries estimated the prevalence to be 14.6% for major depressive disorder and symptoms of depression, 14.2% for PTSD, 9.6% for generalized anxiety disorder, 8.5% for suicidal ideation, and 5% for alcohol addiction [15]. In Canada, 64.7% of national and 36.7% of provincial police officers screened positive for one or more mental health disorders [16, 17]. The PTSI prevalence among police appeared to exceed the general population [18]. PTSD symptom reports (i.e., ranging from the past week to past month) indicated that 47.7% of national and 19.5% of provincial Canadian police officers experienced PTSD symptoms [16, 17], as compared to only 6.1% in the Canadian general population [19].

Sleep troubles [20], poorer quality of life [21], cognitive impairments [22], and absenteeism [23] have also been reported in studies of police officers. The challenging work conditions may impact professional commitment [22], job turnover [24], and interactions with citizens [25]. The interacting workplace challenges also increase safety risks for police, their colleagues, their families, and their communities.

Mental health in police settings represents a public health concern with critical consequences [26]. Efforts are needed to develop and widely implement best practices for managing PTSI among police officers, ensuring their continued ability to protect the population [9, 27, 28].

There are several long-standing mental health training programs (e.g., Critical Incident Stress Management, Psychological First Aid, Road to Mental Readiness) designed to minimize the effects of PPTE exposures [29]. Nevertheless, public safety professionals believe all such programs have room for improvement [30]. Furthermore, despite the recognition of police officers being at high risk for PTSI, proactive programs including training and early intervention designed to mitigate PPTE exposure sequalae remain scarce in police environment, with very little empirical evidence for effectiveness [13, 15, 31]. There are new training programs being developed, deployed, and tested (e.g., emotional resilience skills training [32]) as part of continuous improvement efforts to provide proactive and responsive evidence-based solutions to support the mental health of public safety professionals. As the work continues, additional systematic evaluation is warranted to further assess and improve upon potentially complementary programs.

Psychological First Aid (PFA)

PFA is a longstanding early intervention framework designed to address post-PPTE exposure based on the conservation of resources theory that suggests stress reactions depend on losses and gains in personal or environmental resources [33]. PFA includes successful proactive practices aimed at reinforcing existing resources or adding new resources to limit stress reactions. Most PFA programs are designed to foster five putative protective factors: restoring calm, fostering a sense of safety, efficacy and hope; and, fostering social support [34]. The PFA framework includes evidence-informed psychoeducation designed to support proactive PTSI mitigation strategies [35, 36].

The PFA Field Operations Guide was initially designed by the National Child Traumatic Stress Network (NCTSN) for first responders to provide guidelines to help survivors [35]. Administered soon after a PPTE exposure (0–48 h), and in the subsequent days or weeks following a PPTE exposure, PFA is designed to reduce immediate distress, enhance short- and mid-term adaptative functioning, and mitigate PTSI [35, 36]. Most PFA variants include teaching people how to attend to immediate basic needs of others, reduce acute distress, and share basic coping strategies (e.g., promoting sleep). PFA is intended to provide psychoeducation (e.g., about stress reactions), comfort, emotional care, and instrumental support, with assistance provided in a stepwise fashion tailored to the person’s needs [37]. PFA typically comprises of eight core actions: 1 establishing contact in a non-intrusive and caring way; 2 improving safety and comfort; 3 stabilizing; 4 collecting information about reactions, needs and concerns; 5 providing practical assistance in response to the needs and problems; 6 referring to appropriate social support (e.g., family members, friends); 7 providing information on stress reactions and coping strategies to reduce distress; and, 8 referring to appropriate services. There are researchers and clinicians who consider PFA an evidence-informed early intervention, but not a therapeutic treatment [38, 39]. PFA is adaptable, can be quickly mastered with a focus on providing early aid, and can be delivered by non-mental health practitioners [36].

PFA effectiveness

In providers

Previous reviews of PFA effectiveness note improvements in the knowledge and confidence of PFA providers to support people in acute distress [40, 41]. There is evidence that, when compared to no training, PFA can enhance mental health literacy, skills required to respond appropriately to PPTE, and confidence to respond to PPTE among police officers trained to provide PFA [42, 43]. Higher PFA skills, knowledge and attitudes (d = 0.35), self-efficacy (d = 0.20), and professional quality of life (d = 0.04), were observed 2 months after PFA training in emergency medical responders when compared to no-training [44].

In recipients

We could not find any studies evaluating PFA intervention effects among police populations who were recipients of this intervention. Meta-analytic results from evaluating resilience promotion and multimodal programs, such as PFA, evidence small to modest reductions in PTSI symptoms in at-risk workers [45]. There are also reports of positive effects (e.g., quality of life, coping skills) and reduced anxiety-, depressive-, and trauma-related symptoms [46, 47] in populations exposed to PPTE. A single-arm PFA clinical trial using a single PFA session, delivered 3 days after a company fire, increased self-reported resilience of 65 government agency employees from pre-intervention to 1-week follow-up [48]. Another single-arm PFA clinical trial using a single PFA session delivered 6 months after a climatic disaster with 98 survivors evidenced that, when assessed immediately after the PFA session, participants reported enhanced self-efficacy for using skills to cope with the disaster [49]. Another single-arm PFA clinical trial reported reduced symptoms of PTSD and major depressive disorder among adult survivors of a recent (< 6 months) trauma (e.g., violence) at 3- and 6-month follow-up [50]. A single PFA session conducted with healthy adults who experienced a stressful event (e.g., laboratory stress task) found that participants had reduced symptoms of anxiety when compared to active control conditions (e.g., group conversation, social acknowledgement [51, 52]). Despite reports of PFA interventions producing positive impacts, mixed effects have also been reported, such as a study noting no differences in PTSD and depression symptoms [53].

Implementation of PFA

There is substantial stigma and barriers to mental health care among police officers [54, 55], nevertheless, PFA implementation among police officers is achievable. In a pilot study, we demonstrated PFA implementation feasibility within a Canadian provincial police organizational unit [56]. Per the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence PTSD guidelines [57] for treating PTSD among first responders [58], the pilot incorporated participatory action research (PAR) to tailor a specific NCTSN PFA program to meet the needs of police (for more details, see [56]). In the pilot, there were 26 police officers trained by an expert to provide PFA. After providing PFA for more than 6 months, providers and recipients reported that the program was well-suited to policing, filled psychological support gaps, and was considered acceptable and practical to encourage help-seeking, and reduced stigma [56]. Participants also provided feedback regarding the barriers and facilitators to PFA program implementation and effectiveness.

Facilitators

Using a co-created PFA specifically for police reportedly made implementation easier. In general, access to occupation-specific content can increase program uptake [59]. Customizing programs is recommended for maximizing feasibility of resilience programs in high-risk organizations (i.e., involving regular PPTE exposures [60, 61]). The pilot study participants also suggested peer support providers of PFA facilitated program implementation because peers were deemed credible, trustworthy, and relatable [56]. Peer support can be helpful in normalizing experiences, increasing hope, and decreasing stigma in first responders [62, 63]. Police officers are typically more receptive to peer-to-peer mental health resources than external resources [47, 62]. Peer-to-peer programs can also be scheduled while on-duty, which can facilitate implementation [61]. Interventions scheduled during off-duty hours may be harder to implement for many reasons (e.g., reduced time spent with family, employment contracting violations; [64]). Our pilot reported peers were in best position to enable openness in police officers and help transition to second-level care. Peer support is also recognized as a principle of care by the NICE PTSD guidelines and recommended in high-risk organizations with interdependent culture [65, 66].

Barriers

Our pilot study results [56] were congruent with results from other studies (e.g., [67]) in highlighting how insufficient time, not enough providers, confidentiality concerns, and limited program awareness constitute barriers for implementing and sustaining any mental health program in policing. A meta-analysis reported stigma, scheduling concerns, and not knowing where to acquire help were the most frequently endorsed barriers accessing mental health care among first responders [54]. Mental health accessibility for police officers involves being able to seek and perceive care [68], and being able to self-identify experiences of compromised mental health which would benefit from care [67].

PFA implementation in policing appears feasible, but there has been no large-scale systematic evaluation of feasibility and effectiveness. An existing evaluation includes a single urban unit of a police organization with access to PFA in a pilot study preceding the current study [56]. A large-scale evaluation is needed in order to expand psychological support and improve the capability of police officers to identify experiences of mental distress, and to promote self-care and help-seeking strategies [59, 67]. There is a gap in empirical studies assessing the effectiveness and safety of PFA in ecologically valid settings [38, 40, 46, 47, 53]. PFA needs to be examined as a stand-alone intervention, coupled with experiential insights from both recipients and providers. Furthermore, only a few studies have been conducted in police populations [42, 43, 56], and none have evaluated PFA intervention effectiveness. Based on the PFA framework and our pilot results [56], we designed a large-scale evaluation to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of an enhanced version of our pilot PFA program to mitigate PTSI among police officers.

An enhanced PFA program for Canadian provincial police officers

The enhanced version of our pilot PFA program includes three modules: 1) PTSI awareness e-learning; 2) PFA training delivered by “local” trainers; and, 3) a peer-to-peer PFA intervention.

PTSI awareness E-learning

PTSI awareness e-learning (5 sessions, each 15 min) is currently being co-developed by the research team and the pedagogical center of the University of Montreal in collaboration with the Quebec Provincial Police i.e., the Sûreté du Québec (SQ). Development is based on the PFA framework, previous research on public safety professionals [55, 69, 70], and input from users involved in mental health app development [71]. The e-learning is designed to provide police officers with psychoeducation about PTSI, how to recognize signs of distress in themselves and their colleagues, and promote self-care strategies and help-seeking. The e-learning format facilitates knowledge translation, is cost-effective, and brief; all features that are intended to reduce barriers officers encounter when using mental health programs [54, 56, 72].

PFA training

The face-to-face training (3 sessions of 3h30 + 1 refresher session of 2 h) in the delivery of the peer-to-peer PFA intervention provides police officers with psychoeducation about responding to PPTE in general and for responding to colleagues experiencing acute distress. The refresher session is performed 2 months after the initial training and a community of practice convenes monthly to support continuing education in providing PFA. The NCTSN training was adapted for police officers, translated into French, and tested in our pilot study [56]. The PFA training adaptation was based on the authors’ experience with police officers seeking psychotherapy services at the Montreal Trauma Studies Center. Rather than mental health professionals or disaster response workers offering the training, we developed the PFA training for delivery by the SQ’s internal trainers. The PFA training adaptation aligns with NCTSN training and includes the eight PFA core actions (see Supplementary Material), as well as additional problem solving material in action five, a section on PTSI reactions, and a new section added to action seven on the theoretical model for developing or maintaining trauma reactions [73, 74]. Our training also aligns with the NCTSN training by using clinical vignettes, role-plays, and short video clips on PTSI reactions, PFA administration, and typical victim needs after PPTE exposure. The clinical vignettes were adapted to the police context (e.g., the scenario describes a police officer intervening in intimate partner violence which is more common than addressing a natural disaster). A toolbox is provided containing a PFA field operation guide and wallet card, a decision-tree, an action-tracking grid, a worksheet for practicing the problem-solving technique, educational sheets (e.g., descriptions of PPTE reactions; of strategies to cope with PPTE), and a list of additional helpful resources. The training adaptation also accords with the NICE [57] PTSD guidelines for administering the primary care PTSD screen (PC-PTSD-5; [75]) to participants weekly for 4 weeks. The intervention is designed to monitor stress reactions (i.e., watchful waiting), adjust the intervention, and to redirect participants if/when necessary.

Peer-to-peer PFA intervention

The peer-to-peer PFA intervention is designed to promote adaptive functioning and reduce PTSI among recipients. Again, adapting the NCTSN intervention for police based on the author’s expertise, we broadened the context for using the PFA. PFA trained police officers deliver the peer-to-peer PFA intervention in person or remotely (e.g., by phone). Rather than being restricted to the immediate aftermath of a PPTE, PFA intervention can be offered at any time and in response to isolated or repeated PPTE exposures and for any work-related distress (e.g., anxiety, exhaustion), excluding work relationship issues (e.g., tension with a colleague). The length of the intervention depends on the needs of recipients. Recipients are also asked to respond weekly to the PC-PTSD-5 for 4 weeks.

Context

The SQ is our study partner and the largest police service in Quebec, comprised of approximately 5700 police officers across four districts. The SQ serves 1042 municipalities to maintain peace, and public order, and safety.

Study design

The multi-phase study uses a feasibility and effectiveness PAR with mixed methods (see Fig. 1). Our program is new and unstandardized, using descriptive formative approaches to inform the novel implementation, ongoing development, and iterative conceptual and practical adjustments. SQ members meaningful involvement includes applying a PAR framework [76, 77], collaborating regularly with researchers, and having frequent advisory meetings to co-develop an ecologically-valid and acceptable PFA program tailored for the SQ.

Project timeline. Notes. The timeline was developed in collaboration with the SQ; phases can occur simultaneously

The SQ’s PFA training capacity will be expanded using a train-the-trainers model [78] designed to train 322 PFA providers; as such, there are now ten SQ trainers trained to provide PFA training in the south district. Expansion to the other SQ districts is organized to start in the East, then West, and end in the North. Train-the-trainers model can be effective for knowledge translation in setting with limited-resources [79], feasible for policing contexts (e.g., [80]), time-saving, sustainable, and scalable [81].

Theoretical approach

The study occurs in five phases (see Fig. 1) aligning with two implementation science frameworks and standards for implementation study reporting (i.e., StaRI checklist; [82]; see Supplementary Material).

Phased PFA implementation framework

The PFA implementation framework for high-risk organizations [37] supports implementation prior to outcome evaluation in following phases: adaptation of PFA to environmental needs; organization-wide deployment; optimization of PFA acceptability and practicability; and, identification of effects.

Dynamic sustainability framework

The dynamic sustainability framework [83] assesses the sustainability potential of the PFA program by offering an iterative approach that integrates learning, problem solving, and intervention adaptation into the implementation process. The iterative approach is intended to support ongoing required program adaptations.

Recruitment

The CIUSSS de l’Est-de-l’Île-de-Montréal, Canada provided ethical approval (reference: 2025–3713). All participation is voluntary. The SQ enable their officers to participate during work hours. To recruit e-learning participants (goal 7), managers and supervisors will use the listserv to invite all officers to complete the e-learning. PFA trainers (goals 1, 2, 3, 4) comprised of eight advisors and two police officers are already trained. For the recruitment of PFA providers (goals 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8), occupational health and safety advisors and the SQ communication service will promote the PFA training (e.g., posters, intranet) to inform interested officer about the study. After the verification of inclusion criteria and PFA training, providers will be encouraged to share information with others who are thought to be good providers (i.e., snowball recruitment) to continue recruitment. To recruit recipients (goals 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9), the advisors and the SQ communication service, will promote the PFA intervention (e.g., posters, intranet). Providers will invite recipients to participate in the study, always clarifying how receiving PFA is not contingent on study participation. After participants are feeling better, they will be asked to forward the information to other colleagues who they think could benefit from the intervention (i.e., snowball recruitment).

Data collection

Feasibility component (qualitative outcomes)

Participants’ perspectives of the adapted PFA program with respect will be assessed through semi-structured interviews and PFA provider reports (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Material), with a focus on accessibility, demand, practicality, and implementation (goals 1, 2, 3, 4; co-primary outcomes). Three versions of semi-structured interviews were co-developed with the SQ. Trainer interviews will occur 0 to 3 months following the training they provide to providers, provider interviews will occur 3 to 6 months following their training, and recipient interviews will occur at least 2 months after their first intervention session. Research team members will conduct all interviews by phone. Interviews will be voice-recorded and transcribed. Pilot interviews were an average duration of 23 min for trainers, 25 min for providers, and 35 min for recipients [56]. Providers will complete reports after each PFA session to provide feedback, detail the origin and purpose of the request for PFA, the PFA actions taken, and the recipient’s comments and behaviors.

PFA recipients: police officers who received PFA intervention. T0: within a week of starting the PFA intervention. T1: 1 month after starting the PFA intervention. T2: 2 months after starting the PFA intervention

Effectiveness component (qualitative outcomes)

The provider perceptions of PFA training effectiveness (goal 5; co-primary outcome) and the recipient perceptions of PFA intervention effectiveness (goal 6; co-primary outcome) will be captured during interviews (see Fig. 2).

Effectiveness component (quantitative outcomes)

All the questionnaires (see Fig. 2) are psychometrically valid and will be administered using Qualtrics.

The effectiveness of PTSI awareness e-learning on mental health literacy and stigma (goal 7; co-primary outcomes) will be assessed by administering, pre and post e-learning, the Mental Health Knowledge Schedule (MAKS; [84]) and the Opening Minds Survey of Workplace Attitudes—Short Form (OMSWA-SF, [85]) to the e-learning participants. The MAKS is a 15-item self-report measure that assesses mental health literacy, and the OMSWA-SF is a 9-item self-report measure intended to assess stigmatizing attitudes in the workplace.

The perceived effectiveness of the PFA training on the provider’s self-efficacy (goal 8; co-primary outcome), measured pre and post PFA training, will be assessed using the 48-item Peer Supporters PFA Questionnaire [43].

The effectiveness of the PFA intervention for improving putative protective factors for PTSI (goal 9; co-primary outcomes) will be assessed with the following self-report questionnaires: the 30-item Professional Quality of Life (ProQOL; [86, 87]), the 50-item Work Safety Scale (WSS; [88]), the 21-item French Ways of Coping Checklist (WCC; [89]) which assesses three coping styles (i.e., problem-focused coping; emotion-focused coping; seeking social support); the 6-item General Self-Efficacy short form (GSE-6; [90]), and, the 6-item State Hope Scale [91]. The effectiveness of the PFA intervention for reducing PTSI symptoms (goal 9; secondary outcomes) will be assessed using the 8-item PTSD Checklist-5 short-form (PCL-5; [92]), the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; [93]), and, the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7; [94, 95]). All questionnaires will be administered to recipients from within a week of starting the PFA intervention (T0), 1 month after (T1), and 2 months after (T2).

Demographic and occupational data

Demographic and occupational data will be collected in the pre-e-learning questionnaire for e-learning participants, in the pre-training questionnaire for providers, and at T0 for recipients. Collected information includes: age, ethnicity, years of occupational tenure, sex assigned at birth, current gender identity assessed using the 2-item Revised Multidimensional Sex/Gender Measure [96, 97], and gender role assessed using the Storm Scale [98].

Sample size estimate

Feasibility and effectiveness components (qualitative outcomes)

Based on similar studies [56, 99], empirical saturation for qualitative analyses (goals 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) will be reached with 10 trainers, 30 providers, and 20 recipients.

Effectiveness component (quantitative outcomes)

Up to 5700 officers will be invited to complete the e-learning (goal 7). Power analysis (G*Power 3) is based on a study in which authors inspected the PFA training effectiveness (goal 8) on confidence in taking care of distressed persons [100] using a paired t-test indicated that 175 providers were necessary to obtain a power of 0.95 with a p value ≤ 0.05. Power analysis (G*Power 3) based a previous study in which authors inspected the PFA intervention effectiveness (goal 9) on PTSD symptoms [101] using an Anova repeated measures within factors indicated that 64 recipients were necessary to obtain a power of 0.95 with a p value ≤ 0.05. Anticipating a 50% attrition rate, we will recruit 128 recipients. The power analysis is based on a secondary outcome because no rigorous study was available regarding the effect of intervention on the putative protective factors being assessed for the current study.

Data analyses

Feasibility and effectiveness components (qualitative outcomes)

We will apply a thematic analytic approach [102, 103] involving a detailed inductive and deductive examination of the interview transcripts and provider reports to iteratively identify themes. The codification process will include: 1) preparation of raw data; 2) careful and thorough reading; 3) production of initial codes (three separate codebooks will be developed i.e., trainers, providers, recipients); 4) identification and description of themes; and, 5) refinement of themes [102]. Coding can be done either manually or through NVivo 11 software. An intercoder reliability will have to be sufficient to pursue analysis. Themes will be then separately analyzed by groups using a semi-grounded, interpretivist orientation to obtain a better understanding of experiences of PFA. Contradiction data will be treated as meaningful insights into the complexity and variability of participant experiences. Contradiction data will be explicitly reported in our results when relevant, especially if they challenge dominant themes or provide useful nuance.

Effectiveness component (quantitative outcomes)

Statistical analyses will be performed using R, Version: 4.1.0 software. The alpha value for statistical significance will be set at p ≤ 0.05. Estimates of the effect size and a 95% confidence interval will be reported. Descriptive analyses and linear mixed effects models will be used (https://osf.io/7khgs/?view_only=33260c704ffc46ffb75a704320283ccf).

Exploratory analysis (demographic and occupational outcomes)

Self-reported sex assigned at birth, gender/sex identity, gender role, and ethnicity will be used when appropriate as moderators in quantitative analyses and as sensitizing concepts in qualitative analyses.

Discussion

The current study will provide evidence regarding the feasibility and effectiveness of a large-scale implementation of a PFA program adapted from the NCTSN for police. The results will help inform trauma-informed practices and provide evidence of the effectiveness of PFA as an intervention intended to support police.

Relevance and significance for the field

In Canada, approximately 37% of municipal/provincial police officers screen positive for mental health disorders [16], underscoring the need for more evidence-informed supports. The current study should help promote officers’ abilities to identify and manage signs of distress in themselves and their colleagues, as recommended for mitigating PTSD [104]. Bolstering putative protective factors through training and peer support can be a practical tool for supporting mental health. The current study is intended to promote and share PTSI management practices, as well as create and distribute PTSD-related psychoeducational material [28]. Peer dissemination of mental health literacy through e-learning, delivering PFA training through trained-trainers (community-engaged implementation), and delivering PFA intervention through peers may help facilitated content uptake and use. If successful, the PFA program would extend psychological supports to geographically remote officers who are often under-resourced. Training trainers and 322 providers should increase service capacity and user choices, while decreasing individual burden on providers, promoting organizational self-efficacy, and enhancing program sustainability. If successful, the PFA program may also mitigate PTSI and therein reduce related economic burdens (e.g., sick leave). The current study results may also inform broader deployments of PFA for other high-risk organizations.

Scientific relevance and significance

Observing the lack of research and the longstanding need for mental health programs in policing [9, 27], our study will provide valuable and useful contributions. Debriefing is usually occurred in field practice whereas evidence of its effectiveness is lacking [105, 106]. More evidence-informed and flexible post-PPTE interventions are needed [58, 107]. Results from the current study may inform PFA utility and ecological validity for police.

Feasibility component

Using mixed methods will help inform the rationale for program implications [108] and help produce generalizable results [109]. Assessing acceptability, demand, practicality, and implementation outcomes for trainers, providers, and recipients should help inform implementation strategies for police environments and scaling potential [110–112]. If successful, the PFA program has dissemination advantages to address inequitable access to support [113]. The study goals to advance the field of proximal support research (i.e., a system for offering and receiving help from relatives and colleagues, based on respect, co-responsibility and mutual agreement).

The current study will provide key information to further refine a PFA program within the police culture and for the development of recommendations to facilitate future deployments. PFA workplace implementations remain nascent, and organizations are seeking guidelines. Our findings will contribute to the advancement of knowledge in the field of implementation of post-PPTE interventions in high-risk contexts.

Effectiveness component

The current study will assess the effectiveness of delivering an adapted PFA to police officers. PFA interventions are reported to be beneficial in a general working population [46, 47], but effectiveness in a police context remains to be assessed. The project assesses both PTSI symptoms and putative protective factors for PTSI as recommended to evaluate trauma-focused interventions [114, 115]. The underlying assumption of PFA is how supporting putative protective factors for PTSI should mitigate symptoms [116–119]. Targeting modifiable PTSI correlates (e.g., coping strategies) with training may help protect the health of first responders who routinely face trauma in their line of work [120]. Based on a large sample (N > 1000 police officers), adaptative coping (e.g., problem-focused coping) tends to be associated with lower PTSD symptoms [10]. The current study will provide contributions to existing literature by clarifying the effectiveness of PFA among police.

Potential limitations

The absence of control conditions limits our ability to understand the specific effectiveness of the PFA program. Future studies could include a randomized controlled trial, possibly with a waitlist condition. Using the SQ may limit generalizability of the results. Assessing participants only after the first PFA intervention session precluded accounting for pre-existing factors. The choice was made in accordance with the SQ needs. Finally, using a train-the-trainers model without ongoing fidelity assessments precluded may impact program effectiveness [121]. Assessing the quality of all trainings provided by all trainers to all providers is logistically unfeasible in our study; however, the research team will be regularly available to address concerns and discuss about the program.

Conclusion

The current study is designed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a large-scale implementation of a PFA program adapted for police officers in Canadian police officers. Results will provide insights regarding the implementation and impact of PFA among police, with the possibility for extending the PFA program to other professions within public safety.
