Myeloprotection effect of trilaciclib in lung cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy: a real-world retrospective study
Authors: Mingming Wang, Yalong He, Daihan Dong, Liang Han, Hong Yan, Xuyu Cui, Yong Zhang, Fang Zhang, Liqiang Song, Shuoyao Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChemotherapy-induced myelosuppression (CIM) is a frequent complication in lung cancer, leading to treatment delays, dose reductions, and increased healthcare utilization. Trilaciclib, a first-in-class CDK4/6 inhibitor with myeloprotective properties, has been approved for extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC), but real-world evidence in broader lung cancer populations is limited. This retrospective real-world study aimed to evaluate the myeloprotection effects of trilaciclib in lung cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of lung cancer patients who received chemotherapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University between January 2023 and June 2024. Patients were categorized into trilaciclib group and non-trilaciclib group. Baseline characteristics, myelosuppression grades, drug dosages for myelosuppression treatment, chemotherapy delays, hospitalizations, and alopecia were collected and analyzed. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to adjust for baseline imbalances.ResultsA total of 108 patients were included (54 per group). Overall myelosuppression incidence did not differ significantly (74.1% vs. 87%, P=0.09), but grade ≥3 myelosuppression was markedly reduced in the trilaciclib group [40% vs. 66%, IPTW-adjusted odds ratio (OR) =2.90, P=0.02]. The trilaciclib group required fewer therapeutic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) doses [median 2 vs. 6, IPTW-adjusted rate ratio (RR) =1.74, P=0.02] and had a lower incidence of grade ≥3 alopecia (77.8% vs. 93.2%, P=0.04), but not significant after IPTW adjustment. Myelosuppression-related hospitalization was significantly reduced (13% vs. 50%, IPTW-adjusted OR =7.77, P<0.001), while chemotherapy delays and other supportive medication use were similar.ConclusionsIn a real-world cohort of patients with locally advanced or metastatic lung cancer receiving chemotherapy, trilaciclib was associated with a lower incidence of severe myelosuppression, reduced G-CSF utilization, and fewer hospitalizations. These findings should be considered exploratory, and may support the potential role of trilaciclib as a myeloprotective strategy to optimize treatment continuity and reduce healthcare burden.

Highlight box

Key findings

• In this retrospective real-world study of 108 lung cancer patients (54 per group) receiving chemotherapy, trilaciclib significantly reduced grade ≥3 myelosuppression (40% vs. 66%, P=0.02), therapeutic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) doses (median 2 vs. 6, P=0.01), grade ≥3 alopecia (77.8% vs. 93.2%, P=0.04), and myelosuppression-related hospitalizations (13% vs.50%, P<0.001). Overall myelosuppression incidence was similar (74.1% vs. 87%, P=0.09).

What is known and what is new?

• Chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression is a common complication in lung cancer, often causing severe hematologic toxicity, treatment disruptions, increased supportive care needs, and higher healthcare utilization. Trilaciclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, is approved for myeloprotection in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) based on randomized trials showing reductions in severe neutropenia and supportive interventions.

• This manuscript adds real-world evidence of trilaciclib’s benefits in a broader lung cancer population (including non-small-cell subtypes), demonstrating reduced severe myelosuppression, G-CSF use, alopecia, and hospitalizations outside the controlled trial setting limited to ES-SCLC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Trilaciclib provides proactive multilineage myeloprotection, potentially improving treatment continuity, reducing healthcare burden, and enhancing patient outcomes in real-world practice. Clinicians should consider incorporating trilaciclib prior to chemotherapy in lung cancer patients at risk of myelosuppression to optimize regimen delivery and minimize complications, particularly in broader populations beyond its current approval.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a predominant cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a considerable percentage of individuals diagnosed with locally advanced or metastatic illness (1). Chemotherapy is the primary therapeutic option for such patients, encompassing both small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet it is associated with significant adverse effects, particularly myelosuppression. Myelosuppression, characterized by the decreased production of blood cells, is a common and severe complication of chemotherapy. It can lead to neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, resulting in increased susceptibility to infections, fatigue, and bleeding complications (2).

The current management strategies for chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression (CIM) involve the administration of growth factors such as granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs). Despite their effectiveness in alleviating neutropenia and anemia, these agents come with their own set of limitations, including high cost, the risk of stimulating cancer cell proliferation and potential cardiovascular side effects (3). Furthermore, these interventions are generally reactive instead of preventative, addressing the symptoms of myelosuppression after their emergence rather than preventing their occurrence.

Trilaciclib, a novel myeloprotective agent, has emerged as a promising solution for preventing CIM. As a CDK4/6 inhibitor, trilaciclib temporarily arrests the cell cycle of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the bone marrow, decreasing their vulnerability to the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy (4). Clinical trials have demonstrated that trilaciclib significantly decreases the occurrence of severe neutropenia and the need for supportive care interventions such as G-CSF and blood transfusions (5). Currently, trilaciclib is approved for the treatment of extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC), but no clinical studies have evaluated its efficacy in preventing myelosuppression in other types of lung cancer, and real-world evidence on its use in lung cancer patients remains limited.

This retrospective real-world study aims to evaluate the myeloprotective effects of trilaciclib in patients with locally advanced or metastatic lung cancer undergoing chemotherapy, providing comprehensive real-world evidence on its impact on myelosuppressive adverse events and the necessity for supportive interventions. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2687/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, real-world study was conducted to evaluate the bone marrow-protective effects of trilaciclib in patients with lung cancer undergoing chemotherapy. A total of 108 patients with SCLC or locally advanced/advanced NSCLC were enrolled at the Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University between January 2023 and June 2024. Patients were stratified into the trilaciclib group or the non-trilaciclib group based on receipt of trilaciclib. Inclusion criteria: patients aged ≥18 years; newly diagnosed SCLC or locally advanced/advanced NSCLC (stage III–IV) confirmed by lung biopsy pathology; receipt of either chemotherapy alone or chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. Exclusion Criteria: a history of other malignant tumors; patients diagnosed with early-stage (I–II) NSCLC. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (No. KY20242216, date: 2024.07.10). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, who consented to the use of their clinical data for research purposes.

Treatment regimen

In this study, all patients received either chemotherapy alone or chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy. Chemotherapy regimens included platinum-based agents: carboplatin [area under the curve (AUC) =5, day 1], nedaplatin (80 mg/m2, day 1), or cisplatin (75 mg/m2, day 1). Agent selection was based on pathological subtype: patients with lung adenocarcinoma received pemetrexed (500 mg/m2, day 1) every 3 weeks; those with lung squamous cell carcinoma received albumin-bound paclitaxel (130 mg/m2, days 1 and 8); and patients with SCLC received etoposide (100 mg/m2, days 1–3). Immunotherapy consisted of anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 antibodies administered every 3 weeks, with individualized selection based on clinical evaluation and patient preference. In the trilaciclib group, trilaciclib was administered as a 30 min intravenous infusion at 240 mg/m2, 4 h prior to chemotherapy. The non-trilaciclib group did not receive trilaciclib.

Data collection

Patient data were obtained through a standardized data collection form, which included: demographic information: age, gender, smoking history; clinical characteristics: lung cancer stage, histological subtype, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status; treatment details: chemotherapy regimen [the risk classification of myelosuppression caused by chemotherapy drugs is based on the following reference (6)], immunotherapy status, trilaciclib dosing schedule; laboratory data: complete blood count (CBC) results (specifically white blood cell count, neutrophil count, platelet count, and hemoglobin level) before and after administering trilaciclib and chemotherapy.

Endpoints and grading criteria

The primary endpoint was the incidence and severity of CIM. Myelosuppression was defined and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. The grading system is outlined as follows: grade 1: absolute neutrophil count (ANC) ≥1,500 cells/mm3, platelet count ≥100,000 cells/mm3, hemoglobin level ≥10 g/dL; grade 2: ANC 1,000 to 1,499 cells/mm3, platelet count 75,000 to 99,999 cells/mm3, hemoglobin level 8 to less than 10 g/dL; grade 3: ANC 500 to 999 cells/mm3, platelet count 50,000 to 74,999 cells/mm3, hemoglobin level 6.5 to less than 8 g/dL; grade 4: ANC <500 cells/mm3, platelet count <50,000 cells/mm3, hemoglobin level <6.5 g/dL.

Secondary endpoints included the dosage of medications for myelosuppression management, chemotherapy delays, hospitalizations, and alopecia. Additional hospitalization refers to unplanned hospital admissions considered by the treating physician to be associated with myelosuppression or related complications. Alopecia was graded as follows: grade 0, no alopecia (no hair loss); grade 1, mild alopecia (less than 50% hair loss); grade 2, moderate alopecia (more than 50% hair loss but not complete), at which point a wig may be proposed; grade 3, severe but reversible alopecia (near-total or total hair loss), with the recommendation of a wig; grade 4, complete irreversible alopecia (total and permanent hair loss), with the recommendation of a wig.

Subgroup analyses

To explore the potential heterogeneity of treatment effects, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by tumor type (SCLC vs. NSCLC) and disease stage (stage III, stage IV, limited-stage, and extensive-stage). Given the limited sample size within each subgroup, these analyses were considered exploratory.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to assess baseline imbalance, with an SMD <0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model including age, sex, smoking status, pathological type, clinical stage, performance status (PS) score, risk of CIM, and immune checkpoint inhibitor use. All patients included in this study had baseline hematologic parameters within normal reference ranges prior to chemotherapy initiation, as required by standard clinical practice. No clinically meaningful differences were observed between groups (Table S1). Therefore, baseline hematologic parameters were not included in the propensity score model. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to create a weighted cohort. The IPTW model preserved the sample size of the study cohort, and no study participants were dropped, which was advantageous compared with the propensity score matching (PSM) method. Binary outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression, and continuous skewed outcomes were analyzed using regression. Effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (ORs) or rate ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted utilizing SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software version 4.3.2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 108 lung cancer patients were included (Figure 1), comprising 54 patients who received trilaciclib prior to chemotherapy and 54 patients who did not. Initially, a total of 352 patients were screened. Among them, 82 patients with stage I–II lung cancer, 23 patients with concomitant other malignancies, and 76 patients who did not receive chemotherapy were excluded. Additionally, 63 patients with missing key clinical data were excluded, including but not limited to chemotherapy regimen details, trilaciclib dosage and schedule, pre- and post-chemotherapy hematologic parameters (white blood cell count, ANC, platelet count), and follow-up data related to myelosuppression. Before weighting, several baseline characteristics showed moderate imbalance between the trilaciclib and non-trilaciclib groups, as indicated by SMDs (SMD >0.1), particularly age, pathological type, and clinical stage (Table 1). Myeloprotection efficacy

As shown in Table 2, the risk of CIM was reported as moderate in most patients in both groups (trilaciclib: 74.1%, non-trilaciclib: 87%). In the unweighted analysis, the incidence of any-grade myelosuppression was numerically higher in the non-trilaciclib group but did not reach statistical significance (OR =2.35, 95% CI: 0.86–6.39, P=0.09). After IPTW adjustment, this difference became significant (OR =3.91, 95% CI: 1.36–11.23, P=0.01). The incidence of grade ≥3 myelosuppression was significantly higher in the non-trilaciclib group in both unweighted and IPTW-weighted analyses (66% vs. 40%, OR =2.91, 95% CI: 1.21–6.97, P=0.02; IPTW-adjusted OR =2.90, 95% CI: 1.23–6.86, P=0.02), indicating a protective effect of trilaciclib against severe myelosuppression.

Alopecia

Overall alopecia incidence was similar between groups (81.5% vs. 83.3%, P=0.80, Table 4). However, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the non-trilaciclib group experienced grade 3 or above alopecia (77.8% vs. 93.2%, P=0.040). After IPTW adjustment, the association between trilaciclib use and reduced risk of grade ≥3 alopecia was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (IPTW-adjusted OR =2.32, 95% CI: 0.53–10.07, P=0.26).

Similar non-significant results were noted across hematologic parameters, suggesting consistent efficacy regardless of prophylaxis timing.

Medication use, chemotherapy delays and hospitalizations

Trilaciclib recipients required fewer doses of G-CSF (RR =1.72, 95% CI: 1.36–2.18, P=0.01), and the difference remained significant after IPTW adjustment (RR =1.74, 95% CI: 1.09–2.77, P=0.02; Table 6). Use of erythropoietin and thrombopoietin was similar between groups (all P>0.05). Table 7 indicates that the incidence of chemotherapy delays due to myelosuppression was not significantly different between the Trilaciclib and non-trilaciclib groups (51.9% vs. 50%), with no significant difference observed in either the unweighted or IPTW-weighted analyses (all P>0.05). The non-trilaciclib group had a significantly higher rate of additional hospitalizations due to myelosuppression (50% vs. 13%, OR =6.71, 95% CI: 2.70–18.66, P<0.001), which remained significant after IPTW adjustment (OR =7.77, 95% CI: 2.78–21.76, P<0.001). Associated hospitalization costs were lower in the trilaciclib group, but these differences did not reach statistical significance in either unweighted or weighted analyses (all P>0.05).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses stratified by tumor type and disease stage are presented in Figures S1,S2. In unadjusted analyses, a higher incidence of grade ≥3 myelosuppression was observed in the non-trilaciclib group within the SCLC and extensive-stage subgroups. However, these associations were no longer statistically significant after IPTW adjustment (all P>0.05). Given the limited sample size within each subgroup, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory.

Discussion

In recent years, despite remarkable advances in novel therapeutic strategies such as immunotherapy and targeted therapy for advanced lung cancer (7-9), chemotherapy remains an indispensable component of treatment regimens (10,11). However, CIM is a common adverse effect that not only increases the risk of infection and bleeding but may also lead to treatment delays or dose reductions, thereby compromising therapeutic efficacy (12-14). Trilaciclib, a selective CDK4/6 inhibitor, effectively protects HSPCs from the toxic effects of chemotherapeutic agents, thereby reducing the incidence of severe hematologic adverse events caused by chemotherapy (4,15). The present study demonstrated a significant reduction in grade ≥3 myelosuppression among patients receiving trilaciclib, consistent with its previously established myeloprotective effects in clinical trials (5,16-19). In the present study, IPTW-adjusted analysis revealed a more pronounced reduction in both any-grade and grade ≥3 myelosuppression in patients receiving trilaciclib compared with the unweighted analysis. This enhanced effect after adjustment likely reflects the balancing of baseline characteristics between the Trilaciclib and non-Trilaciclib groups, minimizing potential confounding factors such as age, performance status, cancer subtype, and prior treatment history. These findings suggest that trilaciclib may preserve bone marrow function and enhance chemotherapy tolerance.

Currently, trilaciclib has been approved for the prevention of CIM in adult patients with ES-SCLC (20). Clinical trial data presented at the World Conference on Lung Cancer (WCLC) further indicated that trilaciclib may also mitigate CIM in patients with NSCLC (21). Although its indication has not yet been formally approved in this setting, the underlying mechanism of action suggests a potential role for trilaciclib in preventing myelosuppression across additional lung cancer subtypes. In this study, subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by tumor type and disease stage. Although the direction of effect was generally consistent, statistical significance was not observed across subgroups, likely due to limited sample sizes. Therefore, findings in non-ES-SCLC populations should be considered exploratory.

In patients receiving chemotherapy combined with ICIs, trilaciclib similarly demonstrated (18). Moreover, studies have shown that trilaciclib preserves peripheral lymphocyte levels and enhances T-cell activation, suggesting its potential to maintain and augment immune system function (22). Nevertheless, its synergistic role in patients treated with ICIs requires further validation through prospective studies. Our findings also revealed that trilaciclib significantly reduced the use of G-CSF, indicating a decreased need for growth factor support, thereby reinforcing its protective effect against chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (5,23). This reduction may minimize adverse events associated with growth factor administration, lower healthcare costs, and improve patient quality of life. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the use of erythropoietin or thrombopoietin, implying that trilaciclib primarily confers myeloid protection. This is consistent with its mechanism of action, whereby reversible CDK4/6 inhibition transiently arrests cells in the G1 phase, shielding them from chemotherapy-induced cytotoxicity. Furthermore, in unadjusted analyses, patients in the trilaciclib cohort had a lower incidence of grade ≥3 alopecia. However, this difference was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after IPTW adjustment, indicating that baseline covariates partially explained the observed effect. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted as exploratory. Nonetheless, the biological rationale remains plausible, as hair follicle cells rely on CDK4/6 signaling and CDK4/6 inhibitors have been shown to mitigate taxane-induced follicular damage (24), warranting further investigation in lung cancer populations.

Our study revealed no significant differences in the incidence of bone marrow suppression between primary and secondary prophylaxis groups within the trilaciclib cohort. This suggests that trilaciclib is equally effective whether used as a primary or secondary preventive measure, providing flexibility in its clinical application. In this study, a lower rate of hospitalization associated with myelosuppression was observed in the trilaciclib group. However, this endpoint was based on physician-assessed clinical need rather than standardized criteria and did not require confirmed febrile neutropenia. Therefore, variability in admission practices may have influenced the observed association, and the magnitude of effect should be interpreted with caution. Although differences in hospitalization costs did not reach statistical significance, the overall trend suggested reduced healthcare resource utilization, in line with previous findings (17,19,25,26).

Nevertheless, several limitations of our study should be considered when interpreting the results. As a retrospective real-world study, it was subjected to selection biases. Treatment decisions regarding trilaciclib use were made by treating physicians based on clinical judgment, which may have introduced confounding by indication. Although IPTW was applied to balance observed covariates, not all potential confounders could be accounted for. In particular, baseline hematologic parameters were not included in the weighting model. However, all patients met standard clinical criteria for chemotherapy initiation, with baseline blood counts within normal ranges and no clinically meaningful differences between groups. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Given the relatively small sample size, the precision of effect estimates was limited, as reflected by the width of the confidence intervals. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts are necessary to validate our findings and further explore the mechanisms of trilaciclib in myeloprotection. Importantly, safety outcomes related to trilaciclib were not systematically evaluated in this study. Although prior clinical trials have demonstrated a favorable safety profile, the lack of real-world safety data limits a comprehensive assessment of the benefit-risk balance. This should be considered an important limitation, and future studies should incorporate detailed safety evaluation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our real-world data suggest that trilaciclib may reduce CIM and associated clinical burden in patients with lung cancer. However, given the study limitations, particularly population heterogeneity, potential residual confounding, and small sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Prospective, adequately powered studies are warranted to confirm these observations and further define the role of trilaciclib in broader clinical settings.
