Traumatic middle meningeal arteriovenous fistula presenting with long delayed-onset facial nerve palsy without temporal bone fracture: illustrative case
Authors: Shigeki Takada, Koki Mitani, Tomonori Ichikawa, Nobutake Sadamasa, Waro Taki
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDTraumatic facial nerve palsy (FNP) without temporal bone fracture (TBF) has a delayed onset in some cases; however, long delayed-onset FNP in this setting has not been reported. The middle meningeal vein (MMV) is one of the venous drainage routes from the facial nerve. Herein, the authors describe a rare case of traumatic middle meningeal arteriovenous fistula (MMAVF) presenting with the long delayed-onset FNP without TBF.OBSERVATIONSA 42-year-old man with pulsatile tinnitus and left FNP was admitted to our hospital 4 weeks after head trauma without TBF. Cerebral angiography revealed an MMAVF between the middle meningeal artery and the MMV on the left side. Seven days after admission, the FNP showed slight improvement, and preoperative angiography revealed decreased shunt flow of the MMAVF. Transarterial coil embolization was successfully performed. Postoperative angiography showed no residual fistula. Two weeks after the procedure, there was complete resolution of the FNP. This clinical course was correlated with the angiographic findings, suggesting that the long delayed-onset FNP was caused by the traumatic MMAVF without TBF.LESSONSIn patients presenting with long delayed-onset FNP after head trauma without TBF, the vascular lesion must be evaluated to exclude MMAVF.Keywords: traumatic middle meningeal arteriovenous fistula, facial nerve palsy, transarterial coil embolizationABBREVIATIONS: ATDV = anterior temporal diploic vein, AVF = arteriovenous fistula, CT = computed tomography, ECA = external carotid artery, FNP = facial nerve palsy, MMA = middle meningeal artery, MMAVF = middle meningeal arteriovenous fistula, MMV = middle meningeal vein, MRA = magnetic resonance angiography, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging, TBF = temporal bone fracture

Traumatic arteriovenous fistula (AVF) involving the middle meningeal artery (MMA) is referred to as “traumatic middle meningeal arteriovenous fistula” (MMAVF). It is a rare entity with a reported incidence of approximately 1.8% in patients with head trauma. MMAVF manifests within days of the trauma in approximately 60% of patients and within 3 months in 95% of patients.1 Previous reports have described a variety of traumatic MMAVFs, including AVFs between the MMA and the middle meningeal vein (MMV), diploic vein, pterygoid venous plexus, cavernous sinus, sphenoparietal sinus, petrosal vein, or greater petrosal sinus. The reported symptom patterns include headache, pulsatile tinnitus, exophthalmos, neurological deficit, and unconsciousness; however, none of the previously reported cases of MMAVF have had facial nerve palsy (FNP).1–14 The MMV is one of the routes of venous drainage from the facial nerve.15 In general, traumatic FNP is classified as immediate-onset FNP and delayed-onset FNP. Delayed-onset FNP usually occurs up to 2 weeks after head trauma. Traumatic FNP without temporal bone fracture (TBF) typically presents as delayed-onset FNP; however, long delayed-onset FNP has not been reported in this setting.16–22 Herein, we describe a rare case of a traumatic MMAVF between the MMA and the MMV presenting with long delayed-onset FNP without TBF.

Illustrative Case

History and Examination

A 42-year-old man was admitted to our hospital because of headache, pulsatile tinnitus, and left FNP (Fig. 1A). Four weeks before admission, he had experienced head trauma from a fall from his bike. Two weeks before admission, the patient had developed tinnitus and headache and consulted an otolaryngologist. However, no abnormalities were detected on examination. On the day of admission, he had developed left FNP and came to our hospital. Ophthalmologically, no abnormal findings were observed. Head computed tomography (CT) showed no skull fracture (Fig. 1B). Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) revealed an MMAVF with a fistulous connection between the left MMA and the left MMV (Fig. 1C and D). Cerebral angiography showed that the MMAVF was draining to the left anterior temporal diploic vein (ATDV), sphenoparietal sinus, pterygoid plexus, cavernous sinus, and petrosquamous sinus, without associated cortical venous reflux; in addition, the left internal jugular vein was occluded (Fig. 2A–D). On cone-beam CT, the width at the labyrinthine segment of the facial canal was 1.03 mm (Fig. 2E).

Preoperative left ECA angiography (A–D) and cone-beam CT (E). Left anteroposterior (A and C) and lateral (B and D) ECA angiograms revealed a left MMAVF fed by the MMA and draining through the MMV into the left ATDV, sphenoparietal sinus, pterygoid plexus, cavernous sinus, and petrosquamous sinus, without associated cortical venous reflux. There was also occlusion of left internal jugular vein. Cone-beam CT (E) showed that the labyrinthine segment (arrow) of that facial canal was narrow (1.03 mm).

Operation

Seven days after admission, endovascular intervention was performed with the patient under general anesthesia. There was slight improvement in the FNP on the day of operation. The right femoral artery was accessed using a 6-Fr sheath, and a 6-Fr guiding catheter (Launcher Straight Guiding Catheter, Medtronic) was introduced over a hydrophilic 0.035-inch soft-tip angled guidewire and navigated into the left external carotid artery (ECA). Contrast injection in the left ECA revealed decreased shunt flow of the MMAVF (Fig. 3A and B). An occlusion balloon catheter (3 mm × 5 mm, Shouryu, Kaneka Medics) was placed in the left MMA and inflated to temporarily occlude it. Contrast injection in the left ECA confirmed that this MMAVF was fed only by the MMA (single feeder). Consequently, we diagnosed this case as a traumatic MMAVF. After the occlusion, the balloon catheter was withdrawn, a distal access catheter (3.2-Fr/3.4-Fr GuidePost, 120 cm, Tokai Medical Products) was placed in the left ECA, and a microcatheter (Headway DUO, Terumo) was advanced into the left MMA using a microguidewire (Chikai 14, 200 cm, Asahi Intecc). Microcatheter injection revealed that the fistulous point of the MMAVF was located immediately distal to the foramen spinosum (Fig. 3C and D). The microcatheter was navigated into the MMV through the fistulous point. The MMV and MMA were embolized from the distal to proximal direction, including the fistulous point, using detachable coils (Axium Prime, Medtronic; i-ED coil, Kaneka Medix; Fig. 3E and F). Postoperative left ECA angiography demonstrated total obliteration of the fistula (Fig. 3G and H).

Left anteroposterior (A) and lateral (B) ECA angiograms showing the decreased shunt flow of the MMAVF. Microcatheter injection (C and D) showed that the fistulous point of the MMAVF was located immediately distal to the foramen spinosum. The microcatheter was navigated into the MMV through the fistulous point. The MMV and MMA were embolized from distal to proximal, including the fistulous point, using detachable coils (E and F). Postoperative anteroposterior (G) and lateral (H) ECA angiograms showed that the MMAVF was completely obliterated.

Postoperative Course

Immediately after the procedure, the bruit and headache were completely resolved. The patient showed gradual improvement in the FNP and was discharged on postoperative day 5. Postoperative MRA confirmed obliteration of the fistula. Two weeks after the procedure, the patient had completely recovered from the FNP (Fig. 4).

MRA (left) on day 1 after coil embolization showed obliteration of the MMAVF. The facial photograph (right) on day 14 after treatment showed complete disappearance of the left FNP.

Discussion

Observations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report of a traumatic MMAVF presenting with long delayed-onset FNP without TBF. Head trauma is the second most common cause of FNP after idiopathic FNP (Bell’s palsy).17,23 Traumatic facial nerve injury is often associated with TBFs. TBFs are classified as longitudinal or transverse, based on their orientation with respect to the long axis of the petrous pyramid. Traumatic FNP can sometimes occur without evidence of TBF.16,19,20,24 Diamond and Frew18 first classified traumatic FNP as immediate and delayed onset. Traumatic FNP without TBF typically has a delayed onset. There is some ambiguity regarding the etiology of delayed-onset FNP after head trauma without TBF. Some studies have suggested that microtrauma can result in delayed-onset FNP due to edema, delayed arterial spasm, or external compression by hematoma.16,25 In a study by Turner et al.,24 nearly 50% of cases with delayed-onset FNP became clinically evident within the first 4 days after head trauma.16 Our patient presented with peripheral FNP 4 weeks after a head trauma, suggesting that the long delayed-onset FNP in our case was not a typical delayed-onset FNP and was caused by a different mechanism.

In the present case, the most likely cause of the ipsilateral FNP was venous congestion of the facial nerve caused by the MMAVF. A total of 14 cases of MMAVF without TBF have been reported to date (Table 1).3–5,7,8,10–12,14,26–29 Presenting symptoms in these cases have included tinnitus, headache, chemosis, and exophthalmos; however, none of these cases had FNP. In the present case, the fistulous point was between the MMA and the MMV, and the draining routes were as follows: (1) MMV to the superior sagittal sinus through the ATDV, (2) MMV to the cavernous sinus through the sphenoparietal sinus, (3) MMV to the cavernous sinus through the emissary vein of the foramen ovale and pterygoid plexus, and (4) MMV to the transverse sinus through the petrosquamous sinus. There were more draining routes in the MMAVF in this case than in previous cases, and the ipsilateral internal jugular vein was occluded. Our hypothesis regarding the cause of FNP in our patient is consistent with the venous anatomy around the facial canal in the temporal bone. Venous drainage of the facial nerve generally accompanies the named arteries within the nerve sheath and ultimately drains into the MMV, cavernous sinus, stylomastoid vein, and internal jugular vein.15,30 Therefore, the MMAVF can cause venous congestion of the MMV, leading to facial nerve edema followed by ipsilateral peripheral FNP. The clinical course of our patient was consistent with our hypothesis. Our patient developed tinnitus and headache 2 weeks after head trauma. Four weeks after the trauma, he developed peripheral FNP on the left side. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed the MMAVF on the left side. Steroid pulse therapy was started shortly after admission. Seven days after admission (the day of endovascular treatment), there was mild improvement in the FNP, and preoperative angiography showed decreased shunt flow of the MMAVF. Fourteen days after endovascular treatment, the FNP was completely resolved. Consequently, the angiographic findings correlated with the clinical course of the FNP.

Among the 14 previously reported MMAVF cases without ipsilateral TBF, our case is the only one that presented with FNP.3–5,7,8,10–12,14,26–29 This can be attributed to two factors. First, our case had more draining routes than the previous cases, and the internal jugular vein was occluded. This abrogated the normal venous drainage from the facial nerve, which is through the MMV, cavernous sinus, stylomastoid vein, and internal jugular vein.15,30 Second, the width of the labyrinthine segment of the facial canal in the present case was relatively small.31–36 Within the bone canal, the facial nerve is subdivided into three segments: labyrinthine, tympanic, and mastoid. The labyrinthine segment is the narrowest and shortest portion of the fallopian canal, where the facial nerve is vulnerable to trauma. The facial nerve occupies more than 80% of the space in the labyrinthine portion of the fallopian canal, where ischemia or other processes can cause edema of the facial nerve.20 A study by Celik et al.35 showed that the diameter of the labyrinthine segment of the facial canal in CT images is an anatomical risk factor for Bell’s palsy; in their study, the mean diameter of the labyrinthine segment in the affected temporal bone was 1.09 mm. The labyrinthine segment is the weakest point of the arterial supply of the nerve, and Bell’s palsy is frequently seen due to the involvement of this segment of the facial nerve.36 Taken together, in the present case, the FNP was likely caused by venous congestion of the facial nerve and the facial nerve edema in the facial canal due to the MMAVF.

Regarding angiographic outcomes of traumatic MMAVFs, the reported spontaneous occlusion rate of traumatic MMAVF was 13% and decreased shunt flow was observed in 4.9% of cases before treatment.13 In our case, too, preoperative angiography demonstrated decreased shunt flow of the MMAVF. Regarding clinical outcomes of traumatic MMAVFs, the reported rates of focal deficit, severe deficit, and death were 16.4%, 8.2%, and 6.6%, respectively.13 Several previous reports have indicated that traumatic MMAVFs resulted in intraparenchymal hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or subdural hemorrhage.3,8,26,37,38 Transarterial coil embolization for MMAVF is the most common treatment and is feasible, effective, and minimally invasive.13 Therefore, endovascular coil embolization should be considered after diagnosis.

Lessons

Traumatic MMAVF without TBF can cause FNP with a long delayed onset. In patients presenting with long delayed-onset FNP after head trauma without TBF, the vascular lesion must be evaluated with MRI or CT angiography to exclude MMAVF.
