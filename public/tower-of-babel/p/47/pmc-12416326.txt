Targeted decompression to address long-standing anatomical distortion in a case of extensive, bilateral en plaque meningiomas: illustrative case
Authors: Stefanie Bauer, Karina Hofmann, Miriam Weiss, Christian Musahl, Lukas Andereggen, Gerrit A Schubert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDMeningioma en plaque (MEP) is a rare subtype of meningioma with a carpet-like growth pattern, often causing hyperostosis. Even rarer is the presentation of bilateral MEP posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Management of MEP usually entails early complete resection. Follow-up may be considered for asymptomatic and slow-growing tumors.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the case of a 52-year-old patient with progressive optic neuropathy due to bilateral hyperostotic frontotemporal MEP. Long-standing intracranial hypertension caused by mass effect had led to formation of a suprachiasmatic cyst deflecting the optic chiasm. The authors aimed to lower the intracranial pressure indirectly by performing a targeted, right-sided craniectomy with tumor removal and mesh cranioplasty. Postoperatively, reconstitution of brain anatomy and stabilization of the optic neuropathy were observed.LESSONSTo the authors’ knowledge, this is the first case report addressing targeted decompression for severe, long-standing anatomical distortion due to bilateral hyperostotic MEP. Complete resection as recommended for MEPs would have been associated with a high risk of perioperative morbidity in this case. In case of neurological deterioration due to decompensated intracranial hypertension, indirect decompression can effectively address chronic distortion of anatomy. While meningioma is the most common primary tumor of the central nervous system in the adult,1 meningioma en plaque (MEP) is relatively rare, accounting for only about 2%–9% of all meningioma cases2 Cushing and Eisenhardt first used the term “en plaque” to classify sphenoid wing meningioma, alongside the alternative term “globoid.”3 Unlike the globoid meningiomas, MEPs are characterized by a carpet-like proliferation and are usually associated with hyperostosis.4,5 They can cause significant clinical challenges due to their infiltrative nature and potential impact on adjacent structures such as the cavernous sinus, the superior orbital fissure, and the orbit itself.2 The usual location of MEPs is the spheno-orbital region and, more rarely, the convexity or foramen magnum.2,4 On cerebral imaging, differences between primary bone diseases and MEP with hyperostosis may be subtle and often missed.6 Histologically, MEPs are most commonly WHO grade 1 tumors with a low proliferation index.2 Therapy of MEPs usually consists of early surgery with the goal of maximal safe resection and possibility of adjuvant radiation therapy in cases of subtotal resection.2

Illustrative Case

We present the case of a 52-year-old female with progressive bilateral exophthalmos over 15 years and recent visual disturbance due to bilateral optic nerve atrophy. First evaluated in 2009 for headaches, visual disturbances, and exophthalmos, initial findings suggested migraine and Hashimoto thyroiditis. Cerebral imaging revealed symmetric frontotemporal hyperostosis causing significant cerebral compression, leading to a suspected diagnosis of fibrous dysplasia (Fig. 1). However, a biopsy confirmed MEP (WHO grade 1). Given the risk of perioperative morbidity, follow-up with ophthalmological monitoring was chosen at an external ophthalmological clinic, with imaging reconsidered if visual deficits progressed.

Preoperative coronal (A) and axial (B) MR and CT images illustrating massive bilateral hyperostosis with compression of the brain parenchyma. Formation of a suprachiasmatic cyst caused caudal deflection of the optic chiasm (C and D; red arrows), with signal alteration indicating optic neuropathy.

In June 2024, progressive bilateral optic neuropathy was noted on optical coherence tomography (OCT). The patient presented for treatment evaluation in our multidisciplinary tumor conference. It was postulated that the meningioma masses caused long-standing intracranial hypertension, with gradual distortion of brain anatomy over time (Fig. 1A and B), among others causing formation and growth of a suprachiasmatic cyst, with progressive caudal deflection of the optic chiasm and the optic tracts (Fig. 1C and D). Tight intraorbital compartments in the context of Hashimoto thyroiditis were also noted and considered as a contributing factor for the neuropathy. However, on MRI, the most recent changes included enlargement of the suprachiasmatic cyst, abrupt and more pronounced downward distension of the optic structures when exiting the optic canal, and new neuropathic signal changes within the optic tracts (Fig. 1C and D). Thus, alleviation of the pressure on the optic structures was deemed most urgent. Although direct cyst fenestration was considered, the risk of abrupt pressure shifts and further distortion led us to instead pursue indirect compression.

The extent of the planned resection included resection of the right spheno-orbital mass, skeletalization of the orbital fissure, and decompression into the temporal fossa. Intraoperatively, the mass was gradually reduced with sequential burr holes, dissection and removal with Kerrison rongeurs, and later navigated drilling of the sphenoid ridge (Fig. 2A). The internal sheath of the dura was found to be partially calcified and adhesive to the parenchyma in the central portion of the exposure but could eventually be mobilized. Extent of resection was again controlled with navigation. Slow expansion of the brain was observed during surgery. A dural substitute and titanium mesh were used to cover the defect (Fig. 2B). The surgery took about 4.5 hours, and total blood loss was estimated at 600 ml. The patient recovered from surgery promptly without new deficits. Postoperatively, the retinal nerve fiber layer was still thin nasally; however, it was mildly improving compared with the preoperative OCT examination. Postoperative imaging illustrating the extent of resection and reconstruction with the mesh (Fig. 3A) revealed gradual reconstitution of normal anatomy on the right side (Fig. 3B).

Intraoperative photographs showing navigated, osteoclastic resection of tumor mass (A), followed by duraplasty and mesh reconstruction (B).

Osteoclastic decompression and mesh reconstruction (A) resulted in partial reconstitution of normal anatomy (B).

The patient is doing well on follow-up after 1 year, is satisfied with the cosmetic result of the reconstruction, and is now monitored with regular cerebral MRI and ophthalmological examinations. The most recent MRI showed regression of the suprachiasmatic cyst with relocation of the optic chiasm (Fig. 4A and B), as well as regression of the frontal encephalocele (Fig. 4C and D) and resolution of uncal hernation (Fig. 4E and F).

Preoperative coronal T2-weighted MR images showing that long-standing intracranial hypertension had resulted in downward deflection of the optic chiasm due to an enlarged suprachiasmatic cyst (A), formation of an encephalocele at the frontal base into the sphenoid sinus (C), and uncal herniation (E). Postoperatively, the suprachiasmatic cyst regressed (B), with relocation of the optic chiasm, regression of the encephalocele (D), and retraction of the uncus (F).

Discussion

Bilateral MEPs pose unique challenges due to their infiltrative nature and proximity to critical structures.5 To our knowledge, this is the first case report presenting a bilateral MEP with such extensive spread and complex distortion of normal anatomy, illustrating the necessity for an individualized decision-making process. The patient presented with bilateral proptosis, retro-orbital pressure, and progressive visual decline, with imaging revealing tight packing of the orbit, but also significant bilateral involvement of the frontal and temporal bones, alongside sphenoid wing lesions. These findings necessitated a critical analysis of the most relevant clinical problem, with careful balance between the potential benefits of surgical intervention and the associated risks.

Observations

The cornerstone of management for MEP is resection, with a focus on complete tumor removal where feasible, as most MEPs will eventually become symptomatic due to the proximity to the orbit and the cavernous sinus.2,7 Complete resection is often limited in spheno-orbital meningiomas, and early reports presented high rates of morbidity and mortality with the attempt of complete resection.8,9 In the era of microsurgery, however, the paradigm has shifted toward early surgery with the attempt of complete resection to avert progression to a symptomatic tumor.2,8,9 On presentation, the extensive bilateral involvement posed unique challenges that precluded a straightforward resection. A literature review yielded only a few other cases of bilateral MEP; the proposed treatment plans usually entailed complete resection whenever feasible and adjuvant radiotherapy in cases of subtotal resection. However, none of the cases presented such extensive disease with distortion of anatomy.5,10–12 In our presented case, complete bilateral resection was deemed too hazardous due to the procedure’s extent and invasiveness. A key decision point was to directly address the progressive suprachiasmatic cyst, which caused a 90° caudal deflection of the optic structures. Stereotactic aspiration or transsphenoidal fenestration of the cyst was considered but deferred due to the unpredictable pressure changes and possible new anatomical shift with potential aggravation of herniation. Furthermore, the durability of a direct cyst intervention was considered limited, as aspiration alone would not address the underlying cause of cyst formation—chronic intracranial hypertension due to long-standing mass effect. Consequently, a high risk of cyst recurrence was anticipated if the meningioma-induced pressure distortion remained untreated. To avoid this, it was decided to address the volume effect caused by the suprachiasmatic cyst indirectly by targeted tumor resection on the right side, expecting that normalization of pressure would lead to gradual deflation and regression of the cyst, decompression of the optic structures, reconstitution, and relocation of brain anatomy. This indirect approach allowed gradual decompression of the optic chiasm and offered a more sustainable long-term outcome without manipulating the cyst directly or triggering abrupt pressure shifts.

Lessons

This case highlights the importance of individualized treatment strategies in managing complex bilateral MEPs. The decision to prioritize indirect decompression over direct aspiration of the suprachiasmatic arachnoid cyst was largely influenced by the severe distortion of anatomy and long-standing nature of the intracranial hypertension to allow for gradual reconstitution. This approach achieved the primary goal of halting visual decline and alleviating symptoms.
