The neuronal A-to-I RNA editome of Solenopsis invicta expands conserved recoding repertoire between two ant species
Authors: Jiyao Liu, Ling Ma, Qiuhua Xie, Wanzhi Cai, Hu Li, Fenghao Liu, Youming Hou, Lu Peng, Yuange Duan
Published: 2026

Background

Metazoan adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) mRNA editing resembles A-to-G variation and provides epitranscriptomic plasticity that avoids the pleiotropic effect of DNA mutations [1, 2]. Such temporospatial flexibility conferred by RNA editing is particularly useful in eusocial animals which need to generate various phenotypically distinct social castes using the same set of genomes. Studies in several eusocial insects like honeybee (Apis mellifera) [3, 4], bumblebee (Bombus terrestris) [5], and leaf-cutting ant (Acromyrmex echinatior) [6] have identified a few caste-biased editing sites, providing an epigenetic explanation for the animals’ physiological and behavioral plasticity. However, among these hymenopteran RNA editomes, despite the hundreds of nonsynonymous editing (recoding) sites identified in bees, much fewer (only 57) recoding sites were reported in leaf-cutting ant, presumably due to the stringent mapping and variant calling procedures [6]. This quantity-accuracy trade-off limits our understanding of how general RNA editing is involved in the adaptation of eusocial ants.

Compared with the bee lineage (e.g. Apoidea), ant societies are typically larger, reaching millions of individuals [7]. Ants exhibit reproductive division of labor, pheromone-mediated communication, and long-term nest maintenance, and are therefore often described as “superorganisms” [8]. Based on such amazing eusociality of ants, more ant species need to be sequenced to replenish the A-to-I RNA recoding profile in the ant clade. However, the de novo identification of RNA editing sites is usually constrained by the quantity-accuracy trade-off because stringent pipelines can reduce false positive rates but might also exclude many true positive RNA editing events. In contrast, a recently developed orthology-based methodology alleviates such trade-off [3]. Known A-to-I editing sites from one species (e.g. in species A) serve as editing candidates in another species B. If A-to-G signals were detected in the species B transcriptomes at orthologous sites, then this variation is likely an authentic RNA editing event. Therefore, this orthology-based scheme for editing detection can be performed under looser criteria and retrieve more editing sites missed by the stringent pipeline [3]. Nevertheless, due to the lack of matched DNA resequencing, the newly retrieved editing sites should be interpreted with caution, and auxiliary evidence like the differential editing levels across samples can be helpful [9, 10].

In this study, we profiled the A-to-I RNA editome of red imported fire ant (Solenopsis invicta), a highly invasive and destructive ant species, discovering over one hundred editing sites located in coding region. Then, using the orthology-based methodology [3], we largely expanded the known editing sites in leaf-cutting ant A. echinatior, and revealed novel caste-specific recoding sites between gyne and worker. Taken together, by generating an RNA editing atlas for newly characterized red imported fire ant and performing evolutionary analyses, our study expands the RNA editome resources available for ants, highlights the effectiveness of the orthology-based methodology in RNA editing analysis, uncovers caste-specific recoding events, and provides candidate sites for future studies on the mechanisms underlying phenotypic and behavioral plasticity in eusocial insects.

Results

A-to-I RNA editome of red imported fire ant S. invicta

To replenish the ant RNA editome, we collected workers of red imported fire ant S. invicta from two habitats and sequenced the head transcriptomes and matched genomes (Fig. 1A and Additional file 1: Supplementary Table S1). These next generation sequencing (NGS) data were used for RNA editing identification. Using our and others’ well-established bioinformatic pipeline (Materials and Methods), we totally obtained 3833 RNA–DNA differences (RDDs), among which 2995 (78.1%) were A-to-G variations (Fig. 1B), suggesting high-confidence candidate A-to-I RNA editing sites under our filtering criteria (Additional file 2: Supplementary Table S2). The distribution of RNA editing level, defined as the edited reads divided by total reads covering a particular site, is skewed to low level and has a median value of 0.19 (Fig. 1C), consistent with the editing profile in leaf-cutting ant [6]. Next, in accordance with the editing distribution in most metazoans [11], 2626 (87.7%) of the A-to-I editing sites were located in introns, while we annotated 91 (3.0%) recoding and 25 (0.8%) synonymous sites (Fig. 1D). Recoding genes were enriched in functional pathways like synaptic transmission, ion channels, and circadian rhythms (Fig. 1E), which were typical neuronal functions known for RNA editing [12, 13].

Orthology-based methodology identifies novel recoding sites in A. echinatior

To understand the conservation level of the RNA editing sites, especially those recoding sites, identified in S. invicta, we performed the orthology-based methodology to project the S. invicta editing sites to the A. echinatior coding sequence (Fig. 2A). Among the 91 recoding and 25 synonymous sites in S. invicta, 61 recoding (67.0%) and 9 synonymous (36.0%) sites had a conserved adenosine in the A. echinatior CDS (but not necessarily edited), suggesting an overall higher conservation level for the genomic sequence corresponding to recoding sites.

Among these candidate adenosine sites, 45 CDS (coding sequence) editing sites (40 recoding and 5 synonymous sites) had editing detected in at least one of the nine A. echinatior samples (Materials and Methods). Interestingly, when we compared the 40 recoding sites retrieved by conservation analysis with the 57 recoding sites identified by the original A. echinatior study [6], we discovered that only 12 sites were overlapped, while 28 sites (49.1% of the 57 sites) were newly identified in A. echinatior (Fig. 2B). This result demonstrates the power and effectiveness of the orthology-based methodology in expanding the RNA editome. Note that in this manuscript, we either discuss the S. invicta editing sites alone or investigate those conserved editing sites in A. echinatior which were inferred from S. invicta sites. Moreover, all subsequent Sanger validation was done in S. invicta. Therefore, for simplicity, we will try to use the S. invicta gene IDs and genomic loci even when we are describing an A. echinatior editing site. This scheme is consistent throughout the manuscript.

Conserved editing sites in ants show caste specificity in A. echinatior

The original A. echinatior study highlighted two caste-specific recoding sites in gene nsl1 (non-specific lethal 1, a regulatory of NSL complex, also known as wah or waharan) [6]. Although this caste specificity was exciting since it advanced our understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms of caste differentiation, such number of differential editing sites (DESs) were too few given the limited number of total 57 recoding sites. To test whether we can obtain more caste-specific DESs using our new sites, we respectively employed the strict and loose criteria for defining DESs between gynes and workers (Materials and Methods).

Among our 40 recoding sites detected in A. echinatior, six sites were identified as DESs under strict criteria, meaning that they had significantly different editing levels between gynes and workers (Fig. 2C). All six DESs exhibited a “worker > gyne” trend. Notably, two recoding sites, p.988 Ser > Gly in transcript Sinv006584.1 and p.1008 Ile > Met in transcript Sinv006908.1, belonged to the newly retrieved sites (Fig. 2D and E). Gene Sinv006584 encodes an F-actin binding protein [14]. Sinv006908 encodes a regulator of Ty1 transposition protein 107 BRCT domain [15]. The Sinv006584.1 p.988 Ser > Gly editing event in S. invicta workers was validated by Sanger sequencing which exhibited similar editing level to that in NGS data (Fig. 2D). The p.1008 Ile > Met recoding in Sinv006908.1 will be validated in later section.

Under the loose criteria, two additional recoding DESs were identified, one of which is the p.950 Ser > Gly site in transcript Sinv010572.1 (nsl1, wah) (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, two Gln > Arg recoding sites in nsl1 were reported as DESs in the original study [6], and our work identifies an additional Ser > Gly DES in the same gene. Although the editing levels of this p.950 Ser > Gly site was low in S. invicta, it was successfully validated by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 3B and C).

Novel neuronally recoding genes in ants

To understand whether the 28 newly recovered A. echinatior recoding sites belong to previously known recoding genes or novel genes without reported recoding sites, we compared the genes bearing recoding sites in the original report [6] and our results. The 28 novel recoding sites mapped to 17 A. echinatior genes (and all our 40 recoding sites mapped to 21 genes), among which ten genes were not documented by Li et al.’s study (Fig. 4A). This gene set has expanded nearly half of the original recoding genes, and suggests that the orthology-based approach is powerful in advancing our understanding of the gene regulatory network of RNA editome in different species.

Red arrows indicate the editing sites, and red rectangles indicate the codons

The ten novel genes contained 17 editing sites in CDS (14 recoding and three synonymous sites) (Fig. 4B). For example, A. echinatior gene AECH14085 (S. invicta orthologous gene Sinv006791) encodes a SAC3/GANP protein that functions as a TREX-2 mRNA export complex [16], and has four recoding and two synonymous sites, but most sites have editing levels lower than 0.1. In contrast, gene AECH27275 (Sinv009265) encodes the transmembrane region of a neurotransmitter-gated ion-channel and has one recoding site (p.256 Asn > Asp) and one synonymous site (p.255 Leu > Leu) (Fig. 4C). The editing events on these two sites were validated by Sanger sequencing of S. invicta heads (Fig. 4D). Moreover, gene AECH17382 (Sinv006908) has two recoding sites in the same codon (p.1008 ATA), where the editing level at the third codon position (ATA > ATG, Ile > Met) is extremely high (Fig. 4C and D), and the S. invicta editing level at the first codon position (ATA > GTA, Ile > Val) is barely detectable (Fig. 4D), possibly derived from hitchhiking effect [17].

Discussion

The temporospatial plasticity conferred by RNA editing has been hypothesized to antagonize the pleiotropy of DNA mutations and facilitate the adaptation of organisms [18, 19]. However, apart from the few experimentally validated adaptive RNA editing sites in fungi [20, 21], the benefits of numerous RNA editing sites in animals remain unclear, and it is even speculated that the majority of editing sites might be promiscuous and non-adaptive [22–24]. This leaves an open question that how we can narrow down those functional and adaptive editing sites in animals.

Evolutionary genomic analysis on human and mouse A-to-I RNA editomes revealed that recoding events were overall less conserved than the neutral control, indicating that recoding might not be utilized as a major means of molecular diversifier [23]. Only very few recoding events were broadly conserved across mammals [25], among which the Q > R recoding at glutamate receptor GRIA2 was the most conserved and was the only experimentally validated indispensable recoding event required for neuronal homeostasis [26, 27]. In contrast, in invertebrates like cephalopods, tens of thousands of recoding sites were identified and appeared broadly conserved across multiple octopus, squid, and cuttlefish species, suggesting a positive selection on recoding events [13, 28]. In addition to inter-species conservation, the flexibility of editing level under different environmental conditions served as another criterion to choose candidate editing sites for functional assays [29, 30]. In insects, RNA editing has been extensively studied in Drosophila [31, 32], where the recoding events were also positively selected [2], but the overall abundance of editing sites was much lower than cephalopods, with less than ten thousand editing sites in total and about three thousand editing sites in CDS [33]. Apart from this model insect, RNA editomes have been investigated in a few Hemiptera species like true bug [12], assassin bug [34], and invasive lace bug [35], as well as in several eusocial Hymenoptera insects as discussed below. In general, a few recoding sites in neuronal genes were broadly conserved across different insect orders, while the majority of editing sites were moderately conserved within each order or lineage-specific.

Eusocial insects represent a miracle in evolution where different social castes have distinct division of labor [7]. Given the fact that the behaviorally and physiologically differentiated castes came from the same (or a few) queen(s), eusocial organisms should be in great demand for epigenomic regulation to achieve such phenotypic plasticity [36]. Compared to other RNA modifications (e.g. m6A) which functionally rely on reader proteins [37–39], A-to-I RNA editing itself can function as a mutation and increase molecular diversity [1, 40, 41]. Previous works revealed a few caste-specific and conditionally regulated editing sites in honeybee and bumblebee [3, 5, 10]. But for leaf-cutting ant A. echinatior, only two caste-specific recoding sites were revealed due to the limited number of total recoding sites identified from the highly stringent bioinformatic pipeline [6]. In addition to these species, RNA editome has not yet been studied in other hymenopterans.

Several strategies can expand the RNA editing repertoire and potentially retrieve more caste-specific sites in eusocial Hymenoptera. (1) Obtaining a large number of additional transcriptomes/resequencing of the same species (e.g. A. echinatior) and hoping that more editing sites can be identified. This strategy is of high cost and low significance. (2) As we did in this study. We obtained the relevant data and identified RNA editing sites in another ant species S. invicta, and then aligned the S. invicta editing sites to the A. echinatior genome and retrieved novel editing sites, including potential caste-specific sites filtered out by the original study [6].

Our strategy has the following benefits: (1) Since S. invicta itself is an important invasive species, its transcriptome and RNA editome alone can serve as valuable resource for future study; (2) As explained, aligning the S. invicta editing sites to the A. echinatior genome identified new editing sites or genes. These sites/genes are conserved between two ants. For RNA editing sites, cross-species conservation itself is a strong indicator of functional importance [42]; (3) We directly identified new caste-specific recoding sites using the A. echinatior transcriptomes freely available from the original study [6]. This convenience omits the need for obtaining new samples of different castes of either ant species.

Indeed, the samples used in this study were collected from nearby locations, such limited sample range might only capture a fraction of the species-wide RNA editome. Because the red imported fire ant is an invasive species, it might require phenotypic plasticity to achieve enhanced fitness for successful colonization. It is possible that more editing sites could be retrieved by including samples from more distant or diverse locations which encompass a comprehensive range of ant populations. This is an essential direction for future study. In the work presented here, we at least showed that the current S. invicta editome has successfully expanded the ant editome compared to previous A. echinatior work.

Moreover, one may argue that the Sanger validation on conserved editing sites was only performed in S. invicta, which cannot directly prove the editing in A. echinatior. Nevertheless, confirmation of editing in S. invicta is the prerequisite for conserved editing. A. echinatior is not accessible to us and we acknowledge that this is a limitation. Future works might validate editing in multiple species to show the merit of the orthology-based methodology in identification of novel conserved editing sites.

In conclusion, our work expands the RNA editome in ants, highlights the merits of the orthology-based methodology in identification of RNA editing sites, uncovers caste-specific recoding events in leaf-cutting ant A. echinatior, and provides candidate sites for future functional studies.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Red imported fire ant (Solenopsis invicta) colonies were sampled from two locations in Fuzhou, Fujian Province, China: (i) cropland on the Jinshan Campus, and (ii) a weedy wasteland area near the campus tennis courts.

Prior to collection, all equipment and containers (rubber gloves, rain boots, shovels, and the inner surfaces of 30-L plastic buckets) were dusted with talcum powder prepared in 75% ethanol to prevent escape. For each colony, the entire aboveground mound together with ~ 30 cm of the underlying soil was excavated and promptly placed into the prepared buckets. Samples were transported to the laboratory and kept undisturbed for 24 h to allow colony settling and nest re-establishment.

After stabilization, water was added dropwise (~ 1–2 drops s⁻1) until the soil was completely submerged. When ants assembled into a floating raft, the raft was gently collected using a strainer and transferred to talcum-coated plastic rearing boxes (35 × 25 × 10 cm). Colonies were maintained indoors at 25 ± 1 °C and 60–75% relative humidity. Each box contained a 9-cm Petri dish (perforated for ventilation and covered with aluminum foil) as a nesting chamber. Mealworms (Tenebrio molitor) and 20% honey solution were provided ad libitum until experiments.

Genome and transcriptome sequencing

Because of the small body size of workers, five individuals were pooled as one biological replicate to obtain sufficient RNA input while limiting excessive sample heterogeneity for RNA-editing analyses. Under a stereomicroscope, heads were separated using sterile forceps. Head tissues were immediately placed in 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. The remaining bodies (with the gut removed) were collected separately and frozen in liquid nitrogen for genomic DNA extraction.

Total RNA was isolated using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and genomic DNA was extracted with the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China), following the manufacturers’ instructions. Nucleic acid concentration and purity were evaluated with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to confirm suitability for downstream NGS sequencing.

NGS Sequencing was conducted at BGI (China) on the DNBSEQ platform. RNA libraries were prepared for strand-specific eukaryotic transcriptome sequencing, whereas DNA libraries were subjected to whole-genome resequencing. Both datasets were generated using paired-end 150-bp reads (PE150). For Sanger sequencing, all primers used in this study were listed in Additional file 1: Supplementary Table S3.

Note that since many RNA editing sites are associated with neuronal functions, it might be reasonable to dissect brains, rather than use heads, for RNA sequencing. While we acknowledge that brain transcriptomes might be more delicate, here, we provide the following rationales for our design to sequence heads: (1) Small body size for S. invicta. The dissection of ant brains is skillful and must be operated in RNA-later solution and under stereomicroscopes. Considering the trade-off between tissue purity and RNA degradation during dissection, our experience tells us that RNA quality is more essential in RNA editing studies, convincing us to use ant heads. (2) In animals (not limited to insects), RNA editing has been detected in many tissues outside the brain, such as antenna, eyes, and other nervous/non-nervous systems [13, 43, 44]. Therefore, using ant heads, not only brains, to identify RNA editing sites is plausible. (3) Head transcriptomes are shown to work well in RNA editing studies. For extremely small insects like invasive lace bug, the “pooled heads RNA plus pooled body DNA” strategy has been proven successful in the identification of bona fide RNA editing repertoire [35]. Even for model insect Drosophila, head transcriptomes are widely used in RNA editing studies [44, 45]. For the above reasons, we used ant heads to perform RNA sequencing.

Mapping the RNA-seq and DNA-seq data

For each sample, DNA resequencing reads were aligned to the S. invicta reference genome sequence (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_016802725.1/; UNIL_Sinv_3.0) using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 with default settings parameters [46]. The RNA-seq reads were mapped to the genome using two aligners STAR v2.4.2 [47] and BWA-MEM v0.7.17, respectively. Default parameters were used. Only concordant alignments supported by both mappers were retained to mitigate spurious RNA-editing calls arising from misalignment [48]. For both DNA-seq and RNA-seq, alignments were further filtered by mapping quality (q > 10). Variant calling was done by samtools mpileup v1.11 [49], requiring base quality Q > 30 to reduce sequencing errors. Variants were annotated by software SnpEff version 4.5 [50], using the -canon option to restrict annotations to the canonical transcript of each gene (defined as the transcript with the longest CDS).

Identification of A-to-I RNA editing sites

At each variant position detected in either DNA-seq or RNA-seq, we recorded sequencing depth/coverage (Cov), the number of reads supporting the reference allele (Ref), and the number supporting the alternative allele (Alt). To assess whether a given variant could be explained by sequencing error, we applied a binomial test as in previous work [48]. Resulting P values were adjusted for multiple testing to obtain FDR values [51]. Sites with FDR < 0.05 were considered reliable variants; sites failing this threshold were treated as potential artefacts.

To define bona fide RNA editing events, we focused on RNA–DNA differences (RDDs) where reliable variation was present in RNA-seq (Cov ≥ 5 and FDR < 0.05) but not supported in DNA-seq despite adequate depth (Cov ≥ 10 and FDR > 0.05). A site satisfying these criteria within a sample was designated an “edited” site. For the two worker samples, only sites classified as edited in both samples were retained.

Orthology-based methodology to identify conserved editing sites

The RNA-seq of leaf-cutting ant were retrieved from a previous study [6], the data including nine samples: three gynes, three small workers, and three large workers.

Conserved editing sites across species were identified using our previously established pipeline [3], restricting analyses to editing sites located in coding sequences. Briefly, orthologous genes were defined as reciprocal best BLASTP hits between species (BLASTP v2.2.28; default parameters) [52]. Protein sequences and corresponding CDSs of orthologs were aligned using MAFFT v7.158b [53]. Editing sites from one species were projected onto the CDS of the other species according to the alignment. Candidate positions were then evaluated directly from transcriptomic data by aligning RNA-seq reads to CDS sequences with BWA-MEM v0.7.17 [46]. Sites showing A-to-G variation that passed the binomial test (FDR < 0.05) in at least one sample were regarded as reliable edited sites in the target species.

Differential RNA editing sites between different groups

Differential editing sites between two groups (e.g., three gynes versus six workers in the leaf-cutting ant dataset) were identified using either stringent or loose criteria. First, Fisher’s exact test was performed on pooled Ref and Alt read counts from the two groups to obtain a site-wise P value, where smaller values indicate stronger differences in pooled editing levels. In parallel, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were conducted on per-sample editing levels (three versus nine comparisons), yielding an additional P value per site. All P values were corrected for multiple testing to generate FDR values [51].

Under the stringent definition, differential editing sites (DESs) were required to satisfy FDR < 0.1 in both Fisher’s exact test and the Wilcoxon test. Under the loose definition, DESs were called by Fisher’s exact test alone with FDR < 0.1, consistent with the original leaf-cutting ant study [6].

Statistical tests

Statistical tests and graphical works were performed in R studio (R version 3.6.3).
