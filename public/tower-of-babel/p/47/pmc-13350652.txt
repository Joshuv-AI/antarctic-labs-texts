Safety and Feasibility of Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing Following Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement
Authors: Mohammed Kamareddine, Farah Olleik, Colleen Hanley, Scott Goldman, William A Gray, Ali Keramati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) is a novel therapy for severe tricuspid regurgitation. However, conduction disturbances requiring pacemaker implantation occur in up to 24.7% of patients. Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) has not been systematically evaluated post-TTVR, where lead passage across the prosthesis may compromise valve integrity. The objective of this paper was to report the feasibility, safety, and performance of LBBAP after TTVR.Case SummaryTwo pacemaker-naïve patients requiring pacing post-TTVR were retrospectively analyzed.DiscussionBoth patients underwent TTVR and developed high-grade atrioventricular block within 72 hours. LBBAP was successful in both cases with no procedural-related complications. At 30 days, pacing parameters remained unchanged with no device-related adverse events. Echocardiography at 1 and 30 days demonstrated ≤ mild tricuspid regurgitation, no lead-valve interaction, and stable right ventricular function.Take-Home MessagesLBBAP post-TTVR is feasible with excellent short-term safety and performance while maintaining valve integrity. Our series reinforces its safety and adds systematic echocardiographic follow-up.Key words: atrioventricular block, conduction system pacing, lead-valve interaction, left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP), transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR)

Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is linked to excess mortality and repeated heart failure admissions; however, it is historically undertreated. Transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) has emerged as a promising therapy for severe TR. The Edwards EVOQUE orthotopic valve has emerged as a novel tool to manage tricuspid valve insufficiency and achieved near-complete TR elimination in the TRISCEND II (Investigation of Safety and Clinical Efficacy after Replacement of Tricuspid Valve with Transcatheter Device) trial. However, 24.7% of pacemaker-naïve participants require a new permanent pacemaker (PPM) at 30 days.1 The need for pacing in this context presents unique challenges because a traditional transvenous right ventricular lead must traverse the newly implanted tricuspid bioprosthetic valve. This raises concerns about potential valve interference, lead entrapment, and TR recurrence, prompting consideration of alternative implantation sites/pacing strategies.

In the TRISCEND II trial, of the 45 patients who required a pacemaker, 1 patient received a dual-chamber leadless system; 8 received conventional dual-chamber devices (6 with a right-atrial + coronary sinus configuration, 1 with epicardial leads, and 1 with right atrial + right ventricular leads placed after surgical tricuspid valve replacement); 18 underwent single-chamber leadless pacemakers; 11 obtained single-chamber transvenous units (5 via coronary sinus left ventricular [LV] leads, 3 via isolated LV leads, 1 via a right atrial lead, 1 epicardial lead, and 1 right ventricular lead); and 6 patients underwent implantation of biventricular (triple-chamber) cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) systems (4 using a single coronary sinus lead, 1 with 2 coronary sinus leads, and 1 with dual LV leads). This wide range of pacing strategies highlights the current lack of consensus and variability in expert opinion regarding the optimal pacemaker type for patients undergoing TTVR.

Left bundle area pacing (LBBAP) represents a paradigm shift in the field of pacing, merging the physiological benefits of conduction system pacing with technical reliability. Engagement of the left bundle and its robust arborizing Purkinje network results in rapid synchronous ventricular activation. This pacing modality has outperformed right ventricular pacing and coronary sinus pacing in some limited studies.2, 3, 4

Here we present the first 2 cases of LBBAP post-EVOQUE, highlighting its feasibility, safety, and the critical role of lead design and technology in preserving valve integrity.

Methods

Design

This was a single-center, retrospective case series.

Patient selection and data sources

Two pacemaker-naïve patients developed guideline-based pacing indications after TTVR implantation and underwent LBBAP. Data were derived from operative notes, device interrogations, and echocardiograms.

Follow-up

Key follow-up data included the feasibility of achieving selective left bundle branch pacing, absence of mechanical interference with the tricuspid prosthesis, acute capture thresholds, short-term electrical performance over 30 days, and preservation of prosthetic valve function. Device interrogations were performed before discharge and at 30 days for both patients. Echocardiography was performed at baseline, postimplant (24 hours), and 30 days in both patients.

Results

Baseline characteristics and key procedural and follow-up outcomes are summarized in Table 1, Table 2, Table 3. Both patients underwent successful LBBAP implantation with stable electrical performance at 30 days, had no device-related complications, and had preserved prosthetic valve function. Patient 1

Patient 1 was an 84-year-old woman with a past medical history of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction with a LV ejection fraction of 35% to 45%, multiple comorbidities including atypical atrial flutter with 2:1 conduction, and severe TR. She underwent successful implantation of a 52-mm EVOQUE valve with significant improvement in TR intraoperatively (improvement from severe to mild). During the procedure, she underwent electrical cardioversion with restoration of normal sinus rhythm. However, postoperative electrocardiogram showed newly developed right bundle branch block and progressive first-degree atrioventricular (AV) block with PR interval from 174 to 338 milliseconds. Over the course of the next 24 hours, conduction system abnormalities progressed to persistent high-grade AV block (2:1 second-degree AV block) (Figure 1A), and the decision was made to proceed with implantation of a dual-chamber pacemaker.

The patient was brought to the electrophysiology laboratory. Through a 9-F sheath, a steerable C304-His sheath was inserted and advanced into the right ventricle over the wire under alternating right anterior oblique/left anterior oblique views to ensure no interaction of the wire, sheath, and/or lead with the prosthetic tricuspid valve. The sheath was positioned just beyond the valve cage. Using the sheath, a Medtronic 3830 pacing lead into the septum until selective left bundle area capture was obtained (isoelectric, left ventricular activation time [LVAT]: 74 milliseconds, interpeak interval: 52 milliseconds, rSR' pattern in lead V1). Nonselective capture was also observed at higher outputs as shown in Figure 1B, supporting physiological LBBAP (Figure 1B). The other lead parameters were stable with a nonselective LV capture threshold 0.375 V at 0.4 milliseconds, R-wave 8 mV, and impedance 627 Ω (Table 3). The sheath was then carefully brought back over the lead and slit under fluoroscopic guidance. The right atrial lead and the generator were inserted in standard fashion. The dual-chamber pacemaker (Azure XT DR) was programmed in DDD mode with settings of 80 to 120 beats/min. The total procedure time was 94 minutes (fluoroscopy: 7.2 minutes), with no complications; the procedure was successful on the first attempt (Table 3).

Echocardiography after implantation showed stable LV function, mild transvalvular TR, and no evidence of impingement of the leaflets by the lead. This remained unchanged at 30 days with follow-up echocardiography (Figure 1C, Table 2), demonstrating a well-seated valve with a mean gradient of 5 mm Hg at heart rate of 100 beats/min, stable mild TR, and unchanged right ventricular function (moderate dysfunction). At 30 days, ventricular pacing burden was 51%. Capture threshold was 0.75 V at 0.4 milliseconds, R-wave 19.8 mV, and impedance 570 Ω (Table 3). No device-related adverse events occurred. Right ventricular function remained moderately to severely reduced, consistent with baseline. The device interrogation showed stable parameters with electrocardiogram morphology, consistent with an appropriate left bundle area pacing morphology.

Patient 2

An 82-year-old man with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction with an LV ejection fraction of 65% to 70%, coronary artery disease, permanent atrial fibrillation with baseline slow ventricular rate on no AV nodal blocking agents, and torrential TR underwent successful implantation of a 52-mm EVOQUE valve. TR improved significantly to mild transvalvular and trace paravalvular regurgitation. The patient developed complete AV block within 24 hours after the procedure (Figure 2A); therefore, the decision was made to proceed with implantation of a single-chamber pacemaker.

The implantation technique was similar to patient 1 and successful on the first insertion attempt with the lead positioned in the left bundle region (Figure 3). LBBAP demonstrated output-dependent transition between selective and nonselective capture during threshold testing, supporting physiological conduction system recruitment. At discharge, the device was programmed at a higher output, and the paced rhythm therefore reflected nonselective LBBAP with isoelectric stim to QRS interval, LVAT of 76 milliseconds, QR pattern in V1, interpeak interval of 26 milliseconds, and discordant polarity in leads II and III as shown in Figure 2A. Selective and nonselective left bundle capture was obtained (isoelectric stim to QRS interval, LVAT: 76 milliseconds, QR pattern in V1, interpeak interval: 26 milliseconds, discordant polarity in leads II and III), as shown in Figure 2B and Table 3. Procedural duration was 52 minutes (fluoroscopy: 6.5 minutes) with no complications. Other lead parameters were within acceptable range with device interrogation at implantation demonstrating a LBBAP capture threshold of 0.8 V at 0.4 milliseconds with an R-wave sensing amplitude of 14.8 mV and lead impedance of 1,064 Ω.

Echocardiography after implantation revealed stable LV function and trace transvalvular TR, which was unchanged from intraprocedural echocardiogram (Figure 2C, Table 2). There was no evidence of valve impingement. The patient had an uneventful recovery and was discharged home.

At 30 days, ventricular pacing burden was 86%. Capture threshold was 0.5 V at 0.4 milliseconds, R-wave was >20 mV, and impedance was 513 Ω (Table 3). The EVOQUE valve remained well seated with a mean gradient of 4 mm Hg at heart rate of 60 beats/min, there was no residual TR or lead impingement, and RV function was unchanged (moderate dysfunction). No device-related adverse events occurred.

Discussion

This case series demonstrates the feasibility of LBBAP after transfemoral EVOQUE TTVR, with early evidence of safety for both the pacing system and the bioprosthetic valve. To our knowledge, these are the first reported cases to include systematic echocardiographic follow-up after LBBAP implantation in post-TTVR patients. Both patients had successful LBBAP lead implantation with low acute capture thresholds and showed stable pacing outputs at short-term follow-up. Serial imaging confirmed that a transvenous lead traversing the EVOQUE prosthesis did not cause significant acute valve dysfunction or regurgitation; TR stayed trivial or mild, and valve integrity was preserved.

Our experience addresses an important management gap for the growing number of patients receiving TTVR. High-grade AV block is relatively frequent after TTVR; however, the optimal pacing solution has not been well defined.5,6 Conventional right ventricular apical leads risk impinging on, or becoming trapped by, the prosthesis. Surgical tricuspid replacements therefore favor epicardial wires or leadless pacemakers, and the same dilemma now exists after transfemoral procedures. Leadless systems remove the lead-valve interaction but cannot deliver CRT, or defibrillation, have finite battery life, and are difficult to retrieve.7,8 A transvenous solution combining physiological activation with minimal valve disruption remains desirable. Leadless pacemakers were considered in both cases and no absolute contraindications to leadless pacing (eg, inferior vena cava filter, mechanical tricuspid valve) were present. Patient 1 developed high-grade AV block after cardioversion to sinus rhythm and was managed with a dual-chamber system to preserve AV and interventricular synchrony. In addition, the anticipated ventricular pacing burden favored a physiological pacing strategy rather than right ventricular–only pacing. Patient 2 could have been a candidate for single-chamber leadless pacing; however, because complete heart block resulted in an expected high ventricular pacing burden, we favored conduction system pacing to minimize the risk of pacing-induced dyssynchrony and to preserve physiological activation.

In the TRISCEND II pivotal trial of EVOQUE TTVR, pacemaker practice was highly variable: of 45 patients requiring pacemakers, 1 received a dual-chamber leadless system, 8 received conventional dual-chamber devices, 18 received single-chamber leadless units, 11 received single-chamber transvenous systems, and 6 received biventricular CRT implants. This heterogeneity underscores the absence of a preferred strategy.1

Conduction system pacing offers a physiological alternative that may limit tricuspid interference while preserving ventricular synchrony. His-bundle pacing (HBP) can occasionally be achieved at the annulus without extending into the right ventricle. A recent case report by Estévez-Paniagua et al9 illustrated HBP in a patient after TTVR, where the pacing lead was implanted at the septal annulus and did not protrude through the bioprosthetic valve; at 15-day follow-up, there was no residual TR and the prosthetic valve function remained intact. Still, the prosthetic frame can distort the periannular region, making secure fixation difficult, and HBP is prone to higher thresholds or loss of capture—especially when block is infranodal. LBBAP bypasses these limitations by targeting the distal conduction system deeper in the septum, typically yielding lower, more stable thresholds and broader capture zones.

In our series, we selected Medtronic 3830 lead for conduction system pacing due to its unique design features that make it particularly suitable for this patient population. The 3830 lead has a narrow 4.1-F profile and a lumenless cable-based design which may reduce mechanical interaction with the leaflets.

LBBAP bypasses potential AV node and His bundle injury by pacing the distal conduction system, providing a more physiological alternative to traditional right ventricular pacing. This approach yielded excellent acute and subacute pacing parameters and QRS interval durations of 120 to 130 milliseconds, reflecting near-physiological ventricular activation. Consistent with prior studies, low capture thresholds (<1.0 V) and high sensing amplitudes (∼10 mV) were achieved and remained stable at 1-month follow-up. No lead dislodgment or loss of capture occurred. LBBAP may also reduce pacing-induced right ventricular dyssynchrony, which is particularly relevant in TTVR patients with heart failure; however, no short-term change was observed. By engaging the native conduction system, LBBAP may preserve or improve LV function in pacing-dependent patients.4,10

Our series extends these findings to tricuspid valve replacement, showing LBBAP can be safely performed post-TTVR with excellent pacing outcomes comparable with those without a tricuspid prosthesis. The presence of the EVOQUE valve did not hinder engagement of the left bundle. Moreover, early echocardiographic follow-up confirmed that the bioprosthetic valve tolerated a transvenous pacing lead without immediate dysfunction. This systematic imaging follow-up distinguishes our report—prior studies emphasized valve performance and clinical outcomes, whereas we specifically assessed valve-lead interaction. Both valves remained well functioning (no significant regurgitation or stenosis) at 1 month, confirming short-term valve safety.

Despite its advantages, LBBAP presents several challenges in patients with prosthetic tricuspid valves. The technique requires transseptal lead fixation through the basal right ventricular septum, which necessitates careful navigation across or near the tricuspid valve apparatus. In the setting of TTVR, this can be technically demanding due to the presence of a rigid valve frame, which may limit sheath maneuverability and increase the risk of mechanical interaction with the prosthesis. Care should be taken to avoid traversing any potential space between the valve frame and the tricuspid annulus.11,12 Although the Medtronic 3830 lead's small profile may reduce leaflet interference, there remains a theoretical risk of lead entrapment, abrasion, or leaflet tethering, particularly if the lead traverses or lies adjacent to the valve cage. These considerations underscore the importance of meticulous technique, fluoroscopic and electrogram guidance, and individualized procedural planning in patients undergoing LBBAP after TTVR.

Although the outcome of our patients was encouraging, several limitations warrant discussion. Follow-up in our series was limited to 1 month. The long-term effect of LBBAP pacing lead on prosthetic valve remains unclear. Ongoing surveillance will be essential to assess valve integrity over time. Additionally, the choice between HBP, LBBAP, or other forms of pacing should be individualized. HBP may be preferable in cases of AV block, thereby avoiding transvalvular hardware. Conversely, LBBAP may be more appropriate for infra-Hisian block or when long-term stability is a priority.

Conclusions

LBBAP after EVOQUE TTVR was feasible and safe in this series, offering a practical solution to post-TTVR conduction block. Early follow-up shows that this strategy can provide reliable pacing support and preserve tricuspid prosthesis function. Larger studies are needed to confirm these findings, but our report provides important initial evidence to guide electrophysiological management in TTVR patients.
