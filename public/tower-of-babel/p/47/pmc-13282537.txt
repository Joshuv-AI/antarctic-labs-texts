MAX-SHOCK: A Pragmatic Randomized Controlled Trial Comparing Biphasic Defibrillators Used in Routine Cardioversion of Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Mehrdad Golian, F. Daniel Ramirez, Mark Cleland, Alper Aydin, Martin S. Green, Andres Klein, Girish Nair, Pablo Nery, Calum Redpath, Darryl Davis, Simon P. Hansom, Zhenyu Li, George A. Wells, David H. Birnie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atrial fibrillation (AF) frequently requires rhythm control with electrical cardioversion, with approximately 10%-20% of patients undergoing electrical cardioversion annually. Despite its widespread use, cardioversion failure remains common, and clinical practice varies considerably with respect to shock energy, pad positioning, and defibrillator technology. Commonly used biphasic defibrillators differ in not only maximum energy output but also proprietary waveform characteristics and impedance-compensation algorithms, factors that may independently influence cardioversion success. To date, these devices have not been compared directly in a randomized clinical trial.The maximum energy shocks trial is a single-centre, prospective, randomized, single-blinded controlled study comparing 2 widely used external defibrillators: the Physio-Control LIFEPAK 20 (Physio Control, location), capable of delivering up to 360 J, and the ZOLL Medical R-Series (ZOLL Medical, Chelmsford, MA), with a maximum programmable energy of 200 J. Rather than isolating shock energy alone, the trial evaluates real-world device performance, recognizing that waveform design and adaptive algorithms differ among manufacturers. We hypothesize that the 360-J–capable defibrillator will be associated with a higher rate of successful cardioversion when used within a standardized stepwise protocol.Adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with persistent AF scheduled for elective cardioversion are randomized in a single-blinded fashion. The primary outcome is successful cardioversion, defined as restoration of sinus rhythm with at least 2 consecutive sinus or atrial-paced beats. A total of 356 patients will be enrolled to assess superiority.This trial represents the first randomized comparison of real-world defibrillator system performance in AF cardioversion and will help inform future cardioversion protocols and defibrillator selection.Clinical Trial RegistrationNCT06556667.

Symptomatic atrial fibrillation (AF), is often treated acutely by electrical cardioversion (ECV) to restore normal heart rhythm.1, 2, 3, 4 An estimated 1.5 million cardioversions are performed annually in the US,5,6 but a proportion of these procedures fail.3,7, 8, 9 The reported success rates of ECV with modern biphasic defibrillators range broadly and are derived from relatively small cohorts. In the Euro Heart Survey on AF, cardioversion was successful in 88% of 712 patients, whereas the the Biphasic Energy Selection for Transthoracic Cardioversion of Atrial Fibrillation (BEST AF) trial reported a 90% success rate among 380 patients.10,11 Considerable research has explored factors that have the potential to improve success, including energy escalation, paddle position, and pressure application. However, one variable that has not been explored is the maximum energy level available in a defibrillator. Most modern biphasic defibrillators have a maximum available energy delivery level of 200 J; however at least one vendor has a maximum of 360 J. Although the idea that more energy would lead to more success might seem to be true intuitively, other variables are important, including the waveform of energy delivery.

The primary objective of this study is to evaluate whether using a biphasic defibrillator—LIFEPAK20 (Physio-Control Inc, Redmond, WA)—with a maximum energy setting of 360 J leads to an increased success rate of ECV in AF patients, as compared to the R series defibrillator (ZOLL Medical Corporation, Chelmsford, MA), which has a maximum 200-J energy setting.

Methods

Trial design

Maximum energy shocks (MAX-SHOCK) is a single-centre, randomized, prospective, single blinded trial (NCT06556667) designed to evaluate the efficacy of a 360 J–maximum shock defibrillator (Physio-Control LIFEPAK20), compared to that of a 200 J–maximum shock defibrillator (ZOLL Medical R Series).

Study population

Inclusion criteria

Patients who met the following criteria were included: age > 18 years; having persistent AF; being scheduled for elective cardioversion.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with the following were excluded: (i) known left-atrial appendage thrombus; (ii) contraindication to appropriate anticoagulation; or (iii) inclusion in an overlapping cardioversion-related randomized controlled trial.

Study procedures

Pre-randomization

Subjects will be assessed by the investigator as well as research staff. The following data will be collected at a minimum, per standard of care: (i) age, sex, and race; (ii) height and weight; (iii) Congestive Heart Failure, Hypertension, Age ≥ 75 Years, Diabetes Mellitus, Stroke, Vascular Disease, Age 65 to 74 Years, Sex Category CHA2DS2-VASc score; (iv) comorbidities such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, amyloid, obstructive sleep apnea, hyperthyroidism, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; (v) New York Heart Association class; (vi) current medications; (vii) duration of uninterrupted AF; and (viii) most recent echocardiogram results (including left ventricular ejection fraction, left atrial volume index, and valve lesions).

Practical primary outcome

The primary outcome is ECV success defined as ≥ 2 consecutive sinus beats or captured atrial-paced beats in patients with implanted cardiac devices. This definition is consistent with the clinically relevant distinction made in current guidelines between failed ECV and successful ECV with failure to maintain sinus rhythm (ie, with early recurrent atrial fibrillation). Primary endpoints will be adjudicated by a blinded endpoint committee.

Secondary outcomes are as follows: (i) first shock success; (ii) sustained success (defined as sustained sinus or atrial paced rhythm documented on 12-lead ECG prior to hospital discharge); (iii) median number of shocks (steps 1-3); (iv) number of crossovers in each group (see step 4); and (v) sex difference in primary outcome.

Sample Size

Recent studies have suggested that an 88%-90% success rate is achieved with various cardioversion protocols.10,11 Our previous study found a 99.2% success rate (386 of 389) with the Physio-Control LIFEPAK 20 using the same algorithm as in Figure 1.9 With a minimal clinically important difference of 6%, as assessed by steering committee, with 80% power and a 1-sided test for alpha = 0.05, the sample size required is 178 per group.

Planned analyses

The primary analysis will be intention to treat. Descriptive statistics including 95% confidence intervals will be calculated for all baseline variables using means, medians, standard deviations, and interquartile ranges for continuous outcomes, and rates and proportions for discrete outcomes for each treatment arm without P-values. For the primary outcome, 2 arms will be compared using the χ2 test. Baseline characteristics of the treatment arms will be compared. If any clinically significant differences are identified, a logistic regression analysis will be conducted to compare the primary endpoints between the 2 treatment arms, adjusting for these differences. Several secondary analyses will be conducted, including exploring the effect of sex on all outcomes. To make possible the analysis of sex-specific differences in outcomes, we will attempt to recruit an approximately equal number of male and female patients in each arm. Safety endpoints (including skin rash and stroke) will be analyzed in the intention-to-treat population and summarized by treatment arm as counts and proportions with 95% confidence intervals. Between-group comparisons will be performed using the Fisher exact test. Effect estimates will be reported as risk differences and risk ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Study setting and timelines

The study will be conducted at the University of Ottawa Heart Institute, a large tertiary cardiac care centre. The number of patients eligible for this trial is approximately 250 per year. We conservatively estimate that we can enroll 200 patients annually; recruitment started in April 2025.

Institutional review board

The study was approved by the University of Ottawa Heart Institute review board. Both arms of the study are considered within reasonable clinical practice in Canada, and hence, a waiver of individual consent to participate in the study was approved by the ethical review committee.

Discussion

ECV is a critical aspect of the acute management in patients with symptomatic AF, but it is unsuccessful in about 10% of patients.1, 2, 3,8,12 Current practice patterns, including initial energy selection, pad positioning, number of shocks, and applied pressure, are often guided by individual preference or institutional habit rather than robust comparative evidence.1, 2, 3 Notably, despite the widespread use of modern biphasic defibrillators, no randomized trial to date has directly compared the efficacy of commonly used devices.

Despite the ubiquity of cardioversion for AF, national and international guidelines offer limited and sometimes contradictory guidance on device selection and shock energy.1, 2, 3 For example, the Canadian and European Society of Cardiology guidelines give no recommendation on the starting energy dose or the type of biphasic defibrillator machine to use, whereas many North American centres routinely use higher starting energy, at 200 J. Manufacturer guidelines also vary and do not offer direct comparisons or comparative evidence-based protocols.

The significant variability in current clinical cardioversion protocols has been described. For example, a recent survey of 57 European centres found that nearly two-thirds of hospitals started with a 100-J biphasic shock for AF, whereas the remaining third started with 200 J.13 Considerable differences in electrode placement were also reported in this survey, with 58.7% of centres using an anterolateral position and the remainder using an anteroposterior approach.13 We and others have shown previously that physicians seldom apply sufficient force, even when prompted to do so, and even when using handheld paddles.9 Despite this, only limited contemporary data are available to guide procedural improvements to make to achieve higher ECV success rates. Variability in energy levels, electrode placement, and applied force during cardioversion are factors that potentially affect outcomes but lack standardization based on modern evidence.1, 2, 3,13

One of the key procedural elements in ECV is the selection of the shock energy level. Although some practitioners escalate in 50-100–J increments, others begin with high-energy shocks at the outset. The rationale for using lower initial energies has been based largely on historical safety concerns, but contemporary biphasic waveforms have improved safety profiles markedly.9,10,14 Moreover, low-energy shocks may increase the risk of failed cardioversion, necessitating use of multiple shocks and a higher cumulative level of energy delivery, potentially increasing both patient discomfort and procedural inefficiency.10,13,15 On the other hand, initiating cardioversion with a higher energy level may increase first-shock success and reduce the total number of shocks required.3 The ability to deliver a higher level of defibrillation energy and current may be especially advantageous in patients with characteristics associated with reduced cardioversion success, including significant obesity, elevated transthoracic impedance, and previous failed cardioversion attempts.

Although the idea that more energy would lead to more success might seem correct intuitively, other variables are important, including the waveform of energy delivery. An important difference between the 2 most used defibrillators in clinical practice—the Physio-Control LIFEPAK 20 and the ZOLL Medical R-Series—is not only their maximum energy output but also their waveform technology. The former uses a rectilinear biphasic (RLB) waveform, whereas the latter employs a biphasic truncated exponential (BTE) waveform. These differences in waveform and current delivery may significantly influence the effectiveness of cardioversion.15,16

From a patient-centred perspective, a successful cardioversion on the first attempt may minimize sedation time, reduce procedure duration, reduce chest-wall irritation, and it likely improves the overall experience. Failed cardioversion, on the other hand, may necessitate repeat procedures, escalation of antiarrhythmic therapy, or prolonged hospitalization.1,3,17,18 Therefore, improving the likelihood of first-shock success carries both clinical- and institutional-level relevance. Such improvement also helps guide institutions in deciding on defibrillator replacement and purchases.

Several small studies have attempted to optimize cardioversion protocols, including variations in pad positioning and applied pressure, with mixed results.8,9,19,20 However, these studies have not accounted for the defibrillator device itself as a source of variability. To our knowledge, the MAX-SHOCK trial is the first randomized controlled study designed to directly compare 2 leading biphasic defibrillators with differing maximum energy capacities (360 J vs 200 J) and differing biphasic waveform delivery, using a standardized stepwise protocol. We hypothesize that the 360-J defibrillator will demonstrate superior success rates compared to the 200-J device, especially at lower chest impedance levels. Secondary outcomes, including the number of shocks required, and sex differences will help with the broader clinical implications of defibrillator selection.

Should this trial demonstrate superiority of higher-energy shocks or one defibrillator over the other, it will provide the guidance for evidence-based recommendations that could standardize and improve cardioversion practices nationally and internationally.

Conclusion

This randomized study will be the first to compare the Physio Control LIFEPAK 20, with a maximum energy of 360 J, to the ZOLL Medical R-Series, with a maximum energy of 200 J. No data are currently available that directly compare the cardioversion efficacy of these 2 commonly used defibrillators. The MAX-SHOCK trial will provide guidance and standardization on device selection and further reinforce the efficacy of the standardized protocol at atrial fibrillation cardioversion, which may improve patient outcomes and aid in device selection in cardioversion centres.
