Triangular reentrant honeycomb metamaterial structure for broadband sound attenuation using shape memory polymers
Authors: Musa’ab Ejaz, Nabihah Sallih, Yasir Mujahid, Venkata Somi Reddy Janga, Mohamed Latheef, Tong Boon Tang, Leong Yin Liong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPassive noise cancellation (PNC) earmuffs are an effective solution for mitigating workplace noise exposure, ensuring compliance with the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) and Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) guidelines. However, conventional earmuffs require removal to allow for peer communication, exposing users to hazardous noise. This study addresses this issue by proposing a triangular reentrant honeycomb metamaterial design that facilitates the passage of low-to-mid-frequency waves (e.g. speech up to 3000 Hz) while blocking high frequency noise. Finite element modelling was used to evaluate the bandgaps created by the unit cell design and the effects of lattice layers on the resultant frequency response. Experiments performed using an impedance tube showed that the optimized metamaterial design achieved a peak sound absorption coefficient of 1 at 2392 Hz and a transmission loss of 10–11 dB within the 3000–6400 Hz frequency range. This design was then tested as an insert of an earmuff to characterize speech intelligibility and hearing protection using pink noise up to 95 dB. The results demonstrate the potential of such design for PNC earmuff applications, enabling sufficient peer-to-peer communication while maintaining noise protection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20643-0.Keywords: Metamaterial design, Bragg scattering, Local resonance, Helmholtz resonator, Sound absorption coefficient, Transmission lossSubject terms: Mechanical engineering, Acoustics

Introduction

Metamaterials are artificially engineered structures that exhibit unconventional mechanical and acoustic properties, which could be achieved by designing unit cell and lattice structures. Metamaterials exhibit specialized properties, such as negative mass density, negative Poisson’s ratio, and high stiffness1–3, which cannot be achieved by conventional materials. Another unique feature that metamaterials exhibit is bandgap formation4–6. These bandgaps or stopbands are formed when elastic/acoustic waves cannot pass through the metamaterial structures at specific frequencies, allowing selective frequency filtration.

The mechanisms by which these bandgaps are formed are Bragg scattering, local resonance combined with Helmholtz resonance, and inertial amplification. For Bragg scattering mechanism, the wavelength of the incoming wave matches with a lattice constant of the unit cell in the lattice crystal structure, causing a destructive interference between the incident waves and reflected waves, resulting in a bandgap as demonstrated by Yi et al.7 and Li et al.8. This concept works very well for mid-to-high frequencies (greater than 1000 Hz). However, the lattice constant needs to be sufficiently large for lower frequencies to match the larger wavelength (at more minor frequencies) based on the universal wave equation. On the other hand, for the local resonance mechanism, the wave propagation is controlled by adding resonators in a lattice structure, which creates anti-resonance frequencies and hence causes wave attenuation. This out-of-plane mechanism can also be achieved by (i) compliant structures with sharp corners and edges which effectively act as resonators9–11, or (ii) by employing the well-known Helmholtz resonator concept12,13. In comparison to Bragg scattering mechanism, local resonance does not need to match the wavelength of the incoming waves as resonators created in the structure block the waves from passing through; hence, the size of unit cells is reduced to almost two orders of magnitude. However, the local resonance mechanism usually produces narrow band gaps, causing it to be unable to attenuate wider frequency ranges effectively.

In contrast to local resonance, inertial amplification involves a mechanical mechanism to magnify the resonator’s effective inertia. This method generates anti-resonance frequencies, where the enhanced inertia cancels the elastic force14–16. Inertial amplification allows a larger bandgap to be achieved with almost twenty times less mass when compared with local resonance. In addition, the depth of bandgap profiles achieved by inertial amplification is higher than other mechanisms. However, by adding lightweight inert elements, the overall structure stiffness decreases; hence, the applications of inertial amplification are minimal.

Since different mechanisms work for other frequency ranges, past researchers have tried to combine multiple mechanisms within a unit structure to achieve more attenuation at a wider range of frequencies. A combination of local resonance and Bragg scattering has been widely studied for various applications since local resonance works well for lower frequencies, while Bragg scattering attenuates at higher frequencies as shown by Tikani et al. and Panahi et al.17,18. A similar combination of these mechanisms was also shown to work for a broad frequency range by Timorian et al.19. In addition, a combination of Bragg scattering, local resonance, and inertial amplification was investigated by Mazzoti et al.20 in a unit cell design. Based on the selected mechanism, the unit cell has been designed to attenuate incoming sound at specific frequencies by distorting the wave propagation as it passes through the cells where some waves were reflected, refracted, and/or absorbed accordingly. Acoustic metamaterials have been used in hearing protection devices in previous research work. Fumiya et al. proposed an acoustic notch filtering earmuff utilizing Helmholtz resonator arrays that can provide sound reduction based on the resonant frequency24. The HR array adopted in that study achieved a 40 dB noise reduction at the target resonant frequency, which is higher than that achieved by conventional earmuffs (average 34–35 dB). Similarly, Kevin et al. used Helmholtz resonators in developing passive earplugs to reduce occlusion effect, particularly at low frequencies25. The impedance of the meta-earplug was matched with that of the ear canal to overcome the occlusion effect. For earmuff application, the primary area of interest has been to achieve higher sound insulation by using a passive sound blocking mechanism. This sound insulation has been correlated with the high noise reduction values26,27. However, the effect of sound absorption and its correlation with noise reduction have not been widely investigated. The reason for this is that noise reduction depends on multiple factors such as diffusion of sound waves, structural transmission, and barrier properties. Since the sound absorption and noise reduction focus on different aspects of acoustic characterization, past studies have often address them separately rather than correlating them directly. Nonetheless, for selective noise attenuation for earmuff application, a combination of selective sound absorption and insulation could be more effective to allow for speech intelligibility while providing sufficient hearing protection. Researchers have investigated different unit cell architectural designs to achieve all these changes in sound wave propagation. These designs include tetra-chiral honeycomb designs28–30, spherical and cylindrical masses21,31, ribs and triangles18,32, and star-shaped unit cell structures33,34. These unit cells are arranged as a lattice structure to provide the required elastic/acoustic wave attenuation. The behaviour of these regular lattice structures is studied using numerical method which is based on the Floquet-Bloch theory. By applying this method, bandgap formation and analysis of wave propagation can be carefully studied and analyzed, as shown by Valencia et al.35. The measurement of frequency response function (FRF) in decibels (dB) based on the excitation and observation points has also been used to investigate wave transmission through the lattice structure, as shown by Emad et al.18. As a mean to attenuate wave propagation effectively, Liu et al.22 proposed a unit cell with a specially designed asymmetric geometry. Various design parameters based on this unit cell study were optimized to obtain the highest bandgap width, using a finite element model developed in COMSOL environment. Numerical simulations were used to calculate the characteristic transmission curve based on the displacements at the excitation and observation points to verify the existence of the bandgap. In recent research work, tunable Helmholtz resonators have been used to achieve multiple absorption peaks36,37. These adjustable resonators can be readily optimized to achieve the target acoustic performance. The method used to manufacture the acoustic metamaterial has primarily been through additive manufacturing. As the manufacturing method significantly influences overall performance, it is essential to select optimized parameters and processes tailored to achieve the intended outcome. Gioia et al. investigated various types of additive manufacturing techniques used to produce the innovative metamaterial designs38. They concluded that fused deposition modelling (FDM) offers a cost-effective manufacturing approach, and with optimized parameters, it can deliver acoustic performance comparable to that of stereolithography (SLA). Luis et al. conducted an in-depth assessment of additive manufacturing technologies for acoustic applications, highlighting that 39% of such applications utilize fused deposition modelling (FDM) due to its ability to produce complex geometries with high dimensional accuracy39. The study also highlights the suitability of additive manufacturing for practical use in areas such as architectural acoustics and personal hearing protection. Overall, these prior studies demonstrate the effectiveness of various metamaterial structural designs in attenuating elastic and acoustic waves, as well as their feasibility for efficient and practical manufacturing.

Recent research has also demonstrated the use of hierarchical multi-mechanism elastic metamaterials20, modulator-stacked40, and sandwich acoustic absorbers41, however, the present design distinguishes itself by the use of a compact design having angled triangular cavities and folded channels, making it readily adoptable for a wide variety of applications. While prior studies have explored the integration of Helmholtz resonators and Bragg scattering for sound attenuation (as shown in Fig. 1), the present work addresses the persistent challenge of maintaining broadband absorption, particularly across the mid-to-high frequency ranges, by spatially tuning multiple mechanisms to achieve sound absorption and noise reduction in this targeted frequency range. In addition, previous studies have leveraged on the shape-shifting capability of shape memory materials for broadband acoustic attenuation42,43; however the present work distinguishes itself by demonstrating wideband attenuation through the material’s intrinsic acoustic properties. This holds significant potential for developing speech-intelligible PNC earmuffs. This approach would enable major speech content frequencies (100–3000 Hz) aligning with the speech banana to pass through while maintaining effective noise protection as shown in Fig. 244,45.

The frequency range encompassing the keywords of human speech is commonly referred to as the speech banana45.

This study aims to design an optimized triangular reentrant honeycomb metamaterial structure that integrates multiple acoustic attenuation mechanisms, enabling high sound absorption coefficient and transmission loss values across the medium-high frequency range (3000–6400 Hz). Numerical modelling using finite element analysis was conducted to calculate the FRF in decibels and evaluate the effect of unit cell and number of unit cell layers on attenuation. Bandgaps were evaluated for the unit cell design using COMSOL Multiphysics acoustic-structure interaction. Experimental testing using an impedance tube was conducted to evaluate the acoustic performance of the proposed metamaterial. The tests systematically examined factors such as cell arrangements, Helmholtz resonator configurations, specimen thickness, and directions of cell opening. These evaluations provide clear evidence of the practicality and effectiveness of the metamaterial design. The optimized metamaterial design was then adopted as an earmuff insert, and noise reduction with speech intelligibility were evaluated and compared against off-the-shelf earmuffs. By combining these mechanisms in a compact and innovative lattice structure, this research paves the way for advancements in vibroacoustic sound-absorbing applications, particularly in environments requiring both communication and noise protection.

Methodology: experimental testing and numerical modelling

This section provides detailed information on experimental testing and numerical modelling used for the current study. The concept behind the geometry of the unit cell, the FRF analysis modelling strategy, the experimental testing procedure for the impedance tube, and the design of experiments (DOE) are detailed in the following sections.

Geometry of unit cells

The unit cell design was selected based on the concept used for sound attenuation inspired from Liu et al.22. The Bragg scattering mechanism matches the wavelength of the incoming sound waves, causing a large bandgap. The local resonance mechanism with Helmholtz resonator was created using structures with sharp edges, corners, and narrow neck cavities. A detailed drawing of the unit cell design used in the current study is shown in Fig. 3. The sharp corners and triangular cell shown in the design are used to create a local resonance mechanism, targeting sound waves at low-to-mid frequencies (500–2000 Hz), whereas the honeycomb structure is used to trap mid-high frequency (3000–8000 Hz) waves through Bragg scattering mechanism. The wall thickness is limited to 0.5 mm due to the layers thickness limitation in fused deposition modelling technique used to fabricate the samples.

Materials for unit cell and lattice structure

The selection of material for the earmuff application depends on its ability to absorb the incident energy and not to allow the sound waves to pass through the material46. The intrinsic material property that indicates its ability to absorb and dispense energy is called the damping coefficient or Tan δ. It is the ratio of loss modulus (E″) to the storage modulus (E′). Shape memory polymers (SMPs) exhibit a strong inherent ability to absorb sound waves, as shown by their high tan delta values reported in previous studies, surpassing those of conventional thermoplastic polymers. The range of Tan δ for conventional thermoplastic polymers is between 0.3 and 1.2, whereas for SMP the Tan δ can vary in the range of 0.98–2.2547–49. This intrinsic capability of SMP material to attenuate sound waves would be particularly useful in blocking off the high intensity low frequency sound waves (up to 3000 Hz), since this requires designing large structures (for longer wavelengths), which is not possible with the design space limitations of the earmuff application.

In this study, shape memory polymer with a composition of 70 wt% of thermoplastic polyurethane and 30 wt% of polylactic acid which was prepared through melt blending was employed (termed as SMF70T-R60). The melt blended polymer was then converted into 3D printing filament using extrusion process, with filament diameter of 1.75 ± 0.5 mm. The resultant material properties are shown in Table 1. The frequency dependent Tan δ values of the SMP formulation are added in the Supplementary file 1.

Numerical modelling for modal and acoustic analysis

The modal analysis of the samples was performed using Altair SimSolid to evaluate their dynamic characteristics effectively. The 3D geometries of the samples were imported from CAD files. Material properties of SMP shown in Table 1 were assigned to the samples. The samples were unconstrained, meshless computational approach in SimSolid was employed, eliminating the need for traditional meshing and enabling accurate analysis of the complex sample geometries. The modal analysis was configured to extract natural frequencies and corresponding mode shapes within the frequency range of interest (0–6400 Hz). This approach provides a detailed understanding of the vibrational behavior and resonance characteristics of the metamaterial samples. The extracted mode shapes illustrate how the structure deforms at each natural frequency, providing essential information on displacement patterns and nodal distributions. This understanding is crucial for optimizing the unit cell design, ensuring that specific vibrational modes contribute to the formation of bandgaps and enhance selective frequency filtration.

A three-dimensional numerical model was developed to replicate the experimental impedance tube set up and to explicitly evaluate the acoustic pressure distribution, with the aim of confirming the presence and effect of Helmholtz resonance within the air domain, as shown in Fig. 6. The modelled sample, with a 30 mm diameter, incorporates the Helmholtz Resonator (HR) reflecting the geometries used in physical testing. The overall tube dimensions replicate those employed in experimental measurements of the sound absorption coefficient.

Numerical model setup for the acoustic analysis replicating the experimental absorption coefficient setup.

To enhance computational efficiency, the physical geometry of the tube was excluded and replaced with acoustically rigid boundaries, while the interface between the large and small tube segments was modelled using absorbing impedance elements. This approach effectively simulates full absorption, equivalent to the effect of foam liners at the tube transition.

The simulation employed solid tetrahedral elements to discretize both the air and structural domains. A total of 139,369 elements were used for the air volume, with mesh sizes ranging from 4 mm down to 0.8 mm. The metamaterial sample was meshed with higher fidelity, using 105,860 elements ranging from 2 mm to 0.4 mm, to capture complex structural responses and localized resonance effects. The mesh design satisfied the minimum element size (8.93 mm) criterion for the upper limit of the simulated frequency range (6400 Hz), ensuring at least six elements per wavelength in the air domain51.

Material properties corresponding to the SMF70T-R60 shape memory polymer were assigned to the sample. At the air-structure interface, two-dimensional acoustic impedance elements (CAABSF) were implemented to replicate realistic boundary conditions. These elements are specifically designed to simulate energy interactions between structural surfaces and the acoustic field. The acoustic impedance was defined as a purely resistive value of 0.0006324, in accordance with Altair OptiStruct’s unit system (N, mm, s, MPa), derived from the polymer’s density and the speed of sound.

Coupled fluid-structure interaction (FSI) was enabled using the ACMODL feature in Altair OptiStruct. A harmonic pressure excitation of 94 dB (equivalent to 1 Pa) was applied at the entrance of the tube, sweeping across a frequency range of 0 to 6400 Hz to evaluate the metamaterial’s broadband acoustic response.

Experimental characterization of vibration and displacement of unit cell structure

The characterization of vibrational response and displacement of a unit cell structure was performed using a Single Point Scanning Laser Doppler Vibrometer (SLDV) by Polytec GmbH. The specimen excitation was performed using an electrodynamic shaker (MODAL Shop) that excites the specimen at various natural frequencies. By analyzing the velocity and displacement of the structure under excitation dynamic properties, including resonance frequencies, mode shapes, and damping behavior of the samples can be evaluated. The single point laser beam can be made to precisely focus on the point of interest at the unit cell or lattice structure. A sweep spectrum using the peakhold function was used to excite the specimens. Before data acquisition, the SLDV system was calibrated according to the user manual and standard operating procedures. The laser point alignment was performed using a calibrated grid reference to minimize beam angle deviation. The SLDV was initially operated in a single point velocity mode to verify the linearity of response and to eliminate potential offset before the actual measurement. All measurements were conducted in a similar testing environment to reduce uncertainty in results. Each measurement was repeated three times at identical measurement points to assess repeatability, with the variation shown in results to ensure accuracy and consistency. The manufacturer specified uncertainty of ± 2% of the measured sound attenuation data for the frequency range of 100–10 kHz was considered in the calculations. The experimental setup used for this measurement is shown in Fig. 7. The underlying objective of this characterization is to evaluate the magnitude of displacement and sound attenuation for different parts of unit cells in a lattice structure. In addition, it can also be used to validate the effect of different portions of the unit cell structure acquired through modal analysis. Design of experiment

A few factors have been investigated in this study as part of the design of the experiment; these include the cell arrangements, Helmholtz resonator arrangements, thickness of specimen, and directions of cell opening. The different directions of unit cell opening indicate the degree to which the sound attenuation could be affected when considering it with respect to the wave propagation. In an orientation parallel to the incoming sound waves (PW), the waves are attenuated across the full depth of the unit cell structure, and the complete structure vibration creates excitations. In an orientation transverse to incoming sound waves (TW), the attenuation is primarily conducted by the design itself, and the excitations are caused by the air passing through the structure. Both PW and TW orientations are shown in Figure 8 for clarity purposes.

Speech intelligibility test

The speech intelligibility was assessed through Sentence Recognition Threshold tests in both quiet (SRTQ) and noise (SRTN) environments, following ISO 9921 Assessment of Speech Communication protocols. The test on human participants was approved by the ethics committee of Universiti Pendidikan Sultan Idris with reference number 2024-0594-01. When conducted in a semi-anechoic chamber at the National Aerospace Agency Malaysia, the test was maintained in a background noise level of 25 dB. All participants were screened with the audiometric hearing test to determine the level of hearing loss in terms of decibels (dB), from a certified clinic. Only participants without hearing loss of greater than 25 dBHL according to the DOSH standard were selected to participate in the testing. Two human speakers with ± 2 dB variation in speech loudness were chosen, and two judges were appointed to record results. The sound source-to-listener azimuthal angle was fixed at 0°, with a 1 m distance between speaker and listener. All participants were familiar with English, and they were briefed on test methodology prior to the test session. Test durations inside the anechoic chamber were limited to 15 min per person, with monitoring facilitated by an internal camera for safety reasons. Results and discussion

This section provides a detailed account of the results and discussion arising from the numerical and experimental analyses. Initially, the numerical model setup for FRF analysis was verified by benchmarking the FRF results against established findings in the literature to ascertain its accuracy. Subsequently, the model was employed to conduct a comparative FRF analysis to investigate the influence of unit cells and the number of lattice layers. Bandgap results from COMSOL are discussed. The experimental results, including sound absorption coefficient and transmission loss, were systematically compiled and analyzed for each specimen in accordance to the design of the experiments. Finally, speech intelligibility and noise reduction were tested for the prototype and compared against commercially available earmuffs.

Validation of simulation results

The simulation results on FRF model developed in this study, as explained in Sect. 2.1, using the published design by Emad et al.18 were compared and validated (see Fig. 14). The results show that the simulation results attained with the current study were similar in trend to those achieved in literature. It can also be observed that in comparison to FRF results from the published study, FRF results from this study are much closer to their experimental results. Although there are variations in FRF magnitude, the overall trend corresponds well. These variations could be due to model simplifications, such as ignoring the influence of air, meshing the model with 2D elements, etc. The validated numerical model is used to analyze the impact of the proposed unit cell lattice layers on attenuation.

Validation of developed model by comparing with published frequency response function data.

Effects of number of lattice layers

The effects of unit cell layers addition on the lattice structure can be shown by the reduction in FRF using Eq. 1. Three different cases were evaluated, as shown previously in Fig. 4. Firstly, the FRF results were calculated without any unit cell and lattice layers between the excitation and observation points. Then, two and four lattice layers were compared with their effect shown in Fig. 15. It can be observed that by adding unit cell layers, FRF drops significantly across all frequencies from 0 to 6400 Hz. FRF values between − 10 to -60 dB were observed for the case with no unit cell layers. This is because the unit cells’ absence causes less filtration between the excitation and observation points. Hence, the reduction observed is mainly due to the excitation at different modes. FRF values between − 32 and − 96 dB are observed when two-unit cell layers are added. This trend is because the unit cell design creates a local resonance mechanism across frequencies due to the sharp triangles and corners, which causes a sudden reduction in FRF values. Similarly, when four-unit cell layers are placed between the excitation and observation points, FRF values between − 29.8 and − 110 dB are observed. This sizable drop in FRF values is approximately equivalent to no wave propagation. The main reason for this higher reduction with four layers is due to attenuation caused by additional unit cells, with more resonators blocking off the transmission of sound waves, resulting in a higher drop in values across frequencies. Hence, it can be concluded that an increase in the number of layers has a significant influence in reducing FRF magnitude.

Effect of number of layers on frequency response function.

Bandgap analysis

The bandgaps were calculated based on the method described in Sect. 2.4, to evaluate the frequency range where the wave propagation is attenuated due to the destructive interference, local resonance, and/or other mechanisms. The results of this analysis are shown in Fig. 16. The dispersion graph reveals a complete wide isotropic bandgap in the frequency range of 2800–7000 Hz, indicating that wave propagation is effectively restricted within this region. The presence of this bandgap suggests that the unit cell design could inhibit the transmission of acoustic waves within this broad frequency range, through various wave attenuation mechanisms. The lower boundary of the bandgap at 2800 Hz signifies the onset of wave suppression, while the upper boundary at 7000 Hz marks the frequency beyond which wave propagation resumes. Notably, a directional (anisotropic) bandgap is also observed at low frequencies (100–600 Hz), where wave propagation is inhibited along the X – M (vertical) and M – Γ (diagonal) paths but permitted along the Γ – X (horizontal) direction. This behavior is due to asymmetries in the unit cell geometry, which modulate wave attenuation depending on direction. Importantly, the structure can exhibit frequency selective behavior by allowing low-frequency waves (up to 3000 Hz) to transmit with minimal attenuation, while effectively blocking higher-frequency, potentially harmful noise components above 3000 Hz.

Dispersion diagram for unit cell structure along the first Brillouin zone for analyzing bandgap.

Acoustic properties of metamaterial design

In this section, results and analysis for testing in an impedance tube for various specimens according to the design of the experiment are shown. Sound absorption coefficient results are discussed first, followed by transmission loss.

Sound absorption coefficient of shape memory metamaterial

a) Sound absorption for BGS specimen:

BGS specimens were tested first for sound absorption coefficient, and the results are shown in Fig. 17. The variability in the results is represented by the shaded band. It can be observed that the variation in sound absorption coefficient is within ± 0.1 across all repetitions. This highlights the narrow tolerance range and confirms the consistency and repeatability of the measurement approach. The PW specimens exhibited a higher sound absorption capacity when compared to the TW specimens. The best performing PW specimen (BGS-Rect-D1-PW-30) achieved a sound absorption peak of 0.96 at 1952 Hz and can retain a sound absorption value between 0.60 and 0.90 for a complete testing frequency range up to 6400 Hz. The best performing TW specimen (BGS-Rect-D1-TW-30) can only achieve a sound absorption peak of 0.38 at 2800 Hz and can only retain sound absorption in the range of 0.2–0.4. This trend is due to the sound waves being attenuated across the full depth of the unit cell structure for PW specimen, and the excitations are created by the vibration of the complete lattice structure, resulting in higher sound absorption values. However, in the TW specimen, sound absorption is primarily caused by the unit cell facing the sound source only, not the complete lattice structure, resulting in lower sound absorption values.

Sound absorption coefficient for Bragg scattering specimen.

Another observation that can be made based on the shown trend is the importance of the thickness of specimen. For both TW and PW specimens, it can be observed that 30-mm thick specimens performed better when compared to 10-mm and 20-mm thicknesses. The reason for this observation is that a thicker specimen could dissipate more energy through absorption, resulting in reduced wave transmission through the material and, hence, higher sound absorption capabilities. Based on these results, it can be concluded that the PW specimen with 30 mm thickness provides the highest sound absorption values.

b) Sound absorption for HR specimen:

HR specimens were tested using the same experimental setup to evaluate the effect of various parameters on their sound absorption coefficient. Figure 18 shows the results for various combinations of HR specimens. The results show that by increasing the thickness of the HR specimen, the sound absorption peak shifts towards lower frequencies. This can be explained by using the fundamental concept of resonance frequencies of HR. A thicker specimen increases the volume of HR, which reduces the resonance frequency and causes an early bandgap opening, hence shifting the sound absorption peak towards lower frequencies. It can also be observed that by using full perforation in HR (P1), the absorption peak is higher in comparison with alternative perforation (P2). This observation is because the surface area of the resonator cavities exposed to sound waves for full perforation arrangement is 18.45 mm2, while the alternative perforation arrangement has only 12.57 mm2. This substantial increase (47.5%) in the area of the resonator cavities exposed to sound waves causes a higher peak of sound absorption. It can also be observed that a 10 mm thick specimen shows a peak of sound absorption for both full and alternative perforations. This can be explained by using the HR resonance frequency formula shown in Eq. 1. The estimated resonance frequency for 10 mm thick specimens is 5846 Hz, and experimentally, it is found to be at 4000 Hz, which is not too far from the theoretical calculations. These discrepancies might be due to (i) issues with 3D printing where intricate design features could not be replicated well (see Table 7) and (ii) porosities in lattice structure might have also caused a shift in the resonance frequencies.

To support the contribution of Helmholtz resonance to the overall acoustic performance, the pressure distribution inside the sample was evaluated numerically and the pressure contour is presented at frequency of 3520 Hz. As shown in Fig. 21, the sectional pressure contour clearly reveals a strong buildup of acoustic pressure within the cavities of the Helmholtz resonators. This localized amplification is a signature behaviour of resonance, where the air mass in the neck oscillates in response to the incoming wave, causing energy to be trapped inside the chamber.

Simulated pressure contour at 3520 Hz showing sectional view of the acoustic field within the impedance tube model.

The zoomed-in view further illustrates how the pressure is concentrated in each resonator cavity while remaining significantly lower in the surrounding air domain. This confirms that the HR chambers are acting as tuned resonators, effectively absorbing sound energy at their natural frequency. The observed pattern aligns well with theoretical Helmholtz behaviour and is consistent with the sound absorption peak (3512 Hz) seen in the experimental results near this frequency. Although other methods such as eigenmode visualization or impedance profiling could also be used to verify the resonance effect, the pressure field distribution presented here offers direct visual confirmation. It demonstrates not only the presence of resonance, but also the precise location and intensity of the trapped acoustic energy inside the HR chambers.

c) Sound absorption for a combination of HR and BGS specimen:

A combination of HR and BGS specimens was tested to achieve a higher sound absorption across the frequencies. The results of all the different combinations of HR and BGS alternatively facing the speaker, according to the design of the experiment, are shown in Fig. 22. It can be seen that with HR facing the sound source, the sound absorption peak is obtained after 1000 Hz, and retention of sound absorption can be achieved across the frequency range of 2500–6400 Hz, whereas with BGS facing the sound source the sound absorption cannot be retained. The reason for these observations is that when BGS is facing the speaker, the sound attenuation starts early due to local resonators in the structure. However, the HR cannot retain this sound absorption since it shows the highest absorption at the resonance frequency only. However, when the HR is placed in front, the peak absorption at resonance frequency is effectively retained by the BGS specimen attached. Another reason why the combination of HR and BGS specimen works well is due to the coupling effect. When HR and BGS are placed in series (i.e., sound waves pass through one and then the other), the interaction between their local resonances and impedance profiles results in constructive coupling, leading to enhanced absorption Hence, these results show that the best performing specimen (30-HR-P1-BGS) can achieve a peak of 1.0 sound absorption at 2424 Hz and retain an absorption value greater than 0.8 up to 6400 Hz. Based on these results, broadband retention of a high sound absorption coefficient (greater than 0.8) across the mid-high frequency range can be successfully achieved, allowing the reduction of high sound pressure level (SPL) across these frequencies. These results are consistent with the bandgap results shown in Fig. 15, with a broad bandgap region after 2800 Hz.

Sound absorption coefficient for a combination of BGS and HR specimen.

The best performing specimen (30-HR-P1-BGS) combined both the sound attenuation mechanisms effectively and hence achieved a sound absorption of greater than 0.8 for frequencies of interest i.e. 2500–6400 Hz. e) Benchmarking on SMP sound absorption performance against conventional thermoplastic materials:

The sound absorption coefficient was evaluated for solid specimens without any metamaterial design to assess the effect of intrinsic material properties. This analysis allows for a clear understanding of how the material itself, independent of geometric or structural modifications, contributes to the acoustic performance. This is because the SMPs have higher internal damping due to the high tan delta values, as highlighted in Sect. 2.2. Hence, based on these results, it can be concluded that the SMP materials have a higher intrinsic capability to absorb sound waves in comparison to conventional thermoplastic polymers.

Transmission loss of shape memory metamaterial

a) Transmission loss for BGS specimen:

Transmission loss testing was performed for both BGS and HR specimens to evaluate the ability of metamaterial design to block sound waves from passing through. The transmission loss results for BGS specimens using unit cell design are shown in Fig. 26. Again, it can be observed that the variation in transmission loss (shown by the shaded region) is within ± 1 dB across all repetitions. This highlights the narrow tolerance range and confirms the consistency and repeatability of the measurement approach. A similar trend to those shown in sound absorption can be observed; PW specimens exhibit higher transmission loss when compared to TW specimens. The best performing PW specimen with the nomenclature of BGS-Rect-PW-20 achieved a transmission loss peak of 12.42 dB at 4800 Hz, and it can retain transmission loss value between 7.62 and 11.82 dB throughout the testing frequency range of 500–6400 Hz. The best performing specimen among TW specimens is BGS-Rect-TW-30 can only achieve a transmission loss peak of 1.45 dB at 6000 Hz, and it can only retain a transmission loss in the range of 0.5–1.45 dB throughout the testing frequency range of 500–6400 Hz. This trend can be explained by the number of voids in the unit cell design. Unit cell structure for TW specimen directly faces the sound source. Hence, sound waves can pass through the holes and voids in unit cell design, resulting in a lower transmission loss. On the other hand, sound waves are attenuated across the full depth of the unit cell structure for the PW specimen. Hence, it can block sound waves more effectively, resulting in higher transmission loss values.

Transmission loss for Bragg scattering specimen.

b) Transmission loss for HR specimen:

HR specimen transmission loss was tested to evaluate the performance of HR and the effects of various parameters on the transmission loss. Figure 27 shows the results for all different combinations of HR. The results show that by increasing the thickness of the HR specimen, transmission loss peak shifts towards lower frequencies, similar to that observed for the sound absorption test. The main reason for this can be that a thicker specimen increases the volume of HR, which reduces the resonance frequency, causes an early bandgap opening, and, hence, a shift of transmission loss peak towards lower frequencies. It can also be observed that by using full perforation in HR (P1), the transmission loss peak is higher than those of alternative perforation (P2). The reason behind this observation is that in full perforation, the surface area of the resonator cavities exposed to sound waves is 18.45 mm2, while with alternative perforation, it is only 12.57 mm2. This substantial increase (47.5%) in the area of the resonator cavities exposed to sound waves causes a higher peak of transmission loss, as was observed in the case of sound absorption.

c) Transmission loss for a combination of HR and BGS specimen:

A combination of HR and BGS specimens was tested to achieve higher transmission loss across the frequencies. The results of all different combinations of HR and BGS alternatively facing the speaker, according to the design of the experiment, are shown in Fig. 28. It can be seen that with HR facing the sound source transmission loss up to 10 dB can be achieved, whereas when BGS is facing the sound source high transmission loss values cannot be achieved (except for 30-BGS-HR-P2). The reason for these observations is that when BGS is facing the speaker, the first layer is very thin (0.5 mm) with local resonators on the side (not directly facing the incident sound source). Hence, more sound can travel through the specimen, resulting in lower transmission loss values. However, when the HR layer was placed at the front, the peak transmission loss of about 10 dB was achieved at 1800–2000 Hz resonance frequency, which was then retained effectively by the BGS specimen attached to it throughout the testing frequency range up to 6400 Hz. Hence, the best performing specimen (30-HR-P2-BGS) can be used to achieve high transmission loss (greater than 10 dB) values (after 1800 Hz) and can retain these high values for the testing range up to 6400 Hz. The speech intelligibility of the in-house prototype was characterized and compared against off-the-shelf earmuffs (UVEX K200, 3 M™ PELTOR™ Optime™ II, and 3 M™ PELTOR™ Optime™ III) in a semi-anechoic chamber with a background noise of 25–26 dB. The results for both quiet and noisy environments are shown in Fig. 31. For sentence recognition threshold in quiet environment, it can be clearly observed that the in-house prototype performed much better in comparison to other conventional earmuffs. It can achieve the minimum passing score as set by ISO 9921 Assessment of Speech Communication for both CVC and PB words. However, for sentences assessment, only 80% listening accuracy was achieved by the in-house prototype. For sentence recognition threshold in noise environment tested at 80–85 dB, it can again be clearly observed that the in-house prototype performed much better in comparison to other conventional earmuffs. However, the fair rating of 50% or higher speech intelligibility (according to ANSI S3.5) was met for sentences assessment.

Speech intelligibility results for sentence recognition threshold in: (a) quiet, and (b) noise.

The reason for not achieving the required threshold as outlined by the standards can be attributed to two design flaws. The first design flaw can be due to the cup itself which has micro gaps between cup and the cushion plate, allowing noise leakages into the ear, hence, affecting the speech intelligibility. This can be potentially overcome by designing the earcup with a slot that eliminates the need for a cushion plate and allows the fitting of the cushion directly onto the earcup. The second design flaw can be due to the inserts where HR does not conform fully to the cavity of the cup. Rather, it has some gap between the cup wall and itself, hence, hindering the resonance frequency of the HR by entrapped air interference. This limitation can be addressed by redesigning the Helmholtz resonator with a curved geometry that conforms precisely to the inner contour of the earcup, thereby eliminating any clearance and improving acoustic coupling. Both these flaws are illustrated in Fig. 30. It is expected that once these issues are addressed, further improvement in speech intelligibility can be achieved. Given the brevity of this paper, such detailed R&D work can be explored in future studies for further refinement and improvement.

Finally, insertion loss testing was performed using Head and Torso Simulator with a pink noise source at 95 dB to validate the hearing protection performance of the in-house prototype. The results of hearing protection testing against commercial earmuffs are shown in Fig. 32. It is found that the in-house prototype provides less reduction, (for frequencies less than 2500 Hz) to allow major speech content energies to pass through while after 2500 Hz, the noise reduction provided by the in-house prototype is similar to the commercially available earmuffs. The insertion loss can be further improved if the gap between the cup and HR insert is removed as this will enable higher noise reduction. Hence, based on the results, an optimized SMP metamaterial earmuff is proposed that can allow speech to pass through while providing the required hearing protection. The importance of hearing protection at low frequencies is lacking in the current metamaterial design. This gap in hearing protection can be overcome by intrinsic material capability of shape memory polymers. However, this warrants further research work in material formulation which is beyond the scope of this paper. Despite the fabrication and scalability challenges associated with additive manufacturing of intricate metamaterial structures, the production cost of the current prototype remains comparable to that of conventional passive earmuffs. This cost similarity, achieved despite the design complexity, reinforces the practical viability of shape memory metamaterial integration in hearing protection applications.

Conclusions

This study successfully designed and evaluated a triangular reentrant honeycomb metamaterial structure, offering a compact and efficient solution for vibro-acoustic sound attenuation. The proposed design integrates Bragg scattering, local resonance, and Helmholtz resonator mechanisms to achieve superior acoustic performance. The optimized metamaterial achieved a peak sound absorption coefficient of 1.0 and maintained high sound absorption (greater than 0.8) across the 3000–6400 Hz frequency range. Furthermore, a transmission loss of 10–11 dB was achieved, confirming the effectiveness of the design. The optimized metamaterial design was then adopted as an insert for earmuff and its speech intelligibility and noise reduction capability was evaluated. In comparison to conventional earmuffs, in-house prototype exhibits a higher speech intelligibility and provide sufficient noise reduction.

Systematic investigations revealed the significant influence of design parameters such as specimen thickness, cell arrangements, Helmholtz resonator perforations, and direction of cell opening on acoustic properties. Notably, thicker BGS specimens exhibited higher absorption and transmission loss within the target frequency range, while HR with full perforation and rectangular unit cell arrangements enhanced acoustic performance by reducing voids and optimizing local resonance mechanisms.

The study highlights the practicality of adopting the triangular reentrant honeycomb metamaterial as an insert or lining in passive noise control earmuffs. This innovation facilitates selective frequency filtration, allowing low-to-mid frequency waves (e.g. speech up to 3000 Hz) to pass through while blocking hazardous high frequency sound. Such metamaterial designs may allow for compliance with HSE and OSHA standards while maintaining communication efficiency. The limitations identified in the current design and the enhancement of low-frequency noise reduction can be addressed in future works through the manufacturing and design assembly improvements highlighted in this work. Given the potential of this metamaterial design, future research work can leverage on shape memory material capability by segregating its effect from the metamaterial design to further enhance the noise reduction while allowing speech to pass through. In addition, further exploration of adaptive SMPs capable of dynamically altering acoustic properties with temperature, combined with machine learning or topology optimization to refine cell geometry for enhanced performance, can be pursued while extending the approach to other acoustic applications such as architectural acoustics, modular wall systems, and aircraft interiors.
