BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutations and their predictive value for treatment outcomes in patients with chronic myeloid leukemia treated with first-line imatinib in a low-income setting: Experience from Côte d’Ivoire
Authors: Kouassi Gustave Koffi, Sara Akou Bognini, Dohoma Alexis Silué, Ismael Kamara, Ines Kouakou, Ruth Dieket, Emeraude N’dhatz, Boidy Kouakou, Danho Clotaire Nanho, Yannick Kouassi, David Tea Okou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to analyse the incidence of BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutations in patients newly diagnosed with or undergoing treatment for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) in Côte d’Ivoire, as well as to evaluate the predictive factors associated with treatment outcomes.MethodsWe evaluated 42 patients with suboptimal molecular response who underwent BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutation screening. Sequencing was performed in collaboration with the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center, Seattle, USA.ResultsAmong the 42 patients, 16 (38.1%) were found to have known BCR::ABL1 point mutations. A total of nine distinct mutations were identified, with T315I being the most common (5). Three patients harbored compound mutations: one had T315I + M244V, another had G250E + E459K, and the third had H396P + E459K. The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate was significantly lower in patients with BCR::ABL1 mutations (85%; 95 % CI: 51.0%–96.1%) compared to those without mutations (100%). However, there was no statistically significant difference in OS between patients with T315I mutations (83.3 %; 95 % CI: 27.4%–97.5%) and those without T315I (83.3%; 95% CI: 5.9%–98.8%). Being in the chronic phase of the disease and having a low-risk ELTS score were identified as protective factors against the development of mutations.ConclusionOur findings support the recommendation for BCR::ABL1 mutation screening in CML patients with an inadequate initial response or evidence of loss of response. Screening is also advised at progression to the accelerated or blast phase, and in patients with high-risk ELTS scores, and mutation profiles may serve as important prognostic indicators.

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a clonal myeloproliferative disorder of a pluripotent hematopoietic stem cell, first described by John Hughes Bennett in 1845. It was the first malignancy to be associated with a specific chromosomal abnormality the Philadelphia (pH) chromosome resulting from a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 [1,2]. This genetic rearrangement leads to the formation of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene, which encodes an oncoprotein with constitutive tyrosine kinase activity.

Targeted therapy with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has revolutionised CML management, transforming it from a fatal disease into a chronic, manageable condition [3]. However, access to diagnostic tools and treatment remains limited in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Over the past decade, the introduction of imatinib mesylate through the Glivec International Patient Assistance Program (GIPAP) has made frontline treatment more accessible in Côte d’Ivoire, significantly improving patient outcomes [4].

Monitoring BCR::ABL1 transcript levels in peripheral blood using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) is the most sensitive method for evaluating therapeutic response. Achieving and maintaining deep molecular responses is strongly associated with favorable long-term outcomes [5].

Despite these advancements, a subset of patients exhibit suboptimal responses or resistance to TKIs, often due to point mutations in the BCR::ABL1 kinase domain. The T315I mutation, in particular, is associated with high-level resistance to first-generation TKIs such as imatinib. Early detection of such mutations is critical for timely therapeutic adjustments and improved outcomes.

This study aimed to determine the frequency of BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutations among patients with CML treated with imatinib in Côte d’Ivoire and to identify predictive factors for mutation occurrence and treatment outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patients and inclusion criteria

This prospective study was conducted at the Clinical Hematology Department of the Teaching Hospital of Yopougon (CHU-Y), the national referral center for hematological disorders in Côte d’Ivoire. Patients with newly diagnosed CML were enrolled between May 2018 and October 2024 and registered in a leukemia database established in collaboration with the Max Foundation via the Max Access Program.

Eligible patients had a confirmed diagnosis of CML based on peripheral blood morphology and the presence of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene detected by RT-qPCR. All participants were enrolled in the GIPAP and provided written informed consent. Diagnostic samples were collected on dried blood spot (DBS) filter paper, which preserves nucleic acids for downstream analysis. This cost-effective approach was provided free of charge to patients.

Among 72 patients initially treated with first-line imatinib (400 mg/day), 42 who demonstrated suboptimal molecular response underwent BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutation testing.

BCR::ABL1 kinase domain mutational analysis

Mutation analysis was performed in collaboration with the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center in Seattle, USA. RNA was extracted from DBS cards containing 50 μL of blood. The BCR::ABL1 fusion transcript was amplified using RT-PCR, followed by nested PCR targeting the kinase domain. Sequencing was conducted in both forward and reverse directions using Dye Terminator Chemistry. Mutations were identified by comparing results with the wild-type sequence.

Treatment protocol

Patients received imatinib according to standard protocols. Those in chronic phase received 400 mg/day orally, while patients in accelerated and blastic phases received 600 mg or 800 mg/day, respectively. Dose adjustments were made based on patient tolerance and therapeutic response. Hydroxyurea was administered briefly when required. No other concomitant chemotherapy was used.

Cytogenetic evaluation and molecular response

Molecular monitoring was performed using RT-qPCR, with results expressed as the BCR::ABL1/ABL1 ratio on the International Scale (IS). Transcript levels were measured every three months during the first year and every six months thereafter. Molecular response was classified according to the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2020 guidelines [6].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS version 25.0. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were made using Student’s t-test and Fisher’s exact test. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of mutation detection to the date of last follow-up or death. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent was obtained from all participants, including provisions for voluntary withdrawal at any stage. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

Understanding the mutational status of BCR::ABL1 is essential for optimizing therapeutic decisions. Of the 72 patients with CML enrolled in our study, 42 underwent mutation screening, and BCR::ABL1 mutations were identified in 16 patients (38 %). Nine different point mutations were detected. Notably, both patients diagnosed at the blastic phase carried the T315I mutation.

Generally, BCR-ABL kinase domain mutations have been reported in up to 50 % of patients who develop resistance to imatinib (IM) [7]. Identifying these mutations at the point of IM treatment failure is crucial, as it can help guide the choice of the most appropriate therapy to either prevent or overcome resistance [8]. Clinically relevant IM-resistant mutations include G250E, Y253F/H, E255K/V, T315I, M351T, and F359V [9].

In our cohort, T315I was the most frequently observed mutation, found in 6 out of 16 mutation-positive patients (37.5 %). This aligns with data from Latin America [10] and findings by Tokarski J. et al. [11]. However, other studies have reported different findings, with some not identifying T315I as the most prevalent mutation [7,12], and one not observing it at all [9]. The reasons for these discrepancies remain unclear, although Jabbour et al. [7] suggest that the frequency of T315I may have increased due to the use of TKIs that are active against other mutations, or due to patient inclusion at different disease stages—similar to our study.

The T315I mutation is of particular clinical concern because it confers complete resistance to first- and second-generation TKIs, and is thus associated with poor prognosis [13]. This resistance is due to the substitution of threonine with isoleucine at position 315 in the p210^BCR-ABL1 protein. This substitution eliminates a critical drug-binding site, as isoleucine's steric hindrance prevents the formation of hydrogen bonds with TKIs [11].

Three patients in our study harbored more than one mutation across sequential samples (T315I/M244V, G250E/E459K, and H396P/E459K), but no simultaneous (compound) mutations were observed. In the study by Heloísa Zorzi Costa et al. [14], two patients did present simultaneous mutations (E189G/V299L and E255K/T315I), possibly due to clonal evolution of the disease.

In our study, the occurrence of BCR::ABL1 mutations was associated with lower projected 5-year overall survival. However, the difference in survival between patients with or without the T315I mutation was not statistically significant—likely due to the limited sample size.

Other studies have shown that both the presence of BCR::ABL1 mutations and specifically the T315I mutation negatively impact 5-year overall survival and response to imatinib [15,16]. In a study by Jingjing Liu et al. [15], overall survival was 80 % in the mutation-positive group versus 100 % in the mutation-negative group (p < 0.01). For T315I specifically, survival dropped to 47 % versus 98 % in patients without the mutation (p < 0.01), demonstrating its poor prognostic value.

The T315 residue acts as a gatekeeper, and when mutated to T315I, it blocks TKI access to the kinase’s hydrophobic pocket while still allowing ATP binding [17]. The T315I mutation is one of the most challenging due to its broad resistance profile [16]. Unfortunately, patients with this mutation are generally resistant to all available TKIs, including imatinib, bosutinib, nilotinib, and dasatinib. However, they may still benefit from ponatinib or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Ponatinib was developed specifically to address resistance to IM and has shown high activity against ABL kinase mutations, including T315I, by binding to the kinase domain in its inactive conformation [18,19].

Our findings emphasize the importance of mutation screening in CML patients who show an inadequate initial response or loss of response. Screening is also recommended when disease progresses to the accelerated or blastic phase. In low-income countries, however, substantial barriers remain—despite free drug access and improved diagnostics and monitoring.

This study suggests that certain baseline demographic and clinical features, such as being diagnosed at an advanced disease phase and having a high-risk ELTS score, may serve as predictors of BCR::ABL1 mutation. However, the study had limitations, particularly due to financial constraints and lack of technical resources, which prevented many patients with imatinib resistance from being tested for mutations.
