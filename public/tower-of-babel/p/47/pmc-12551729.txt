Neighborhood disadvantage and fecundability in a cohort of US Black women
Authors: Amelia K. Wesselink, Mary D. Willis, Sharonda M. Lovett, Chen Sheng, Wendy Kuohung, Jacqueline Hicks, Junenette L. Peters, Shanshan Sheehy, Julie R. Palmer, Lauren A. Wise, Yvette Cozier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Neighborhood context is a root cause of health disparities, but few epidemiologic studies have measured the effect of neighborhoods on fertility, a reproductive outcome with known racial disparities.Methods:The Black Women’s Health Study is a prospective cohort study of US Black women who enrolled in 1995. Participants completed questionnaires at baseline and every 2 years afterwards. We linked participant addresses to US Census block group data on six variables representing neighborhood disadvantage and used factor analysis to derive a composite neighborhood disadvantage score. In 2011, participants reported how many months it took to conceive each of their planned pregnancies and whether they had ever tried to conceive for 12 or more months without success. We included 2,085 participants who contributed 2,712 pregnancy attempts during 1995–2011. We fit proportional probabilities regression models with generalized estimating equations to estimate fecundability ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI). We stratified models by individual-level educational attainment.Results:Relative to the lowest quintile of neighborhood disadvantage, fecundability ratios for participants in quintiles 2, 3, 4, and 5 were 0.89 (95% CI = 0.79, 1.01), 0.94 (95% CI = 0.83, 1.06), 0.84 (95% CI = 0.74, 0.96), and 0.89 (95% CI = 0.79, 1.02), respectively. The association persisted only among participants with a college degree or higher.Conclusion:Higher neighborhood disadvantage was associated with reduced fecundability in this cohort of Black women, particularly among those with high educational attainment. These results support the relevance of studying place-based risk factors for subfertility and have important implications for advancing reproductive justice.

What this study adds

Black women have higher rates of infertility and lower access to fertility treatment relative to White women, yet are underrepresented in fertility scholarship. We examined the association between neighborhood disadvantage and fecundability, the per cycle probability of conception, among approximately 2,100 participants in a prospective cohort study of Black women. We found that neighborhood disadvantage was associated with reduced fecundability, particularly among highly educated Black women. This work emphasizes the importance of studying place-based risk factors for subfertility and has important implications for advancing reproductive justice.

Introduction

Infertility, defined as the inability to conceive after 12 months of unprotected intercourse, affects 10–15% of couples in the US.1–4 As couples increasingly delay childbearing to their later reproductive years, rates of infertility are expected to increase. Infertility can exact a significant emotional and psychological toll on affected couples5,6 and is associated with long-term adverse health outcomes.7 Despite the public health burden of infertility, few population-level risk factors have been identified.

Several US-based studies have shown that Black women have higher rates of infertility relative to White women.2–4,8 However, Black women are less likely to seek medical care for infertility,9,10 have lower access to care when they do seek it,11–15 and have lower treatment success rates relative to White women.12,14,16–19 Despite these disparities, Black women have been underrepresented in fertility research.20 This may at least partially stem from historically-rooted racial stereotypes about Black women’s sexuality and fertility,21 which have been reinforced by public policy designed to reduce fertility among Black women (e.g., forced sterilization, family caps on public assistance).22 In fact, the underrepresentation of Black individuals in infertility scholarship, which has been called a form of “stratified reproduction,”20 has precluded the pursuit of research questions of high relevance to Black women and prevented a thorough understanding of the causes of racial disparities in infertility.

Due to historical and contemporary structural racism,23,24 and subsequent racial and economic residential segregation, stark racial disparities in neighborhood quality exist.25–29 Neighborhood context is a root cause of health disparities,30 and neighborhood disadvantage has been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including stillbirth, preterm birth, and low birth weight.31 Neighborhood disadvantage could similarly impact fertility through effects on the physical and social environment that lead to individual exposures, stressors, and resiliency factors that are embodied to affect menstrual function, hormone levels, and inflammation (Figure 1). However, only one epidemiologic study has examined the role of neighborhoods in shaping infertility risk. In a preconception cohort study of 6,356 US pregnancy planners (<5% Black), higher neighborhood disadvantage, operationalized using the Area Deprivation Index, was associated with reduced fecundability, the per cycle probability of conception.32 Fecundability is a sensitive measure of fecundity, the biological capacity to reproduce, among non-contracepting couples engaged in regular intercourse.33

In the present analysis, we leveraged data from a large cohort study of US Black women to estimate the effect of neighborhood disadvantage on fecundability.

Methods

Study design

The Black Women’s Health Study (BWHS) is a prospective cohort study of 59,000 self-identified Black women residing across the US.34,35 Participants aged 21–69 years enrolled in 1995 and were recruited from subscribers of a magazine commonly read by Black women, members of the Black Nurses’ Association, and friends and relatives of early respondents. Participants completed a baseline questionnaire on demographic and behavioral characteristics, reproductive and contraceptive histories, health care utilization, and medical history. Every 2 years since 1995, participants are asked to complete follow-up postal or online questionnaires to update their health information and obtain information on various exposures. Beginning in 2003, participants were offered a web-based option for completing the questionnaire.36 For the present analysis, we used data collected from baseline (1995) through the 2011 questionnaire cycle. The Institutional Review Board at the Boston University Medical Campus approved the study protocol. Participants provided informed consent by completing questionnaires.

Assessment of neighborhood disadvantage

On each questionnaire, participants provided their residential address, which we geocoded at the US Census block group level. We extracted 29 variables measuring dimensions of wealth, income, and education for each Census block group, as described previously.37–40 The 2000 Census was used for residential addresses in 1995–2009; the 2010 Census was used for residential addresses in 2011. The neighborhood disadvantage score was derived previously in the BWHS and is described in detail elsewhere.41 Briefly, based on factor analysis with Varimax rotation, we selected the six variables with the highest factor loadings: median household income; median housing value; percent households receiving interest, dividends, or net rental income; percent adults aged ≥25 that have completed college; percent employed persons aged ≥16 in white collar occupations; and percent of families with children not headed by a single female. The first factor explained 67% of the variability in the six included variables. We used regression coefficients from the factor analysis to weight the variables to generate a combined neighborhood disadvantage score, with higher scores representing higher neighborhood disadvantage. For each pregnancy attempt in the sample, we used the participant’s most recent address, hypothesizing that current neighborhood conditions were most etiologically relevant to their fecundity.

Assessment of fecundability

Fecundability, the per menstrual cycle probability of conception, is a sensitive couple-based marker of fertility.33 We operationalized fecundability using time-to-pregnancy in months. On the 1995, 2003, 2005, 2007, and 2011 questionnaires, we asked participants, “Have you ever tried for 12 or more months to become pregnant without success?” and if so, “How old were you at that time?” On the web-based version of the 2011 questionnaire, participants reported whether they had ever given birth, and for each birth, the calendar year, whether the pregnancy was planned, whether they used fertility medications to conceive, and the number of months it took them to get pregnant (for planned pregnancies only). We did not include unplanned pregnancies in our analysis because time-to-pregnancy is undefined for these pregnancies.42 We defined each reported planned pregnancy or experience of infertility as a pregnancy attempt.43,44

Assessment of covariates

We ascertained the following covariate data on all questionnaires unless otherwise indicated: marital status (1995, 1997, 1999, and 2005 questionnaires), educational attainment (1995 and 2003 questionnaires), annual household income (2003 questionnaire), highest level of education attained by the participant’s primary caregiver when they were a child (2009 questionnaire), smoking history, alcohol use, hours/week of vigorous physical activity, height (1995 questionnaire), weight, parity, gravidity, and age and calendar year of each pregnancy (2011 web-based questionnaire).

Exclusions

We restricted our analysis to participants who completed the 2011 supplemental web questionnaire, were premenopausal (based on self-report) and aged ≤45 years in 1995, and had no history of infertility in 1995 (Figure 2). Among this group, 6,918 reported one or more births, 448 reported that they had never given birth and had experienced infertility, and 2,920 reported neither a birth nor infertility. From the resulting 13,915 pregnancy attempts (i.e., births plus experiences of infertility), we excluded pregnancy attempts occurring before 1995, participants >45 years old, pregnancies conceived using fertility treatment but with a time-to-pregnancy <12 months, and pregnancies that were unplanned. Finally, we excluded participants whose address closest to each pregnancy attempt could not be geocoded to the block group level or whose residential block groups did not have the relevant Census data, yielding 2,712 pregnancy attempts for analysis.

Statistical analysis

We categorized neighborhood disadvantage scores into quintiles, with quintile 1 representing the least disadvantaged neighborhoods and quintile 5 representing the most disadvantaged neighborhoods. We estimated time-to-pregnancy based on self-reported months on the 2011 web-based questionnaire. We censored participants at 12 months, when individuals typically seek fertility treatment. We fit proportional probabilities regression models to estimate fecundability ratios (FR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association of quintiles of neighborhood disadvantage with fecundability.33 FRs <1 are indicative of an exposure that is associated with longer time-to-pregnancy. In our primary analysis, we used generalized estimating equations with robust standard errors to account for multiple observations per participant (range: 1–6 pregnancies per participant). We also fit restricted cubic splines to examine the shape of the exposure-outcome association. In secondary analyses, we restricted to (a) the first pregnancy attempt during the study period and (b) the most recent pregnancy attempt during the study period.

In our main analyses, we adjusted for age at pregnancy, year of pregnancy, geographic region of residence, and the highest level of education attained by the participants’ primary caregivers, as a proxy for early life socioeconomic status. We did not adjust for individual-level socioeconomic status, as this may be a downstream effect of neighborhood disadvantage. However, because individual socioeconomic status may influence where people live, we ran secondary analyses, additionally adjusting for individual-level educational attainment. In addition, because smoking is a strong determinant of fertility and may be related to neighborhood context, we ran secondary analyses restricted to nonsmoking participants.

We stratified models by individual-level educational attainment (<college degree vs. college degree or higher), age at pregnancy attempt (<35 vs. ≥35 years), and parity (nulliparous vs. parous).

We multiply imputed missing outcome and covariate data using the fully conditional specification method. We did not impute missing neighborhood disadvantage scores. Missingness was generally low: 2.5% of pregnancy attempts were missing time-to-pregnancy data, and covariate missingness ranged from 0% (e.g., age) to 9.6% (income). We generated 20 imputed data sets and statistically combined results across data sets.

Results

The median time-to-pregnancy among attempts that resulted in a birth was 3 months (interquartile range = 1, 6), with some evidence of digit preference in reporting (e.g., peaks at 6 and 12 months). Approximately 25% of pregnancy attempts took 12 or more months to conceive. The mean age at pregnancy attempt was 33.9 years (standard deviation [SD] = 4.4; Table 1). Over 75% of participants had a college degree, and most had an annual household income ≥$50,000. At the time of the pregnancy attempt, around half of the participants had a previous birth. Mean age at first birth was 30.9 years (SD = 5.5), and 33% had a history of unplanned pregnancy. Most participants lived in an urban area (99%). Almost 90% of participants moved at least once during the study period.35 On average, the neighborhood disadvantage score of the new address was lower than that of the original address.

Neighborhood disadvantage was strongly inversely associated with individual-level educational attainment, household income, and the highest level of caregiver education (Table 1). Participants living in neighborhoods with low levels of disadvantage were more likely to be married or living as married, were less likely to be current smokers, had a lower body mass index, older age at first birth, and were more likely to have a history of unplanned pregnancy. Other covariates were not strongly associated with neighborhood disadvantage.

Participants in the top four quintiles of neighborhood disadvantage had lower fecundability relative to participants in the lowest (least disadvantaged) quintile (Table 2). FRs for participants in quintiles 2, 3, 4, and 5 (vs. 1) were 0.89 (95% CI = 0.79, 1.01), 0.94 (95% CI = 0.83, 1.06), 0.84 (95% CI = 0.74, 0.96), and 0.89 (95% CI = 0.79, 1.02), respectively. Results were slightly attenuated when we additionally adjusted for individual-level educational attainment (Table 2). The restricted cubic spline analysis showed a relatively monotonic association between higher neighborhood disadvantage and reduced fecundability (Figure 3). Results were similar when we restricted to first pregnancies during the study period, and were slightly stronger when we restricted to the most recent pregnancies. Models restricted to nonsmokers were nearly identical to the main results (data not shown).

Discussion

In this large cohort of US Black women, higher levels of neighborhood disadvantage were associated with reduced fecundability, a couple-based measure of fertility. There was evidence of effect modification by individual-level educational attainment: among participants with a college degree or higher, higher neighborhood disadvantage was associated with reduced fecundability, whereas among participants with less than a college degree, it was not. The association was also slightly stronger among nulliparous participants but was similar across strata of age.

Our findings are consistent with the one existing study examining the effect of neighborhood disadvantage on fecundability. In Pregnancy Study Online (PRESTO), a preconception cohort study of 6,356 US couples trying to conceive, the FR comparing participants living in the highest versus lowest decile of within-state Area Deprivation Index score was 0.77 (95% CI = 0.65, 0.92).32 Associations persisted after adjustment for individual-level educational attainment, and an association was observed only among participants with a college degree or higher. The consistent findings of an association between neighborhood disadvantage and fecundability in PRESTO and BWHS are compelling, particularly given the methodological differences between the two studies. PRESTO enrolls couples during the preconception period and measures time-to-pregnancy prospectively,45 whereas in BWHS, time-to-pregnancy was reported retrospectively. The two studies used different measures of neighborhood disadvantage, although both were designed to capture neighborhood constructs related to wealth, income, and education. Finally, the PRESTO population comprised a range of racial and ethnic groups (<5% of whom identified as Black), whereas BWHS is restricted to Black women only.

There are several pathways through which neighborhood disadvantage could influence fertility (Figure 1). Socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods are more likely to have health-harming physical environments, including higher levels of air pollution,25,46,47 poorer water quality,48 lower access to healthy foods,49 and fewer green spaces.50 Neighborhood disadvantage can affect the social environment, and residents of socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods have higher allostatic load,51 cortisol,52 and chronic stress53 than residents of advantaged neighborhoods. Neighborhood disadvantage is also related to social and political capital and opportunity for upward mobility, which have important effects on residents’ well-being.54 These exposures can have downstream effects on menstrual function,55–58 hormone levels,59–61 and inflammation.62 These mechanisms are particularly important for Black individuals who, due to historical and contemporary structural racism23,24 are more likely to reside in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods.63

We found evidence of an association between neighborhood disadvantage with reduced fecundability only among women with higher educational attainment. A previous study in BWHS likewise found that the association between census tract median household value and hypertension was stronger among participants with higher educational attainment.64 There are several potential explanations for this finding. First, income incongruity, a measure of relative income that describes the extent to which an individual’s household income differs from the median household income of their neighborhood (e.g., highly educated individuals living in disadvantaged neighborhoods), has been associated with adverse reproductive outcomes, including preterm birth,65 and may capture a separate dimension of privilege or disadvantage not included in our neighborhood disadvantage index. Second, highly educated women living in disadvantaged neighborhoods may experience different internalized and external pressures and stressors than women with low education living in the same neighborhoods. Specifically, highly educated women living in disadvantaged neighborhoods may endorse the Strong Black Woman role or Superwoman schema, a multidimensional culture-specific framework that characterizes Black women’s racialized and gendered experiences of and responses to stress.66 The dimensions of “feeling an intense motivation to succeed” and “feeling an obligation to help others” have been linked to poor health and may be particularly salient for women living in disadvantaged neighborhoods.67 Finally, the sociological concept of disadvantage saturation posits that individuals who are already highly disadvantaged in one context may be less influenced by disadvantage in other contexts.68 Previous studies have shown that neighborhood disadvantage has a stronger effect on college attainment among youth from more resourced families.69,70 Therefore, living in a disadvantaged neighborhood may have little added effect among women without a college degree beyond the disadvantage they are already experiencing due to their low educational attainment.

Our study had several limitations. First, participants retrospectively reported their time-to-pregnancy in 2011, which was up to 16 years after their pregnancies (median = 10 years). Therefore, we expect some outcome misclassification and digit preference in reporting. We expect that outcome misclassification was nondifferential with respect to neighborhood disadvantage, likely leading to attenuation of results. In fact, when we restricted to the pregnancy closest to 2011, for which reporting of time-to-pregnancy may be more accurate,42 associations were slightly stronger than the main analysis. In addition, only a small proportion of the BWHS cohort completed the 2011 supplemental questionnaire on pregnancy history, which limited our sample size.

Second, we used a composite neighborhood disadvantage score derived from Census block group data. Census block groups are administrative divisions that may not accurately reflect an individual’s definition of their neighborhood. The variables used to define the neighborhood disadvantage score were selected to represent dimensions of wealth, income, and education. However, scores did not capture all aspects of neighborhood advantage, and we did not have sufficient data to identify mechanisms driving the observed associations. In addition, we assessed the neighborhood disadvantage of residential neighborhoods only; we did not have information on other locations where participants spent significant time.

Third, we did not collect data on neighborhood disadvantage and individual-level socioeconomic status across the life course. While early life neighborhood context can influence opportunity for economic advancement, individual socioeconomic status can also influence where people have the option to live. We did not have sufficient time-varying data to distinguish between these two pathways. We observed attenuation of our results when we adjusted for individual educational attainment (a proxy for socioeconomic status). This could reflect unmeasured confounding of our main results by individual socioeconomic status or overadjustment by controlling for a variable on the causal pathway. Future studies with data collection across the life course may be able to further disentangle the role of neighborhood versus individual socioeconomic status in shaping fertility.

We restricted our analysis to participants with planned pregnancies, as we did not have information on all participants at risk of pregnancy (i.e., having regular unprotected intercourse). However, pregnancy intentions exist on a spectrum, and the concept of “planned” pregnancy is culturally specific and may reflect an upper-middle-class norm.71,72 In addition, our study population was, on average, older than the general population of pregnant individuals, due to the late introduction of the time-to-pregnancy survey (2011) and the ages of study participants at cohort enumeration (i.e., ages 21–69). In addition, around 75% of study participants had a college degree or higher. Therefore, our results may not be generalizable to the broader population of individuals at risk of pregnancy, notably those who do not identify as “trying to conceive,” younger reproductive-aged women, and women with lower educational attainment. Finally, we only asked about time-to-pregnancy for pregnancies that resulted in birth. While the exclusion of pregnancies that ended in a loss may lead to bias, previous retrospective time-to-pregnancy studies indicate that this bias is likely minimal.42,73

Conclusion

Inclusion of Black women in fertility research is essential for identifying and intervening on causes of racial disparities in infertility. We leveraged the largest cohort study in existence focused on the health of Black women to study risk factors for subfertility and found that higher neighborhood disadvantage was associated with reduced fecundability, particularly among Black women with high educational attainment. Neighborhood disadvantage may contribute to the higher rates of infertility seen in US Black women. These results indicate the importance of studying place-based risk factors for subfertility and have important implications for advancing reproductive justice.
