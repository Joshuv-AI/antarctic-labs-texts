Evaluation of SARS‐CoV‐2 Variants After the Declaration of End of COVID‐19 Pandemic: BA.2.86 (Pirola) and EG.5 (Eris) Variants as Global Public Health Concerns
Authors: Md Rabiul Islam, Md Sohan, A S M Roknuzzaman, Mehedi Islam, Mohammad Shahriar, Mohiuddin Ahmed Bhuiyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) caused by SARS‐CoV‐2 was first reported to the World Health Organization (WHO) on December 31, 2019. The WHO declared the COVID‐19 pandemic on March 11, 2020, after considering its impact on global public health. Therefore, we aim to assess the existing SARS‐CoV‐2 variants to understand their pathogenicity and threat after the pandemic phase of COVID‐19 so that the healthcare authorities can take prompt preventive measures.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive literature search to gather information from publicly available sources. We extracted the relevant data from Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus.ResultsThe virus infected over 770 million people and caused more than 6.9 million deaths worldwide. Since the inception of COVID‐19, the world has developed several therapeutic drugs and vaccines. Healthcare authorities worldwide have administered more than 13 billion vaccine doses. On May 5, 2023, the WHO declared the end of the COVID‐19 pandemic based on its global threat and impacts on human health. However, SARS‐CoV‐2 continues to mutate to create new variants that can threaten global public health anytime. We observed that BA.2.86 (Pirola) and EG.5 (Eris) variants as global public health concerns.ConclusionGenomic evolution of SARS‐CoV‐2 variants is a global public health concern for immune escape, vaccination effectiveness, therapeutic and diagnostic strategies. Therefore, we recommend close surveillance of these variants to determine their potential to cause severe disease and countermeasures.Keywords: BA.2.86, communicable disease, COVID‐19, EG.5, public health, SARS‐CoV‐2 variants

Background

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19), caused by the novel coronavirus known as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), is an infectious disease primarily affecting the respiratory tract but also impacting various organs in the body. The World Health Organization (WHO) was notified of the initial report on December 31, 2019, and subsequently classified the situation as a global pandemic on March 11, 2020. Since its initial appearance, the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus has spread extensively, affecting a global population of over 760 million individuals and causing significant fatalities exceeding 6.0 million [1, 2]. This widespread transmission can be attributed to the virus's ability to undergo frequent mutations and its great transmissibility. A comprehensive range of measures was implemented to address this catastrophe, encompassing initiatives such as enhancing public consciousness, executing large‐scale vaccination campaigns, and deploying therapeutic approaches. Globally, the administration of almost 13 billion vaccine doses has resulted in a notable improvement in the prevailing circumstances [3, 4]. As a result, the WHO formally announced on May 5, 2023, the termination of the pandemic classification for COVID‐19 [5]. Nevertheless, the virus exhibits an ongoing process of evolution via mutations, hence eliciting apprehensions regarding the possible appearance of variants with heightened lethality.

According to recent data provided by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the United States, there has been a notable 7.7% escalation in hospitalization rates and a corresponding 4.5% increase in mortality rates during the initial week of September 2023, in comparison to the preceding week [6]. This implies that there is a possibility of the emergence of highly altered versions of SARS‐CoV‐2 that exhibit enhanced lethality. This likelihood is particularly pronounced in the absence of rigorous post‐pandemic adherence to health safety measures, continuous surveillance efforts, and meticulous implementation of vaccination programs [7, 8, 9].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the existing SARS‐CoV‐2 variations that are now in circulation and analyze their potential impact on global public health. Additionally, we will suggest preventive strategies to mitigate the spread of these variants. To do this, we performed a comprehensive literature search in Scopus and PubMed to extract information related to coronavirus variants. We only selected literature written in English for this review.

XBB.1.5 Variant

The XBB.1.5 variety, a specific subvariant of the Omicron variant, has been colloquially referred to as “Kraken” within the scientific community [14, 15]. Significantly, it exhibits an extra F486P mutation within its spike protein compared to XBB.1, resulting in a relatively reduced vulnerability to immunological responses while enhancing its binding affinity to ACE‐2 receptors [11]. The distinctive attribute has played a significant role in facilitating its swift dissemination. In the preceding year, a considerable proportion, around 50%, of the COVID‐19 infections in the United States were attributed to the XBB.1.5 variant [16]. As of June 20, 2023, the virus had disseminated throughout 112 nations, with the preponderance of genetic sequences originating from the United States (47.1%), Canada (8.5%), and Austria (4.9%).

Nonetheless, a notable reduction in its occurrence from 36.7% to 23.3% during the initial weeks of May and June 2023 [17]. The symptoms attributed to XBB.1.5 exhibit similarities to those observed in prior Omicron subvariants [18, 19, 20]. A novel vaccination has been created with the objective of reducing the severity of diseases, hospitalization rates, and deaths associated with the XBB.1.5 variation. The Moderna SPIKEVAX COVID‐19 vaccine has been approved by Health Canada and MHRA for use as a booster to address the specific variation in question [21, 22].

XBB.1.16 Variant

The XBB.1.16 variant was initially reported on January 9, 2023, and was subsequently classified as a VOI by the WHO. XBB.1.16 demonstrates genetic similarities with its parental variety, XBB (XBB.1.16 and XBB.1.5), although it displays unique mutations in the amino acid sequences of the S protein, namely K478R and E180V. As of June 5, 2023, the aforementioned variant has been recorded in 66 nations, encompassing a cumulative count of 19,847 genetic sequences that have been detected. India exhibited the highest prevalence of XBB.1.16, with a rate of 40.7%. Subsequently, the United States of America recorded a prevalence of 10.9%, while Australia observed a prevalence of 6.5%. From mid‐April 2023 to mid‐May 2023, there was a notable increase in XBB.1.16 infections, as shown by a rise in global prevalence from 10.2% to 16.8%. Nevertheless, the already accessible data does not suggest significant harm to the general health of the world population due to this particular mutation [23]. Novavax, an international corporation, is now developing a vaccine specifically designed to target the XBB.1.16 variation. The company asserts that its vaccine has demonstrated encouraging outcomes in animal‐based research [24].

EG.5 Variant

The variant EG.5, also called Eris, was initially reported by the WHO on February 17, 2023. It was subsequently classified as a VUM on July 19, 2023. The subvariant EG.5 (Eris) exhibits a spike amino acid profile closely resembling its parent subvariant, XBB.1.5 (Kraken). However, it also possesses an extra amino acid mutation, F456L, in the spike protein. On August 8, 2023, the WHO designated EG.5 and its sub‐lineages as a VOI after conducting a risk assessment [25]. The distinctive mutations distinguish it from other strains, potentially heightening its societal hazard. Based on data provided by the CDC, it was seen that over the period spanning from September 3 to September 16, 2023, a total of 24.5% of diagnosed cases in the United States were explicitly linked to EG.5. The incidence of EG.5 has exhibited a consistent upward trend on a global scale, with documented instances escalating from 7.6% on June 25, 2023, to 17.4% by August 9, 2023 [26]. According to reports dated September 15, 2023, it has been observed that EG.5 exhibits mild to moderate symptoms despite its 1.2 to 1.3 times higher infectivity compared to the XBB subvariant of SARS‐CoV‐2 [27]. To address this particular subvariant, Moderna, Novavax, and Pfizer have developed updated versions of booster doses that specifically target XBB.1.5, which is a closely related variant to the progenitor of EG.5, known as XBB.1.9.2. The anticipated booster doses are projected to provide substantial immunity in comparison to currently available vaccinations targeting the EG.5 variant. Certain antiviral medications, namely nirmatrelvir and ritonavir, have demonstrated efficacy in combating viral infections [28, 29].

BA.2.75 Variant

The BA.2.75 variant is a specific subvariant of the Omicron variant. Within the broader BA.2 group (BA.2.74, BA.2.76, BA.2.12.1, BA.2.75), both BA.2.75 and BA.2.12.1 hold notable importance. The prevalence of BA.2.75 has exhibited a substantial surge in India, although its incidence in other nations is somewhat restricted compared to India [30, 31, 32]. Research findings indicate that the BA.2.75 variant demonstrates a substantial 57‐fold enhancement in its affinity for ACE‐2 receptors when compared to the BA.5 variant. Moreover, it surpasses the Alpha version by exhibiting a more than 3000‐fold increase in receptor binding affinity [33].

Nevertheless, hospitalization is infrequently required due to the disease induced by BA.2.75. The prevalence of BA.2.75 is reported mainly in Asia; however, its presence has also been identified in the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, and Canada. Preliminary examinations have detected specific changes, including G446S, R493Q, K147E, and W152R, within the S protein of BA.2.75 [34, 35]. Of these mutations, the G446S variant has been suggested to potentially impact immunological resistance [36]. Upon further examination, it was discovered that nine more mutations are present in the spike protein when compared to the BA.2 variant [37]. A study has evaluated the efficacy of bivalent booster doses, revealing that bivalent boosters demonstrate enhanced neutralizing action against all omicron subvariants, including BA.2.75, compared to monovalent boosters [38, 39].

CH.1.1 Variant

CH.1.1 represents a variant of the SARS‐CoV‐2 virus. The origin of its emergence may be traced back to July 2022 in India, stemming from the Omicron BA.2.75 lineage. It elicited considerable apprehension within the public health domain during its first dissemination [40]. This particular strain later spread to multiple nations, including the United Kingdom, the United States, Denmark, and Japan, playing a vital role in the prevalence of CH.1.1‐associated diseases. Scientists have named it “Orthrus.” Significantly, this particular variant exhibits the L452R mutation inside its S protein, a mutation that has been previously identified in the BA.4/5 and Delta variants. This suggests that the variant may possess a heightened ability to transmit [41]. Instances of CH.1.1‐associated disease have primarily been documented in Europe, followed by Asia, Oceania, and North America, whereas South America has exhibited a relatively low incidence rate [42]. Antiviral medicines, including remdesivir, nirmatrelvir, ensitrelvir, and molnupiravir, have demonstrated efficacy in the management of illnesses associated with CH.1.1. The potential effectiveness of integrating these therapeutic interventions with bivalent mRNA vaccines in addressing CH.1.1 infections is noteworthy [43].

XBB.2.3 Variant

The XBB.2.3 variation, known as Omicron, is believed to have originated in India and was thereafter disseminated to around 50 nations by the end of July 2023, encompassing the United States and the United Kingdom. In India, researchers have successfully discovered six distinct lineages of this particular variant, among which the lineage XBB.2.3.2 has garnered significant attention due to its notable efficacy. This specific variant displays four notable mutations, including S:D253G and S:P521S mutations in the S protein, as well as ORF7a:A13V and ORF1a:G2091S mutations in the open reading frames (ORF). It has been found to have a higher transmission rate compared to XBB.1.16, primarily attributed to its increased binding capacity to ACE‐2 receptors [44]. On September 23, 2023, the Department of Health in the Philippines released a report indicating that the XBB variants, specifically XBB.2.3, persistently impact a substantial population portion. During the period spanning from September 11 to September 12, 2023, a total of 184 occurrences were recorded. Among these cases, 154 were identified as being associated with XBB variations, with a significant majority being tied explicitly to the XBB.2.3 variant [45]. Owing to its quick spread, the FDA in the USA has declared that booster shots targeting XBB.2.3, XBB.1.5, and XBB.1.16 will be given in the fall of 2023 [46].

BA.2.86 Variant

On August 17, 2023, the WHO officially classified BA.2.86, known as “Pirola,” as a currently monitored variant. The global dissemination of this unique variety has been observed in multiple nations, namely Denmark, Israel, South Africa, the United Kingdom, and the United States [47]. Using variant‐specific quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), researchers have successfully detected the presence of the ΔH69/V70 mutation within the BA.2.86 variant [48]. A comprehensive analysis of global surveillance endeavors has documented a cumulative count of 122 genetic mutations, of which a majority of 79 instances have been observed in the S gene. Although there is a scarcity of epidemiological data regarding SARS‐CoV‐2 BA.2.86, the prevailing consensus among scientists is that its impact is expected to be less substantial compared to previous Omicron variants [49, 50]. As to the findings of scientist Bloom, antibodies provide the most effective defense against infection, while highly altered forms such as BA.2.86 have the potential to evade neutralization mechanisms. However, it is essential to note that antibodies can still offer a certain level of protection against highly altered viruses by employing more comprehensive mechanisms. Considering these considerations, Bloom believed that implementing widespread vaccination initiatives on a global scale should mitigate the potential risks associated with the altered variations, thereby reducing their threat level compared to past iterations of the Omicron variety [51, 52]. The efficacy of COVID‐19 medication against the BA.2.86 variant has been determined by the CDC. Furthermore, the CDC anticipates that the forthcoming revised vaccines will effectively mitigate the severity of the disease and decrease the rates of hospitalization associated with this particular variant [53].

BA.2.86 and EG.5 Variants Seem to Be Different From Others

The SARS‐CoV‐2 virus undergoes continuous mutation, resulting in the emergence of novel forms. Among the aforementioned varieties, EG.5 distinguishes itself by mutations present in its spike amino acid sequence, which confer the ability to elude the immunological response mounted by the host organism. As a result, it is seeing rapid global proliferation. The immune evasion capacities, heightened transmissibility, and severity of disease exhibited by both EG.5 and other variants of SARS‐CoV‐2 are contributing to the emergence of subsequent waves of infections [54]. The EG.5 variant has been identified in a minimum of 66 countries and has been attributed to more than 68.3% of COVID‐19 infections in China. Recognizing this situation, the WHO reclassified it as a VOI on August 1, 2023, after initially designating it as a VUM [55]. Unfortunately, the changes discovered in EG.5 have facilitated enhanced binding to receptor sites on human cells, thereby evading the action of antibodies [28].

The present COVID‐19 variant persists in exerting its impact on a significant number of people, as evidenced by recent reports that have identified it as the prevailing strain inside the United States, responsible for the majority of reported cases. According to the CDC, on August 18, 2023, EG.5 was identified as the agent responsible for an additional 20.6% of infections. Within a fortnight, namely, by September 1, 2023, it had resulted in an additional 21.5% of illnesses [56].

On the other hand, the mutations seen in the S gene of BA.2.86 may present a substantial peril to the human population. The many mutations observed in this particular variation could confer the ability to elude the immune system, thereby classifying it as a variation of concern. The global prevalence of BA.2.86 has been seen following the formation of the omicron variant. This variant is distinguished by more than 30 mutations in the long chain of the S protein, which contribute to an enhanced ability to enter host cells. As of August 2023, the BA.2.86 variation had been detected in 14 countries. A report released on September 11, 2023, in England, disclosed that the BA.2.86 variant impacted approximately 37 individuals, out of which seven necessitated hospitalization and two were detected in the emergency room. Furthermore, Scotland recorded a total of five occurrences on the aforementioned day [57].

The prevalence of mutations, the heightened transmissibility, and the worrisome attributes exhibited by EG.5 and BA.2.86 have fundamentally altered our comprehension of the worldwide pandemic, underscoring the ease with which SARS‐CoV‐2 variants can disseminate among susceptible communities. The co‐occurrence of several mutations within a single variation of SARS‐CoV‐2 implies that the virus has potentially evolved a proficient mechanism to maximize its infectivity among individuals. These findings emphasize the possible risk to global public health.

Suggested Preventive Measures

The available evidence indicates that BA.2.86 and EG.5, among the assortment of COVID‐19 variations, present noteworthy issues for society owing to their substantial mutation count in comparison to preceding variants. According to experts, it is important to be attentive and closely monitor these new variants. Still, it is not anticipated that the situation would rise to the same degree as observed with the delta or omicron variants. The potential for a surge in infections resulting from emerging variations exists following the official announcement of the end of the COVID‐19 pandemic in May 2023. The observed phenomenon can be attributed to the gradual deceleration of the vaccination procedure and individuals' unwillingness to comply with health safety protocols. Hence, it is crucial to diligently observe these deviations and enhance public consciousness regarding adhering to established health and safety measures. Furthermore, tailored vaccinations may be required to address the challenges posed by these evolving variations, requiring additional investigation and innovation to mitigate the related health hazards effectively. Enhancing immunization rates is crucial as well.

The importance of conducting thorough research to understand the unique attributes of this newly identified subvariant should be emphasized. Simultaneously, it is imperative for scientists to investigate crucial elements, including the rates of transmission, sensitivity to antibodies, patterns of mutation, affinities for receptor binding, and skills for immune evasion exhibited by BA.2.86 and EG.5, alongside other VOIs and VUMs. In addition, it is of utmost importance to prioritize advancing dependable diagnostic methods that can effectively validate the existence of these subvariants. There is a great imperative for scientists to investigate these domains further to elucidate the characteristics of these variants thoroughly, therefore augmenting endeavors in surveillance and monitoring. Implementing this complete strategy will contribute to the mitigation of this highly transmissible subvariant, which has the potential to exhibit increased immune evasion. Additionally, it would facilitate the development of proactive strategies for implementing control measures and preparedness protocols to mitigate subsequent waves of Omicron infections, thus safeguarding the global population amid the present COVID‐19 pandemic.

Researchers have predicted that there is a chance that additional subvariants will emerge in the future, which could result in the COVID‐19 pandemic lasting longer. In the event of such a circumstance, it is possible that the current COVID‐19 vaccines may experience a decline in their ability to provide adequate immunity and necessitate modifications. Concurrently, it will be imperative to advance the progress of next‐generation vaccines, which possess the ability to withstand mutations to combat the continuously emerging variations or subvariants effectively.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our research highlights the critical significance of controlling the occurrence and transmission of SARS‐CoV‐2 mutations. The Omicron variants exhibit a wide range of genetic mutations that have the potential to increase their predominance and confer a competitive advantage in terms of growth rate in specific countries relative to other contemporary strains. The BA.2.86 (Pirola) and EG.5 variants (Eris) are global public health concerns. Therefore, we recommend a rigorous monitoring effort focused on these variants to identify any potentially severe disease‐inducing effects they may have.
