The electrocardiogram reimagined: AI-ECG as a prognostic beacon for valvular heart disease
Authors: Antonis A Armoundas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical Abstract Graphical Abstract

This editorial refers to ‘Artificial intelligence-enhanced electrocardiography to predict regurgitant valvular heart diseases: an international study’, by Y. Liang et al., https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf448.

Introduction

Valvular heart disease (VHD) stands as a formidable global health challenge, affecting >41 million individuals worldwide, and contributing substantially to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.1 While timely medical, surgical, or transcatheter interventions have demonstrably improved outcomes for clinically significant VHD, a persistent clinical unmet need lies in the early identification of patients at high risk of developing or progressing to severe disease. Current clinical guidelines often recommend regular echocardiographic surveillance for patients with mild VHD without systematic predictive models, imposing significant strain on healthcare resources and potentially missing crucial windows for proactive intervention in asymptomatic individuals.2,3 In this evolving landscape, the ubiquitous, inexpensive, and non-invasive 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), when augmented with artificial intelligence (AI), has emerged as a promising and scalable tool for cardiovascular screening and risk stratification.4 The recent study by Liang et al.,5 published in this issue of the European Heart Journal, represents a pivotal advancement of AI-ECG beyond the diagnosis of prevalent VHD to the crucial domain of predicting future regurgitant VHD (rVHD) progression.5

The foundation: AI-ECG for prevalent VHD diagnosis

Early foundational studies laid the groundwork by showcasing the ability of AI-ECG to detect various forms of VHD,6 or determining the severity of various forms of VHD.7 However, a common limitation of these early models was their primary focus on diagnosing prevalent disease at a single point in time, with less emphasis on predicting future progression or accounting for the time to event. The study by Liang et al. directly addresses this critical gap, by innovating in its methodological approach to transition from mere diagnosis to dynamic, time-dependent prediction.5

A new frontier: predicting rVHD progression

While diagnosing prevalent VHD is undoubtedly valuable, predicting its future progression represents a significant and clinically impactful leap forward in proactive patient management. Current clinical guidelines, however, often lack systematic predictive models for VHD progression, frequently recommending unselective, resource-intensive echocardiographic surveillance for patients with mild VHD.2,3

The study of Liang, et al.5 directly addresses this unmet clinical need. Their central hypothesis was that an AI-ECG model could not only accurately diagnose VHD but also predict its future progression to clinically significant forms. Their methodological innovation is a key differentiator: they employed a discrete-time survival loss function within a residual convolutional neural network architecture. This sophisticated approach allows the AI-ECG models to simultaneously diagnose existing rVHD and predict its future development over time, explicitly accounting for the time to event and censorship, inherent in longitudinal clinical data. The results are compelling and clinically meaningful (Graphical Abstract). In the internal test set, the AI-ECG survival models accurately predicted future significant mitral regurgitation (MR) (C-index 0.774), aortic regurgitation (AR) (0.691), and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) (0.793). These findings were confirmed in an external test set from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC), Boston, USA. The external validation, while showing a modest decrease in C-indices (MR 0.698, AR 0.647, and TR 0.702), crucially demonstrated the generalizability of the models across geographically and ethnically distinct cohorts,8,9 noting that despite the fact that the models that were trained predominantly on an East Asian population, they exhibited comparable performance across diverse ethnic groups of the predominantly White, Black, and Hispanic American external test set.

Unpacking the ‘why’: biological plausibility and explainability

The study by Liang et al. makes a significant contribution by exploring the underlying biological mechanisms driving the predictive power of AI-ECG, and demonstrating that AI-ECG predicts rVHD progression by detecting subclinical cardiac chamber remodelling. This is an important finding, as it provides a pathophysiological basis for the predictive capabilities of AI, suggesting that AI does not merely provide for a statistical correlation, but is discerning early myocardial electrical activity changes that precede or occur concurrently with the development of rVHD.

Secondary rVHDs: often caused by underlying myocardial conditions (e.g. cardiomyopathy) that lead to chamber dilatation and dysfunction, which in turn affect valve function. For instance, left ventricular dilatation and dysfunction can lead to secondary MR, while right ventricular and atrial enlargement can cause secondary TR.7

The study further enhances transparency through explainability analyses using variational autoencoders, identifying specific ECG morphologies associated with predicted rVHD progression. The reported findings (Graphical Abstract) align remarkably well with known cardiac electrical and structural changes associated with specific valve lesions, bolstering the biological interpretability of the AI-ECG predictions and fostering greater clinician confidence.

The authors also candidly address the observed lower performance for AR prediction compared with MR and TR. They attribute this disparity to the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms of AR, which often involves distortion or dilation of the aortic root and/or ascending aorta—structures devoid of significant electrical activity, and thus less detectable by ECG. Furthermore, the attenuated contribution of left atrial involvement in AR compared with MR and TR, coupled with the greater resilience of the left ventricle to pressure fluctuations, could diminish predictive accuracy.

Critical appraisal and limitations

While the study by Liang, et al. represents a significant leap forward in predictive cardiology, some inherent limitations should be acknowledged, and potentially become areas for future development.

Methodological considerations

The retrospective nature of data collection introduces inherent patient selection bias. Patients undergoing transthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs) were probably selected based on specific clinical indications, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to a broader, unselected population. Although a sensitivity analysis in the BIDMC outpatients, with or without baseline echocardiogram, provided some reassurance regarding selection bias, future prospective studies utilizing non-symptom-driven ECGs in random screening populations are essential for a robust assessment of the utility of the model.

Furthermore, the echocardiographic assessment of rVHD severity relied on the interpretation provided by cardiologists, which introduces the potential for interobserver variability. While the misdiagnosis rate is likely to be very low in these experienced centres, it is conceivable that a small number of incident significant rVHDs might have been initially misdiagnosed as mild or normal. The study has also faced limitations in data completeness, as certain patient characteristics, such as comorbidities and symptom status, were not consistently available across cohorts. The indication for echocardiography was also not recorded. This lack of comprehensive clinical context limits the ability to fully adjust for all potential confounders. Lastly, due to limited patient numbers, the performance of the model in individuals who had undergone prior valve surgery could not be robustly assessed.

Performance nuances and specific challenges

Despite demonstrating generalizability, the models exhibited a modest decrease in C-indices in the external test set compared with the internal test set (Graphical Abstract). This observation can be attributed to inherent differences in patient characteristics and clinical practice settings between the derivation and external validation cohorts, including variations in disease incidence. For clinical deployment, this implies that while a model might be broadly applicable, local fine-tuning or continuous local learning from real-world data might be necessary to maximize performance within specific healthcare systems or patient populations.

Notably, a diminished performance of AR prediction, yielding a lower C-index compared with MR and TR, was observed in the external set (Graphical Abstract). This disparity is likely to be attributed to distinct pathophysiological mechanisms: AR often involves distortion or dilation of aortic structures (aortic root and/or ascending aorta) that are largely devoid of significant electrical activity, making them inherently less detectable by ECG. The slower progression trajectory of AR also means fewer positive instances for the AI model to learn from during training, potentially compromising its predictive accuracy. This suggests inherent limits to the utility of AI-ECG for certain conditions, regardless of model sophistication. It rather emphasizes the notion that AI-ECG should be viewed as a complementary tool within a multi-modal diagnostic approach, rather than a standalone solution for all cardiac pathologies.

While the study focuses on prediction, it reveals a general limitation: despite high accuracy and sensitivity, the positive predictive value (PPV) is low; a low PPV implies that a substantial proportion of AI-ECG ‘positives’ might not actually develop the disease. This could lead to unnecessary follow-up echocardiograms and patient anxiety, potentially straining resources even while attempting to optimize them. This highlights a crucial challenge for clinical implementation: the balance between sensitivity (missing no high-risk patients) and PPV (avoiding false alarms). For AI-ECG to be truly impactful as a screening tool, strategies to improve PPV or clear pathways for managing false positives (e.g. risk stratification tiers) are essential before widespread adoption.

Clinical translation and prospective validation

Despite the promising results, the authors acknowledge that the modest performance, particularly in the external test set, suggests that the current models may not yet be ready for clinical deployment, requiring rigorous prospective evaluation in dedicated clinical trials. Such trials are essential to demonstrate that AI-ECG-guided surveillance strategies indeed lead to earlier detection, timely intervention, and, ultimately, superior patient outcomes.

Clinical implications and the path forward

Existing guidelines often recommend unselective, regular echocardiographic surveillance for patients with mild VHD, a practice that is resource intensive and may not be optimally targeted.2,3 The AI-ECG models developed in this study, may transform this approach by facilitating risk-stratified surveillance. By accurately identifying patients at high risk of developing future moderate or severe rVHD, AI-ECG could enable more intense follow-up for these individuals, ensuring timely monitoring and prompt intervention, potentially preventing adverse outcomes such as heart failure or arrhythmias. Conversely, low-risk individuals could benefit from reduced frequency of echocardiographic surveillance, optimizing resource utilization and reducing patient burden. The time-dependent predictions of the models further enable dynamic adjustments to clinical evaluations, supporting personalized treatment planning by identifying high-risk patients who might benefit from earlier medical, surgical, or transcatheter interventions, in alignment with the contemporary shift towards earlier intervention in appropriate candidates.11

Moreover, integration of AI-ECG with wearable devices offers a tantalizing prospect. Continuous ECG monitoring from wearables could feed these AI models for real-time risk assessment, allowing for even earlier detection and intervention in a highly accessible manner.4,12–15 This could fundamentally shift VHD management from reactive to proactive, improving patient outcomes and potentially reducing the overall burden on healthcare systems.

Despite these immense promises, the authors maintain a sense of scientific prudence, acknowledging that the current models may not be immediately ready for widespread practical deployment, highlighting the iterative nature of AI development in medicine. The study’s limitations, including its retrospective nature and potential for selection bias (though mitigated by sensitivity analyses), reliance on echocardiographer interpretation, lack of symptom status data, and limited data on patients with prior valve surgery, underscore the need for further research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study by Liang, et al.5 represents a significant and compelling stride in AI-ECG research. It builds upon a foundation of prior work in AI-ECG diagnostics and decisively pushes the frontier towards predictive cardiology, a critical step in enabling truly proactive and personalized patient care for VHD. The successful transnational validation, coupled with the exploration of biological plausibility and explainability, positions this work as a beacon for future innovation.
