Promoting Workplace Breastfeeding Practices
Authors: Naveen Ramesh, Archana Siddaiah, Bobby Joseph
Published: 2023

The labor force participation rate for women aged 15–64 years was 25% for India in 2021.[1] Periodic national surveys have indicated declining female labor force in India in the past 2 decades.[1] There are many barriers that women face related to accessing decent work, security, competing burdens of work, and family responsibilities including child care. However, the Periodic Labour Force Survey Report 2022–23 released by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation shows that the female labor force participation rate in the country has marginally improved.[2] Female labor force participation is a driver of growth, and therefore, participation rates indicate the potential for a country to grow more rapidly. Therefore, it is important to have policies that are conducive for women to participate in labor force.

In this regard, the International Labour Organization (ILO) has come up with gender-responsive policy frameworks to enable women’s participation. One such policy is the Maternity Protection Convention.[3] Along with maternity benefits, employment protection, and non-discrimination, article 10 of the convention specifies the rights of breastfeeding mothers. The article states, “A woman shall be provided with the right to one or more daily breaks or a daily reduction of hours of work to breastfeed her child” and “The period during which nursing breaks or the reduction of daily hours of work are allowed, their number, the duration of nursing breaks and the procedures for the reduction of daily hours of work shall be determined by national law and practice.”[3] ILO recommends a breastfeeding-friendly workplace as having a “comfortable, private space to express breast milk, access to a fridge to store it, a clean and safe environment, as well as day-care facilities and family-friendly working time arrangements for both women and men”. Since the ILO convention on maternity protection, nursing breaks for breastfeeding mothers during working hours have been part of the international standards on maternity protection. Legislation in at least 92 countries provides for breastfeeding breaks, in addition to regular breaks, for nursing mothers.[4] However, India is yet to ratify this convention and therefore does not have a legislation for encouraging breastfeeding by working mothers.[5] Nonetheless, India has enacted Maternity Benefit Act, which entitles working women paid leave from 12 to 26 weeks immediately following delivery or miscarriage.[6] A recent provision that has been added to this is the availability of creche facilities in workplaces having more than 50 employees.

Article 24 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child states that every infant and child have the right to good nutrition.[7] Ensuring society and parents have information about the advantages of breastfeeding and supporting mothers to breastfeed infants is an important measure to achieve this right. This can be done by community engagement, mother support groups, and educational campaigns on breastfeeding. Breastfeeding has numerous advantages for the baby as well as the mother. Ensuring optimal breastfeeding for all children in the first 2 years of life has the potential to save lives of at least 800,000 under 5 children every year.[89] A meta-analysis reported protection against childhood infections and malocclusion, increases in intelligence, and probable reductions in overweight and diabetes. For lactating women, breastfeeding gave protection against breast cancer, improved birth spacing, and also protection against ovarian cancer and type 2 diabetes mellitus.[8] In spite of clear benefits, breastfeeding practices are still far from recommendations. Globally, the prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding among infants younger than 6 months is only 38%.[10] Breastfeeding practices can be affected by several factors, with employment among them.

The World Breastfeeding Week (WBW) is celebrated every year between August 1 and 7 in commemoration of the Innocenti Declaration of 1990. WBW started in the year 1992, and since 2016, it has been in sync with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG). The World Health Assembly in 2018 recognized WBW as an important strategy to promote breastfeeding. WBW aims to create awareness and global action on themes related to breastfeeding.[11] World Alliance for Breastfeeding Action (WABA) coordinates the global WBW campaign through their network of individuals and organizations who work toward promotion and protection and support the cause of breastfeeding. The objective of this campaign is to inform, anchor, engage, and galvanize action on breastfeeding.[11]

The WBW theme for the year 2023 is ‘Enabling Breastfeeding Making a difference for working parents’, which is in line with area 4 of WBW-SDG 2030. The objective of WBW 2023 is to ‘Inform’ people about working parents’ perspectives on breastfeeding and parenting; ‘Anchor’ optimal paid leave and workplace support as important tools to enable breastfeeding; ‘Engage’ with individuals and organizations to enhance collaboration and support for breastfeeding at work; and ‘Galvanise’ action on improving working conditions and relevant support for breastfeeding.[11] In this year’s WBW, UNICEF and WHO, under its theme “Let’s make breastfeeding and work, work!”, emphasized on the need for greater breastfeeding support across all workplaces to sustain and improve progress on breastfeeding rates globally. The reasons for this theme for the year 2023 are based on the fact that maternity protection is not available for more than half a billion working women, paid breaks and facilities for breastfeeding are mandated in just 20% of the countries, and less than half of the children born to working women are exclusively breastfeed for 6 months.[12] The campaign for this year’s theme is to focus on breastfeeding and working women. Working women should not be caught between their work and breastfeeding their children. The campaign provides an opportunity to promote and support exclusive and continued breastfeeding by all working women irrespective of where they work.[13]

In India, the majority of women workforce is employed in informal sectors such as textiles, brick kiln industries, construction industries, and so on. An informal sector is marked by a lack of structured legal policies for safeguarding rights of employees. Women particularly will not be able to avail schemes such as maternity leave or systems to support breastfeeding practices while working. A study done in Delhi reported that more than half of women working in informal sectors reported returning to work within 6 months, leading to early weaning.[14] Another cross-sectional study done among hospital employees reported that more than one-third of mothers perceived poor workplace breastfeeding support and exclusive breastfeeding was done by only 58% of women.[15] A systematic review revealed that workplace interventions are important, promoting breastfeeding among working mothers.[16]

Policies such as the ILO convention on maternity protection and campaigns by UNICEF and WHO enable breastfeeding practices, and they also promote a higher rate of return to work after maternity through boosting the morale of the working mother. The key message for the policy makers, employers, managers, and colleagues is that breast milk provides all the essential nutrients required by the infant, which has a positive lifelong impact, which in turn helps build healthy workforce for the future. All working women, irrespective of their workplace and type of contract, should be eligible for at least 12 weeks but preferably more than 6 months of paid maternity leave, flexible working options (teleworking or working from home, part time work, and crèche/on-site childcare facilities), and paid time off to feed the child on return to work. Each and every workplace must have a champaign for workplace women’s right, including a supporting environment during pregnancy and after birth for promoting breastfeeding. For this, employers should be authorized to establish breastfeeding rooms and an exclusive and properly equipped area for mothers to breastfeed their children, express milk, and store it.[17] Formulating policies targeting both formal and informal sectors to support working mothers is the need of the hour. Such supportive policies can potentially boost rapid growth and progression of our country.
