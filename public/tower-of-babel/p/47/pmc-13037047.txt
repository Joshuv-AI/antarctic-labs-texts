Can we protect refugee children from mental health problems?— a scoping review of modifiable factors to inform preventive interventions
Authors: Rita Gonçalves, M J Marques, Jordan Sibeoni, Sónia Dias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing number of refugee children worldwide raises urgent public-health concerns regarding their mental health and well-being. Preventive approaches can mitigate risks before the onset of disorders, yet their development requires a clear understanding of modifiable determinants of mental health. Previous reviews have rarely applied a public-health and prevention lens to distinguish between modifiable and non-modifiable factors. This scoping review aimed to identify and organize potentially modifiable determinants of refugee children’s mental health to inform the design of multi-level preventive strategies.MethodsFollowing the Joanna Briggs Institute and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, we conducted systematic searches in PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science for studies published from 2011 to January 2025. Eligible studies reported qualitative or quantitative data on modifiable factors associated with mental health outcomes among refugee and asylum-seeking children and adolescents (0–18 years). Data were analyzed using a socio-ecological framework encompassing individual, family, community, and policy/institutional levels.ResultsForty-nine studies were included. Despite methodological diversity, we identified multiple interrelated modifiable factors influencing mental health, such as coping and language proficiency (individual), parental mental health and family routines (family), social support and discrimination (community), and asylum policies or access to education and health services (policy/institutional). These factors interact dynamically across levels, shaping both vulnerability and resilience.ConclusionsThis review provides a prevention-oriented, public-health framework for action on refugee children’s mental health. Targeting these interconnected determinants through coordinated, multi-level, and co-designed interventions may enhance resilience, promote equity, and guide policy priorities for refugee child mental-health promotion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04619-z.

Background

The growing number of refugees, particularly children and adolescents [1, 2], presents new public health challenges, including mental health problems [2, 3]. Prevalence rates of post-traumatic stress (PTSD), depression, and anxiety disorders among refugee children, have been reported as high as 53%, 33%, and 32%, respectively [4]. These disorders are associated with a range of etiological factors occurring across the migration process—from pre-migration trauma, to uncertainty and instability during displacement, and post-migration challenges, such as discrimination, social isolation, and poor living conditions [5–10].

Mental health problems represent a significant burden, resulting in high human and socioeconomic costs and jeopardizing the healthy integration of young refugees into host countries [2, 3, 11]. Given the still limited effectiveness of treatments, there is an urgent need to shift toward a public health approach that prioritizes the prevention of mental disorders by addressing their underlying determinants [11, 12].

From a public health perspective, prevention aims to modify risk and protective factors before the appearance of any symptoms of disease, moving the focus beyond treating individual illness to promoting population-level well-being [13, 14]. This requires distinguishing between non-modifiable and modifiable risk and protective factors [13, 15]. Modifiable factors are those social, environmental, or behavioral determinants that can be changed or mitigated. While factors such as pre-migration trauma are unchangeable, a range of others—including parenting practices, school support, access to stable housing, and community inclusion—are potentially modifiable through interventions or public policies [12, 14, 16].

Health-related decisions and behaviors, especially for children, are embedded within family, community and cultural contexts, often beyond the control of individuals [13, 16]. People rarely make health decisions in isolation but rather through interaction and support from other people, institutions, and organizations [15–17]. Therefore, prevention cannot rely solely on individual-level change but must also address the social and structural determinants. This ecological understanding represents the foundation of a public health approach to child mental health, emphasizing modifiable determinants, multi-level action, and early intervention [16–18].

Although there is a growing body of literature on the topic of refugee child mental health, few studies have explicitly adopted a prevention-oriented perspective to distinguish which factors are potentially modifiable and can inform preventive strategies [7–10]. Previous socio-ecological reviews have provided valuable mappings of risk and protective factors [9, 10], yet they have generally focused on identification and description rather than translation into intervention. The present review extends this body of work by bridging theoretical models with a practical, prevention-focused framework, explicitly emphasizing modifiable factors and their underlying mechanisms to guide the design of multi-level strategies for promoting mental health among refugee children.

Furthermore, reviews of existing preventive interventions have identified several limitations [19–21], including a predominant focus on single settings (most often family or school), limited coordination across intervention levels, insufficient attention to contextual diversity across host settings, a reliance on diagnosis-specific rather than transdiagnostic approaches, and scarce investigation into the mechanisms underlying change [19, 21–23]. This highlights a persistent gap between theory and practice.

Given these gaps, this scoping review pretends to examine the evidence on modifiable factors associated with refugee children’s mental health. By organizing the available evidence through a prevention-oriented, public health perspective, we aim to clarify which factors are amenable to change, inform the design of preventive strategies, and guide cross-sectoral policies. To our knowledge, this is the first review to apply this specific, prevention-focused approach, which we argue is essential for translating theory into effective public health action.

Methods

A scoping review was selected as the most appropriate approach to address the question: “What is the current evidence on modifiable factors associated with mental health in refugee children, and how can this inform preventive interventions?” [24]. A review protocol was developed by the authors, although not registered; it is available upon request. The review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodological guidance for scoping reviews [25, 26], and reporting adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension (PRISMA), in its checklist for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [27].

Operationalization of modifiable factors

Modifiable factors were defined as determinants of mental health that can be altered or mitigated through behavioral, psychosocial, clinical, or policy interventions. This conceptualization aligns with public health frameworks for preventable risk [15, 28, 29], including the classification adopted in the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 Injuries and Risk Factors Study [30], widely used in population health research. Factors were organized according to a socioecological model, encompassing multiple and interacting levels of influence [17, 31]. Under this approach, modifiable factors are distinguished from non-modifiable characteristics, such as age, gender, country of origin, or pre-migration trauma exposure [32].

A coding framework was developed to classify factors as modifiable or non-modifiable, across four interconnected levels of intervention—individual, family, community, and institutional—to capture the multidimensional nature of determinants influencing refugee children’s mental health. These levels provided a structured basis for analysis but are inherently interconnected, consistent with Bronfenbrenner’s ecological model [31], which emphasizes the dynamic interdependence between individual, relational, and structural contexts. Non-modifiable factors were recorded to contextualize findings but excluded from the analysis. Two reviewers independently applied these criteria during data extraction, resolving discrepancies through team discussion until consensus was reached. Eligibility criteria

The review question was developed using the JBI mnemonic—Population, Concept, and Context—to guide the inclusion process [25, 26]. Studies were included if they addressed modifiable factors associated with mental health outcomes (concept) in refugee and asylum-seeking children and adolescents (population), with no restrictions on geographical setting (context).

Studies were considered if they met the following criteria: (1) focus on modifiable factors, defined as factors that can be changed or influenced through behavioral, psychosocial, clinical, or policy interventions (see Table 1); (2) include participants with a mean age of 19 years or younger, consistent with the World Health Organization (WHO) definition of children and adolescents [33]; (3) focus on cross-border forced migrants, including refugees and asylum seekers; (4) report original empirical data, from quantitative, qualitative or mixed-methods studies, (5) published between 2011 and January 2025, (6) peer-reviewed, (7) published in English, French, Spanish, or Portuguese.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) focused solely on non-modifiable factors (e.g., pre-migration trauma, gender, ethnicity); (2) did not address child or adolescent mental health as a primary outcome; (3) included non-forcibly displaced or unspecified migrant populations; (4) focused primarily on adult populations (mean age over 19 years); (5) were published before 2011; (6) did not present primary data (e.g., reviews, editorials, commentaries, policy reports, or clinical guidelines).

Secondary studies, such as systematic reviews, were excluded to maintain a focus on primary evidence and avoid reinterpretation of previously synthesized data. This approach ensured a clearer picture of the nature of original research based on modifiable factors.

The evidence was gathered from 2011 considering major socioeconomic (the Global Financial Crisis [34]) and geopolitical events (the onset of the Syrian war [35]) that significantly influenced global migration patterns and research attention on refugee populations [2, 36].

For consistency, the term “refugee” is used throughout the review to refer to the population, unless a study focuses on a specific group (e.g., asylum seekers or unaccompanied minors). Mental health was conceptualized according to the broad and positivistic WHO framework, encompassing both mental disorders and other psychological conditions associated with distress or impaired functioning and well-being [12].

Search strategy

We conducted an initial systematic search in PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science between November 2021 and January 2022, covering studies published from January 2011 to January 2022. A second, updated search was performed in July 2025 to identify additional studies published between February 2022 and January 2025. In both rounds, we also screened the reference lists of all included articles to identify additional eligible studies.

The choice of databases was guided by the scope of the review, focused on refugee children’s mental health, a subject at the intersection of psychiatry, psychology, and public health. PubMed provides extensive biomedical and public health coverage, PsycINFO captures psychological and developmental literature, and Web of Science offers broad multidisciplinary and social science indexing. Preliminary scoping demonstrated that these three databases were adequate to capture the breadth of relevant evidence while maintaining a manageable screening volume. This approach is consistent with the methodological guidance of JBI and the PRISMA-ScR framework, which recommend balancing comprehensiveness with feasibility. The complete search strings, filters, and date ranges for each database are presented in Appendix 1.

Study selection

Selected studies were uploaded into Zotero software for database organization and duplicate article removal. The selection process consisted of title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening, both performed independently by two reviewers. After reading all potentially relevant articles, only studies that met the inclusion criteria were selected. Reasons for excluding articles during full-text screening were recorded. Disagreements between reviewers regarding study eligibility were resolved through discussion and consensus. Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data from the included studies using an Excel spreadsheet. Data extraction collected information on author(s), year of publication, country, purpose of the study, conceptual framing, population and context characteristics, methods, modifiable factors identified, and main findings. The extraction form is provided in Appendix 2.

Results

Overview of the studies

Of the 2970 records retrieved, 2450 remained after removing duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 146 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, with 47 meeting the inclusion criteria. An additional six articles were identified through citation searches, of which two were eligible, resulting in a total of 49 studies included in the review. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

The studies included were published between January 2011 and January 2025. The children represented a wide range of geographic origins and resettlement contexts, with the highest representation from Middle Eastern countries (e.g., Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan), followed by Europe (e.g., Germany, Norway, United Kingdom), Australia and North America (Canada and United States of America). A smaller number of studies were conducted in Asia and Africa, primarily on neighboring host countries including Ethiopia, Libya, South Korea, Uganda, Pakistan, India, and Thailand, mostly in camp or settlement settings.

Thirty-six studies (n = 36) focused on accompanied refugee children, while thirteen (n = 13) specifically addressed unaccompanied refugee minors (URM), highlighting distinct vulnerabilities of this population. Most studies included first-generation refugee children; only two studies (n = 2) also included children of refugees (second generation). Study designs and methodologies

Most studies (n = 42) employed quantitative methodologies, predominantly cross-sectional designs (n = 32), followed by a smaller number of short-term prospective studies (n = 9) and one retrospective study (n = 1). The remaining studies used qualitative (n = 6) or mixed-methods (n = 1) approaches, mainly involving interviews or focus groups. Among these, two explored the perspectives of refugee mothers, three focused on refugee adolescents, and two incorporated multiple sources, including health and humanitarian providers or direct field observations. Mental health outcomes were most frequently assessed through validated symptom scales for depression, anxiety, PTSD, and internalizing or externalizing behaviors. In contrast, qualitative studies adopted a broader psychosocial framing, emphasizing well-being, resilience, and adaptation within migratory experiences. Collectively, the studies conceptualized refugee children’s mental health across a continuum—from clinical psychiatric disorders to resilience and psychological adjustment—reflecting theoretical and methodological heterogeneity.

Conceptualization of mental health across studies

Conceptualizations of refugee children’s mental health varied across the included studies, reflecting diverse theoretical framings and methodological approaches. Most quantitative studies adopted a clinical perspective, assessing the presence or severity of mental disorders or symptoms such as post-traumatic stress, depression, or anxiety. These studies commonly used standardized psychometric instruments (e.g. the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire—SDQ, the Child PTSD Symptom Scale—CPSS, or the Hopkins Symptom Checklist—HSCL), consistent with a biomedical orientation. However, many of these studies incorporated an ecological or social determinants model to examine how contextual variables—such as family dynamics, acculturative stress, discrimination, or socioeconomic conditions—influence mental health. These determinants were often captured through structured questionnaires or scales assessing daily life stress, trauma exposure, social adjustment, or acculturation.

Qualitative and mixed-methods studies applied a more interpretative lens, exploring subjective experiences of distress, adaptation, and resilience within specific sociocultural and migration contexts.

This conceptual diversity highlights both the complexity of refugee children’s experiences and the multifactorial nature of mental health. It also presents challenges for interpretation and comparison, given the heterogeneity in conceptual definitions, designs and outcome measures used across studies. Tables 3 and 4 together illustrate how refugee children’s mental health has been conceptualized and measured, situating each study within its contextual and methodological framework.

Levels of intervention

Most studies examined both modifiable and non-modifiable factors, the latter commonly including age, gender, and pre-migration experiences such as exposure to war and trauma. Notably, few studies explicitly differentiated between modifiable and non-modifiable factors, limiting their applicability for preventive intervention design.

To address this gap, the current review followed the Social Ecological Model [17, 31] to organize findings across four interdependent levels of influence:Individual level: child’s psychological and behavioral traits, that can be addressed through individualized interventions.Family level: parental and household factors, typically targeted by family-based interventions.Community-level: broader social environments, including schools, neighborhoods and peers, often targeted through community-based interventions [82];Policy/institutional: structural determinants influenced by laws, public policies, and institutions, including those influencing housing, asylum, and access to services.

Modifiable factors associated with mental health outcomes

The following section summarizes the main findings across these interconnected intervention levels, highlighting the most frequently examined modifiable factors and their associations with refugee children’s mental health.

Individual level

Psychological factors

Post-migration daily routines, sleep quality and daily stress predicted sadness, anger regulation difficulties [45], and depressive symptoms [62] among children both in settlements and resettled in host communities. Poor general health also predicted poor mental health outcomes in Syrian refugees living in settlements [60]. In reverse, optimism and good self-esteem predicted low mental health symptoms [60]. Limited proficiency in the host country’s language was also consistently associated with higher levels of psychological distress [55, 70].

Some psychological traits associated with mental health outcomes were also reported. Children personal strengths and difficulties [62], temperamental trait of environmental sensitivity [60], and individual coping strategies [60, 64] appear to be significant predictors of depressive symptoms. In Ukrainian refugee children resettled in Poland, emotional coping, and bereavement were associated with higher levels of depression, whereas emotional coping and diminished problem-solving capabilities were associated with higher levels of anxiety [64]. In other study, avoidant coping and paradoxically also support-seeking and problem-solving strategies predicted high mental health symptoms in Syrian refugees living in settlements with their caregivers [60].

Among qualitative studies, Syrian adolescents living in host communities refer the “sense of hope about their new lives,” self-efficacy and self-esteem [83] as protective; whereas feelings of “hopelessness,” “lack of autonomy,” and negative coping strategies, as aggression or self-harm, avoidance, and social withdrawal are associated with mental health problems [79, 81, 84]. Conversely, acceptance, social coping, and belief in religion have been identified as protective factors in two different studies with URM [81, 84].

Family level

Parental mental health

The mental health of parents/caregivers is one of the most well-studied factors in literature. Caregivers’ distress [40, 52, 59, 61, 85], especially parental PTSD [39, 54, 61, 85], parenting-related stress [39], parental acculturative stress [49], and family history of psychiatric disorders [41, 46, 48, 49, 54, 57, 60, 62, 65, 66], have been associated with different children’s emotional and behavioral symptoms in multiple studies, thorough different settings. Conversely, parental resilience is associated to better children’s resilience and better mental health outcomes [41, 64]. Qualitative studies in different settings also mentioned parents’ or other caregivers’ mental health, depression, or anger, as important factors affecting refugee children [78, 80].

Family dynamics

Family discord [43], domestic violence, poor quality of parenting and life at home [43, 59, 62], abuse or maltreatment [43, 60], insecure attachment [47], and parenting styles, such as lack of emotional warmth, rejection, overprotection by both parents [47], or maternal psychological control [60], have been associated with behavioral, emotional, and learning disorders, in multiple studies across different contexts. Conversely, family cohesion have positive effects on depressive symptoms of adolescents living in camps [62]. Family routines, such as dinnertime and bedtime, have been linked as strong predictors of anger regulation in children resettled with their families in host countries [45].

Parental support and frequent contact with family abroad were associated with fewer mental health issues for both accompanied and URM [63, 70, 74, 75]. Despite the distance, URM living in group homes felt more support from their family abroad, than from peers or mentors in Germany [75].

Qualitative studies, both with refugees resettled in host communities and living in camps, have also mentioned family dynamics (e.g. harsh parenting style, parentification) [80], parental alcohol consumption, exposure to domestic violence, neglect and abuse [77]. Children reported “sadness, worry, fear, and isolation”, externalizing behaviors, and alcohol use [77].

Father’s lower level of education and unemployment status are associated with an increased risk of emotional problems and overall psychiatric disorders among children [42, 66]. Mother’s employment status (part-time, unemployed, or on leave) was also associated with increased emotional and behavioral problems in preschool-aged children [49]. Family financial stability appears to play a modifying role in parent − child resilience and depression associations [41].

Maternal literacy appeared to moderates the strength of caregiver–child mental health associations, suggesting that caregiver’s mental health had a stronger predictive impact on child mental health in households where the mother was literate [59].

Community level

Social and community support

Community coherence and connectedness were associated with fewer internalizing and externalizing problems [38, 40, 75] and PTSD symptoms (particularly among females) [38]. Lower levels of “social trust”—defined as “an individual’s belief that people generally treat others fairly rather than seeking to maximize self-interest at others’expense” [86]—appear to predict poor mental health among Syrian adolescents and potentially mediate the effects of gender and age, as females and older adolescents tend to present lower levels of social trust [44].

Perceived social support from peers, school, and the wider community directly predicts children’s mental health outcomes [44, 60, 69, 74], with bullying, feelings of loneliness, social isolation, and social stressors associated with higher mental health symptoms in accompanied children already resettled in host communities [45, 60, 62].

Regarding URM, mental health difficulties were predicted by lower levels of social support, fewer resources in the host country [55], and social hardships (lack of friends, daily worries about family and friends) [68]. Lower support from mentors appear to increase the risk of PTSD, depression, and anxiety, whereas lower support from peers increased the association between stressful life events and anxiety symptoms [75]. Additionally, regular school attendance [70] and positive feelings toward school predicted fewer mental health problems for refugees, URM, and children of refugees [63].

Findings from qualitative studies also refer to community factors. Refugee mothers mentioned the impact of “hostility and bullying from peers,” difficulties in integrating into school and other recreational activities, a lack of opportunities to create social networks and challenges in “making friends and meaningful relationships in the new country” [78, 80]. Similarly, URM referred to the “lack of trusting relationships in the new country” and social isolation, as major factors affecting their mental health [81]. “Social support from friends and school,” “having same-language social relations during the first years” [83], and participation in the community, including religious practices, sports, or other hobbies [84], have been found to be protective to mental health outcomes.

Transcultural and acculturation-related factors

Several studies have identified factors specifically related to transcultural challenges. The term “acculturative stress” refers to a range of experiences arising from adapting to a new cultural environment. These stressors can be broadly categorized into three domains: challenges related to ethnic identity, discrimination, and interactions with host country and co-ethnic (heritage) culture (often referred to as out-group and in-group hassles) [73]. These experiences interact with individual acculturation styles, thus predicting PTSD, depression symptoms, and trajectories of depression, beyond trauma history [38, 73].

Two studies analyzed large URM samples in Norway [73, 74] focusing on acculturation. In both studies, heritage culture competence had a significant impact on depressive symptoms, reducing levels of perceived discrimination [73, 74]. While host country competence was also associated with depressive symptoms, it did not affect the trajectories of depression during the follow-up [73].

Additionally, acculturation style of “competition” was also significantly associated with internalizing and externalizing problems in girls, but not in boys [38]. In contrast, the “marginalized” style impacts the severity of PTSD symptoms, without evidence of gender-specific effects [50].

Experiences of discrimination, prejudice, and stigma—such as “being subjected to derogatory remarks about one’s cultural background” or “feeling treated differently from the native population”—were also linked to poor mental health outcomes across various settings [68, 72, 74, 76].

These findings were echoed in qualitative studies, in which refugee mothers, relocated both in Middle-eastern countries and in Europe, emphasized the impact of perceived discrimination, hostility from locals, language, and cultural barriers on their children’s ability to build new relationships [78, 80, 83]. Similarly, URM reported perceptions of discrimination, feeling “labeled as asylum seeker” [81].

Institutional/policy level

Asylum regulations

Numerous studies have examined factors directly related to the asylum process. Low levels of support and negative asylum outcomes were associated with greater psychological distress in URM [71]. Rejected asylum seekers reported significantly higher depressive symptoms than those granted asylum [56]. Prolonged visa insecurity was also associated with poorer child mental health, increasing also post-migration family stressors and exacerbating maternal PTSD [61]. Feelings such as low levels of satisfaction with life post-resettlement, “chronic stress,” “fear of deportation,” “insecurity, instability, and uncertainty,” predicted conduct problems, mental distress, and depressive symptoms in children from different ages and host settings [42, 70, 79, 81, 84].

Longer transit times have been linked to increased referrals to mental health services [51]. Similarly, longer periods in refugee camps [57] and transitory displacement, irrespective of duration, were associated with higher levels of depression, avoidance/numbing, and total trauma symptoms [48]. Mandatory detention in Australia was associated with higher prevalence of social, emotional, and behavioral problems [87], and more referrals to mental health services [51].

In contrast, in a Dutch study with refugee children living in reception centers with their family, none of the variables related to asylum procedures (length of stay, number of relocations, legal status) were significantly associated with mental health difficulties, according with parent, teachers, and child self-assessments [65].

URM living in foster care presented lower levels of internalizing symptoms and depression than those living alone, likely due to the increased social support available [67]. Similarly, URM placed in reception centers for adults experienced higher psychological distress than those placed in centers specifically for youth [71].

Finally, in a qualitative study, healthcare and humanitarian aid providers working in reception camps, pointed to distal determinants, such as organizational failures at state level and national and international policies [79].

Family reunification policies

Several studies have found that being separated from nuclear family or fathers, living solely with mothers or in smaller family nucleus are associated with emotional symptoms, PTSD, and social problems, both in refugees living in camps and those resettled in host countries [51, 65, 88]. The delay of family reunification procedures significantly predicted higher levels of depressive symptoms in URM in long-stay facilities in Greece [69]. Conversely, living with biological parents (in comparison to other caregivers) was significantly associated with a reduction in anxiety symptoms in adolescents [52].

Access to services and social rights

Adolescent and parents’ perception of easy access to health, educational, and general services in the host country were found to be associated with less internalizing and externalizing problems [38, 40], as well as fewer PTSD symptoms.

Constraints in access to education, including disrupted prior schooling, lack of access to education in refugee camps or detention centers, and education in children’s non-primary languages, were found to be associated with language developmental delays and increased referrals due to neurodevelopmental concerns in school-age children [51].

One study conducted with adolescents in two camps in Uganda found a significant association between child labor and depressive symptoms [53]. In the same refugee camps, teenager pregnancy, as well as sexual and physical abuse, were independently associated with depression, suicidal ideation, and self-harm among adolescent girls [58].

Qualitative studies have also mentioned child labor [77, 80], as well as other challenging living conditions in the host country, such as “poverty,” “housing difficulties,” “barriers accessing to care,” and “challenges with the education system” [80, 83]. Furthermore, refugees residing in refugee camps have identified living conditions in these settlements, including difficulties accessing education, social isolation, and insecurity [77].

Discussion

This scoping review gives a contribution to synthesizing evidence on modifiable factors influencing the mental health of refugee children. Our analysis demonstrates that many risk and protective factors are amenable to change through interventions across individual, family, community, and policy levels. Importantly, these factors do not operate in isolation: they interact to shape both vulnerability and resilience.

While our findings are consistent with prior socio-ecological and bioecological reviews [5, 6, 8–10], this review makes a distinct contribution by moving beyond descriptive mapping to provide a prevention-oriented, public health framework, focusing on modifiable determinants and the interconnectedness between levels. This shift in focus—from understanding etiology toward informing prevention—is essential for translating research into practical strategies that can reduce the burden of mental illness among refugee children.

Intervention levels and preventive strategies

A key objective of this review is the organization of modifiable factors into a “menu” of potential intervention targets. Because these factors are interdependent, effective prevention requires coordinated, multilevel strategies rather than single-level approaches. Below, we present key modifiable factors by level, with examples of feasible, evidence-informed preventive approaches.

Individual level

At the individual level, psychological traits and behaviors—such as chronic stress, poor sleep, limited host-language proficiency, and maladaptive coping—were consistently associated with distress [42, 44, 45, 81, 84]. Conversely, optimism, self-efficacy, and good general health predicted better outcomes [60, 83]. Culturally adapted psychoeducation, stress-management, emotion-regulation, and problem-solving skills training, delivered through schools or primary care settings, as well as educational interventions to promote language competence have shown feasibility and acceptability in different refugee populations [21, 23, 89–92]. Regular mental health screening using culturally validated tools can support early identification and referral [9, 19, 93]. Evidence also suggests that transdiagnostic interventions, which target shared psychological mechanisms, may be more appropriate than disorder‑specific interventions [19, 94, 95].

Family level

Family factors, including parental mental health, parenting practices, family dynamics, and routines, are among the strongest predictors of child outcomes [29, 47, 52, 59, 60, 62]. Parental distress, harsh or inconsistent parenting, and parental control were linked to emotional and behavioral problems, whereas positive parent–child relationships and family cohesion were protective [59, 62, 63, 75]. In this level, evidence supports parent-skills training, culturally tailored psychoeducation [96–99], and family-engagement programs [96, 99, 100]. Interventions that support predictable family routines (e.g., mealtimes and bedtimes) and enhance parent–child communication, may buffer the effects of post‑migration stressors [29, 45, 101].

Additionally, mental health assessments and screenings for parents/caregivers, vocational training, and employment support may indirectly reduce children’s distress by improving parents’ mental health, alleviating economic strain and promoting family stability [19, 21, 95, 96, 100]. Importantly, family-based programs should be culturally responsive and participatory, involving peer facilitators from similar backgrounds to strengthen trust and relevance [96, 99].

Community level

At the community level, social support, school integration, and transcultural competence emerged as key modifiable factors [38, 44, 63, 69, 74]. Social isolation and discrimination were consistently associated with poorer outcomes, while peer support and community connectedness predicted resilience [38, 43, 44, 73, 74]. Schools represent critical places for the implementation of preventive actions targeting children [19, 23, 92], not only for psychoeducation but also for fostering belonging and intercultural competence and promoting social connections outside the family [9, 21, 63, 100]. School-based interventions that combine anti‑discrimination policies, peer relationship promotion, social–emotional learning curricula, and teacher cross‑cultural training have shown promising results in improving mental health and emotional well-being [21, 23, 91, 92, 100, 102].

In addition, the engagement of broader community structures—such as sports clubs, arts groups, and religious organizations—provides opportunities for children to build social networks and cultural identity [21, 103–106]. Empowering community structures and training key-community professionals, such as those who interact with children, promotes inclusive environments and helps mitigate barriers in accessing services [89, 107–109]. Future research should focus on effective preventive interventions targeting these and other members of the host community, as receiving societies play a pivotal role in the adjustment and integration of refugee children. Addressing community‑level discrimination through public education and cultural dialog campaigns is equally critical [110, 111].

Institutional/policy level

Finally, factors including asylum regulations, family reunification policies, access to services, and socioeconomic conditions constitute some of the most powerful modifiable determinants of refugee’s children mental health [56, 67, 68, 71, 87, 88]. Policy‑induced insecurity, such as prolonged visa uncertainty or detention, exacerbates stress and undermines stability, affecting the feasibility of interventions at other levels [61, 67, 69, 73]. Conversely, rapid and transparent asylum processes, prompt family reunification, provision of age appropriate placements for URM, and universal access to education and healthcare can substantially improve outcomes [61, 110, 112–114]. Provision of cultural mediators and language-translation services further reduces barriers to care [108, 109, 111, 112].

Although evidence remains limited, studies suggest that relocating families from deprived to more economic diverse neighborhoods can improve adolescents’ mental health and reduce risky behaviors [115, 116]. Addressing these determinants demands intersectoral coordination and sustained political commitment but could offer high population-level impact.

Interdependence and coordination across levels

The modifiable factors identified are deeply interconnected [16, 17]. For instance, visa insecurity (institutional) intensifies parental stress (family), disrupts children’s sleep (individual), and affects school attendance and peer relationships (community). Consequently, preventive interventions should deliberately coordinate across levels, for example, combining parent-support groups, school staff cultural-sensitivity training, and child peer-mentoring alongside policies that ensure housing stability for families.

Equity, ethics, and participatory co-design

Developing interventions with refugee communities, not merely for them, is both an ethical obligation and a mandatory requisite for effectiveness [117–119]. Community‑based participatory research (CBPR) involves refugees and host communities throughout the research and intervention process—from problem identification to implementation and evaluation [117, 120]. Participatory co-design ensures that interventions are culturally resonant, address priorities defined by the community itself, and are adjusted to the specifications of local contexts [117, 121]. Successful CBPR projects with refugee communities demonstrate the importance of this approach in building trust and promoting shared decision‑making [82, 94, 103, 104, 120].

The best ethical practices also include safeguarding confidentiality, preventing re-traumatization, fair compensation for participants, and paying special attention to specific vulnerable populations, such as unaccompanied minors and marginalized subgroups [118, 119, 122].

Implications of this review

By systematically identifying modifiable determinants and organizing them within a preventive, multi-level framework, this review provides a practical tool for clinicians, policymakers, and researchers. It advances prior socio-ecological reviews by moving from descriptive synthesis of factors to practical oriented action—highlighting where and how change can be achieved.

The review also underscores gaps in current interventions. Existing interventions for refugee mental health have prioritized treatment over prevention and focused on single mental health diagnosis (especially PTSD) [19, 94, 95]. Conversely, none of the modifiable factors identified were diagnosis-specific, reinforcing the value of transdiagnostic, holistic approaches [16, 19, 94, 95]. Moreover, few interventions account for the interconnected and multilayered nature of the risk factors. Interventions often address isolated aspects of a child’s life, focusing on a single level (e.g. school or family)—rather than the dynamic interaction across individual, family, community, and policy levels [16, 21]. Future research should develop integrated models that mirror the complexity of refugees lived experiences, consistent with WHO recommendations for a “multidimensional” and “holistic framework” [20, 22, 123]. Finally, a critical gap in the current evidence base, which our review also reflects, is the lack of engagement with the lived experiences of refugee communities. Integrating community perspectives throughout design and implementation will be essential to ensure acceptability, equity, and sustainability [117, 118].

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review has several strengths, including a systematic and comprehensive search strategy, its novel focus on modifiable factors and the application of a socio-ecological framework explicitly oriented toward prevention.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged, both in relation to the conduct of the review and the methodological characteristics of the included studies. The evidence base is dominated by cross-sectional designs, which are useful for identifying associations but limit the ability to establish temporal sequencing or infer causal relationships between modifiable factors and mental health outcomes. Among the fewer longitudinal studies available, follow-up periods were often short, constraining insights into longer-term outcomes. Additionally, most studies relied on self- or parent-reported measures, increasing susceptibility to recall and social desirability bias. While most studies used standardized diagnostic interviews or adapted instruments, reporting of psychometric properties and cultural adaptation procedures was inconsistent, which complicated the assessment of reliability and validity across diverse contexts. Considerable heterogeneity in study design, sample characteristics, cultural and resettlement settings further limit the direct comparability of findings. Moreover, literature predominantly focused on older children and adolescents, with comparatively little evidence on younger children, despite the critical importance of early years in shaping psychological health [18].

An additional limitation concerns the challenges inherent in conducting and synthesizing mental health research in transcultural contexts. Psychiatric constructs and diagnostic categories are culturally embedded, and instruments developed in Western settings may not be fully conceptually or psychometrically equivalent when applied across diverse refugee populations [124, 125]. Even when translated or culturally adapted, standardized measures may not adequately capture culturally patterned expressions of distress, increasing the risk of construct non-equivalence and over- or under-reporting of symptoms [126, 127]. Furthermore, symptom-focused approaches in humanitarian settings may overlook locally salient meanings of suffering, resilience, and adaptation, and be inadequate to reflect the dynamic interaction between daily stressors, social environments, and mental health outcomes [128]. These challenges are particularly relevant given that most studies included in this review relied on cross-sectional designs, which can identify statistical associations but cannot establish predictive or causal relationships over time. Future research would benefit from culturally grounded, longitudinal, and mixed methods approaches that explicitly address transcultural validity.

Additional limitations related to the review process itself should also be acknowledged. First, the review’s focus on identifying objectively defined modifiable factors may have constrained the integration of more exploratory insights from qualitative studies. Second, organizing factors by ecological level necessarily simplifies complex interactions, although it provides a pragmatic framework for identifying potential points for action. Third, although our search strategy covered three major databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science), we did not include other sources (such as Scopus, Embase, CINAHL, or Sociological Abstracts). This decision was based on feasibility considerations and evidence that these databases largely cover the most pertinent content. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that some relevant studies in education or sociological fields may not have been retrieved. Gray literature was also excluded due to time and resource constraints and the difficulty of ensuring a representative and methodologically robust sample of non–peer-reviewed sources. While this exclusion may have introduced publication bias, focusing exclusively on peer-reviewed studies reduced heterogeneity and enhanced methodological rigor. Finally, limiting the review to studies published from 2011 onward may have excluded earlier evidence, potentially introducing situational bias. Nevertheless, previous reviews conducted before 2011 identified similar factors, suggesting that this restriction is unlikely to have affected the conclusions [5, 6, 22].

Conclusions

Refugee children’s mental health is shaped by a complex interplay of factors operating across individual, family, community, and policy levels. Many of these factors are potentially modifiable, offering opportunities for preventive action. This review provides a prevention-oriented framework to guide action, emphasizing that the most effective strategies will be those that are integrated, multi-level, and co-designed with refugee communities.

Future research should prioritize the development of culturally adaptable, transdiagnostic approaches grounded in evidence, to promote long-term well-being of refugee children. By moving from a paradigm of treatment to one of holistic prevention and health promotion, we can more effectively support the resilience and long-term well-being of refugee children, enabling them to thrive in their new lives.
