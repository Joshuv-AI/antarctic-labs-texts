Impact of systemic steroids on the efficacy of first line imatinib treatment of patients with advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs)
Authors: Sejin Kim, Hyung-Don Kim, Eo Jin Kim, Min-Hee Ryu, Yoon-Koo Kang
Published: 2024

Background

Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) is the most common mesenchymal tumor of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. Mutations in KIT and PDGFRA are the most common genetic changes associated with GIST [2–4]. Imatinib, a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor that blocks the signaling pathways of KIT and PDGFRA, is the robust first-line treatment for GIST [5–7]. While imatinib is generally well-tolerated, the development of adverse events may pose a challenge to effective imatinib treatment for GIST patients. Effective management of adverse events to maintain an adequate dose intensity of imatinib to ensure its anti-tumor efficacy is one of the key therapeutic aspects.

Skin rash is one of the most common adverse events of imatinib, with a prevalence reportedly ranging from 11 to 67% [8, 9]. While mild skin rash caused by imatinib can be managed with topical corticosteroids and anti-histamines without any need to interrupt imatinib treatment, severe skin rash (grade ≥ 3), which occurs in up to 10% of patients can adversely affect the compliance of imatinib and frequently requires imatinib interruption or dose reduction [8, 10, 11]. In a previous Phase II study, we demonstrated that imatinib-associated severe skin rash (i.e., grade ≥ 3 skin rash or grade 2 skin rash with grade 2 pruritis) can be effectively controlled by systemic steroid treatment without interruption or dose reduction of imatinib in patients with GIST [12]. In that study, 22 out of 29 patients (75.8%) achieved successful treatment of their skin rash without the need for dose interruption or modification.

However, the impact of systemic steroid use on the efficacy of imatinib in treating GIST remains to be studied. In addition to the direct anti-tumor effect of imatinib through inhibition of the KIT and PDGFRA signaling pathways, imatinib has been shown to have positive immunomodulatory effects. In a mouse model of GIST, one study found that imatinib had indirect effects on the immune system, activating CD8 + T cells and inducing regulatory T cell apoptosis within the tumor [13]. Additionally, another study showed that imatinib induced the production of interferon gamma by natural killer cells, which correlated with its anti-tumor efficacy [14, 15]. Considering that systemic steroids may inhibit T cell immunity [16] and suppress NK cell activity [17–19], systemic steroids might affect the immunomodulatory effects of imatinib, thereby decreasing its efficacy.

Therefore, in this retrospective study, we aimed to investigate the impact of the use of systemic steroids on the efficacy of first line imatinib treatment in patients with advanced GIST.

Patients and methods

Study population

The study patients were identified from a prospective registry of GIST patients at Asan Medical Center (Seoul, Republic of Korea). A total of 427 consecutive patients who started palliative first-line imatinib for metastatic or recurrent GIST between October 2014 (the time-point from which administration of systemic steroids without imatinib dose reduction became the instititional policy for the management of skin rash) and February 2022. For patients who were transferred from another hospital after starting imatinib, only those who were transferred within 3 months of the initiation of imatinib were included in this study (n = 107). Other patients were also excluded from the analysis for these reasons: patients with PDGFRA D842V mutations (n = 5), localized but unresectable cases (n = 23), R1 resection (n = 27), concurrent other cancers (n = 36), and those who were treated with systemic steroids for imatinib-induced pneumonitis (n = 2), because it was our policy not to re-introduce imatinib to patients who develop pneumonitis as it can be fatal. Finally, 277 patients were included as the study population.

Imatinib treatment

Imatinib was administered in accordance with the NCCN [20], ESMO [7], or Asian consensus [6] guidelines. All patients diagnosed with metastatic or recurrent GIST initiated palliative first-line treatment with imatinib, beginning with an initial daily dose of 400 mg. Patients visited the clinic one month after starting imatinib, and after that, they attended every 2 to 3 months for regular follow-up. History taking and laboratory tests were conducted during each visit to monitor for adverse events, and CT scans were performed every 3 months to evaluate their disease status. Treatment continued until disease progression or the development of intolerable toxicities. Several strategies were implemented to manage the adverse events. For example, patients with significant anemia (Hb < 10 g/dL) but without iron or vitamin B12 deficiency received erythropoietin. Patients with generalized edema were advised to decrease their salt intake and were administered diuretics. The imatinib dose was carefully modified if the adverse event continued despite proper management with dose modification of imatinib guided by imatinib plasma level monitoring. Close observation prevented unnecessary interruptions of imatinib treatment.

Skin rash and use of systemic steroids

Patients with a skin rash of grade ≥ 3 or grade 2 with grade 2 pruritis were administered systemic steroids and were classified as the steroid group. Patients who neither developed a skin rash nor required steroids for a mild skin rash were classified as the control group. The steroid group was further classified into treatment success and failure subgroups based on whether they required imatinib dose modification or interruption. The definition of treatment success for skin rash was based on maintaining imatinib treatment without dose modification for at least 15 weeks while using steroids, as previously described [12]. In principle, systemic steroids were administered based on a schedule: prednisolone 30 mg/day for 3 weeks, tapered off at 3-week intervals over 12 weeks.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for the baseline characteristics of the patients. Treatment response was evaluated for patients with at least one measurable lesion based on modified RECIST v1.1. Progression free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the date of initial imatinib administration to the date of disease progression or death from any cause, whichever came first. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of initial imatinib administration to the date of death from any cause. Survival outcomes were compared between the steroid group and control group. The cut-off date for data analysis was 31st July 2022. The survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log-rank test was used to compare survival outcomes between two groups, and the effect size was analyzed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. All variables from univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.6.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 277 patients, 30 patients (10.8%) were treated with systemic steroids for skin rash, while the control group consisted of 247 patients (89.2%) (Table 1). Patients in the steroid group were older, with a median age of 62.5 years (range, 46.3–79.0) and 57.1 years (range, 23.2–87.0) in the steroid and control groups, respectively. The primary site of the tumor, largest diameter of the tumor, and the tumor mutation type were comparable between the two groups. The median duration of imatinib treatment before systemic steroids treatment was 77 days (range, 11–733 days).

Impact of systemic steroids on the efficacy of imatinib

There was no significant difference in the overall response rate (44.4 vs. 58.5%, p = 0.246) or disease control rate (100 vs. 97.9%, p = 0.999) between the steroid and control groups, respectively (Table 2). With a median follow-up duration of 35.0 months (range, 1.8–93.8), PFS and OS were comparable between the two groups (median PFS, 49.6 vs. 66.4 months, p = 0.53; median OS, not reached vs. not reached, p = 0.67, respectively). The 3-year PFS was 67.7% in the steroid group versus 65.1% in the control group (p = 0.53). The 3-year OS was 91% in the steroid group versus 89.9% in the control group (p = 0.67) (Fig. 1).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of PFS and OS

Efficacy of imatinib dose maintenance in the steroid group

Among patients in the steroid group (N = 30), 6 patients (20%) were classified as the treatment failure group. In this group, 3 patients had a dose reduction of imatinib when starting steroid treatment, and two patients temporarily stopped taking imatinib for a week and then resumed it with a reduced dose while concurrently using steroids. One patient discontinued imatinib due to imatinib-induced azotemia during steroid tapering for skin rash.

Regarding the steroid dosage, 23 patients (66.7%) began with the planned protocol (starting from prednisolone 30 mg/day). Four patients in the treatment success group and 3 patients in the treatment failure group were given a lower dosage of steroids at the physicians’ discretion. While on steroid tapering, 7 patients were treated for a longer duration than initially planned.

We compared the clinical course with imatinib treatment between the treatment success group and the treatment failure group. For survival outcomes, the treatment success group showed a trend of higher rates of 3-year PFS and OS (3-year PFS, 73.3% vs. 44.4%, p = 0.34; 3-year OS, 95.8% vs. 75.0%, p = 0.15) (Supplementary Fig. 1). The proportion of KIT exon 9 mutation, which was an independent variable for PFS and OS, was not significantly different between the two groups (8.3% vs. 16.7%, p = 0.29). There was a trend toward a higher proportion of patients starting with a dose of prednisolone 30 mg/day in the treatment success group compared with the treatment failure group (83.3% vs. 50.0%, p = 0.12).

Grade 1–2 hyperglycemia was the most common (20%) adverse event with systemic steroids, but all patients fully recovered within 3 months of steroid discontinuation. There were no cases of opportunistic infections in the study population.

Discussion

In this registry-based retrospective analysis, we aimed to investigate whether the use of systemic steroids for severe skin rash affected the efficacy of imatinib for treating advanced GIST patients. The efficacy outcomes were not different according to the use of systemic steroids. Among patients treated with systemic steroids, those who maintained the imatinib dosage showed a trend for favorable survival outcomes. The results of the current study provide practical insights into the use of steroids for alleviating skin rash and the maintenance of the imatinib dose for the treatment of advanced GIST. To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the effect of systemic steroids on imatinib treatment efficacy for GIST patients.

Imatinib is generally well-tolerated, and adverse events are usually manageable, but the adverse events sometimes lead to interruption or dose modification of imatinib. The importance of maintaining the imatinib dose intensity has been emphasized, leading to discussions about strategies to continue its administration even in the presence of adverse events [21]. For severe skin rash, steroid treatment combined with imatinib did not have a significant impact on overall efficacy, suggesting that it is possible to continue imatinib treatment while using systemic steroids. Moreover, successful maintenance of imatinib throughout steroid therapy was associated with favorable treatment outcomes, supporting the clinical relevance of plasma imatinib concentration. This finding reaffirms the importance of maintaining the imatinib dose intensity in line with previous research [22–24].

On the other hand, there were cases where permanent discontinuation of imatinib is recommended despite the administration of systemic steroids. A representative example is imatinib-induced pneumonitis. In our registry, two patients experienced imatinib-induced pneumonitis, which was treated with systemic steroids. Imatinib was permanently discontinued in these patients due to its life-threatening nature. Although it has not been reported in our institution, discontinuation of imatinib is also recommended in imatinib-induced severe hepatitis [21, 25].

For cancer patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), there has been a report indicating that the use of steroids increased the risk of death and disease progression compared to those who did not receive systemic steroids [26]. Also, prednisone over 10 mg/day for over 2 weeks during anti-PD1 therapy may be associated with worse survival outcomes [27]. These results suggest that systemic steroids may counteract the anti-tumor efficacy of ICIs. Given the potential immunomodulatory effects of imatinib [16–19], there are concerns regarding the potential adverse impact of systemic steroids on the immunomodulatory effects of imatinib by decreasing the activation of CD8 + T cells and NK cells. However, the findings from this study indicated that the use of systemic steroids did not negatively affect the efficacy of imatinib.

Imatinib is metabolized by the CYP450 enzymes, with CYP3A4 playing a key role in converting into its active metabolites [28]. Prednisolone, administered in this study, is also metabolized by CYP3A4 [29]. However, its fraction metabolized by CYP3A4 is relatively low, indicating that this pathway plays a minor role in its overall metabolism [30]. Additionally, prednisolone is not considered a potent inducer of CYP3A4 [31], suggesting a relatively low risk of drug-drug interaction (DDI). Together with our results that the use of systemic steroids did not affect the efficacy of imatinib, these suggest that the effect of DDI between the two drugs would be clinically minimal.

Our study has some limitations. Its retrospective nature and the single center-based analysis may limit the interpretation and generalizability of our data. Nevertheless, given the limited reports on the use of systemic steroids in GIST patients, our results could provide practical insights into using systemic steroids along with imatinib for GIST patients. In addition, it should be noted that the dosage and schedule of steroid use were based on the protocol described in our previous phase II study [12]. In most of the study patients, skin rash was effectively controlled with our regimen; however, considering that some patients required a longer duration for steroid tapering, and some patients failed to maintain the imatinib dosage despite the use of systemic steroids, further validation of the steroid dose and schedule may be required.

In conclusion, the use of systemic steroids for the control of imatinib induced severe skin rash while maintaining imatinib treatment did not adversely affect the efficacy of imatinib in patients with advanced GIST.
