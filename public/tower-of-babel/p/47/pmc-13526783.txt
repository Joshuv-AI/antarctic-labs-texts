Dopamine–endocannabinoid crosstalk drives exercise-induced bidirectional plasticity in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Federica Campanelli, Gioia Marino, Giuseppina Natale, Maria De Carluccio, Tabitha N Rodriguez, Michelle Smeyne, Richard Jay Smeyne, Veronica Ghiglieri, Paolo Calabresi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder marked by loss of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons. Epidemiological evidence indicates that lifelong physical activity reduces both PD incidence and slows disease progression. Here, we examine how voluntary exercise induces long-lasting neuroprotection of the corticostriatal function in a murine model of α-synuclein–driven nigrostriatal pathology. In preformed fibril (PFF)-injected mice, long-lasting voluntary wheel running prevents degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic terminals and improves motor performances. Intensive exercise normalizes spontaneous glutamatergic transmission and preserves corticostriatal long-term potentiation (LTP) in striatal spiny projection neurons. Exercise-induced LTP is dependent on activation of dopamine (DA) DA1, GluN2B-expressing NMDA and CB1 endocannabinoid (eCB) receptors. Pharmacological modulation of exercise-induced plasticity shows that, while in physiological conditions eCBs regulate synaptic depotentiation, persistence of LTP in active α-syn mice occurs independently of the eCB system, highlighting a critical interaction between DA and eCBs in long-term synaptic regulation. These findings identify voluntary exercise as a robust neuroprotective intervention with therapeutic relevance for early PD.Subject terms: Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disease that manifests in both motor and non-motor deficits. Although there are a variety of pharmacological interventions for treatment of these symptoms, it is generally acknowledged that none slow the progression of the disease. However, over the past 10 years it has been found that aerobic exercise can improve both motor and cognitive performances in PD1. Clinical trials have also demonstrated the efficacy of motor exercise in improving brain plasticity2. For example, aerobic exercise enhances the functional connectivity between sensorimotor cortex and basal ganglia3. It also improves cognitive activity, and reduces global brain atrophy. Thus, aerobic exercise appears to stabilize disease progression in the corticostriatal sensorimotor network3.

In parallel, clinical interest in the effect of exercise has raised steadily. Early pioneering studies primarily considered exercise as a symptomatic intervention (i.e., tertiary prevention). More recently, however, clinical investigations have proposed exercise as a potentially disease-modifying therapy,

able to both protect against time of onset and slow the progression of the disease (i.e., secondary prevention)1. For these reasons, participation in aerobic exercise programs has become a key component, along with pharmacological inventions, in PD treatment plans. While the phenomenological effects of exercise are well known, the synaptic and molecular mechanisms underlying its efficacy still need to be uncovered.

Data from preclinical studies and human trials indicate that aerobic exercise may facilitate structural and functional brain changes in control subjects as well as in PD patients4,5.

We have already demonstrated that intensive treadmill prevents deficits in striatal plasticity and early motor and cognitive deficits in rats receiving an intrastriatal injection of alpha-synuclein (α-syn) preformed fibrils (PFFs)6. We also have shown that treadmill exercise ameliorates motor performances and visuospatial learning by rescuing corticostriatal long-term potentiation (LTP) and dendritic spine density alterations. These beneficial effects of treadmill exercise on synaptic plasticity require changes in NMDA receptor subunits and an increase in striatal BDNF levels6. In addition to these synaptic mechanisms, recent findings also suggest a link between endocannabinoids (eCBs) and physical exercise7. The eCB system has been demonstrated to be involved in critical brain functions including brain plasticity, learning and memory, inflammation, as well as in the regulation of motor exercise7,8. Other electrophysiological studies utilizing striatal spike-timing-dependent protocols have found that eCB system regulates, in conjunction with dopamine (DA), the thresholds for long-term potentiation (LTP)/depression (LTD) in the striatum9.

Differences between forced and voluntary modes of exercise have recently been addressed in relation to brain monoamines and emotional behavior and it is clear that each form of exercise may provide benefits by different mechanisms10. In terms of PD pathology, voluntary exercise also appears to inhibit microglial activation and associated neuroinflammation induced by microglial ferroptosis in PD experimental murine model11. Based on mechanistic differences between forced and voluntary forms of exercise, there is a clear need to further investigate the synaptic plasticity changes induced by voluntary exercise in physiological conditions as well as in experimental PD models. In the present study we found that voluntary intensive exercise restores bidirectional corticostriatal and structural plasticity preserving neuronal connectivity. The novel mechanisms underlying this beneficial effect involve complex cooperation between DA, glutamate and eCB systems.

Results

Experimental design and exercise paradigm

To investigate the effects of voluntary physical activity on α-syn-PFF–induced pathology, we designed a voluntary wheel running (VWR) protocol that allowed continuous monitoring of locomotor activity while minimizing stress and external constraints. At day 0, α-syn-PFFs were stereotaxically injected into the striatum. Seven days post-injection, mice assigned to the VWR group initiated the exercise paradigm. Animals were individually housed in cages equipped with wireless running wheels, enabling the continuous and unbiased recording of locomotor parameters, including total distance run, cumulative activity, and running speed. Mice had free access to the running wheels five days per week for a total duration of 90 days, allowing voluntary engagement in physical activity without the use of aversive stimuli. PBS sedentary mice were housed in identical cages with locked wheels to control for environmental enrichment.

At 90 days post-injection, following completion of the exercise protocol, animals underwent a battery of behavioral tests. Subsequently, immunohistochemical analyses and electrophysiological recordings were performed to assess the impact of long-term voluntary exercise on PFF-induced neuronal and functional alterations (Fig. 1A).

Motor performances are improved after 90 days of voluntary exercise in α-syn-PFF injected mice

To determine the average amount of running that PFF (n = 14 animals) and PBS (n = 12 animals) mice performed during the night period (16 h), mice were allowed to run undisturbed for 90 days, over which time their running activity was recorded (Fig. 1A, B). In regard to the total distance run, PBS active mice, during the active period, exhibited higher levels of running activity for the first 7 days that slowly tapered off and subsequently stabilized to reach a plateau by day 90. In the PFF active mice, we observed active mice ran approximately 50% of the distance (approximately 4000 m) than PBS active ones (approximately 8000 m) during the 1st week (Fig. 1B; one-way ANOVA: F (3, 48) = 7.268, P = 0.0004), but by the end of the 90 days, their running distance were comparable (Fig. 1B). Based on cumulative counts, in the 1st week, PFF active mice showed a significant reduction in the number of rotations compared to PBS active mice (Fig. 1B; one-way ANOVA: F (3,48) = 6.079, P = 0.0014). In contrast, during the last week, no difference was observed. Regarding average running speed, in the 1st week, PFF active mice covered approximately 4 m/min over a 16-h period, compared to about 5 m/min in the PBS active group (Fig. 1B; one-way ANOVA: F (3,48) = 1.559, P = 0.211).

To assess muscle strength and motor coordination following 90 days of voluntary running in PFF or PBS mice, we measured both grip strength and hanging time. In the grip strength paradigm, we observed that the PFF sedentary group showed significantly lower grip strength values compared with PBS sedentary mice, indicating motor function impairment (Fig. 1C, n = 10 mice/group; two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 27) = 11.73, P < 0.0001). However, the PFF active group exhibited a significant improvement in grip strength compared to their sedentary counterparts to levels comparable to the PBS active mice. In the hanging wire task, no differences in latency to fall were observed in PBS-injected mice from the sedentary or active conditions. However, PFF sedentary mice displayed a significant reduction in the latency to fall compared to PBS-injected mice (Fig. 1D, n = 10 mice/group; two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 27) = 22.35, P < 0.0001), which was rescued in PFF active. This suggests that PFF-injected mice and housed in sedentary conditions, experience a marked impairment in both fine motor coordination and muscular endurance, likely resulting from the progressive neurodegenerative effects induced by PFF pathology, with each measure being rescued by 90 days of voluntary exercise in PFF active mice. These findings suggest that voluntary physical activity may exert a protective effect on motor function impairments associated with α-syn pathology.

To evaluate if exercise altered exploratory behavior and general locomotor activity, PBS and PFF groups (sedentary and active, n = 10 mice/group) were subjected to the open field task. Statistical analysis across all experimental groups revealed no significant differences in total distance traveled (m), average speed (m/s), and time spent in the center (s) over 600 s (Fig. 1E; two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: total distance traveled, F (3, 27) = 0.6227, P = 0.6065; average speed, F (3, 27) = 1.010, P = 0.4035; time spent in the center, F (3, 27) = 4.459, P = 0.0114). However, a more detailed analysis showed that both active groups exhibited a significant increase in immobility time compared to sedentary mice, indicating a possible reduction in spontaneous activity (Fig. 1E; two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 27) = 7.178, P = 0.0011).

Voluntary exercise mitigates nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration and limits α-syn spread to the substantia nigra

Immunofluorescence analyses were carried out to study the nigrostriatal degeneration of the dopaminergic pathway induced by PFF or PBS injections into the striatum. PFF sedentary mice showed a significant decrease of striatal TH+ terminals with respect to the PBS sedentary (Fig. 2A, D, n = 6 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 15) = 31.39, P < 0.0001). Importantly, we found that PFF active mice led to a preservation of striatal TH+ terminals compared to the PFF mice housed in sedentary conditions, preserving levels comparable to those of active PBS-injected mice. Similarly, measurement of DAT+ immunofluorescence revealed that the sedentary condition exacerbated fiber loss in PFF-injected mice compared to the PBS sedentary group. In this case, voluntary exercise exerted a milder protective effect on DAT+ fibers in PFF active animals, reaching the levels observed in PBS active mice (Fig. 2B–E, n = 5 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 12) = 8.304, P = 0.0029). In addition, to evaluate p129-αSyn distribution and its potential relationship to nigrostriatal neurodegeneration in the dorsolateral striatum, additional fluorescent immunolabelling was conducted. We found no significant differences in p129-αSyn levels between sedentary and active PFF-injected mice (Fig. 2C–F, n = 5 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 12) = 11.66, P = 0.0007). Both groups exhibited significantly greater p129-αSyn accumulation than their respective PBS-injected mice.

Although reduced TH and DAT levels do not necessarily indicate terminal loss per se, the parallel and proportional decreases in striatal TH and DAT (~50%) and SNpc dopaminergic neuron loss (~55%) observed in our study, together with α-syn spreading and synaptic plasticity alterations (consistent with previous α-syn-PFF models establishment), strongly support a close relationship between dopaminergic marker reduction and nigrostriatal fiber degeneration or loss.

Our findings suggest that while p129-αSyn accumulation in the dorsolateral striatum was similar between sedentary and active PFF-injected mice, only the sedentary condition was associated with substantial degeneration of nigrostriatal terminals. This implies that voluntary exercise confers neuroprotection independently of p129-αSyn burden in this region.

Based on data obtained from dopaminergic nigrostriatal terminals, we then examined if voluntary exercise altered the transmission of p129-αSyn from the striatum to the SNpc. In PFF sedentary mice we measured an increase of p129-αSyn immunofluorescence with respect to the PBS sedentary condition (Fig. 2G, H, n = 6 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 15) = 8.016, P = 0.0020). In contrast, PFF active mice showed a marked reduction in p129-αSyn accumulation compared to the PFF sedentary group, reaching levels comparable to those of their respective PBS groups.

To evaluate the propagation of α-syn pathology throughout the brain, we analyzed the distribution of p129-αSyn in the dorsomedial striatum and primary motor cortex (M1). Both sedentary and active PFF-injected mice showed a significant increase in p129-αSyn levels compared to their respective PBS group (Supplementary. Fig. 1A, n = 5 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 12) = 82.27, P < 0.0001), with no statistical difference observed between the sedentary and active PFF groups. The O.D. analysis of p129-αSyn in the primary motor cortex (M1) - a region implicated in motor coordination - revealed similar findings: no significant difference between the sedentary and active PFF groups, but both exhibited higher levels compared to their PBS groups (Supplementary. Fig. 1B, n = 6 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 12) = 29.30, P < 0.0001).

We then used stereological analysis of TH+ dopaminergic neurons in the SNpc to determine if striatal PFF injection induced neuron loss and if 90 days of voluntary exercise rescue this phenotype. We found that striatal PFF injections induced a substantial neuronal loss in the sedentary group compared to PBS-injected animals (Fig. 2I–J, n = 6 mice/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 15) = 11.15, P = 0.0004). However, in PFF active mice, there was a significant protection of SNpc TH+ neuron loss compared to the PFF sedentary group, reaching values similar to those of the PBS active group.

Voluntary exercise influences intrinsic excitability and rescues altered glutamatergic spontaneous transmission of striatal spiny projection neurons (SPNs) in α-syn-PFF-injected mice

To explore whether α-syn-PFF injection and/or voluntary exercise influenced basal ganglia neuronal excitability, we first assessed the activity of striatal SPNs using patch-clamp and intracellular recordings to characterize intrinsic and synaptic membrane properties (n = 12 cells/group). In current-clamp experiments, the application of hyperpolarizing current steps (range: from 0 to –400 pA, in 100 pA increments, 500 ms) revealed no differences of the current-voltage relationship in SPNs across all experimental conditions (Fig. 3A; PBS sedentary vs PBS active: two-way ANOVA interaction x time: F (4, 130) = 0.483, P = 0.7484 and PFF sedentary vs PFF active: two-way ANOVA interaction x time: F (4, 160) = 0.962, P = 0.430). Furthermore, resting membrane potential (RMP) of corticostriatal SPNs values remained unchanged in PFF- and PBS-injected mice, both active and sedentary (PBS sedentary: −86.13 ± 4.93 mV; PBS active: −88.88 ± 3.18 mV; PFF sedentary: −89.33 ± 4.59 mV and PFF active: −89.59 ± 2.70 mV; Fig. 3A; two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 33) = 1.403, P = 0.2592). The analysis of the number of action potentials (APs), triggered by depolarizing current steps (from 0 to 600 pA), revealed a difference in between PFF active and PFF sedentary mice, indicating an increase in SPNs excitability after 90 days of voluntary exercise (Fig. 3B center: PBS sedentary vs PBS active: two-way ANOVA interaction x time: F (6, 266) = 6.670, P = 0.674 and PFF sedentary vs PFF active: two-way ANOVA interaction x time: F (6, 273) = 3.993, P = 0.0008). Specifically, at 200 pA, statistical analysis denoted that PFF active mice exhibited a higher number of evoked APs in SPNs compared to both PFF sedentary and PBS active groups (Fig. 3B right: two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 33) = 9.470, P = 0.0001). However, this difference was no longer observed at 400 pA, the first step that elicited a comparable response across experimental groups (Fig. 3B right: two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: F (3, 33) = 1.100, P = 0.3628).

Moreover, the analysis of glutamatergic transmission showed increased spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic current (sEPSC) frequency, but not amplitude, in SPNs of PFF sedentary mice with respect to the PBS sedentary animals. This augmented frequency of glutamatergic spontaneous transmission, resembling an increase in glutamate release from presynaptic vesicles, was not observed in PFF active mice, which instead showed a reduction in this parameter, suggesting a protective effect of physical activity (Fig. 3C; n = 7 cells/group, two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test: frequency, F (3, 18) = 12.97, P < 0.0001; amplitude, F (3, 18) = 0.9633, P = 0.4315).

Corticostriatal LTP of α-syn-PFF SPNs is restored after voluntary exercise and it is dependent not only on endogenous DA and NMDA-mediated transmission, but also on eCB signaling

To determine whether our voluntary exercise protocol affects striatal synaptic plasticity, through ex vivo electrophysiological recordings, we studied SPNs across all experimental groups, examining corticostriatal LTP, a DA-dependent form of plasticity essential for the control of voluntary movements. We demonstrated that this form of plasticity was maintained in PBS mice, in both the sedentary and active groups (Fig. 4A; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-high frequency stimulation (HFS), n = 6 cells, PBS sedentary: t = 9.51, df = 11, P < 0.0001 and PBS active: t = 11.99, df = 11, P < 0.0001), but was absent in the PFF sedentary group 90 days post-injection (Fig. 4A; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 6 cells, PFF sedentary: t = 1.48, df = 19, P > 0.05). Interestingly, electrophysiological analysis in PFF active mice showed that, neurons were able to express LTP in corticostriatal synapses (Fig. 4A; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 6 cells, PFF active: t = 42.20, df = 13, P < 0.0001), demonstrating that 90 days of voluntary exercise has neuroprotective effects and preserves long-term striatal plasticity (Fig. 4A; two-way ANOVA: interaction x groups, PFF sedentary vs PFF active, F (14,225) = 20.77, P < 0.0001).

Given that corticostriatal LTP depends on the concomitant activation of D1 and GluN2A-containing NMDA receptors12, and considering that striatal SPNs integrate both glutamatergic and dopaminergic inputs, the precise timing and interaction of these signals are essential for elucidating the mechanisms underlying exercise-induced LTP in the PFF active group.

Delivery of HFS on corticostriatal fibers, in 0 Mg conditions, in the presence of a D1 receptor blocker (SCH23390 10 μM) was able to prevent LTP in SPNs of PFF active animals; conversely, following application of D2 receptor antagonist (Sulpiride 5 μM) this form of synaptic plasticity was maintained (Fig. 4B; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 9 cells, PFF active + SCH23390: t = 0.69, df = 17, P > 0.05 and n = 6 cells, PFF active + Sulpiride: t = 11.56, df = 11, P < 0.0001). We also found that bath application of NMDARs GluN2B or GluN2A antagonists (Ifenprodil 3 μM; NVP-AAM007 50 nM) prevented the induction of LTP in SPNs of PFF active mice (Fig. 4B; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 6 cells, PFF active + Ifenprodil: t = 2.04, df = 11 and PFF active + NVP-AAM007: t = 0.37, df = 11, P > 0.05), showing a clearly different response to this glutamate receptor blocker application respect to a control condition.

Since eCB signaling is an important modulator of striatal LTD induction, we explored whether LTP, which is restored by exercise in PFF active mice, still depends on eCB activation. Application of the CB1 receptor antagonist, SR141716 (5 μM), was able to block the LTP in PFF active mice (Fig. 4C; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 6 cells, PFF active + SR141716: t = 1.39, df = 11, P > 0.05). However, CB2 receptor antagonism with SR144528 (5 μM), on SPNs of PFF active mice, upon delivery of HFS, under the same conditions exerted no effects on this form of synaptic plasticity (Fig. 4C; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 6 cells, PFF active + SR144528: t = 8.57, df = 11, P < 0.0001).

Our finding that this CB1 receptor inhibitor in PBS groups led to a shift of NMDA-dependent LTP plasticity toward LTD (Supplementary Fig. 2; Paired t test, pre- versus 20 min post-HFS, n = 6 cells, PBS sedentary plus SR141716: t = 5.41, df = 11, P < 0.01; n = 6 cells, PBS active plus SR141716: t = 3.97, df = 11, P < 0.001), demonstrates- for the first time- a role of CB1 receptors in this form of synaptic plasticity NMDA-mediated in the striatum.

CB1 receptor antagonism restores synaptic depotentiation in active PBS mice but not in PFF mice

To investigate bidirectional striatal synaptic plasticity, we examined the effects of a low-frequency stimulation (LFS) protocol applied following LTP induction in SPNs of all experimental groups. As expected, low-frequency stimulation was able to reverse the potentiated EPSP amplitude to baseline levels in the PBS sedentary group, indicating a synaptic depotentiation (Fig. 5A; left panel: Paired t test, post-HFS versus 10 min post-LFS, n = 5 cells, PBS sedentary: t = 7.69, df = 11, P < 0.001). However, this response was not observed in SPNs of both PBS active and PFF active groups, revealing an impairment in reversing LTP after LFS (Fig. 5A; left panel: Paired t test, post-HFS versus 10 min post-LFS, n = 6 cells, PBS active: t = 0.59, df = 11, P > 0.05; Unpaired t test, PBS sedentary versus PBS active: t = 4.58, df = 20, P < 0.001; right panel: Paired t test, post-HFS versus 10 min post-LFS, n = 6 cells, PFF active: t = 1.06, df = 11, P > 0.05). To explore whether eCB signaling differentially modulated bidirectional striatal plasticity in the active groups, we applied a CB1 receptor blocker (SR141716, 5 μM) during the LFS protocol. We found that bath application of SR141716 restored the capacity of SPNs to express depotentiation after 10 min of LFS only in the PBS active group (Fig. 5B; Paired t test, post-HFS versus 10 min post-LFS, n = 5 cells, PBS active plus SR141716: t = 11.30, df = 9, P < 0.0001). In contrast, in SPNs of the PFF active group, CB1 receptor antagonism failed to restore baseline synaptic responsiveness (Fig. 5B; Unpaired t test, PBS active plus SR141716 versus PFF active plus SR141716, n = 4 cells, t = 45.73, df = 16, P < 0.0001). These results suggest that in PBS active mice, the persistence of synaptic potentiation is associated with an CBR1-dependent mechanism. This mechanism appears to be impaired or dysfunctional in PFF active mice, that is likely due to a selective disruption of eCB signaling.

Discussion

Recent clinical and experimental studies have helped to clarify the impact of causative genes in the pathogenesis of PD, whereas the contribution of environmental influences remains more difficult to define13,14. Although many potential protective and risk factors for PD have been identified, rigorous prevention trials or successful disease-modifying strategies have not emerged yet. Epidemiological evidence, however, consistently highlights physical activity and exercise as a prominent modifiable protective lifestyle factor against PD14–17. Accordingly, longitudinal studies have found that PD patients who exercise regularly experience a slower progression of motor and an improved quality of life. At the same time, exercise reduces the risk of developing PD, possibly interacting with key pathogenic processes1,18.

Recent preclinical studies have shown that exercise reduces α-syn aggregate accumulation and spreading in PD mice6. Voluntary exercise, a highly accessible exercise protocol, has been demonstrated to improve neurological function in PD models11,19,20. However, while some molecular and biochemical mechanisms that underlie neuroprotection have been identified5,21,22, further investigation is still needed.

The striatum is a strategic gate for processing cortical information and a major site of motor memory formation23. Synaptic plasticity at corticostriatal synapses underlies long-term changes in neuronal activity, which are essential for adaptive behavioral function, and depends on dopaminergic input from midbrain neurons, which modulate goal-directed actions and reinforcement learning23–28. The interplay between dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling is crucial for bidirectional striatal plasticity, including LTP, LTD, and synaptic depotentiation, processes that are impaired in PD due to the loss of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons29–34.

A reproducible progressive model of PD has been achieved by bilateral intrastriatal injection of α-syn fibrils35–37. In a previous study that used the PFF model, we have shown that forced intensive treadmill exercise reverses the loss of LTP and motor deficits, and that increased striatal BDNF levels and rescue of dopaminergic innervation are the major mechanisms underlying this amelioration6. Here, we investigated the potential protective effects of voluntary wheel running as an alternative to forced exercise, which has been shown to have the potential to induce significant stress in rodents38.

In non-motor domains, it has been demonstrated that voluntary exercise ameliorates deficits in learning and memory in mouse models of hippocampal impairment by reducing microglial activation and reversing synaptic damage39 enhances hippocampal theta rhythm and cognition40. This non-pharmacological intervention also increases striatal DA release with a mechanism requiring BDNF19 and facilitates putative glymphatic and meningeal lymphatic vessel flow in humans18. Taking into consideration these important existing findings, we investigated the complex interaction between dopaminergic, glutamatergic and eCB systems in the protective role of voluntary exercise on striatal bidirectional plasticity in the α-syn PD model.

Three major findings have been identified in this study. First, we found that voluntary motor exercise (wheel running) protects from motor impairment induced by intrastriatal injection of α-syn-PFFs and significantly enhanced grip strength and hanging wire performance that was decreased secondary to PFF-induced pathology. Second, we found that voluntary wheel running reduced nigrostriatal dopaminergic degeneration, preserved SNpc DA neuron terminals, and limited α-syn propagation from the striatum to the SNpc, indicating a strong link between physical activity and protection of the nigrostriatal system. Our interpretation is based on the observation that a partial DA denervation of the striatum (around 50%), induced by intrastriatal injection of α-syn-PFFs, is sufficient to block LTP in the SPNs, indicating that this form of synaptic plasticity is vulnerable even in the presence of partial denervation, in agreement with previous findings41. Under this condition, a subchronic treatment with L-Dopa rescues the deficit in corticostriatal plasticity at different phases of the α-synucleinopathy induced by this model, indicating that loss of DA is the main mechanism underlying the loss of LTP36,42.

Third, we found that voluntary exercise exerted a protective effect on corticostriatal synaptic plasticity in PD mice, preserving LTP and normalizing the glutamatergic hyperactivity at presynaptic level. This voluntary exercise-dependent LTP measured from SPNs of PFF-injected mice shares two molecular features with the LTP induced by forced exercise6. It depends on endogenous DA acting on D1, but not D2 receptors, and is blocked by both GluN2A- and GluN2B- containing NMDA receptor antagonists. In PBS sedentary animals, corticostriatal LTP requires only GluN2A-containing NMDA receptors, as GluN2B subunits are typically expressed during development but not in adult rodents. This evidence strongly supports the hypothesis that voluntary exercise induces a form of “rejuvenation” of the molecular mechanisms underlying LTP in SPNs of PFF active mice.

A distinctive feature of the corticostriatal LTP observed after voluntary exercise in PFF active mice is its dependence on CB1, but not CB2 receptor activation. While striatal LTD is known to rely on retrograde endocannabinoid signaling via CB1 receptors, our findings reveal that CB1 receptor activation also mediated exercise-induced LTP. It is also known that levels of one of the primary endogenous CB1 receptor agonists, anandamide (AEA), increase during development in the dorsal striatum43. This finding is in line with the literature suggesting that physical exercise stimulates the production of eCBs in the brain and other tissues44–46. Moreover, the eCB system has a key role in in various forms of synaptic plasticity expressed in different brain areas including the dorsal striatum9,47–50.

Finally, we found that while CB1 receptors are essential for bidirectional plasticity under experimental conditions, in α-syn–injected mice undergoing exercise, LTP—but not depotentiation—requires CB1 receptor activation. Notably, “depotentiation”, or “reversal of LTP”, represents a fundamental mechanism of brain plasticity through which previously induced potentiation can be reversed by LFS. This phenomenon, mainly observed in hippocampal and striatal synapses, is thought to erase unessential information. The loss of depotentiation has been reported in several neurological and psychiatric disorders, including L-Dopa–induced dyskinesia, Huntington’s disease, dystonia, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and substance use disorders32,51

Surprisingly, depotentiation was absent in mice after prolonged voluntary exercise. A distinct pharmacological profile emerged between PBS- and PFF–injected mice: while LFS-induced depotentiation was expressed in PBS active condition after CB1 receptor antagonist application, this effect was absent in PD mice. These results indicate that, although eCBs regulate synaptic depotentiation under experimental conditions, persistence of LTP in PFF active mice occurs independently of the eCB system (Fig. 6). Interestingly, we have previously shown that the depotentiation seen in both L-DOPA–treated non-dyskinetic rats and intact controls was prevented by activation of the D1 subclass of DA receptors or inhibition of protein phosphatases52.

Schematic representation of the bidirectional synaptic plasticity changes in different experimental conditions.

Changes in EPSP amplitude (% of control) over time following HFS and LFS protocols in dorsolateral striatal SPNs. A PBS sedentary condition: HFS induces a stable LTP in SPNs that requires the activation of D1 receptors, GluN2A-containing NMDARs, and CB1 receptors. Following LTP induction, the application of LFS protocol produces a normal depotentiation, leading to a reduction of synaptic strength. B PBS active condition: HFS still induces LTP in SPNs through the activation of D1 receptors, GluN2A-NMDARs, and CB1 receptors. However, LFS fails to induce depotentiation, indicating a persistent potentiation. Notably, pharmacological blockade of eCB1 receptors restores depotentiation, suggesting an enhanced eCBs system tone after exercise. C PFF sedentary condition: HFS is unable to induce LTP in SPNs due to reduced endogenous DA levels. As a consequence, synaptic potentiation is impaired, and no stable increase in EPSP amplitude is observed. D PFF active condition: HFS is able to induce LTP in SPNs, which requires the activation of D1 receptors, GluN2A/2B-containing NMDARs, and CB1 receptors. Nevertheless, LFS does not induce depotentiation, indicating persistent synaptic potentiation. In this condition, CB1 receptors antagonism is not sufficient to restore depotentiation, supporting the hypothesis of a persistent activation of D1/NMDA receptors signaling pathways52.

A similar mechanism may underlie the effects we observed after voluntary exercise in our parkinsonian PFF mice. In this case, however, the inability to express a depotentiation in response to a LFS seems to mediate a prolongation of synaptic potentiation in early PD following voluntary motor exercise. In line with our hypothesis, it has been recently shown that motor training induced synaptic potentiation on apical dendrites of layer 5 pyramidal neurons, as well as astrocytic Ca2+ rises in the mouse motor cortex53. Reducing astrocytic Ca2+ led to synaptic depotentiation during motor training and subsequent impairment in performance improvement. However, the activation of adenosine receptors prevented repetitive dendritic Ca2+ activity and synaptic depotentiation, revealing a critical physiological function of astrocytic Ca2+ in preventing synaptic depotentiation by limiting repetitive dendritic activity during learning53.

Utilizing high frequency stimulation (HFS)-dependent LTP, we failed to detect differences between these two subpopulations of SPNs in this and in previous studies from our group, where recordings of D1-positive SPNs from BAC Drd1a-tdTomato mice and of putative D2-SPNs showed no difference in corticostriatal synaptic plasticity between these two populations54. Future studies should clarify whether distinct synaptic and plastic responses can be observed in distinct subtypes (D1- and D2-bearing) of striatal neurons in response to exercise in this model. It would also be interesting to use different experimental approaches to induce corticostriatal synaptic plasticity, such as spike-time dependent plasticity protocols31,34,55.

In conclusion, these findings establish voluntary exercise as a powerful non-pharmacological strategy that preserves dopaminergic integrity and synaptic plasticity, promoting neural resilience and counteracting α-syn–driven neurodegeneration in PD models. This neuroprotective effect involves a parallel interaction between dopaminergic and eCBs signaling pathways, through which exercise dynamically regulates corticostriatal LTP and maintains functional circuit homeostasis.

Altogether, this new evidence provide an interesting perspective for the understanding of the pathophysiology of PD, the amelioration of this condition by voluntary exercise and the possibility to target the eCBs system as a novel strategy to reduce disability in PD.

Methods

Animals

Two- to three-month-old male C57Bl/6 mice (Centro Ricerche Sperimentali UCSC, n° 33/2019-UT, n = 120 animals) were employed in this study. All animals were group-housed (four per cage) under standardized conditions, including a 12-h light/dark cycle and a constant temperature of 22–23 °C, with unrestricted access to food and water. Every effort was made to minimize the number of animals used and reduce their discomfort, in accordance with European Directive 2010/63/EU. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Italian Ministry of Health and conducted in compliance with national regulations governing animal research and the guidelines set forth by the Federation of European Laboratory Animal Science Associations (FELASA).

Preparation of α-syn-PFF and surgery procedures

Recombinant monomeric human α-synuclein (α-syn) was obtained from Proteos Inc., and Preformed Fibrils (PFFs) were prepared according to their standardized protocol56. This protocol has been validated by the Michael J Fox Foundation to guarantee the reproducibility of PFFs (approximately 4 μg protein). PFFs were stored frozen and, immediately before injection into recipient animals, thawed and sonicated. At the time of surgery, animals were anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine mixture and fixed to the stereotaxic frame to receive a unilateral striatal injection of Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS, containing NaCl, KCl, and posphates of Na and K) or α-syn-PFFs. The injection was performed using a 10-μl Hamilton microsyringe fitted with a 26-gauge steel cannula. A volume of 0.88 μl was injected in the left hemisphere, into the dorsomedial striatum, at a rate of 0.38 μl/min, and the cannula was left in place for an additional 3 min after the injection to allow for proper diffusion. The stereotaxic coordinates used were: anteroposterior: +0.86 mm, mediolateral: +1.88 mm, and dorsoventral: −2.5 mm, according to the Franklin and Paxinos atlas.

Voluntary wheel running (VWR)

For the voluntary wheel running (VWR protocol) exercise, each mouse was individually placed in a cage equipped with a running wheel (Actimetrics Wireless Low-Profile Running Wheel, Lafeyette Instuments, model: ACT-557-WLP) connected via Wi-Fi to a centralized monitoring system (Scurry Activity System, Lafayette Instrument, model 86165), which continuously recorded parameters such as total distance run, cumulative activity (measured as cumulative counts), and speed57. To prevent social isolation, each mouse was housed in the wheel-equipped cage during the active phase of the circadian cycle but after each running session, animals were returned to their original home cages with their cage mates. This set-up ensured the accurate tracking of voluntary locomotor activity while minimizing stress associated with isolation58. Mice had access to the running wheels five days per week (Monday–Friday) for 90 days. PBS sedentary mice were placed in similar cages with locked wheels to control for environmental enrichment (Fig. 1A).

Behavioral tests

Grip strength test: this test was conducted using a grip strength meter (GSM, Bioseb Instrument). The system was calibrated before each session, and force measurements were recorded approximately every 0.1 s. To perform the test, each mouse was gently held by the base of the tail and allowed to grasp a metal grid with its forepaws. The mouse was then pulled back in a smooth motion perpendicular to the bar, prompting it to resist and apply a grip force for about one second. Grip strength was recorded in grams and normalized to body weight (grams of force per gram of body mass). For each animal, the mean value from three consecutive trials was used for statistical analysis59,60.

Hanging wire test: this test measures the time it takes for the mouse to resist in a hanging position. This task is used to evaluate muscle strength, coordination, and neuromuscular function. Basically, animals were placed on a wire hanging 50–60 cm above ground for a maximum of 180 s, and they have to remain suspended with their bodies using their forelimbs or all four limbs. In detail, mice started with a fall score of 10, which was reduced by 1 point each time they fell during the test. The time of each fall event was also recorded. Each time a mouse fell, the timer was paused to replace the mouse in the center of the wire again61.

Open field task: this is a widely used behavioral test in rodents to assess locomotor activity and exploratory behavior. In this test, a mouse is placed in an open square arena (typically 50 × 50 × 38 cm) and allowed to explore freely for about 600 s. Locomotion is evaluated by measuring total distance travelled, average speed, time spent at the center and immobility time using automated tracking software62.

Immunofluorescence and confocal analysis

Brain tissues were collected from euthanized mice after deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of Rompun and Zoletil. Following transcardial perfusion with PBS (0.1 M, pH 7.4), brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h. Samples were subsequently cryoprotected in 30% sucrose prepared in 0.1 M PB for an additional 24 h. Coronal sections (30 μm thick) were obtained using a cryostat (Leica CM1900) and stored at 4 °C in 0.1 M PB containing 0.02% Sodium Azide until further processing.

To evaluate the integrity of the nigrostriatal pathway, immunostaining for Tyrosine Hydroxylase (TH) and the Active Dopamine Transporter (DAT) was performed separately. Four rostrocaudally matched striatal sections per mouse were selected and stained as following. Tissue slices were incubated for 48 h at 4 °C under continuous shaking with primary antibodies diluted in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PB: mouse anti-TH (1:600, MAB318, Merck Millipore) and rat anti-DAT (1:1000, MAB369, Millipore).

In addition, to investigate the spreading of phosphorylated form of α-syn (p-αSyn, Ser129), double-labelling immunofluorescence has been conducted on the striatum, SNpc and motor cortex (M1). Sections were incubated with rabbit anti-p129-αSyn (1:100, ab51253, Abcam) using the same protocol described above. For cellular structure identification, Neurotrace™ 455 green fluorescent Nissl stain was applied to visualize neurons and glia. Following the primary antibody washing step, all the slices were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies: anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 and anti-rat Alexa Fluor 555 (1:200, ThermoFisher).

Confocal imaging was carried out using a Nikon APM1 laser scanning microscope at 20× magnification. To highlight p129-αSyn spreading, additional digital zoomed images have been captured from each brain region (Fig. 2). Optical density (O.D.) measurements of TH- and DAT-positive fibers, and p129-αSyn signals were performed using ImageJ software. For each section, two regions of interest (ROIs) were randomly selected: 200 × 200 μm² for TH and DAT, and 636 × 636 μm² for p-αSyn and results have been reported as the mean ROI value across the included sections for each animal (n = 5/6 mice per group). All O.D. values were expressed as a percentage relative to control levels (% compared to control). PBS sedentary mice were set to 100% and values for the other groups were calculated proportionally relative to these ones.

Immunohistochemistry and stereological analysis

To quantify dopaminergic neuronal survival, ten serial sections per animal (n = 6 mice per group) encompassing the SNpc were selected and processed for stereological analysis. Immunohistochemical staining and subsequent quantification were performed following established protocols, as described previously6. Unbiased stereological count of total TH+ cells was obtained using the Stereo Investigator system (MicroBrightField Europe e.K., Magdeburg, Germany), according the methodology already described63. A three-dimensional optical fractionator probe (50 µm × 50 µm × 20 µm in the x, y, and z dimensions, respectively) was applied to generate unbiased estimation of total TH⁺ cell numbers.

Ex vivo electrophysiological recordings

Ex vivo experiments were conducted using intracellular and whole-cell patch-clamp techniques, as previously described6. Brain corticostriatal slices (n = 76 mice) were cut using a vibratome at a thickness of 280 μm for intracellular recordings and 240 μm for whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. Slices will be transferred to a recording chamber perfused with oxygenated Krebs solution (95% O₂ / 5% CO₂) at room temperature, with a flow rate of 2.5–3 ml/min. During the recording, the temperature is maintained at 32 °C using automated temperature control. The Krebs solution will contain (in mM): 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 MgCl₂, 1.2 NaH₂PO₄, 2.4 CaCl₂, 10 D(+)-glucose, and 25 NaHCO₃.

Spiny projection neurons (SPNs) were recorded and visualized using infrared differential interference contrast microscopy (Olympus) in the dorsolateral striatum. Resting membrane potential (RMP) was determined immediately upon breaking into the cell membrane. Action potentials (APs) firing rate were determined at depolarizing current steps. For evoked synaptic responses, stimulation electrodes were located in the corpus callosum. Glutamatergic excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs) were evoked by electrical stimulation. To facilitate NMDA receptor activation, magnesium ions were omitted from the external solution during LTP induction. LTP was induced using a high-frequency stimulation (HFS) protocol: three 3-s trains at 100 Hz, with 20-s inter-train intervals12. The low-frequency stimulation (LFS) protocol (2 Hz for 10 min) was delivered to reverse a previously induced LTP to baseline levels and trigger synaptic depotentiation6.

For spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs), patch-clamp electrodes were filled with the intracellular solution, mentioned above. All experiments were done in the presence of Picrotoxin (50 μM), a GABAA receptor antagonist. For pharmacological modulation of LTP, the following antagonists were used: for D1R, SCH23390 (10 μM); for D2R, sulpiride (5 μM); for subunit-specific NMDAR, NVP-AAM007 (50 nM) and Ifenprodil (3 μM); for CB1 and CB2 receptors, SR141716 (5 μM) and SR144528 (5 μM), respectively.

Both patch-clamp and intracellular recordings with sharp electrodes were carried out to study intrinsic membrane properties and synaptic plasticity. When results were similar, and the extent of changes comparable, values (expressed in percentage of control) obtained from the two approaches were pooled and plotted together.

Statistical analysis

Electrophysiological data were analyzed using Clampfit (version 11) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1). EPSP amplitude changes were expressed relative to baseline, defined as the average response during a stable 10–15 min period before drug application or stimulation. LTP was assessed by comparing EPSPs 20 min after HFS to baseline using paired t-tests. Group means were compared with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. Differences across experimental groups and membrane properties were analyzed with two-way ANOVA, where appropriate. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Behavioral outcomes were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, with treatment (e.g., PBS vs. PFF) and physical activity (sedentary vs. active) as between-subject factors. Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was conducted when required to interpret significant interactions or main effects.

For immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analysis, all experimental groups were compared by means of two-way ANOVA by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test.
