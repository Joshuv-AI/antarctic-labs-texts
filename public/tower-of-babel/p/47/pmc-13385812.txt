Dairy cows infected with influenza A(H5N1) reveals low infectious dose and transmission barriers
Authors: Carolyn Lee, Natalie N Tarbuck, Hannah J Cochran, Bryant M Foreman, Patricia Boley, Saroj Khatiwada, Alok Dhakal, Khadijat O Adefaye, Jennifer Schrock, Mohammad Jawad Jahid, Thamonpan Laocharoensuk, Raksha Suresh, Olaitan Shekoni, Erika Stevens, Sara Dolatyabi, Christina Sanders, Elizabeth Ohl, Devra Huey, Juliette Hanson, Kara Corps, Renukaradhya Gourapura, Richard J Webby, Cody J Warren, Scott P Kenney, Andrew S Bowman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHighly pathogenic avian influenza A(H5N1) virus exhibits a strong tropism for the bovine mammary gland, challenging our understanding of influenza A virus host range and tissue specificity. We performed experimental studies with an influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus in female lactating dairy cattle to define the infectious dose, routes of exposure, and factors linked to morbidity and mortality. Here, we demonstrate that intramammary inoculation with as few as 10 TCID50 establishes a robust infection and shedding of high-titer virus in milk. Despite this low infectious dose, H5N1 does not readily transmit via contaminated milking equipment and close contact with infected animals. High-dose intramammary exposure results in severe disease and mortality, while respiratory and oral exposures are less likely to establish productive infection and associated morbidity. This study challenges current hypotheses of H5N1 transmission on dairy farms, raising important questions about potential agent, host, or environmental cofactors contributing to viral spread.Subject terms: Influenza virus, Viral transmission

Introduction

Since 2021, highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) viruses of the influenza A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b have undergone extensive genetic diversification and caused widespread outbreaks globally. While traditionally associated with wild birds and poultry—often resulting in devastating impacts to the poultry industry—the ongoing H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b panzootic is notable for its unprecedented geographic spread and the diverse range of mammalian species infected. Importantly, this clade has exhibited mammal-to-mammal transmission in multiple settings, marking a significant shift in the epidemiology of HPAI viruses. Mammal-to-mammal transmission has been reported on fur farms in Europe1,2, marine mammals in South America3–6, and dairy cattle in the United States7,8, accompanied by evidence of mammalian adaptation9. These developments raise significant concerns regarding the heightened risk of zoonotic transmission.

The emergence of influenza A(H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b in dairy cattle, first reported on March 25, 2024, represents an unexpected shift in the ecology of the virus with unknown zoonotic potential and implications for new evolutionary pathways. The B3.13 genotype introduced to dairy cattle by a single spillover event from wild birds8, quickly spread to multiple U.S. states facilitated by cattle movement, contributing to widespread outbreaks totaling 1106 confirmed outbreaks in dairy herds in 20 U.S states to date10. A little over 1 year into the outbreak, an additional introduction of a distinct virus genotype (D1.1) from wild birds to dairy cows was identified11, underscoring the susceptibility of dairy cattle to influenza A(H5N1) and continued circulation of the virus. Cattle are known to be susceptible to influenza D virus12, but susceptibility of cattle to influenza A virus was previously rarely investigated13–17, exposing substantial gaps in our understanding of pathogenicity and transmission that delayed identification of the virus in dairy cattle. Unlike in previously identified hosts where influenza A(H5N1) primarily affects the respiratory, gastrointestinal, or nervous systems, dairy cattle exhibit highest viral titers in milk7,18–20, indicating that the mammary gland is the primary site of viral replication. This marks a notable shift in tissue tropism that needs to be explored. A broad spectrum of disease from mild to fatal has been reported in dairy cows7,18–22, necessitating studies that explore contributing factors to morbidity and mortality. Infected cows have exhibited clinical signs including mastitis, marked declines in milk production, altered milk appearance, reduced feed intake, and, in some cases, evidence of systemic infection. The long-term impact of severe disease on milk production remains to be seen. While cow-to-cow transmission is not yet well understood, milking equipment is hypothesized to play a role. Of note, spillover of the virus from dairy cattle to wild birds, poultry, cats, and dairy farm workers has also been documented7,8,19,23, highlighting the complex transmission pathways at play. The risk of further transmission to other livestock within or near dairy cattle premises is a growing concern, given the potential for further adaptation and increased zoonotic transmission of influenza A(H5N1) viruses.

Here, we experimentally inoculate peak-lactation Holstein cows to address several questions related to pathogenicity, including (1) dose response and associated clinical disease, (2) mechanisms for cow-to-cow transmission, (3) routes of infection, and (4) transmission to other relevant livestock animals that may be exposed to cattle, infected milk, or be at risk for spillover (e.g., calves, poultry).

Results

H5N1 infects the bovine mammary gland at remarkably low doses

Prior experimental infections of lactating dairy cattle have shown that intramammary inoculation with influenza A(H5N1) virus causes clinical disease and milk production losses similar to those observed during natural outbreaks on farms. These studies typically used infectious doses ranging from 1 × 104 to ~1 × 106 50% tissue culture infectious dose [TCID50]18,20,22. Here, we aimed to determine the minimal infectious dose required to establish productive infection in the bovine udder.

The bovine udder consists of four independent mammary glands (quarters), each draining into a single teat24. Because each quarter functions as a distinct anatomical unit, we evaluated the kinetics of infectious virus production following inoculation of different doses of a dairy cow strain influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus (range: 1 × 101 to 1 × 103 TCID50) into separate teat quarters of four peak-lactation dairy cows. One quarter (right rear, RR) was left uninfected to serve as an internal control and to monitor for potential cross-contamination during milk sampling (Fig. 1A). Clinical mastitis, together with a change in milk color and consistency (Supplementary Fig. 1), was observed in all virus-exposed quarters by 3 days post-inoculation (DPI), consistent with on-farm observations21,25; no such changes occurred in the uninfected quarter. Additionally, all cows showed clinical signs of disease, characterized by persistent fevers (>40 °C), reduced feed intake, and decreased milk production (Supplementary Fig. 1). Infectious virus in milk from each quarter was quantified via TCID50 assay. Strikingly, similar viral replication kinetics were observed in quarters infused with 1 × 102 and 1 × 103 TCID50 of virus (LF and RF), both reaching peak titers of >1 × 1012 TCID50/mL by 3 DPI. Although the lowest infectious dose (1 × 101 TCID50) resulted in delayed viral growth kinetics, it still led to high titers exceeding 1 × 1011 TCID50/mL. No infectious virus was detected in the control quarter (Fig. 1B), indicating a lack of spread of the virus beyond infected quarters. Together, these findings demonstrate that 10 TCID50 of influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus are sufficient to establish robust infection in the bovine mammary gland. Because all administered doses led to productive infection, the true minimal infectious dose lies below the lowest dose evaluated.

H5N1 does not readily transmit via milking equipment

The rapid spread of the bovine H5N1 epizootic across U.S. dairy herds indicates that cow-to-cow transmission is occurring, yet the precise mode of transmission remains undefined. Contaminated milking units and/or exposure within milking parlors have been hypothesized as likely routes of virus spread26,27. To test these hypotheses, we conducted an experiment in high biocontainment where infected and sentinel cows were co-housed, with shared access to feed, water, and milking equipment over a 14-day period. In two separate rooms, one peak-lactation dairy cow was infused intramammary with 1 × 103 TCID50 of a dairy cow origin influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus per teat. Twelve hours post-inoculation, the infected cow was milked, and the contaminated milking claw was immediately transferred to use on the sentinel cow for milking. This process was repeated twice daily for 14 days, after which the infected cows were euthanized. Sentinel cows were monitored for an additional 5 days (Fig. 2A). Infected cows developed a fever and dramatically declined in milk production 2 days post-inoculation, whereas sentinel animals remained unaffected throughout the study (Fig. 2B, C, left and middle panels). Titration of live virus from a composite milk sample collected from each quarter revealed high viral loads reaching peak titers of >1 × 1012 TCID50/mL in experimentally-infected animals, but no detectable virus in milk from sentinel cows at any time point (Fig. 2B, C, right). These results indicate that under the conditions tested, including direct use of contaminated milking claws and close contact, transmission of influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus to naïve cows did not occur.

High-dose intramammary H5N1 exposure results in mortality

Increased mortality has been reported in U.S. dairy herds naturally infected with influenza A(H5N1)19,21. Since our prior studies involved low infectious doses of intramammary infused virus (≤1 × 103 TCID50 per teat; Figs. 1 and 2), we next questioned whether a higher infectious dose could recapitulate the more severe clinical outcomes observed in affected herds. To test this, two peak-lactation dairy cows were infused intramammary with 1 × 107 TCID50 per teat of a dairy cow origin influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus (Fig. 3A). Within 24 h, both cows exhibited high fever (rectal temperature >40 °C), along with rapid decreases in milk output and feed intake (Fig. 3B). Cow #58 became lethargic, unwilling to stand, and was euthanized 2 days post-inoculation. Cow #59 exhibited similar clinical disease progression and was euthanized 4 days post-inoculation. At necropsy, macroscopic evaluation of the udder revealed necrotizing mastitis (Fig. 3C). Microscopic evaluation revealed multifocal lesions ranging in severity from moderate to severe and included partial to complete necrosis of alveoli and surrounding stroma, with surrounding edema and inflammatory cell infiltrates. In some tissue sections, entire lobules lacked expected normal mammary architecture. Immunohistochemical labeling for viral antigen revealed multifocal to coalescing, specific intracellular labeling of intact epithelial cells in alveoli of the gland cistern and duct, and similarly intense labeling in the lining epithelium of the teat cistern and meatus (Supplementary Fig. 2). Consistent with these results, viral RNA was detected at high levels across all four quarters (Fig. 3D). Together, these findings demonstrate that high-dose viral exposure to the mammary gland induces severe disease characterized by extensive tissue necrosis and inflammation. The severity of disease, which necessitated humane euthanasia of affected animals, likely reflects combined effects of viral dose and host inflammatory responses rather than viral burden alone.

Oral H5N1 exposure in calves is subclinical with limited spread

Given the high titers of infectious virus detected in milk following intramammary infection (Figs. 1–3), we next assessed whether ingestion of unpasteurized, virus-laden milk poses a health risk to calves. Twelve hours post-inoculation, milk was collected from the two peak-lactation dairy cows from the high-dose intramammary study infused with 1 × 107 TCID50 per teat of influenza A(H5N1) virus (cows used in Fig. 3), pooled, and bottle-fed to four 10-day-old Holstein calves twice daily over two consecutive days (four total feedings; Fig. 4A). Calves were monitored daily for behavior, rectal temperature, and viral replication via nasal and oral swabs. While viral RNA was sporadically detected in swabs (Fig. 4B, C), rectal temperatures remained within normal limits, and calves exhibited no clinical signs—maintaining normal appetite, energy levels, and behavior. Two calves were necropsied on day 6 and two on day 14 post-exposure, and tissues were assessed for viral RNA by RT-qPCR. Viral RNA was inconsistently detected, and only two tissue samples had Ct values < 35, suggesting limited and likely non-productive infection (Fig. 4D). No gross pathological abnormalities were observed at necropsy. Microscopic examination of the reticulum, rumen, omasum, abomasum, bronchus, and trachea were performed for each calf and compared to an uninfected control calf of similar age and housing conditions. No obvious differences were detected in the respiratory tissues in influenza A(H5N1)-exposed calves compared to the control calves. Scattered throughout the reticulum, rumen, and omasum in three of four influenza A(H5N1)-exposed calves were small foci of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and neutrophils in the submucosa in larger quantities than are found in comparable control calf tissues. These inflammatory cells occasionally extend into and between mucosal epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, these results suggest that oral exposure to high titers of influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus via unpasteurized milk is insufficient to cause overt disease or establish productive infection in young calves under these experimental conditions.

High-dose aerosolized H5N1 causes subclinical pathology

Mild to no respiratory signs have been reported in H5N1-affected dairy herds in the U.S.19. To validate these field observations, we experimentally exposed peak-lactation dairy cattle to a high dose (1 × 108.5 50% egg infectious dose (EID50)) of aerosolized (intranasal) influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus (Fig. 5A). Viral RNA was only sporadically detected in nasal swabs by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 4), while no RNA was detected in milk, and no clinical signs of disease were observed throughout the study. At necropsy, however, macroscopic lesions were observed in the respiratory tract, including petechiae extending into the nasopharynx at both early (day 5) and late (day 18) timepoints (Fig. 5B, C). Bilateral hemorrhages were present in the larynx (Fig. 5D), and lymphadenitis was observed in the mandibular lymph nodes (Fig. 5E). Microscopically, respiratory tissues from cows exposed to influenza A(H5N1) had minimal tissue-level changes compared to an uninfected, normal control cow of similar age and housing conditions. Multifocal minimal increases in interstitial neutrophils and/or lymphocytes were present in lungs from infected cows. One cow additionally had an epithelial lesion in the trachea with infiltrates of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and fewer neutrophils underlying and infiltrating the mucosal epithelium. The mucosa is segmentally disrupted with epithelial degeneration and loss of cilia (Fig. 5F). Thus, although clinical disease was not apparent, our findings suggest that influenza A(H5N1) can cause subclinical pathology in bovine respiratory tissues.

To assess the potential for airborne transmission, we co-housed eight Cornish-cross broiler chickens in cages flanked by the virus-exposed cows, beginning 1 day post-inoculation (Fig. 5A). Chickens were monitored daily for clinical signs, and oral and cloacal swabs were collected and tested by RT-qPCR at DPI -1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 13, and 17. All chickens remained healthy, with no viral RNA detected in swabs at any timepoint or in lung or tracheal tissues at necropsy. Together, these findings indicate that respiratory infection in cows occurs without overt disease and is unlikely to support airborne transmission under these experimental conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that a low dose of virus is sufficient to establish robust intramammary infection, which may underlie the widespread and efficient transmission of influenza A(H5N1) in dairy herds. However, sentinel cows repeatedly exposed to contaminated milking equipment and cohoused with infected cows did not become infected, indicating that cow-to-cow transmission observed on dairy farms may depend on factors that are not easily replicated under experimental conditions in high biocontainment research settings. In contrast, high-dose intramammary inoculation recapitulated severe clinical outcomes observed in the field, including reduced milk production, systemic illness, and disease requiring euthanasia—indicating a potential dose-dependent effect on disease severity at highly susceptible mammary tissue sites. Calves bottle-fed infected milk and cows exposed via intranasal aerosolization failed to establish productive infections, underscoring the critical role of tissue tropism in determining disease outcome. Given the sustained spread of influenza A(H5N1) in dairy cattle, establishing reproducible challenge models—such as those described here—is essential for advancing our understanding of viral pathogenesis and transmission dynamics.

The bovine mammary gland comprised of four quarters is highly compartmentalized, allowing us to evaluate kinetics of infectious virus in each quarter independently. Surprisingly, similar growth kinetics were observed for each quarter inoculated with different infectious doses ranging from 1 × 101 to 1 × 103 TCID50, all reaching viral titers >1 × 1011 TCID50/mL. Notably, the control quarter remained unaffected confirming the functional independence of each quarter in the udder, with no evidence of inter-quarter spread of influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus. The onset of clinical mastitis was consistent with these findings, emphasizing virus-induced mastitis as a manifestation of influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype infection. Together, these findings support that the bovine mammary gland is highly susceptible to influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype virus and that a productive infection yielding high viral titers can be established with as few as 10 TCID50 of virus. By comparison, the lowest infectious dose explored so far in other experimental inoculation studies with dairy cows was 1 × 104 TCID5022. Further studies investigating the clinical outcomes associated with such a low infectious dose are needed to corroborate field observations.

The notion that such a low infectious dose is sufficient to establish infection is somewhat contradicted by our inability to demonstrate transmission to sentinel animals via contaminated milking equipment or direct contact. Infected cows developed a fever corresponding with a significant decline in milk production. Infectious virus was shed in the milk of infected cows for 8 days, yet sentinel cows remained unaffected. α2,3-linked sialic acid receptors are abundant in the bovine mammary gland28,29, and it is apparent in this study and others18,20,22 that direct inoculation in containment using a teat cannula results in infection. However, this experimental method is not representative of natural transmission pathways which remain to be determined and then reproduced. After the first confirmed case of influenza A(H5N1) in dairy cows in Texas in March 2024, the virus disseminated rapidly across the country8,19, with some farms reporting clinical disease in up to 20% of the herd21. The rapid and widespread distribution of influenza A(H5N1) among dairy cows—affecting over 1000 herds in 17 states to date—highlights the notable efficiency of transmission and extreme infectivity of the virus. While contaminated milking equipment is strongly hypothesized to be the primary route of exposure on-farm26,27, our experimental findings did not confirm this, falling short of an explanation for the apparently frequent on-farm transmission. Sentinel animals did not shed infectious virus throughout the study; however, in the absence of serological data, definitive conclusions regarding exposure or non-productive infection in these animals cannot be made. Similarly, although infected cows shed high titers of infectious virus in milk, we did not directly assess the level of virus present on milking equipment and therefore cannot define the magnitude of exposure experienced by sentinel animals. Considering these study limitations, together with features of the BSL-3-Ag environment—including high air-exchange rates, smaller group sizes, reduced contact opportunities, and stringent cleaning protocols—there may be reduced opportunities for evaluating transmission under experimental conditions compared with commercial dairy environments. Other agent, host, and environmental cofactors remain plausible drivers of farm-level transmission and warrant further investigation. These findings underscore important gaps in our understanding of how bovine-adapted H5N1 spreads within dairy herds.

Significant economic losses to dairy producers have been reported due to morbidity and mortality associated with influenza A(H5N1) infection on-farm21. In this study, two dairy cows were inoculated intramammary with an infectious dose tenfold higher than previously reported20. Both were humanely euthanized before the end of the planned study period due to severe clinical disease, including sustained high fever, extreme lethargy (e.g., unwilling to stand), and loss of appetite, meeting endpoint criteria for euthanasia. Macroscopic necrotizing mastitis was observed in these animals, highlighting significant tissue disruption due to robust viral replication in mammary tissue. Viral titers of 104.0 to 108.8 TCID50/mL have been reported in milk from infected cows on-farm19, which has significant implications to animal health and production as demonstrated by mortality associated with exposure to 106 and 107 TCID50 under experimental conditions. Importantly, our study and others20 demonstrate that oral exposure of calves to milk containing a very high viral load resulted in limited viral replication without overt disease, indicating that oral exposure and vertical transmission via milk likely does not pose a significant risk. Of note, clinical disease and seropositivity have also been reported in dry dairy cows on-farm21, suggesting the potential for transmission of the virus beyond milking equipment. The long-term effects of influenza A(H5N1) infection in dairy cows needs to be explored further, but some reports suggest that decreased milk production persists for >30–60 days past clinical disease due to viral-induced mastitis and damage to milk-producing epithelial cells19,21. Together, these findings underscore the burden of influenza A(H5N1) to the dairy industry and highlight the need for mitigation strategies to reduce transmission and protect animal health.

While the mammary gland appears to be a primary site of viral replication in dairy cows, the initial route of transmission from wild birds remains unclear. This uncertainty, along with the potential for respiratory exposure in milking parlors18,27, prompted our investigation of intranasal inoculation as a possible route of infection. The macroscopic lesions observed in the respiratory tract suggest that influenza A(H5N1) B3.13 genotype can cause subclinical pathology in bovine respiratory tissues. However, the absence of clinical disease (e.g., fever, decreased milk production, and feed intake), or virus present in milk at any timepoint indicates that this route of infection likely does not contribute to systemic disease and introduction into mammary tissue. Additionally, chickens co-housed in open air cages adjacent to intranasally inoculated cows did not become infected as evidenced by lack of viral nucleic acid in swabs and overt disease despite salivary contact between an inoculated cow and chicken feed. We attribute this lack of transmission to multiple factors including limited viral shedding from nasal secretions and potentially high number of air changes (i.e., BSL-3 engineering controls) in animal holding rooms.

Although human-to-human transmission of influenza A(H5N1) virus has not been reported, the persistence of the virus in dairy cattle and evidence of mammalian adaptation9, coupled with the broad and unprecedented host range, are alarming from a public health perspective. A second, separate introduction of the D1.1 genotype virus, primarily circulating in wild birds, was reported in dairy cows in early 2025. Unlike the B3.13 virus circulating in cattle, this D1.1 genotype has been associated with severe disease in humans, resulting in two deaths30–32—a highly concerning feature should this genotype continue to spread unfettered. Further, the co-circulation of B3.13 and D1.1 viruses in dairy cows increases the risk of reassortment and viral evolution and complicates efforts to control the influenza A(H5N1) outbreak. Additional studies are needed to characterize the immune response and assess the level of protection following re-exposure to both homologous and heterologous influenza A(H5N1) viruses. The mechanism of transmission within and between dairy herds is a critical question that remains and urgently needs to be answered.

Methods

Research ethics and safety

All animal experiments conducted at The Ohio State University were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Ohio State University under protocol #2024A00000045. Influenza (A)H5N1 research was approved by the Ohio State University Institutional biosafety committee under approval #2017R00000017 and all virus experiments were performed in The Ohio State University Plant and Animal Agricultural Research (PAAR) Biosafety Level 3 (BSL3) large animal facility in Wooster, OH, USA.

Virus isolation and propagation

The A/bovine/Ohio/B24OSU-342/2024 (H5N1) virus (GenBank IDs: PP836412.1–PP836419.1) was isolated from a milk sample collected during a dairy cattle outbreak in Ohio33. The virus was initially propagated through two serial passages in 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs, incubated at 35 °C for up to 48 h. The EID50 titer was determined by serial tenfold dilutions in embryonated eggs, following standard protocols as described previously34. The egg-isolated virus stock was subsequently amplified in Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells through two low multiplicity of infection (MOI) passages. The resulting amplified virus stock was used for all studies described herein, unless otherwise specified. Full genome sequencing analysis of the cell culture passaged B3.13 virus stock used in this study showed 99.9% pairwise identity in PB2 and 99.95% in PA when compared to the original isolate (GenBank IDs: PP836412.1–PP836419.1), with synonymous mutations in PA (T81C) and PB2 (G1881A), and nonsynonymous mutations in PB2 (A1183T and K2029E). No mutations were detected in the remaining segments, and neither of the observed nonsynonymous mutations are currently recognized as markers of mammalian adaptation or increased virulence.

Whole genome sequencing

Purified RNA of the cell culture passaged B3.13 virus stock was submitted for IAV whole genome sequencing. IAV amplification PCR was performed using the Invitrogen SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR System with Platinum Taq DNA Polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per manufacturer’s instructions. PCR product was purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) following manufacturer instructions. Quantification was performed with the Qubit Flex using the Qubit 1X dsDNA BR Assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Library prep was performed using the Illumina DNA Prep kit and the Illumina DNA-RNA UD Indexes Set B (Illumina). The library was spiked with a 30% addition of PhiX and sequenced on the iSeq 100 using the iSeq 100 i1 Reagent v2 (300-cycle). Fastq files were assembled into sequences using the Iterative Refinment Meta-Assembler (IRMA) v1.3.035. Assembly was performed using default parameters and the FLU module.

Plaque assay titration

MDCK cells (ATCC) were maintained in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM; Corning, Manassas, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For infectious virus titration by plaque assay, MDCK cells were seeded at 1 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates. The following day, growth medium was removed, and cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) prior to infection with tenfold serial dilutions of virus in serum-free EMEM (0.8 mL per well). Cells were incubated with virus for 1 h at 37 °C with gentle rocking every 15 min. After incubation, the inoculum was removed and 3 mL of overlay medium (EMEM supplemented with L-glutamine and PenStrep, 2.8% Avicel, and 1 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin) was added. Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following incubation, overlays were removed, cells were washed twice with PBS, and fixed with 20% methanol containing 0.2% crystal violet in PBS for 20 min. Plates were rinsed with deionized water, plaques were enumerated, and viral titers were calculated as plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL).

Cow intramammary inoculation

Healthy multiparous female Holstein cows (n = 10) in peak-lactation were transported to the PAAR facility. Prior to entry, all animals tested negative for influenza A(H5N1) virus by RT-qPCR. Before inoculation, teats were disinfected sequentially with povidone-iodine (Betadine) and 70% ethanol. Intramammary inoculation was performed using a sterile teat cannula (Jorgensen Laboratories, J-12 Teat Infusion Cannula), delivering 2.5 mL of virus inoculum (diluted in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium [DMEM]) into the indicated teat(s). Following inoculation, teats were gently massaged to facilitate upward distribution of the inoculum. Cows were milked every 12 h using dedicated milking claws for each animal, except during transmission studies, in which contaminated claws from the infected cow were transferred to sentinel cows. Milk samples were collected either as pooled samples (from all four quarters) or individually from each teat, depending on the experimental design. Clinical monitoring included twice-daily recording of rectal temperature, milk yield (by weight), and feed refusal. A California Mastitis Test (CMT) was performed each morning. Cows were humanely euthanized at the study endpoint or earlier if predefined humane endpoints were met. Viral RNA was quantified from homogenized tissues using RT-qPCR. Where indicated, infectious virus in milk was measured by TCID50 or PFU assays.

Calf oral inoculation

Ten-day-old male Holstein calves (n = 4) and intramammary-inoculated cows were housed in separate BSL-3 animal rooms. All calves were fed milk replacer twice daily until infected milk was available. Twelve hours after high-dose intramammary inoculation of healthy Holstein cows in peak-lactation (n = 2; Fig. 3), the cows were milked, the milk was pooled, and calves were fed 1.5 pints of milk (1400 mL) during the initial feeding. This was followed by 0.5 pints per feeding (470 mL) for the next three feedings (total duration: 48 h). In each case, infected milk was administered first, followed by a 30-min hold time before providing the remaining volume as milk replacer. Aliquots of milk were taken prior to each feeding and back-titrated using plaque assays, confirming high titer (>1 × 108 PFU/mL) of influenza A(H5N1) milk fed to calves (see Fig. 4). Nasal and oral swabs were collected daily and tested for the presence of influenza A virus by RT-qPCR. Necropsies were performed at 6- and 14-days post-exposure (n = 2 groups of two calves). Tissues were collected and homogenized, and tested for the presence of influenza A virus by RT-qPCR.

Cow aerosol inoculation

Healthy multiparous female Holstein cows in peak-lactation (n = 2) were intranasally inoculated with 2.5 mL of virus inoculum (diluted in DMEM) at 1 × 108.5 50% egg infectious dose [EID50] per nostril using a mucosal atomization device (MAD Nasal™, Teleflex) following morning milking. Clinical observations were recorded daily as described above. Cows were humanely euthanized at 5 days post-inoculation (DPI; n = 1) and 18 DPI (n = 1). Tissues were collected at necropsy, and viral RNA loads were quantified from homogenized samples using RT-qPCR.

Chickens co-housed with IN inoculated cows

Female Cornish-cross broilers (n = 8) were co-housed with intranasally inoculated Holstein cows beginning at 1 DPI. All hens tested negative for influenza A(H5N1) viral RNA by RT-qPCR prior to cohabitation. Oral and cloacal swabs were collected and tested for the presence of influenza A(H5N1) RNA via RT-qPCR at DPI -1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 13, and 17. Hens were humanely euthanized at 7 DPI (n = 4) and 17 DPI (n = 3). One hen was euthanized prematurely on 10 DPI due to a co-morbidity unrelated to the study. Lung and tracheal tissues were collected at necropsy, and influenza A virus RNA loads were quantified using RT-qPCR.

Tissue homogenization

Frozen tissue samples were thawed at room temperature and transferred to TissueLyser II (Qiagen) homogenization cassettes. Samples were homogenized at 20 Hz for 60 s, followed by 30 Hz for 60 s. The cassettes were then flipped, and the homogenization cycle was repeated to ensure complete tissue disruption.

RT-qPCR for influenza A virus RNA load detection

RNA was extracted from biomaterials (e.g., milk, nasal swabs) and tissue homogenates using either the MagMAX™ Viral/Pathogen Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems) or the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Extracted RNA was tested for the presence of viral RNA using RT-qPCR with either the VetMAX™-Gold SIV Detection Kit (Applied Biosystems) or the Avian Influenza Type A (H5) Primers and Probe Set (Integrated DNA Technologies). Viral RNA copy numbers (copies/µL) were quantified using standard curves generated from serial fourfold dilutions of a positive control with known concentrations provided in the VetMAX™-Gold SIV Detection Kit. Samples with Ct values < 38 were considered positive. Reactions were performed on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System and analyzed using the a QuantStudio™ design and analysis software (Applied Biosystems, version 2.8.0).

Infectious virus titration by TCID50 assay

Biomaterials (e.g., milk, nasal swabs) and tissue homogenates with Ct values < 32 were subjected to infectious virus titration using a TCID50 assay in MDCK cells. Samples were diluted 1:10 in infection medium (EMEM supplemented with 0.3% bovine serum albumin [BSA] and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution [Gibco]), then serially tenfold diluted and plated (100 µL per well) in triplicate onto confluent MDCK cells (5 × 105 cells/well, plated the day prior in 96-well plates). After 1 h of adsorption, 100 µL of virus propagation medium (EMEM with 0.3% BSA, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, and 1 µg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin) was added. Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cytopathic effect (CPE) was assessed microscopically, and TCID50 titers were calculated using the Reed–Muench method.
