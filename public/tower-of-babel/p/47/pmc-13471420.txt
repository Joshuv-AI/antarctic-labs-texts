Structural and vascular characteristics of PHOMS in pediatric pseudopapilledema
Authors: Selda Celik Dulger, Fırat Uygur, Ismail Murat Seyhun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the structural and vascular characteristics of peripapillary hyperreflective ovoid mass-like structures (PHOMS) in pediatric pseudopapilledema and to investigate their relationship with retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness and optic nerve head parameters.MethodsThis retrospective study included 38 pediatric patients with pseudopapilledema (with or without optic disc drusen [ODD]) and 36 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Structural optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA) parameters were analyzed, including global and sectoral RNFL thickness, ganglion cell complex (GCC) metrics, Bruch’s membrane opening (BMO) diameter, choroidal vascularity index (CVI), radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) density, and macular vascular parameters. In PHOMS-positive eyes, maximum PHOMS diameter was measured on enhanced depth imaging OCT. Group comparisons were performed using generalized estimating equations. Correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate associations between PHOMS size and structural or vascular parameters.ResultsIn GEE analyses including all PHOMS-positive eyes (n = 40) and healthy control eyes (n = 72), global RNFL thickness was significantly greater in PHOMS-positive eyes (111.68 ± 22.68 vs. 97.55 ± 8.54 μm, p < 0.001). BMO diameter was significantly larger (p = 0.001) and FAZ area significantly smaller (p = 0.001) in PHOMS-positive eyes. Although RPC density differed between groups (p = 0.045), no consistent differences were observed in GCC thickness, CVI, or other OCTA-derived vascular parameters. Maximum PHOMS diameter showed a significant positive correlation with global RNFL thickness (r = 0.526, p = 0.010). In eye-level analyses including ODD-positive, ODD-negative, and healthy eyes, RNFL thickness differed significantly among groups (p < 0.001), whereas no consistent differences were observed in vascular or ganglion cell parameters.ConclusionsThe findings of this study suggest that PHOMS in pediatric pseudopapilledema are predominantly associated with structural optic nerve head alterations. Although differences were observed in selected OCTA-derived parameters, no uniform pattern of microvascular change was identified. The observed association between PHOMS size and RNFL thickness is consistent with the concept of localized axonal crowding and remodeling at the optic nerve head. Further longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are required to clarify the clinical significance of these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-05130-y.

Introduction

Peripapillary hyperreflective ovoid mass-like structures (PHOMS) are increasingly recognized on enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography (EDI-OCT) as peripapillary hyperreflective configurations located above the level of the Bruch’s membrane opening [1, 2]. Initially considered a variant or precursor of optic disc drusen (ODD), PHOMS are now defined by the Optic Disc Drusen Studies (ODDS) Consortium as a distinct morphologic entity characterized by lateral displacement and herniation of prelaminar nerve fibers [2].

PHOMS have been reported across a broad spectrum of clinical conditions, including optic disc drusen, tilted discs, myopia, papilledema, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH), and inflammatory optic neuropathies [1, 3, 4]. Population-based studies in children have demonstrated a prevalence of approximately 8–9% in otherwise healthy cohorts, with associations noted between PHOMS, axial elongation, and optic disc tilt [5, 6]. In adult populations, PHOMS have also been identified in large epidemiologic studies, further supporting their non–disease-specific nature [7].

The relationship between PHOMS and ODD remains controversial. While some pediatric studies suggest that PHOMS frequently coexist with deep ODD and may represent an early stage in drusen evolution [8], other reports confirm the presence of isolated PHOMS in the absence of detectable drusen or raised intracranial pressure [9]. Multimodal imaging, including B-scan ultrasonography and fundus autofluorescence, has been emphasized as essential for differentiating PHOMS from buried drusen, particularly in children where drusen may be difficult to visualize clinically [2].

Beyond their association with structural disc anomalies, PHOMS have also been linked to elevated intracranial pressure. In children with syndromic craniosynostosis, PHOMS were found to correlate strongly with elevated intracranial pressure and concurrent papilledema, and resolution was observed following surgical decompression [10, 11]. Similarly, studies in patients evaluated for suspected papilledema have demonstrated that PHOMS may occur in IIH but are not specific to this diagnosis [9].

PHOMS are increasingly considered a nonspecific OCT finding of axonal distension and crowding at the optic nerve head rather than a disease-specific entity. However, whether PHOMS are accompanied predominantly by localized structural alterations or by associated microvascular changes remains unclear, particularly in pediatric pseudopapilledema. Combined structural and OCTA-based evaluation of retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), ganglion cell complex (GCC), BMO (Bruch’s membrane opening) morphology, choroidal vascularity, and retinal microvascular parameters may therefore provide additional insight into the structural and vascular characteristics associated with PHOMS in pediatric pseudopapilledema.

The aim of the present study was therefore to evaluate structural and vascular characteristics of PHOMS in a pediatric pseudopapilledema cohort, with particular emphasis on RNFL thickness, PHOMS size, BMO diameter, and OCTA-derived vascular parameters.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included pediatric patients under 18 years of age with blurry disc margin who were followed in the Neuro-ophthalmology Unit between January 2023 and June 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara Etlik City Hospital Ethics Committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the retrospective design, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Patients were diagnosed with pseudopapilledema by an experienced neuro-ophthalmologist based on clinical examination, multimodal imaging findings, and stability of optic nerve head appearance during follow-up. Additional neuro-ophthalmologic investigations, including brain MRI, lumbar puncture, and visual field testing, were performed when clinically indicated, particularly in the presence of headache, visual symptoms, suspicious optic disc edema, or neurologic concern. In these cases, brain MRI findings and cerebrospinal fluid opening pressure measurements were within normal limits. All included patients had preserved visual acuity and clinically stable optic nerve appearance during follow-up. No progressive visual dysfunction suggestive of optic neuropathy was identified in the pseudopapilledema group. Patients with true papilledema, inflammatory or ischemic optic neuropathy, or uncertain diagnosis were excluded. ODD diagnosis was based on combined B-scan ultrasonography and EDI-OCT findings in accordance with the Optic Disc Drusen Studies Consortium recommendations [2]. On EDI-OCT, ODD were identified as hyporeflective structures with hyperreflective margins located above the lamina cribrosa. Hyperreflective foci on B-scan ultrasonography were present in all ODD cases. Fundus autofluorescence was performed in selected cases to support the diagnosis. Eyes with uncertain distinction between PHOMS and buried ODD were excluded (Fig. 1). None of the included patients had received systemic acetazolamide or other diuretic therapy before OCT/OCTA imaging.

A total of 38 patients with pseudopapilledema were included in the study. ODD was identified in 36 eyes of 18 patients. PHOMS were present in 40 eyes from 23 patients, including six eyes with coexisting ODD. For correlation analyses involving PHOMS size, one PHOMS-positive eye per patient was selected, resulting in 23 eyes. For eye-level analyses, all PHOMS-positive eyes were included.

Thirty-six age- and sex-matched healthy controls were included. Healthy control subjects had a BCVA of 20/20 or better, normal anterior and posterior segment examinations, normal visual fields, structurally normal optic nerve heads, and no history of ocular, neurologic, or systemic disease.

Exclusion criteria included age outside 5–18 years, incomplete clinical or imaging data, clinically significant media opacity, motion artifacts, spherical equivalent greater than ± 6.0 diopters, previous intraocular surgery, and any ocular, neurologic, or systemic disease affecting retinal or optic nerve structure. Images with poor centration, motion artifacts, segmentation errors, or insufficient image quality were excluded from analysis. All OCT and OCTA images were reviewed for segmentation accuracy before quantitative analysis. The study focused on pediatric patients aged 5–18 years because PHOMS and pseudopapilledema are diagnostically challenging in childhood, particularly in cases with buried ODD. To minimize potential age-related bias, control subjects were age-matched to the study group, and no significant age difference was observed between groups.

For correlation analyses involving PHOMS size, one eye per patient was included to avoid intra-subject dependency. In bilateral cases, the eye with the larger maximum PHOMS diameter was selected, whereas, in unilateral cases the affected eye was analyzed. One eye per control subject was randomly selected. Correlation analyses were therefore performed using a single-eye approach. For group comparisons between PHOMS-positive eyes and healthy controls, all eligible eyes were included and analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for inter-eye correlation.

For eye-level group comparisons evaluating pseudopapilledema subgroups, all eligible eyes were included in the analyses, comprising 36 eyes with ODD, 40 eyes from pseudopapilledema patients without ODD, and 72 eyes from healthy controls. To account for inter-eye correlation, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust standard errors were used.

All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examination including BCVA, refractive error, axial length (IOLMaster 500, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and dilated fundus examination.

The following parameters were included in the analysis: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), axial length (AL) and spherical equivalent (SE); structural OCT measurements including global and sectoral retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness, focal loss volume (FLV), global loss volume (GLV), Bruch’s membrane opening (BMO) diameter, and optic disc area; peripapillary choroidal thickness and choroidal vascularity index; and OCTA-derived vascular parameters.

Macular OCTA parameters included vessel density measurements of the superficial and deep capillary plexuses (whole image, foveal, parafoveal, and perifoveal regions), foveal avascular zone (FAZ) area, flow density (FD), and flow area measurements. Peripapillary vascular density was assessed using radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) measurements in sectoral analyses.

Image acquisition

All OCT examinations were performed after pupillary dilation and between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM to minimize diurnal variation, particularly for choroidal measurements [12].

Peripapillary RNFL thickness was measured using SD-OCT (Cirrus HD-OCT 5000, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) with an optic disc-centered scan protocol.

EDI-OCT and OCTA imaging were performed using the RTVue XR Avanti system (Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA, USA). Macular OCTA scans were obtained using a 6 × 6 mm cube centered on the fovea, and optic nerve head scans were obtained using a 4.5 × 4.5 mm cube centered on the disc. Superficial and deep capillary plexus vessel densities, radial peripapillary capillary density, foveal avascular zone area, and flow area parameters were automatically calculated using the device software. Only OCT and OCTA images with a signal strength index (SSI) ≥ 7 were included in the analysis. All scans were carefully reviewed for image quality, motion artifacts, centration errors, segmentation inaccuracies, and projection artifacts before quantitative analysis. Images with significant artifacts or insufficient quality were excluded. Projection artifacts were minimized using the device’s built-in correction algorithm.

The presence or absence of PHOMS was determined according to the Optic Disc Drusen Studies (ODDS) Consortium definition using optic disc-centered horizontal and vertical enhanced depth imaging (EDI) OCT scans [2]. PHOMS were defined as peripapillary hyperreflective ovoid mass-like structures located above the level of the Bruch’s membrane opening. For quantitative analysis, the horizontal and vertical diameters of each PHOMS were measured on EDI-OCT images. The larger of the horizontal and vertical diameters was defined as the maximum PHOMS diameter to represent PHOMS size (Fig. 2).

CVI was calculated on EDI-OCT images using Fiji software (ImageJ, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) following a previously described binarization method [13]. A standardized 1000-µm peripapillary choroidal region extending from the Bruch’s membrane opening was manually selected. Images were converted to 8-bit format and binarized using the Niblack auto-local threshold. CVI was defined as the ratio of luminal area to total choroidal area (Fig. 2).

Bruch’s membrane opening (BMO) diameter was measured on the horizontal EDI-OCT B-scan passing through the center of the optic disc, as previously described in optic nerve head morphometric studies [14]. BMO diameter was defined as the linear distance between the nasal and temporal edges of the Bruch’s membrane opening. In eyes with coexisting optic disc drusen (ODD), BMO measurements were not performed due to potential distortion of the Bruch’s membrane contour and difficulty in reliably identifying BMO margins (Fig. 2).

Manual measurements of PHOMS diameter, BMO diameter, and CVI were performed by the same observer using a predefined protocol. Intraobserver reproducibility was assessed in a randomly selected subset of eyes using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests together with graphical evaluation methods, including histograms and Q–Q plots. For smaller sample analyses, the Shapiro–Wilk test was preferred. Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared between two groups using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Correlations between normally distributed continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation analysis, whereas Spearman’s rank correlation was used for non-normally distributed variables.

Associations between categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test with Yates’ correction.

To account for inter-eye correlation, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable working correlation structure and robust standard errors were used for eye-level group comparisons of quantitative variables. In adjusted GEE models comparing PHOMS-positive eyes and healthy controls, RNFL thickness was adjusted for spherical equivalent, whereas GCC, CVI, RPC density, and macular OCTA-derived vascular parameters were adjusted for both spherical equivalent and global RNFL thickness. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

There was no significant difference between the groups in terms of sex distribution (female: 71.1% in the patient group vs. 69.4% in the control group, p = 1.000). The mean age was 12.8 ± 4.0 years in the patient group and 12.9 ± 3.8 years in the control group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.913). Correlation analyses

Correlation analyses were performed within the PHOMS-positive group (n = 23 eyes).

Maximum PHOMS diameter demonstrated a significant positive correlation with global RNFL thickness (r = 0.526, p = 0.010). No significant correlations were observed between maximum PHOMS diameter and BMO diameter, ganglion cell complex thickness, mean choroidal vascularity index (CVI), or peripapillary radial capillary density (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Within the PHOMS-positive group, BMO diameter was not significantly associated with optic disc area (r = 0.243, p = 0.348), RNFL thickness, maximum PHOMS diameter, or spherical equivalent (all p > 0.05). Group comparisons using GEE

In eye-level analyses including ODD-positive eyes, non-ODD pseudopapilledema eyes, and healthy controls, a significant difference was observed only for global RNFL thickness (p < 0.001). RNFL thickness was significantly higher in the ODD group (132.27 ± 30.36 μm) compared with both the non-ODD pseudopapilledema group (102.17 ± 16.08 μm) and healthy controls (97.55 ± 8.54 μm). No significant difference was found between the non-ODD and healthy control groups. No statistically significant differences were observed among groups for mean CVI, average GCC thickness, or peripapillary radial capillary density (all p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Supplementary analyses

Additional structural and vascular parameters were analyzed using GEE with Bonferroni correction and are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

FLV values were higher in the ODD group (p = 0.010), whereas GLV and average GCC thickness did not differ significantly.

Among vascular parameters, most macular and peripapillary OCTA metrics did not differ significantly across groups. A sectoral difference was observed in nasal-inferior RPC (p = 0.006); however, no consistent vascular pattern was observed across sectors.

Sectoral RNFL measurements were significantly higher in the ODD group across multiple quadrants (all p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this pediatric pseudopapilledema cohort, PHOMS-positive eyes demonstrated increased RNFL thickness, larger BMO diameter, and smaller FAZ area compared with healthy controls. Although differences were also observed in RPC density, no consistent pattern of vascular alteration was identified across the evaluated OCTA-derived parameters. In addition, eye-level analyses comparing ODD-positive, non-ODD pseudopapilledema, and healthy control eyes similarly demonstrated RNFL differences in the absence of consistent GCC or vascular alterations. These observations suggest that PHOMS may be more closely related to localized structural remodeling than to diffuse vascular alteration.

Our findings are generally consistent with previous pediatric and myopic PHOMS studies reporting increased RNFL thickness in association with PHOMS [8, 15]. In highly myopic eyes, larger PHOMS area has been associated with increased RNFL thickness [15]. Similarly, pediatric cohort studies have demonstrated PHOMS in otherwise healthy children, frequently in association with optic disc configuration and myopia [5, 6]. In the present study, PHOMS-positive eyes also demonstrated increased RNFL thickness without significant differences in average GCC thickness.

PHOMS were initially described as peripapillary hyperreflective configurations above the level of the Bruch’s membrane opening and are now recognized as a distinct morphologic entity separate from optic disc drusen (ODD) [1, 2]. Importantly, PHOMS have been observed in both optic disc edema and pseudopapilledema, supporting the notion that they are not specific to a single underlying pathology [16]. However, their relationship with ODD remains debated. A recent pediatric study reported that PHOMS were consistently observed in conjunction with deep or superficial ODD and suggested that RNFL thickening may reflect early drusen stages, with subsequent thinning as drusen become superficial [8]. In most PHOMS-positive eyes in our cohort, clinically detectable ODD was absent, and RNFL thickening persisted independently of visible drusen. Furthermore, ultrasonographic differentiation studies emphasize that PHOMS and ODD represent structurally distinct entities, even when coexisting [4]. Together, these findings support the concept that PHOMS can occur as an isolated structural configuration rather than merely as a secondary manifestation of buried drusen.

The vascular findings in PHOMS remain heterogeneous across the literature. Borrelli et al. first demonstrated vascular flow signals within PHOMS using OCTA, suggesting that PHOMS may contain intrinsic vascular components [17]. Some OCTA studies have reported reduced peripapillary vessel density or altered radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) signals in eyes with large PHOMS [3], whereas others have demonstrated preserved or even increased peripapillary vascular parameters despite structural optic nerve head alterations [10]. Kirik et al. evaluated peripapillary choroidal vascularity in myopic pediatric eyes with PHOMS and reported reduced choroidal area parameters with relatively higher CVI values [18]. In our cohort, FAZ area was smaller and RPC density was lower in PHOMS-positive eyes, whereas CVI, SCP, and DCP vessel density measurements remained comparable between groups. Similarly, ODD-positive eyes demonstrated RNFL alterations; however, unlike PHOMS-positive eyes, no significant differences were observed in the evaluated vascular parameters.

Recently, Xiao et al. evaluated adolescents with PHOMS using swept-source OCTA and reported increased RPC vessel density, particularly in eyes with larger PHOMS, together with visible blood flow signals within PHOMS lesions [19]. In contrast, PHOMS-positive eyes in our cohort demonstrated lower RPC density compared with healthy controls. The reasons for this discrepancy remain uncertain but may relate to differences in study populations, including age and refractive characteristics, as well as differences in OCTA technology, image acquisition, and segmentation protocols. Moreover, the absence of consistent changes in CVI, SCP, and DCP measurements suggests that any vascular alterations associated with PHOMS may be localized rather than indicative of diffuse microvascular involvement.

Clinically, the predominance of structural alterations, including increased RNFL thickness and larger BMO diameter, in the absence of ganglion cell loss and widespread vascular abnormalities may help differentiate PHOMS from early optic neuropathies and reduce unnecessary diagnostic escalation in asymptomatic pediatric patients.

An additional strength of the present study is the comprehensive multimodal evaluation of PHOMS-related structural and vascular characteristics within the same pediatric cohort. In addition to RNFL measurements, we simultaneously assessed GCC parameters, macular and peripapillary OCTA-derived vascular metrics, and choroidal vascularity indices, allowing a broader evaluation of both neural and microvascular features associated with PHOMS.

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design and relatively small sample size. Although the sample size is modest, the use of GEE and consistent findings across analyses provide some support for the robustness of the main observations. BMO measurements were not performed in ODD-positive eyes because of potential distortion of Bruch’s membrane margins. Although OCT and OCTA images were carefully reviewed for segmentation accuracy and image quality, subtle segmentation errors or imaging artifacts may still have influenced quantitative measurements. OCTA measurements in pediatric populations may also be affected by device-related artifacts and the absence of magnification correction. In addition, the inclusion of a broad pediatric age range may have introduced variability related to ocular growth, refractive development, and optic nerve head biomechanics. Finally, longitudinal studies with larger pediatric cohorts are needed to clarify the structural and vascular significance of PHOMS over time.

In conclusion, the findings of this study suggest that PHOMS in pediatric pseudopapilledema are predominantly associated with structural optic nerve head alterations. Although differences were observed in selected OCTA-derived parameters, including FAZ area and RPC density, no uniform pattern of microvascular change was identified. The observed association between PHOMS size and RNFL thickness is consistent with the concept of localized axonal crowding and remodeling at the optic nerve head. Further longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are required to clarify the clinical significance of these findings.
