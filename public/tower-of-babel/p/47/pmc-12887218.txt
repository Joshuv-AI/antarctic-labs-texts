Human Variant in the Cardiac Troponin I Switch Domain Causes Diastolic Dysfunction in a Novel Mouse Model
Authors: Elizabeth Silver, Jason M Duran, Betul Beyza Gunes, Axel J Fenwick, Sofie Maisel, Tsui‐Min Wang, Ana Maria Manso, Marina Sampaio Cruz, Yusu Gu, Joshua Fong, Aysen Shathaya, Chao Chen, Angel Soto‐Hermida, Wei Feng, Yoshitake Cho, Julius Bogomolovas, Jill M Gelow, Ahmad Masri, Kelly A Frazer, Robert S Ross, Ju Chen, Catherine A Makarewich, Anthony Cammarato, Andrew D McCulloch, Paul Bushway, Eric D Adler
Published: 2025

Cardiac troponin I (cTnI), encoded by TNNI3, is a highly conserved subunit of the troponin complex that inhibits contraction by blocking actin–myosin interaction. 1 The cTnI switch helix (residues 149–160) binds actin when calcium is absent and troponin C when calcium is present, facilitating tropomyosin movement and cross‐bridge formation. 1 , 2 Pathogenic TNNI3 mutations are associated with diverse phenotypes, including hypertrophic and restrictive cardiomyopathies, even within a family carrying the same genetic variant. 3 Currently, no targeted therapies exist for thin‐filament cardiomyopathies. Animal models have clarified TNNI3‐related disease 4 ; however, no models with switch‐domain mutations have been studied.

A known pathogenic switch‐domain variant is alanine‐to‐valine substitution at residue 157 (A157V, ClinVar ID: 43388). A family with heterozygous A157V cTnI and associated cardiomyopathy, including 2 cardiac transplant recipients, was identified through clinical practice with informed consent obtained under IRB protocols.

To model this variant, a knock‐in mouse was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 to introduce the point mutation in Tnni3 (c.470C>T, A158V) in C57BL/6J mice. The mouse cTnI sequence contains an additional amino acid, making A158V equivalent to human A157V. All mouse protocols were International Animal Care and Use Committee‐approved. Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Serial transthoracic echocardiography was performed to evaluate the effect of A158V on cardiac function. There were no significant differences in any echocardiographic parameters between homozygous A158V (A158VHom) and wild‐type (WT) mice at any age (Figure [A]).

Invasive hemodynamics confirmed preserved systolic function in both young (3–4 months) and old mice (12–14 months) mice, with no differences in maximum derivative of pressure over time (dP/dT) by genotype (Figure [B], left panel). However, minimum dP/dT was significantly impaired in both young heterozygous (A158VHet) and A158VHom mice compared with WT controls and worsening with age in A158VHom (Figure [B], middle panel). Old A158VHom mice also demonstrated significantly reduced maximum left ventricular pressures versus WT at 2 μg/kg per min dobutamine with a trend towards lower pressures at higher doses (Figure [B], right panel). Baseline left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure did not differ, but longitudinal analysis showed an increased decline in minimum dP/dT with age in A158VHom mice beyond the decline observed in WT mice. No significant fibrosis was present on Masson’s Trichome‐staining in any group (Figure [C]). Overall, these results suggest A158VHom mice develop relaxation impairment without fibrosis that worsens with age.

To assess sarcomere‐level effects of A158V, ventricular myofibrils from old mice were studied using the fast solution switching method. A158VHom myofibrils showed prolonged linear relaxation versus WT myofibrils (0.202 versus 0.167 s, respectively, P=0.005) with no significant differences in active tension (45.5 versus 41.1 mN/mm2, respectively, P=0.091) (Figure [D]). These findings support in vivo evidence of impaired relaxation without systolic dysfunction.

Phosphorylation of cTnI at Ser23/24 (pTnI) by protein kinase A through β‐adrenergic stimulus promotes cardiac relaxation. 5 Western blot of pTnI and cTnI showed a trend towards reduced pTnI in A158VHom mice compared with WT (P=0.064, Figure [E] and [F]). Decreased Ser23/24 phosphorylation due to blunted β‐adrenergic response has been linked to diastolic dysfunction in TNNI3‐cardiomyopathy models 5 and may contribute to impaired cardiac relaxation in this model.

Bulk RNA‐sequencing from left ventricular tissue from aged mice revealed genotype‐dependent transcriptional changes. Principal component analysis demonstrated clear separation between A158VHom and WT, with A158VHet clustering intermediately (Figure [G]). Differential expression identified 351 significantly altered protein‐coding genes in A158VHom versus WT. Stress‐ and inflammation‐related genes (Timp1, Serpina1, Serpinb1a, Lcn2) were upregulated, while regulators of structure and metabolism (Atcay, Tmem150c, and Tchp) were downregulated (Figure [H]). Gene set enrichment analysis showed activation of energy metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation pathways and suppression of contraction‐related pathways in A158VHom (Figure [I]). Gene‐level contributor plots from enriched pathways supported a graded effect across genotypes: A158VHom diverged most from WT, while A158VHet showed intermediate changes that were directionally concordant with A158VHom but attenuated (Figure [J]), consistent with the intermediate hemodynamic phenotype (Figure [B], middle panel). In contrast, few genes were differentially expressed between A158VHet and WT, limited to Tnc upregulation (matricellular remodeling), and Acot10 downregulation (fatty acid metabolism). Comparison between A158VHom and A158VHet revealed higher Acot10 expression in A158VHom, but overall few genes differed between these groups. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the A158VHom mutation drives distinct transcriptional remodeling despite the absence of structural changes detectable by echocardiography or histology, suggesting that transcriptome changes in myocardial energetics and contraction may directly impact hemodynamic changes that precede pathological findings in the myocardium.

In summary, the murine Tnni3 A158V mutation, homologous to human A157V associated with familial cardiomyopathy, produces impaired left ventricular filling without hypertrophy. Functional differences included reduced minimum dP/dT, prolonged myofibril relaxation, and transcriptional variation with activation of metabolic pathways and suppression of contraction‐related programs. This model highlights that the A158V mutation alone can drive molecular remodeling in the absence of overt structural disease. Because genetic models of diastolic dysfunction are uncommon, this mouse model may be broadly useful for studying diastolic disorders and investigating how cTnI switch‐domain variants impair cardiac relaxation.
