A combined PIE-FRET and FCS assay to monitor RNA dynamics and cleavage by SARS-CoV-2 Nsp15
Authors: Kenya Gordon, Zoe M Wright, Cameron R Stephens, Meredith N Frazier, Benjamin S Clark, Isha M Wilson, Irene Silvernail, Robin E Stanley, Sharonda J LeBlanc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNsp15 is an endoribonuclease, highly conserved among coronaviruses, that helps the virus evade detection in host cells by cleaving uridine-rich viral RNA sequences. Those sequences would otherwise trigger immune response pathways. Its essential role in pathogenesis and highly conserved nature make Nsp15 an attractive target for therapeutic intervention. While its crystal structure and uridine specificity are well-established, the influence of RNA structural dynamics and divalent cations on Nsp15 activity remains less understood. Leveraging single-molecule Pulsed Interleaved Excitation (PIE)-FRET and Fluorescence Correlation Spectroscopy (FCS) in combination, we developed an assay to track RNA cleavage by Nsp15 variants in real time and monitor the conformational dynamics of hybrid RNA/DNA substrates. Using our methodology and analysis strategies, we obtained clear indicators of RNA cleavage with both PIE-FRET and FCS data analysis. Our assay also revealed signatures of unique dynamic behavior in uridine-containing RNA substrates, indicating that divalent cations enhance substrate flexibility, which is associated with a faster observed reaction rate of RNA cleavage by Nsp15 in the presence of Mn2+. We propose that divalent cations induce a conformation that may be more favorable for cleavage in single-stranded RNA substrates that mediate the efficient nuclease activity of Nsp15.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52571-y.Keywords: FRET, FCS, Nsp15, RNA dynamics, Single-moleculeSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology

Introduction

Several human coronaviruses (CoVs) cause serious human diseases including severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), Middle East respiratory syndrome (MERS), and most recently SARS-CoV-21–3. These CoVs are structurally composed of nucleocapsid proteins that assemble into a capsid structure encasing the viral genetic material with spike proteins protruding from a bilayer envelope that allow the viruses to recognize and invade target host cells4. As positive sense RNA viruses, CoVs contain single-stranded RNA that can be immediately translated when released into the host cell cytoplasm. The products of translation, polyproteins pp1a and pp1ab, then undergo proteolysis yielding 16 non-structural proteins (Nsps) that are responsible for a variety of functions including degrading and inhibiting translation of host RNA5, generating new replication sites by modifying the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of the host6, and synthesizing more copies of viral RNA that are used to produce additional virions7.

Nsp15, one of the better conserved nonstructural proteins in coronaviruses8, is a homohexameric endoribonuclease that aids in the evasion of host immune systems by cleaving viral RNA during processing. The double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) intermediates produced during processing of the viral genome are known to elicit an immune response in host cells9, but fragmentation of these viral RNA sequences by Nsp15 disrupts the immune response signaling pathways making viral replication more efficient10,11. Animal studies have shown that when CoVs are Nsp15-deficient, the immune response of the animal host is more effective against the viral infection, suggesting that Nsp15 plays an important role in supporting the proliferation of coronaviruses in host cells12. Thus, a better understanding of the enzymatic activity of Nsp15 may provide insight with applications for CoV therapies.

Nsp15 primarily cleaves 3’ of uridine (U) in RNA substrates, though the sites in coronaviral genomic and/or subgenomic RNA that are targeted by Nsp15 have been debated for over a decade8,13,14. Directly detecting cleavage products of Nsp15 during infection by coronaviruses that affect humans is prohibitively difficult, due to (1) the presence of multiple competing ribonucleases expressed by both the virus and host, and (2) the level of biohazard risk associated with culturing highly infectious pathogens. Characterization approaches that work around these challenges, such as studying Nsp15 homologs in non-human coronaviruses or recombinant Nsp15 overexpressed in non-pathogenic bacteria, have provided critically important windows into Nsp15’s mechanism of action, though each model system is necessarily limited in the information it can provide. Thus, multiple complementary experimental approaches are necessary to build a complete understanding of this protein.

Existing literature paints a picture of Nsp15 as a highly modular ribonuclease with a broad scope of possible substrates. In vivo models of infection (using cells infected with a mouse coronavirus, MHV) have detected Nsp15 cleavage sites in both the 5’ polyU tail of the negative strand11 and regulatory regions of the positive strand of viral genomic RNA15. In vitro biochemical assays using purified recombinant SARS-CoV-2 Nsp15 have shown that Nsp15 can act on a variety of RNA substrates, including dinucleotides16, short17 and long single-stranded RNA (ssRNA)18, and dsRNA19– though Nsp15 will cleave at different Us even in the same sequence of RNA, depending on whether that RNA is single- or double-stranded19. Structural studies using X-ray crystallography20 and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)21 have revealed that Nsp15’s active site is small and accommodates only the target U. Further, with the exception of the target U in the active site, Nsp15 forms only non-sequence specific interactions with dsRNA14,19,22. To bind dsRNA, Nsp15 uses modular H-bonding and electrostatic interactions with the RNA sugar phosphate backbone, and hydrophobic and aromatic interactions with the bases of the target U and its immediate neighbors. In fact, the binding efficiency of Nsp15 to dsRNA is not significantly affected by the absence of Us in the dsRNA23, due to these non-sequence specific interaction modes. No structural data to date has captured interactions between Nsp15 and more than a few nucleotides of ssRNA17,18,21, suggesting interactions with ssRNA are likely dynamic, variable, and/or transient. This body of literature has recently begun to converge on RNA structure and spontaneous RNA dynamics as critically important signals used by Nsp15 to select its cleavage targets14,23. For example, a recent study observed a correlation between spontaneous solvent accessibility of a U in dsRNA (assessed by NMR spectroscopy) and cleavage efficiency of that U by Nsp1514. However, direct measurements of RNA dynamics in the absence and presence of Nsp15 have not been reported.

An additional ongoing question in the literature concerns the role of Mn2+ ions in Nsp15’s cleavage efficiency. While millimolar concentrations of Mn2+ increase the catalytic activity of Nsp1524, Mn-induced conformational changes of Nsp15 have not been observed in spectrographic assays16. Recent cryo-EM structures of Nsp15-substrate complexes also show no evidence of manganese within the Nsp15 active site17,21, leaving the specific role of the manganese cofactor undetermined. The finding that the manganese co-factor is not implicated in direct interaction with Nsp1517,21 suggests that divalent ion interactions with the RNA substrate itself should not be overlooked. This is further supported by recent analyses that demonstrate Nsp15’s reliance on metal ions is substrate dependent, having a greater impact on ssRNA than dsRNA18, and essentially no impact on dinucleotides16. Divalent metal cations are known to impact RNA structure and catalyze cleavage and binding reactions25–27, and cations have been observed interacting with nucleotides both by binding site-specifically as well as contributing to atmospheric binding28,29. In addition, while interactions between metal ions and RNA nucleobases have been shown to stabilize RNA structure for cleavage by other RNA nucleases30–32, the roles of ion environment in RNA substrate conformational dynamics and cleavage target recognition are not well studied for Nsp15.

To monitor the dynamics33–35 of RNA substrates and its interactions with Nsp15, we employed simultaneous Fluorescence Correlation Spectroscopy (FCS) and Pulsed Interleaved Excitation Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (PIE-FRET). These experiments were informed by previous cryo-EM and biochemical studies17,21, and provide an important complement to those techniques. FCS is a technique that utilizes intensity fluctuations of freely diffusing fluorescent reporters to experimentally characterize molecular dynamics such as diffusion36–39. Measuring FRET efficiency at the single molecule level allows for nanoscale spatial resolution and sub-millisecond time resolution of conformational changes and transient biological events40,41. PIE is a method by which two pulsed lasers excite the donor and acceptor of a FRET pair in an alternating pattern42,43. PIE-FRET allows for the detection of conformational states and dynamics of biomolecules diffusing in solution. Combined, the measurements provide the spatial and temporal resolution necessary to probe the transient dynamics of the RNA cleavage targets for Nsp15. Distinct from traditional biochemical assays, we can simultaneously track the subpopulations produced as cleavage of the RNA substrate occurs in the presence of Nsp15 variants, providing valuable insights into the role of substrate dynamics and divalent cations in the cleavage reaction. Interestingly, our biophysical approach enabled detection of uridine-dependent microsecond dynamics in an RNA substrate that are influenced by the presence of divalent cations and may contribute to specificity of Nsp15 for cleaving uridine.

Our approach builds on established FRET-FCS strategies for resolving conformational fluctuations in biomolecules44–48. Cross-correlation FCS46 and FRET-FCS have been used to detect dynamic exchange between fluorescent states and to distinguish genuine conformational dynamics from diffusion and photophysical effects47,48. More recent reviews have further emphasized the value of FRET-FCS for extracting kinetic information from heterogeneous systems44. To relate the observed FRET populations to structural models, we also used quantitative distance reconstruction with FRETraj49, an accessible-volume-based approach widely used to convert FRET efficiencies into structural constraints while accounting for dye mobility and linker geometry. This framework is especially important for RNA substrates, which can exhibit intrinsic sequence-dependent conformational dynamics on fast timescales even in the absence of protein, as recently shown by Nüesch et al.50. In parallel, structural studies of SARS-CoV-2 Nsp15 have established a mechanistic basis for uridine recognition and base flipping14,17,21, while also highlighting that RNA context and substrate conformation are central to specificity. Together, these prior studies motivate a solution-based, time-resolved FRET-FCS approach for disentangling intrinsic RNA motions from Nsp15-dependent and ion-dependent changes in substrate conformational behavior.

Materials and methods

Protein expression and purification

SARS-CoV-2 Nsp15 plasmids (WT, W333A, and H235A) were created as described in prior work21.Transformed C41(DE3) Escherichia coli (E. coli) competent cells were used to inoculate 10 mL of Terrific Broth (TB) containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin and grown at 37 °C and 210 rpm to an optical density (OD) of 0.8. The starter culture was transferred to 1 L of TB containing 100 µg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C and 210 rpm. At an OD of 0.2, the temperature was lowered to 16 °C and after reaching target temperature, cells were induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). The cells expressed Nsp15 for ~ 16 h overnight before harvesting. Cells were stored at −80 °C until purification.

To begin purification, pelleted cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM βME, 5 mM imidazole). A Roche cOmplete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor tablet (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, 11873580001) and 2 drops of Triton X were added to the cell slurry. Cell slurry was then sonicated for a total time of 6.5 min. After centrifugation for 50 min at 15,000 rpm and 4 °C, the cleared lysate was recovered. 2 mL of TALON metal affinity resin was equilibrated in lysis buffer and incubated with the cleared lysate for 45 min at 4 °C. The solution was poured into a 10 mL gravity column and the beads were washed with lysis buffer. Elution buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 250 mM imidazole) was used to elute his-tagged Nsp15 from the TALON resin, and the elutions containing protein were combined and concentrated by centrifuging for 10 min at 3000 g. Nsp15 was then buffer exchanged with a PD-10 gravity column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, 17–0851-01) into a low salt buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol) prior to thrombin cleavage. Nsp15 in low salt buffer was supplemented with 2 mM βME, 2 mM CaCl2, and 20 units of thrombin (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, T7513). The cleavage reaction was incubated for 4 h on a shaker at room temperature. Afterwards, phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) was added to quench the cleavage reaction, and the sample was passed through TALON resin after the beads were re-equilibrated in low salt buffer. A Superdex-200 column equilibrated in size exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM βME, 5 mM MnCl2) was used to isolate active Nsp15 hexamers. SDS-PAGE gels confirmed the presence of Nsp15 (monomer MW ~ 42 kDa).

Sample preparation and data acquisition

Nsp15 and fluorescently labeled RNA/DNA substrates were diluted to 5 nM in cleavage buffer (30 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 5 mM of divalent cation chloride solution). 40 µL droplets were then pipetted onto a coverslip, and to collect data the laser spot was focused 40 μm into the droplet. Acquisition times were 60–120 s, and samples were kept on ice between measurements that were taken at room temperature. For all experiments, the laser power density ranged from 2.4 to 12 kW/cm2.

Time-resolved fluorescence microscopy and spectroscopy

Time-resolved fluorescence measurements were collected using a custom MicroTime 200 (PicoQuant, Berlin, Germany) with SymPhoTime64 software for data acquisition and analysis. The modular time-resolved confocal microscope system is built around an inverted Olympus IX-83 microscope body with a side port for optics and a piezoelectric z stage. It is equipped with three picosecond pulsed diode lasers (485, 531, and 636 nm) and a laser driver module (SEPIA II) capable of operating in pulsed (up to 80 MHz) and continuous wave (cw) modes. The lasers are cleaned up with narrowband filters, fiber-coupled into the main optical unit (MOU), directed to a main quad-band dichroic mirror, and focused onto the sample with a UPlanSApo – Superchromat 60 × 1.2 numerical aperture (NA) water immersion lens (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). A fast galvo beam scanning module (FLIMbee) with a 0.5 ms dwell time (lower limit) is used for laser beam scanning. Fluorescence from the sample is collected with the same objective and spatially filtered through an exchangeable circular confocal pinhole (100 mm used for these experiments) and directed to one or two single photon avalanche diodes (SPADs – Excelitas). For Förster/fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) experiments with 531 and 636 nm laser excitation, the fluorescence is spectrally filtered using a dichroic mirror (635 long pass) placed between the two detectors and bandpass filters in front of each detector to separate donor (582/64, Channel 2) and acceptor (690/70, Channel 1) emission. For TCSPC, a multichannel event timer with 10 ps resolution and 650 ps deadtime (MultiHarp 150) in time-tagged time-resolved (TTTR) measurement mode applies time tags to individual photons detected at each SPAD, both relative to the laser pulse (microtime/nanotime) and relative to the start of the experiment (macrotime). Generating real-time histograms of the different time tags enables simultaneous fluorescence intensity, lifetime, and correlation data collection.

Operating the lasers in pulsed interleaved excitation (PIE) mode enables selective excitation and detection of separate donor, acceptor, and FRET signals on the nanosecond (ns) time scale. The laser excitation is synchronized in the SymPhoTime software such that the 531 and 636 nm lasers pulse in an alternating manner, successively exciting acceptor and donor molecules directly. Detected single photons are associated with the exciting laser pulse and the arrival detector to separate donor, acceptor, and FRET signals.

Fluorescence lifetime correlation spectroscopy (FLCS) background correction

Before correlation, we applied FLCS62 background correction to each FCS curve with a custom Python script. This technique facilitates the statistical separation of fluorescence contributions on a single-photon level. Statistical weights were calculated for each detected photon based on the normalized decay patterns of the sample and the background. We modeled the total decay curve, D, as a linear combination of two contributions: Species A (the sample fluorescence) and Species B (the constant background and detector afterpulsing). To define these patterns for each signal (IGG, IGR, IRR), we utilized a tailfitting approach where the portion of the decay following the peak intensity was fit to a linear combination of a mono- or bi-exponential decay plus a constant offset. The constant offset was assumed to be evenly distributed across all microtime channels to account for detector afterpulsing. Following the FLCS framework, we constructed a filter function that assigns a specific weight to each photon based on its microtime channel. To ensure the model focused on the fluorescence lifetime signatures, weights for microtimes preceding the peak intensity were set to zero. These statistical filters were then applied during the software correlation to correct background and detector afterpulsing contributions to the FCS correlations.

Pulsed interleaved excitation Förster resonance energy transfer (PIE-FRET)

FRET relies on the distance-dependent intensity or fluorescence lifetime changes of fluorescent reporters due to non-radiative energy transfer to report on the spatial separation between the fluorescent molecules63. The efficiency of that energy transfer depends on the fluorophore separation distance, d, as EFRET = [1+(d/R0)6]− 1, where R0 is the Förster radius, the distance at which a specific FRET pair achieves 50% FRET efficiency64. In pulsed interleaved excitation (PIE), picosecond pulsed diode lasers alternate pulses on the nanosecond timescale. Photons are collected for each excitation window allowing data to be recorded for both donor and acceptor excitation wavelengths quasi-simultaneously. The sample fluorescence is allowed to decay completely before excitation by the next laser pulse to minimize crosstalk. Using PIE, low-FRET populations can be distinguished from donor-only populations by filtering for acceptor photons that are collected during donor excitation65, and more complex samples containing mixed populations or dynamic FRET states can be monitored more effectively.

Structural modeling and FRET prediction

To model the RNA/DNA hybrid, initial structural models for the single-stranded(ss) RNA, ssRNA + Nsp15, and DNA anchor components were generated using Alphafold 370. For the Nsp15-RNA structural modeling, only a single monomer unit was included. We wrote a custom Python code to join and refine these models using the Rosetta software suite71. High-resolution refinement and energy scoring were performed via RosettaScripts72 using the FastRelax protocol. All simulations utilized the ref2015 all-atom energy function73 to optimize the RNA-DNA hybrid interface. A total of 25 independent structural trajectories were generated, and the score for each model is given in Rosetta Energy Units (REU). FRETraj49 was used to model the position of dyes and their accessible volumes, followed by the FRET efficiency calculation. The applied FRETraj parameters are shown in Supplementary Table S14.

Results

PIE-FRET and FCS report on cleavage and conformational dynamics of U-containing substrates

In this work, we used diffusion-based single-molecule spectroscopy to monitor the cleavage of RNA substrates by SARS-CoV-2 Nsp15, towards a more in-depth understanding of the cleavage mechanism. We developed a versatile fluorescence-based assay by designing donor-acceptor labeled RNA/DNA hybrid substrates (HFA, HFU, MFA, MFU, Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S1) to investigate the impact of Nsp15 mutation on cleavage efficiency, and the influence of divalent cation environment on the reaction. The ability to measure PIE-FRET and FCS on these substrates simultaneously enables reporting cleavage by observing a shift in PIE-FRET efficiency, and real time tracking of reaction subpopulations based on size (Fig. 1D). We isolated and correlated the donor (IGG), acceptor (IRR), or FRET (IGR) signals and applied FCS fitting and time-resolved analysis of the PIE-FRET data. Our analysis suggests that RNA substrate dynamics contribute to uridine discrimination and cleavage.

In all experiments, the hybrid RNA/DNA oligo has an acceptor on the 5’ RNA end and the complementary DNA oligo facilitates addition of a donor molecule and a biotin site for future surface attached studies. Upon annealing, the 10-base separation between the carefully selected donor-acceptor FRET pair (Atto550-Atto647N) leads to a high FRET signal (E ~ 1, Fig. 2A) for HFA/HFU. The 18-base separation between the donor-acceptor FRET pair (AF546-Atto647N) for MFA/MFU substrates was designed to be sensitive to dynamic changes in the RNA with a mid-FRET signal (0.4 < E < 0.8). Importantly, all experiments were conducted on diffusing molecules, making sample preparation straightforward.

To explore nucleotide base specificity of Nsp15, high-FRET substrates containing either an adenine (HFA) or uridine (HFU) in the RNA sequence (Fig. 1A, B, Supplementary Table S1) were incubated with equimolar wildtype Nsp15 and monitored while diffusing in solution for 2 hours. Previous work showed that Nsp15 has a strong specificity for cleaving RNA 3’ of uridines11,17. Substrates were diluted to 5 nM and PIE-FRET and FCS were collected simultaneously on a droplet of diffusing molecules (Fig. 1C) to report cleavage by Nsp15. First, 40 µL of the substrate-protein solution was pipetted onto a thin coverslip and excited via PIE at 20 MHz, and three consecutive 90-second measurements were collected. Triplicate 90-second measurements were then taken every 10 min for 2 h. Upon cleavage by Nsp15, separation of the donor and acceptor should result in the emergence of donor-only and acceptor-only populations in two-dimensional (2D) FRET efficiency (E, Eq. 16) - photon stoichiometry (S, Eq. 14) histograms52. We will refer to those plots as 2D E-S histograms. For acceptor-only labeled molecules S = 0, for donor-only labeled molecules S = 1, and for donor-acceptor pairs S = 0.5. In practice, 0.3 < S < 0.8 can be attributed to single donor-acceptor labeled species74. Over the course of the 2-hour reaction, multiple species may be present in the solution including unannealed substate, annealed substate, cleaved substrate, Nsp15-RNA bound complexes, and the cleaved RNA product (Fig. 1D). Using the combination of PIE-FRET analysis and FCS fitting to a global model we can track all fluorescently labeled species over the course of the experiment.

PIE-FRET efficiency histograms of diffusing RNA substrates report cleavage by Nsp15 as a binary assay (high FRET = annealed/un-cleaved and no FRET = cleaved). Figure 2A shows the PIE-FRET efficiency histograms for the annealed intact and cleaved HFA and HFU substrates. Each 2D E-S histogram is the result of thousands of fluorescently labeled RNA/DNA molecules diffusing through the confocal volume over the course of the acquisition. As expected for the high-FRET substrates, the FRET efficiency histogram for annealed HFA and HFU substates is initially peaked near E = 1, consistent with the 10-base separation between fluorescent reporters. After 2 h of incubation with WT Nsp15, the 2D E-S histogram for HFA remains unchanged while that of HFU shows donor-only and acceptor-only subpopulations emerge at S > 0.8, and S < 0.2, respectively. A control experiment containing a mixture of single-stranded donor-only and acceptor-only HFU oligos shows a similar result without the S = 0.5 population (Supplementary Figure S2A). The decreased subpopulation at E ~ 1, S = 0.5 for HFU is due to spatial separation of the FRET pair and indicates cleavage of the RNA. Fluorescence lifetime analysis also indicates cleavage by monitoring the change in the donor fluorescence lifetime of the entire ensemble over the course of an acquisition (Supplementary Figure S3). HFA remains un-cleaved, consistent with the preference of Nsp15 for uridine as a cleavage site as observed previously17.

Along with PIE-FRET data that reports cleavage, we obtain simultaneous FCS data. FCS measures fluorescence intensity fluctuations of reporter molecules as they diffuse through the confocal observation volume. The characteristic time scales over which these fluctuations occur correspond to specific types of dynamic behavior including diffusion at millisecond timescales, triplet and conformational dynamics at microsecond timescales, and rotational dynamics at nanosecond timescales75. FCS analysis can be used to extract specific parameters related to biomolecules of interest including concentration, translational diffusion time, and interconversion time between states by means of signal correlation followed by theoretical modeling54,76. A decay in the correlation curve characterizes dynamics in the fluorescently labeled species across the different timescales54.

FCS correlation curves obtained for WT Nsp15 incubated with HFU at 0 min and 120 min are shown in Fig. 2B. The raw data is correlated using Eq. 1 (autocorrelation) or Eq. 2 (cross-correlation) and is indicated with closed circles, triangles, or squares while the fits are shown as solid or dashed lines. Each correlation was corrected for background and detector afterpulsing using FLCS as described in Materials and Methods. The data were fit globally for several timepoints using Eqs. 8, 9, and 10. The global fitting model can account for the presence of annealed/uncleaved (A), cleaved (C), and unannealed (U) species in solution over the course of the experiment as described in Materials and Methods. Fitting four correlation functions simultaneously [, , , and ] is a robust method for distinguishing conformational dynamics from diffusion and triplet state kinetics in a substrate labeled with a FRET pair48. When PIE mode is applied, and autocorrelations separately detect the donor- and acceptor- labeled species (annealed + unannealed + cleaved), while the cross-correlations track the double labeled species (annealed). Tying parameters such as translational diffusion time (τD), triplet fraction (fT), triplet relaxation time (τT), conformational relaxation time (τC), and average molecule number (N), across all fits enables separation of different sources of fluorescence intensity fluctuations.

The fixed global fit parameters are shown in Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S3. We used HFU alone (without Nsp15 added) measurements to determine the diffusion time of the annealed/uncleaved substrate as ~ 0.68 ms (684 µs) (Supplementary Figure S1, Table S3, Figure S2B). As a proxy for the completely cleaved substrate fragment of the HFU substrate, we measured an Atto647N-labeled 12-nucleotide short oligo which we refer to as SO12 (Supplementary Figure S1C, Table S3, Figure S2B, Table S4). In contrast to the HFU alone curve, the SO12 curve only decays at the millisecond timescale and fits to a pure diffusion model, Eqs. 3 and 5, yielding a diffusion time of 0.23 ms (231 µs). This difference in diffusion time between HFU alone and SO12 is due to the significant dissimilarity in size (Supplementary Table S1) between the cleaved fragment, represented by SO12, and the annealed RNA/DNA molecules. The larger annealed HFU dwells longer in the confocal volume. We also measured the diffusion time for the unannealed donor and acceptor strands, and an annealed donor only strand (Supplementary Table S3). We used these τD values as fixed parameters in the global fitting analysis presented in Figs. 2B and 3. In our analysis, we modeled a negligible or small unannealed fraction (NU = 0.0–0.2) or allowed NU to vary.

Global FCS analysis of the same data set presented in Fig. 2A also supports cleavage of the RNA fragment by Nsp15. The initial amplitudes of the auto- and cross-correlation functions [, ] are highly sensitive to the presence of FRET states. If only one state exists, all four normalized correlation curves will overlap (excluding triplet kinetics). If multiple static or dynamic FRET states are present, the initial amplitudes differ77. These amplitudes can be used to determine the total molecule number and to estimate the fractional populations of different states48,77. Since and are inversely related to the number of molecules in the confocal volume (Eq. 3 and Eq. 4), a higher amplitude indicates a lower concentration. Over the course of 120 min, the apparent total number of donor-labeled species (annealed + cleaved) determined from the initial correlation amplitude (Eq. 8) increases from NG, total = 0.08 to NG, total = 0.5. In contrast, the total number of acceptor-labeled species (annealed + cleaved), determined from (Eq. 9) is NR, total = 0.5, which remains steady over the course of the reaction (Supplementary Figure S6C). The apparent increase in number of donor-labeled molecules over the course of cleavage (Supplementary Figure S6B) is consistent with near complete donor quenching due to FRET in the annealed substrate, and an increase in molecular brightness post cleavage by Nsp15. That is expected due to the high energy transfer efficiency (E ~ 1) for HFU (Fig. 2B). In addition, the amplitudes of the cross-correlation functions are at a minimum after 120 min, indicating the disappearance of the double-labeled species subpopulation.

A key advantage in our approach is the application of pulsed interleaved excitation. Correlating the IRR (red excitation, red emission) direct excitation signal enables detection of the RNA-Atto647N fragment even after the FRET signal (IGR) for an individual molecule disappears due to cleavage by Nsp15 (Fig. 2A). Focusing on the autocorrelated signal from the RNA-Atto647N acceptor, the qualitative differences in the FCS curves of WT/HFU pre-cleavage (Fig. 3A, solid brown) and WT/HFU post-cleavage (Fig. 3A, dashed black) are visibly apparent. We observe that the curve for WT/HFU at 0 min (Fig. 3A, solid brown) decays at both microsecond and millisecond timescales. The millisecond timescale feature is expected as a characteristic of translational diffusion36. Microsecond timescale features in FCS curves can be attributed to photophysical effects such as photoblinking and triplet state occupancy58,78, or conformational dynamics37,75. We show in Supplementary Figure S4 and Table S5 the results of fitting , , , and globally using Eqs. 8, 9, and 10 with as a pure diffusion (Eq. 5) times (*) a triplet model (Eq. 6) or a conformational model (Eq. 7). The reduced chi-square value for each global fit is shown in Figure S4 to indicate goodness of fit. A pure diffusion model is clearly insufficient to globally fit the data (). A pure diffusion * triplet model improves the fit (), and a pure diffusion * conformational model yields a similar result ). Qualitatively, the conformational model fails to adequately fit , while , , and are well fit. For simplicity, we move forward applying the triplet model to all global fits noting that Eq. 6 and Eq. 7 are mathematically similar. In some individual fits for control experiments presented the Supplementary Data, we also applied a conformational model as indicated. The fact that the cross-correlation functions are also well described by either triplet or conformational terms suggests that the labeled substrate undergoes microsecond-scale fluctuations that modulate the FRET state. Although triplet blinking may contribute to the observed correlations, the agreement between auto- and cross-correlation fits supports the presence of genuine substrate dynamics in addition to diffusion48,79.

To further explore the origin of the microsecond timescale features in FCS curves observed for HFU experiments (Figs. 2B and 3A), we performed several control experiments. We measured free Atto647N in water and compared the autocorrelation curve to AlexaFluor647 (Supplementary Figure S5A and Table S6). The Atto647N autocorrelation function fits to a pure diffusion model (fT = 0.0), while the AlexaFluor647 requires a triplet fit (fT = 0.3, τT = 4.1 µs). We constructed an acceptor-only substrate by annealing the hybrid high-FRET RNA/DNA strand to an unlabeled DNA complement (No-FRET A/U, NFA/NFU, Table S1). In the presence of divalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, and Mn2+), Trolox (triplet state quencher), and glucose oxidase/catalase (GOX/CAT, oxygen scavenger), the microsecond timescale feature is not present for the NFA/NFU constructs (Supplementary Figure S5B and Table S6). Lastly, we measured benchmark Lo, Mid, and Hi FRET duplex DNA labeled with Atto550-Atto647N53, which is expected to be static (Supplementary Figure S5C and Table S6). The benchmark DNA autocorrelation functions do not have a microsecond timescale feature in , , or signals and fit well to a pure diffusion model. A slight rise in at fast timescales for the Lo FRET sample (FRET ~ 0.18) is likely due to low signal-to-noise ratio. We concluded from these comprehensive control experiments that the microsecond timescale feature in the autocorrelated IRR signal is only present when the donor is on the complementary strand of HFA/HFU. Based on model fitting and control experiments, we therefore ruled out photoblinking and triplet state occupancy as the sole origin of the microsecond timescale features in , and and attributed it in large part to RNA substrate conformational dynamics.

Intensity fluctuations in the IRR signal are unexpected; however, our findings suggest that the fluctuations are likely due to an environment-dependent photophysical interaction which results from substrate dynamics. We note that the microsecond timescale feature is also present in the IGR signals pre-cleavage (Supplementary Figure S2B, HFU alone). The photophysical origin of the detected fluorescence fluctuations in IRR in the presence of the donor molecule may be related to photoinduced electron transfer and dye-dye interactions80,81. The conformational and triplet models are mathematically similar, both containing an exponential component, and the timescales of the proposed conformational and triplet dynamics may overlap. We applied a pure diffusion * triplet model (Eqs. 5 and 6) in the global fitting analysis to extract fit parameters for HFU in the presence of WT, W333A, and H235A Nsp15 variants (Supplementary Figure S6, S7, and Table S7) and divalent cations (Supplementary Figure S7 and Table S7). The extracted parameters were then used to track the subpopulations present in solution over time.

Nsp15 mutants and divalent cation environment impact Nsp15 cleavage efficiency

We applied global FCS analysis to track the evolution of reaction products revealing how Nsp15 mutants and divalent cations impact the efficiency of Nsp15 cleavage. We used global FCS analysis because it is challenging to monitor the reaction over time with a binary FRET readout and the endpoint parameters discussed above for HFU (Figs. 2B and 3A Table S4, S7) cannot characterize reaction kinetics. Our method of identifying and tracking cleavage reaction species is a unique approach to kinetic analysis with PIE-FRET/FCS. To explore the cleavage of the different mutants over time, we analyzed each triplicate 90- second measurement taken at 10-minute increments over 2 h for HFA and HFU incubated with either WT, W333A, or H235A Nsp15 (Fig. 3A and B). Both W333 and H235 lie in the active site of Nsp15, and prior work confirmed the importance of these residues for efficient cleavage of RNA17,19,21, which motivated the selection of these mutants for further exploration. We globally analyzed the FCS data for HFA and HFU substrates in the presence of Nsp15 variants to separate multiple species and track the relative populations of cleaved and annealed RNA over time. Representative autocorrelation curves for IRR and global model fits are shown in Fig. 3A. A qualitative comparison of the 0-hour and 2-hour FCS fits for WT, W333A, and H235A Nsp15 mutants confirms that the autocorrelation curve changes after 2 h. The increase in amplitude (lower concentration) from 0 to 120 min for the W333A and H235A curves is likely due to dye photobleaching. The global fitting quantitatively untangled the relative differences in cleavage efficiency for the Nsp15 variants incubated with HFU. From global fitting with Eqs. 8–10, we extract the average number of molecules of each species [annealed (NA), cleaved (NC), and unannealed (NU)] in the confocal volume for the green and red excitation/detection channels. Figure 3B shows the fraction of annealed substrate molecules over time determined from the red channel (IRR signal). Fitting parameters for the global model are shown in Supplementary Figure S6 and Table S7. The τD value for each species is a fixed parameter (Table S3). We calculated the fraction of annealed acceptor molecules over time as described in the Supplementary Data. Over the course of 2 h, the fraction of annealed acceptor molecules decreases (Fig. 3B and S6A), while the fraction of cleaved acceptor molecules increases (Figure S6A). The apparent trend is opposite when tracking the donor molecules over time (Figure S6B). Many donor molecules may be completely quenched () in the fully annealed HFA/HFU substrate due to high FRET (Fig. 2A), thus it is challenging to accurately determine N from the green channel signal (IGG/). We therefore focus on fit parameters to track the cleavage reaction.

The fit parameters show the clearest readout of Nsp15 kinetic behavior across conditions. The fraction of annealed molecules is expected to decrease with cleavage, and the fraction of cleaved molecules should increase, as we observe in Figure S6A. The advantages of independent monitoring of the acceptor molecules (IRR/) via direct excitation with PIE are threefold: (1) the large difference in sizes of annealed and cleaved acceptor-containing substrate fragments compared to the similar sizes of donor-containing fragments (Figs. 1D and 2B) (2) avoiding the influence of quenching from FRET which impacts the IGG signal, and (3) a distinct microsecond timescale feature in . Over the course of the 2-hour experiment, the fraction of annealed acceptor molecules decreases at a faster rate for WT (Fig. 3B, brown circles, t1/2 = 35.6 min) compared to W333A (Fig. 3B, blue diamonds, t1/2 = 42.5 min). The H235A mutant has an active site mutation that abolishes Nsp15 function as shown in previous work17, therefore we do not expect cleavage of the HFU substrate. Consistent with that, no change in the annealed substrate fraction was observed for H235A over the course of 2 h (Fig. 3B, red triangles). Incubating WT Nsp15 with HFA also shows no change in the annealed substrate fraction over time (Fig. 3B, black squares), consistent with a strong preference of Nsp15 for uridine17.

While W333A and WT Nsp15 are both active, the annealed fraction of HFU incubated with WT decreased faster than W333A/HFU, indicating more efficient cleavage, consistent with biochemical experiments17. In addition, the annealed fraction at the endpoint was higher for W333A/HFU (40%) compared to WT/HFU (20%), suggesting that the cleavage reaction was incomplete at the 2-hour timepoint. It was previously determined that the W333 residue of Nsp15 engages with the post cleaved uridines of ssRNA substrates17 and helps to stabilize the flipped uridine of dsRNA to support cleavage19, suggesting that the W333A mutation may negatively impact cleavage efficiency. The fraction of annealed HFU showed no change when incubated with H235A (Fig. 3B, red triangles). In prior work, the H235 residue, present within the active site of Nsp15, has been identified as a part of the catalytic triad necessary for nucleotide binding, and the H235A mutation was shown to inactivate Nsp15 cleavage capability17.

The exponential fit parameters for all experiments in Fig. 3B are given in Supplementary Table S8. In recent work, the kinetic rate for Nsp15 cleavage of a ssRNA substrate was reported to be about 0.05 min− 1 for a 10:1 ratio of substrate to Nsp15 without manganese16. The cleavage rate determined from the exponential fit of WT/HFU (Fig. 3B) in our work was approximately 0.0195 min− 1. Considering that our substrate to Nsp15 ratio is 1:1, that result is in good agreement with the previous biochemical study. This highlights the sensitivity of our method that uses small amounts of nucleic acid substrate and protein. We have shown that our global FCS analysis approach can tease out different activity of Nsp15 mutants and estimate kinetic rates (Fig. 3B), which can be applied to study other perturbations.

We applied our global FCS analysis to explore the role of different divalent cations on Nsp15 cleavage activity. Mn2+ has been reported as a preferred cofactor in some Nsp15 homolog studies16,24,82, but prior studies have shown that manganese neither induces conformational changes in Nsp1516 nor is implicated at the binding site during RNA cleavage17. The results are consistent with a different study finding that the cleavage product accumulates more quickly in the presence of divalent ions calcium, magnesium, and manganese compared to the substrate in solution without divalent ions83. Figure 3C shows the annealed fraction of HFU incubated with and without divalent cations over 2 h. Figure 3C indicates that over the course of 2 h, the fraction of annealed HFU in divalent cation-free buffer (none/HFU, blue circles) decreased at a cleavage rate (k) of k = 0.0291 min− 1. Over 2 h of incubation with WT Nsp15, the cleavage rates for Ca2+/HFU (green triangles), Mg2+/HFU (magenta squares), and Mn2+/HFU (orange diamonds) were 0.0108 min− 1, 0.0167 min− 1, and 0.0470 min− 1, respectively. The global modeling and exponential fit parameters for Fig. 3C are provided in Supplementary Figure S7, Table S7, and Figure S8, and Table S9. The cleavage rates in the presence of Ca2+ and Mg2+ are slightly slower than in none/HFU, although the different curve shapes may indicate mechanistic heterogeneity. Consistent with biochemical studies, addition of Mn2+ produces the fastest cleavage rate (k = 0.0470 min− 1). In complementary experiments on mid-FRET substrates (MFA/MFU), we discovered signatures of dynamic substrate behavior heavily influenced by the presence of divalent cations.

We propose that conformational dynamics of the RNA substrate contribute to specific recognition at uridine and cleavage by Nsp15. As shown below, interpretation of FCS fit parameters and time-resolved PIE-FRET analysis reveal distinct signatures of dynamic behavior that depend on RNA sequence and divalent cation conditions.

Divalent cations contribute to substrate dynamics

The prospect of dynamic behavior in the intact HFA/HFU substrates revealed in FCS curves (Figs. 2B and 3A) led us to explore the dynamics of the substrate in further detail. While traditional biochemical assays can report on substrate cleavage, the role of RNA substrate dynamics and divalent cations is harder to investigate with traditional methods. The presence of a decay feature at the microsecond timescale in the HFU autocorrelation curves suggested conformational dynamics, but the FRET efficiency for each conformational state is likely indistinguishable since E ~ 1 (Fig. 2A). FRET-induced fluctuations can result in an additional decay component in the autocorrelations and a corresponding rising component in the cross-correlations48. We designed substrates with mid-FRET (MFA and MFU) for dynamic experiments and performed the global FCS analysis described above for the , , , and correlations. In addition, since the role of manganese in facilitating Nsp15 recognition and specific cleavage at uridine is not well understood and manganese is not known to bind to Nsp1517,21,82, we explored the impact of the divalent cation environment on the conformational dynamics of the substrates in the absence of Nsp15.

Mid-FRET substrates (MFA and MFU) were designed to optimize the distance-dependent sensitivity of FRET efficiency (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S1). The Förster distance of the AlexaFluor546-Atto647N FRET pair was estimated to be 64.13 Å from FPbase, and the separation between the donor and acceptor for MFA/MFU after annealing was approximately 60 Å. This corresponds to a minimum FRET efficiency of approximately 0.6. Each MFA or MFU sample was diluted to 5 nM in HEPES buffer containing the appropriate divalent cation (Ca2+, Mg2+, or Mn2+) and the PIE-FRET/FCS signal was collected for 60 s. We utilized global FCS analysis described above to further characterize substrate dynamics and estimate dynamic relaxation times. We globally fit the autocorrelated and cross-correlated IGG and IRR signals for MFA and MFU substrates to a pure diffusion * triplet model (Eqs. 5 and 6) and extracted N, fT, and τT parameters (Supplementary Table S10). The IGR(FRET) signal was also individually fit to extract N, τD, fT, and τT (Supplementary Table S11). To determine fixed parameters for global modeling, we performed individual fits for the same MFA/MFU experiments. Representative , , with fits are presented in Supplementary Figure S9A and Table S12.

Figure 4A shows the global fits for MFA and MFU in the presence of no divalent cation, Ca2+, Mg2+, or Mn2+. All divalent cations were diluted to 5 mM in the sample buffer. The , , , and correlations are shown for each condition, with none as blue circles, Ca2+ as green triangles, Mg2+ as magenta squares, and Mn2+ as orange diamonds. The microsecond timescale feature is prominent in all four correlation functions, so we fit the data with a pure diffusion * triplet model. A minimum of two species, Ni, for each signal were required to fit the data. We designated the two species as N1 and N2. N1 had an average molecular brightness , averaged from individual fits across all conditions, while N2 had a molecular brightness calculated based on an expected FRET of ~ 0.7 for MFA/MFU (Table S3). Separate triplet fractions, fT, G,1/fT, G,2 and fT, R,1/fT, R,2, for each species in the green donor (G)/red acceptor (R) channels were also necessary to adequately fit the data globally. The fit parameters for the N2 species (fT, G,2/fT, R,2) were fixed with ‘none’ values from individual fits (Supplementary Figure S9A and Table S12, Table S3). Triplet fraction (fT) and triplet relaxation time (τT) report on the local chemical and structural environment of the fluorophore58. A larger triplet fraction indicates that a higher proportion of fluorescent molecules are residing in the non-fluorescent triplet state at any given time due to photophysical interactions with a quencher. The triplet relaxation time represents the average residence time of a fluorophore in the dark triplet state before it relaxes back to the singlet ground state. A longer τT means the molecule is in the triplet state for a greater duration, while a shorter τT indicates the triplet state relaxes more quickly to the ground state.

For MFA, the fitted donor channel triplet fraction ranges from 0.11 in the absence of divalent cations to 0.60 in Mn2+, whereas for MFU it ranges from 0.29 to 0.70 over the same conditions. These increases in suggest that divalent cations perturb AF546 photophysics, but the fact that the fitted values differ between MFA and MFU suggests that local RNA structure also contributes. Consistent with this interpretation, the Atto647N acceptor parameters are also sequence- and cation-dependent, indicating that the fitted and values report on a combination of photophysical quenching and conformational dynamics rather than a single mechanism.

The MFA acceptor triplet fraction ranges from 0.29 in the absence of divalent cations to 0.39 in Mn2+, whereas MFU ranges from 0.12 to 0.26 over the same conditions. Overall, the MFA acceptor (Atto647N) shows a larger triplet fraction than MFU across divalent cation conditions, indicating that the observed changes are not purely photophysical but also depend on local RNA structure. In both substrates, Atto647N is therefore a sensitive reporter of its local photophysical environment and conformational state. For example, the higher Mn2+/MFA (0.39) relative to Mn2+/MFU (0.26) suggests a local environment that is more favorable to quenching in MFA. From none/MFA to Mn2+/MFA, increases from 4.32 µs to 6.64 µs, consistent with a longer-lived quenched state. More generally, shorter values indicate faster return from the triplet state and weaker quencher interaction, whereas longer values indicate a longer-lived triplet state and stronger quenching. Taken together, these findings suggest that structural changes in the RNA are reflected in the Atto647N signal through proximity to a quencher, which contributes to the microsecond-timescale decay in the correlation curves. The conformational dynamics are modulated by both sequence (MFA versus MFU) and divalent-cation condition. For MFA, the higher indicates that more molecules occupy a local environment that favors quenching, consistent with a more compact state. For MFU, the lower indicates fewer molecules occupy such an environment, consistent with a less compact state.

The fitted and parameters likely reflect contributions from both photophysical and conformational dynamics. The two fitted species may correspond to compact subpopulations (species 1), or open or unannealed subpopulations (species 2), so the parameters likely represent a dynamic average over a heterogeneous conformational ensemble. Because the two dynamic processes occur on the same microsecond timescale, they are difficult to disentangle. Our global analysis suggests that the donor signal reports in part on AF546 triplet dynamics, with a triplet relaxation time of approximately 8 µs across conditions. This value is consistent with the annealed donor-only no-FRET control, for which and (Supplementary Figure S9B, Table S12). In the presence of Trolox, and decrease to 0.07 and , respectively, consistent with triplet-state quenching. The slightly higher apparent triplet fraction in AF546 annealed to the Atto647N-RNA hybrid in the presence of divalent cations may therefore reflect structural changes that alter the local environment of AF546. The Atto647N acceptor also reports on structural dynamics through a process with an approximately 5.4 µs relaxation time. Consistent with this interpretation, the acceptor-only no-FRET control in the presence of Mn2+, Trolox, and GOX/CAT showed a negligible triplet fraction (Figure S5B). In the full MFA/MFU construct, the acceptor triplet fraction reaches 0.39 for Mn2+/MFA, and the difference between MFA and MFU supports a sequence-dependent structural effect.

Figure 4B shows the autocorrelations of the IGR(FRET) signal, which were fit with a pure diffusion * triplet correlation model (Eqs. 5 and 6). The individual fit parameters \:N, , , and are listed in Supplementary Table S11. Consistent with this model, the apparent triplet fraction increases from 0.30 for none/MFA to 0.54 for Mn2+/MFA, indicating that a larger fraction of Atto647N-labeled acceptor molecules occupy a quenched state in the presence of divalent cations. For MFU, increases from 3.82 s for none/MFU to 5.17 s for Mn2+/MFU. Mn2+ has been reported to increase triplet-state formation and triplet blinking in cyanine dyes via a paramagnetically enhanced intersystem-crossing mechanism, so a similar contribution could also affect Atto647N under our conditions78. However, because Atto647N is a structurally distinct, more rigid carborhodamine dye, we interpret the cyanine-dye results as mechanistic precedent rather than direct evidence for Atto647N. Moreover, our acceptor-only NFA/NFU control experiments in the presence of divalent ions (Figure S5B) do not show an appreciable triplet feature.

Our FCS measurements should also be interpreted in the context of intrinsic dynamics of single-stranded RNA, which can occur independently of Nsp15 binding. The fast microsecond fluctuations we observe likely reflect a combination of intrinsic dye photophysics and the sequence-dependent conformational dynamics of the RNA substrate. Prior work has shown that single-stranded RNA and its linkers can undergo non-random, sequence-specific motions on the nanosecond-to-microsecond timescale, driven by transient base stacking and local structural transitions even in the absence of protein50. This is particularly relevant for our hybrid substrates, because the RNA segment and dye-linker regions may already sample a heterogeneous conformational ensemble84 before Nsp15 is added, and divalent cations may further modulate that ensemble by altering electrostatic screening and RNA compaction85. In our system, these intrinsic RNA motions may alter the local environment of the fluorescent probe—especially the red Atto647N dye—and nearby nucleobases, potentially facilitating photoinduced electron transfer (PET)80. However, the fact that the microsecond feature does not appear in the control lacking the donor on the complementary DNA strand (Figure S5B) suggests that dye placement and local duplex context also contribute81. This is consistent with the higher apparent triplet fraction observed in the presence of divalent cations. Divalent cations likely constrain intrinsic RNA dynamics by neutralizing the phosphate backbone and stabilizing a more compact conformation85,86, thereby changing the frequency and duration of quenching events. Together, the agreement between the auto- and cross-correlation fits supports substrate dynamics in the microsecond regime, although photophysical triplet blinking may also contribute to the observed signal. By accounting for these microsecond dynamics, we can better disentangle the intrinsic flexibility of the RNA and fluorophore-linker regions from the larger-scale structural rearrangements likely induced by Nsp15 binding and subsequent uridine cleavage. As a final confirmation of substrate conformational dynamics, we turn to PIE-FRET and time-resolved analysis of the same data set.

We used a photon burst analysis algorithm in PAM67(Materials and Methods) to analyze single-molecule diffusion events. Beta and gamma corrections for PIE-FRET analysis were determined by fitting photon data to a reciprocal stoichiometry versus FRET efficiency curve for each population (Supplementary Figure S10). The emergence of the high-FRET state suggests that MFU samples a dynamic ensemble that interconverts between a closed conformation (high FRET) and a more open conformation (mid FRET) that dominates in the absence of divalent cations. The percentage of molecules in each conformational state can be estimated from the Gaussian fit parameters87(Supplementary Table S13). For MFU in the presence of Mn2+, the populations in the E = 0.76 (closed) and E = 0.54 (open) FRET states are nearly 50–50, compared to about 60 − 40 (closed to open) with Ca2+ and Mg2+. We propose that subtle changes in the nucleic acid energy landscape88 may stabilize MFU conformations that promote cleavage by Nsp15 at uridine, depending on the local divalent cation environment.

For comparison, FCS and FRET Efficiency versus donor lifetime plots were generated for double-stranded benchmark DNA oligos52 as a control for an expected static sample (Supplementary Figures S5C - FCS and S12 - FRET). In contrast to MFA/MFU, the E vs. tD(A) populations for benchmark DNA samples are on the static line (Figure S12). Notably, the benchmark DNA FCS curves (Figure S5C) lack a microsecond timescale feature in the decay, thus are fit to a pure diffusion model. That is in stark contrast to HFA/HFU (Figs. 2B and 3A) and MFA/MFU substrates (Fig. 4). Although only one FRET state could be resolved for MFA substrates (Fig. 5A, top), the deviation from the static line suggests this may be due to averaging of indistinguishable FRET states in a compact conformation due to fast switching41,89,91.

It is interesting that the introduction of a single uridine leads to a broader FRET distribution. Taken together with global FCS analysis these data suggest that uridine is structurally more flexible than adenine, and the role of manganese may be to induce substrate conformations that enable more efficient cleavage specifically at uridine. Divalent cations are known to interact with RNA by shielding phosphate backbone charges, reducing electrostatic repulsion, and promoting RNA compaction and conformational flexibility92,93. This provides a plausible explanation for the increased high-FRET population observed for MFU in the presence of Ca2+, Mg2+, and Mn2+ (Fig. 5A). The dynamic behavior inferred from the E vs. plots is consistent with fast, likely microsecond timescale conformational exchange, which can manifest as an averaged FRET state when interconversion is rapid relative to the measurement window89,94,95. Though dynamics appeared to be present in both MFA and MFU based on the E vs. τD(A) histograms (Fig. 5B), the divalent cation environment affected MFU conformation more dramatically as evidenced by the shift in the FRET efficiencies for MFU in the presence of divalent cations, particularly in the case of Mn2+.

Discussion

Over the past several years, the specificity of Nsp15 has been investigated through biochemical, structural, and high-throughput sequencing approaches11,15,17–19,23,96. Collectively, these studies show that Nsp15 acts on a broad range of ss- and dsRNA substrates, while also indicating that local substrate features influence cleavage preference. The present work extends this view by showing that substrate dynamics likely contribute to specificity. This interpretation is consistent with recent studies reporting that Nsp15 preferentially cleaves RNA in less thermodynamically stable regions18,23.

A central outcome of this study is that the combined PIE-FRET and FCS assay provides a way to monitor both cleavage and dynamic behavior within the same experimental framework. The assay distinguished cleavage by wild-type and mutant Nsp15, enabled extraction of kinetic parameters from global fits, and distinguished adenine- and uridine-containing substrates in both cleavage behavior and conformational response. Rather than indicating a single dominant structural state, these observations are most consistent with a dynamic ensemble in which some conformations are more favorable for recognition and cleavage by Nsp15.

To translate the observed FRET states into structural constraints, we modeled fluorophore positions using FRETraj49. RNA-DNA hybrid structures were generated as described in Materials and Methods. FRET efficiencies were estimated using FRETraj with dye accessible volume parameters based on previously reported fluorophore properties and smFRET benchmark methodology52, and the modeling parameters are listed in Supplementary Table S14. Figure 6 shows representative structural models of HFA/HFU (6A) and MFA/MFU (6B). The predicted FRET values are similar to measured values, ranging from E = 0.6 to E = 0.87. Figure 6C presents a structural model for MFU in complex with an Nsp15 monomer unit. The substrate is in a highly compact state, yielding E = 0.95, which suggests that the compact state is favorable for uridine recognition by Nsp15.

This interpretation is supported by prior work on RNA conformational behavior and ion-dependent compaction. RNA molecules populate heterogeneous ensembles in solution, and their structural distributions can be shifted by sequence context, ion atmosphere, and other environmental factors27,88,97–99. In the present system, the hybrid RNA/DNA substrates likely sample multiple conformations before Nsp15 is added, and divalent cations likely reshape that landscape by screening electrostatic repulsion and favoring more compact states. These effects are especially relevant for the uridine-containing substrates, which showed cation-dependent changes in both the FCS and PIE-FRET experiments.

The results are also compatible with previous reports that polyA and polyU sequences differ in stacking propensity, ion association, and structural order84. Although the substrates used here are shorter hybrid constructs rather than homopolymeric RNAs, the same physical principles may still apply locally. In this context, substitution of a single adenine with uridine may alter the local conformational ensemble surrounding the cleavage site, and divalent cations may further reshape that ensemble. The broader FRET distributions observed for MFU are consistent with increased structural heterogeneity, whereas the cation-dependent appearance of an additional higher-FRET population suggests that compaction and local flexibility can coexist within the same substrate ensemble.

The FCS data add an important complementary dimension to this interpretation. Microsecond fluctuations likely reflect a combination of intrinsic dye photophysics and sequence- or construct-dependent motions. Control experiments indicate that dye placement and local duplex context also influence the observed photophysics, suggesting that the microsecond feature may not be attributed to RNA motions alone. Even with this caveat, the combined FCS and PIE-FRET analysis supports the conclusion that the substrate environment sensed by the fluorophores differs systematically between adenine- and uridine-containing constructs and is further modulated by divalent cations.

Together, these findings support a model in which local RNA flexibility contributes to Nsp15 specificity alongside sequence and secondary-structure context. This view agrees with structural and biochemical studies showing that Nsp15 can recognize RNA through local rearrangement, base flipping, and access to less stable regions of the substrate14,18,19,21–23. In this framework, uridine-containing substrates may be cleaved more efficiently not simply because of base identity, but because the surrounding conformational ensemble more readily samples states that are compatible with productive binding and catalysis.

The broader implication of this work is that RNA dynamics should be considered explicitly when evaluating Nsp15 specificity and mechanism. This is particularly relevant for hybrid substrates, where linker composition, fluorophore placement, and cation environment can all influence both the underlying RNA ensemble and the observed photophysics. In addition, the ability to detect these effects at nanomolar or lower concentrations highlights the utility of diffusion-based single-molecule fluorescence measurements for studying nuclease mechanisms under solution conditions.

Our platform also provides several clear directions for future work. The assay should be useful for testing inhibitors of Nsp15 activity, for probing conserved coronavirus RNA elements that may present distinct dynamic signatures100, and for examining how RNA-targeting ligands alter substrate conformational ensembles101. A further step will be to monitor the enzyme directly through fluorescent labeling, although site-specific labeling of the Nsp15 homohexamer will require additional engineering. More broadly, the present approach offers a route to dissect how sequence, ion environment, and structural dynamics together shape biomolecular recognition and RNA processing.
