First Report of Co-Occurring FGF14 (SCA27B) and RFC1 (CANVAS) Repeat Expansions in Two of Three Siblings with Late-Onset Cerebellar Ataxia
Authors: Tasos Tsokkos, Kyproula Christodoulou, Christina Votsi, Anthi Georghiou, Andrea Christofides, Astero Constantinou, Eleni Zamba-Papanicolaou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) and spinocerebellar ataxia type 27B (SCA27B) are two increasingly recognized types of late-onset ataxia caused by biallelic RFC1 AAGGG and heterozygous FGF14 GAA repeat expansions, respectively. We describe three siblings of Greek-Cypriot origin with late-onset cerebellar ataxia. Two brothers carried biallelic pathogenic RFC1 AAGGG expansions and heterozygous FGF14 GAA expansions (338–350 repeats), establishing a dual diagnosis of CANVAS and SCA27B. Both presented with progressive gait ataxia, vestibular dysfunction, and sensory neuronopathy; one also reported episodic symptoms typical of SCA27B. Their sister, heterozygous for RFC1 and carrying a pathogenic FGF14 expansion (325 repeats), showed a pure SCA27B phenotype with episodic fluctuations, but without neuropathy or vestibular involvement. Brain MRI in all three demonstrated mild-to-moderate vermian atrophy. To our knowledge, this is the first documented report of co-occurring CANVAS and SCA27B in the same individuals. The findings expand the phenotypic spectrum of late-onset ataxia and highlight the importance of continued genetic testing, even after an initial diagnosis has been made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12311-025-01921-z.Keywords: SCA27B, FGF14, CANVAS, RFC1, Cerebellar ataxia

Introduction

Late-onset cerebellar ataxias (LOCAs) represent a large and diverse group of neurodegenerative disorders typically manifesting after the age of 30 [1, 2]. In recent years, two important repeat expansion disorders—cerebellar ataxia with neuropathy and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) and spinocerebellar ataxia type 27B (SCA27B)—have emerged as common and often underrecognized causes of LOCA [3–5].

CANVAS is caused by biallelic intronic AAGGG repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene and usually manifests in the mid-50s [6]. It typically presents with a characteristic triad of cerebellar ataxia, bilateral vestibular hypofunction, and sensory neuronopathy, with additional features such as chronic dry cough [7–9], dysphagia [10], and cognitive impairment, including cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome (CCAS) [8]. Brain MRI typically reveals dorsal vermian atrophy [7], and nerve conduction studies (NCS) demonstrate absent or reduced sensory nerve action potentials [5, 6, 8].

SCA27B is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by a GAA repeat expansion in intron 1 of the FGF14 gene, typically presenting between 55 and 65 years. Pathogenic expansions >300 repeats are associated with high penetrance [1, 3]. Clinically, SCA27B is characterized by slowly progressive gait and limb ataxia, downbeat nystagmus, and episodic symptoms such as vertigo or diplopia [1, 4, 11]. Dysphagia occurs in a subset of patients [12], and brain MRI usually shows vermian atrophy [1, 11], while peripheral neuropathy and vestibular areflexia are usually mild or absent [3]. Cognitive impairment in SCA27B is infrequent, although transient episodes of cognitive slowing or confusion have been reported [13].

CANVAS and SCA27B have been recently reported as frequent causes of ataxia in the Greek-Cypriot population [14]. To our knowledge, the co-occurrence of both disorders in the same individuals has not been previously described. Here, we present a novel case series of three Greek-Cypriot siblings with late-onset ataxia who all carry pathogenic FGF14 expansions, with two of them also harbouring biallelic RFC1 expansions.

Materials and Methods

We evaluated three siblings of Greek-Cypriot origin presenting with late-onset ataxia at the Cyprus Institute of Neurology and Genetics in 2024 and 2025. Clinical data were collected through direct neurological examination, review of medical records, and structured interviews. Brain MRI scans were performed between 2022 and 2024, prior to the patients’ presentation to our institute. NCS were subsequently performed in all patients following their presentation. Cognitive symptoms were documented based on patient self-report; no formal neuropsychological assessment was performed.

Genetic testing was performed by the Neurogenetics Department of the Cyprus Institute of Neurology and Genetics. For the RFC1 locus, a combination of flanking PCR, repeat-primed PCR (RP-PCR), and long-range PCR (LR-PCR) was used to detect and confirm biallelic pathogenic AAGGG expansions associated with CANVAS. For the FGF14 locus, a multi-step approach identified normal and expanded alleles, confirmed uninterrupted GAA repeats, and sized expansions using automated electrophoresis.

Case Presentations

Patient 1 (Male, Born 1960)

The proband, the youngest of the three affected siblings, developed symptoms at age 59 with slowly progressive gait imbalance and distal lower-limb dysesthesias. He also reported chronic dry cough which remained stable over time, oscillopsia while walking, slurred speech, word-finding difficulties and intermittent dysphagia, as well as episodes of blurred vision or diplopia. He did not exhibit autonomic symptoms such as orthostatic dizziness or urinary disturbances.

Neurological examination showed gait ataxia with mild limb ataxia, reduced vibration and pinprick sensation in all limbs, a positive head impulse test (HIT) and bilateral gaze-evoked horizontal nystagmus. Reflexes were absent at the ankles. Swallowing assessment revealed mild pharyngeal dysphagia. Dysarthria was absent, though mild dysphonia was noted.

NCS showed reduced sensory nerve action potential amplitudes in both upper and lower limbs, consistent with a sensory neuronopathy. Brain MRI showed mild-to-moderate atrophy of the cerebellar vermis. Genetic analysis confirmed biallelic pathogenic RFC1 AAGGG expansions and a heterozygous pathogenic-range GAA expansion in FGF14 (350 ± 35 repeats), with the other allele in the normal range (9 ± 1 repeats).

Patient 2 (Male, Born 1949)

The eldest sibling developed symptoms at the age of 41 with numbness in the hands. Over the ensuing decades, he developed a slowly progressive gait imbalance, dysphagia, and a chronic dry cough which remained stable over time. He also reported oscillopsia while walking. In later years, he developed word-finding difficulties and cognitive slowing. He denied episodic fluctuations of his symptoms. He did not exhibit autonomic symptoms.

Neurological examination revealed gait and truncal ataxia with mild limb ataxia, reduced pinprick and vibration sensation in all limbs, and a positive HIT. There was no evidence of nystagmus and ankle reflexes were absent. Swallowing assessment showed mild-to-moderate pharyngeal dysphagia. Dysarthria was absent, though mild dysphonia was noted.

NCS showed reduced sensory nerve action potential amplitudes in both upper and lower limbs, consistent with a sensory neuronopathy. Brain MRI showed mild atrophy of the cerebellar vermis. Genetic testing revealed biallelic pathogenic AAGGG repeat expansions in RFC1 and a heterozygous pathogenic-range GAA expansion in FGF14 (338 ± 34 repeats), with the other allele in the normal range (9 ± 1 repeats).

Patient 3 (Female, Born 1953)

This patient developed symptoms at the age of 60, with slowly progressive gait imbalance and episodic fluctuations, marked by alternating periods of improved and worsened mobility, occasional slurred speech, and episodes of blurred vision or diplopia. She also reported a chronic dry cough, which is however attributed to her bronchial asthma, as well as word-finding difficulties and cognitive slowing. The patient did not exhibit autonomic symptoms.

Neurological examination revealed gait ataxia with mild limb ataxia and mildly reduced vibration sensation in the lower limbs. HIT was negative and no nystagmus was observed. Reflexes were present and symmetric. She had minimal pharyngeal dysphagia without dysarthria or dysphonia.

NCS revealed no evidence of peripheral neuropathy. Brain MRI showed mild atrophy of the cerebellar vermis. Genetic testing confirmed a heterozygous RFC1 AAGGG repeat expansion and a pathogenic-range GAA expansion in FGF14 (325 ± 32 repeats), with the other allele in the normal range (9 ± 1 repeats). Her negative result for biallelic RFC1 expansion prompted further investigation due to the presence of cerebellar symptoms. Subsequent testing identified a pathogenic-range GAA repeat expansion in FGF14, which led to targeted genetic testing in her symptomatic brothers.

Family History

The family is of Greek-Cypriot descent. One sibling died at around 60 years of age without neurological symptoms, and another, born in the 1960 s, remains asymptomatic and untested to date. Both parents died in their 50–60 s from non-neurological causes and were not tested. The patients reported investigating their pedigree back four generations and found no evidence of consanguinity between the maternal and paternal lines.

Discussion

This case series describes three Greek-Cypriot siblings with adult-onset cerebellar ataxia in whom genetic testing revealed pathogenic GAA expansions in FGF14 (SCA27B) in all three, and biallelic AAGGG expansions in RFC1 (CANVAS) in two of them. While both conditions have been independently recognized as significant causes of late-onset ataxia [1–4, 14], to our knowledge this is the first report of their co-occurrence in the same individuals, representing a rare combination not previously documented.

The two male siblings (Patients 1 and 2) who harboured both biallelic RFC1 and monoallelic FGF14 expansions exhibited features aligned closely with the classical presentation of CANVAS. Patient 1 also reported episodic symptoms such as diplopia and dysarthria, which are more characteristic of SCA27B [1, 4, 11]. In contrast, the sister (Patient 3), who carried monoallelic RFC1 and monoallelic FGF14 pathogenic expansions, showed a pure cerebellar phenotype with episodic fluctuations, without vestibular dysfunction and with normal NCS, consistent with isolated SCA27B [3, 14, 15].

All three patients reported a chronic dry cough. In Patients 1 and 2, who carried biallelic RFC1 expansions, this feature is consistent with the CANVAS phenotype and may reflect sensory neuropathy involving vagal afferents [5, 9]. In contrast, in Patient 3, who carried only a monoallelic RFC1 expansion, the cough was attributed to her pre-existing bronchial asthma. Additionally, all three exhibited mild to moderate pharyngeal phase dysphagia.

Cognitive impairment, previously considered rare in CANVAS, is now recognized in a substantial proportion of cases, with features suggestive of CCAS [8]. In our series, all three patients had subjective cognitive complaints, emphasizing the importance of recognizing cognitive symptoms as part of the clinical spectrum in both RFC1- and FGF14-associated ataxias.

Interestingly, Patient 2 developed symptoms at age 41, earlier than the typical age range in both SCA27B and CANVAS. This earlier onset, in the context of dual genetic burden, raises the possibility that the co-occurrence of both expansions may contribute to an earlier manifestation of symptoms.

All three siblings had mild-to-moderate atrophy of the cerebellar vermis on MRI (Fig. 1), a pattern seen in both SCA27B and CANVAS [1, 5]. The sensory neuronopathy further supported the distinction between dual-mutation cases and isolated SCA27B.

A notable strength of this report is the diagnostic pathway. The proband was first found to carry a biallelic RFC1 AAGGG repeat expansion, as did his brother, thus confirming the diagnosis of CANVAS. However, their sister, who also presented with cerebellar ataxia, was found to have only a monoallelic RFC1 AAGGG expansion, thereby excluding CANVAS as a diagnosis in her case. This unexpected result prompted further genetic testing in the sister, which ultimately revealed a pathogenic-range GAA repeat expansion in FGF14. Subsequent cascade testing in her symptomatic brothers also confirmed the presence of the FGF14 GAA repeat expansion, establishing the diagnosis of SCA27B in all three siblings. These cases underscore that in late-onset ataxias, discordant or inconclusive genetic findings should prompt careful re-evaluation of the phenotype and, when unexplained, extension of genetic testing. This approach may uncover multilocus contributions, as illustrated in this family, with important implications for accurate diagnosis, prognostication, and counselling.

A limitation of this report is the absence of genetic and clinical data from additional relatives, which currently precludes segregation analysis. Moreover, formal vestibular and neuropsychological assessments were not performed, restricting detailed evaluation of vestibular and cognitive functions.
