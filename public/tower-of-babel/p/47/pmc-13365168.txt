Nationwide Population-Based Cohort Study of Advanced Maternal Age and Obstetric Outcomes in Korean Women: Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Seon Ui Lee, Jin Young Bae, Hyun Sun Ko, Subeen Hong, Hayan Kwon, Tae Gyu Ahn, You Jung Han, Won Joon Seong, Jae Eun Shin, Geum Joon Cho, In Yang Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe proportion of mothers older than 35 years is increasing; however, research examining the age at which pregnancy is considered high risk across different races and countries is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the cut-off age at which adverse obstetric outcomes significantly increase by investigating age-specific incidence rates of major adverse obstetric outcomes in Republic of Korea (South Korea).MethodsWe analyzed the Korean National Health Insurance claims database between 2015 and 2021, focusing on women with obstetric complications classified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition. The maternal cut-off age was defined as the age at which the outcome was most predictive in receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Multivariate logistic regression was used to estimate the adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the association between maternal age and adverse obstetric outcomes.ResultsThe study included 1,862,393 women with singleton pregnancies. Of these, 33.67% were aged ≥ 35 years and 5.07% were aged ≥ 40 years. The incidence of adverse obstetric outcomes increased linearly and positively with maternal age. The maternal cut-off age, determined using receiver operating characteristics analysis, was 35 years for pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH), 33 for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), 33 for placenta previa, 32 for placental abruption, 34 for preterm delivery, and 34 for low birth weight (LBW). In multivariate analyses, adjusted OR revealed adverse obstetric outcomes: PIH (OR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.27–1.32), GDM (OR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.72–1.75), placenta previa (OR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.78–1.85), placental abruption (OR, 1.30; 95% CI, 1.24–1.36), preterm delivery (OR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.29–1.34), and LBW (OR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.31–1.35). For the composite outcome, maternal cut-off age was found to be 33 years (OR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.56–1.59), with an area under the curve of 0.573.ConclusionThis nationwide study defined the cut-off age for advanced maternal age in Korean women, showing that obstetric risks begin to increase in the early 30s. These population-specific thresholds may help guide clinical practice and improve maternal and perinatal outcomes.Keywords: Maternal Age, Pregnancy, Obstetric Outcome, Korea

INTRODUCTION

The term “elderly gravida” was defined by the International Federation of Obstetricians and Gynecologists in 1959 as referring to a woman who becomes pregnant at the age of 35 years or older.1 Several studies have shown that pregnancy at or beyond this age is associated with increased risk of various complications, necessitating more attention to antenatal care for these women.1,2 A meta-analysis and other researchers have also reported that older maternal age is linked to a higher possibility of experiencing pregnancy complications, specifically gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH), and preterm birth or stillbirth.3,4 Additionally, advanced maternal age is associated with low birth weight (LBW) infant and postpartum hemorrhage.5,6

The incidence of GDM increases with age, which is thought to be due to decreased pancreatic β-cell function, decreased insulin sensitivity, and higher body mass index (BMI) with age.4 The normal aging process is also associated with decreased vascular compliance and endothelial dysfunction, which contribute to the development of PIH by disrupting these physiological cardiovascular adaptations to pregnancy.7 As maternal age increases, the risk of preterm birth and LBW due to decreased cardiovascular capacity causing poor placental perfusion may increase.4 Placenta abruption is related to aging of the uterine blood vessels and is also associated with chronic hypertension, a chronic disease caused by age. Placenta previa is associated with an increase in parity, which primarily increases with age.1 Collectively, these mechanisms suggest that maternal age is an important determinant of adverse obstetric outcomes.

Traditionally, studies have classified pregnancies as “advanced maternal age” using predetermined threshold of ≥ 35 years or, in some reports, ≥ 40 years, largely based on historical concerns regarding infertility and chromosomal abnormalities.3,4 However, these definitions were established more than 60 years ago and may no longer reflect current demographic and health characteristics. In Republic of Korea (South Korea), the proportion of maternal age ≥ 35 years has increased substantially in recent decades.2 Given the marked delay in childbearing age and ongoing demographic changes, there is a clear need to reevaluate whether the traditional threshold of 35 years remains appropriate.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the maternal cut-off age at which adverse obstetric outcomes increase significantly in Korea. Using population-based data from the Korean National Health Insurance (KNHI) database, we analyzed the incidence of key complications across maternal ages, stratified by parity, to provide evidence for clinically meaningful definition of advanced maternal age in this population.

METHODS

Data characteristics

Almost all Koreans are covered by the health insurance policies of the KNHI managed by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service, except for 3% of the population covered by the Medical Aid Program. Thus, the KNHI claims database contains claims information for most Koreans except for uninsured procedures such as plastic surgery. As part of the National Health Insurance Corporation healthcare system, the national health-screening program for infants and children, which enrolls eligible children aged 4 to 80 months, began in 2007, and is composed of seven consecutive health examinations according to age (4–9, 9–18, 18–30, 30–42, 42–54, 54–66, and 66–80 months). The national health-screening program consists of history taking, developmental screening, anthropometric examination, visual acuity, physical examination, anticipatory guidance, and a dental examination based on the child's age at the time of the visit.

Dataset

The KNHI claims database was linked to the data of their offspring contained within the National Health Screening Program for Infants and Children (NHSP-IC) database. A flowchart of patient enrollment is shown in Fig. 1. Using the KNHI claims database, we identified all women who delivered singleton infants between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2021. Women were excluded from analysis if their offspring did not undergo at least one of the seven consecutive NHSP-IC or had missing data (dataset 1).

Participant flow chart of the total population.

Data on women’s health conditions, including obstetric diagnoses, were obtained from the KNHI claims database using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition, codes: PIH (O13–O16), GDM (O24.4, O24.9), placenta previa (O44), and placenta abruption (O45). Data on neonatal outcomes, including preterm birth and birth weight, were extracted from the NHSP-IC database. Preterm birth was defined as having a gestational age < 37 weeks, and LBW was defined as birth weight less than 2.5 kg.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were to determine the incidence of adverse obstetric outcomes by maternal age and to identify the maternal cut-off age at which adverse obstetric outcomes increase rapidly in Korean women. The adverse obstetric outcomes analyzed were as follows: PIH, GDM, placenta previa, placenta abruption, preterm birth, and LBW. PIH includes gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and eclampsia. A composite outcome was defined as any of PIH, GDM, placenta previa, placenta abruption, preterm delivery, or LBW.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as percentages. Clinical characteristics were compared using the ANOVA for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A multivariable logistic regression analysis model was used to estimate the adjusted odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for adverse pregnancy outcomes. Age-outcome associations were examined primarily with maternal age as a continuous variable. In additional dichrotomized analyses, maternal age was categorized at the outcome-specific cut-off identified from receiver operating characteristics (ROCs) analysis (e.g., < 33 vs. ≥ 33 years for GDM); the reference group comprised women at or below the cut-off for each outcome. The model was adjusted for major maternal pre-existing conditions, including chronic hypertension and pre-gestational diabetes, as potential confounders. A ROC curve was drawn to present the age cut-off value for adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Statistical analyses were performed using statistical analysis system (SAS) software for Windows, version 9.4 (SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Catholic Medical Center (approval No. VC23ZCSI0263). Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

DISCUSSION

This study identified the maternal cut-off age for an increased risk of adverse obstetric outcomes, including PIH, GDM, placenta previa, placental abruption, preterm delivery, and LBW, as follows: 32 to 35 years in all women, 32 to 33 years in nulliparous women, and 34 to 36 years in multiparous women. Rather than identifying a single cut-off age, our findings show that the threshold for elevated obstetric risk differs by outcome and parity status—ranging from 32 to 36 years—highlighting that certain complications may increase before or after the conventional 35-year mark.

Historically, advanced maternal age has been defined as ≥ 35 years, based on the expected date of birth. This threshold was established based on the evidence of decreased fertility and increased risk of genetic abnormalities identified in children born by women over 35 years of age.8 Several large studies have supported this threshold, reporting increased risks of GDM, PIH, preterm birth, placenta previa, and LBW in women aged ≥ 35 or ≥ 40 years.2,4

However, some investigations have proposed higher thresholds. A retrospective study from Spain suggested 40 years as the critical age for increased obstetric risks, while a Canadian study reported that the risk of preterm delivery was highest in women older than 40 years and lowest in those aged 30–34 years.9,10

In contrast, other studies have indicated that risk increases even earlier than 35 years. A retrospective analysis of 11,343 nulliparous women found that those older than 28 years had significantly higher risks of composite outcomes-including cesarean delivery, preterm birth, prolonged premature rupture of membranes, and intrauterine growth restriction-compared with younger women.11 Another study, using women aged 24–29 years as the reference group, reported significant increases in GDM, preeclampsia, placental abruption, placenta previa, and preterm birth among women aged 30-23 years, suggesting a positive linear association between age and adverse outcomes.12 Similarly, a nationwide Finnish study employing age-specific analysis identified thresholds below 35 years, such as 25 years for GDM and 33 years for gestational hypertension, underscoring that risk rises along a continuum rather than abruptly at 35 years.13

Several studies in Korea have also examined maternal age and obstetric risk. A previous national study reported significantly higher rates of GDM, preeclampsia, preterm labor, placenta previa, placental abruption, and LBW among women aged ≥ 35 years.2 However, that analysis was limited to dichotomous comparisons of ≥ 35 versus < 35 years. Another study involving 29,760 Korean women found that the incidence of GDM, placenta previa, and preterm delivery was already significantly higher in women aged 30–34 years compared to those aged 20–29 years.14 In addition, a study of approximately 25,000 Korean women reported that maternal age ≥ 35 years was associated with LBW, low Apgar scores, and chromosomal abnormalities, while maternal age ≥ 30 years was significantly associated with GDM, placenta previa, cesarean section, preterm delivery, and neonatal intensive care unit admission.15

Studies from other Asian countries provide further context. Research from China and Japan demonstrated proportional increases in adverse outcomes with maternal age but did not propose a clear threshold.16,17 A Taiwanese study applied arbitrary cut-offs of 35 or 40 years, reporting higher complication rates compared with younger women, but did not evaluate age-specific patterns within the < 35 year group.18 Compared with these studies, our analysis provides more precise, data-driven cut-offs tailored to the Korean population.

Our results extend prior literature by analyzing maternal age as a continuous variable across the entire reproductive age spectrum, rather than applying a fixed cut-off. By stratifying outcomes according to parity, we identified outcome- and parity-specific threshold, which enhance clinical applicability.

The slightly later thresholds for GDM and PIH in Korean women compared with Finnish women may reflect both demographic and biological differences.13 In Korea, the mean maternal age at first birth reached 33.5 years in 2021, much higher than in Finland (29 years during 2005–2014).19 Given that the prevalence of GDM in Korea increased from 4.1% in 2004 to > 10.5% in 2011 and exceeded 15% by 2017, this demographic shift likely contributed to the higher observed thresholds.20

Furthermore, Asian women, including Koreans, are characterized by lower BMI but greater visceral adiposity, reduced β-cell reserve, and higher insulin resistance at equivalent BMI, which may delay the clinical manifestation of GDM despite underlying biological susceptibility.21,22 For PIH, the later threshold observed in Korean multiparas (34 years) compared with Finnish women (33 years) may reflect lower baseline blood pressure and BMI in Asian populations, which could delay the onset of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy.23

Therefore, the relatively later cut-off age observed in our Korean cohort may reflect a combination of delayed childbearing patterns and intrinsic physiological buffering at younger ages. This underscores the importance of defining maternal age thresholds within specific racial, metabolic, and demographic contexts.

Unlike prior studies that designated 35 or 40 years as the threshold for advanced maternal age based on historical conventions, our study adopts a data-driven approach to identify age-specific inflection points for each obstetric outcome. Therefore, our results should not be interpreted as a downward revision of the traditional high-risk threshold, but rather as an effort to provide clinically meaningful, outcome-specific risk stratification. This study is particularly relevant in Asian populations, including Koreans, who exhibit distinctive reproductive age patterns, metabolic characteristics, and perinatal outcomes compared to Western cohorts.

This results of this study are expected to serve as an opportunity to once again sound an alarm in the medical community, which has become insensitive to the definition of advanced maternal age, as the number of elderly gravida patients is increasing rapidly. The findings of this study will be useful in the counseling and treatment of high-risk groups who do not meet the traditional advanced maternal age criteria and may help reduce adverse obstetric outcomes.

Our study has a few limitations owing to its retrospective nature. Our study included multicenter hospitals from various regions, making it difficult to standardize the diagnostic tools and treatment protocols across different hospitals, which may have led to detection bias. Moreover, as this study was based on national data, we could not obtain detailed information about individual physical characteristics, BMI, and underlying medical conditions. However, a previous study has shown that maternal age itself is an independent risk factor that increases adverse obstetric outcomes even after adjusting for confounding factors (obesity, smoking, parity and assisted reproductive technology [ART]).9 In addition, although BMI is a well-known risk factor, it generally increases with maternal age, and this collinearity suggests that adjusting for BMI would likely attenuate the associations but not fundamentally alter the maternal age thresholds identified in this study. Therefore, while the lack of BMI adjustment remains a limitation, it is unlikely to invalidate the main findings. Since this study was conducted on the entire population of Korea, all data, including fetal anomalies, were subject to analysis. Although these anomalies are not risk factors for preeclampsia or GDM, they may affect preterm delivery or LBW outcomes.

However, this study also has several strengths. One key strength is its large sample size, which included more than 1,800,000 deliveries. Our data were obtained from the KNHI Program, which covers 97% of the Korean population. Therefore, this study provides more generalizable findings for pregnancies. Importantly, we stratified analyses by parity (nulliparous vs multiparous), enabling outcome- and parity-specific cut-off ages and improving the clinical applicability of our results for counseling and surveillance. This study remains significant as a large-scale population study, even with the relatively modest area under the curve. In contrast to previous studies that found that high-risk pregnancies were females aged 35 or older, this study is significant because it also assessed obstetric risk in females under 35 years of age. Although the cut-off age at which adverse obstetric outcomes significantly increase is relatively low, the negative predictive value is relatively high, as shown in Table 3. This is meaningful because it indicates that pregnancies at ages lower than the cut-off age are relatively safe regarding the studied adverse obstetric outcomes. Further, the study provided specific information on the maternal age-related risk of each disease that requires more attention during pregnancy. These findings may help improve neonatal pregnancy outcomes.

In conclusion, it is important to establish a threshold age for high-risk pregnancies that can be used in clinical practice and that can be adjusted over time in regard to climatic and environmental changes over time. This study focused on establishing a cut off for advanced maternal age in Korea, with the aim of improving obstetric outcomes in the future. Our findings highlight the potential for obstetric complications among patients in their early 30s, which has been overlooked in clinical practice.
