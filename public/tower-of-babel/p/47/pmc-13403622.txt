Background

Aquatic vegetables are freshwater vascular plants with edible organs, which are important specialty crops in China due to their unique nutritional value and economic significance. China has a long history of cultivating aquatic vegetables, ranking first in the world in both cultivation area and yield, and forming a distinctive sector in the national vegetable industry [1]. These vegetables play a vital role in ensuring the supply for high-quality vegetables, enriching the diversity of the vegetable market during the spring and summer off-seasons, and generating foreign exchange through exports [2]. Eleocharis dulcis (Burm. f.) Trin. ex Hensch. (E. dulcis), commonly known as water chestnut, is an economically important aquatic vegetable belonging to the Cyperaceae family. As a perennial aquatic vegetable, it has both medicinal and nutritional value, with its underground corms being sweet and juicy, rich in starch, sugars, proteins, vitamin C, calcium, phosphorus, and iron. These corms are suitable for fresh consumption and cooking. And the plant also exhibits multiple health–promoting functions, such as relieving thirst, aiding digestion, cooling heat, improving eyesight, and eliminating phlegm [3]. Furthermore, E. dulcis can be processed into products like vermicelli, syrup, wine, and candied fruits, which are popular in domestic and international markets. E. dulcis has been cultivated in China for thousands of years, with its main planting area concentrated in the Yangtze River basin and southern provinces [4]. As one of the main aquatic vegetables in Jiangxi Province, it has abundant local germplasm resources, among which "Sanjiang Water Chestnut" from Nanchang and "Quannan Water Chestnut" from Ganzhou, are well-known and recognized as nationally important agricultural products [5]. Currently, research on E. dulcis is relatively limited, focusing mainly on its genomic basis and related applied fields. In terms of genetic resource research, Jiang et al. used RAPD and ISSR molecular markers to analyze the genetic diversity of 24 E. dulcis accessions and classified them into five groups [6, 7]. Zhang et al. optimized the ISSR reaction system and applied it to evaluate the genetic diversity of 35 E. dulcis germplasm resources, dividing them into two major categories-only one accession from Fuzhou, Jiangxi, belonged to the first category, while the rest were grouped into the second category [8]. He et al. developed SSR markers for E. dulcis through transcriptome sequencing and identified 2570 SSR loci [9]. Chen et al. cloned and expressed the chalcone isomerase gene of E. dulcis to provide support for postharvest preservation research [10]. The Institute of Biotechnology, Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences, systematically studied the structure, function, and expression activity of genes related to starch synthesis in E. dulcis [11–13]. Collectively, existing studies on E. dulcis have mainly focus on cultivar breeding [14–16], tissue culture [17, 18], cultivation techniques [19], physiological and biochemical characteristics [20, 21], and the regulatory mechanisms of starch-related synthetic genes [22–24]. Organelle genomes, derived from ancient endosymbiotic bacteria, include mitochondria and chloroplasts, which play essential roles in key biological processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, and ATP synthesis. As the “energy supply station” of eukaryotic cells, mitochondria have semi-autonomous characteristics, with independent genetic material and regulatory mechanisms that are critical for cellular energy metabolism [25]. Compared with animal mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes), plant mitogenomes show greater variability in size and more complex genetic composition, reflecting richer genetic information [26]. Plant mitochondria generally follow a maternal inheritance pattern, although exceptions exist, such as paternal inheritance in Chlorophytum comosum and Cucumis sativus [27, 28]. The typical characteristics of higher plant mitogenomes include approximately 10% coding sequences, low gene density, and abundant repetitive sequences and RNA editing sites, which provide important resources for species identification, phylogenetic analysis, and genetic research [29, 30]. In addition, plant mitochondria play significant roles in responding to environmental stress, regulating energy metabolism, and promoting plant growth potential [31, 32]. Therefore, the assembly and in-depth study of plant mitogenomes are of great significance for understanding plant evolution and genetic regulation. Compared with chloroplast genomes, the assembly of plant mitogenomes is more challenging, and relatively few mitogenomes have been published to date. Plant mitogenomes are prone to genetic variation, with assembled sizes ranging from 66 kb to 11.3 Mb, showing significant differences even among closely related species [33, 34]. Although the nuclear genome [35] and chloroplast genome [36] of E. dulcis have been released on NCBI, its mitogenome remains unreported. This research gap has severely limited our understanding of mitogenomic evolution, structural dynamics, and phylogenetic relationships in Cyperaceae, especially for aquatic vegetable crops. Accordingly, this study aimed to fill this gap by assembling and annotating the complete mitogenome of E. dulcis using a hybrid strategy combining Illumina short-read and PacBio long-read sequencing data. We systematically analyzed the structural characteristics of the E. dulcis mitogenome, including gene composition, repetitive sequences, codon usage bias, and RNA editing events. Through comparative genomic analysis with other Cyperaceae species and major plant lineages, we explored the evolutionary status and structural dynamics of the E. dulcis mitogenome. Additionally, we calculated the non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates of homologous genes and constructed a phylogenetic tree to clarify the phylogenetic relationship of E. dulcis within Cyperaceae. These findings not only provide the first mitogenome data for the genus Eleocharis but also lay a solid data foundation for the conservation and utilization of Cyperaceae germplasm resources, as well as the genetic improvement of E. dulcis.

Results

Mitogenome assembly of E. dulcis

The complete mitogenome of E. dulcis was successfully assembled using a hybrid strategy combining Illumina short-read and PacBio long-read sequencing data. The assembled mitogenome was 2,166,862 base pair (bp) in length, with a base composition of 28.45% A, 28.46% T, 21.57% G, and 21.51% C, resulting in a AT content of 56.92% and a GC content of 43.08%-consistent with the conserved GC content of Cyperaceae sepcies.

A total of 78 genes were annotated in the E. dulcis mitogenome, including 33 protein-coding genes (PCGs) of diverse functional categories, 35 tRNA genes, eight rRNA genes and two pseudo-genes (Fig. 1). The 33 PCGs had a total base length of 29,580 bp, with an average length of 896 bp, accounting for 1.37% of the genome length. Based on their functional characteristics, these PCGs were classified into eight categories: three cytochrome genes, nine NADH-related genes, five ATP synthesis-related genes, one Ubiquinol-Cytochrome c reductase gene, three Cytochrome c oxidase genes, one maturase gene, one transport membrane protein gene, and eleven ribosomal protein genes (Table 1). The eight rRNA genes identified in the E. dulcis mitogenome, included three rrn26 genes (total length 3577 bp), two rrn18 genes (total length 1987 bp), and three rrn5 genes (total length 116 bp), with a GC content of 52.93%. The total length of the rRNA genes is 15,053 bp, accounting for 0.69% of the total mitogenome length. Meanwhile, 35 tRNA genes were detected, with a GC content of 51%, including multiple copies of different tRNA types: five copies of trnD-GTC, four copies of trnE-TTC, six copies of trnM-CAT, two copies of trnP-TGG, two copies of trnS-GCT, two copies of trnS-TGA, and three copies of trnY-GTA. Repeat sequence

Abundant repetitive sequences were detected in the E. dulcis mitogenome, including simple sequence repeats (SSRs), dispersed repeats, and tandem repeats. A total of 532 SSRs were identified (Fig. 2A), among which tetranucleotide repeats are the most abundant, followed by dinucleotide, trinucleotide, mononucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeats. The proportions of each type of SSR were as follows: mononucleotide (10.15%), dinucleotide (30.83%), trinucleotide (10.71%), tetranucleotide (41.73%), pentanucleotide (4.89%), and hexanucleotide (1.69%). Using Reputer software, we identified 23,159 dispersed repeats of ≥ 30 bp in the E. dulcis mitogenome (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Sheet 2), including 10,947 forward repeats and 12,212 palindromic repeats. The longest forward repeat and palindromic repeat were 16,674 bp and 4467 bp, respectively, and the highest proportion of dispersed repeats was in the 30 to 199 bp length. No complementary repeats or reverse repeats were identified in the E. dulcis mitogenome. These results indicate that the E. dulcis mitogenome contains abundant repetitive sequences (Fig. 2D), which may promote genomic rearrangements, affect genome size, and are closely related to genomic function and evolution.

Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis

The RSCU value analysis showed that the E. dulcis mitogenome exhibited a consistent codon preference, with RSCU value of amino acids ranging from 0 to 2 (Fig. 3). A total of 31 high-frequency codons (RSCU value > 1) and seven strongly preferred overlapping codons (RSCU value > 1.5) were identified. Notably, arginine (Arg) was the most frequently used overlapping codon in the E. dulcis mitogenome, which is a key feature of its codon usage pattern.Fig. 3RSCU value of amino acids in the E. dulcis mitogenome

Prediction of RNA editing events

RNA editing sites were prevalent in the PCGs of the E. dulcis mitogenome. A total of 212 RNA editing events were identified from 29 PCGs, mainly manifesting as C to T transitions. The ccmFc gene had the most editing sites (23), followed by matR (20 sites). In contrast, the atp8, cox3, nad3, and rps7 genes each contained only one RNA editing site (Fig. 4). Further analysis showed that the editing events were concentrated on the first and second base positions of the codons, and these RNA editing events led to changes in amino acids, such as leucine (L) to phenylalanine (F), threonine (T) to isoleucine (I), serine (S) to phenylalanine (F), histidine (H) to tyrosine (Y), arginine (R) to cysteine (C), and arginine (R) to tryptophan (W) (Supplementary Table 2). Most of these amino acid changes introduced more hydrophobic amino acids into protein structures, thereby altering protein hydrophilicity, which may play a key role in regulating mitochondrial gene expression and protein function.Fig. 4RNA editing site prediction in E. dulcis

Substitution rates of PCGs

To assess the evolutionary rates of mitochondrial PCGs, we calculated the non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates of 31 homologous PCGs among Cyperus stolonifera, Carex pseudochinensis, Rhynchospora tenuis, and E. dulcis. The average Ka/Ks ratio for these 31 genes was 0.369, and most genes exhibited a Ka/Ks value less than 1, indicating that these genes are under strong purifying selection during evolution, reflecting their high evolutionary conservation. Only the ccmB gene had a Ka/Ks value of 1.22, indicating that this gene is subject to positive selection effects (Fig. 5), which may be related to its specific functional adaptation during the evolution of E. dulcis. The 31 PCGs in the E. dulcis mitogenome showed consistent conservative characteristics with the corresponding genes in other three Cyperaceae species, suggesting that these genes are less affected by genetic variation among related species.Fig. 5The Ka/Ks value in four representative genera of the Cyperaceae family

Synteny analysis

Synteny analysis was conducted to explore the structural dynamics of Cyperaceae mitogenomes. Using Mauve software to compare the mitogenomes of six Cyperaceae species, the results indicated low consistency in synteny among these species (Fig. 6). Only 66 conserved syntenic segments longer than 1000 bp were shared among the six species, which is consistent with the results of Dot plot analysis (Fig. 7), where the sequence similarity between two mitochondrial genomes was low. These syntenic regions and inverted structures not only reveal the conserved patterns of Cyperaceae mitogenomes but also reflect the evolutionary relationships among species and the evolutionary history of the Cyperaceae family, providing new perspectives for exploring their genetic foundations and evolutionary process. Overall, the synteny analysis revealed frequent structural rearrangements but low sequence divergence among Cyperaceae mitogenomes, which is a key evolutionary feature of this family.Fig. 6Mitochondrial collinearity of E. dulcis, and other five typical plants in CyperaceaeFig. 7Dot plot among E. dulcis, and other five typical plants in Cyperaceae

Mitogenome comparative analysis among Cyperaceae

To explore the evolutionary characteristics of the E. dulcis mitogenome, we compared the mitogenome features (genome size and GC content) of 10 eudicot plants, 11 monocot plants (including eight Cyperaceae species), four gymnosperms, two ferns, four mosses, and four algae (Supplementary Table 3). The results showed that gymnosperms had the highest GC content, followed by angiosperms (which showed relatively consistent GC content), while mosses had the smallest mitogenome size, and algae had the lowest GC content. The genome size and GC content of mosses were similar, with a relatively high proportion of PCGs among the genes. Angiosperm mitogenomes exhibited significant size variation but similar GC content (Fig. 8). These data suggest that during the evolutionary process, the size of plant mitogenomes has significantly increased, mainly due to the continuous accumulation of non-coding sequences and the presence of numerous cis-splicing introns. At the same time, the content of mitochondrial tRNA genes has shown a decreasing trend throughout the evolutionary history of plants. Focusing on Cyperaceae species, the size of the E. dulcis mitogenome (2,166,862 bp) was significantly different from that of the other three published Cyperaceae species (C. stolonifera, C. pseudochinensis, and R. tenuis), whose mitogenome sizes ranged from 927,413 to 2,166,862 bp. However, all four Cyperaceae species maintained a relatively conserved GC content of approximately 43%, which is a distinctively feature of Cyperaceae mitogenomes. In terms of the number of PCGs, E. dulcis had 33 PCGs, C. stolonifera had 36, C. pseudochinens had 31, and R. tenuis had 35. A total of 29 PCGs were shared among the four species, including core functional genes such as atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, cob, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFn, cox1, cox2, cox3, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, matR, mttB, rps1, rps3, rps7, rps12, rps19, and rpl5. Comparative analysis of the PCGs among these Cyperaceae species revealed instances of gene loss and pseudogenization: sdh3, sdh4, rps8, rps10, rpl6 and rpl10 were lost in all four species, while rps14 and rpl16 were pseudogenized only in E. dulcis (Fig. 9).Fig. 8Mitochondrial genome size and GC content variation of 32 typical plantsFig. 9Mitochondrial PCGs among 32 typical plants from six different groups (eudicot plants, monocot plants, gymnosperms, ferns, mosses, and algae)

Phylogenetic analysis

To clarify the phylogenetic position of E. dulcis within Cyperaceae and among major plant lineages, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using 31 conserved PCGs from E. dulcis and 37 representative species. Four algae species (Pedinomonas Olivac, Prototheca wickerhamii, Chara vulgaris, and Medakamo hakoo) were used as the out-group, while the other species included 10 eudicots, 11 monocots (including eight Cyperaceae species), four gymnosperms, two ferns and four mosses. The phylogenetic tree showed high reliability, with the average bootstrap support value of five nodes reaching 95.76% (Fig. 10). Within the Cyperaceae clade, E. dulcis and C. esculentus were clustered on the same branch node, confirming that they are closely related, which is consistent with the results of synteny analysis. This phylogenetic relationship provides important insights into the evolutionary position of E. dulcis in the Cyperaceae family and the evolutionary history of the genus Eleocharis.Fig.10Phylogenetic tree derived from 37 species of different clades

Organelle gene transfer

The transfer of genetic material between organelles is a common phenomenon in plant mitogenomes, and these chloroplast-derived sequence fragments usually have relatively low conservation in plants [37]. The chloroplast genome of E. dulcisis 199,711 bp in length, with the sequence accession number PV424040, containing 84 PCGs, 39 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes (Fig. 11A). We identified 69 homologous fragments shared between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of E. dulcis, with a total length of 18,202 bp, accounting for approximately 0.84% of the E. dulcis mitogenome (Fig. 11B, Supplementary sheet 3). The length of these homologous fragments ranged from 35 to 4032 bp. Annotation of these homologous sequences identified six complete transferred genes, including one PCG (ndhC) and nine tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnL-CAA, trnI-CAU, trnN-GUU, trnP-UGG, trnS-GGA, trnW-CCA, trnH-GUG and trnM-CAU). Additionally, partial sequence of 17 PCGs, two tRNA genes, and three rRNA genes were transferred from the chloroplast to the mitogenome. Notably, in the E. dulcis chloroplast genome, ndhC plays a role in the subunits of the NADH dehydrogenase-like complex, and these eleven tRNA genes may have been lost or pseudogenized in the chloroplast genome. Discussion

Hybrid assembly strategy of plant mitogenome

Plant mitogenomes are characterized by complex structures, frequent recombination, abundant repetitive sequences and extensive non-coding regions, making them a challenging hotspot in plant genomics [39]. The selection of sequencing and assembly technologies directly determines the integrity, accuracy and efficiency of mitochondrial genome assembly, and further affects the reliability of subsequent comparative genomics, phylogenetic and evolutionary analyses [40]. Long-read third-generation sequencings (TGSs) represented by PacBio HiFi have inherent advantages of ultra-long reads, which perfectly match the structural complexity of plant mitogenomes and serve as a key breakthrough to overcome traditional assembly bottlenecks. Long fragment repeats, tandem repeats and frequent genomic recombination events widely distributed in plant mitogenomes make it difficult for short-read sequencing to span repetitive regions, resulting in fragmented assemblies and failure to restore the natural circular or reticular topological structures [41, 42]. In this study, PacBio HiFi reads ranging from several kilo-bases to tens of kilo-bases were adopted, which can completely cover long repetitive regions in mitogenomes (Supplementary Fig. 1). This effectively avoids sequence fragmentation, mis-assembly and missing assembly of repetitive regions commonly seen in short-read assembly. Meanwhile, PacBio HiFi features high base accuracy comparable to second-generation sequencing, yielding high-quality original sequences without extensive pre-correction, and greatly reducing the cost and difficulty of subsequent genome polishing [43]. In this research, we assembled TGS using PAMT2 software and also verified the assembly results with HiMt software, confirming the accuracy of this experimental result (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 3).

Although short in read length, second-generation sequencing (SGS) possesses outstanding advantages including high sequencing depth, ultra-high accuracy and low cost, forming ideal complementarity with TGS and guaranteeing the reliability of mitogenome assembly [44]. Abundant AT-rich sequences and short repeats in non-coding regions of plant mitochondria are prone to sequencing errors in TGS. SGS data were used to polish the primary TGS assembly, which markedly reduced base errors across the genome, especially in error-prone regions such as non-coding regions and simple sequence repeats, thus greatly improving sequence reliability [45]. Taking E. dulcis as the research object, this study integrated SGS and TGS datasets and applied multiple assembly strategies to obtain high-quality mitochondrial genome assemblies. The results fully demonstrate the superior performance of combined long-read and short-read sequencing in plant mitogenome research, and provide a feasible paradigm for efficient and accurate assembly of plant mitogenomes.

Mitochondrial genome structure characteristics

Differences in plant mitogenomes are mainly reflected in total base length, gene content, gene arrangement, GC content, and molecular structure [46, 47]. Most plant mitogenomes are single-ring structures, while some species, such as Paphiopedilum micranthum and Salvia japonica, have multi-ring structures [48, 49]. In this study, we obtained a linear mitogenome of E. dulcis with a length of 2,166,862 bp, which is the first reported mitogenome for the genus Eleocharis, enriching the mitogenome data of Cyperaceae [50]. The GC content of plant mitogenomes reflects the adaptive characteristics of species, and the GC content of terrestrial plant mitogenomes ranges from 23.9% to 50.5% [30]. Our comparative analysis across major plant lineages showed the gymnosperms have the highest mitochondrial GC content, angiosperms have relatively consistent GC content, mosses have the smallest mitogenomes, and algae have the lowest GC content. The differences in mitogenome size among plants may be mainly attributed to repetitive sequences, with coding regions having a relatively small impact [51]. Notably, Cyperaceae species including E. dulcis, maintained a highly conserved GC content of approximately 43%, with small fluctuations, and the GC content of their mitogenome was similar to that of their protein genes, indicating that the GC content of Cyperaceae mitogenomes is highly conserved during evolution. This conservation may be related to the functional stability of mitochondrial genes in Cyperaceae species, which is crucial for their adaptation to aquatic and semi-aquatic environments.

Repeat sequences features

SSRs are widely distributed in mitogenomes and play important roles in plant evolutionary adaptation, gene expression regulation, and phenotypic trait formation [34, 52]. Compared with chloroplast and nuclear genomes, mitogenomes evolve at a slower rate, making mitochondrial molecular markers more accurate for species identification [53]. The 532 SSRs identified in the E. dulcis mitogenome provide valuable reference loci for the identification of E. dulcis germplasm resources and genetic diversity analysis. In addition, the E. dulcis mitogenome contains a large number of dispersed repeats longer than 300 bp, while C. stolonifera has relatively few such repeats [54]. The longest dispersed repeat in the E. dulcis mitogenome is 16,674 bp, accounting for 0.77% of the total mitogenome length, indicating that the E. dulcis mitogenome has a more complex structure, higher structural polymorphism, and greater gene sequence variation compared with C. stolonifera. These dispersed repeats may not only cause gene recombination but also lead to gene mutations, thereby regulating the function and expression levels of mitochondrial genes.

Diversity and polymorphism of mitochondrial genome

Codons play a crucial role in organisms, participating in protein synthesis, regulating gene expression, and promoting genetic variation [55]. During protein translation, unique environmental factors and species-specific influences lead to codon usage bias, and the preference for specific synonymous codons plays an important role in shaping the genetic characteristics of species [56]. Our RSCU analysis showed that Cyperaceae species exhibit similar codon preference, which may indicate that these species are subject to similar evolutionary selection pressures, reflecting their close evolutionary relationship. Arginine (Arg) was the most frequently used overlapping codon in the E. dulcis mitogenome, which is a specific feature of its codon usage pattern. This preference may be related to the adaptation of E. dulcis to its aquatic environment, as codon usage bias can affect translational efficiency and protein function, further regulating the physiological and biochemical processes of the plant.

RNA editing is an important post-transcriptional modification that can change genetic information, so a significant portion of proteins produced by plant mitochondria may come from partially edited transcripts. Hydrophilic amino acids play a key role in promoting protein folding, while a decrease in the proportion of hydrophilic amino acids is associated with an increase in protein structure stability [57]. In the E. dulcis mitogenome, most RNA editing events lead to the conversion of hydrophilic amino acids to hydrophobic amino acids, resulting in increased protein hydrophobicity. This change may enhance the stability of mitochondrial proteins, optimize their function in the aquatic environment, and thus improve the adaptability of E. dulcis.

Evolutionary classification based on mitochondrial genome

Plant mitogenomes have unique evolutionary characteristics, including structural rearrangements, significant gene loss or gain, and the transfer of mitochondrial genes to the nuclear genome [58], along with a very low nucleotide mutation rate, which provides unique information for phylogenetic analysis [59]. Analyzing the homology of plant mitochondrial genes can effectively reveal the phylogenetic relationships among species [60, 61]. Our phylogenetic analysis based on 31 conserved PCGs confirmed that E. dulcis is closely related to C. esculentus within the Cyperaceae family, which clarifies the evolutionary position of E. dulcis and provides important insights into the evolutionary history of the genus Eleocharis. This result is consistent with the synteny analysis, which showed low sequence divergence but high structural rearrangement among Cyperaceae mitogenomes, indicating that structural rearrangement is an important driving force in the evolution of Cyperaceae mitogenomes.

Throughout the evolutionary process of angiosperm, mitogenomes have undergone frequent gene loss, resulting in a significant reduction in shared DNA among existing species [60]. Our study found that the genes responsible for electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation subunits are relatively stable, while genes encoding succinate dehydrogenase and ribosomal proteins are more likely to be lost from the plant mitogenome. The content of ribosomal protein genes varies significantly among different plants, which usually reflects different instances of gene loss and transfer to the nuclear genome [62]. In Cyperaceae species, six genes (sdh3, rpl2, rpl10, rps7, rps13, and rps19) are nearly completely absent from the mitogenome, indicating that the loss of these genes may be compensated by other genes during rapid evolution of Cyperaceae, ensuring the normal function of mitochondria.

Methods

Data sources

Raw sequencing data for E. dulcis from second-generation Illumina and third-generation PacBio were downloaded from the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) with the Bioproject ID PRJCA018613 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/search?searchTerm=%20PRJCA018613).

Mitogenome assembly and annotation

First, raw PacBio sequencing data were processed using seqkit software with the “-s, -p” parameter to extract the whole PacBio HiFi data (29.19 Gigabase), PacBio long reads were first de novo assembled into raw mitochondrial contigs using PMAT2 [41], and the Bandage (v0.8.1) software was used for de novo assembly [63]. Next, the assembled Hifi scaffold data sequences were aligned using minimap2 software to identify the draft mitogenome of E. dulcis (https://github.com/lh3/minimap2), and Nextpolish was used to correct the results [64]. Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) [65] was utilized to align the Illumina sequencing data (38.77 Gigabase) to the corrected PacBio sequence. Unicycler (v0.4.8) software was employed to assemble the Illumina short reads and the error-corrected PacBio long reads under default parameters [66]. After manual verification and correction, the mitogenome of E. dulcis was finally obtained.

The annotation process for this mitochondrial genome is as follows: Firstly, PCGs and ribosomal RNA genes are aligned with the published mitochondrial reference sequences of Cyperaceae using BLAST, and manually corrected based on information from closely related species. Secondly, tRNAscan-SE software (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/) [67] is used to annotate transfer RNA genes. Finally, the NCBI open reading frame finder tool (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gorf/gorf.html) is utilized to predict open reading frames (ORFs), with a minimum length threshold set at 102 bp, to eliminate redundant sequences and ORFs that overlap with known functional genes. Alignment sequences longer than 300 bp are mapped to the NCBI non-redundant protein database. GeSeq software [68] is employed for mitochondrial annotation, and Geneious Prime software [69] is used to manually correct the annotation results. OGDRAW software (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) is used to draw the circular map of the mitochondrial genome. Our assembled mitogenome of E. dulcis have been submitted to NCBI with the accession number PV430310.

RSCU

The base percentages of the E. dulcis mitogenome were calculated, and the GenBank format files were analyzed using PhyloSuite software (http://phylosuite.jushengwu.com/dongzhang0725.github.io/documentation/) to extract PCGs [70]. The codon usage of mitochondrial PCGs was analyzed using MEGA_12.0.11 (https://www.megasoftware.net/), and RSCU values were calculated. The RSCU value is the ratio of the observed frequency to the expected frequency of a specific codon when encoding the same amino acid. An RSCU value greater than 1 indicates that the codon is preferred, with larger values indicating a higher preference.

Repeat sequence and codon

SSRs were obtained through the online MISA website [71] (https://webblast.pk-gatersleben.de/misa/), with the following minimum requirements for nucleotide repeat units: mononucleotide repeats must appear at least eight times, dinucleotide repeats at least five times, trinucleotide repeats at least four times, and repeats of four or more nucleotides at least three times. In addition, REPuter software (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) was used to calculate forward repeats, palindromic repeats, reverse repeats, and complementary repeats, with a Hamming distance of 3 and a minimum repeat size of 100 bp for mitochondria [72]. Identification of long tandem repeat sequences was carried out using the Tandem Repeats Finder website [73] (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) with default parameters in the “Advanced” module. CodonW software (v1.4.4) was utilized to analyze RSCU of PCGs (http://codonw.sourceforge.net).

Chloroplast gene transfer and mitogenome characteristics

We first used the fastp software [74] to perform quality assessment on the raw sequencing reads. Subsequently, we employed the GetOrganelle software (https://github.com/Kinggerm/GetOrganelle) [75] to assemble the high-quality cleaned reads into a chloroplast genome, and visualized the contig sequences using the Bandage software [63] to verify the reliability of the assembly results. For genome annotation, we used the annotated chloroplast genome of Eleocharis quinqueflora (OZ060357.1) as the reference sequence, and employed the CPGAVAS2 software (https://47.96.249.172:16019/analyzer/annotate) [76] to complete initial gene prediction. Afterwards, we utilized GeSeq [68] and PGA software [77] to cross-check the annotation results, and manually corrected inconsistent and abnormal annotation sites with the help of UGENE software [78].

BLAST was used to analyze homologous sequences between the mitochondrial and chloroplast genome, and the Circos software package (https://circos.ca/) was used to visualize homologous fragments. To further investigate the evolutionary characteristics of the E. dulcis mitochondrial genome, we compared its PCG composition and GC content with those of other species across major plant lineages.

Phylogeny and synteny

In plant evolution, the loss or gain of PCGs in mitochondria is significant for understanding phylogenetic relationships and ecological status [79]. To explore the phylogenetic relationships of the E. dulcis mitogenome, in addition to the assembled mitogenome of E. dulcis, mitogenomes of Cyperaceae species (such as C. stolonifera), and other families (including Oryza sativa L. from Poaceae, Arabidopsis thaliana from Brassicaceae, and Ginkgo biloba from Ginkgoaceae) were downloaded, with moss plants used as the outgroup.

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using 31 conserved PCGs (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, cob, ccmC, ccmFc, ccmFn, cox1, cox2, cox3, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, matR, mttB, rpl5, rps1, rps12, rps13, rps19, rps3, rps4, and rps7). MAFFT (v7.52) was used for sequence alignment [80], and the phylogenetic tree was constructed usingthe maximum likelihood method (ML) method with MFP as the model in Phylosuite software (http://phylosuite.jushengwu.com/dongzhang0725.github.io/documentation/). Result files were uploaded to Interactive Tree of Life (https://itol.embl.de/) for visualization. Synteny analysis was conducted on the E. dulcis mitogenomes and five representative Cyperaceae species using Mauve software with default parameters, retaining conserved synteny segments longer than 100 bp (https://sourceforge.net/projects/mauve/).

Prediction of RNA editing sites and Ka/Ks value

Potential RNA editing sites of PCGs in the E. dulcis mitogenome were predicted using PREPACT3 software to explore modifications that may affect protein function [81]. The assembled E. dulcis mitogenome was compared with homologous genes from three other representative plant species (C. stolonifera, C. pseudochinensis, and R. tenuis), sequence alignment was performed using MEGA software, and the Ka/Ks values were calculated using DnaSP 6 software to reveal their selection pressure characteristics [82].

Conclusions

In this research, we assembled and annotated the complete mitogenome of E. dulcis for the first time using second- and third- sequencing data, which is the first reported mitogenome for the genus Eleocharis. The E. dulcis mitogenome is a linear molecular structure with a total length of 2,166,862 bp, containing 78 genes (33 PCGs, 8 rRNA genes, 35 tRNA genes, and two pseudogenes). We systematically analyzed the structural characteristics of the E. dulcis mitogenome, including three types of repetitive sequences (SSRs, dispersed repeats, and tandem repeats), RNA editing events, and organelle gene transfer patterns. Comparative genomic analysis across major plant lineages showed that Cyperaceae species maintained a highly conserved GC content (~ 43%) despite significant variations in mitogenome size. Most mitochondrial PCGs were under strong purifying selection, reflecting their evolutionary conservation, while the ccmB gene was subject to positive selection. Synteny analysis revealed frequent structural rearrangements but low sequence divergence among Cyperaceae mitogenomes, and phylogenetic analysis confirmed that E. dulcis is closely related to C. esculentus. These findings not only fill the research gap in the mitogenome of E. dulcis but also provide valuable resources for the conservation and utilization of Cyperaceae germplasm resources, mitochondrial comparative genomics, and genetic improvement of E. dulcis.
