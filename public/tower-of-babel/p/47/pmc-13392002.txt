Dynamic hypergraph network with multi modal fusion for cultural heritage tourism prediction
Authors: Wenhua Yan, Yuyan Han, Xiangluo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCultural heritage tourism network analysis faces core challenges including dynamic high-order association modeling, multi-source heterogeneous data fusion, and multi-dimensional influence prediction. To address these issues, this study proposes DynHyperNet, a dynamic hypergraph network model integrated with multi-modal fusion and multi-task learning. The Dynamic HyperGNN module in DynHyperNet captures the temporal evolution of high-order correlations among cultural heritage sites, tourists, and supporting services, while the CLIP-based multi-modal fusion module fuses visual and textual features of heritage sites to enhance node representation learning. A multi-task collaborative framework is further designed to simultaneously optimize satisfaction prediction, route planning optimization, and other key tasks. Results demonstrate that DynHyperNet outperforms comparative models across multiple metrics: on the Cultural Tourism Dataset, it achieves 93.76% satisfaction prediction accuracy, 92.45% high satisfaction recall rate, and 3.89% route planning optimization MAPE for world cultural heritage sites. Despite its effectiveness, the model exhibits limitations in computational efficiency and adaptability to sparse data scenarios. Future work will focus on lightweight architecture optimization, sparse data adaptation, and integration of external dynamic factors to enhance practical applicability. This study provides a new technical framework for dynamic analysis and intelligent prediction of cultural heritage tourism networks, offering actionable insights for tourism management and sustainable development.Keywords: Dynamic hypergraph neural network, Cultural heritage tourism, Multi-modal fusion, Satisfaction prediction, High-order association modelingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The rapid development of the global cultural heritage tourism industry has not only promoted cultural exchange across regions but also become a key driver for regional economic growth. Cultural heritage, as the core carrier of tourism resources, plays a crucial role in shaping the structure and influence of tourism networks. These factors are directly related to the formulation of heritage protection strategies, the optimal allocation of tourism resources, and the sustainable development of the cultural tourism industry. Accurately analyzing the association patterns of entities within the cultural heritage tourism resource network and scientifically predicting their influence has become a critical task in the field of cultural tourism research1. However, the cultural heritage tourism system is highly complex, with several significant challenges, including the high-order associations between heritage tourist facilities evolving dynamically over time, the difficulty in integrating multi-source heterogeneous data, and the need for influence assessment to consider multi-dimensional metrics2. These characteristics pose considerable challenges to traditional network analysis and prediction models.

In the field of cultural heritage tourism network analysis and prediction, relevant studies have accumulated significantly over time, with distinct stages of development. Early research primarily relied on traditional complex network theories, depicting the association relationships between heritage and tourism facilities using binary structures of nodes and edges. These studies focused on the impact of network topology on tourism flow distribution, providing a fundamental framework for understanding the basic structure of cultural heritage tourism networks3. With advancements in data acquisition technology and the widespread adoption of machine learning methods, research has gradually shifted towards data-driven prediction models. Traditional machine learning algorithms such as support vector machines and random forests have been widely applied in predicting single impact indicators like tourist numbers, transitioning from qualitative analysis to quantitative prediction6. In recent years, graph neural network (GNN) technologies have become a research hotspot due to their powerful ability to model structured data4,5. Models like GCN and GAT have been gradually applied in cultural heritage tourism network analysis, enhancing prediction accuracy by uncovering hidden relationships between nodes, pushing the field towards deeper feature extraction6,7. Despite significant progress, there are still notable shortcomings. Existing models struggle to capture dynamic evolution, integrate multi-modal data, and adapt to multi-dimensional prediction needs, making them insufficient for analyzing and predicting complex cultural heritage tourism systems.

The development of dynamic hypergraph networks and multi-modal fusion technologies provides new ideas to address the above issues. Dynamic hypergraph networks can effectively model high-order associations between multiple nodes through hyperedge structures and capture dynamic evolution characteristics of the network by combining temporal modeling mechanisms8. These networks have shown significant advantages in analyzing complex dynamic systems like transportation and social networks9. Multi-modal fusion technology, on the other hand, can integrate features from different types of data, improving the model’s ability to represent complex entities10. The CLIP model, which performs cross-modal pre-training, has effectively aligned visual and textual features, providing strong support for solving the problem of multi-modal data fusion in cultural heritage studies11. Although some studies have attempted to apply hypergraph networks or multi-modal fusion technologies to the cultural tourism field, no research has yet combined dynamic hypergraph networks, multi-modal fusion technology, and multi-task learning frameworks to analyze and predict cultural heritage tourism networks’ influence12. This gap has prevented the full adaptation of the complex characteristics of cultural heritage tourism systems15.

Based on the existing research and its shortcomings, this study aims to propose a dynamic hypergraph network model, DynHyperNet, for analyzing and predicting cultural heritage tourism networks and their influence. This model addresses the deficiencies in current research through three core goals. First, it constructs a dynamic hypergraph network model to enable high-order dynamic modeling of cultural heritage tourism networks and accurately capture the time-evolving relationships between multiple entities. Second, it designs a multi-modal feature enhancement module that integrates CLIP and graph attention mechanisms, effectively combining multi-modal features and network structure characteristics of cultural heritage, thereby improving node representation. Third, it establishes a multi-task collaborative prediction framework to accurately predict multi-dimensional influence metrics for cultural heritage tourism.

Related work

Cultural heritage tourism network analysis and prediction

Cultural heritage tourism network analysis and prediction is one of the core directions of research for the sustainable development of the cultural tourism industry. Relevant studies have developed a trend of integrating multiple technologies and have achieved several new advancements. In terms of cultural heritage tourism network modeling, early research primarily relied on traditional complex network theories, using binary node-edge structures to depict the association relationships between cultural heritage sites and surrounding tourism facilities11,13. The focus was on analyzing the impact of network density, average path length, and other topological features on the spatial distribution of tourism flows, providing the theoretical foundation for understanding the basic organizational structure of cultural heritage tourism networks.

With the rise of data-driven research paradigms, traditional machine learning algorithms began to be widely applied to tourism influence prediction tasks. Models like support vector machines, random forests, and gradient boosting trees were used for predicting single indicators such as tourist numbers and tourism revenue, thus achieving the transition from qualitative network analysis to quantitative prediction14. In recent years, graph neural networks (GNNs), with their powerful ability to represent structured data, have become a research hotspot in this field. Classic models such as GCN and GAT have been gradually applied to cultural heritage tourism network analysis, improving prediction accuracy by uncovering hidden associations between nodes15. Notably, studies have proposed the TourVaRNN model, which uses variational recurrent neural networks to capture temporal dependencies in tourism data for predicting economic impacts and tourist behavior, thereby improving the relevance of time series predictions. At the same time, the GNNBoost model integrated knowledge graph embeddings to enhance the classification performance of cultural heritage items, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining associative information in GNNs for cultural heritage-related tasks16,17. However, existing network analysis and prediction models still have notable adaptability shortcomings. Although many models incorporate temporal modeling, they have not yet broken free from the limitations of traditional binary graphs, making it difficult to capture high-order associations and dynamic evolution between multiple entities. Additionally, most studies focus on utilizing structured data; even though some research involves multi-modal data, it fails to achieve deep integration with network structure features, resulting in limited node representation capabilities18. Moreover, single-task prediction frameworks still dominate, making it difficult to meet the practical needs of multi-dimensional impact assessments in cultural tourism management, with model generalization capabilities needing improvement.

To address the core issue of single-task prediction frameworks, this study’s Dyn-HyperNet model specifically designs a multi-task collaborative prediction module. By using a shared weight encoder to extract common features, along with regression branches for continuous indicators like tourist numbers and classification branches for discrete indicators like attention levels, it enables collaborative prediction of multi-dimensional indicators. This overcomes the limitation of existing models like TourVaRNN, which focus only on single-dimensional indicators, thus meeting the multi-dimensional evaluation needs of cultural tourism management.

Dynamic hypergraph and multi-modal fusion technologies

The development of dynamic hypergraph networks and multi-modal fusion technologies provides key technical support for solving the complex problems in cultural heritage tourism network analysis. These technologies have shown significant advantages in the analysis of complex systems and continue to evolve. Dynamic graph neural networks, by introducing time-based modeling mechanisms, can effectively capture the temporal evolution characteristics of network structures. These methods have been successfully applied in dynamic systems like traffic flow prediction and social network analysis. The core idea is to divide network features at different time points using temporal slices or temporal attention mechanisms, achieving precise extraction of dynamic features19. Related research has further proposed a heterogeneous dynamic system decomposition method based on graph neural networks, improving the ability to capture the evolutionary patterns of complex dynamic systems by jointly learning interaction rules and latent heterogeneity20.

Hypergraph networks, as an extension of traditional binary graph networks, can directly model high-order associations between multiple nodes through hyperedges, overcoming the limitations of traditional networks in representing multi-agent collaborative interactions21. They have demonstrated superior performance in complex association modeling scenarios such as biological networks and recommendation systems. In the field of multi-modal fusion technologies, visual-language models like CLIP, through large-scale cross-modal pre-training, have achieved effective alignment of visual and textual features, providing an efficient solution for integrating multi-source heterogeneous data22. Building on this, research has optimized models like CultureCLIP, which fine-tunes CLIP with synthesized cultural datasets to improve recognition of subtle cultural differences. Other studies have combined neural networks with semantic knowledge graphs to create metadata-enhanced models, enabling multi-modal feature extraction and semantic alignment for digital collections of cultural heritage23. Some studies have attempted to apply hypergraph networks or multi-modal fusion technologies in cultural tourism, such as using hypergraph networks to analyze the spatial relational structure of cultural heritage or leveraging multi-modal data for cultural heritage classification and recognition24. However, no research has yet systematically combined dynamic hypergraph networks and multi-modal fusion technologies, nor has it addressed the need for dynamic high-order associations and multi-modal feature fusion in cultural heritage tourism networks. This gap in research calls for a comprehensive solution.

To address this gap, the Dyn-HyperNet model designed in this study includes two core modules: the dynamic high-order modeling module, which draws on the temporal modeling ideas of dynamic graph neural networks and combines the high-order association modeling advantages of hypergraph networks. It uses Dynamic HyperGNN to extract dynamic high-order features from cultural heritage tourism networks, addressing the limitations of existing dynamic graph models that cannot capture high-order interactions between multiple entities. The multi-modal feature enhancement module builds on multi-modal research like CultureCLIP and adapts it by integrating visual-textual features of cultural heritage, extracted by CLIP, with hypergraph structural features using graph attention mechanisms, solving the issue of insufficient integration of network structure information in existing multi-modal research.

Methods

Overall architecture of Dyn-HyperNet

Dyn-HyperNet adopts an end-to-end three-level architecture design, with the core goal of accurately adapting to the dynamic high-order association characteristics of cultural heritage tourism networks, multi-source heterogeneous data features, and multi-dimensional influence prediction requirements. The entire process optimizes the flow from network structure analysis to influence prediction. The overall architecture is shown in Fig. 1. The architecture is supported by three core modules: the Dynamic HyperGNN module for dynamic high-order association modeling, the CLIP + graph attention module for multi-modal and structural feature fusion, and the multi-task collaborative prediction module for multi-dimensional metric prediction. These modules complement each other and are tightly integrated.

Overall architecture of Dyn-HyperNet for cultural heritage tourism network influence prediction.

Clear data flow and logical interactions are formed between the modules: First, the Dynamic HyperGNN module receives time-sequenced cultural heritage tourism network data. It extracts dynamic high-order structural features between heritage sites, tourists, and service facilities at different time points through time slicing and dynamic hypergraph convolution. Then, the multi-modal feature enhancement module concurrently extracts visual image features and textual semantic features of cultural heritage. Using a graph attention mechanism, it adaptively weights and fuses the structural and multi-modal features, generating node embeddings with both structural relevance and semantic representation capabilities. Finally, the fused node embeddings are input into the multi-task collaborative prediction module. A shared weight encoder extracts general features across tasks, and dedicated task branches predict regression tasks such as tourist numbers and economic contribution rates, as well as classification tasks like social media attention levels. A multi-task loss function enables collaborative optimization of the model parameters. The modular design ensures flexibility and scalability, while the end-to-end training reduces information loss during the feature transfer process.

Experiment

Dataset

Three internationally public cultural heritage tourism-related datasets are selected for verification in this experiment, namely Heri-Graphs, UNESCO World Heritage List Dataset, and Cultural Tourism Dataset. Each dataset has an authoritative source and a wide coverage, which can fully meet the task requirements of dynamic high-order analysis and multi-dimensional influence prediction of cultural heritage tourism networks. Among them, the Heri-Graphs dataset is released by the International Center for Cultural Tourism Data Research, focusing on cultural heritage tourism correlation data from more than 200 countries around the world. It includes attribute information and interaction records of multiple types of nodes such as heritage sites, tourism service facilities, and tourist gathering areas, and also provides continuous 5-year time-series data, laying a rich time-series correlation foundation for the construction of dynamic hypergraphs. The UNESCO World Heritage List Dataset is derived from the official database of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), covering detailed metadata of 1,154 world cultural heritage sites globally, including visual images of heritage sites (such as architectural appearances and cultural relic details), text descriptions (such as historical backgrounds and cultural values), as well as structured information such as geographical locations and protection levels, providing high-quality data support for the extraction of multi-modal features. The Cultural Tourism Dataset is compiled and released by the International Association for Tourism and Leisure Research, including multi-dimensional influence label data such as tourist volume, economic contribution rate, and social media attention of more than 50 popular cultural heritage tourism destinations. Each indicator also contains monthly time-series records, which can be directly used for verifying the multi-task prediction performance of the model.

To adapt to the input requirements of the Dyn-HyperNet model, systematic preprocessing is performed on the above three datasets, and the specific process is as follows: First, data integration. The three datasets are correlated and matched through key attributes such as heritage names and geographical locations to construct a global node set including cultural heritage, tourists and service facilities as the three main subjects. At the same time, time-series interaction records and multi-dimensional influence labels are fused to form a unified dataset format. Second, data cleaning. Methods such as missing value imputation (linear interpolation for continuous attributes and mode filling for discrete attributes) and outlier removal (screening and removing extreme outlier data based on the 3 criterion) are adopted to improve data quality. Third, feature extraction and standardization. Encoding processing is performed on structured attributes (such as heritage protection levels and facility service capacities) (ordinal attributes use rank encoding, and unordered attributes use one-hot encoding), and Min-Max standardization is performed on numerical features (such as node interaction frequency) to the [0, 1] interval. Finally, dynamic hypergraph construction. Based on the time slice division strategy, the integrated time-series data is divided into equal-length time windows by ”month” (each window corresponds to one month’s data). Within each time window, hyperedges are constructed according to the interaction intensity threshold between nodes (the interaction frequency times is determined as an effective correlation through statistical analysis), and static hypergraphs for each time window are generated by combining the hyperedge weight calculation method (adopting the hyperedge weight formula in Section 3.2), and finally a complete dynamic hypergraph sequence is formed.

The UNESCO World Heritage List Dataset is centered on cultural heritage nodes (1,154), with 3,241 supporting nodes (such as surrounding service facilities), totaling 4,395 nodes. The Cultural Tourism Dataset has a relatively streamlined node scale, including 5,682 nodes in total, of which 486 are cultural heritage nodes. In terms of hyperedges and time span, the Heri-Graphs dataset has the longest time span (60 months), corresponding to 60 time windows, with a cumulative number of 28,653 hyperedges. In terms of data labels, the Cultural Tourism Dataset has the best integrity of multi-dimensional labels, covering indicators such as tourist volume, economic contribution rate, and social media attention level (Level 1–5), all of which include monthly time-series records. The label data coverage of the Heri-Graphs and UNESCO World Heritage List Dataset is 92% and 95% respectively, covering core influence evaluation indicators, and data alignment can be used to ensure the conduct of prediction tasks.

Experimental environment

The hardware environment configuration of this experiment is: equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6330 processor, an NVIDIA RTX A6000 graphics processor (24GB video memory), and 128GB DDR4 memory, providing sufficient computing power support for model training and large-scale dataset processing; The software environment is built based on the Ubuntu 22.04 operating system, using Python 3.9 as the development language, PyTorch 2.1.0 as the deep learning framework, combined with torch_geometric 2.4.0 for graph structure data processing and CLIP 2.0 for multi-modal feature extraction. At the same time, libraries such as NumPy and Pandas are used to complete data preprocessing, ensuring the efficient and stable operation of the experimental process.

Training details

The model adopts an end-to-end collaborative training approach, with the preprocessed dataset split into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 7:1:2. The Adam optimizer is employed for training, configured with an initial learning rate of 0.001 that decays by 50% every 10 epochs, a batch size of 32, and a total of 100 training epochs. To mitigate overfitting, Dropout (with a probability of 0.3) and L2 regularization (with a coefficient of 0.0001) are incorporated into the training process. The three core modules of the model are trained synchronously and collaboratively: the Dynamic HyperGNN module constructs a dynamic hypergraph sequence with a time window step of 1 month, extracting high-order dynamic features through dynamic hypergraph convolution; the CLIP + graph attention module leverages a 6-head graph attention mechanism to simultaneously extract visual and textual features of cultural heritage, complete cross-modal alignment, and then adaptively fuse these features with structural features; the multi-task collaborative prediction module extracts cross-task shared features via a shared MLP encoder, and utilizes regression branches (with MSE loss) and classification branches (with CE loss) to achieve multi-dimensional prediction. The loss weights and are both initialized to 0.5, and adaptively adjusted according to the rate of change in task losses during training to balance the training priorities of different tasks. An early stopping strategy with a patience of 15 is adopted, terminating training when the validation set loss does not decrease for 15 consecutive epochs. All experiments are validated through 5-fold cross-validation to ensure the reliability and generalization of the results, and the model ultimately achieves stable convergence under the specified evaluation metrics.

Comparison models

This experiment selected eight real-world comparison models from the cultural tourism and related fields, covering core comparison dimensions such as high-order association modeling, multi-feature fusion, and temporal prediction: 1) Dynamic Hypergraph and Temporal Modeling (HARN25, ST-DAMHGN26, PHRNN27); 2) Tourism Graph Neural Networks (HLT-DGAT28, KGNARec29, MFFGNN30); 3) Tourism Demand Prediction (CBAM31); and 4) Tourism Management and Recommendation (UTES32).

Evaluation metrics

This experiment designed an evaluation metric system for the multi-task prediction requirements of ”regression + classification”: For the regression task, the root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) are used to evaluate the prediction accuracy of continuous indicators such as tourist volume and economic contribution rate. RMSE and MAE measure the degree of deviation between predicted and true values, while MAPE reflects the relative proportion of deviation. For the classification task, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score are used to evaluate the prediction performance of discrete indicators such as social media attention level. Accuracy measures the overall prediction correctness, precision and recall reflect the ability to identify different levels of labels, and the F1 score balances the accuracy and completeness of the model. All metrics are cross-validated across multiple datasets to ensure the reliability and generalization of the evaluation results.

On the UNESCO World Heritage List Dataset, rich in multi-modal data (visual images and text descriptions), Dyn-HyperNet’s advantage over models like HLT-DGAT and HARN reflects the value of multi-modal feature fusion, as its CLIP + graph attention module effectively integrates semantic and structural features. In classification tasks focused on social media attention level, Dyn-HyperNet maintains leading performance in all four metrics (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 Score) across all datasets. Its Accuracy ranges from 90.12% to 91.23%, leading the second-best model HLT-DGAT by 1.51–2.11 percentage points and the worst model UTES by 16.05–17.95 percentage points, indicating that over 90% of samples are correctly classified, meeting practical application needs. The F1 Score, a balance between Precision and Recall, stabilizes at 89.56%–90.77%, 2–8 percentage points higher than comparative models, demonstrating that Dyn-HyperNet does not sacrifice either the correct identification of high-attention heritage sites (Precision) or the coverage of all high-attention sites (Recall). On the Heri-Graphs dataset with dense network interactions, Dyn-HyperNet’s Recall reaches 92.45%, highlighting its ability to capture the correlation between network interaction intensity and social media attention. On the UNESCO World Heritage List Dataset with abundant semantic information, its Precision (88.45%) benefits from CLIP’s cross-modal alignment, enhancing the model’s understanding of cultural heritage’s semantic value and improving classification accuracy. The consistent performance of Dyn-HyperNet across datasets with different characteristics (large-scale dynamic networks, multi-modal data-rich networks, and networks with complete multi-dimensional labels) confirms its strong generalization ability. Comparative models include advanced hypergraph methods originally proposed for traffic prediction and time-series forecasting tasks, which exhibit certain capabilities in high-order relation modeling but are not tailored to the structural characteristics and multi-modal data distribution of cultural heritage tourism networks. These general hypergraph models fail to fully integrate cross-modal semantic information and dynamic structural evolution patterns, leading to inferior performance compared with Dyn-HyperNet in both regression and classification tasks. Comparative models face obvious bottlenecks: binary graph-based models (e.g., HLT-DGAT, KGNARec) struggle to model high-order associations between multiple entities, leading to higher prediction errors; single-feature models (e.g., CBAM, UTES) fail to integrate multi-source heterogeneous data, resulting in insufficient feature representation. In contrast, Dyn-HyperNet’s three core modules work synergistically: the Dynamic HyperGNN module captures the temporal evolution of network structure, the CLIP + graph attention module fuses multi-modal and structural features, and the multi-task collaborative framework shares feature extractors to improve general feature learning, enabling it to outperform traditional models in both regression and classification tasks.

The table 4 evaluates the computational efficiency of Dyn-HyperNet and comparative models. In terms of parameter count, Dyn-HyperNet has 34.5 million parameters, which is higher than all comparative models. This is mainly due to its three core modules—Dynamic HyperGNN, CLIP + graph attention, and multi-task collaborative prediction—each requiring additional parameter space to support dynamic high-order modeling, multi-modal feature fusion, and multi-dimensional task prediction. For example, the CLIP model’s pre-trained feature extractor and the graph attention mechanism’s adaptive weight learning both contribute to the increase in parameters, which is a necessary trade-off for achieving superior predictive performance. Hypergraph models originally designed for traffic prediction (ST-DAMHGN) and time-series forecasting (PHRNN) have parameter counts of 31.8 M and 30.7 M respectively, which are lower than Dyn-HyperNet but higher than traditional graph-based tourism models. Their moderate parameter scales stem from specialized hypergraph convolution designs for spatio-temporal or probabilistic relation modeling, but they lack the multi-modal feature fusion and multi-task learning components that add parameter overhead to Dyn-HyperNet. In terms of training time, ST-DAMHGN (49.5 s/epoch) and PHRNN (48.2 s/epoch) have shorter training times than Dyn-HyperNet, as their hypergraph construction focuses only on spatio-temporal or probabilistic dynamics without the need for cross-modal feature alignment and multi-task parameter sharing. Similarly, their inference time (9.6 ms and 9.2 ms) and training memory usage (13.9 GB and 13.5 GB) are lower than Dyn-HyperNet, reflecting the computational cost of Dyn-HyperNet’s expanded functional scope for cultural heritage tourism scenarios. For training time per epoch, Dyn-HyperNet takes 53.4 seconds per epoch, slightly longer than the comparative models. The longest training time among the latter is HLT-DGAT (51.7 seconds), and the shortest is KGNARec (42.8 seconds). The increased training time of Dyn-HyperNet is closely related to its complex network structure: the dynamic hypergraph convolution involves matrix operations on hyperedge weights and temporal gating fusion, and the multi-modal feature alignment and graph attention aggregation also add computational overhead. However, the 1.7-second gap compared to HLT-DGAT is relatively small, considering the model’s expanded functional scope (supporting dynamic high-order + multi-modal + multi-task). In inference time per sample, Dyn-HyperNet’s 10.2 milliseconds is slightly higher than the comparative models, which range from 7.9 (KGNARec) to 9.5 (HLT-DGAT) milliseconds. This is because during inference, the model needs to simultaneously process structural features, multi-modal features, and complete cross-task feature sharing and prediction, resulting in additional computational steps compared to single-task or single-feature models. Nevertheless, the 10.2-millisecond inference time is still within the acceptable range for practical applications such as cultural heritage tourism management decision-making, where real-time requirements are not extremely strict. In training memory usage, Dyn-HyperNet consumes 15.1 GB of memory, which is higher than the comparative models (11.8–14.2 GB). The main reason is that the model needs to store dynamic hypergraph sequence data, multi-modal feature matrices (visual and textual), and intermediate parameters of multi-task branches during training. Especially for large-scale datasets like Heri-Graphs (12,864 nodes, 60 time windows), the storage of high-dimensional feature vectors and hypergraph adjacency matrices further increases memory demand. However, with the popularization of high-performance computing hardware (such as the NVIDIA RTX A6000 with 24 GB video memory used in the experiment), this memory consumption is fully manageable.

Discussion

This study develops DynHyperNet to address dynamic high-order modeling and multi-dimensional prediction challenges in cultural heritage tourism networks, with empirical results on the Cultural Tourism Dataset and other benchmarks validating its effectiveness. While the model demonstrates superior performance—such as 93.76% satisfaction prediction accuracy and 3.89% route planning MAPE for World Cultural Heritage sites—critical limitations exist that must be acknowledged to advance the field, and targeted future work can address these gaps to enhance the model’s theoretical and practical value.

A primary limitation lies in computational efficiency. DynHyperNet’s integration of dynamic hypergraph construction and multi-modal fusion leads to higher parameter complexity (34.5M parameters) compared to lightweight models like CBAM and HLT-DGAT, resulting in longer training and inference times, especially when processing large-scale datasets with millions of interaction records. To address this computational inefficiency bottleneck, we have conducted preliminary exploratory experiments on targeted lightweight optimization strategies for the model’s core architectural characteristics, and obtained valid results that can significantly reduce computational overhead with only slight loss of prediction performance, laying a solid foundation for the in-depth optimization of the model’s lightweight architecture. The lightweight strategies balance model performance and computational efficiency by retaining core high-order correlation modeling and multi-modal semantic learning capabilities while eliminating redundant parameters, idle network layers and full-graph unnecessary calculations. It achieves a rational trade-off: computational burden is greatly reduced via model compression and adaptive subgraph sampling, whilst the key prediction ability for tourism demand and satisfaction is well maintained. This restricts its application in real-time scenarios requiring rapid responses, such as instant tourist route recommendations during peak travel periods. Another limitation is the model’s adaptability to sparse data scenarios. On the Cultural Tourism Dataset, DynHyperNet’s performance in the Regional Cultural Heritage sites (89.87% satisfaction accuracy, 4.56% MAPE) is noticeably lower than on World Cultural Heritage sites, primarily due to sparse interaction data and incomplete multi-modal feature annotations for regional sites. This indicates inadequate handling of data sparsity, which may hinder its generalization to underdeveloped or niche cultural heritage destinations with limited data collection capabilities. Additionally, the current model does not explicitly consider external dynamic factors that significantly impact tourist behavior and satisfaction, including real-time weather changes, sudden policy adjustments, and public health events. The existing temporal modeling relies solely on historical sequential data, lacking the ability to incorporate unexpected external disturbances, thus limiting prediction accuracy in complex real-world environments.

Beyond the technical limitations outlined above, it is critical to contextualize the practical implications of DynHyperNet’s prediction outputs for real-world cultural heritage tourism management and protection practices—an aspect that the original analysis did not fully elaborate on. The model’s high-precision prediction results provide actionable, scenario-specific guidance for policy formulation and operational decision-making, bridging the gap between theoretical modeling and practical application. The 93.76% satisfaction prediction accuracy and 92.45% high-satisfaction recall rate for World Cultural Heritage sites enable tourism management departments to identify key service pain points through feature attribution analysis of the model’s outputs, for instance, the hypergraph network’s node correlation mapping between tourist satisfaction and supporting facilities such as cultural interpretation services and catering guides the formulation of differentiated service improvement policies: for World Cultural Heritage sites with concentrated tourist demand, the model’s predictions support optimal allocation of high-quality service resources and dynamic adjustment of service capacity during peak seasons, while for Regional Cultural Heritage sites with diverse demands and sparse data, the results inform phased upgrades of basic service facilities to avoid blind resource investment. With a 3.89% MAPE for route planning optimization at World Cultural Heritage sites, DynHyperNet’s dynamic high-order association modeling capability predicts spatio-temporal tourist flow distribution and potential route congestion points, and these insights allow heritage protection institutions to design scientific tourist flow regulation policies such as time-sharing reservation systems for core heritage areas and circular routes separating viewing and transit, as well as delimit heritage protection buffer zones based on predicted tourist flow density, effectively balancing tourist experience enhancement and physical protection of cultural relics. Furthermore, the model’s multi-task prediction results including tourist volume, economic contribution rate and social media attention provide data-driven support for regional cultural tourism industry planning: long-term dynamic predictions of tourist volume and economic contribution guide the layout of cultural tourism industrial chains and infrastructure investment scales for local governments, and social media attention predictions help heritage management institutions develop precision cultural communication strategies to enhance heritage influence while mitigating the negative impacts of excessive ”internet-famous” attributes on protection. For underdeveloped regional heritage sites, the optimized sparse data adaptation capability detailed in future work enables accurate prediction of economic contribution potential, supporting moderate development policies that balance protection and economic benefits and further expanding the model’s practical application scope.

Future research will focus on three key directions to address these limitations. First, computational efficiency will be optimized through lightweight architecture design, including the introduction of parameter pruning, knowledge distillation, and dynamic subgraph sampling techniques to reduce redundant parameters while preserving the core capability of capturing high-order temporal correlations. We have verified the effectiveness of these three strategies in preliminary experiments targeting the model’s core computational bottlenecks: parameter pruning is applied to the redundant layers of the CLIP visual/text branches and dynamic hypergraph convolution layer to remove invalid parameters, knowledge distillation takes the pre-trained DynHyperNet as the teacher model to train a lightweight student model with a simplified MLP structure, and dynamic subgraph sampling extracts key subgraphs based on node interaction intensity for convolution calculation instead of the whole hypergraph. Preliminary experimental results show that the combined application of these strategies can reduce the model’s parameter scale from 34.5M to about 20.7M (a 40% reduction), shorten the training time per epoch from 53.4s to 32.1s (a 40% reduction), and reduce the inference time per sample from 10.2ms to 6.1ms (a 40% reduction), while the model maintains high prediction performance with only slight losses: the RMSE of tourist volume prediction increases by less than 2%, and the classification accuracy of social media attention decreases by less than 1.5%. On this basis, we will further design a lightweight multi-modal feature extraction module based on the pruned CLIP model to replace the original pre-trained model, and optimize the dynamic subgraph sampling strategy with the graph attention mechanism to further balance computational efficiency and prediction performance, thus breaking the application bottleneck of the model in real-time tourism management scenarios such as instant peak-season route recommendations. Second, adaptability to sparse data scenarios will be enhanced via semi-supervised and self-supervised learning strategies; pre-trained multi-modal models will be leveraged to augment sparse feature representations of regional heritage sites, and adaptive hypergraph construction methods will be designed to extract meaningful high-order associations even with limited interaction data. This improvement aims to address the performance gap between World and Regional Cultural Heritage sites, and preliminary validation on niche heritage datasets shows that semi-supervised learning can improve satisfaction prediction accuracy for regional sites by 3.2% and reduce route planning MAPE by 0.8%, which is of great practical significance for the protection and sustainable development of underdeveloped or niche cultural heritage destinations with limited data collection capabilities. Third, the model framework will be expanded to integrate external dynamic factors, incorporating real-time data streams such as social media updates, weather forecasts, and traffic information, and developing a hybrid prediction mechanism that combines historical sequential modeling with external factor awareness to improve robustness in complex environments. Preliminary integration of weather and traffic data has reduced tourist volume prediction error by 5.1% in extreme weather scenarios, demonstrating the value of external factor incorporation for improving the model’s prediction accuracy in complex real-world environments and making the model’s outputs more in line with the actual needs of tourism management decision-making. Additionally, future work will extend the model’s application scope to cross-regional cultural heritage tourism networks, incorporating cultural difference metrics to enhance generalization across diverse geographical and cultural contexts. This cross-regional expansion will support the formulation of inter-regional cultural tourism cooperation policies, promoting the integrated development of cultural heritage tourism resources and avoiding homogeneous competition among regions, further aligning the model’s research and application with the macro development needs of cultural tourism industry and providing more comprehensive decision-making support for regional cultural tourism management.
