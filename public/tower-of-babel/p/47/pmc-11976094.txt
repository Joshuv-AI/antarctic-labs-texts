Patient safety education in Umm Al-Qura dental school: A comprehensive analysis of curriculum content and student confidence
Authors: Salwa A. Aldahlawi, Hanadi S. Lingawi, Ibtesam K. Afifi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient safety (PS) is a critical component in dental education, yet it is not clear how dental schools include PS education in their curricula.AimTo assess how PS topics are presented in the current Umm Al-Qura undergraduate dental (UQUDENT) curriculum and to investigate the self-perceptions of dental students regarding the PS confidence they have attained from their dental program.MethodThe UQUDENT curriculum was mapped using keywords developed from the WHO PS manual. Courses contained intended learning outcomes (ILOs), and lecture topics were identified and ranked based on the level of PS content. In addition, dental students and interns were asked to fill in a modified version of the Health Professional Education in Patient Safety Survey.ResultsA total of 73.2% of the courses included ILOs or topics related to PS. Keywords related to the teamwork topic were the most common (60.9%), followed by patient engagement (24.3%), medication safety (12.2%), and infection control (9.8%). None of the courses included items on overall PS concepts, understanding systems, or quality improvement in relation to PS.Dental students were most confident in their learning regarding clinical safety and effective communication. They were least confident in what they learned about teamwork with other health professionals. There were no statistically significant differences in PS domain scores between classroom and clinical learning.ConclusionThe UQUDENT curriculum covers many aspects of PS. However, the PS content should be explicitly taught and assessed. Areas such as interprofessional education, clinical risk management, learning from errors, and teamwork should be augmented.

Introduction

Patient safety (PS) is a priority for healthcare workers, focusing on preventing harm to patients. The UK Healthcare Commission defines PS culture as “the product of individual and group values, attitudes, perceptions, competencies, and patterns of behavior that determine the commitment to, and the style and proficiency of an organization's health and safety management” (Cooper, 2000). PS is concerned with the prevention and reduction of medical/dental errors that may have adverse effects on patients and emphasizes the importance of error reporting and analysis.

In Saudi Arabia, improving healthcare is a key aspect of the Saudi Vision 2030. PS plays a vital role in this transformation which lead to establishment of the Saudi Patient Safety Center (Fallatah, 2016, Ministry of Health, 2021). Globally, medical errors cause death for approximately 440,000 patients annually (Institute of Medicine (US) Committee on Quality of Health Care in America, 2000). Therefore, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared PS to be an endemic concern, and launched the World Alliance for PS in 2004 (World Health Organization 2004). This spurred improvement in PS policies and protocols across both medical and dental fields. However, studies, such as those by Ramoni et al., indicate that safety culture in dental practices lags behind medical clinics, highlighting the need for better auditing and education in dental PS (Ramoni et al., 2014).

The importance of auditing PS in dental practice and PS education in the curricula of dental schools has been raised by numerous reports in the media on cases of medical errors. One of those cases was a 17-year-old girl’s death during wisdom tooth extraction in 2011 (Quigley 2011). Since then, many researchers have studied adverse events and PS in dental settings (Hiivala et al., 2015, Obadan et al., 2015, Kalenderian et al., 2021). Research by Obadan et al. identified common dental errors, including delayed treatment and misdiagnosis, which sometimes led to permanent harm or death. Their study found that 24.4 % of patients involved in dental adverse events experienced permanent damage, while 11.1 % of cases resulted in death (Obadan et al., 2015).

Patient safety curricula have been developed by both global and national organizations (Walton et al., 2006, World Health Organization, 2011, Palmer et al., 2019, Canadian Patient Safety Institute 2020). In 2012, the WHO published a multi-professional patient safety curriculum guide to serve as framework for dental and medical schools to implement comprehensive PS education (World Health Organization 2011). Many dental institutes have used the WHO guide to reassess their current PS standards (Vogt et al., 2022). Studies in the UK and Saudi Arabia have shown positive attitudes among dental students toward including PS education in their curriculum (Palmer et al., 2019, AlOlayan et al., 2021, Halawany et al., 2022). However, research from Saudi Arabia also revealed that while students felt confident about PS, there was room for improvement in certain clinical safety aspects and recommends that dental curricula should include PS courses to improve the safety and quality of patient care (AlOlayan et al., 2021, Halawany et al., 2022).

The main aim of this study is to assess how PS issues are presented in the current undergraduate dental curriculum at Umm Al-Qura University (UQUDENT). The secondary aim is to investigate the self-perceptions of students and interns regarding their PS confidence attained from the dental educational program.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of Umm Al-Qura University (IRB # HAPO-02-K-012-2024-06-2190). The study design insured that the participation was voluntary, and responses were anonymous and cannot be traced back to the respondent. Also, no personally identifiable information was collected. By submitting the responses, the students/interns were consenting to participate in this study.

This descriptive, cross-sectional study included two main parts. The first part involved identifying and ranking PS content by mapping the current undergraduate dental curriculum at UQUDENT against the objectives of the WHO. To determine which subjects contained PS content, a list of keywords was used to identify the relevant subjects (Table 1). Keywords were developed from the WHO PS manual (World Health Organization 2011). Course intended learning outcomes (ILOs), subject descriptions, assessments, and lecture topics with keywords linked to each WHO topic were identified. Keywords linked to WHO topics related to PS.

This was followed by the ranking of PS content in each course by adopting the scale used by Brackley-O’Grady et al. (Bracksley-O’Grady et al., 2022). A rank of 0 meant that the content was not included in the course; 1 meant that the content was mentioned in the course (one keyword appeared in lectures or subjects); 2 meant that the content was partially included in the course (two or more keywords appeared in lecture topics or at least one course ILO); 3 meant that the content was addressed in the courses (three or more keywords appeared in at least two ILOs and lectures); and 4 meant that the content was embedded in the course. To facilitate the analysis of the levels of learning, Bloom’s taxonomy was utilized as the framework. The verbs used in ILOs were used to determine the level of learning as low (basic understanding), intermediate (competent application), or high (design and creation).

The second part was concerned with the self-perceptions of undergraduate dental students and dental interns’ PS confidence.

Study population

The study invited fourth-, fifth-, and sixth-year UQUDENT undergraduate dental students, as well as dental interns who graduated from UQUDENT and were undergoing their training rotation at UQU Dental Hospital to participate. Interns who were not UQUDENT graduates or training outside UQU Dental Hospital were excluded to ensure the sample's reliability, as these participants were not exposed to UQUDENT’s newly implemented PS policies. Additionally, preclinical students (first to third year) were excluded. Open-Epi sample size calculator, which indicated that a minimum of 113 participants was required from the population of 158 UQUDENT clinical students (with a 95 % confidence interval and a 5 % margin of error).

Participants were asked to complete a modified version of the Health Professional Education in Patient Safety Survey (H-PEPSS). The questionnaire was sent to the study participants as a link via WhatsApp. Convenience and snowball sampling were used to ensure maximum participation.

Data collection method

Data collection was carried out using Google Forms for the modified version of the H-PEPSS questionnaire which was previously validated (Ginsburg et al., 2012, Halawany et al., 2022). The questionnaire consisted of demographic questions and seven PS domains (PSD). The domains included: (PSD I) clinical safety skills (four items), (PSD II) safety culture (three items), (PSD III) teamwork with other health professionals (three items), (PSD IV) effective communication (three items), (PSD V) managing safety risk (three items), (PSD VI) understanding human and environmental factors (two items), and (PSD VII) recognizing and responding to immediate risks (two items). The participants were asked to evaluate their PS confidence in classroom and clinical settings for each domain using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Additionally, a section with 10 questions addressed wider cases of safety and the participants' comfort in revealing PS concerns. The Google Form was designed to accept only fully completed questionnaires. Duplicate responses have been prevented by enabling Google Forms’ built-in limit to one response.

Statistical analysis

The data collected were entered into a Statistical Package for the Social Sciences database (version 23.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Demographic data (gender and academic level) were presented as numbers and percentages. Responses recorded on the five-point Likert scale were grouped into agree (4, 5) and disagree (1, 2), and overall percentages were obtained. Data on self-reported PS confidence in the classroom and in the clinic were analyzed separately using mean and standard deviation, and an unpaired t-test was used to evaluate the statistically significant differences between the classroom and clinic scores. For effect size, Cohen's d was calculated (0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large). A p-value of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant.

Results

The UQUDENT curriculum contained 40 courses that included over 5 years of training (from the second year to the sixth year). In addition, the internship year also has its own course specifications. Years 4, 5, and 6 and the internship year were considered the clinical years in which students were involved in patients’ treatment in the clinic. There were 13 courses that covered basic sciences or research-oriented subjects, with the rest of the courses being clinical courses (containing course materials involving preclinical and clinical skills).

UQUDENT curriculum mapping for patient safety content

A total of 30 courses (73.2 %) included ILOs or topics related to PS, of which eight were basic science courses.

Keywords related to the teamwork topic were the most common (25 courses, 60.9 %), followed by keywords related to patient engagement (10 courses, 24.3 %), medication safety (five courses, 12.2 %), infection control (four courses, 9.8 %), and clinical risk assessment (four courses, 9.8 %). The least covered topics in the curriculum were invasive procedures (one course, 2.4 %), errors (one course, 2.4 %), and human and environmental factors (three courses, 7.3 %). Of the 30 courses that included ILOs or topic content covering PS, PS teaching was embedded within the course in five courses (12.2 %), PS teaching was addressed in seven courses (23 %), PS was partially addressed in 10 courses (33 %), and PS was mentioned in eight courses (26 %). Oral Surgery, Comprehensive Care Clinics (CCC), Pediatric Dentistry, Local Anesthesia & Pain Control, and Introduction to Dental Experience were the courses that included the most PS-related ILOs and topics. Thirty-one percent of the courses included ILOs that were considered to be high or intermediate level according to Bloom’s Taxonomy (Table 2).

PS-related ILOs or topics were delivered utilizing a variety of methods, such as lectures, problem-based learning (PBL), student-directed learning (SDL), group projects, and clinical training. None of the teaching methods included case studies about PS content per se or simulation. Assessment also included a variety of written, checklist-evaluated, practical, and clinical exams.

Response to the reduced H-PEPSS model

Of the 159 questionnaires distributed, 113 responses were received, with a response rate of 71 %. Students’ responses to the reduced H-PEPSS model regarding PS domains in the classroom and clinic.

More than 50 % of the dental students strongly agreed/agreed they were confident in what they had learned in all aspects of patient’s safety domains. Over 80 % of the students were confident in what they had learned in hand hygiene and infection control. However, fewer students (<50 %) were confident in what they had learned about teamwork with other healthcare professionals, especially in aspects of managing conflicts, shared decision-making, leadership, and encouraging team members to speak up, question, challenge, promote, and be accountable for PS issues. A higher percentage of students disagreed /strongly disagreed on being confident in what they had learned regarding dental systems, the role of human and environmental factors in PS, and clinical risk management (Table 3).

Wider cases of safety and comfort when revealing students’ concerns

Most of the dental students (76 %) agreed that the possibility of what was “safe” for them to do in the clinic was very clear to them as students. In all areas related to wider cases of PS, the students’ agreement levels were above 60 %.

Regarding comfort in revealing their concerns, 58 % of the students agreed that they could approach a colleague if they noticed unsafe practices and reported that it was difficult to question the decisions or actions of those with more authority. Discussion

The results of this study showed that PS education is included in a significant portion (73 %) of the courses taught in the UQUDENT curriculum. PS − related intended learning outcomes were introduced as early as the second year and were consistently incorporated throughout the six years of the program. While most of PS- education occurs in clinical courses, PS teaching is embedded in one-third of the courses at intermediate or advanced levels. This integration was reflected in the UQUDENT students’ confidence PS knowledge, as evidenced by their responses to the H-PEPSS.

Although 30 courses covered aspects of safe practices in the UQUDENT curriculum, they were not explicitly labeled as PS-related topics. Only a few, such as hand hygiene, medication safety, and infection control, were directly identified as PS topics. This is in alignment with previously published observations on PS education in medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and physiotherapy (Cresswell et al., 2013). However, the literature on PS education in dental settings is limited (Kiersma et al., 2011). A recent systematic review found only 10 studies on PS education and culture in dental institutes underscoring the need for revision of dental curricula to highlight PS elements (Abutayyem et al., 2021).

Teamwork and communication skills emerged as the most emphasized PS-related topics in the UQUDENT curriculum. It was delivered mostly through PBL, SDL, group presentations, and group assignments, methods known to effectively develop teamwork (Wang et al., 2021, Sezgin and Bektas, 2023). Most dental schools introduced communication skills early in the dental curriculum (Dargue et al., 2023). This is critical as communication breakdown is a major threat to patients’ safety in medical care (Guttman et al., 2021).

UQUDENT students expressed satisfaction with their learning in communication, though they were less confident about interprofessional teamwork. The lowest mean scores were related to the statements of managing conflict with other healthcare professionals, sharing authority, leadership, and decision-making, especially in the clinic. This lower confidence could be attributed to their limited interaction with other healthcare professionals during training, as students typically treated low-risk patients. Additionally, none of the ILOs addressed conflict resolution or shared decision-making, suggesting that interprofessional PS education focusing on collaboration and conflict management should be strengthened. Including simulation through a well-designed case scenario and video-recording performances and role play in the curricula can serve as tools to connect theoretical knowledge with clinical practice. It can also enhance the recognition of areas for improvement, especially in communication, leadership, and interprofessional education (Poirier et al., 2017, Popkess et al., 2017).

Mapping of the curriculum also revealed limited coverage of human factors, which address how environmental, organizational, and individual elements can affect PS. Similarly, only 38 % of UK dental schools were found to cover human factors as part of their PS education (Dargue et al., 2023). Although students displayed moderate confidence in their understanding of these factors, many aspects of PS are learned during supervised clinical encounters by observing the reaction of the clinical educator to the situation. Role models and hidden curricula play an influential role in translating safe clinical practices into the clinical setting (Lawrence et al., 2018, Tocco Tussardi et al., 2021).

UQUDENT Hospital has an active PS committee that develops and implements PS policies and conducts regular mortality and morbidity meetings to review incidents, analyze root causes, and make recommendations. These meetings provide an opportunity for students to engage with the PS culture in a supportive environment, though their attendance is optional. Encouraging student participation in these meetings could foster a stronger safety culture.

The survey also showed that more than 60 % of students were confident in their understanding of topics like learning from errors, human factors, and safety culture. However, a comparison with other studies, such as one by Halawany et al., which included postgraduate dental students, found that UQUDENT undergraduate students had lower confidence levels (Halawany et al., 2022).This discrepancy may be attributed to the different academic stages of the participants.

The analysis of wider cases of PS identified a low percentage of students’ agreement on items of comfort in revealing their concerns about PS (53–58 %). These results support the previous suggestion that dental practitioners underestimate dental errors, considering them non-life-threatening and that their reporting is not important (Wright et al., 2017). In addition, students may be afraid of punishment and of receiving blame from their colleagues and supervisors (Chohan et al., 2022). Therefore, teaching about error communication, disclosure responsibility, and misconceptions about error causes should be reinforced in the dental curriculum (Tocco Tussardi et al., 2021).

When comparing the students’ perceptions in classroom and clinical settings, there were no statistically significant differences in the mean PS domain scores between the classroom and clinical scenarios, similar to what has previously been reported (Doyle et al., 2015, Halawany et al., 2022).

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use curriculum mapping to address the level of and gaps in PS education in the dental curriculum. We also compared the findings by exploring the student’s confidence in their learning regarding different PS aspects. However, it has some limitations. It was conducted at a single institution, limiting the generalizability of the findings, and it relied on self-reported data, which could introduce bias. Additionally, the survey instrument (the reduced H-PEPSS model) is a lengthy questionnaire that may have affected participant responses. The cross-sectional nature of the study and the potential for non-responder bias were also cited as limitations.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, while the UQUDENT curriculum covers many aspects of PS, more explicit and comprehensive teaching and assessment of PS are needed. Areas such as interprofessional education, clinical risk management, learning from errors, and teamwork need to be augmented. topics related to systems thinking and improving PS quality require greater attention.

Patient safety at UQUDENT should be reinforced in the form of clinical simulation, quality improvement activities and collaboration between dental clinics and classrooms to cultivate a stronger PS culture. Encouraging student participation in PS committees and mortality and morbidity meetings is also recommended to reinforce PS attitudes.

Future research should investigate the long-term impact of PS education on patient outcomes, patient satisfaction, and the efficiency and financial performance of dental practices. Research should also focus on identifying human and institutional factors that contribute to PS breaches. Incorporating PS into dental education has profound implications, ensuring that future dentists are not only technically proficient but also committed to providing safe, high-quality care.
