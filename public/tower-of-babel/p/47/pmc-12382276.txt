Identification and expression of a novel antimicrobial peptide Gloverin and its antibacterial effect against Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Kaiqi Lian, Yuhang Wang, Yayun Jiao, Xiangfeng Meng, Zengjun Ma, Yuanchen Zhang, Lingling Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have attracted extensive attention in various fields. Gloverin is a group of AMPs derived from Lepidoptera insects. In this study, a novel gloverin CpGlv was identified through sequence alignment. The open reading frame (ORF) of mature CpGlv, devoid of its signal peptide region, was recombinantly expressed in Escherichia coli. The antibacterial activity of the fusion protein CpGlv against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) was then assessed by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to observe the ultrastructure of S. aureus cells. Furthermore, the effects of CpGlv on the cell wall and membrane permeability of S. aureus were evaluated by detecting the leakage of intracellular nucleic acids, proteins, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and β-galactosidase, as well as through the propidium iodide (PI) staining experiment. Finally, the influence of CpGlv on the S. aureus biofilm formation was investigated using the crystal violet staining assay.ResultsThe results showed that the ORF of CpGlv contained a nucleotide sequence of 591 bp. The expressed fusion protein CpGlv had a molecular weight of approximately 24 kDa. The purified CpGlv exhibited significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus, with a MIC of 40 µg/mL. 2×MIC of CpGlv caused S. aureus cells to exhibit adhesion, swelling, deformation, and even rupture. Further, CpGlv resulted in the leakage of intracellular nucleic acids, proteins, ALP, and β-galactosidase in S. aureus. After PI staining, there was intracellular fluorescence intensity in S. aureus treated by CpGlv. Additionally, 1/4×MIC of CpGlv significantly reduced the biofilm formation of S. aureus.ConclusionA novel gloverin CpGlv was identified, expressed, and purified, and the recombinant CpGlv exhibited antimicrobial activity against S. aureus by increasing the cell membrane and wall permeability, and inhibiting the biofilm formation, laying a foundation for in-depth research on the antibacterial mechanism and application of CpGlv.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04197-9.

Background

Bacteria are the most common foodborne pathogens in public health, such as Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli (E. coli), Salmonella, Campylobacter, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Yersinia pestis, Listeria monocytogenes, and so on [1, 2]. S. aureus, a representative Gram-positive foodborne pathogen, poses a serious threat to the food industry and food safety [3]. Meanwhile, S. aureus is a major pathogen causing widespread infections in both community and hospital settings [4]. The clinical manifestations of S. aureus infections are largely determined by its virulence factors, with disease severity ranging from mild skin and soft tissue infections to life-threatening conditions such as scalded skin syndrome or toxic shock syndrome [5, 6]. The polysaccharide adhesin produced by S. aureus can promote biofilm formation to further result in chronic infections and antibiotic resistance [7]. S. aureus may enter the food chain during various processes such as food collection, slaughter, processing, packaging, and storage, which causes foodborne diseases [8]. S. aureus contamination does not usually affect the appearance of food, but its toxins cause food poisoning after ingestion, seriously threatening public health. Currently, the control of such foodborne bacteria mainly relies on the application of preservatives and disinfectants. Bactericides in low concentration may exacerbate bacterial cross-resistance, while bactericides in high concentration cause environmental pollution and endanger human health [9]. Therefore, it has received widespread attention to develop green and safe antibacterial agents, such as plant extracts, bacteriocins, or antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) [10–12].

AMP is a small-molecule peptide composed of 10–50 amino acids. AMPs are commonly found and important part of the innate immune system in plants, animals, and microorganisms [13]. Gloverin is a type of glycine-rich and thermostable alkaline AMPs, which were found in Lepidoptera insects [14]. Gloverins usually have a good antibacterial effect on Gram-negative bacteria, especially the rough LPS-type mutant E. coli. However, some reports have shown that Gloverins also show antimicrobial activity against Gram-positive bacteria [15, 16]. The E. coli expression system is the most commonly used prokaryotic expression system for AMPs, which has the advantages of high yield, easy operation, and low cost [17]. Su et al. cloned the BMGlvA2 gene fragment into pET28a(+) and recombinantly expressed BMGlvA2 using E. coli Rosetta (DE3) [18]. The recombinant BMGlvA2 showed antibacterial activity against E. coli, Salmonella typhimurium, S. aureus, and Bacillus subtilis. The mechanisms of AMPs against bacteria include binding to target microbial cell walls or membranes, disrupting normal metabolism, and interfering with DNA replication [19, 20]. Song et al. [21] reported that it was observed through scanning electron microscopy that the novel scorpion venom AMP analogue GK-1 caused holes and cracks in the cell membranes of MRSA and Candida albicans, leading to leakage of cellular contents and even cell death. Fungal copsin binds lipid II and blocks cell wall biosynthesis [22]. Actin X2 inhibited the activity of two key enzymes SDH and MDH involved in the Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) cycle in Xanthomonas citri subsp. citri cells [23]. G(IIKK)3I-NH2(G3) inhibited bacterial adhesion, affected the initial formation of biofilms, interacted with extracellular DNA, and disrupted the three-dimensional structure of mature biofilms in Streptococcus mutans [24].

In this study, Conogethes punctiferalis (C. punctiferalis) gloverin (CpGlv) was identified by comparing the known Gloverin sequences with transcriptome data from C. punctiferalis. Bioinformatic analysis and prokaryotic expression of CpGlv were performed, followed by evaluation of the recombinant CpGlv protein’s antibacterial activity and preliminary mechanism against S. aureus. This study lays a foundation for further research on the antibacterial mechanism and clinical application of CpGlv.

Materials and methods

Materials

S. aureus CMCC(B) 26,003 was preserved in our laboratory. Isopropyl -β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), dialysis bag MD34, and resazurin were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Protein molecular weight markers were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (China) Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The His-tag protein purification kit (soluble protein) was purchased from Jiangsu CWBIO Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Multi-tag protein was bought from Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). ECL luminescent solution was procured from Beijing Pulilai Gene Technology Co., Ltd. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) kit, 2-Nitrophenyl beta-D-galactopyranoside (ONPG), and propidium iodide (PI) were purchased from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Crystal violet staining solution and bacterial DNA extraction kit were obtained from Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Identification and bioinformatics analysis of CpGlv sequence

The transcriptome database of C. punctiferalis was subjected to BLASTn alignment with the published Gloverin gene sequences, and a novel Gloverin gene sequence was found from C. punctiferalis. The ORF gene sequence of CpGlv was deduced into an amino-acid sequence, and the signal peptide was predicted by the SignalP (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-6.0/) [25], thereby obtaining an amino-acid sequence of the mature CpGlv.

The functionally conserved regions of CpGlv were predicted by Conserved Domains (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). Multiple alignment analysis was performed among the amino acid sequences of CpGlv and Gloverins from other species using DNAMAN v9.0 software (Lynnon Corp., Quebec, Canada). The physicochemical properties of the mature CpGlv were analyzed using ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/cgi-bin/protparam) [26]. The structure of CpGlv was predicted using AlphaFold 3 (AF3) software [27] and visualized through the PyMOL molecular graphics system (version 2.6.0 Schrödinger, LLC). The predicted structure of CpGlv was further evaluated by the PROCHECK online server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/software/PROCHECK/), QMEAN server(https://swissmodel.expasy.org/qmean/rLBbjZ), and ProSA tool (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php) [28].

Expression and purification of the fusion protein CpGl

The codon-optimized CpGlv gene sequence was synthesized by Nanjing Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China) and directionally cloned into the pET-28a(+) vector through NdeI-XhoI restriction enzymes, followed by sequencing verification. The recombinant pET-28a-CpGlv was transformed into E. coli BL21 (ED3) competent cells to express the Fusion Protein CpGlv using IPTG induction. According to the instructions of the His-tag protein purification kit (soluble protein), the soluble fusion protein CpGlv was purified. Used a 0.22 μm filter membrane to remove bacteria from the supernatant after ultrasound, added an equal volume of Binding Buffer, loaded onto the column, washed off impurities with Binding Buffer, and collected the target protein with an appropriate amount of Elution Buffer. Added the collected protein solution into a dialysis bag and placed it into pre-cooled PBS for dialysis. Change the pre-cooled PBS every 6 h for 5 times. The expression and purification of CpGlv were analyzed by ‌Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis ‌(SDS-PAGE). It is respectively 5% and 12% for the percentage of stacking and separating gels used in the SDS-PAGE analysis. The purity of CpGlv was quantified by SDS-PAGE and ImageJ software. The purified CpGlv and Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL were quantified to determine the concentration of CpGlv by SDS-PAGE and ImageJ software.

The supernatant of the lysed expression strain and the Multi-tag positive sample were subjected to SDS-PAGE and then electro-transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was rinsed 4 times with PBST and blocked with 5% skim milk at 37 °C for 1 h. After rinsing with PBST, it was incubated with mouse-derived anti-His monoclonal antibody at 4 °C overnight. After rinsing with PBST, HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After rinsing with PBST, a chromogenic substrate was added for development.

Determination of the MIC of CpGlv against S. aureus

S. aureus in the logarithmic growth phase was centrifuged at 6,000 g and room temperature for 4 min and collected, washed twice, and then resuspended in LB medium to a concentration of 2 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. Using the micro-dilution method [12], the final concentrations of CpGlv were adjusted to 80, 60, 40, 30, 20, 10, 5, and 2.5 µg/mL over time. Ampicillin at the concentration of 200 µg/mL was used as the positive control, and PBS as the negative control. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h, after which 10 µL of 6 mM resazurin solution was added to each well, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 37 °C for 3.5 h to observe the color change. The principle of detecting bacteria with resazurin is based on its characteristics as an oxidation-reduction indicator, which reflects the metabolic activity of bacteria through color changes.

Effect of CpGlv on the ultrastructure of S. aureus

S. aureus in the logarithmic growth phase was centrifuged at 6,000 g and room temperature for 4 min, and the bacterial precipitation was washed twice and resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. The treatment group was added with CpGlv at a final concentration of 2×MIC, and PBS was used as the negative control group. The samples were incubated in a constant temperature water bath at 37 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged to collect the precipitate, which was resuspended in electron-microscopy fixative, and fixed at room temperature for 2 h. The samples were sent to Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. for further processing and observation as previously reported by our laboratory [12].

Effect of CpGlv on the leakage of nucleic acids and proteins in S. aureus

S. aureus in the logarithmic growth phase was centrifuged at 6,000 g and room temperature for 4 min, and the bacterial precipitation was washed twice and resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. CpGlv was added to the final concentrations of 0, 1, 2, and 4×MIC. The samples were incubated in a constant temperature water bath at 37 °C. 200 µL of the bacterial solution was sampled every hour, and centrifuged at 8,000 g and 4 ℃ for 5 min to collect the supernatant, of which the absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm was respectively measured using an ultra-micro spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Effect of CpGlv on the cell wall and membrane permeability in S. aureus

S. aureus in the logarithmic growth phase was centrifuged at 6,000 g and room temperature for 4 min, and the bacterial precipitation was washed twice and resuspended in PBS to a concentration of 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. CpGlv was added into the bacterial suspension with the final concentrations of 0, 1, 2, and 4×MIC. 0.3% (v/v) of Triton X-100 was used as the positive control. The samples were incubated in a constant temperature water bath at 37 °C for 1 h, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. According to the instructions of the alkaline phosphatase kit, the absorbance was measured for the samples and standards at 405 nm using an automatic.

multi-function microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA). The ALP activity was calculated based on the standard curve drawn and the definition of enzyme activity units. Referring to the method in reference [12], ONPG was added to the supernatant to a final concentration of 3 mM, incubated at 37 °C for 8 h, and the absorbance was measured at 420 nm using an automatic multi-function microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA).

The treated samples were centrifuged to collect the precipitate, which was washed twice and resuspended with PBS. PI staining solution was added, and the samples were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The fluorescence intensity was detected at an excitation wavelength of 536 nm and an emission wavelength of 617 nm using an automatic multi-function microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA).

Effect of CpGlv on the biofilm formation of S. aureus

S. aureus in the logarithmic growth phase was diluted by LB medium at a 10-fold dilution to the final concentration of 1 × 107 CFU/mL, which was transferred to a 12-well culture plate with 2 mL per well. CpGlv was added to achieve the final concentrations of 0, 1/4, and 1/2×MIC, respectively. The sterile LB medium was set as the blank control. The samples were incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h. The liquid in the wells was discarded, and the planktonic bacteria were carefully washed away with normal saline. The 12-well cell culture plate was air-dried naturally, added with 1 mL of methanol to fix for 30 min, washed with normal saline, and then air-dried naturally. 0.5% (v/v) of crystal violet staining solution was used to stain for 20 min, washed with normal saline, and air-dried naturally. The biofilm morphology was observed and photographed using an inverted microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). 1 mL of 33% acetic acid solution was used to completely dissolve the biofilm. 20 µL of the sample and 180 µL of acetic acid solution were mixed in a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 600 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times. Experimental data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (La Jolla, CA, USA). The experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences between groups were analyzed for significance using t-tests. * represents a significant difference (P < 0.05). ** represents an extremely significant difference (P < 0.01).

Results

Identification and analysis of CpGlv sequence

Through sequence alignment, the CpGlv sequence with a length of 1498 bp was identified (accession number C_AA109084.1). The ORF gene of CpGlv was located using EditSeq in the Lasergene software and had 591 bp in length. The corresponding amino acid sequence was deduced from the ORF gene sequence (Fig. 1). As predicted by SignalP, the signal-peptide cleavage site was between A16 and Q17, and the mature peptide sequence of CpGlv ranged from Q17 to F196.

Using Conserved Domains analysis, the region from V68 to R184 within CpGlv was identified as the conserved domain characteristic of Gloverin-like proteins. Multiple alignments of amino acid sequences demonstrated that CpGlv shared 41 highly conserved amino acid residues with five other CpGlv proteins. Notably, these proteins exhibited substantial conservation within their functional regions, and all of them possessed the conserved FGTLG motif (Fig. 2).

Physicochemical property of CpGlv

The physicochemical properties of CpGlv were predicted through ProtParam. The results indicated that the molecular formula of CpGlv was C862H1281N257O276S2, with a theoretical molecular weight of 19.72 kDa. The theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of CpGlv was 5.45, and the grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) was − 0.838, suggesting that CpGlv was an acidic and hydrophilic protein. Among the amino acid residues, Gly accounted for a relatively high frequency of 15.6%. The total number of negatively charged residues (Asp + Glu) was 24, and the total number of positively charged residues (Arg + Lys) was 18. Its extinction coefficient was 26,930, and the instability index (II) was 22.62, less than 40, classified as a stable protein. The predicted half-life of CpGlv was 0.8 h in vitro mammalian reticulocytes, and 10 h in E. coli.

Structure analysis of CpGlv

Using AF3 to predict the structure of CpGlv (ipTM = 0.62), the results showed that the secondary structure of CpGlv includes two α-helix structures and ten antiparallel chains, which together formed a three-dimensional glove-like structure that is conservative among the gloverins (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, the surface charge distribution of CpGlv is uneven, with the N-terminus mainly carrying positive charges and the C-terminus mainly carrying negative charges (Fig. 3B). The predicted structure of CpGlv was further validated by the PROCHECK online server, QMEAN server, and ProSA tool. In the Ramachandran plot 3D of CpGlv, the amino acid residue number in the most favoured regions was 126 (89.4%), and the amino acid residue number in the additional allowed regions was 15 (10.6%), while there was no the amino acid residue in the generously allowed and disallowed regions (Fig. S1A). The global quality score of the 3D modeled CpGlv was a QMEAN score of − 1.15, above − 4.0 (Fig. S1B). The 3D modeled structures of CpGlv showed its native confirmation within the range of the plots (Fig. S1C). The validation results of the 3D modeled CpGlv showed the relatively high quality of predicted proteins.

The results revealed that the fusion protein CpGlv was expressed in both supernatant and precipitate when induced overnight at 15 °C with IPTG at a concentration of 0.2 mM (Fig. 4A). The recombinant plasmid pET-28a-CpGlv contained a 6×His tag, which was used to purify and identify the target protein. The results suggested that the soluble fusion protein CpGlv was purified and presented a single band at approximately 24 kDa (Fig. 4B), slightly higher than the theoretical prediction of 19.72 kDa, possibly due to the influence of adding a 6×His tag and protein spatial structure. The purity and concentration of CpGlv were respectively 80% and 0.4 mg/mL (Fig. S2).

Effect of CpGlv on the cell wall and membrane permeability in S. aureus

It is commonly used to evaluate the permeability of cell membrane and wall for detecting the leakage of ALP and β-galactosidase in S. aureus [29]. The results showed that 1×MIC of CpGlv could significantly increase the ALP and β-galactosidase leakage of S. aureus in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 8A and B). As shown in Fig. 8C, S. aureus treated with CpGlv could be stained by the PI dye, and 1 ×MIC of CpGlv could significantly enhance the fluorescence intensity in S. aureus. The results suggested that CpGlv increases the permeability of the cell wall and membrane in S. aureus.

Effect of CpGlv on the biofilm formation of S. aureus

Compared with the PBS control group, 1/4×MIC of CpGlv decreased the biofilm density of S. aureus. When S. aureus was treated with 1/2×MIC of CpGlv, its biofilm showed an interwoven network structure and failed to form a complete structure (Fig. 9A). Then, every group of biofilm was dissolved and quantitatively analyzed by determining the absorbance at 600 nm. The results showed that the absorbance value of the group treated by 1/4×MIC of CpGlv was substantially lower than that of the PBS-treated group (Fig. 9B), suggesting that subinhibitory concentrations of CpGlv reduced the biofilm formation in S. aureus.

Discussion

S. aureus is one of the most important opportunistic pathogens in foods such as meat, eggs, and milk, and causes severe infections in humans and animals [30]. S. aureus exhibits high pathogenicity because of its wide distribution, high contamination rate, and rapid transmission [31]. Meanwhile, healthy humans or animals usually carry S. aureus in their nasal cavities and intestines, which provides favorable conditions for the contamination, transmission, and drug resistance of S. aureus [32]. Therefore, the development of natural antibacterial agents or preservatives has become a current research hotspot [33]. Insects produce a large number of AMPs, which serve as the first line of defense against bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites [34]. Gloverin is a glycine-rich AMP that is first isolated from the hemolymph of Hyalophora gloveri pupae and has only been found in Lepidoptera insects [14, 35]. In this study, a gloverin CpGlv from C. punctiferalis was first identified and reported by comparing the transcriptome database of C. punctiferalis with the known Gloverin gene sequence. The results of multiple alignment indicated that CpGlv was relatively conserved in the structural region, especially the highly-conserved FGTLG region, suggesting that Gloverins exhibited structural conservation in different species [15, 35]. Nonetheless, different Gloverins show various activities against microorganisms. TnClv, HpClv, and BmGlvs have notable antimicrobial effects on LPS rough mutant E. coli, but no antimicrobial effects on Gram-positive bacteria [15, 35, 36]. MsGlv identified by Xu et al. [37] binds to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and Gram-positive lipoteichoic acid (LTA), but has almost no antimicrobial activity on E. coli and S. aureus. In addition, MsGlv exerts antibacterial effects against Bacillus cereus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Cryptococcus neoformans, which may be related to the binding ability of MsGlv with peptidoglycan (PG), Laminarin O-specific antigen, and outer core carbohydrates. BMGlvA2 can effectively inhibit Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli (DH5α, Rosetta, and K88) and Salmonella typhimurium, and also has an inhibitory effect on some Gram-positive bacteria, such as S. aureus and Bacillus subtilis [18]. SeGlv, which was isolated from Spodoptera exigua by Hwang et al. [38], exhibited antibacterial effects against Gram-positive bacteria, but not against Gram-negative bacteria E. coli. In contrast, the CpGlv identified in this study exhibited significant antibacterial activity against S. aureus.

When AMPs are classified according to their net charges, the majority of them are cationic antimicrobial peptides (CAMP), and a small proportion are neutral antimicrobial peptides or anionic antimicrobial peptides (AAMP) [39]. Most of the Gloverin family are basic cationic AMPs, and their antibacterial mechanism mainly includes binding to the negatively charged bacterial cell membrane, affecting the stability of membrane potential, and ultimately leading to the leakage of intracellular contents [14, 40]. Because the number of negatively charged amino acid residues in CpGlv is much larger than that of positively charged amino acid residues, CpGlv belongs to an anionic peptide. To explore whether the antibacterial mechanism of CpGlv differs from that of other Gloverins, this study evaluated its effects on the cell wall and membrane permeability and quorum sensing in S. aureus. Preliminary experiments showed that CpGlv resulted in severe leakage of nucleic acids and proteins of S. aureus. ALP is located between the cell membrane and wall, and β-galactosidase is located inside the cell membrane. Therefore, it was usually used to evaluate the Cell Wall and Membrane Permeability of some bacteria that determine the ALP and β-galactosidase leakage [41, 42]. PI can penetrate the damaged cell membrane and bind to DNA to emit fluorescence, but it cannot pass through the intact cell membrane [43]. The results showed that CpGlv led to the ALP and β-galactosidase leakage of S. aureus and enhanced the intracellular fluorescence intensity in S. aureus. The results verified that CpGlv increased the permeability of the cell wall and membrane in S. aureus, further to result in the leakage of intracellular contents and ultimately the death of bacteria. AAMP is considered a supplement to CAMP and has different mechanisms of action. Studies have shown that bacterial cell membrane is a key target of AAMP, and AAMPs are usually amphiphilic structures that promote the interaction between AAMPs and cell membrane [44]. As a representative of AAMP, Paulmann et al. [45] proposed that DCD-1 L may mainly bind to negatively charged bacterial phospholipids through the cationic N-terminal portion, forming transmembrane ion channels and leading to membrane disintegration. Lc1687, an AAMP, found in large yellow croaker (Larimichthys crocea) could also target and disrupt the cell membrane of S. aureus, Vibrio vulnificus, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, and Scuticociliatida to cause leakage of their contents [33]. In this study, CpGlv could increase the cell membrane permeability and cause the leakage of intracellular contents and even death in S. aureus, possibly due to the uneven charge distribution of CpGlv, which allows the C-terminal cation to bind to the negatively-charged cell membrane. However, the specific binding mechanism is still unclear. Biofilms are mainly composed of extracellular polymer matrices. Many antibiotics, such as aminoglycoside antibiotics, can’t penetrate this reinforced matrix due to electrostatic repulsion [46]. The data suggested that 1/4×MIC of CpGlv reduced the biofilm formation of S. aureus, which may be due to the fact that CpGlv inhibited the production of intercellular polysaccharide adhesins [47]. These findings lay a foundation for in-depth research on the biological activity and antibacterial mechanism of CpGlv.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a novel Gloverin AMP CpGlv was identified, expressed, and purified. The recombinant protein CpGlv had a significant antibacterial effect against S. aureus by increasing cell wall and membrane permeability, leading to the leakage of intracellular contents and inhibition of biofilm formation. This study provides an important foundation for in-depth research on the antibacterial mechanism and application of CpGlv.
