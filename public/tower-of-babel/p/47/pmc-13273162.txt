Extensive Age-Balanced and Subject-Varied mmWave Radar Dataset of Referenced Records for Vital Signs
Authors: Felipe Parralejo, José A Paredes, Fernando J Álvarez, África Vicario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContactless measurements of vital signs can provide new ways of monitoring assisted living residents or unreachable patients. Millimetre-wave (mmWave) radars allow for such monitoring and are a privacy free solution that has proven to be effective in the last years. The dataset presented in this paper consists of mmWave radar records of 110 participants collected using two commercial 60 GHz Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave radars for vital signs applications. All measurements are referenced against a class IIa medical device which provides ECG and accelerometer data. This dataset includes subjects of different ages, and with varied health status – 15 individuals present heart-related issues. For each participant tests were carried out in different postures, with post-exercise and holding breath experiments. Extensive demographic information about the participants is included so that researchers can study the influence of these on their vital signs estimations. This dataset can be useful for the community to test algorithms to extract heart rate, improve state-of-the-art processing methods and perform statistical studies with a large age-balanced and subject-varied population.Subject terms: Health care, Electrical and electronic engineering, Biomedical engineering, Preclinical research, Databases

Background & Summary

Recent years have witnessed significant advancements in radar-based monitoring1–4, especially in the field of vital signs5–8, which has encouraged the publication of several datasets collected to tackle different problems. These are useful for the community as their availability reduces the workload of radar-based vital sign projects, enabling researchers to train and evaluate better estimation algorithms, avoiding the tedious task of collecting data9.

However, there are certain limitations in their data collection. First, those studies do not include enough participants to draw conclusions that can apply to the entire population, and all participants are roughly the same age. In addition, people with unusual health conditions are not always included, but they might be considered as primary targets of these systems. Data are usually collected only when subjects are at rest, although a few datasets include post-exercise measurements. Besides, some of the radars employed are ad-hoc devices, affecting the generalizability of the dataset. These limitations hinder the development of solutions or conclusions that can be applied to the real world. Not all publicly available datasets have all these limitations, but none of them seems to cover all, as can be extracted from the summary of the published datasets listed in Table 1. Each one of these will be briefly commented below.

Shi et al. pioneered work on radar-recorded heart sounds, providing 223 minutes of synchronized data with digital stethoscope validation10. Their data collection procedure included angle and posture variations, breath-holding and post-exercise measurements, and speaking tests as well. In a later work, some of these authors presented a larger dataset featuring 24 hours of synchronized radar and reference measurements under various physiological challenges validated in a clinical setting with physician oversight11. In this dataset, tilt table tests were included, and the Valsalva manoeuvre was used to produce hemodynamic changes in the circulatory system. Yoo et al. developed a radar dataset specifically developed for children under the age of 1312, which could enable infants age-group classification systems to be used in smart environments that could response according to the age and necessities of its inhabitants.

Gong et al. tackled the challenging problem of body movement artefacts by collecting radar data under ambulant daily living conditions13. They incorporated movement power detection for different body parts and studied its influence on heart rate estimation. Edanami and Sun contributed a medical radar signal dataset using dual-frequency Doppler radars, accompanied by synchronized ECG and respiratory belt measurements14. Chen et al. bridged the gap between mechanical and electrical cardiac activity through the development of a millimetre-wave radar system to contactlessly measure cardiac mechanical activity and reconstruct ECG15. Their contactless ECG monitoring dataset features 10 hours of comprehensive 4D cardiac motion signals from 35 participants.

Schroth et al. presented a multi-person radar dataset collected using a robot-mounted radar system16, capturing 62 scenarios with up to five persons in various positions and through different obstacles for search and rescue applications. Eren et al. also focused on search/rescue applications as well as on ambient assisted living through their ultra-wideband radar breathing dataset that features LiDAR reference data17. Most recently, Sadeghi et al. contributed a novel dataset which includes scenarios with elevated heart rates and participants with diverse physiological profiles, such as asthma and meditation practitioners18. And Lei et al. incorporated multi-target monitoring capabilities through their radar dataset, incorporating muscle electric signal waveforms as a reference for validation19.

From all these works, it can be extracted that there is an existing tendency to use readily available hardware, as most recent papers used commercial radar devices. In fact, only 2 out of the 10 presented works opted for ad-hoc designed radars. The most common manufacturer was Texas Instruments (TI), being present in half of the works through their Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radar modules. The operating frequencies of these were 60 GHz or 77 GHz. This trend toward commercial devices is significant as it enables better reproducibility of research results and makes it easier for other researchers to build upon existing work. Regarding the consistency of the datasets, four papers specifically noted incomplete test participation: Sadeghi’s study had only 4 of 10 participants complete elevated heart rate scenarios18, young children in Yoo’s study had difficulty following protocols, causing body movements12, Shi reported some subjects could not complete all scenarios10 and Lei noted some participants had limitations in performing tasks19. The lack of data for some participants can result in deteriorated systems that cannot properly generalize to all individuals. In addition to this, the demographic information about participants is not extensive, even absent in some datasets. The information provided is only age, weight, gender and height. This limited information hinders the extraction of stronger statistical conclusions.

For all the reasons stated above, we present a large age-balanced and subject-varied radar-based vital sign dataset with 110 participants including extensive demographic information. It was collected for 6 months (February-July 2025) following a rigorous procedure ensuring that all participants could perform all the different tests, and including both healthy subjects and individuals with heart-related problems. The dataset presents different postures and post-exercise tests, as well as demographic information such as age, weight, gender, height, weekly workout hours, alcohol and tobacco consumption levels, pathologies indicated by each participant; and blood pressure, heart rate and blood oxygen saturation level prior to the recording of the measurements. Following the trend in most recent works, this dataset was collected using two commercial millimetre-wave (mmWave) radars with a working frequency of 60 GHz and the measurements were referenced against a class IIa medical device accessory.

Methods

Participants

The dataset contains recordings of 110 participants, 50 male and 60 female, with ages ranging from 22 to 76. Most of the participants were healthy, but 15 informed about having heart or breathing-related diseases clinically diagnosed by external medical personnel. A complete summary of the statistics about the participants can be found in Table 2, and histograms for age, weight, height, and heart rates (from ECG data) are presented in Fig. 1. Besides, per-subject data are provided in the dataset. The study was approved by the Bioethics and Biosafety Committee of the University of Extremadura (No. 263/2024), and all participants signed an informed consent form, allowing for the distribution of their data. Besides, all the information included in the dataset has been pseudonymized to comply with the Spanish Organic Law on the Protection of Personal Data20 and Biomedical Research21.

Radar Devices

Two FMCW mmWave radars were employed to collect the data: the IWR6843ISK-ODS22 module connected to the MMWAVEICBOOST carrier platform23, both manufactured by Texas Instruments and presented in Fig. 2; and a TI’s IWR6843ISK variant by NodeNs Medical Ltd.24 which can be seen in Fig. 3. Both devices work at 60 GHz, have 4 RX antennas, a maximum bandwidth of 4 GHz and make use of the same firmware provided by Texas Instruments. Even though both have an onboard chip with 3 TX antennas, only two of the transmitters were used to allow for a higher transmission rate for TI’s radar. The IWR6843ISK-ODS has a 60° beamwidth at −3d Bm both in azimuth and elevation, which, according to the manufacturer, provides 120° field-of-view (FoV) in both directions. The IWR6843ISK also has a 60° beamwidth in azimuth, and only 20° in elevation, resulting in 120° FoV in azimuth and 30° in elevation25. This device was rotated 90° so that the head-foot direction was aligned with the azimuth direction for both radars. The radars were placed at ~0.5 m in front of the thorax and pointing at the chest to measure its motion, as represented in Fig. 4. This distance was chosen following the results found in26, where the lowest frequency estimation error was found for a radar-subject distance of 0.5 m. This was obtained for the front configuration, which is the closest to the one used in this work. Additionally, so that both radars were aligned with the head-to-foot direction of the body, the elevation antennas were activated for the IWR6843ISK-ODS, which was used to record the measurements while subjects were seated, whereas the azimuth antennas were used for the NodeNs device, which was used during the lying tests.

MmWave radar and reference device experimental setup.

Each of them emits a series of chirp-like signals that are reflected by targets in the scene and collected back by the receiving antennas. The incoming signal contains information on the modulations produced in the emitted signal caused by respiratory and cardiac processes27. The combination of the emitted signal with the received one through an electronic mixer produces the intermediate frequency (IF) signal. For each pair of transmit and receive antennas, one intermediate (IF) signal is obtained. Due to the emission of multiple chirps, multiple IF signals are present at the reception end, and these can be analysed through a Doppler Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to study velocities28. Another FFT can be performed over the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) samples to study distances and locate the subject within the radar field of view. To study vital signs, the phase of the signal is extracted from the subject’s range bin using arctangent demodulation (see29 for more information). To reduce phase random noise and increase the vital signs signal strength, the phase is extracted from the distance FFT obtained from the zero Doppler component, which is equivalent to averaging the reception chirps and then computing the distance FFT. Each recorded radar frame produces one sample of the phase signal, which is unwrapped to account for values outside the range [0, 2π]. For a static subject, R(t) = R0 + r(t), where R0 is the mean distance to the subject. The motion produced solely by the movement of the chest, r(t), can be obtained by subtracting the mean value of the phase signal to Eq. (1), giving rise to the vital signs signal. After that, a bandpass filter can be used to study breathing and heart rate signals separately31. For breathing rate, the range of frequencies that this filter must be able to retain is from small values above 0 Hz, in the case of slow breathing that occurs during sleep (hypopnea), to ~1.1 Hz in the case of severe pneumonia or maximal physical exercise32. In the case of heart rate, the frequency range is higher, from slightly below 1 Hz33 to ~3.5 Hz34. The acquisition of radar data was performed through serial communication. Raw data were received and stored considering each participant’s anonymous ID and each test scenario, and a timestamp was saved when the first data were received for synchronisation purposes. All the recordings were later processed to convert the binary data into signals and perform the aforementioned FFTs.

Reference Device

The Movesense Medical Device35 was used as a reference sensor for heart rate and breathing validation. It is a class IIa medical device accessory, in conformity with the EU medical device directive 93/42/EEC and other relevant standards. Its technical specifications are provided in Table 4. This device was chosen because of its lightness and easy-to-use design. The sensor is attached to a chest belt, as can be seen in Figs. 4 and Fig. 6, which most participants were familiar with, which allowed users to put it on themselves without external intervention, increasing participants’ comfort and privacy.

The Movesense Medical Device was used to acquire electrocardiogram (ECG) signals for heart rate validation, and accelerometer data measured from participants’ chests for breathing validation. The recordings were performed using a Windows system connected through Bluetooth to the device, and were linked to a timestamp for synchronisation purposes.

Synchronisation

All devices were connected to a common computer, which served as a time reference for all measurements. Each sensor’s recording includes a timestamp. All Movesense timestamps were referenced with respect to its internal clock and have been modified to use the computer’s reference by saving a synchronisation timestamp when the first measurement was received in the computer. Radar timestamps have been computed by saving a synchronisation timestamp when the first frame was received in the computer, and using the frame periodicity from the radar parameters. Although both devices are referenced using the same computer, perfect synchronisation cannot be guaranteed. As will be shown in the Data Records section, radar measurements show a slight systematic delay with respect to the reference device, but it is negligible for the sake of the application.

The data provided in the dataset have been shrunk to contain only the period during which data from all sensors were available.

Measurement Protocol

The measurements were carried out by the same person and following the same protocol. Demographic data were collected through a Google form, which participants filled out when applying for the study. The elders who were unable to fill out the form digitally provided the information in the test room. Before starting the tests, subjects had their blood pressure, heart rate and oxygen saturation level measured using standard medical equipment: the OMRON M2 Basic36 blood pressure monitor and the Beurer PO 4037 pulse oximeter. Then, they were instructed on how to behave during the measurement procedure. They were told to stay calm, avoid movements and stay silent to prevent undesired chest movements. All participants performed all the tests.

The recording time was measured in radar frames and remained constant for all tests conducted by the same participant. Most recordings were 500 frames long, i.e., 500 Tp = 50 s, using the parameters in Table 3. However, some interesting participants were recorded for 600 frames, for instance, those with abnormal heart conditions.

Four tests were carried out with each participant: lying and sitting at rest, and then the same post-exercise. The experimental configuration of the radars and the subjects can be seen in Fig. 7.

Data Records

The database is publicly available in Zenodo38. It is distributed in folders, one for each participant, and one folder for each test. This structure can be seen in Fig. 9.

Database folder structure for one participant.

Reference accelerometer and ECG measurements are stored in the files movesense_acc.csv and movesense_ecg.csv. Each row of these files contains the raw data as sent by the Movesense device – x, y and z acceleration components and a timestamp in the case of accelerometer data, and millivolts and a timestamp for the ECG data. For the tests in which participants were instructed to hold their breath, the USB push-button data were stored in the file non_breathing_ts.csv containing the begin and end timestamps of the non-breathing time. The chirp configuration is stored in the file radar_chirpConfig.json. Radar measurements are stored in the compressed file radar_rFFTs.zlib, packed using the pickle object serialisation library and saved using the zlib compression library, both part of the standard libraries of Python 3.11.0. The file contains a Python list of the range FFTs as received from the radar and the range bins computed using the chirp configuration. For each experiment, the number of FFT operations received by the radar is determined by the number of virtual antennas nv used. This can be extracted as nv = nrnt where r and t indicate receiver and transmitter respectively. The shape of the provided FFT arrays is (nv, nb), being nb the number of range bins. Radar timestamps are saved as a Python list in the file radar_timestamps.csv. An example Jupyter notebook, named ExampleCode.ipynb, is provided with the dataset, which includes a detailed guide to working with all these files.

Breathing correlation

This first test is the easiest and quickest to perform because of the higher amplitude and slower frequency of the breathing signal. Using the examples from Fig. 8, it can be checked that breathing cycles correlate for radar and accelerometer data. More specifically, for tests 1 to 4, the number of breathing cycles is 7, 7, 12 and 13 in both devices’ data.

Heart rate correlation

Taking as an example test 1, the frequency spectra for the radar phase and the ECG data can be compared. For both spectra, there should exist a strong peak where the resting heart rate of the subject is located, and these must match between devices. If raw radar phase is used, then the breathing signal is detected as the strongest frequency in the radar spectrum, because of the greatest amplitude of the signal, as shown in Fig. 10a. However, a weaker peak is visible near the reference heart rate. Applying an Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) filter, such as a 4th-order Butterworth filter, with a bandpass between 0.8 Hz and 2.0 Hz the breathing signal is removed from the radar phase31, and the heart rate component is clearly visible in the spectrum, as presented in Fig. 10b. This filter is valid for the rest experiments, but for the post-exercise ones the upper cut-off frequency needs to be increased to 3.5 Hz to account for bigger heart rates.

Frequency spectra for radar phase and ECG data. The strongest frequency in each has been marked. (a) Raw ECG and radar phase data. (b) Removing breathing from radar phase.

IBIs correlation

IBIs are directly related to heart rate and can be calculated for ECG and mmWave radar data as the time between consecutive beats. The values from both devices must correlate to validate heart recordings in radar data. Following the previous validation test, the filtered radar phase from test 1 will be used together with the ECG reference data. Considering the slight time delay discussed in the Synchronisation section, ECG R-peaks and radar phase maximum peaks are found as shown in Fig. 11a. Then, IBIs are calculated for both set of measurements and compared using a box plot, which can be seen in Fig. 11b. Both measurement methods exhibit a similar tendency, with mean values of 915.98 ms for ECG and 918.87 ms for radar phase. The proximity of the mean and median for both methods indicates a symmetric distribution around the central value. However, the IBI distribution obtained from radar phase demonstrates greater dispersion, as indicated by a wider interquartile range and the presence of more extreme outliers. In addition, it also demonstrates a larger standard deviation, which indicates greater variability. However, despite these differences, the overlapping interquartile ranges and close mean values show that both methods produce comparable IBI estimates.
