Balancing early thrombus removal with durable decompression in Paget-Schroetter syndrome
Authors: Rafael Demarchi Malgor
Published: 2026

I read with interest the study by Elbaranes et al1 evaluating catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, and computer-assisted vacuum thrombectomy for acute Paget-Schroetter syndrome. In a domain characterized by limited sample sizes and diverse treatment pathways, this report is of significant value as it addresses a practical inquiry: can contemporary thrombectomy techniques reduce the need for lytic exposure and subsequent procedures in young patients who are seeking expedited functional recovery?

Although the efficacy of the device is crucial, the overarching implication is of greater importance. The removal of an acute thrombus can restore vessel patency and alleviate symptoms; however, it does not address the fundamental compression associated with venous thoracic outlet syndrome. Consequently, the early need for reintervention in this series is not merely a technical issue; it serves as a biological reminder that Paget-Schroetter syndrome involves both thrombosis and obstruction. Our recently published American Venous Forum Clinical Practice Guideline on the care of patients with upper extremity deep venous thrombosis2 reinforces this etiology-driven framework, emphasizing that management should extend beyond clot removal alone and that patients with primary upper extremity deep venous thrombosis require individualized consideration of decompression-based strategies.

The finding that supports mechanical thrombectomy is intriguing; however, it remains exploratory. This study involved a small, non-randomized, era-dependent cohort, and the observed improvements over time may be attributed to factors such as patient selection, operator experience, surveillance, and advancements in peri-procedural care, in addition to the catheter platform itself. Consequently, this study should not be interpreted as evidence against first rib resection or as proof of the superiority of the device. Instead, it refines the critical clinical question: who benefits from single-session thrombus removal, who requires early thoracic outlet decompression, and what outcome should define success. Until prospective multicenter data become available, the most robust conclusion is also the most clinically relevant: in Paget-Schroetter syndrome, clot extraction is a beginning, not an endpoint.
