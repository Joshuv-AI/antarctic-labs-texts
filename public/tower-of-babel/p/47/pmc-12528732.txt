Exploring the regulatory mechanisms of paraptosis-related prognostic genes in gastric cancer using single-cell sequencing and transcriptome analysis
Authors: Zhengliang Liu, Ying Gao, Wenjie Zhang, Linchuan Li, Huanxin Ding, Shuangchang Zhang, Xushuo Fu, Dexu Zhang, Yunlu Cai, Jiankang Zhu, Yun Zhang, Guangyong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies have suggested a potential role for paraptosis-related genes (PRGs) in cancer pathogenesis, yet their prognostic significance and therapeutic efficacy in gastric cancer (GC) remain largely unexplored. This study aims to investigate the function of PRGs in GC and offer valuable insights for clinical assessment of patient prognosis. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and the cancer genome atlas (TCGA)-GC transcriptomic data were acquired from public databases, with PRGs derived from Literature. Differential expressed genes 1 (DEGs1) were identified through scRNA-seq differential analysis. Prognostic genes were determined via regression analysis, followed by construction of prognostic models and nomograms to validate risk scores as independent predictors. Immune infiltration analysis assessed prognostic genes’ impact on the tumor microenvironment. Cell trajectory analysis elucidated critical differentiation patterns between GC and normal groups, while cell communication analysis revealed interactions between key cellular subpopulations. In this study, 3,177 DEGs were identified from scRNA-seq. A risk model using 8 prognostic genes predicted GC patient survival outcomes effectively. These genes were also validated as independent prognostic factors in a nomogram. High-risk patients showed significantly increased immunotherapy resistance. T cells and fibroblasts were more differentiated in GC patients, while neutrophils had the highest interaction intensity in both GC and normal groups, with the CXCL8-CXCR2 interaction being most significant (p < 0.01). This study identified 8 prognostic genes (ABRACL, RANBP1, RAMP1, CCT2, AKR1A1, NPTN, ITGA8, CLU) and combined single-cell and transcriptome analysis to offer new insights into potential GC treatment strategies. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a primary epithelial malignancy originating in the stomach and is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths globally1. Its incidence (5.6% of all newly diagnosed cancer cases) and mortality (7.7% of all cancer-related deaths) rank fifth and fourth, respectively, among all malignant tumors worldwide, constituting a significant threat to human health2,3. The development of GC is a multifactorial process, influenced by both environmental factors and genetic susceptibility4. GC is frequently associated with Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection, and common risk factors include smoking, alcohol consumption, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, obesity, and poor dietary habits3–6. Recent studies on molecular mechanisms have further elucidated the underlying pathways involved in GC. For instance, upregulation of the long non-coding RNA WT1-AS has been shown to inhibit the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, consequently suppressing cell migration and inducing cell cycle arrest, while simultaneously promoting autophagy in GC cells7. Currently, the main treatment strategies for GC comprise surgical resection and combination therapies, which include radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, all of which can significantly improve patient prognosis8. Recent studies demonstrate that the natural compound myricetin exhibits anti-gastric cancer activity through suppression of the EGFR/ERK signaling pathway9. Network pharmacological analysis reveals that P-hydroxycinnamaldehyde effectively suppresses gastric cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis in vitro10. Furthermore, emerging techniques such as infrared and Raman spectroscopy have become promising diagnostic tools for GC11. However, Due to the subtle and nonspecific nature of early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, often failing to seize the optimal window for surgical intervention. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify more reliable prognostic biomarkers to predict the outcome of GC and to explore additional potential therapeutic targets, thus providing valuable guidance for the treatment of GC patients.

Paraptosis is a distinct form of cell death, characterized by mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum swelling, as well as cytoplasmic vacuolation12,13. The mechanisms that induce paraptosis are diverse and include upregulation of insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) expression, proteasome inhibition, activation of the endoplasmic reticulum stress response, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and the influx of calcium ions (Ca²⁺) into mitochondria or the opening of ion channels12,14,15. Paraptosis plays a significant role in various processes, including development, neurodegenerative diseases, viral and bacterial infections, and glaucoma12,16. Some studies have shown that numerous chemical agents effectively induce paraptosis in cells, playing crucial roles in cancer processes such as autophagic degradation, drug resistance, and angiogenesis. This highlights the potential importance and applicability of paraptosis, a non-traditional form of cell death, in cancer therapy17,18. A study revealed the regulatory functions of paraptosis-related genes in lung adenocarcinoma and constructed a paraptosis-related risk score model, which has significant value in predicting patient prognosis19. However, research on paraptosis in gastric cancer is scarce. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of paraptosis and the associated signaling pathways in gastric cancer could help identify potential therapeutic targets and provide new insights for the development of personalized treatment strategies.

Single-cell sequencing, which encompasses genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, is a crucial tool for deciphering cellular and molecular complexity. Compared to traditional transcriptome sequencing, single-cell sequencing enables the analysis of gene expression at the single-cell level, revealing the heterogeneity within cell populations20. This is indispensable for understanding complex biological processes and disease mechanisms. Its high sensitivity enables the detection of low-abundance genes or transcripts that are averaged out in traditional transcriptome sequencing, providing more comprehensive gene expression data. This advantage is particularly significant in cancer research21,22.

This study employs bioinformatics approaches to integrate single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and bulk RNA-seq data, systematically investigating the mechanistic roles of programmed cell death-associated prognostic genes in GC progression. Multiple analytical approaches were implemented, including functional enrichment analysis, prognostic risk modeling with nomogram construction, immune infiltration profiling, molecular regulatory network establishment, pseudotime trajectory analysis, and cell communication network exploration. These approaches were designed to elucidate the roles of these genes in shaping immune microenvironment characteristics, activating biological pathways, and participating in molecular regulatory networks. The findings provide novel insights into the biological significance of these genes in GC progression and prognostic evaluation, offering a theoretical foundation for further mechanistic investigations.

Materials and methods

Data collection

To gather transcriptomic and clinical data for patients, we acquired single-cell and bulk transcriptome data, the scRNA-seq data for GC patients were acquired from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE163558 (GPL24676) comprised 3 samples of primary gastric tissue tumour (GC group) and 1 normal sample of adjacent non-tumour stomach tissue (normal group). The gene expression profiles, clinical information and survival information of the TCGA-GC cohort were acquired from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The TCGA-GC contained 382 tumour samples of stomach tissue, of which 350 had survival information, and 32 normal samples of stomach tissue. The 350 samples in the TCGA-GC set were randomly divided into 210:140 (6: 4), where 210 samples were utilized for prognostic model construction (training set) and 140 samples were utilized as the testing set. The above datasets were selected based on the sample size of the datasets, the year of data upload, and the completeness of the survival information of the samples. Additionally, 66 paraptosis related genes (PRGs) were obtained from the literature23.

Processing of the scRNA-seq data

In the GSE163558 dataset, the PercentageFeatureSet function in the Seurat (v 5.0.1) package24 was utilized for quality normal to filter out cells with less than 200 genes and less than 3 cells covered with genes, cells with greater than 20% of mitochondrial genes25,26 and cells with less than 200 and greater than 6000 genes.In order to normalise the data, the ScaleData function in the Seurat (v 5.0.1)24 package was utilized. Similarly, the ElbowPlot function in the Seurat (v 5.0.1) package24 was employed for the purpose of plotting the gravel map to show the contribution of the top-ranked principal components (PCs) to cellular variation. After completing the principal components analysis (PCA) dimensionality reduction, we used the Seurat (v 5.0.1) package24 (resolution = 0.4) to cluster the cells using the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) method. The FindAllMarkers function was employed to identify specific highly expressed genes in various cell types (|log2Fold Change (|log2FC)| > 1, adj. p < 0.05). Then different cell types were annotated utilizing singleR (v 1.0.5) package27 and cellmarker. Finally, the ggplot2 (v 3.4.1) package28 was utilized to plot the proportions of different cell types in samples between different groups and the chi-square test was utilized to obtain the discrepancy between the groups in terms of cells.

Identification of single-cell and transcriptome differential expressed genes

As a further step to screen out genes associated with differences between GC and normal groups, in the GSE163558 dataset, the FindMarker function was employed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs1) in clusters of differentiated cells. Meanwhile, the screening results were visualized in the Manhattan plot by scRNAtoolVis (v 0.0.5) package29 (|log2FC| > 0.5, adj. p < 0.05). Subsequently, the transcriptome DEGs2 were obtained by Dseq2 (v 1.34.0) package30 in the TCGA-GC (|log2FC|>0.5, adj. p < 0.05). The ggplot2 (v 3.4.1)28 was utilized for volcano plot and heat map and DEGs2 could visualized.

Screening of PRGs related module genes

To obtain differentially expressed PRGs genes, PRG scores in the TCGA-GC were calculated using the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm in the GSVA (v 1.42.0) package31, which visualized the results of PRGs genes that differ in expression between the GC and normal group in violin plot. According to PRGs scores in different samples, samples in TCGA-GC were divided into high- and low-scoring groups, utilizing the optimal cut-off value for scores (minprop = 0.1) as the boundary. Then Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves for the two scoring groups were plotted employing log-Rank test in survminer (v 0.4.9) package32.

This study used the WGCNA (v 1.71) package33 to identify module genes that were highly correlated with the phenotype of GC. First of all, hierarchical clustering of all samples using the euclidean distance of the sample expression profiles was used to identify and exclude outliers. Subsequently, we set R2 = 0.90 to screen for soft thresholds (β) above the red cut Line. Gene adjacency was then calculated, which led to computing gene similarity, from which a gene dissimilarity coefficient was derived to create a hierarchical clustering tree of genes. In accordance with the specifications of the dynamic tree cutting algorithm, the minimum number of genes per gene module was established at 100, while the module merge cut Height was set at 0.1. A correlation was constructed based on the ssGSEA scores in order to illustrate the relationship between the scores and the modules. Moreover, we calculated a matrix of correlation coefficients between the module eigenvectors and the scores, and subsequently constructed a heat map to vividly visualize and illustrate the obtained correlation results. The modules with the highest positive correlation with ssGSEA scores were identified as the key module, the genes in the key module were recorded as module genes for subsequent analysis.

Identification of candidate genes and pathway analyses

DEGs1 and DEGs2 were obtained from the single-cell level and bulk transcriptome respectively. These differentially expressed genes from different sources are each involved in specific and diverse biological processes. Through in-depth research on them, we can gain a profound understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of processes such as cell differentiation, development, senescence, and apoptosis within living organisms. Candidate genes were obtained from the intersection between DEGs1, DEGs2 and module genes. The biological functions and signal pathways for candidate genes were analyzed for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)(www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html)34–36 enrichment utilized clusterProfiler (v 4.2.2) package37. A protein-protein interactions (PPIs) network for candidate genes was constructed utilizing STRING (https://string-db.org) (confidence level > 0.7). Finally, Cytoscape (v 3.5.2) software was utilized to visualize the network.

Screening of prognosis-related genes

In order to obtain prognostic genes with prognostic value more accurately and reliably, this study sequentially employed univariate Cox regression analysis and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis. These two methods play different and complementary roles in the process of screening prognostic genes. Statistically significant prognosis-related candidate genes were obtained using univariate Cox regression analysis (hazard ratio (HR) ≠ 1, p < 05) and proportional hazard (PH) hypothesis test by cox.zph function for survival (v 3.5.3) package38 in training set. The forestplot (v 3.1.3) package39 was utilized to acilitate the visualisation of results for prognosis-related candidate genes. Moreover, the LASSO analysis was performed on prognosis-related by Glmnet (v 4.1-2) package40. By shrinking the regression coefficients of unimportant genes to zero, the prognostic genes were screened out. The differential expression levels of prognostic genes in the GC and normal groups were analysed by the Wilcoxon rank sum test in training set (p < 05), with the results visualized in box-and-line plot by ggplot2 (v 3.4.1) package28.

According to prognostic genes scores in different samples, samples in training set were divided into high- and low-risk groups, utilizing the optimal cut-off value for risk scores as the boundary. Then risk score distributions were plotted using the survminer (v 0.4.9) package32, and survival outcome distributions were plotted using the ggrisk (v 3.1) package41 to assess the value of prognostic model. KM curve for the risk groups was plotted by survminer (v 0.4.9) to determine the difference in survival between two risk groups. Then, risk model was assessed by plotting the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve using the survival ROC (v 1.18.0) package42 for reliability of model. Finally, in the testing set, the generalizability of the risk model was assessed by risk curve, survival chart, KM curve and ROC curve.

Construction and validation of GC nomogram

In the TCGA-GC, univariate cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the risk scores and clinical characteristics of GC patients by survival (v 3.5.3) package [29]. Nomogram was constructed by rms (v 6.5-1) package43. Subsequently, the calibration curve and ROC curve were employed to evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the nomogram.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) enrichment analysis

To further explore biological function and pathway differences between two risk groups, ClusterProfiler performed the GSEA enrichment analysis (v 4.2.2) package37 based on the c5.go.v2023.2.Hs.symbols.gmt background gene set from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) (p.adjust < 0.05) (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

Analysis of mutated landscapes

To investigate the genetic differences between two risk groups, the maftools package (v 2.22.0) of the R language (v 4.2)44 was employed to analyse two cohorts of patients with mutation data. Ultimately, the high-frequency mutated genes were visualized in a waterfall plot.

Immune microenvironment analysis and prediction of immunotherapy response

A comprehensive analysis of immune infiltration cells was conducted for GC samples in the TCGA-GC using the ssGSEA algorithm of GSVA (v 1.42.0) in the R language. Moreover, a heat map was generated to illustrate the proportional distribution of different immune cells. In order to illustrate the distinction between different types of immune infiltrating cells in the two risk groups, a Wilcoxon test was employed for analysis. Moreover, immune cells with significant differences were identified as differential immune cells.

Finally, the tumour immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) score was employed to evaluate the response to immunotherapy in GC patients. A higher TIDE score has been demonstrated to indicate a greater immune escape of tumour cells.

Regulatory network construction

To obtain the molecular regulatory mechanisms of the prognostic genes, the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/) was utilized as a predictor of microRNAs (miRNAs) for prognostic genes and then long non-coding RNAs (lncRNA)-miRNA-prognostic gene molecular regulatory networks were mapped by Cytoscape (v 3.9.1)45 software. The upstream transcription factors (TFs) for prognostic genes were predicted by ENCODE database (https://www.encodeproject.org/) and the visualized TF-messenger RNA (TF-mRNA) regulatory network by Cytoscape (v 3.9.1).

Identification of key cells

In order to investigate the differential expression of prognostic genes among different cells in the GC and normal groups of the single-cell data set, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed. Ultimately, cells displaying a notable differential expression of prognostic genes (in excess of 50% of them) in differential cells were defined as key cells.

Analysis of intercellular interactions

Intercellular communication was determined by the CellChat (v 1.5.0) package46 and intercellular ligand-receptor interaction was analyzed by Celltalker (v 0.0.7.9000) package47. The thicker the connection line between ligand and receptor reflects stronger potential interactions between cells.

Cell trajectory analysis

For studying the mechanism of prognostic genes within key cells, the key cells were classified into distinct subpopulations through the use of UMAP downscaling. Subsequently, the differentiation of key cells was simulated using the Monocle (v 2.26.0) package48 based on highly variable genes.

Cell cultures

The human gastric mucosal epithelial cell line GES1 and human gastric cancer cell lines HGC27 and AGS used in this study were purchased from Procell (Wuhan, China). GES1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco); HGC27 cells were cultured in MEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin; AGS cells were cultured in F12k medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Incubated all cells in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 humidified incubator.

Samples

This study utilized 10 GC samples and corresponding normal gastric mucosal tissue samples, all of which were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University. All procedures were performed by the same experienced surgical team. The project has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (2021S1039) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their participation in the study.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using an RNA extraction kit (Feijie, China). The concentration and quality of the extracted RNA were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on a Roche LightCycler 480 (Roche) with FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche, Switzerland). Relative mRNA quantification was performed using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH gene as the internal reference. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v 4.2) software, Cytoscape (v 3.9.1), and GraphPad (v 10.2.3) platforms. The data were expressed as standard deviation (SD) of the mean. A Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon test was employed to ascertain any significant differences between two groups.

Result

Following the initial screening, a number of 18,361 cells and 24,162 genes were obtained on the basis of the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 1A-B). After normalizing and downscaling the data, 30 PCs and 15 cell subpopulations were obtained (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C-D). A total of 15 types of cell populations were annotated to 9 cell types (|log2FC| > 1, adj.p < 0.05), such as monocyte (Fig. 1E-G). Additionally, the histogram results indicated neutrophils had higher prevalence in the GC group, while B cells were more abundant in the normal group than in the GC group (Fig. 1H). Subsequently, the chi-square results showed that a grand total of 8 cells exhibited statistically significant differences between the GC and normal groups (p < 0.05), such as monocyte (Table 2). Ultimately, a number of 4,337 genes were distinguished between the GC and normal groups through FindMarker functional screening (|log2FC| > 0.5, adj.p < 0.05). Of these, 3,453 genes were up-regulated and 884 were down-regulated. After de-weighting the screened genes, a grand total of 3,177 genes had differentially expressed and were named as differentially expressed genes (DEGs1) (Fig. 1I).

Construction and validation of prognostic risk models

The objective was to further screen prognostic genes with prognostic value from candidate genes and construct risk models in training set. The univariate Cox regression analysis showed 15 genes associated with survival (p < 0.05), such as SLBP (Fig. 5A). Screening with LASSO regression analysis resulted in 8 prognostic genes (lambda.min = 0.01299735) in training set, including ABRACL, RANBP1, RAMP1, CCT2, AKR1A1, NPTN, ITGA8 and CLU (Fig. 5B-C). The algorithmic formula for computing this risk score was as follows: riskscore = ABRACL × (−0.011) + RANBP1 × (−0.026) + RAMP1 × (0.092) + CCT2 × (−0.026) + AKR1A1 × (−0.207) + NPTN × (0.159) + ITGA8 × (0.096) + CLU × (0.014). Furthermore, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test demonstrated that all 8 prognostic genes exhibited significant differential expression between two groups in TCGA-GC (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5D).

The risk scores were then used to divide the GC patients into 140 high-risk and 70 low-risk groups (optimal cut-off values = −0.1006767). The number of deaths rises significantly as the risk score in the sample rises, as could be seen from the scatter plot and the risk curve (Fig. 5E-F). Furthermore, patients in high-risk group had lower survival on KM curve (Fig. 5G). The all AUC values were greater than 0.6 indicated that the risk model we constructed was reliability (Fig. 5H). The prognostic model evaluation results obtained from testing set and TCGA-GC were consistent (Supplementary Fig. 1–8).

Construction and validation of GC nomogram

Independent prognostic analyses are essential for establishing robust clinical decision support systems. Therefore, in TCGA-GC, univariate Cox regression analysis identified the risk score as an independent prognostic factor for GC from risk score, N-stage, M-stage, T-stage, gender, age and histology. The result showed that the risk score was associated with prognosis of GC survival (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). In order to assess the predictive capacity of the 8 prognostic genes for GC, a prediction model nomogram was developed in the TCGA-GC and the predictive performance of the 8 prognostic genes was evaluated (Fig. 6B). The calibration curve demonstrated that the bias-corrected curve was in close proximity to the ideal curve (Fig. 6C). Based on the result of the ROC curve, all AUC values of the nomogram were greater than 0.6, which indicated that the prediction accuracy of nomogram was reliable (Fig. 6D-F).

Neutrophil was differential immune cell

In addition, immune infiltration analyses were performed. These were used to investigate the impact of prognostic genes on the tumour microenvironment. Between the two risk groups, there was a significant difference in neutrophils in TCGA-GC using the Wilcoxon test, making the neutrophil a differential immune cell in this study (Fig. 8A-B). The results of the study demonstrated that the exclusion and dysfunction scores were elevated in the high-risk group (Fig. 8C- D). This suggested that the tumour microenvironment in the high-risk group was difficult for immune cells to enter the tumour tissue and that immune cell function was significantly suppressed in the high-risk group. In addition, TIDE scores also showed more immune escape and immunosuppression in the high-risk group, which means more resistant to immunotherapy (Fig. 8E).

In order to obtain the key cells, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test demonstrated that there were significant differences in the GC and normal groups between T cells and fibroblasts (p < 0.05). Hence, this study designated the above two cells as key cells (Fig. 10A-C).

To further gain insight into the mechanism of action of prognostic genes in T cells and fibroblasts, T cells was classified into 14 subgroups and fibroblasts into 7 subgroups by means of UMAP downscaling (Fig. 11A). This study demonstrated the existence of differences in cell differentiation times within different subgroups and the taxa they occupy. The intensity of the colour in the graph was indicative of the timing of cell differentiation, with darker shades representing earlier periods (Fig. 11B-C). Validation of paraptosis-related prognostic genes by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The qRT-PCR results showed that ABRACL, CCT2, and RANBP1 were upregulated in gastric cancer cell lines, while AKR1A1, ITGA8, NPTN, and RAMP1 were downregulated, which was consistent with our previous expression analysis results in the TCGA cohort (Fig. 13A). The qRT-PCR results revealed that ABRACL expression did not show a significant difference between gastric cancer and normal tissues. However, the expression of CCT2 and RANBP1 was upregulated in gastric cancer tissues, while AKR1A1, ITGA8, NPTN, and RAMP1 were downregulated. Notably, CLU was downregulated in both gastric cancer cell lines and gastric cancer tissue samples, which contrasts with the expression analysis from our TCGA cohort (Fig. 13B). This might have been related to sample heterogeneity and the small sample size of the qRT-PCR detection.

Discussion

GC ranks fifth in terms of incidence and fourth in terms of mortality among malignant tumors globally. Its high incidence is associated with various pathogenic factors, including Helicobacter pylori infection, and its early symptoms are often inconspicuous1,2. As a result, GC has increasingly become a significant health risk, particularly in the context of global economic growth2,5. With the advancement of bioinformatics and sequencing technologies, research on gastric cancer risk prediction models and potential therapeutic targets has proliferated. Paraptosis, as a unique form of programmed cell death, remains underexplored in the context of GC. We integrated Single-cell and RNA-seq data to construct a risk prognostic model based on paraptosis-related genes. This study investigated the mechanisms underlying the role of paraptosis-related prognostic genes in GC and explored aspects such as immune infiltration, molecular regulation, and cell communication, providing new insights and perspectives for discovering potential therapeutic targets for GC.

Through a series of analyses, this study identified eight paraptosis-related prognostic genes (ABRACL, RANBP1, RAMP1, CCT2, AKR1A1, NPTN, ITGA8, and CLU). Actin-Binding Rho-Activating C-Terminal-Like Protein (ABRACL), as a regulator of the actin cytoskeleton and cell motility, is closely associated with the invasion and metastasis of various cancers49. Previous studies have shown that ABRACL is upregulated in GC tissues, and its high expression level is significantly associated with lower overall survival50. In addition, studies using Western blot analysis, flow cytometry, and caspase-3 assays have further confirmed the significantly increased expression levels of ABRACL and CBX4 in GC tissues and cells. These results indicate that these genes play a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of GC51.

Molecular chaperone protein CCT2 aids in the proper folding of proteins, thereby effectively maintaining cellular homeostasis and dynamic balance. Increasing evidence supports the crucial role of CCT2 in tumorigenesis of certain cancers52. Studies have shown that the relative expression levels of TSPAN31, METTL1, and CCT2 are positively correlated in GC. CCT2 is co-expressed with TSPAN31 and METTL1, and regulates the proliferation, migration, and apoptosis of GC cells53. This is consistent with our findings, suggesting that CCT2 plays an indispensable role in GC.

NPTN is a surface molecule containing multiple immunoglobulin-like extracellular domains54. Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression of NPTNβ in breast cancer cell lines significantly enhances tumor growth and angiogenesis in vivo, promoting breast cancer growth and metastasis54. In gliomas, NPTN acts as a tumor suppressor gene, inhibiting glioma cell proliferation and migration55. Although no literature so far has directly linked NPTN to GC, the results of this study indicate a potential association. Therefore, further experimental validation is warranted to confirm these findings, which may provide new directions for GC treatment.

RANBP1 plays a crucial role in the nucleocytoplasmic transport of proteins, nucleic acids, and microRNAs, and is essential for spindle checkpoint formation56. As a key factor in maintaining mitotic stability, RANBP1 determines the efficiency of mitosis, suggesting its close association with cancer initiation and progression56. RANBP1 is highly expressed in lung cancer stem cells (LCSCs) and glioma stem cells (GSCs), with its upregulation closely linked to the expression of cancer stem cell markers and regulatory factors, leading to poor patient prognoses. Additionally, RANBP1 influences epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and regulates the tumor microenvironment, highlighting its critical role in tumorigenesis and progression57. RANBP1, as part of a gene fusion, combines with ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase) to form the RANBP1-ALK gene fusion. This fusion is thought to play a critical role in the pathogenesis of highly aggressive epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma (EIMS), characterized by ALK-positive staining in tumor cells, which drives the aggressive clinical progression of the tumor58. Based on our analysis, we reasonably speculate that RANBP1 is associated with GC. However, further experimental validation is required to confirm its potential as a therapeutic target.

Previous studies using the GEPIA database and Kaplan-Meier plotter have investigated the expression of ITG genes and their significance in GC prognosis. The results showed that most ITG genes are highly expressed in GC, while ITGA8 is lowly expressed in GC. According to RNA-seq data, the high expression of ITG family members (A2, A5, A11, AV, and B1) is negatively correlated with overall survival (OS) in patients. Sequencing of GC tissues and GSEA data analysis suggests that ITGs may be involved in biologically relevant processes and signaling pathways associated with cancer59. The study also found that ITGA8 is highly expressed in high-grade serous ovarian cancer60, while it is lowly expressed in colon adenocarcinoma tissues, where it could serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker for colon adenocarcinoma61. Previous research findings, combined with our analysis, suggest that ITGA8 may serve as a reference indicator for gastric cancer prognosis and holds promise as a novel target for future diagnosis and treatment of GC.

The receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) assists in the localization of the calcitonin-like receptor (CLR) to the cell membrane62. Previous studies have shown that RAMP1 is significantly associated with the prognosis of GC patients, and our analysis results confirm this conclusion63. Additionally, RAMP1 is highly expressed in osteosarcoma cells compared to normal osteoblast cell lines, and it can serve as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target for osteosarcoma64. In melanoma, overexpression of RAMP1 is associated with CD8 + T cell exhaustion and poor clinical prognosis. Modulating the expression or function of RAMP1 can influence the immune regulatory effects of CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) on CD8 + T cells, thereby regulating antitumor immunity65.

Clusterin (CLU) is a highly conserved glycoprotein with multiple isoform-specific functions, widely distributed across different species. By regulating various signaling pathways, CLU plays a crucial role in prostate cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, liver cancer, colon cancer, bladder cancer, and pancreatic cancer, affecting processes such as programmed cell death, metastatic potential, invasiveness, proliferation rate, and overall cell growth66. In this study, we observed an interesting phenomenon: bioinformatics analysis indicated high expression of CLU in cancer tissues, whereas subsequent experimental validation, including qPCR analysis of cell lines and cancer tissues, showed lower expression levels of CLU in cancer tissues. This inconsistency may primarily arise from several factors: first, the public databases used for bioinformatics analysis might have sample selection biases or limitations in data processing; second, the relatively small sample size in the experimental validation may not fully represent the overall situation; moreover, differences in experimental conditions, technical procedures, and reagents may also influence the results. To more accurately elucidate the role of CLU in GC, future studies should consider these factors and employ larger sample sizes and multiple experimental methods for further validation.

In our study, significant differences were observed between T cells and fibroblasts in tumor versus normal groups, with enhanced activity of these two cell types in the GC group. As a result, we considered them as key cells in our analysis. Tumor-specific T cells can be detected in the blood circulation and tumor microenvironment of most metastatic epithelial cancer patients67. T cells play a crucial role in cancer by identifying and directly attacking cancer cells, while coordinating with other immune cells to suppress tumor growth and metastasis68,69. T cells in the tumor microenvironment are also critical for effective immune surveillance70. Two key antitumor subgroups, cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) and TH1 cells, play significant roles in the tumor-associated immune response. CTLs promote tumor cell lysis and release toxic granules such as perforin, granulysin, and granzymes71, while TH1 cells initiate and enhance CTLs functions, and promote the recruitment of CTLs, NK cells, and type 1 macrophages72,73. The Th1/Th2 ratio can serve as a prognostic marker for GC and be used to evaluate the surgical outcomes of GC resection74. The Th17 cell population is closely associated with the development and progression of GC. Studies have shown that IL-17 is closely correlated with increased levels of MAPK activation and neutrophil recruitment, which in turn promotes chronic inflammation and drives GC development75.

In the tumor microenvironment (TME), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) have been shown to play various roles in tumor progression76. They promote cancer cell proliferation, drug resistance, and immune evasion by secreting growth factors, inflammatory ligands, and extracellular matrix proteins77. CAFs are a diverse cell population within the tumor microenvironment and have a significant impact on tumor evolution and patient prognosis78. The mechanisms underlying the heterogeneity of CAFs in GC are not yet fully understood, which have hindered the clinical application of CAF-targeting therapies. However, existing studies indicate that CAFs are characterized by the co-expression of specific molecular markers such as high levels of INHBA and FAP, and they progressively accumulate as cancer develops79. In line with our analysis, we hypothesize that the accumulation of CAFs may be related to their diverse differentiation states. As cancer progresses, CAFs may undergo different differentiation trajectories, exhibiting varying functions and characteristics. They interact with T cells and other immune cells to collectively influence tumor progression. Furthermore, CAFs regulate the chemosensitivity of GC cells by secreting exosome-derived miR-52280. CAFs play multifaceted and crucial roles in cancer. Within the tumor microenvironment, they promote cancer cell proliferation, drug resistance, and immune evasion by secreting a variety of substances. In GC, although the mechanisms underlying its heterogeneity are not fully understood, there are specific molecular markers that accumulate as the cancer progresses. CAFs in different states interact with immune cells, influencing tumor progression.

Single-cell sequencing combined with transcriptomic analysis offers high sensitivity and accuracy, revealing cellular heterogeneity, providing comprehensive gene expression information, and offering broad research applications and complementary advantages in multi-omics studies81. These strengths make this technology an indispensable tool in life science research and clinical applications. Through single-cell sequencing coupled with transcriptomic analysis, this study deeply investigates the prognostic genes associated with GC. This integrated analysis overcomes the limitations of single-omics studies, providing a comprehensive perspective on understanding the system biology of the disease82–84.

In conclusion, this study employs bioinformatics approaches to integrate single-cell and RNA-seq data to explore the mechanisms of apoptosis-related prognostic genes in GC. It investigates the immune infiltration characteristics, biological pathways, and regulatory mechanisms of these genes, providing new insights for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for GC. Additionally, an innovative GC prognostic signature, encompassing eight apoptosis-related genes, has been developed. This signature demonstrates high predictive accuracy and offers valuable references for clinical treatment decisions in GC. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The experimental validation in this study is limited, and more clinical samples are needed for further validation. Ultimately, we will continue to focus on the study of gastric cancer-related prognostic genes to benefit patients with GC.
