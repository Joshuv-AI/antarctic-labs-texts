Multidimensional sampling framework reveals plant-driven effects on microbial spatial heterogeneity and niche differentiation in a natural ecosystem
Authors: Seong-Jun Chun, Yingshun Cui, Jihoon Kim, Jun-Woo Lee, Kyong-Hee Nam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe identification of complex spatial patterns of microbial communities in relation to their ecological niches is fundamental to understanding the mechanisms of ecological interactions among diverse organisms. This study introduces a novel three-dimensional (3D) sampling approach to examine the spatial dynamics of microbial populations and niche differentiation influenced by plant-mediated effects in natural ecosystem.ResultsMicrobial communities across horizontal and vertical dimensions were systematically mapped, and we found that the total microbial diversity, particularly among eukaryotes, increased more than ten-fold compared to that obtained via single-grid sampling, emphasizing the importance of spatial heterogeneity in shaping microbial dynamics. Moreover, the 3D framework enabled us to identify taxa specifically associated with particular plants, offering insights into plant–microbe interactions, pathogen prevalence, and ecological consequences of plant-driven effects on local communities.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings demonstrate that 3D sampling approach provides a reproducible and scalable methodology for investigating microbial spatial heterogeneity, pathogen ecology, and niche differentiation in natural environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00793-4.Keywords: 3D microbiome sampling approach, Plant–microbe interactions, Spatial heterogeneity, Soil microbial diversity, Environmental complexity, Niche differentiation

Background

Understanding the spatial organization of microbiomes is essential to unraveling the ecological processes that govern microbial community assembly, function, and plant–microbe interactions [1, 2]. The lack of spatial resolution has been recognized as a major limitation in understanding how rhizosphere communities are structured, especially in the context of plant-driven effects across heterogeneous soil environments [3]. As Brunel et al. [3] noted, such conventional approaches may overlook important microbial patterns, highlighting the need for spatially explicit frameworks. In natural environments, microbial communities are shaped not only by microhabitat conditions but also by spatial gradients and plant influence, forming a three-dimensional (3D) mosaic of microbial niches. These patterns are especially pronounced in patchy environments, where diverse plant species coexist and interact with microbial communities through both above- and below-ground processes [4, 5]. Previous studies have demonstrated that microbial composition often shifts markedly with soil depth, reflecting gradients in oxygen, organic matter, and root-derived inputs [6, 7]. Such vertical stratification highlights the importance of depth in shaping microbial communities. However, conventional sampling strategies are limited in capturing such complexity, which can obscure spatial heterogeneity and restrict our understanding of localized ecological interactions. Moreover, the ecological characteristics of microbes, including niche overlap and partitioning among closely related taxa—remain poorly resolved in space. Without fine-scale, 3D mapping, key processes such as competition, facilitation, and environmental filtering are often inferred rather than directly observed. This limits our ability to assess how plant-derived influences (e.g., root exudates, spatial proximity) shape the assembly and spatial structuring of microbial communities.

To address these challenges, we developed a novel three-dimensional (3D) microbiome sampling framework that systematically captures microbial and plant community structures across both horizontal and vertical axes. Our objective is to investigate microbial spatial heterogeneity in plant-rich ecosystems, specifically asking: How does microbial diversity vary across 3D spatial gradients, and what are the fine-scale patterns of plant–microbe associations? This approach enables a fine-scale investigation of microbial spatial heterogeneity and niche differentiation in complex plant-rich ecosystems, not only uncovering distinct plant–microbe associations but also detecting pathogen-specific distributions and niche specialization patterns that remain hidden under traditional methodologies.

Methods

Study site and three-dimensional sampling framework

This study was conducted in a natural weed-dense ecosystem in South Korea (Fig. 1a) to investigate plant-induced influences on microbial dynamics and niche differentiation using multidimensional approach. The sampling area contained several wild plants, including Brassica juncea, Cirsium pendulum, Erigeron sp., Triticum aestivum, Veronica anagallis-aquatica, and Vicia villosa (Fig. 1a, b). To investigate the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities, a multidimensional sampling process was employed to systematically partition and collect plant and soil samples across the vertical and horizontal dimensions. In the first step, a 30 × 30 cm plot was defined within the field and vertically marked using thin, sterile steel plates. The plates were inserted into the soil to precisely delineate the sampling area and prevent contamination. The soil outside the plot was removed to a depth of 15 cm to ensure that only the target area remained intact for further sampling.

Three-dimensional (3D) microbiome sampling framework and ASV diversity analysis. a Images of sampling area and approximate grid of divided cubes b Schematic representation of 3D microbiome sampling framework, illustrating the spatial grid and vertical layers with roots of different plant species. c Average number of ASVs per cube (left) and total accumulated ASVs (right) for bacteria, fungi, and eukaryotes in different sampling layers and root samples. d Rarefaction curve calculated using the total number of ASVs of bacteria, eukaryotes, and fungi, from different randomly selected cubes within each sampling layer and root sample. e Examples of three-dimensional spatial patterns of microbes exhibiting niche differentiating and spatial heterogeneity. The x- and y-axes represent horizontal positions within the 30 cm × 30 cm surface grid, while the z-axis indicates vertical sampling layers (phyllosphere, topsoil, root, subsoil). An interactive 3D version of this plot is provided as Supplementary File S1

The grid was subdivided into 36 cubic units, each with a horizontal side length of 5 cm, to accurately capture the spatial relationships. To evaluate the vertical heterogeneity, samples were collected from three distinct layers: the phyllosphere layer (total leaf surfaces), topsoil layer (upper 1 cm), and subsoil layer (5 cm below the topsoil) and the belowground root samples (including rhizosphere soil attached to the root surfaces) (Fig. 1a). In this study, “topsoil” and “subsoil” are used as operational terms specific to our sampling scheme, defined by depth. The surface centimeter was separated as an independent layer because it contained dense plant residues, whereas such material was much reduced below 1 cm. The depth ranges were therefore chosen to capture fine-scale gradients within the organic layer and can be flexibly adjusted depending on site conditions and research objectives. For phyllosphere sampling, all the plant material located at 10 cm above the ground surface was removed. Although the vertical structure of the phyllosphere could be stratified into finer layers, similar to the approach often used in soil sampling, we adopted this cutoff to ensure both practicality in fieldwork and reproducibility. Afterward, the plant material within each 5 × 5 cm unit of the grid was harvested systematically, including the leaves and stems. The harvested materials were finely chopped and thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneity, after which representative subsamples were collected for analysis. The topsoil layer was defined as the uppermost 1 cm of the soil. To extract this layer, sterile blades were used to carefully slice and remove the topsoil from each 5 × 5 cm unit, maintaining the structural integrity of the soil. The topsoil was passed through a 5 mm mesh sieve to remove rocks and plant debris. After sieving, the collected topsoil was homogenized, and a subsample was taken to capture the microbial diversity from this layer. After the topsoil had been removed, the subsoil layer, from 1 to 6 cm below the surface, was sampled. The entire subsoil content within each 5 × 5 cm unit was collected and then passed through a 5 mm mesh sieve to remove rocks and plant debris. After sieving, the remaining subsoil was mixed thoroughly to ensure microbial distribution. A 500 mg subsample was collected for DNA analysis. Throughout the entire sampling process, sterilization protocols were strictly followed. Tools such as blades, sieves, and containers were sterilized using ethanol and flames between each sampling to prevent cross-contamination. The subsamples collected from all layers were immediately stored on dry ice. Upon arrival at the laboratory, samples were transferred to a − 80 °C deep freezer for storage.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

Bacterial, eukaryotic, and fungal community analyses were conducted according to the methods described by Cui et al. [8]. Since 18S rRNA has limited resolution for fungi, we used ITS primers to capture fungal diversity separately [9]. Thus, the term “eukaryotes” hereafter refers specifically to non-fungal taxa, including protists and other small eukaryotic microbes. DNA was extracted using the FastDNA™ SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of extracted DNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, DE, USA). Bacterial 16S rRNA gene, eukaryotic 18S rRNA gene, and fungal ITS1 regions were amplified using the primer sets 341F/805R, TAReuk454FWD1/TAReukREV3, and ITS1F_KYO1/ITS2_KYO2, respectively [10–12].

Dual-PCR amplification, purification, and quantification were performed to prepare Illumina amplicon libraries. The forward overhang adapter (5′- TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-3′), reverse overhang adapter (5′- GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-3′), and i5 and i7 indexes were used for dual-PCR amplification. For 16S rRNA gene sequencing of phyllosphere and root sample, antimitochondrial peptide nucleic acid (5′-GGCAAGTGTTCTTCGGA-3′) and antiplastid peptide nucleic acid (5′-GGCTCAACCCTGGACAG-3′) were added to reduce the amplification of host DNA (0.25 μM final concentration for each peptide nucleic acid) [13]. PCR amplification conditions varied depending on the target gene. For the bacterial 16S rRNA gene, the protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 20 s, annealing at 55 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. For the fungal ITS region, the same cycling program was used but extended to 30 cycles. For the eukaryotic 18S rRNA region, the PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 10 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 20 s, annealing at 53 °C for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, then 15 additional cycles with an annealing temperature of 48 °C, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Negative controls (nuclease-free water as template) were included in each PCR run, and no amplification was detected. PCR assays were performed on a ProFlex PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) using TaKaRa Ex Taq™ Hot Start Version (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan). PCR products were purified with AmpureXP beads (Beckman Coulter, IN, USA) at a 1:1 ratio and quantified using Quant-iT™ PicoGreen® dsDNA detection kits (Invitrogen, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed using an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp; Macrogen).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Raw reads were processed using the DADA2 pipeline, and taxonomy was assigned using the Silva and UNITE databases [14–16]. In detail, filtering was performed with the DADA2s ‘filtration’ command with the following settings for the rRNA gene data set: truncLen = c(250,220), trimLeft = c(16,17), maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,2), truncQ = 2, rm.phix = TRUE. Chimeric ASVs were removed using the method ‘consensus’ and ‘removeBimeraDenovo’ commands. The DADA2 formatted Silva database (release 132.1) was used to align and classify the 18S rRNA gene sequences. For the ITS1 gene, filtering was performed with the DADA2s ‘filterAndTrim’ command with the following settings: minLen = 50, maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,2), truncQ = 2, rm.phix = TRUE. The UNITE database (UNITE General FASTA release for Fungi 2. Version 10.05.2021.) was used to align and classify the sequences. The reads assigned to the chloroplast, mitochondrial, and archaeal sequences were removed from the 16S rRNA gene dataset. In the 18S rRNA data, the ASVs assigned to fungi were removed, whereas the ASVs classified under the phylum Archaeplastida were extracted to visualize the plant diversity. These ASVs were further identified at the species level using NCBI BLAST analysis, and the results were matched with those of the plant species observed in the field. The ASVs that occurred only as singletons, doubletons, or tripletons were excluded from further analysis. Additionally, rare ASVs that appeared in fewer than five samples across the entire dataset were excluded. The obtained raw sequences are available in NCBI under the accession number PRJNA1214518–20.

All statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.3.2) [17]. Three-dimensional visualizations of microbial community distributions were generated using the “plotly” package in R [18]. Plots were created using ‘type = scatter3d’, which specifies a 3D scatter plot, and ‘mode = markers’, which displays each ASV as a marker (dot). The x- and y-axes represent the horizontal positions of samples within the 30 cm × 30 cm surface grid, while the z-axis represents the vertical sampling layers (phyllosphere, topsoil, root, and subsoil). The marker size and color were dynamically adjusted to reflect the relative abundance of each ASV, with color scale options used to control the gradient colors. Multiple ASVs were combined using subplot().

Results

This study presents a high-resolution, spatially explicit 3D sampling framework capable of highlighting microbial distributional patterns that conventional bulk or 2D planar sampling approaches fail to capture. A total of 128 samples were obtained across all layers, including 36 cube units each from the topsoil, subsoil, and root layers, and 20 samples from the phyllosphere layer due to the absence of plant material in some cubes. From these samples, we identified 3688 bacterial ASVs, 1777 eukaryotic ASVs, and 1274 fungal ASVs. As expected, the average number of ASVs per individual cube (~ 150 cm3) was highest in the subsoil and root layers, followed by the topsoil and lowest in the phyllosphere layer (Fig. 1c). Notably, microbial diversity in the phyllosphere was significantly lower than that in other layers. However, when all cube units were combined to represent the total sampling volume (~ 5400 cm3), the total number of ASVs detected was substantially higher across all biological kingdoms. Particularly, eukaryotic diversity showed a more than tenfold increase when integrating across the full 3D grid compared to estimates derived from any single sampling point, highlighting their pronounced spatial heterogeneity. These results highlight a critical limitation of traditional bulk or point-sampling strategies, which often fail to capture the true extent of microbial diversity present in natural ecosystems. The data support the necessity of high-resolution, multidimensional sampling frameworks to fully uncover the spatial complexity of microbial communities. The rarefaction curve analysis revealed that microbial diversity, as measured by the total number of observed ASVs, increased significantly with the number of spatially distributed sampling cubes, eventually reaching a plateau (Fig. 1d). This result indicates that even within a relatively small 30 × 30 cm grid, spatial heterogeneity in microbial composition is profound and non-random, emphasizing the ecological relevance of fine-scale sampling resolution.

The framework was also applied to assess plant-associated microbial spatial structuring. Using the 18S rRNA gene to resolve host plant genotypes, we visualized the 3D spatial distribution of multiple plant species (Fig. 2). For instance, as shown in Fig. 1a, Brassica juncea was located at the center of the sampling area and showed a skewed distribution in both the topsoil and root layers, aligned with the direction of its main root observed in the field (Fig. 2b). Vicia villosa dominated one side of the grid, while Veronica anagallis-aquatica was primarily distributed on the opposite side. Interestingly, Triticum aestivum occupied only a limited area in the root and topsoil layers, but was more broadly distributed in the phyllosphere, suggesting an expanded aerial presence despite a relatively smaller belowground footprint. These spatial patterns highlight the heterogeneous colonization of plant species and their potential influence on localized microbial community structures.

Distribution of six major plant ASVs across all three layers and root samples. The x- and y-axes represent horizontal positions within the 30 cm × 30 cm surface grid, while the z-axis indicates vertical sampling layers. An interactive 3D version of this plot is provided as Supplementary File S2

Microbial ASVs closely linked to specific plant species showed strong spatial association patterns (Fig. 3). Some ASVs were consistently enriched in proximity to the host plant, while others showed inverse spatial distributions. For example, even within a highly heterogeneous and multidimensional plant community, clear spatial associations were observed. As shown in Fig. 3a, Methyloligellaceae ASV1365 was exclusively detected in the phyllosphere of B. juncea, indicating a highly specific plant–microbe interaction. In contrast, ASV35, classified under Pseudomonas, exhibited an inverse spatial pattern, being absent where B. juncea was present. Moreover, Massilia ASV1164 displayed a spatial distribution that closely mirrored that of Vicia villosa, while ASV130, affiliated with the phylum Ciliophora, showed a strikingly similar pattern to the distribution of Veronica anagallis-aquatica (Fig. 3b). These results highlight the power of the 3D sampling framework to detect fine-scale plant–microbe spatial associations, even in complex natural ecosystems where multiple host species co-occur.

The x- and y-axes represent horizontal positions within the 30 cm × 30 cm surface grid, while the z-axis indicates vertical sampling layers. Additionally, 3D mapping approach revealed heterogeneous distributions of multiple well-known pathogens within the 30 × 30 cm sampling block. For example, the presence of Lagena radicicola (EASV97), an oomycete pathogen [19], was detected in the topsoil layer (Fig. 4). Similarly, Spongospora subterranea f. sp. subterranea (EASV56), the causal agent of powdery scab in potato [20], and Vermamoeba vermiformis (EASV136), a free-living amoeba with opportunistic pathogenic potential [21], also displayed spatially discrete and patchy distributions across the soil grid (Fig. 4). Notably, these taxa were not uniformly distributed but localized to particular coordinates and layers, highlighting that even within a small soil block (30 × 30 cm), pathogen occurrence is highly heterogeneous. Such spatial resolution offers valuable insights into potential hotspots of pathogen–host interaction and transmission routes within natural ecosystems.

3D scatter plot showing spatial distribution of selected pathogenic ASVs. The x- and y-axes represent horizontal positions within the 30 cm × 30 cm surface grid, while the z-axis indicates vertical sampling layers. An interactive 3D version of this plot is provided as Supplementary File S5

Using 3D spatial data, we identified clear cases of intra-genus niche partitioning, particularly within Massilia and Sphingomonas (Fig. 5). Specifically, in the case of Massilia (Fig. 5a), several ASVs with high sequence similarity, such as ASV8, ASV11, and ASV17, all sharing over 99% identity in the 16S rRNA gene, exhibited clear spatial differentiation. ASV8 was primarily found in the topsoil layer, whereas ASV11 and ASV17 were predominantly detected in the phyllosphere. While some degree of niche overlap was observed, their distributions displayed localized partitioning, indicating spatially distinct ecological preferences despite close phylogenetic relatedness. A similar pattern was observed in Sphingomonas (Fig. 5b), where ASV348 was mainly associated with the topsoil, whereas ASV98 and ASV730, despite sharing more than 98.7% identity, were more frequently detected in the phyllosphere. This suggests that even fine-scale genetic variants within the same genus can exhibit divergent habitat preferences, possibly reflecting micro-niche specialization.

Discussion

Understanding microbial communities requires an appreciation of their inherent spatial complexity [1–3]. At the microscale, microbes exist as tightly interconnected networks within heterogeneous environments, where spatial structure underpins community composition and microbial interactions [22]. Techniques like fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and hyperspectral imaging have revealed remarkable microscale structures and niche differentiation [23–25]. However, while micron-scale observations provide depth, they are limited in spatial scope and cannot capture the broader ecological dynamics operating in natural ecosystems where plants and microbes form hierarchical interaction networks across centimeter- to meter-scale environmental heterogeneity [2]. The three-dimensional sampling approach introduced in this study addresses this gap by providing a meso-scale framework that bridges microscale niche theories with large-scale ecological processes. Unlike traditional bulk sampling methods that average microbial signals across space, our results reveal that microbial distributions are highly structured in three dimensions, with vertical and horizontal gradients shaping community assembly.

As shown in Fig. 3, the methodological advantage of the 3D framework lies in its ability to capture precise spatial relationships between plants and associated organisms. Such fine-scale patterns of host-specific association and exclusion reflect real ecological dynamics in natural heterogeneous environments. Unlike conventional rhizosphere–bulk soil sampling, which identifies plant-associated taxa but cannot resolve whether their distributions overlap precisely with root zones in mixed stands, the 3D framework enables spatially explicit mapping of microbial taxa at centimeter resolution. In natural ecosystems where roots of diverse plant species intermingle, this framework also reveals the three-dimensional distribution of roots themselves, providing a basis for interpreting plant–microbe spatial interactions. These examples are provided as illustrations of the framework’s capacity, rather than as exhaustive community analyses, and highlight the additional insights that this approach can provide compared with bulk sampling strategies. Importantly, our results suggest that different plant species establish spatially discrete microbiome zones, with compositional boundaries likely influenced by root architecture, exudation profiles, and interspecific plant competition.

Beyond mapping plant-associated microbial distributions, the 3D framework also enables the detection and visualization of pathogens in situ. While conventional rhizosphere–bulk soil comparisons typically report presence/absence or relative abundance, they cannot resolve whether pathogens are clustered near specific hosts, dispersed through the soil, or spatially segregated in microhabitats. In our dataset, Spongospora subterranea f. sp. subterranea exhibited distinct hotspots across the block, and Lagena radicicola appeared localized in certain topsoil coordinates (Fig. 4). These heterogeneous patterns, observed even within a confined 30 × 30 cm grid, demonstrate that pathogens occupy unique ecological niches at centimeter scales. This fine-scale resolution has practical implications. For plant pathology, it provides a means to identify high-risk zones for pathogen colonization and transmission. For microbial ecology, it highlights the importance of spatial structuring in shaping host–pathogen dynamics. Thus, beyond describing community-level heterogeneity, the 3D framework enables pathogen ecology and epidemiological modeling in natural soils, with potential applications for both agricultural management and conservation microbiology.

Finally, we also observed clear patterns of niche partitioning among ASVs within the same genus (Fig. 5). These results suggest that even closely related microbial strains may undergo divergent ecological specialization to avoid competitive exclusion, an idea rooted in classic resource partitioning theory [26]. These intra-genus divergences, observed at centimeter scales, provide strong empirical evidence that micro-niche differentiation is a relevant structuring mechanism in natural microbial communities. The spatial mapping of plant ASVs and their microbial associates represents another important innovation. By combining plant and microbial spatial data, we showed that root exudation and physical proximity to plant tissues could influence the local microbial landscape, supporting the role of plant-driven environmental filtering. These associations go beyond spatial distribution; spatial co-location across layers and cubes provides strong support for ecological causality.

While this study primarily utilized three-dimensional bubble plots to visualize microbial spatial distributions, the potential applications of this framework extend well beyond static representation. Integrating this spatially explicit sampling method with advanced computational modeling approaches could unlock deeper insights into host–microbe interactions, microbial dispersal dynamics, and spatially mediated competitive or inhibitory effects. By coupling spatial microbiome data with ecological or biophysical models, future research may uncover interaction mechanisms and ecological processes that have remained elusive under conventional sampling paradigms. Thus, this 3D framework holds promise not only as a descriptive tool, but also as a predictive and analytical foundation for exploring the spatial ecology of complex microbiome systems.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive, fine-scale, and multidimensional framework that advances approaches in microbial ecology by integrating spatial and host-associative data. The method not only uncovered microbial taxa with plant-specific associations but also revealed spatial patterns of host–pathogen interactions and intra-genus niche partitioning that would remain undetectable with conventional sampling strategies. In conclusion, this multidimensional approach offers a powerful and scalable platform for spatial microbiome research, enabling precise analysis of plant–microbe-environment interactions. We suggest that this approach provides a valuable framework for ecological microbiology, particularly in heterogeneous and plant-rich ecosystems where fine-scale spatial structuring must be resolved to understand ecosystem processes and resilience.
