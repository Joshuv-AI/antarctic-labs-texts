Investigation of importance Raman shifts in liquid biopsy diagnostics of prostate cancer
Authors: Przemysław Mitura, Wiesław Paja, Paweł Płaza, Radosław Starownik, Iga Kuliniec, Michał Godzisz, Krzysztof Bar, Joanna Depciuch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the potential of Raman spectroscopy for liquid biopsy in prostate cancer using serum samples. We evaluated four machine learning models and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to classify prostate cancer based on Raman data. Support Vector Machine (SVM) demonstrated the best performance, achieving high accuracy, sensitivity, and F1 scores, with the highest overall. Random Forest (RF) also showed strong results, with accuracy of 0.87. The analysis identified two key spectral bands: 1306 cm−1 and 2929 cm−1, as potential biomarkers for prostate cancer. The PCA revealed that particular it is possible to differentiate serum collected from control and prostate cancer patients. Correlation analysis showed that the 2929 cm−1 band was significantly associated with PSA levels, MRI PIRADS, and lymph node metastasis (pN+), while the 1306 cm−1 band showed strong correlation with PSA and MRI PIRADS. These findings suggest that Raman spectroscopy, particularly the 2929 cm−1 band, holds promise as a reliable method for prostate cancer detection in liquid biopsy applications.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men in many developed countries1. Its incidence has risen steadily over the past decades, largely due to increased life expectancy, heightened awareness, and the widespread use of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing2. Despite advances in early detection and treatment, prostate cancer remains a significant clinical and public health challenge3. The disease is characterized by a heterogeneous biological behavior, ranging from indolent, clinically insignificant tumors to aggressive forms associated with rapid progression and poor prognosis. The complexity of its pathogenesis, involving genetic, epigenetic, hormonal, and environmental factors, continues to be the subject of intensive research4. PCa predominantly affects older men, with a median age at diagnosis of approximately 67 years5. In many cases, the disease is asymptomatic at early stages and is often detected incidentally through elevated PSA levels or abnormal findings on digital rectal examination (DRE). Further diagnostic evaluation is based on multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) to identify suspicious lesions within the prostate gland, followed by targeted core needle biopsy of the identified areas. Lesion assessment on mpMRI is standardized using the Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System version 2.1 (PI-RADS v2.1), developed by the American College of Radiology6. PI-RADS assigns scores from 1 to 5 to lesions within the peripheral and transition zones of the prostate based on imaging characteristics. A PI-RADS score of 1 or 2 indicates a low likelihood of clinically significant cancer, PI-RADS 3 denotes an equivocal finding, and scores of 4 or 5 suggest a high probability of clinically significant disease7.

Adenocarcinoma accounts for the vast majority of prostate cancer cases (≥ 99%)8. Histopathological confirmation is established via prostate biopsy, with tumor grading based on the Gleason scoring system, which has been further refined by the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) into five prognostic grade groups to better reflect clinical outcome9,10. Although widely used, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing for prostate cancer detection is limited by its lack of specificity, leading to overdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsies11. As a result, there is increasing interest in the identification of novel, non-invasive diagnostic tools and biomarkers that offer enhanced accuracy and specificity in the detection of prostate cancer12. Among emerging diagnostic approaches, vibrational spectroscopy—particularly Fourier-transform Raman (FT-Raman) spectroscopy—has shown promise in differentiating between malignant and non-malignant samples based on molecular composition13. FT-Raman spectroscopy enables the identification of characteristic vibrational modes of key biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, which may undergo structural and compositional changes during carcinogenesis14. By analyzing Raman spectral shifts in serum samples, researchers can potentially identify diagnostic biomarkers associated with prostate cancer, paving the way for earlier detection and more personalized treatment strategies14.

Zhang et al. created a novel microfluidic device (CMD) with a nanowire forest trap substrate (NFTS) was developed to efficiently capture circulating tumor cells (CTCs) from whole blood achieving up to 93.6% capture efficiency. It enables in-situ single-cell phenotype analysis via SERS, offering high diagnostic sensitivity and promising clinical utility for CTC capture and heterogeneity assessment15. In the other study, Authors applied surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) and multivariate statistical algorithms to differentiate between prostate cancer (PCa), benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and healthy controls using plasma samples. They showed that among 106 samples, distinct spectral differences enabled initial group separation. Classification using PCA-LDA achieved high accuracy for PCa vs. normal (96.8%) and BPH vs. normal (97.5%), while PLS-SVM achieved perfect classification (100%). In distinguishing PCa from BPH, PLS-SVM outperformed PCA-LDA, showing higher sensitivity (88.46% vs. 65.40%), specificity (87.80% vs. 75.60%), and overall accuracy (88.06% vs. 71.06%). These results highlight PLS-SVM’s greater potential for early PCa screening16. Stefancu et al., showed using serum samples obtained from 54 patients it is possible with 94% overall accuracy to detect prostate cancer using SERS17. In addition, Raman spectroscopy of extracellular vesicles isolated from patient plasma has been shown to differentiate cancerous from non-cancerous states with high specificity (4). These advances collectively highlight the diagnostic potential of Raman-based liquid biopsies18. Therefore, the aim of this study is to investigate the diagnostic relevance of specific FT-Raman spectral shifts in serum samples for the detection of prostate cancer. By applying FT-Raman spectroscopy to liquid biopsy samples, we seek to identify characteristic vibrational features that distinguish serum from prostate cancer patients and healthy controls. The study focuses on determining which Raman bands are most significant in reflecting biochemical alterations associated with prostate malignancy. Ultimately, the goal is to evaluate the potential of FT-Raman spectral biomarkers as a non-invasive, reliable diagnostic tool that could complement or improve upon current prostate cancer screening methods.

Results

Figure 1a shows Raman spectra of serum collected from control and prostate patients. In both group band at 827 cm−1, which is typically attributed to the ring breathing mode of tyrosine, an aromatic amino acid was visible, while band around 889 cm−1 related to C–C stretching vibrations in protein backbones. Similarly, the bands at 915 cm−1 and 958 cm−1 also represent C–C stretching modes, likely associated with protein structures. One of the most characteristic bands in biological Raman spectra is at 1001 cm−1, corresponding to the symmetric ring breathing mode of phenylalanine, another aromatic amino acid. At 1085 cm−1, the Raman signal can be attributed to C–C stretching vibrations, commonly found in both lipids and carbohydrates. The band at 1124 cm−1 is related to C–N stretching, again pointing toward the presence of proteins. A broad band at 1270 cm−1 is associated with the amide III band, involving a combination of C–N stretching and N–H bending modes in proteins. Moving toward higher frequencies, the band at 1340 cm−1 is generally assigned to CH2 and CH3 wagging vibrations, reflecting contributions from both lipid chains and protein side groups. The 1448 cm−1 band is similarly associated with CH₂/CH₃ bending, another common feature in lipid and protein-rich samples. The 1556 cm−1 band corresponds to the amide II band, primarily due to N–H bending and C–N stretching in proteins. This is followed by bands at 1595 cm−1 and 1614 cm−1, which are associated with C=C stretching vibrations in aromatic amino acids like tryptophan and tyrosine19]– [20. The 1656 cm−1 band is one of the most important and is assigned to the amide I vibration, mainly due to C=O stretching in the peptide backbone. Lastly, the band at 1787 cm−1 is attributed to C=O stretching vibrations in ester functional groups, typically found in lipid components of serum. In the high-frequency region of the Raman spectrum, the bands observed at 2871 cm−1 and 2929 cm−1 are primarily attributed to C–H stretching vibrations in lipids21]– [22. The 2871 cm−1 band corresponds to the symmetric stretching of CH3 groups. This vibration is generally associated with methyl groups found in fatty acid chains. The more intense band at 2929 cm−1 is assigned to the asymmetric stretching of CH2 groups, which are characteristic of long hydrocarbon chains in fatty acids. Finally, the 3064 cm−1 band arises from aromatic C–H stretching vibrations, indicative of the presence of aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan.

The loading plot presented in Fig. 2b shows how individual Raman shifts contribute to PC1 (green line) and PC2 (magenta line). In PC1, prominent features are visible near 2885–2940 cm−1, which are typically associated with CH-stretching vibrations from lipids. Together, the PCA score and loading plots suggest that while the separation between prostate cancer and control serum samples is not complete, there are underlying spectral differences, particularly in the CH-stretching region—that contribute significantly to the variance.

Discussion

This study highlights the potential of Raman spectroscopy as a liquid biopsy tool for prostate cancer detection using serum, comparing spectral data from two ranges: 800–1800 cm−1 and 2800–3000 cm−1. The results indicate that Raman spectroscopy, especially the band at 2929 cm−1, shows promise in distinguishing between healthy and cancerous samples, consistent with findings from previous research in prostate cancer diagnostics.

Several studies have investigated the use of Raman spectroscopy on serum to identify biomarkers for prostate cancer. Li et al. employed surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) combined with machine learning algorithms, achieving high accuracy in distinguishing prostate cancer cases from healthy controls. Their study highlighted the effectiveness of SVM in handling complex spectral data, aligning with our findings that SVM outperforms other machine learning models in this context23. Similarly, Zhao et al. utilized SERS and multivariate statistical algorithms to detect prostate cancer in plasma samples. Their approach achieved a classification accuracy of 100% for distinguishing prostate cancer from benign prostatic hyperplasia, further validating the utility of Raman-based techniques in prostate cancer diagnostics16. Additionally, recent advancements have incorporated multimodal approaches, combining Raman spectroscopy with other imaging modalities. Doherty et al. demonstrated that integrating Raman chemical imaging with digital histopathology using machine learning improved diagnostic accuracy for prostate cancer detection, particularly in distinguishing between Gleason grades 3 and 4.24

In our study, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model achieved an accuracy of 0.88 and a sensitivity of 0.94 in the 800–1800 cm−1 spectral range. These results are comparable with other Researchers, which showed a sensitivity of 96.5% and specificity of 95% when classifying blood plasma samples16.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that Raman spectroscopy, particularly the analysis of the 2929 cm−1 band, may serve as an effective tool in prostate cancer diagnostics. Combined with other clinical parameters such as PSA levels and MRI PIRADS scores, it could allow for early detection and monitoring of disease progression. However, as with other studies, ours is subject to certain limitations, such as a relatively small sample size and the retrospective nature of the analysis. Therefore, further prospective studies with larger patient cohorts are needed to confirm our findings and evaluate the reproducibility of the identified spectral biomarkers.

In summary, our findings demonstrating the potential of Raman spectroscopy as a non-invasive, accurate method for prostate cancer detection, especially when using serum samples. While bands at both 1306 cm−1 and 2929 cm−1 show promise, the 2929 cm−1 band offers superior performance for distinguishing between healthy and diseased samples. The integration of Raman spectroscopy with machine learning models, particularly SVM, offers a robust approach for liquid biopsy in prostate cancer diagnostics, paving the way for early detection and personalized treatment strategies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Raman spectroscopy has strong potential as a liquid biopsy tool for prostate cancer detection in serum samples. The results indicate that Raman spectroscopy can effectively differentiate between healthy individuals and prostate cancer patients, with the 2929 cm−1 band emerging as a particularly promising biomarker. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that this band provides clearer separation between groups compared to the 1306 cm−1 band, making it a more reliable indicator for classification. Machine learning models, particularly the Support Vector Machine (SVM), showed the best overall performance, achieving high accuracy, sensitivity, and F1 scores across both spectral ranges. Furthermore, the correlation analysis highlighted the 2929 cm−1 band’s significant associations with PSA levels, MRI PIRADS, and pN+, suggesting its potential role in both prostate cancer detection and assessment of cancer progression. The 1306 cm−1 band, while correlated with PSA and MRI PIRADS, appears less informative for grading or staging cancer. These findings support the utility of Raman spectroscopy, particularly the 2929 cm−1 band, as a non-invasive diagnostic tool for prostate cancer, offering promise for early detection and monitoring of disease progression through liquid biopsy.

Materials and methods

Materials

The study protocol, including all procedures, methods, and analyses, was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Medical University of Lublin (Approval No. KE-0254/101/04/2023). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and informed consent was obtained from all subjects. In this study, serum samples were collected from 53 men with histologically confirmed prostate cancer and 40 healthy controls to evaluate the potential of Fourier-transform Raman spectroscopy (FT-Raman) in distinguishing between the two groups. All patients were hospitalized at the Department of Urology and Oncological Urology, University Teaching Hospital No. 4 in Lublin, between May 2023 and February 2024. Blood samples were collected from patients in the morning after an overnight fast, during routine medical procedures. The samples were centrifuged at room temperature (1500×g for 15 min) to separate the serum. The obtained serum was then stored at − 80 °C until further spectral analysis. All patients diagnosed with prostate cancer underwent radical prostatectomy. The exclusion criteria included the presence of metastatic disease prior to surgery, a history of previous radiotherapy, and a history of other malignancies. The mean age of participants in the control group was 60 ± 15 years, compared to 67 ± 6 years in the prostate cancer group. The average PSA concentration in prostate cancer patients was 10 ± 12 ng/mL.

Patients were stratified by biopsy Gleason score (bGleason): 19 patients with bGleason ≤ 6, 30 with bGleason 7, 2 with bGleason 8, and 2 with bGleason 9. Postoperative Gleason scores (pGleason) included 5 patients with pGleason 6, 43 with pGleason 7, 3 with pGleason 8, and 2 with pGleason 9. Additionally, postoperative grading based on the ISUP system classified 5 patients as ISUP grade 1, 36 as grade 2, 7 as grade 3, 3 as grade 4, and 2 as grade 5.

Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) findings were also considered in patient stratification. Based on lesion size, patients were grouped into two categories: 24 patients with lesions < 15 mm and 29 patients with lesions ≥ 15 mm. Methods

Raman spectra collected and processing

FT-Raman measurements were carried out with a Nicolet NXR 9650 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The instrument utilized an Nd: YAG laser operating at 1064 nm in combination with a germanium detector. Spectra were collected across the 150–3700 cm−1 range using a laser power of 1 W. A non-focused laser beam with an approximate spot size of 100 μm was applied, and the spectral resolution was set to 8 cm−1. Each spectrum was acquired by averaging 64 scans and analyzed using Omnic software (Thermo Scientific). Before spectra, 4 uL of serum was placed into the CaF2 slides and waited to dry. For each individual sample, three spectra were recorded. In total, 159 spectra were collected from serum samples of prostate cancer patients, while 120 spectra were obtained from control subjects. Prior to analysis, spectra were normalized via vector normalization using OPUS 7.0 software (Bruker Optik GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) and smoothed employing the Savitzky–Golay filter.

FT-Raman spectra analyses and correlation with medical parameters

To evaluate whether the Raman spectral intensities of specific functional group regions differed significantly between the control group and prostate cancer patients, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The analysis was applied to selected Raman peaks corresponding to major biochemical components commonly found in serum. Following the ANOVA, a post hoc Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was conducted to identify which pairwise group comparisons exhibited statistically significant differences in spectral intensity. All statistical analyses were performed using Past 4.17 software, and a p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant. Subsequent analysis of the preprocessed FT-Raman spectra involved the application of PCA and various Machine Learning (ML) algorithms, including decision trees, k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed together for spectral range 800–1800 cm−1 and 2800–3000 cm−1 after preprocessing, including baseline correction using Rubberband correction and 64 points, normalization. The PCA was computed using the Past 4.17 software, and the first two principal components were visualized to assess the variance structure across samples. The ML models were then employed to evaluate diagnostic performance using metrics such as accuracy, F1-score, precision, and recall. For machine learning input, data matrices were constructed where columns represented Raman shift values and rows contained corresponding intensity values for each sample. The final column indicated the sample classification: 0 for control and 1 for cancer-positive cases. Following this, a decision tree was employed to pinpoint the most discriminative FT-Raman wavenumbers for differentiating between the two patient groups, thereby proposing potential urinary FT-Raman markers for prostate cancer. To assess model robustness, the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) strategy at the patient level was implemented, whereby each sample (i.e., 3 spectra for a specific patient) was used once as a test case while the remainder served as the training set. Finally, to examine the relationship between the selected FT-Raman markers and clinical parameters of prostate cancer, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted. Raman shifts identified by the decision tree were correlated with clinical variables such as cGleason score, PSA levels, risk stratification, and MRI PIRADS. The correlations were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 and were computed using PAST 4.0 software.
