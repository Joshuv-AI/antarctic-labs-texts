Tuberculosis meningoencephalitis mimicking lung cancer with metastasis: a case report
Authors: Seolah Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This case report describes a 73-year-old male with gait disturbance and headache, presenting with widespread necrotic and contrast-enhancing brain lesions alongside lung nodules. Initial differential diagnosis strongly favored lung cancer metastasis due to these findings. Despite largely negative cerebrospinal fluid and molecular tests, systemic evaluation with positron emission tomography-computed tomography showed increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in both lung and lymph nodes, further suggesting malignancy. However, a crucial neck lymph node biopsy ruled out cancer. Empirical antituberculosis therapy led to significant clinical improvement, though subsequent brain imaging revealed a paradoxical reaction. This case provides a striking example of the diagnostic dilemma of differentiating tuberculous meningoencephalitis from metastatic lung cancer, emphasizing the critical roles of clinical suspicion, biopsy, and understanding paradoxical responses in challenging presentations.

Introduction

Tuberculous meningoencephalitis is a severe form of central nervous system tuberculosis (TB) that often presents with nonspecific clinical, cerebrospinal fluid, and radiologic findings, making early diagnosis challenging. In rare cases, disseminated tuberculomas and systemic lesions can closely mimic metastatic malignancy, leading to substantial diagnostic uncertainty. Recognition of these atypical presentations is important for timely diagnosis and appropriate management.

Case Report

A 73-year-old male patient was admitted to the emergency department due to gait disturbance that began 1 week prior. He reported experiencing mild fever, abdominal discomfort, and a dull headache for 3 weeks, leading him to visit a local hospital where he was diagnosed with chronic cholecystitis due to slightly elevated liver enzyme levels. However, even after treatment for cholecystitis, his headache persisted, and he started experiencing weakness in his right leg, prompting his visit to the emergency room. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia, for which he was medicated. He had also been diagnosed with latent TB 2 years prior and completed the full course of medication.

Upon neurological examination, he presented with mildly slurred speech, subtle weakness of the right leg and ataxic heel-to-shin tests of both legs but was otherwise neurologically intact. His temperature was 37.3 °C, and other vital signs were within the normal ranges. The definitive nature of his condition, particularly whether it involved a neurological disorder, remained unclear until magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) results became available.

His initial brain MRI revealed numerous disseminated necrotic cores with perilesional edema in the bilateral cerebral hemispheres, basal ganglia, cerebellum, and brainstem. The lesions showed ring-shaped contrast enhancement at their cores (Figure 1A). Additionally, multifocal leptomeningeal enhancement was observed along the sulci and brainstem surface. Given these findings, the differential diagnosis was brain metastasis or infection, while autoimmune encephalitis was considered less likely.

Following the MRI, various examinations were conducted to evaluate the nature of the lesions. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed an opening pressure of 18 cmH2O, 2 red blood cells/µL, 10 white blood cells/µL (15% neutrophils, 84% lymphocytes, 1% eosinophils), a protein level of 121.9 mg/dL, a glucose level of 78 mg/dL (serum glucose level, 136 mg/dL), and a CSF adenosine deaminase level of 4.0 IU/L. Bacterial, fungal, and TB cultures were negative. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays for viral (cytomegalovirus, enterovirus, herpes simplex virus type 1/2, human herpesvirus 6, parechovirus, varicella-zoster virus, Epstein-Barr virus), bacterial (Escherichia coli, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae), and fungal (Cryptococcus neoformans, Cryptococcus gattii) pathogens, as well as TB, were all negative.

A systemic evaluation was also performed to rule out hidden malignancy or an unidentified source of fever. A contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a 27-mm irregularly shaped nodule in the right upper lobe apicoposterior segment, along with innumerable miliary nodules scattered throughout both lungs (Figure 2A). Several enlarged lymph nodes were noted in the neck but not in the chest (Figure 2D). An abdominal CT scan showed no evidence of malignancy.

At this juncture, the primary impression was lung cancer with miliary metastases in the lung and brain. However, to exclude pulmonary TB, a sputum Xpert MTB/RIF assay (Cepheid) was performed, which detected very low positivity. Real-time PCR of TB was repeated 2 days later for both CSF and sputum, and both tests were negative. This situation created a diagnostic dilemma regarding whether the patient had metastatic TB or malignancy.

A whole-body positron emission tomography scan was subsequently performed (Figure 2C). It showed increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in numerous nodules in both lungs, including the right upper lobe. Increased uptake was also noted in multiple lymph nodes in both hilar regions, both sides of the neck, porta hepatis, portocaval, aortocaval, and left para-aortic areas. The nature of these lesions, whether metastatic or inflammatory, remained unclear, necessitating a biopsy. A biopsy from a neck lymph node was performed and came back negative for malignancy, and no histopathological features of TB, such as caseating necrosis, were identified.

Despite the weak evidence for TB, anti-TB medication (HERZ [isoniazid, ethambutol, rifampin, and pyrazinamide] + levofloxacin) and dexamethasone to ameliorate edema were initiated empirically. With this treatment, the patient’s gait and speech normalized, and his headache resolved. He was discharged on anti-TB medication and methylprednisolone with a tapering schedule. However, he returned to the clinic earlier than scheduled, complaining of poor oral intake and constant fatigue. Follow-up brain and chest imaging showed decreased attenuation of the right upper lobe lesion and shrunken lymph node involvement (Figures 2B, E), while the ring enhancement size of the brain lesions appeared to have increased (Figure 1B). The extent of perilesional edema had also increased. However, no new necrotic foci were observed. These findings were considered more indicative of a paradoxical reaction to medication rather than the progression of TB. He was once again admitted for pulse steroid therapy, which resulted in neurological improvement. MRI after 4 months of anti-TB medication revealed a dramatic decrease in the size and number of lesions in the brain (Figure 1C).

Discussion

Diagnosis of central nervous system TB presents a well-known clinical challenge due to its often nonspecific presentations and radiological findings that can mimic other conditions [1,2]. While contrast-enhanced MRI is the preferred modality for identifying manifestations such as meningeal enhancement and tuberculomas [3,4], these features, particularly ring-enhancing lesions, show considerable overlap with the features of neoplasms like gliomas and metastases [4]. Tuberculomas can present as solitary or multiple lesions, and their appearance on MRI varies with the stage of maturation, from noncaseating granulomas to solid or liquid caseating lesions that exhibit rim enhancement, a pattern also seen in tumors and other infections [5].

The diagnostic dilemma is further compounded by atypical and often limited sensitivity laboratory findings [6,7]. CSF analysis can yield normal or near-normal results [8], and crucial confirmatory tests like acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smears and even PCR can be negative [9]. Studies have consistently shown that the sensitivity of the CSF AFB smear is poor [10], and while CSF PCR has been reported to have a sensitivity of up to 70%, it is not uncommon to obtain negative results even in culture-confirmed cases. CSF culture has a moderate sensitivity of 50%–70% but requires a prolonged incubation of up to 6–8 weeks, complicating and delaying definitive management [11]. This highlights the importance of maintaining a high degree of clinical suspicion for TB, even when initial microbiological tests are inconclusive.

Building upon the established diagnostic ambiguity, the literature also recognizes the possibility of dual pathology, where TB and malignancy coexist [12]. This phenomenon, noted for decades, addresses the clinical query of whether some lesions could be malignant while others are tuberculous. The coexistence of TB and cancer is a tangible risk, particularly in endemic regions [13,14], and can manifest within the same organ or even a single lymph node [15], making histopathological interpretation exceptionally complex. An underlying malignancy can induce an immunocompromised state, which may reactivate latent TB, creating a scenario of dual pathology. This is further complicated by cases where TB radiologically mimics metastasis or vice versa, underscoring the need to consider TB when new lesions appear in a cancer patient [16,17].

The seemingly abrupt onset of disseminated TB in this patient could mean either reactivation of partially treated latent TB or a newly acquired infection that disseminated in an individual with longstanding diabetes mellitus. Following the empirical initiation of antituberculous therapy (ATT), the phenomenon of paradoxical reactions presents a critical diagnostic hurdle, often mimicking treatment failure. A paradoxical reaction is defined as the clinical or radiological worsening of preexisting tuberculous lesions, or the emergence of new ones, in a patient who initially showed improvement on appropriate medication. This phenomenon has been shown to be remarkably common, occurring in over half of human immunodeficiency virus-negative TB meningoencephalitis patients [18]. Radiologically, this reaction frequently manifests as the enlargement of existing tuberculomas, the appearance of new tuberculomas, or worsening meningeal enhancement, findings that are virtually indistinguishable from tumor progression on imaging [17]. The underlying mechanism is believed to be an exaggerated immune response to mycobacterial antigens released from killed bacilli [19], rather than active mycobacterial replication. This understanding is crucial, as it provides the rationale for continuing ATT, often with the addition of corticosteroids or an increase in dosage, despite alarming imaging findings. The reaction typically occurs within the first few months of starting therapy but can be delayed, underscoring the need for long-term vigilance [18].

In the case described here, the diagnostic challenge lay in balancing two competing hypotheses: metastatic malignancy versus disseminated TB. The combination of a spiculated lung nodules, miliary pulmonary lesions, widespread ring-enhancing brain lesions, and FDG-avid lymphadenopathy strongly favored metastasis at presentation. However, the absence of histopathological evidence of malignancy on lymph node biopsy, along with the patient’s subacute febrile course and prior history of latent TB, prevented premature diagnostic closure. Empirical ATT was initiated based on cumulative clinical suspicion, and subsequent systemic improvement with transient radiologic worsening in the brain ultimately supported the diagnosis of TB. This case underscores the importance of iterative diagnostic reassessment in complex presentations.

Despite advances in the diagnostic and prognostic tools for tuberculous meningitis (TBM), significant gaps remain. Future research should focus on developing more sensitive and specific point-of-care diagnostics that can rapidly differentiate TBM from its mimics, particularly in resource-limited settings. There is also a need for reliable biomarkers to predict which patients will develop paradoxical reactions, allowing for proactive management and potentially mitigating the need for prolonged corticosteroid exposure. Furthermore, studies investigating the long-term neurological and systemic outcomes of TBM patients experiencing severe paradoxical reactions would provide invaluable insights into optimizing treatment strategies and improving patient quality of life.

This case vividly illustrates the profound diagnostic challenges associated with TBM, particularly when presenting atypically and mimicking malignancy. The patient’s initial normal CSF findings, negative molecular tests, and imaging suggestive of widespread metastatic disease created a complex clinical picture. The eventual resolution of lung and lymph node lesions and the clinical improvement on anti-TB therapy, despite transient radiological worsening in the brain consistent with a paradoxical reaction, strongly supported the diagnosis of disseminated TB. This case underscores the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for TB, even in the face of noncontributory laboratory results and confusing imaging findings. It also highlights the diagnostic utility of lymph node biopsy in ambiguous cases and the clinical significance of recognizing paradoxical reactions to avoid premature discontinuation of life-saving anti-TB treatment.
