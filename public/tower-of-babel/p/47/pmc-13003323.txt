Oncogenic GPR161 Drives Melanoma Proliferation and Metabolic Activity through TXNIP Inhibition
Authors: Yuna Roh, Jinhyeon Choi, Jin-Seong Hwang, Yeo-Jin Lee, Taesang Son, Sarang Kim, Nayeon Gu, Hyun Seung Ban, Eunsun Jung, Jang-Seong Kim, Tae-Su Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Melanoma progression is driven by both oncogenic signaling and metabolic reprogramming; however, the roles of G-protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) in these processes remain unclear. Here, we identified GPR161 as an oncogenic GPCR that is significantly upregulated in melanoma and associated with poor survival in advanced-stage melanoma. Functional studies revealed that GPR161 promotes melanoma cell proliferation and migration, whereas its suppression attenuates these malignant phenotypes. Using promoter analysis and chromatin immunoprecipitation–quantitative polymerase chain reaction, we demonstrated that signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) binds directly to and transcriptionally activates GPR161. Inhibition or silencing of STAT3 reduced GPR161 expression and impaired melanoma cell growth. Transcriptomic profiling further identified thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) as a key downstream target negatively regulated by GPR161. GPR161 depletion increased TXNIP expression, leading to reduced glycolytic capacity and proliferation under both physiological and high-glucose conditions. STAT3 knockdown recapitulated these effects by establishing a STAT3–GPR161–TXNIP regulatory axis. Analysis of the cancer genome atlas datasets confirmed an inverse correlation between GPR161 and TXNIP expression and showed that low TXNIP levels predicted poor overall survival. Together, our findings revealed that GPR161 promotes melanoma malignancy by linking STAT3 activation to TXNIP suppression and metabolic enhancement. This study identified GPR161 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in melanoma.

Introduction

Skin cancer (SC) is the most common form of malignancy, and it is estimated that one in five individuals will develop skin cancer at some point in their lifetime. Skin cancers are broadly categorized into non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) and melanoma, with melanoma representing a highly aggressive malignant tumor that arises from melanocytes located in the basal layer of the epidermis. Melanoma develops through the accumulation of various genetic alterations induced by ultraviolet (UV) exposure, including the activation of oncogenes, inactivation of tumor suppressor genes, and impairment of DNA repair mechanisms, ultimately leading to uncontrolled proliferation of melanocytes [1, 2]. Epidemiologically, melanoma accounts for approximately 4.6% of all newly diagnosed cancers in the United States, with an estimated 76,100 new cases reported in 2014. Although early detection significantly improves prognosis, approximately 1,000 deaths occur annually due to melanoma, representing about 1.7% of all cancer-related deaths. Notably, melanoma is responsible for nearly 75% of all skin cancer–related mortality, underscoring its disproportionately high lethality. Furthermore, the global incidence of melanoma continues to rise steadily each year, emphasizing the urgent need to deepen our understanding of its underlying molecular mechanisms and to develop more effective therapeutic strategies [3, 4].

The diagnosis of melanoma commonly involves assessing general clinical symptoms, performing physical examinations, obtaining imaging studies, and performing laboratory tests such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) measurements [5, 6]. Current treatment approaches primarily include surgical intervention, adjuvant therapy, radiotherapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), and systemic therapy [5, 7]. These treatments often induce significant discomfort, frequently damage normal tissues, and fail to achieve adequate cytotoxicity in malignant cells. These limitations highlight the critical need for a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms that drive melanoma progression, which could inform the development of more effective and selective therapeutic strategies [8].

G-protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) form the largest family of cell-surface receptors, with over 800 members comprising more than 2% of the human genome. They detect various chemical and sensory signals and regulate key physiological processes, including neurotransmission, secretion, immune responses, and cardiovascular functions. GPCR dysfunction contributes to many diseases, which reflects the broad physiological roles of GPCRs; moreover, approximately 30–40% of approved drugs act on these receptors. GPCRs signal not only through canonical heterotrimeric G proteins but also through an expanding set of alternative pathways [9, 10].

Numerous studies and datasets have reported GPCRs, which perform various functions in the body, to be closely associated with cancer progression. Since the discovery of MAS as the first GPCR with oncogenic potential in 1986, a direct link between cellular transformation and GPCRs has been proposed. Subsequent studies revealed that wild-type GPCRs can exhibit tumor-promoting activity when excessively exposed to abundantly produced or circulating ligands in the surrounding environment [11-13]. Dysregulation of GPCR signaling pathways is now recognized as a major contributor to various diseases, and abnormalities in G-protein signaling have been implicated in cancer initiation and progression. Analyses of publicly available gene expression datasets have shown that numerous GPCRs are overexpressed in different tumor types [14]. Collectively, these findings suggest that strategies targeting GPCRs and their downstream signaling components could offer important opportunities for developing new approaches for cancer diagnosis, prevention, and therapy [15].

GPCRs regulate key biological processes, such as cell differentiation, proliferation, and migration, and therefore, are well recognized as contributors to tumorigenesis, including melanomagenesis. While GPCRs essential for melanocyte development and homeostasis, such as ET/EDNRB, MSH/MC1R, and WNT/FZD, are expected to play roles in melanoma, several unexpected GPCRs, including GRM1, GRM3, GRM5, PAR1, CXCR4, CCR7, CCR10, and GPER1, have been reported to contribute to melanoma development [11, 16].

GPR161, an orphan GPCR belonging to the class A rhodopsin family, plays an important role in normal development. An 8-bp mutation in GPR161, which is expressed in the lateral neural folds and developing lenses, was identified as the cause of the vacuolated lens spontaneous mouse model, which exhibits neural tube defects and congenital cataracts [17]. In addition, recent studies have shown that GPR161 localizes to primary cilia and regulates sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling [18]. In addition to these developmental roles, GPR161 contributes to tumor progression in various cancers, including breast cancer and medulloblastoma [19, 20].

However, the association between GPR161 and melanoma and the underlying molecular mechanisms have not been clearly defined. Therefore, in this study, we conducted a series of experiments to elucidate the role of GPR161 in melanoma and the underlying mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Melanoma Cell Culture

The human melanoma cell lines SK-MEL-2, SK-MEL-28, SK-MEL-1, G361, and Colo829 were obtained from the ATCC (USA) and cultured in DMEM or RPMI1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing 10% FBS, streptomycin-penicillin (100 U/ml). Cells were incubated at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Melanoma cells were cultured in glucose-free DMEM supplemented with either 5 or 12 mM D-glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to assess the effect of glucose concentration. The human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293 was obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Republic of Korea) and maintained under identical DMEM culture conditions. All cell lines were authenticated by STR profiling and routinely tested for Mycoplasma contamination using the e-Myco plus Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (iNtRON Biotechnology, Republic of Korea).

siRNA and Plasmid Transfection

A mammalian expression vector was constructed by subcloning the full-length human GPR161 coding sequence fused to GFP and an N-terminal Flag tag into the pcDNA3.1 backbone (Invitrogen, USA). Melanoma cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells in 60-mm culture dishes and incubated for 24 h for transient overexpression. Plasmid DNA (5 μg) was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24–48 h of incubation at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere, cells were harvested or subjected to downstream assays.

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specific for human GPR161 or STAT3, as well as a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC), were purchased from Dharmacon (USA). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Knockdown efficiency was verified by quantitative real-time PCR at 48 h post-transfection.

Reverse Transcription-Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the NucleoZol reagent (Macherey-Nagel, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara Bio, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences used were as follows: TXNIP forward 5'-TGTGTGAAGTTACTCGTGTCAAA-3' and reverse 5'-GCAGGTACTCCGAAGTCTGT-3'; GPR161 forward 5'-TGGATCTTTGGTGTAGTGTGGT-3' and reverse 5'-ATGACCCCGAGGGTTAGCAT-3'; STAT3 forward 5'-GTGACAGT TCCCAATGGAG-3' and reverse 5'-CTTGCAGGAAGCGGCTATAC-3'; ACTB (β-actin) forward 5'-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3' and reverse 5'-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3'. β-Actin was used as the internal control, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Cell Growth and Migration Assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at the indicated densities, and the CCK-8 reagent was added at specific time points. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

Cell migration was evaluated using Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size; Corning, USA). A total of 3 × 105 cells in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, and the lower chambers were filled with medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed. In contrast, migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed sequentially with 4% paraformaldehyde and methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich), and counted under a microscope.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay

STAT3-binding motifs in the GPR161 promoter (E1: −64 bp, E2: −11 bp) were predicted using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) and verified with the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (https://epd.epfl.ch/). ChIP was performed using Dynabeads Protein A/G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to a modified protocol. Briefly, 1 × 107 melanoma cells were crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with 0.125 M glycine for 5 min. Cells were lysed in ChIP lysis buffer on ice for 10 min, and chromatin was fragmented to ~250–750 bp by sonication (Sonics & Materials, Inc., USA). The diluted lysates were pre-cleared with 50 μl of Dynabeads Protein A/G for 1 h at 4°C, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with either anti-STAT3 antibody (Cell Signaling, USA 1:100) or normal rabbit IgG control (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA, 1:1000). Bead-bound immune complexes were washed sequentially with low-salt RIPA, high-salt RIPA, LiCl, and TE buffers, then eluted in 0.1 M NaHCO3/1% SDS. The chromatin was treated with RNase A and proteinase K, reverse crosslinked at 65°C overnight, and purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The enriched DNA was analyzed by qPCR using primers flanking the predicted STAT3-binding sites in the GPR161 promoter.

RNA Sequencing

SK-MEL-2 melanoma cells were transfected with either a GPR161-targeting siRNA (siGPR161) or a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC) (Dharmacon) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. At 48 h post-transfection, the cells were harvested for total RNA extraction using NucleoZol (Macherey-Nagel). RNA quality was verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and only samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 8 were used for sequencing. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc., USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end reads. Raw reads were quality-filtered using Trimmomatic, aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR, and quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using StringTie. Differentially expressed genes between siGPR161 and siNC groups were identified using DESeq2 (|log2 fold change| ≥ 1, adjusted P < 0.05).

Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR) Assay

ECAR was measured using a Seahorse XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies), following the manufacturer’s instructions for the Glycolysis Stress Test. One day prior to the assay, XF96 sensor cartridges were hydrated with Seahorse Calibrant and incubated overnight at 37°C in a non‐CO2 incubator. SK-MEL-2 cells were transfected as indicated and seeded into XF96 cell culture plates at a density of 1.5–2.0 × 104 cells/well, allowing 12–16 h for attachment. One hour before measurement, cells were washed twice and equilibrated in Seahorse XF Base Medium (pH 7.4) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, but without glucose or pyruvate, for 45–60 min at 37°C in a non‐CO2 incubator. Sequential injections were performed in the following order: (A) D-glucose (final concentration 10 mM), (B) oligomycin (1 μM), and (C) 2‐deoxy‐D‐glucose (2-DG, 50 mM). ECAR values were normalized to the total protein content per well as determined by the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Glucose‐Dependent Proliferation Assay

SK-MEL-2 cells were cultured in glucose-free DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with either 5 mM or 12 mM D-glucose (Sigma‐Aldrich) and 10% FBS to assess glucose-dependent growth. Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at 2 × 103 cells/well and maintained under the respective glucose conditions for the indicated time points (24, 48, and 72 h). Cell viability was measured using Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8; Dojindo Laboratories) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37°C, and absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments). All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Bioinformatic Analysis

Publicly available transcriptomic and clinical datasets were analyzed to investigate the expression patterns and clinical significance of GPR161 in melanoma. Survival data for patients with skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) were obtained using Q-omics software [21], and patients were stratified into GPR161-high and -low groups based on the median GPR161 expression value for Kaplan–Meier survival analyses. Correlations between gene expression levels in SKCM were assessed using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (GEPIA2; http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/), which integrates normal, tumor, and GTEx-derived expression datasets [22].

We utilized Gene Expression database of Normal and Tumor tissues (GENT2; http://gent2.appex.kr/gent2/) to compare GPR161 expression between normal and tumor tissues across various cancer types, including melanoma. Additionally, RNA-sequencing–based transcriptomic profiles and corresponding clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) SKCM cohort were retrieved from the UCSC Xena portal (http://xena.ucsc.edu). Normalized mRNA expression values, reported as reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) or transcripts per million (TPM), were used to evaluate correlations between GPR161 and the genes of interest.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4. Experimental data are expressed as mean ± SD, unless stated otherwise. The statistical significance between two independent groups was assessed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For experiments involving multiple groups, two-way ANOVA was applied according to the study design, followed by appropriate post-hoc multiple comparison tests, including Sidak’s, Dunnett’s, or Tukey’s tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Upregulation of GPR161 in Melanoma and Its Role in Promoting Cell Growth and Migration

We first examined the expression of GPR161 using GENT2 and TCGA datasets to determine its clinical relevance in cancer. Both analyses revealed that GPR161 was significantly upregulated in melanoma tissues compared to normal tissues (Figs. 1A, 1B, and S1). Stratification by clinical stage further showed that patients with high GPR161 expression in advanced-stage tumors (stages III–IV) exhibited markedly reduced overall and disease-free survival (Fig. 1C). We performed proliferation and migration assays using SK-MEL-2 and SK-MEL-28 cells to investigate the functional role of GPR161 in melanoma. Suppression of GPR161 expression by siRNA significantly reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 1D and 1E), whereas GPR161 overexpression enhanced proliferation in both cell lines (Fig. 1F and 1G). Similarly, GPR161 expression was positively correlated with cell migration ability, with knockdown reducing the number of migrated cells and overexpression increasing the migration of SK-MEL-2 and SK-MEL-28 cells (Fig. 1H and 1I). These results demonstrated that GPR161 is aberrantly upregulated in melanoma and contributes to tumor progression by promoting cell growth and migration.

STAT3 Directly Regulates GPR161 Transcription and Enhances Melanoma Malignancy

We analyzed the GPR161 promoter region using the JASPAR database to identify its upstream transcriptional regulators and identified two putative STAT3-binding motifs (E1 and E2) (Fig. 2A). Because STAT3 is a well-established oncogenic factor in melanoma [23], we examined its involvement in GPR161 regulation. ChIP-qPCR assays showed that STAT3 bound only to the E1 motif. In contrast, no significant change was observed in the E2 motif (Fig. 2B). Consistently, siRNA-mediated STAT3 knockdown reduced GPR161 transcript levels (Fig. 2C). These results indicated that STAT3 primarily regulates GPR161 transcription through its interaction with the E1 motif.

Pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 with Stattic significantly suppressed the proliferation and migration of SK-MEL-2 and SK-MEL-28 cells. Stattic treatment significantly reduced the viability of melanoma cells compared to the control and led to a marked decrease in cell migration (Fig. 2D and 2E). Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that Stattic treatment decreased GPR161 mRNA expression in both cell lines (Fig. 2F). These findings suggested that STAT3 is an upstream signaling factor that regulates GPR161 expression and that the STAT3–GPR161 axis promotes melanoma malignancy.

GPR161 Negatively Regulates TXNIP Expression and Modulates Melanoma Metabolic Activity

We performed transcriptomic profiling of SK-MEL-2 cells transfected with either siGPR161 or siNC to elucidate the downstream pathways regulated by GPR161 (Fig. 3A). Differential expression analysis revealed that GPR161 knockdown resulted in a distinct gene expression signature, with several cancer-associated genes among the most significantly altered (Fig. 3B). Among these, TXNIP emerged as one of the most strongly upregulated genes, and the data also confirmed efficient GPR161 silencing (Fig. 3C). qRT-PCR confirmed that TXNIP mRNA levels increased upon GPR161 knockdown, whereas GPR161 overexpression suppressed TXNIP expression (Fig. 3D).

Since TXNIP is a critical negative regulator of glucose uptake and glycolysis [24], we assessed the metabolic phenotypes associated with GPR161 depletion. Under both physiological (5 mM) and high glucose (12 mM) conditions, GPR161 silencing significantly suppressed cell proliferation in a time-dependent manner in SK-MEL-2 and SK-MEL-28 cells, with a more pronounced reduction observed at 72 h than at 24 h (Fig. 3E). Consistently, Seahorse extracellular flux analysis demonstrated that siGPR161 cells exhibited markedly reduced basal glycolysis and glycolytic capacity, as indicated by decreased ECAR responses following glucose and oligomycin injections. In contrast, ECAR was effectively abolished by 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) treatment. Conversely, GPR161 overexpression enhanced ECAR responses, indicating increased glycolytic activity (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these findings indicate that GPR161 positively regulates glycolytic metabolism, thereby promoting the proliferative capacity of melanoma cells under normal and high-glucose conditions. Furthermore, STAT3 silencing recapitulated the effects of GPR161 knockdown, reducing GPR161 expression and concomitantly increasing TXNIP levels in SK-MEL-2 cells (Fig. 3G). These findings indicated that STAT3 positively regulates GPR161, which in turn suppresses TXNIP, thereby promoting melanoma metabolic activity and proliferation.

TXNIP Is Downregulated in Melanoma and Negatively Correlates with GPR161 Expression

Analysis of TCGA melanoma datasets revealed that TXNIP expression is significantly lower in tumor tissues than in normal samples (Fig. 4A). Consistent with our in vitro findings, TXNIP levels negatively correlated with GPR161 expression in samples from patients with melanoma (Fig. 4B). Survival analysis further demonstrated that, in contrast to the poor prognosis associated with high GPR161 expression, low TXNIP expression was associated with reduced overall survival in patients with advanced-stage melanoma (stages III–IV) (Fig. 4C). These results support the clinical relevance of the GPR161–TXNIP axis and indicate that loss of TXNIP may contribute to melanoma progression.

Discussion

Despite advances in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, patients with metastatic melanoma continue to experience poor outcomes, and long-term survival remains limited [25]. Melanoma progression is driven in part by the persistent activation of oncogenic signaling networks, including MAPK and STAT3, which sustain tumor growth, enable immune evasion, and contribute to therapeutic resistance [23]. In this study, we identified GPR161 as a clinically relevant molecular driver. High GPR161 expression in patients with late-stage melanoma was associated with significantly poorer overall survival, indicating its potential prognostic value. Functional experiments showed that siRNA-mediated silencing of GPR161 suppressed the proliferation of SK-MEL-2 and SK-MEL-28 cells, whereas GPR161 overexpression enhanced their growth. Collectively, these findings suggest that melanoma cells rely on GPR161 signaling to maintain aggressive behavior and highlight GPR161 as a potential therapeutic target that links oncogenic signaling and tumor metabolism.

STAT3 is a transcription factor activated by cytokines and growth factors that regulates a wide range of biological processes, including cell proliferation, migration, and survival, while playing critical roles throughout the course of skin carcinogenesis, thereby making it an attractive target for melanoma prevention and therapy [26]. Based on our observation of GPR161 overexpression in SC and previous studies demonstrating the pivotal role of STAT3 in skin tumorigenesis, we hypothesized that STAT3 regulates GPR161 expression. Our findings demonstrate that STAT3 directly binds to the GPR161 promoter to modulate its transcription and the STAT3–GPR161 axis functionally contributes to melanoma progression. GENT2 analysis revealed that GPR161 is also overexpressed in other cancer types, including gallbladder and head and neck cancers, compared to their corresponding normal tissues (Fig. S1). Given that STAT3 has been shown to promote the progression of gallbladder and head and neck cancers [27, 28], the STAT3–GPR161 axis may similarly contribute to tumor promotion in cancer types beyond melanoma.

TXNIP forms intermolecular disulfide bonds with reduced thioredoxin to inhibit its activity, thereby impairing the function of TRX-dependent proteins such as PTEN, ASK-1, and the NLRP3 inflammasome. Independent of this thioredoxin-binding property, TXNIP also suppresses cellular glucose uptake, thereby positioning it as a key regulator that links mitochondrial respiration, glycolysis, and the cell growth characteristics of the Warburg phenotype [29, 30]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that TXNIP exerts tumor-suppressive functions primarily by inhibiting glucose uptake and aerobic glycolysis [31, 32]. We performed transcriptomic profiling to elucidate the downstream pathways regulated by GPR161 in melanoma and identified TXNIP as one of the genes that exhibited the strongest negative correlations with GPR161. We subsequently validated this inverse relationship through multiple experimental approaches and metabolic assays related to TXNIP function, confirming that higher GPR161 expression enhances cellular metabolism. Collectively, these findings indicate that GPR161 promotes metabolic activation by suppressing TXNIP expression. According to analyses based on TCGA and GEO datasets, TXNIP is expressed at low levels in most cancers and is inversely associated with patient prognosis [33].

Mechanistically, our observation that STAT3 knockdown significantly elevates TXNIP expression (Fig. 3G), consistent with the effect observed upon GPR161 silencing, together with evidence that STAT3 increases GPR161 expression and that GPR161 reduces TXNIP levels, suggests that TXNIP repression is at least partly STAT3-dependent. To further clarify the mechanistic basis of the GPR161–TXNIP axis, we also examined established GPCR signaling pathways. GPR161 has been reported to couple predominantly with the Gαs subclass of heterotrimeric G proteins. Previous studies have demonstrated that GPR161 activates the cAMP–PKA signaling cascade via Gαs, thereby suppressing Hedgehog pathway activity [18, 34, 35]. Collectively, these findings indicate that GPR161 can function as an upstream regulator of the Gαs–cAMP–PKA axis. Several independent reports also support a link between elevated cAMP signaling and reduced TXNIP levels. In pancreatic β-cells, activation of the cAMP–PKA pathway promotes proteasome-dependent degradation of TXNIP [36], while ATGL-mediated lipolysis elevates cAMP and enhances TXNIP degradation [37]. Moreover, PKA activation attenuates TXNIP-dependent amplification of NLRP3 inflammasome–induced pyroptosis [38]. Together, these studies are consistent with a mechanism in which cAMP–PKA activation negatively regulates TXNIP expression. Integrating these insights with our own findings, we propose that GPR161 may suppress TXNIP expression through activation of the Gαs–cAMP–PKA signaling pathway. This mechanism may operate in parallel with the STAT3-dependent regulation described above, highlighting the potential complexity of the GPR161–TXNIP regulatory network. Further studies will be required to delineate how STAT3-dependent and cAMP–PKA–dependent mechanisms interact to orchestrate TXNIP repression.

In summary, this study identified GPR161 as a key regulator of melanoma malignancy controlled by STAT3 that acts, at least in part, through the metabolic suppression of TXNIP. These findings expand the current understanding of GPCR-mediated oncogenic pathways and provide a strong rationale for the development of therapeutic approaches targeting the STAT3–GPR161–TXNIP axis in melanoma.
