Nanofiber-reinforced ECM-based bioink for hybrid biofabrication of adventitia-mimetic anisotropic vascular constructs
Authors: Zhiyuan She, Yingqian Zhou, Shan Mou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTissue-engineered blood vessels are essential for vascular grafting. Despite significant progress, current research predominantly focuses on the macroscopic scale of vascular grafts, neglecting the anisotropic microstructure of the vascular wall, particularly the adventitia, which plays a key role in the mechanical stability and functional performance of native vessels. Consequently, this aspect remains underexplored in current vascular tissue engineering strategies, limiting the full potential of vascular graft development.MethodsIn this study, we developed a novel hybrid biofabrication strategy combining electrospinning and coaxial extrusion printing with nanofiber-reinforced bioinks to fabricate vascular constructs that mimic the anisotropic structure of the vessel wall. The bioink, composed of a gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel matrix, contains short electrospun polycaprolactone fibers and vascular-derived extracellular matrix, which improve the mechanical stability, printability, and biocompatibility of the bioink while also providing micro/nano-scale structure.ResultsUsing coaxial extrusion printing, we successfully fabricated vascular constructs with well-aligned fibers and cells oriented along the long axis of the artificial vessel, mimicking the micro-/nano-architecture of the adventitia. Finite element analysis revealed that shear stress during extrusion induced fiber and cell alignment, creating anisotropic structures that enhance the potential for vascular tissue engineering applications. In vivo studies demonstrated that these constructs promote host integration and vascular ingrowth without causing excessive inflammation.ConclusionsA promising strategy for fabricating vascular grafts with biomimetic adventitia-like structures is presented in this study, thereby providing a versatile platform for engineering vascular and other anisotropic tissues.Keywords: Anisotropic biofabrication, Vessel tissue engineering, Nanofiber reinforcement bioink, Extrusion printing, Hybrid biofabrication

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for one in every four deaths, highlighting the critical need for functional vascular graft applications [1]. Recent advancements in tissue engineering have enabled the fabrication of biomimetic blood vessels [2, 3]. Most researchers have focused on the macroscopic scale of blood vessels, successfully fabricating large-caliber vascular grafts, small-diameter (< 6 mm) vascular artificial blood vessels, and vascular networks [4–6].

Despite significant advances, most tissue-engineered blood vessel (TEBV) studies have overlooked the replication of the micro/nano-architecture of the vascular wall, particularly the adventitia, limiting the application of vascular grafts [7–9]. As the outermost and most complex layer of the vascular wall, the adventitia consists of axially aligned collagen bundles that provide additional structural integrity, reinforcement, and damage tolerance [10]. These aligned fibers act as a supportive “jacket,” resisting overdistension under pulsatile blood flow [11, 12]. Moreover, the microstructural organization of collagen into fibrils and microfibrils significantly improves its flexibility and fracture resistance, as cracks are effectively arrested from propagating across these discrete subunits. Consistent with these findings, adventitial fibroblasts and vascular progenitor cells adopt elongated morphologies and longitudinal alignment during vascular stress and remodeling. Such orientation may contribute to vascular remodeling and regeneration [13, 14]. In this context, replicating this anisotropy is essential for constructing biomimetic TEBVs.

Recent advances in biofabrication have attempted to address these limitations through various approaches, including electrospinning [15, 16], three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting [17, 18], and decellularized scaffolds [19, 20]. However, simultaneously modulating microstructural organization and macroscopic construct integrity using a single technique is difficult. Electrospinning generates fibrous scaffolds that resemble the extracellular matrix (ECM) but provide limited control of 3D architecture [21], whereas extrusion-based 3D bioprinting enables precise macroscale patterning but is insufficient for regulating micro-/nano-scale architecture. Alignment strategies using magnetic, electric, or optical fields have demonstrated potential; however, they are technically complex, cell-unfriendly, or exhibit limited scalability [22–24]. Hence, a simple and effective method for producing anisotropic adventitial structures is absent.

To bridge these gaps, we developed a hybrid manufacturing strategy that integrates electrospinning with coaxial extrusion-based bioprinting to engineer fiber-reinforced bioinks. Short electrospun fibers produced from vascular-derived extracellular matrix (VdECM) and polycaprolactone (PCL) composites were incorporated into a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel matrix, forming a printable fiber-reinforced bioink (Fig. 1). Decellularized ECM (dECM) scaffolds are biomaterials derived from human or animal organs/tissues using decellularization techniques that remove immunogenic cellular components. These scaffolds are primarily composed of ECM components, including collagen, elastin, and fibronectin [25]. The physicochemical signals and biological properties of dECM create an ideal non-immunogenic environment for cell seeding, with a natural 3D structure and numerous bioactive components [26]. Consequently, dECM is considered an ideal choice for regenerating functional organs/tissues. Moreover, PCL incorporation improves the electrospinning capabilities and confers increased mechanical strength [27]. This fiber-reinforced bioink combines excellent biocompatibility and mechanical properties, as well as suitability for extrusion-based coaxial printing. We prepared it to investigate anisotropic vascular micro-/nano-structures.

Schematic diagram of engineering a highly biomimetic VdECM-based vascular scaffold

During coaxial extrusion printing, the shear stress within the nozzle induced longitudinal fiber alignment, generating anisotropic architectures that mimic the adventitial bundles of native vessels (Fig. 1). This strategy highlights three key innovations: (i) Developing a printable fiber-reinforced bioink, (ii) integrating electrospinning and coaxial bioprinting, and (iii) using shear-induced alignment to recreate adventitia-like anisotropy. Unlike previous electrospinning–printing hybrid approaches that primarily focus on macroscopic reinforcement or multilayer assembly, this study introduces a nanofiber-reinforced ECM-based bioink that enables in situ formation of micro/nano-scale anisotropy during extrusion.

These innovations not only facilitate the reconstruction of adventitia-like anisotropic architectures that improve biomimicry of native vascular structures but also provide a versatile strategy for fabricating anisotropic tissues. Given that many functional tissues, including tendons, cardiac muscles, and peripheral nerves, rely on anisotropic organization for physiological properties, this approach could be applied in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of vessel-derived extracellular matrix

Preparation of vessel-derived extracellular matrix

Porcine aorta was obtained from a local slaughterhouse. The surface adventitia of the vessels was removed and sectioned into 5 × 5 mm pieces. These pieces were disinfected by immersing them in 75% ethanol for 30 min. After pretreatment, the pieces were decellularized using 0.3% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for 24 h, followed by 3% TritonX-100 for another 24 h. A 0.15 M NaCl solution containing 75 U/mL DNase was prepared to digest the cellular components of the blood vessels for 24 h. The samples were rinsed thrice with deionized water after each treatment. The sheets were further cleaned using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and oscillated for 48 h, followed by lyophilization in a vacuum freeze-drier. After decellularization, the lyophilized vessel pieces were milled into a fine powder using a grinder and digested with 1 mg/mL pepsin in 0.01 M HCl solution for 48 h to obtain a loose structure. After filtration, the pH of the solution was adjusted to 7.4 with 10 N NaOH solution. Finally, the neutralized solution was lyophilized in a vacuum freeze-dryer and stored for subsequent use.

Histological and morphological characterization of VdECM

The gross morphology of native and decellularized vessel sheets was photographed before and after decellularization. To evaluate the removal of cellular components and the preservation of matrix structure, native and decellularized vessel samples were fixed, embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). In addition, DAPI staining was performed to visualize nuclei in native and decellularized vessel tissues.

To observe the structural changes of VdECM before and after pepsin digestion, the morphology of the undigested decellularized vessel powder and the lyophilized digested VdECM was observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Residual DNA quantification

Residual DNA content was quantified to evaluate the decellularization efficiency of VdECM. Briefly, DNA was extracted from native and decellularized vessel samples, and the DNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. The DNA content was normalized to the tissue weight and expressed as µg/mg tissue.

Detergent residue assessment

Residual SDS in VdECM was assessed using a methylene blue colorimetric assay. Briefly, VdECM powder was homogenized in PBS and centrifuged to remove insoluble debris. The supernatant was mixed with methylene blue solution, followed by the addition of chloroform at a water phase-to-chloroform ratio of 1:2 (v/v). After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, the organic phase was collected, and the absorbance was measured at 650 nm. PBS without SDS was used as the negative control, and 0.3% SDS solution was used as the positive control.

Quantification and immunohistochemical analysis of ECM components

To evaluate the preservation of major ECM components after decellularization, hydroxyproline, elastin, and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) contents were quantified in native and decellularized vessel tissues. Briefly, native and decellularized samples were weighed and homogenized for sample extraction. Hydroxyproline content was measured as an indicator of collagen content, GAG content was determined using a biochemical assay, and elastin content was measured using an ELISA kit according to the corresponding manufacturers’ instructions. The measured values were normalized to the wet weight of the tissue samples and expressed as µg/g wet tissue for hydroxyproline and GAG, and ng/g wet tissue for elastin.

For immunohistochemical analysis, native and decellularized vessel samples were fixed, embedded, sectioned, and stained with antibodies against collagen I, collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin. The stained sections were observed under a light microscope to evaluate the retention and distribution of representative ECM proteins after decellularization.

Fabrication of nanofibrous membrane by electrospinning

PCL and VdECM were dissolved in HFIP at room temperature and homogenized using an ultrasonic homogenizer for 15 min to prepare the PCL/VdECM electrospinning solution (w/v = 12.5%), with a mass ratio of 4:1. The electrospinning machine was operated at optimal parameters of 26 kV (voltage), 1 mL/h (flow rate), and 15 cm (needle distance). The fibers were collected on the surface of a grounded aluminum foil. For PCL nanofiber membrane preparation, PCL was dissolved in HFIP at a concentration of 10 wt% and stirred for 48 h at room temperature. The solution was pumped at 1 mL/h using 26 kV of voltage. The electrospun membrane was lyophilized in a vacuum freeze-drier to remove residual solvent.

Characterization of the nanofibrous membrane

The morphology of electrospun PCL/VdECM and PCL membranes was observed using field emission electron microscopy at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV. The samples were sputter-coated with gold for 180 s before scanning. The fiber diameters were statistically analyzed using the ImageJ software. The diameters of electrospun and dispersed electrospun nanofibers were calculated using 100 randomly selected nanofibers. The mechanical properties of electrospun PCL/VdECM and PCL membranes were elucidated using a uniaxial testing machine at a constant speed of 10 mm/min. The membranes were cut into rectangular pieces (1 × 2 cm) and soaked in PBS for 2 h before mechanical measurement. The Young’s modulus, strain at break, and ultimate tensile strength were calculated from the stress-strain curve obtained from the original data.

Biocompatibility evaluation of the membrane in vitro

Culture of human dermal fibroblasts

Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; Cat. No. CP-H103). Cells were cultured in fibroblast culture medium consisting of low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days, and cells at passages 3–6 were used for subsequent experiments.

Cell seeding

Before cell seeding, the nanofibrous membranes were cut into small square pieces (1 × 1 cm).

The pieces were then sterilized and placed in a 24-well plate. When the cells reached the logarithmic phase, HDFs were digested and seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well on different samples. The PCL nanofiber membrane group served as the control.

Biocompatibility evaluation of the nanofibrous membrane

After seeding HDFs onto the membranes for 1,4, and 7 d, cell viability was determined using a Live/Dead Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit. The cells were incubated in media containing CAM and PI. The membranes were washed with PBS and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Fabrication of GelMA/Fiber composite ink

Before mixing, the electrospun membranes were cut into small pieces (2 × 2 mm) and dispersed in tertiary butanol at a concentration of 1 mg/mL using a homogenizer at 10,000 rpm for 30 s to obtain PCL/VdECM short-fiber dispersions. The dispersions were then frozen at − 20 °C and freeze-dried for 12 h to acquire dry PCL/VdECM short fibers. Thereafter, PCL/VdECM short fibers at concentration gradients of 0, 1%,1.5%, and 2.0% (w/v) were introduced in the 5% (w/v) GelMA solution with a methacrylation degree of 60%. Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) was used as the photoinitiator at a concentration of 0.5% (w/v). The mixture was dissolved by heating in a 37 °C water bath and stirred to obtain Gel, Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 composite ink.

Photocrosslinking was performed using long-wavelength UVA light at 405 nm ± 5 nm with a nominal power of 60 W, a luminous flux of approximately 2800 mW, and an irradiation lens diameter of 4.45 cm.

Characterization of Gel and Gel-F composite ink

Formulated Gel, Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 composite ink were shaped in cylindrical mold and optically crosslinked with 405 nm UV for 30 s to form cylindrical hydrogel blocks with a bottom surface diameter of 8 mm and a height of 6 mm. Static compression tests were conducted using a universal testing machine equipped with compression platens, applying a compressive strain of 70% at a loading speed of 10 mm/min. Compressive strength and Young’s modulus were calculated from the generated stress-strain curves. Additionally, cyclic loading-unloading tests were conducted (up to 10 cycles) by loading at 10 mm/min to a compressive strain of 45% and unloading back to 0% at the same strain rate. The cyclic compression test curve was formulated and statistically analyzed using the obtained data.

Because Gel-F1 demonstrated the highest mechanical performance, it was selected as the bioink for subsequent experiments and designated as Gel-F. The interior surface morphology of Gel and Gel-F composite ink was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). The lyophilized samples were first incised to expose the inner surface, which was then coated with a thin layer of gold before scanning. To characterize the morphology and dispersion of short fibers within the GelMA matrix, VdECM/PCL short fibers were mixed with GelMA precursor solution and observed under fluorescence microscopy. The length and width of individual short fibers were measured using ImageJ software. The aspect ratio was calculated as the ratio of fiber width to fiber length. 100 fibers were analyzed to obtain the length distribution and aspect ratio distribution. The dispersion quality of short fibers in GelMA was evaluated based on fluorescence images by observing the distribution uniformity and the presence of visible fiber aggregation. Rheological analysis of the precursor hydrogel solutions of Gel and Gel-F was conducted at 37 ℃ using a rheometer with pare-plate geometry and 405 nm light systems. The viscosity and stress were determined using rotational shear deformation with a shear rate range of 0.1 s− 1 to 1000 s− 1 at 37 °C. Time-sweep oscillatory tests were conducted for 5 min at 1 Hz and 1% strain. The dynamic rheological observations were conducted at 10–45 ℃ (heating rate = 5 °C min− 1) with a constant frequency of 1 Hz.

Biocompatibility evaluation of the Gel-Fiber composite ink in vitro

To perform the cell viability assay, HDFs were seeded onto Gel and Gel-Fiber composite ink placed in the 24-well plate and optically crosslinked. Live and dead cell staining was performed after culturing for 1,3, and 7 d.

To evaluate the morphology of fibroblasts cultured in the bioink, phalloidin/DAPI staining was performed on days 3 and 7. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100, and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (Roche) for 1 h. TRITC-phalloidin working solution was added to the 24-well plate, followed by sample addition and incubation for 30 min at room temperature in the dark to observe the cytoskeleton. DAPI staining solution was then added to the plate. After sample immersion, the nucleus was counterstained for 30 s, and the cells were observed using a confocal fluorescence microscope.

3D coaxial printing of bio-inspired vessel with micro-nano structure

A biological 3D printer (SUNP BIOMAKER 2) and a customized 8G + 17G coaxial needle were used for extrusion-type coaxial printing. The printing needle moved at a constant traveling speed of 1 cm/min. In the microfluidic printhead, the inner 17G needle was used to deliver deionized water, forming a hollow structure. The 17G inner needle had an inner diameter of approximately 1.05 mm and an outer diameter of approximately 1.5 mm, while the outer 8G channel had an inner diameter of approximately 3.5 mm and an outer diameter of approximately 4.0 mm. The hollow channel was embedded with uncrosslinked Gel, Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 composite inks, forming the vessel wall. The speed of the inner syringe was 1 mm/min (0.12 mL/min), whereas that of the outer syringe was 2 mm/min. The ink was crosslinked under 405 nm UV light immediately after extrusion at a constant room temperature of 22 °C to create the bio-inspired vessel. After printing, transverse sections of the vessels were imaged, and the outer diameter, lumen diameter, and wall thickness were quantified using ImageJ software. During bi-layer coaxial bioprinting, the system included the following: (i) An outer layer of Gel-F1 with red fluorescent-labeled fibers (1 mm/min extrusion), (ii) a middle layer of Gel-F1.5 with blue fluorescent-labeled fibers (2 mm/min), and (iii) an inner lumen-forming deionized water channel (1 mm/min). Following concurrent extrusion and UV crosslinking, bi-layered vascular structures were constructed, collected in culture dishes, and photographed using confocal laser scanning microscopy for structural characterization.

Characterization of the vessel

To determine the diameter and fiber distribution area of bionic blood vessels, the printed vessels were cut into 8 cm long samples. The diameter and fiber area were measured using the ImageJ software.

To assess water absorption capacity, samples with known dry weights were immersed in deionized water for 1, 5, 10, 20, 30, and 60 min. The diameter of bionic blood vessels was determined using the ImageJ software.

To observe the arrangement of nanofibers in bionic vessels, the red fluorescent-labeled fibers in the composite ink and the wall of the bionic vessel were observed and photographed using a laser confocal microscope. Cells encapsulated in Gel and Gel-F hydrogels were fluorescently stained with green-fluorescent phalloidin (for cytoskeleton) and DAPI (for nuclei). With the vascular longitudinal axis set as a 0° reference, the alignment angles of fibers and cells were determined using quantitative statistical analysis.

Burst pressure testing was performed to evaluate the pressure-bearing capacity of the printed tubular constructs. Briefly, one end of the printed vessel was connected to a syringe pump, while the other end was connected to a pressure sensor for real-time pressure monitoring. Deionized water was infused into the lumen of the vessel at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min until rupture occurred. The burst pressure was recorded as the maximum pressure immediately before the abrupt pressure drop caused by vessel rupture. The burst pressure of vessels with different fiber densities was measured and statistically analyzed.

Suture retention testing was performed to evaluate the resistance of the printed vessels to suture pull-out. Briefly, one end of the printed vessel was fixed to the lower clamp of a universal testing machine. A single stitch was placed near the edge of the vessel wall using a 4 − 0 suture, and the suture was fixed to the upper clamp. The sample was stretched at a constant rate of 10 mm/min until the suture was pulled out or the vessel wall was torn. The maximum force recorded at failure was defined as the suture retention strength.

Compliance testing was performed to evaluate the pressure-induced diameter change of the printed vessels. Briefly, the printed vessel was connected to a pressure-controlled perfusion system and gradually pressurized. The outer diameter of the vessel was recorded at defined intraluminal pressures. Compliance was calculated from the relative change in vessel diameter normalized to the corresponding pressure difference. The compliance values of vessels with different fiber densities were calculated and statistically analyzed.

Cyclic tensile testing was performed to evaluate the mechanical stability of the printed vessels under repeated deformation. Briefly, vessel samples were mounted on a universal testing machine and subjected to repeated loading–unloading cycles at a tensile speed of 10 mm/min. The strain range was set from 5% to 50%, and each sample was tested for 10 consecutive cycles. The stress–strain curves were recorded during cyclic loading, and representative curves from the 1st, 5th, and 10th cycles were used to evaluate the cyclic mechanical behavior of vessels with different fiber densities.

Axial and circumferential tensile tests were performed to evaluate the direction-dependent mechanical properties of the printed vessels. For axial tensile testing, vessel samples were fixed between the clamps of a universal testing machine. For circumferential tensile testing, ring-shaped vessel samples were mounted using two parallel hooks to apply tensile loading in the circumferential direction. All samples were stretched at a constant rate of 10 mm/min until failure. The stress–strain curves were recorded, and tensile strength and strain at break were determined from the fracture point. The axial and circumferential tensile moduli were calculated from the linear region of the corresponding stress–strain curves within 5–15% strain.

Axial and radial compression tests were performed to evaluate the compressive resistance of the printed vessels. Briefly, vessel samples were placed between the compression plates of a universal testing machine. For axial compression, the samples were compressed along the longitudinal axis of the vessel. For radial compression, the samples were placed horizontally and compressed in the transverse direction. All samples were compressed at a constant rate of 1 mm/min until the strain reached 30%. The stress–strain curves were recorded during axial compression, and the axial compressive modulus was calculated from the linear region within 5–15% strain. For radial compression, because the contact area changed continuously during transverse compression of the tubular samples, the compressive response was normalized as force per unit sample length. The radial compressive stiffness was calculated from the linear region of the normalized force–strain curve within 5–15% strain.

In vitro endothelialization was performed to evaluate the endothelial cell compatibility of the lumen surface of the printed vessels. Briefly, GFP-labeled HUVECs were seeded into the lumen of Gel-F1 printed vessels and cultured for 7 d. After culture, the samples were observed and photographed using a laser confocal microscope. The endothelial cell distribution on the luminal surface was evaluated from the longitudinal view and transverse view, and 3D reconstruction was performed based on the longitudinal confocal images.

Animal experiments

Animal studies were conducted with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong University of Science and Technology. Six-week-old male BALB/c Nude mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in the SPF barrier facility of the Translational Medicine Center, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. BALB/c Nude mice are immunodeficient mice, and no previous experimental procedures were performed before this study. Nude mice were randomly divided into four groups using a random number table: the Gel group (Gel vessel scaffolds without fibers or cells, used as the control group), the Gel-F group (Gel-F vessel scaffolds without cells), the Gel-H group (Gel scaffolds with HDFs), and the Gel-F/H group (Gel-F vessel scaffolds with HDFs). Sample allocation to bilateral dorsal implantation sites was randomized where feasible. All animals were housed under the same conditions, and the procedures for implantation, sample collection, histological processing, and image analysis were performed using consistent protocols across groups to minimize potential confounders. The investigators performing animal allocation and scaffold implantation were aware of the group assignments because the implanted scaffolds differed in composition. Histological and immunohistochemical image acquisition and data analysis were performed by investigators blinded to the group allocation where feasible.

The experimental unit was an individual mouse. A total of 16 mice were used in this animal experiment, with four mice allocated to each group (n = 4 animals per group). Two vascular scaffold samples were implanted in each mouse. The sample size was determined based on previous experience with similar subcutaneous implantation studies and ethical considerations to minimize animal use. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. No specific inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied to animals or implanted samples during the experiment or data analysis. No animals, implanted samples, or data points were excluded from the analysis.

After one week of acclimatization in the SPF barrier facility, mice in all groups underwent the same surgical procedure, with the only difference being the type of implanted scaffold. The mice were anesthetized by inhalation of 1.5–2% isoflurane before surgery. After adequate anesthesia was achieved, the dorsal skin was disinfected, and a 1-cm dorsal incision was made. After blunt subcutaneous dissection, two vascular scaffold samples were implanted subcutaneously at bilateral dorsal sites in each mouse, and the incision was closed with 4 − 0 silk sutures. At the predetermined time points, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane anesthesia, and explants were collected on postoperative days 14 and 28. This subcutaneous implantation model was used to evaluate the in vivo biocompatibility, tissue integration, vascularization, and inflammatory response of the vascular scaffolds. H&E staining was used to assess tissue integration and general host response, CD31 immunohistochemical staining was used to evaluate vascularization, and CD68 immunohistochemical staining was used to assess macrophage infiltration and inflammatory response. No single primary outcome measure was used for sample size calculation, as no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed.

The animal experiments in this study were reported in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0.

Statistical analysis

For in vitro experiments, quantitative analyses were generally performed using three independent samples per group unless otherwise specified. Independent samples refer to separately prepared specimens or independently processed experimental units, rather than repeated measurements of the same sample.

For the in vivo subcutaneous implantation study, the experimental unit was an individual mouse, and the reported sample size refers to the number of animals rather than the number of implanted constructs. A total of 16 mice were used, with four animals allocated to each group (n = 4 animals per group). Each animal received two subcutaneous implants at bilateral dorsal sites. Sample allocation to implantation sites was randomized where feasible. Histological and immunohistochemical images were acquired and analyzed using consistent criteria, and blinded analysis was performed where applicable.

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise noted. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed before parametric analysis. If the assumptions for parametric tests were not met, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test was used for multiple-group comparisons. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Preparation and characterization of vessel-derived ECM

The preparation process of VdECM derived from porcine aorta is depicted in Fig. 2A. ECM is composed of a mixture of structural and functional molecules except cellular components, which were separated from the dense tissue of the porcine aorta by cutting and slicing the samples into 5 × 5 mm pieces. H&E staining revealed that before processing, a large number of nuclei were stained deep blue, with clear bright red collagen fibers appearing as a parallel arrangement of waves. After treatment with 0.3% SDS and 3% Triton X-100, elastic and collagen fibers were arranged into a mesh, essential to maintain the original form and structure. H&E staining revealed that most of the cell nuclei (blue dots) were removed from the vessel tissue, while DAPI staining revealed fewer cell nuclei (blue dots) (Fig. 2B). To further quantitatively verify the decellularization efficiency, residual DNA content was measured in native and decellularized vessel samples. As shown in Fig. 2D, the DNA content was significantly decreased from 0.268 ± 0.039 µg/mg tissue in native vascular tissue to 0.044 ± 0.008 µg/mg tissue in VdECM, corresponding to an approximately 83.74% reduction after decellularization. These results quantitatively confirmed the effective removal of cellular and nuclear components from the porcine aortic tissue, supporting the successful preparation of decellularized VdECM. Additionally, even after removing the nuclei from the vessel tissue, the decellularized vessel pieces were still too compact to dissolve in the electrospinning solvent. Consequently, the pieces were milled into powder and stirred in pepsin with dilute hydrochloric acid over 48 h using “Freytes method” to obtain looser structure and solubilize a substantial portion (up to 99%) of acid-insoluble collagen (Fig. 2C). The microstructure of the decellularized powder and lyophilized VdECM was observed using SEM, revealing that compared with granular decellularized powder with a rough surface, the microstructure of digested VdECM appeared to be a multilayer sheet with a smooth surface. Residual detergent was further evaluated to assess the biosafety of VdECM after SDS-based decellularization. Since 0.3% SDS was used during the decellularization process, SDS residue was detected using a methylene blue colorimetric assay. As shown in Fig. 2E, the absorbance value of the VdECM group was 0.095 ± 0.009 a.u., which was comparable to that of the 0% SDS control group (0.103 ± 0.026 a.u.) and showed no significant difference. In contrast, the 0.3% SDS positive control exhibited a markedly higher absorbance value of 1.215 ± 0.111 a.u., which was significantly higher than both the 0% SDS and VdECM groups. These results indicate that residual SDS in VdECM was substantially reduced after washing and was close to the background level of the SDS-free control, supporting the effective removal of detergent residues from the decellularized vascular matrix.

The preservation of major ECM components was further evaluated by wet tissue weight-normalized biochemical quantification and immunohistochemical staining. Hydroxyproline content was measured as an indicator of collagen content and expressed as µg/g wet tissue. As shown in Fig. 2F, the hydroxyproline content was 163.55 ± 11.20 µg/g wet tissue in native vascular tissue and 112.57 ± 14.82 µg/g wet tissue in VdECM, corresponding to an approximately 68.83% retention after decellularization.Elastin content was quantified by ELISA and normalized to wet tissue weight. The elastin content decreased from 73.80 ± 12.52 ng/g wet tissue in native tissue to 33.18 ± 5.60 ng/g wet tissue in VdECM, corresponding to approximately 44.96% retention (Fig. 2G). In contrast, GAG content was largely preserved after decellularization. The GAG content was 388.53 ± 15.22 µg/g wet tissue in native tissue and 374.89 ± 10.94 µg/g wet tissue in VdECM, corresponding to approximately 96.50% retention, with no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. 2H).

In addition to these wet tissue weight-normalized biochemical assays, immunohistochemical staining was performed to further confirm the retention of representative ECM proteins. As shown in Fig. 2I, positive staining for collagen I, collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin was observed in VdECM, indicating that multiple structural and bioactive ECM components were retained after decellularization. Together, these results indicate that although partial loss of collagen- and elastin-associated components occurred during decellularization, substantial ECM components, particularly GAGs and representative matrix proteins, were preserved in VdECM.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the decellularization process effectively removed cellular components and reduced residual SDS to a background level while preserving substantial amounts of major ECM components, including collagen-associated matrix, GAGs, and representative structural and bioactive proteins. These findings support the successful preparation of VdECM with improved material validation and provide a bioactive matrix component for subsequent electrospinning and bioink construction.

Fabrication and characterization of vessel-derived ECM/PCL composite nanofiber membranes

To improve the ability of electrospun, PCL was added to VdECM to construct the spinning system. The nanofiber membranes were created by electrospinning, according to the solution proportion described in the Method section. The morphology observed by SEM revealed that fibers in single PCL electrospun membranes appeared thinner under the same electrospinning conditions, while fiber fusion increased, resulting in droplet formation. Conversely, the electrospun membrane of PCL/VdECM exhibited uniform fibers with a larger diameter (Fig. 3A). The superior hydrophilicity of the PCL/VdECM electrospun membrane is illustrated in Fig. 3B. The nanofiber diameter analysis revealed that the average fiber diameter of the PCL membrane was 55.88 ± 13.49 nm based on 100 fibers, mostly ranging from 20 to 90 nm. The average fiber diameter of the PCL/VdECM membrane was 98.34 ± 12.08 nm based on 100 fibers, mostly ranging from 70 to 130 nm (Fig. 3C, D). A statistically significant difference was observed between the two, indicating that the incorporation of VdECM increased the diameter of the PCL fibrous membrane.

Since the membranes are intended to be used in a body-fluid environment, their mechanical properties were assessed in a wet state. The mechanical properties of the electrospun membranes are depicted in Fig. 3E–H. The Young’s modulus of the PCL/VdECM membrane (4.0070 ± 2.2080 MPa) was significantly higher than that of the PCL membrane (0.3953 ± 0.0758 MPa), indicating greater hardness and resistance to deformation under stress. Moreover, the ultimate tensile strength of the PCL/VdECM membrane was approximately six times higher than that of the PCL membrane (2.3770 ± 0.8966 MPa versus 0.3735 ± 0.0590 MPa), indicating that VdECM blended with PCL increased the mechanical strength of the electrospun membrane. The strain at break for PCL/VdECM and PCL membranes was approximately 60% (51.13% ± 3.40% and 66.16% ± 26.45%), indicating no difference in elongation.

Biocompatibility of the membranes in vitro

To evaluate the biocompatibility of the membranes, HDFs were seeded onto PCL and PCL/VdECM membranes. After culturing for 1,4, and 7 d, a Live/Dead cell assay was used to evaluate cell viability (Fig. 3I). One day after seeding with HDFs, no obvious differences were observed between PCL and PCL/VdECM membranes, and the cells grew well on both membranes. The density of live cells was significantly higher on PCL/VdECM membranes than on PCL membranes after three and seven days. The cells demonstrated a higher proliferation on PCL/VdECM membranes than on the PCL membranes. Statistical analysis of cell counts at 100× magnification (Fig. 3J) revealed that the number of cells on PCL/VdECM membranes was approximately double that on PCL membranes, indicating a faster proliferation rate. Furthermore, cells on PCL membranes appeared round and isolated, with minimal connections. A few elongated cells can be observed on the PCL/VdECM membrane on day 1. The length of the pseudopodia and the spreading of the cells gradually increased after four days, with most cells exhibiting elongated spindles, mutual connections, and better fusion.

Cell proliferation assays revealed that PCL/VdECM membranes supported significantly higher cell proliferation than PCL-only membranes, indicating enhanced biological activity of PCL/VdECM membranes.

Fabrication and characterization of Gel and Gel-F composite ink

The average diameter of the PCL/VdECM membrane was 93.2 ± 17.0 nm. The membrane was cut into segments and homogenized via a homogenizer to obtain PCL/VdECM short fibers. These randomly dispersed short fibers were added to GelMA solution to fabricate composite inks of 5% GelMA (Gel), 5% GelMA + 1.0% Fiber (Gel-F1), 5% GelMA + 1.5% Fiber (Gel-F1.5), and 5% GelMA + 2.0% Fiber (Gel-F2). The incorporation of short fiber components contributed to a homogeneous milky white appearance after photo crosslinking, which gradually deepened with increasing nanofiber concentration (Fig. 4A). Static compression tests revealed that both Young’s modulus and compressive modulus increased after incorporating 1% and 1.5% PCL/VdECM short fibers (Fig. 4B). The compressive modulus was calculated using the first 2%–10% of the straight line of the stress-strain curve. The results revealed that compared to the 7.023 ± 1.502 KPa of the Gel scaffold, the compressive modulus of Gel-F1 (12.45 ± 2.129 KPa) and Gel-F1.5 (10.74 ± 0.6791 KPa)was significantly higher (**p < 0.001, *p < 0.05; Fig. 4C). Notably, the compressive modulus decreased as the nanofiber concentration increased to 1.5%–2%, which could be due to insufficient crosslinking of the GelMA because of being filled with nanofibers inside GelMA.

Furthermore, cyclic loading-unloading tests up to 10 cycles in wet conditions confirmed the elastic recovery capability of these porous scaffolds by reflecting the energy dissipation capacity based on the formation of a closed hysteresis loop. The energy dissipation of the first cycle was 3.32% (Gel), 20.91% (Gel-F1), 21.47% (Gel-F1.5), and 20.95% (Gel-F2). The energy dissipation for the first cycle significantly increased with the incorporation of short fibers, more than six times that of the Gel. Later in the loop, Gel demonstrated minimal energy loss, while Gel-F1/1.5/2 composite scaffold demonstrated no significant effect on the energy loss during each cycle. By the 10th cycle, the average energy dissipation was 0.34 ± 0.19% (Gel), 12.95 ± 0.29% (Gel-F1), 13.90 ± 0.26% (Gel-F1), and 14.03 ± 0.19% (Gel-F2) (Fig. 4D, E).

SEM profiles (Fig. 4F) revealed that the Gel scaffold exhibited a loose, highly porous structure. After zooming in, the inner face appeared smooth with slight wrinkles, while Gel-F scaffolds exhibited a thicker composition and a rougher inner surface than Gel scaffolds, revealing fiber incorporation consistent with the general observation.

The length distribution, aspect ratio, and dispersion quality of short fibers within GelMA were further characterized. Quantitative analysis showed that the short fibers had an average length of 406.27 ± 167.19 μm, with most fibers distributed within the range of approximately 200–550 μm. The aspect ratio, calculated as the ratio of fiber width to fiber length, was 0.090 ± 0.040, and the distribution was mainly concentrated around 0.05–0.10 (Fig. 4G, H). These results indicate that the fragmented fibers maintained an elongated morphology, which is favorable for shear-responsive orientation during extrusion. Short fibers were dispersed throughout the GelMA matrix without obvious large-scale aggregation. The relatively uniform dispersion of short fibers within GelMA is important for maintaining bioink homogeneity, stable extrusion behavior, and reproducible fiber-reinforced microstructure formation. Together, these results provide quantitative structural information for interpreting the shear-associated alignment behavior of short fibers during coaxial printing.

Rheological analyses were conducted to evaluate the viscosity, thermosensitivity, and photosensitivity. Gel-F and Gel precursor solutions exhibited shear-thinning behavior, as evidenced by decreasing viscosity with increasing shear rates (Fig. 4I, J), which is essential for extrusion-based bioprinting. Gel-F and Gel demonstrated rapid crosslinking within 100 s under 405 nm UV irradiation (Fig. 4K), while their precursor solutions underwent gelation at approximately 18 °C (Fig. 4L). These results indicate that fiber incorporation does not compromise the photosensitivity or thermosensitivity of GelMA.

Cell viability and morphology in Gel and Gel-F composite hydrogels

Live/dead staining at days 1, 3, and 7 post-seeding revealed that fibroblasts exhibited proliferative behavior in Gel-F bioink, with few dead cells observed (Fig. 5A). Conversely, cells in pure Gel exhibited almost no proliferation. Quantitative analysis demonstrated significantly higher relative cell density in Gel-F compared to Gel at all time points (Fig. 5B). Phalloidin/DAPI staining revealed rounded and small cell morphology in Gel, whereas Gel-F promoted cell spreading with characteristic spindle-shaped morphology, pseudopodia extension, and intercellular connections by days 3–7 (Fig. 5D). Statistical analysis of relative cell area confirmed enhanced cell spreading in Gel-F composite bioink (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrate the key role of PCL/VdECM fibers in cell proliferation and maturation.

3D coaxial printing and characterization of the bio-inspired vessel with micro-nano structure

The Gel-F composite ink exhibited temperature-dependent phase transitions, remaining liquid above 23 °C and transitioning to an over-gelation phase below 21 °C. The optimal printable phase was achieved precisely at 22 °C (Fig. 6B). After printing at 22 °C and UV crosslinking, the bio-inspired vessel appeared colorless and transparent with a smooth surface (Gel). White fibers dispersing in transparent GelMA were observed in the biomimic vessels after fiber introduction. With increasing nanofiber content, the appearance of bionic blood vessels became uniformly white, and the color gradually deepened as expected. The side view revealed that the blue vessel lumen was smooth, with a uniform diameter (Fig. 6C).

The statistical results of the cross-sectional dimensions of biomimetic blood vessels containing different concentrations of nanofibers revealed distinct changes in lumen diameter, outer diameter, and wall thickness. The lumen diameter was 1770.00 ± 50.89 μm in the Gel group, 1620.00 ± 32.71 μm in the Gel-F1 group, 1550.00 ± 22.73 μm in the Gel-F1.5 group, and 1720.00 ± 41.35 μm in the Gel-F2 group. The outer diameter was 4551.04 ± 127.42 μm (Gel), 3975.90 ± 220.11 μm (Gel-F1), 3707.74 ± 206.71 μm (Gel-F1.5), and 4353.51 ± 78.35 μm (Gel-F2). The corresponding wall thickness was 1390.00 ± 41.10 μm, 1180.00 ± 31.34 μm, 1075.00 ± 20.62 μm, and 1315.00 ± 35.25 μm, respectively (Fig. 6D). These results indicate that fiber incorporation influenced the final cross-sectional geometry of the printed vessels, including lumen size and wall thickness. Among these groups, Gel-F1 showed relatively favorable printing fidelity, with the outer diameter remaining close to the preset coaxial printing dimension while maintaining a continuous lumen structure. With the increase in fiber content in the composite bioink, the fiber coverage rate of the bionic vascular wall increased from 40.68% (Gel-F1) to 81.13% (Gel-F1.5). When the nanofiber concentration was increased to 2%, the wall of the bio-inspired vessel was almost completely covered with PCL/VdECM nanofibers (Fig. 6E).

The analysis of the diameter of the bionic vessels during water absorption revealed that the Gel vessels exhibited rapid water absorption and expansion within the first 5 min, followed by a gradual increase for the next 55 min. The diameter increased significantly from 3.914 ± 0.210 mm (after lyophilization) to 4.788 ± 0.140 mm (5 min), an increase of 22%. The diameter ultimately stabilized at 5.057 ± 0.102 mm (129% of the initial value before water absorption). Water absorption of the Gel-F1 vessels was similar to that of Gel vessels; the diameter increased from 3.914 ± 0.210 mm (after lyophilization) to 4.192 ± 0.283 mm (5 min), and settled at 4.826 ± 0.245 mm, 128% greater than before (Fig. 6G). To assess the water absorption ability of the bio-inspired vessels, the weight of the vessels before and after water absorption was measured. Water absorption rates were 1199 ± 46.21% (Gel), 1054 ± 33.72% (Ge-F1), 995.6 ± 65.6% (Gel-F1.5), and 1011 ± 41.38% (Gel-F2) (Fig. 6F). These results revealed that despite a slight difference in volume after water absorption between Gel and Gel-F vessels, the addition of nanofibers reduced the water absorption rate and ability of the composite bioink.

A three-layer coaxial needle was used to print bionic vessels with double walls and fluorescent particles of different colors, dyeing the fibers with various concentrations to elucidate the double-layer tube wall structure (Fig. 6M). The inner wall with 1% fibers labeled with blue fluorescence exhibited a partially integrated and relatively blurred interface with the outer wall with 1.5% fibers labeled with red fluorescence (Fig. 6N). This result suggests that the coaxial printing strategy could generate bilayer-like tubular constructs with different fiber concentrations, although the layer boundary was not sharply defined due to interfacial integration between hydrogel phases during extrusion and crosslinking. Therefore, the bilayer structure was interpreted mainly as a demonstration of architectural and compositional tunability rather than direct evidence of layer-specific functional advantages. Based on duplex coaxial printing, bio-inspired vessels with adjustable nanofiber concentrations can be fabricated, providing a potential approach for constructing vascular-mimetic tubular structures with spatially varied fiber content.

Arrangement and morphology of nanofibers and cells in the wall of the bionic vessels

Figure 6A depicts the directional organization of fibers and cells after coaxial extrusion, which may be associated with shear forces generated within the printing nozzle. 3D coaxial printing was used to construct the biomimic vascular structure, during which extrusion-related shear stress may facilitate the preferential orientation of nanofibers along the printing direction, thereby forcing the nanofibers in bioink to align along the direction to mimic the micro-nano structure of the natural blood vessels. To evaluate fiber arrangement, a disc-shaped composite bioink sample was fabricated before printing and compared with bionic vessel samples incised and flattened using a laser scanning confocal microscope. The nanofibers were randomly arranged in the composite bioink before printing (Gel-F R), while the thicker fibers were distributed in the direction of the long axis of the vessel in the wall of the bio-inspired vessels (Gel-F SA) (Fig. 6H). Setting the blood vessel long axis to 0°, the angle of the fiber arrangement was statistically analyzed, revealing no centralized tendency, and nanofibers were distributed in all directions, ranging from − 90° to 90° in the bioink sample before printing. However, the alignment angles of vessel nanofibers were concentrated along the long axis of the vessel, ranging within 30°(Fig. 6I). These results suggest that coaxial extrusion contributed to the preferential alignment of nanofibers within the vessel wall, forming a simplified anisotropic fibrous architecture.

Cell-loading printing implies the incorporation of fibers to optimize cell behavior. Moreover, cytomorphology was analyzed, and phalloidin/DAPI staining after culturing for 7 d revealed that human dermal cells in Gel scaffolds distributed diffusely with a small spreading area. Relatively, cells in Gel-F vessel scaffolds spread and elongated well along the fibers, establishing a network connection between cells (Fig. 6J, K), potentially due to short fiber-conferred contact guidance and directionality, as well as the inductive effect of the bioactive CDM on cells. These results revealed that the introduction of VdECM/PCL nanofibers promoted HDF growth and provided a suitable microenvironment for cells. Finite element analysis of the shear stress distribution within the coaxial nozzle revealed a significant increase in shear stress at pipe-diameter constrictions, particularly along the wall boundary (Fig. 6L), suggesting that the nozzle geometry and extrusion process may create a shear environment favorable for fiber orientation. However, this simulation should be interpreted as theoretical support for a possible alignment mechanism rather than direct quantitative validation of fiber movement during extrusion.

Vascular-relevant functional characterization of the printed bionic vessels

To further evaluate whether the printed bionic vessels possessed vascular-relevant functional properties, a series of mechanical and biological assessments were performed, including burst pressure, suture retention strength, compliance, cyclic tensile behavior, axial and circumferential tensile properties, axial and radial compressive resistance, and in vitro endothelialization. These assessments were conducted to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the pressure-bearing capacity, handling-related mechanical stability, deformation behavior, anisotropic mechanical performance, and luminal endothelial compatibility of the printed tubular constructs.

First, burst pressure testing was performed to evaluate the pressure-bearing capacity of the printed bionic vessels. As shown in Fig. 7A, one end of the vessel was connected to a syringe pump, while the other end was connected to a pressure sensor. Deionized water was infused into the lumen at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min until vessel rupture occurred. The burst pressure was recorded as the maximum pressure immediately before the abrupt pressure change caused by rupture. The Gel vessels showed a relatively low burst pressure of 2.32 ± 0.63 kPa. After incorporation of VdECM/PCL short fibers, the burst pressure increased to 4.43 ± 0.24 kPa, 6.03 ± 0.75 kPa, and 6.67 ± 0.66 kPa in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7B). These results indicate that fiber incorporation improved the resistance of the printed tubular constructs to internal pressurization-induced rupture.

Suture retention strength was then evaluated to assess the resistance of the printed vessels to suture pull-out and localized tearing. As shown in Fig. 7C, a single stitch was placed in the vessel wall using a 4 − 0 suture, and the construct was stretched using a universal testing machine until failure occurred. The Gel vessels showed a relatively low suture retention strength of 0.013 ± 0.006 N. After incorporation of VdECM/PCL short fibers, the suture retention strength increased to 0.023 ± 0.012 N, 0.033 ± 0.006 N, and 0.037 ± 0.006 N in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7D). Compared with the Gel group, the Gel-F1.5 and Gel-F2 groups exhibited significantly higher suture retention strength. These results suggest that the embedded short fibers reinforced the vessel wall and improved its resistance to localized suture-induced tearing and pull-out.

Compliance was further measured to evaluate the pressure-induced deformability of the printed vessels. The Gel vessels exhibited the highest compliance, with a value of 9.79 ± 1.92%/100 mmHg. After incorporation of VdECM/PCL short fibers, the compliance decreased to 6.30 ± 1.09%/100 mmHg, 3.98 ± 0.70%/100 mmHg, and 4.89 ± 0.94%/100 mmHg in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7E). Compared with the Gel group, the Gel-F1 group showed a reduced but not statistically significant compliance, whereas the Gel-F1.5 and Gel-F2 groups exhibited significantly lower compliance. These results indicate that fiber incorporation reduced the deformability of the vessel wall under pressure and increased the structural stiffness of the printed tubular constructs. This decrease in compliance is consistent with the enhanced burst pressure and suture retention strength, suggesting that the embedded short fibers reinforced the hydrogel wall and improved its resistance to pressure-induced expansion.

Cyclic tensile testing was then conducted to evaluate the stability of the printed vessels under repeated deformation. The vessels were subjected to 10 consecutive loading–unloading cycles within a strain range of 5–50%, and representative stress–strain curves from the 1st, 5th, and 10th cycles are shown in Fig. 7F. All groups maintained continuous cyclic stress–strain profiles without abrupt mechanical failure during the repeated loading process. Compared with the Gel group, the fiber-containing vessels exhibited higher stress responses under the same strain range, especially in the Gel-F1.5 and Gel-F2 groups. This result indicates that VdECM/PCL short fibers improved the load-bearing capacity of the printed vessels during cyclic deformation.The stress–strain curves showed typical hysteresis behavior during repeated loading–unloading cycles, reflecting the viscoelastic nature of the hydrogel-based tubular constructs. Notably, the fiber-reinforced groups maintained relatively stable mechanical responses across the selected cycles, suggesting that the incorporation of short fibers enhanced the resistance of the vessel wall to repeated tensile deformation. These results further support the reinforcing effect of VdECM/PCL fibers and indicate improved cyclic mechanical stability of the printed bionic vessels.

Axial tensile testing was further conducted to evaluate the longitudinal mechanical behavior of the printed vessels. As shown in Fig. 7G, the vessel samples were stretched along the longitudinal axis until rupture occurred. The representative stress–strain curves showed that fiber-containing vessels withstood higher tensile stress than the pure Gel vessels (Fig. 7H). Quantitative analysis showed that the axial tensile strength increased from 4.53 ± 1.26 kPa in the Gel group to 8.65 ± 0.58 kPa, 13.48 ± 1.26 kPa, and 12.31 ± 2.51 kPa in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7I). The strain at break was 53.99 ± 4.77% in the Gel group and increased to 73.10 ± 3.89%, 86.60 ± 13.64%, and 65.36 ± 22.59% in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively. In addition, the axial tensile modulus increased from 4.69 ± 1.26 kPa in the Gel group to 9.66 ± 0.62 kPa, 12.54 ± 1.70 kPa, and 17.29 ± 2.76 kPa in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7J). These results indicate that VdECM/PCL short fibers enhanced the axial load-bearing capacity and resistance to tensile deformation of the printed vessels.

Circumferential tensile testing was then performed to assess the mechanical behavior of the vessels in the circumferential direction. As shown in Fig. 7K, ring-shaped vessel samples were stretched circumferentially until failure. The representative stress–strain curves showed improved circumferential tensile response in the fiber-containing groups compared with the Gel group (Fig. 7L). The circumferential tensile strength increased from 2.43 ± 0.45 kPa in the Gel group to 4.50 ± 0.84 kPa, 7.75 ± 0.82 kPa, and 6.14 ± 0.52 kPa in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7M). The strain at break was 58.54 ± 5.96% in the Gel group and increased to 80.67 ± 3.68%, 83.85 ± 10.49%, and 78.05 ± 5.36% in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively. The circumferential tensile modulus increased from 3.58 ± 0.62 kPa in the Gel group to 4.57 ± 0.43 kPa, 6.59 ± 0.53 kPa, and 5.92 ± 0.55 kPa in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7N). These results demonstrate that fiber incorporation also improved the circumferential tensile properties of the printed vessels, suggesting enhanced mechanical stability of the tubular wall under direction-dependent tensile loading.

Axial and radial compression tests were further performed to evaluate the resistance of the printed vessels to compressive deformation. For axial compression, the vessel samples were compressed along the longitudinal direction at a rate of 1 mm/min until the strain reached 30% (Fig. 7O). The stress–strain curves showed that the fiber-containing groups generated higher stress responses than the Gel group under the same compressive strain (Fig. 7P). The axial compressive modulus increased from 5.69 ± 1.24 kPa in the Gel group to 9.80 ± 2.12 kPa, 12.88 ± 0.54 kPa, and 16.71 ± 4.91 kPa in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7Q). These results indicate that the incorporation of VdECM/PCL short fibers enhanced the resistance of the printed vessels to axial compression.

Radial compression was then conducted to assess the ability of the tubular constructs to resist transverse compression and radial collapse (Fig. 7R). Because the contact area changed continuously during radial compression of the curved vessel wall, the compressive response was normalized as force per unit sample length. The normalized force–strain curves showed increased radial compressive resistance in the fiber-containing groups compared with the Gel group (Fig. 7S). The radial compressive stiffness increased from 22.03 ± 4.40 N/mm in the Gel group to 30.26 ± 4.55 N/mm, 32.29 ± 9.76 N/mm, and 43.04 ± 8.35 N/mm in the Gel-F1, Gel-F1.5, and Gel-F2 groups, respectively (Fig. 7T). These results suggest that VdECM/PCL short fibers reinforced the vessel wall and improved the structural stability of the printed vessels under both axial and radial compressive loading.

In vitro endothelialization was finally performed to assess whether the luminal surface of the printed vessels could support endothelial cell attachment. GFP-labeled HUVECs were seeded into the lumen of Gel-F1 vessels and cultured for 7 d, followed by confocal observation. As shown in Fig. 7U, GFP-positive cells were observed along the inner surface of the vessel in both the longitudinal and transverse views. In addition, 3D reconstruction of the longitudinal image further confirmed the distribution of endothelial cells on the luminal side of the tubular construct. These results indicate that the inner surface of the printed vessel could support the attachment and maintenance of endothelial cells in vitro, suggesting a favorable luminal microenvironment for endothelialization.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the incorporation of VdECM/PCL short fibers improved the vascular-relevant mechanical properties of the printed bionic vessels and that the luminal surface of the constructs could support endothelial cell attachment in vitro.

Biocompatibility assessment of Gel-F vascular grafts in vivo

The four groups of bioprinted vascular constructs included GelMA (Gel), GelMA with PCL/VdECM electrospun fibers (Gel-F), GelMA with HDFs (Gel-H), and GelMA containing both fibers and HDFs (Gel-F/H). These constructs were subcutaneously implanted in nude mice, and explants were collected at 14 and 28 d for subsequent analyses (Fig. 8A). Macroscopic examination of explanted scaffolds revealed visible host blood vessel infiltration (yellow arrows) in Gel-F and Gel-F/H groups, whereas GelMA and Gel-H constructs exhibited minimal visible vasculature (Fig. 8B). H&E staining revealed differences in host tissue responses: Gel scaffolds demonstrated almost no cellular infiltration and maintained a clear boundary with surrounding tissues, whereas Gel-F scaffolds exhibited obvious cellular penetration accompanied by nascent microvessel formation (yellow arrows; Fig. 8C). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly greater cell penetration depth in Gel-F versus Gel at 14 and 28 d (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, Gel-F/H constructs demonstrated higher cellular density than Gel-H controls (Fig. 8E) with established microvascular networks (Fig. 8C), validating our design hypothesis that fiber reinforcement synergizes with cellular components to promote host integration.

Immunohistochemical analysis of CD31 confirmed enhanced vascularization in the scaffolds, with Gel-F and Gel-F/H groups demonstrating significant host-derived blood vessel ingrowth at 14 and 28 d compared to the avascular Gel group and minimally vascularized Gel-H controls (Fig. 8F). Quantitative assessment revealed statistically higher vessel density in Gel-F versus Gel and Gel-F/H versus Gel-H (Fig. 8G), confirming the vascularization patterns observed in H&E staining. CD68 staining revealed no significant increase in macrophage infiltration (CD68+ cells) in the Gel-F group compared to Gel controls at 14 and 28 d post-implantation. Similarly, Gel-F/H scaffolds and Gel-F constructs demonstrated similar CD68 expression levels (Figs. 8H, I). These results demonstrate that fiber incorporation significantly improves the biocompatibility of GelMA-based scaffolds without eliciting additional inflammation.

Discussion

TEBVs have witnessed significant advancements in recent years [28]. Most studies have focused on adjusting the macroscopic dimensions of blood vessels and the architecture of vascular networks, thereby neglecting the microstructure of the vessel wall [4, 29]. The clinical application of TBEVs is hampered by two major challenges: (i) Difficulty of reproducing the multiscale anisotropic architecture of native vessel adventitia, particularly the axially aligned adventitial fibers essential for mechanical stability and compliance, and (ii) scarcity of manufacturing techniques and bioinks capable of simultaneously controlling microstructural organization of vessel wall and macroscopic vessel integrity. In this study, a hybrid biofabrication strategy has been proposed that integrates electrospinning and coaxial extrusion printing with a printable fiber-reinforced bioink to construct blood vessels with adventitia-like anisotropic architectures. This approach encompasses three levels of innovation: (i) The development of a mechanically robust and biocompatible PCL/VdECM fiber reinforcement bioink, (ii) a hybrid biofabrication process combining electrospinning and coaxial extrusion printing to ensure macroscopic integrity while enabling precise microscale control of the vessel wall architecture, and (iii) the successful reproduction of adventitia-like anisotropic structures that closely mimic the native organization of vascular fibers.

Components of dECM have long been recognized for their intrinsic bioactivity and immune tolerance, providing a favorable microenvironment for tissue remodeling and regeneration [2, 25, 30]. In this study, the reduced nuclear staining and residual DNA content confirmed effective decellularization of porcine aortic tissue, while the SDS residue assay indicated that detergent residues were reduced to a near-background level. These results provide an important safety basis for using VdECM in subsequent fiber fabrication and bioink construction. Meanwhile, biochemical and immunohistochemical analyses showed that key ECM components, including collagen-associated matrix, elastin, GAGs, collagen I, collagen IV, fibronectin, and laminin, were retained to different extents after decellularization. The preservation of these structural and adhesive matrix components may contribute to the improved hydrophilicity, cell affinity, and bioactivity of VdECM/PCL fibers compared with synthetic PCL alone. Therefore, the validated VdECM provides both biochemical cues and matrix-derived functionality for constructing bioactive fiber-reinforced vascular scaffolds. (Fig. 2). Electrospinning technology uses electrostatic forces to spin solutions into nano-scale fibers [31], thereby emulating the nanofibers of ECM inside blood vessel walls.

Combining VdECM and PCL through electrospinning produced uniform nanofibers with improved biocompatibility, hydrophilicity, and mechanical performance compared to PCL alone (Fig. 3). The higher spinnability and mechanical strength can be attributed to the increased solution viscosity and intermolecular interactions introduced by dECM components [32, 33]. Moreover, the improvement in in vitro cell adhesion and proliferation indicates that the incorporation of ECM proteins improved the cell affinity of fibers.

Building upon this, the short electrospun fibers were homogenously dispersed in a GelMA hydrogel to create a composite bioink (Gel-F). Fiber incorporation improved the mechanical capacity of the scaffolds, which can be attributed to the high specific surface area of the nanofibers, which facilitates the formation of sacrificial hydrogen bonds that dissipate stress upon fracture [34]. Superior mechanical strength and compressive resistance are essential for artificial blood vessels to withstand the thousands of repeated impacts of blood flow [35]. Additionally, bioink containing fibers preserved the essential rheological properties, including shear-thinning behavior, thermosensitivity, and photosensitivity (Fig. 4). Moreover, incorporating fibers significantly improved the biological performance of the bioink (Fig. 5) owing to the VdECM-derived bioactive molecules (for instance, structural proteins and glycosaminoglycans) in the nanofibers and optimal mechanical cues for cell support. The limited proliferation observed in pure GelMA may be related to the relatively dense photocrosslinked network formed by 60% methacrylated GelMA and 0.5% LAP-mediated UV crosslinking, as well as the resulting matrix stiffness. Such a hydrogel network may restrict fibroblast spreading and remodeling when cells are encapsulated inside the matrix. In contrast, VdECM/PCL fibers may provide additional bioactive cues and structural guidance, thereby improving cell spreading and proliferation.

In this study, human dermal fibroblasts were used as a representative stromal fibroblast-like cell model to evaluate the general cytocompatibility of the bioink, including cell encapsulation, viability, spreading, and cytoskeletal organization. However, we acknowledge that dermal fibroblasts do not fully represent native vascular adventitial cells. The vascular adventitia contains heterogeneous cell populations, including adventitial fibroblasts, progenitor-like cells, immune-related cells, and perivascular cells. Therefore, the current cell experiments should be interpreted as evidence of general fibroblast compatibility and cell-supportive behavior within the fiber-reinforced GelMA matrix, rather than definitive validation of adventitial cell-specific function. Future studies using primary vascular adventitial fibroblasts or multicellular vascular wall-related co-culture systems will be needed to better evaluate the biological relevance of these constructs for adventitia-mimetic applications.

In summary, we developed a nanofiber-reinforced hydrogel-based bioink with excellent mechanical strength, printability, and biocompatibility.

By integrating electrospinning and coaxial extrusion printing, this study offers a potential strategy to interlink micro-/nano-scale fiber fabrication with macroscale construct assembly [36]. Electrospinning provides ECM-like fibrous architecture, while coaxial printing offers spatial control for producing hollow, multilayered tubular structures in a single step.

Compared to conventional bioprinting, this hybrid approach enables the controlled deposition of fiber-reinforced bioinks, ensuring microstructural anisotropy and macroscopic integrity. During extrusion, the shear forces generated within the nozzle induced partial alignment of fibers and cells along the flow direction, resulting in an adventitia-like anisotropic microstructure that recapitulates a simplified anisotropic fibrous feature inspired by native vascular adventitia. This shear-induced alignment not only improves the structural fidelity of printed vessels but may also provide topographical cues for subsequent cell orientation and remodeling.

Additionally, a three-layer coaxial needle was used to print blood vessels with a bilayer structure. This approach enables adjustment of printing parameters, ink composition, and needle dimensions to achieve various multilayered vascular structures tailored to meet diverse clinical needs, thereby offering substantial potential for clinical applications.

We constructed an adventitia-inspired anisotropic micro-/nano-fibrous architecture. The incorporation of PCL/VdECM nanofibers significantly improved the biological performance of the vessel constructs. In vitro, cells within the fiber-reinforced constructs spread more effectively than those in fiber-free controls, demonstrating the biocompatibility of the composite system. Although the observed cellular alignment was likely induced by shear stress during extrusion rather than by intrinsic fiber guidance, establishing an aligned fibrous architecture remains crucial. Although FEA suggested that the coaxial nozzle generated a shear environment favorable for fiber orientation, it does not provide direct experimental validation of fiber movement during extrusion. Therefore, the proposed shear-induced alignment mechanism should be considered a plausible explanation rather than a quantitatively proven mechanism.

This anisotropic organization captures a simplified but relevant feature of native vascular adventitia, where adventitial collagen fibers contribute to structural integrity and direction-dependent mechanical support, although their native organization is distributed, wavy, and often oblique or helical rather than purely uniaxial. Native vascular adventitia contains a complex collagen network with distributed, wavy, and often oblique or helical orientations rather than a single uniaxially aligned fiber population. Previous quantitative studies have reported multiple adventitial collagen fiber families, including axial, circumferential, and oblique orientations relative to the arterial axis. Therefore, the aligned fibers in our printed constructs should not be interpreted as a complete reproduction of native adventitial collagen architecture. Instead, the present design captures a simplified but relevant adventitia-inspired feature, namely anisotropic micro-/nanoscale fibrous organization that may contribute to directional mechanical reinforcement. Future studies using multi-angle printing paths, helical fiber deposition, or layer-by-layer control of fiber orientation will be required to better reproduce the distributed and helical collagen architecture of native adventitia.

The additional vascular-relevant evaluations further clarified the functional implications of fiber reinforcement in the printed tubular constructs (Fig. 7). Compared with conventional hydrogel compression or membrane-based tests, burst pressure, suture retention, compliance, cyclic tensile behavior, direction-dependent tensile properties, and axial/radial compression more directly reflect the mechanical challenges faced by vascular graft-mimetic scaffolds. The overall improvement in pressure resistance, suture-related stability, cyclic mechanical response, and axial/circumferential load-bearing capacity suggests that VdECM/PCL short fibers contributed to the structural robustness of the GelMA-based vessel wall under multiple loading modes. Meanwhile, the reduced compliance in fiber-containing groups indicates increased wall stiffness, highlighting the need to optimize fiber density to balance reinforcement and elastic deformability. In addition, GFP-HUVEC seeding on the luminal surface provided preliminary evidence that the printed lumen could support endothelial cell attachment in vitro. However, this result should not be interpreted as proof of in vivo endothelialization, hemocompatibility, thrombosis resistance, or long-term patency. Therefore, these data support the vascular graft-mimetic potential of the constructs, while further dynamic perfusion, hemocompatibility, thrombogenicity, and orthotopic implantation studies are still required to validate functional vascular graft performance.

Similarly, although the following subcutaneous implantation model provides valuable information regarding biocompatibility, tissue integration, cellular infiltration, and inflammatory response, it cannot evaluate flow-related performance, including thrombosis, patency, hemodynamic stability, or long-term endothelial function. Therefore, the present constructs are more appropriately described as vascular graft-mimetic or adventitia-inspired tubular scaffolds with improved mechanical and biological properties. Future studies involving dynamic perfusion culture, hemocompatibility assays, thrombogenicity evaluation, long-term patency assessment, and orthotopic vascular implantation will be necessary to fully determine their potential as functional vascular grafts.

In vivo subcutaneous implantation further confirmed the biocompatibility and regenerative potential of these constructs(Fig. 8). After 14 and 28 d, fiber-containing groups exhibited more pronounced cellular infiltration and neovascularization than the fiber-free controls, without eliciting additional immune response. These findings indicate that VdECM components and fibrous topography promote host integration and angiogenesis [37], resembling the adventitial microenvironment where the vasa vasorum facilitates vascularization and tissue regeneration. Similar effects of ECM-derived cues on vascular ingrowth have been documented in other decellularized or composite scaffolds. The enhanced biological performance, combined with structural stability and anisotropy, adds to the potential of this hybrid strategy for vascular tissue engineering.

This study demonstrates a simple but powerful strategy for fabricating biomimetic vascular constructs with micro- and macro-scale control. The proposed hybrid process not only advances the structural biomimicry of native vessels but also offers a generalizable framework for engineering other anisotropic tissues. Many functional tissues, including tendons, cardiac muscles, and peripheral nerves [38], depend on aligned fibrous architectures for mechanical and physiological performance, suggesting the broad applicability of this approach in regenerative medicine.

Conclusion

In this study, a hybrid electrospinning-bioprinting strategy was developed with fiber-reinforced bioinks to fabricate anisotropic vascular constructs. By integrating material innovation, precision manufacturing, and biological validation, this approach successfully replicates the micro-/nano-architecture of native vessel walls. The resulting fiber-reinforced bioink exhibited improved mechanical stability, printability, and biocompatibility, making it a promising material for microstructural vascular tissue engineering. Although the anisotropic vascular constructs demonstrated the feasibility of this method, further characterization is required to elucidate their mechanical and biological performance.
