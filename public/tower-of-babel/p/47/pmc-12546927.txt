Risk factors for refracture by different cement leakage after percutaneous vertebroplasty in patients with thoracolumbar compression fractures
Authors: Abuduwupuer Haibier, Lin Hang, Wuluhan Mahan, Alimujiang Yusufu, Kutiluke Shoukeer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To investigate the risk factors for cement leakage-related recurrent fractures in osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures and analyze their correlation with postoperative recurrent fractures in both operated and adjacent vertebrae following percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP). We retrospectively screened 412 patients who underwent unilateral PVP at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between January 2019 and November 2024. Based on follow-up findings, patients were divided into two groups: (1) fracture group (n = 91) and (2) non-fracture group (n = 321). Univariate, LASSO regression, and multivariate logistic regression analyses were employed to identify risk factors for cement leakage after PVP.The dataset was randomly split into training and test sets (7:3 ratio), and a nomogram prediction model for cement leakage was constructed using multivariate logistic regression results. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, Hosmer–Lemeshow calibration curves, and decision curve analysis. (i) Among the 412 included patients, significant differences were observed between the fracture and non-fracture groups in bone mineral density (BMD), preoperative Cobb angle, cement volume, vertebral cleft sign, fracture-to-surgery interval, and cement leakage (P < 0.05). (ii) LASSO regression identified six predictor variables for cement leakage, which were further analyzed using multivariate logistic regression. The results confirmed that BMD, preoperative Cobb angle, cement volume, vertebral cleft sign, fracture-to-surgery interval, and cement leakage were independent risk factors for recurrent fractures after PVP (P < 0.05). (iii) The ROC curve analysis demonstrated strong predictive performance, with an area under the curve of 0.839 (95% CI 0.780–0.897) for the training set and 0.846 (95% CI 0.759–0.932) for the test set. The Hosmer–Lemeshow calibration curves indicated good fit (training set, P = 0.062; validation set, P = 0.081). Decision curve analysis supported the clinical utility of the nomogram in predicting re-fracture risk. This study highlights multiple risk factors for recurrent fractures after PVP. A nomogram incorporating six key predictors was developed, offering a practical tool for assessing re-fracture risk in PVP patients.

Introduction

Thoracolumbar vertebral compression fractures (TLVCFs) represent one of the most common types of spinal fractures, primarily caused by trauma and osteoporosis in elderly populations1. Studies indicate that 90% of all spinal fractures occur in the thoracolumbar region, with compression fractures accounting for 50% of these cases2. The incidence of thoracolumbar compression fractures exhibits a clear age-related increase, with approximately 140,000 new vertebral fractures occurring annually in the United States alone3. These fractures often lead to complications such as chronic low back pain and kyphosis, which not only significantly impair patients’ quality of life but may also increase mortality rates4,5.Current clinical management strategies for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) depend on fracture severity. Patients with mild compression or minimal symptoms typically receive conservative treatment, while those with more severe compression require surgical intervention. Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) has emerged as the primary surgical treatment, involving cement injection into the compressed vertebra to rapidly alleviate pain and restore spinal function6,7.Despite its widespread use, PVP carries a significant risk of cement leakage, with reported incidence rates ranging from 10 to 65%8–10. In severe cases, leakage may cause nerve root or spinal cord compression, and potentially lead to pulmonary embolism. Numerous domestic and international studies have identified cement leakage as an independent risk factor for secondary fractures in both operated and adjacent vertebrae following PVP11–13. However, to date, few investigations have specifically examined how different types of cement leakage affect postoperative outcomes.

Nomogram prediction models provide a visual and intuitive method for analyzing risk factors, and have been widely used in disease prognosis and survival analysis. However, there is currently no effective predictive model for assessing re-fracture risk following percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP). In this study, we retrospectively analyzed clinical data from patients with thoracolumbar compression fractures treated at our institution over a 5 year period. Patients were categorized into fracture (re-fracture) and non-fracture groups. Using LASSO regression and multivariate logistic regression analyses, we identified significant risk factors for post-PVP re-fracture and subsequently developed and validated a nomogram prediction model. Furthermore, we specifically investigated whether different types of cement leakage constitute an independent risk factor for re-fracture. This research aims to establish a practical clinical tool for predicting and preventing re-fractures after PVP procedures.

Subjects and methods

Design

In this retrospective study, potential risk factors were initially screened using chi-square tests, independent samples t-tests, and rank-sum tests. The identified variables were then subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis. The intersection of risk factors identified through both screening methods was subsequently analyzed using multivariate binary logistic regression to determine independent risk factors. Finally, a nomogram prediction model was constructed and visualized using R software.

Time and location

The study was conducted at the Department of Spine Surgery, Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, with patient enrollment and follow-up completed between January 2019 and November 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

①Radiographically confirmed single-level thoracolumbar compression fracture; ②MRI T2-weighted imaging demonstrating vertebral bone marrow edema with adjacent soft tissue involvement;③Meeting surgical indications for percutaneous vertebroplasty with documented informed consent;④Complete clinical follow-up records.This study received ethical approval from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University Institutional Review Board (No. LFYLLSC20230510-01) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Exclusion criteria

①Vertebral burst fractures;②Patients managed non-operatively;③ Multi-level vertebral fractures; ④Cases with concurrent spinal cord compression or nerve root injury; ⑤Presence of neoplastic, tuberculous, or other pathological conditions in the affected spinal segment;⑥ Pathological fractures secondary to underlying bone disorders; ⑦Incomplete clinical follow-up documentation.

Surgical method

Preoperative preparation

Before vertebroplasty treatment, X-rays, CT, MRI, routine blood tests, liver and kidney function tests, serum bone metabolism indices, and bone density measurements were performed.

PVP surgery method

Patients were placed in the prone position. Under C-arm fluoroscopic guidance, the target vertebral body was identified, routinely disinfected, and draped. Local infiltration anesthesia was administered using 2% lidocaine hydrochloride injection (Tianjin Jinyao Group Hubei Tianyao Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; specification: 5 mL: 0.1 mg; State Drug approval No.: H20133209). A unilateral puncture approach was employed for bone cement placement. With C-arm assistance, the puncture needle was adjusted, and a bone hammer was used to gently advance it into the vertebral pedicle. The needle position was reconfirmed under C-arm guidance and advanced to the anterior third of the vertebral midline. Once correct positioning was confirmed, the stylet was removed, and polymethylmethacrylate bone cement was prepared and loaded into the cement injector. Under C-arm monitoring, 3–6 mL of bone cement was slowly injected into the vertebral body, with continuous observation of its distribution. Upon completion, the puncture needle was withdrawn after cement hardening, and the puncture site was covered with a sterile dressing. Intraoperative X-rays were retained. Patients were returned to the ward via flat cart. Six to eight hours postoperatively, they were permitted to ambulate freely with lumbar support14. Instrument details are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, patients remained bedridden for 6–8 h before ambulation with thoracolumbar brace protection. Calcium tablets (calcium carbonate D3 600 mg/day) and vitamin D were orally administered for 1 year following treatment.For all osteoporosis-diagnosed patients, intravenous zoledronic acid (Yangtze River Pharmaceutical Group Sichuan Hailong Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; specification: 100 mL:5 mg; NMPA approval No.: H20183098) was administered the day after vertebroplasty. Prior to administration, 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride injection (Sichuan Keren Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; specification: 500 mL; NMPA approval No.: H51021158) was intravenously infused for hydration. Patients were instructed to increase fluid intake.All adverse reactions were monitored and recorded, including specific manifestations, onset time and duration. Symptomatic treatments including physical cooling and increased hydration were provided. Oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were administered when body temperature exceeded 38.5 °C.Postoperative X-ray and CT examinations were performed at 1 week to evaluate bone cement distribution and vertebral height restoration.All patients were followed up for 6–12 months.

Observed indicators

The following patient characteristics and clinical parameters were recorded: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, hyperuricaemia, hyperlipidaemia), spinal characteristics (scoliosis, history of spinal surgery), fracture details (location [thoracic/lumbar], compression severity [mild/moderate/severe], presence of vertebral fracture signs), time from fracture to surgery (< 7 days, 7–14 days, > 14 days), cement leakage patterns (peri-trochanteric, intravertebral foraminal, intracanal, paravertebral soft tissue, mixed, intradiscal), bone mineral density, preoperative Cobb’s angle, and volume of bone cement injected.

Statistical methods

All data analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and the R statistical computing environment (version 4.3.2; The Comprehensive R Project for Statistical Computing, https://www.R-project.org/). Normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as (x̄ ± s), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range); categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for quantitative data, and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for qualitative data.The dataset was stratified by refracture status and randomly partitioned into training and validation sets (70:30 ratio) using the create Data Partition function. This function, along with all other R packages mentioned below, was sourced from the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN, https://cran.r-project.org/), the official repository for R packages. The specific code used was: library(caret); set.seed(123); index <—createDataPartition(data$refracture, p = 0.7, list = FALSE); train_data <—data[index, ]; test_data <—data[-index, ]). This utilized version 6.0–94 of the caret package with 1000 resampling iterations. The dataset was randomly divided into training and validation sets (7:3 ratio), with the prediction model developed using the training set and validated on the validation set.Univariate logistic regression initially screened potential re-fracture-related factors by comparing fracture and non-fracture groups. These factors were further refined through LASSO regression (using the glmnet package from CRAN) to identify optimal predictors (lambda.1se), which were subsequently incorporated into multivariate logistic regression to determine independent risk factors for post-vertebroplasty re-fracture. Based on these results, a nomogram was constructed using the rms package (from CRAN). Model performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic curves (pROC package, CRAN), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (rmda and caret packages, CRAN).The specific R package functions used for model assessment were as follows:

① Perform the Hosmer–Lemeshow test using library(ResourceSelection); hoslem.test(model$y, fitted(model), g = 10) to assess calibration goodness-of-fit. (ResourceSelection package from CRAN);② Conduct ROC analysis with library(pROC); roc_curve <—roc(test_data$y, predict(model, newdata = test_data, type = “response”)); plot(roc_curve, print.auc = TRUE) to evaluate discrimination performance and visualize AUC. (pROC package from CRAN);③ Generate a nomogram via library(rms); ddist <—datadist(train_data); options(datadist = “ddist”); full_model <—lrm(y ~ × 1 + × 2 + × 3, data = train_data); nom <—nomogram(full_model, fun = plogis, fun.at = c(0.1,0.5,0.9)); plot(nom) for intuitive model visualization and clinical interpretation. (rms package from CRAN);All packages (caret, glmnet, rms, pROC, ResourceSelection, rmda) were installed and loaded from the official CRAN repository using standard R package management functions (e.g., install.packages()). All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Analysis of the number of participants

A total of 412 patients undergoing unilateral percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) were enrolled in this study. Based on radiographic follow-up assessments for recurrent vertebral fractures (including both operated and adjacent vertebrae), the cohort was stratified into two groups: a fracture group (n = 91) and a non-fracture group (n = 321). Complete follow-up data were available for all participants, with no instances of data loss or patient dropout during the study period. Both groups were included in the final analysis.

Lasso regression results

To address potential multicollinearity among variables, we performed LASSO regression analysis for variable selection and optimization. Using sixfold cross-validation, the optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined to be 0.0124. This analysis identified six significant predictive factors: bone mineral density (BMD), preoperative Cobb angle (Pre-op Cobb), bone cement injection volume, intravertebral cleft (IVC), time from fracture to surgery, and bone cement leakage patterns (Fig. 3).

Construction and validation of the prediction model

The six independent predictors identified by multivariate logistic regression analysis—including bone mineral density, preoperative Cobb angle, cement injection volume, vertebral body cleft sign, fracture-to-surgery interval, and cement leakage patterns—were incorporated into a nomogram prediction model (Fig. 4). This nomogram visually represents the multivariate logistic regression analysis results, allowing for straightforward calculation of individual risk factor scores and total scores. Clinicians can easily determine each independent risk factor’s corresponding score from the nomogram, sum these to obtain a total score, and thereby predict the probability of re-fracture following percutaneous vertebroplasty.

Fig. 4Prediction nomogram of risk factors for refracture after percutaneous vertebroplasty.

Prediction nomogram of risk factors for refracture after percutaneous vertebroplasty.

The predictive performance of the nomogram for assessing re-fracture risk following percutaneous vertebroplasty was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. In the training cohort, the model demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.839 (95% CI 0.780–0.897), with optimal cutoff value of 0.837 (0.789–0.878), sensitivity of 0.898 (0.858–0.937), and specificity of 0.619 (0.499–0.739) (Fig. 5A). Similarly, in the validation cohort, the AUC was 0.846 (95% CI 0.759–0.932), with optimal cutoff of 0.839 (0.762–0.899), sensitivity of 0.906 (0.848–0.965), and specificity of 0.607 (0.426–0.788) (Fig. 5B). The AUC values exceeding 0.8 in both cohorts indicate the nomogram has excellent discriminative ability for predicting post-vertebroplasty re-fractures.The model achieved sensitivities of 0.898–0.906 and specificities of 0.607–0.619 in the training and validation sets, respectively. This indicates its superior ability to identify high-risk patients (low missed-diagnosis rate), but positive predictions require confirmation via imaging due to its moderate specificity.

Discussion

With the aging global population, the incidence of osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures continues to rise annually. Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) has become a common treatment for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures that respond poorly to conservative management, effectively alleviating low back pain and enabling early mobilization. However, postoperative re-fractures of both operated and adjacent vertebrae remain a significant concern17. Meta-analyses report widely varying re-fracture rates (3.21–63%), with most studies following patients for 6–12 months18. Recent prospective studies suggest that achieving cement integration between vertebral endplates may improve outcomes by redistributing mechanical stress19, highlighting the clinical importance of identifying predictive factors for re-fracture. This study specifically investigates risk factors for post-PVP re-fractures, with particular focus on how different cement leakage patterns may influence fracture risk.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the medical records of 412 patients who underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty. Among these, 91 patients experienced re-fracture, yielding a prevalence rate of approximately 22.08%—slightly higher than reported in previous studies20. Univariate analysis identified significant differences (P < 0.05) between the two groups in bone density, preoperative Cobb’s angle, amount of cement injected, degree of bone compression, vertebral body cleft sign, fracture-to-operation time, and cement leakage.LASSO regression analysis was employed to improve model stability by penalizing the coefficients of all regression variables, thereby reducing relatively unimportant independent variables to zero21. The final multifactorial logistic regression analysis identified BMD, preoperative Cobb angle, cement injection volume, vertebral body cleft sign, fracture-to-operation time, and cement leakage as significant risk factors for re-fracture after percutaneous vertebroplasty.Based on these results, we developed a column-line diagram prediction model. The model’s differentiation, accuracy, and clinical applicability were validated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, calibration curve, and decision curve analysis. Results demonstrated that the prediction model exhibited strong differentiation, predictive capability, and clinical applicability in both the training and validation sets. The following discussion examines these risk factors for re-fracture after percutaneous vertebroplasty.

The effect of BMD on refracture

The bone density T-value serves as a crucial indicator for assessing osteoporosis. A lower T-value indicates more severe osteoporosis, increasing the likelihood of trabecular fractures when the spine undergoes external forces after PVP22,23. Studies demonstrate that bone resorption intensifies with advancing age in osteoporotic patients, resulting in reduced bone density and elevated fracture risk24. Additionally, elderly patients often exhibit hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal axis imbalance, particularly postmenopausal women who experience significant estrogen decline. This hormonal change further contributes to vitamin D receptor downregulation, bone metabolism disorders, and increased bone fragility25. Some researchers propose that adjacent vertebral re-fracture after PVP in OVCF patients represents a natural progression of osteoporosis, primarily associated with progressively declining spinal BMD26.In this study, ROC analysis of bone density in both patient groups revealed an optimal diagnostic cutoff value of − 3.168 SD, indicating substantially increased adjacent vertebral fracture risk when bone density falls below this threshold. Wu Weifeng et al.27 similarly determined that a bone density T-value of − 3.185 showed high diagnostic value for post-PVP adjacent vertebral re-fracture, aligning well with our findings. Another study28 observed that low-BMD patients exhibited more necrotic bone tissue in fracture regions and lower bone turnover marker levels, suggesting impaired fracture healing capacity in these individuals. Notably, current osteoporosis guidelines29 recommend implementing preventive measures for patients with BMD T-values below − 2.5.

Effect of preoperative Cobb angle on re-fracture

Cui Wisdom et al.30 demonstrated that an increased Cobb angle contributes to greater spinal instability and slower soft tissue repair, thereby elevating the risk of adjacent vertebral re-fracture after PVP. Similarly, Kan et al.31 found that both an increased Cobb angle and anterior shift of the center of gravity predispose patients to adjacent vertebral re-fracture. Li et al.32 observed more pronounced Cobb angle recovery in the re-fracture group, noting that excessive correction of the Cobb angle paradoxically increases re-fracture risk. This may occur because an excessively large Cobb angle disrupts uniform load distribution within the vertebral body, heightening stress on adjacent vertebrae.Luan Haopeng33 reported that while high vertebral anterior margin recovery rates and Cobb angle correction improve kyphosis and restore satisfactory vertebral biomechanics, they may also compress trabecular bone in non-fractured regions. This compromises trabecular continuity and stability in injured vertebrae, leading to uneven stress distribution and excessive loading on adjacent segments, which increases secondary fracture risk. Consequently, clinicians should prioritize moderate vertebral height restoration over achieving idealized radiographic outcomes.Mao et al.34, in a meta-analysis of post-vertebroplasty secondary fractures, identified vertebral fracture cleavage and Cobb angle as statistically significant predictors of secondary fracture incidence. Liu Qianxiong35 further established that a large Cobb angle is an independent risk factor for post-procedural re-fracture, likely because severe spinal scoliosis (associated with higher Cobb angles) raises both cement leakage risk and subsequent fracture susceptibility.Xiao Jinyu36, in a study of 200 OVCF patients undergoing PVP, found that wedge-shaped vertebral compression fractures alter spinal curvature. During cement injection, excessive pressure—particularly when causing intervertebral disc leakage—can damage disc integrity and impair function, increasing adjacent vertebral re-fracture risk due to altered biomechanics.Our study corroborates these findings: univariate analysis revealed that a larger preoperative Cobb angle significantly increases re-fracture risk, while multivariate logistic regression confirmed its association with adjacent and non-adjacent vertebral re-fractures (P < 0.05), aligning with existing literature.

Effect of cement injection volume on re-fracture

Recent studies have shown that the amount of bone cement injected is not statistically correlated with surgical outcome, but is positively associated with the occurrence of leakage, which is more likely to occur when the bone cement distribution is dense37,38. In this study, we found that the proportion of patients with cement injection of dense type was significantly higher in the re-fracture group than in the no-fracture group, and it was an independent risk factor for postoperative re-fracture of adjacent vertebrae. The main reason for this was analysed, which may be related to the excessive amount of bone cement injected or the overly dense distribution, resulting in bone cement leakage. Bone cement injected into the vertebral body can leak to the intervertebral disc through the upper and lower endplates, and the hardening of the bone cement can significantly increase the intervertebral body stress and change its biomechanics, which can lead to fractures of the adjacent vertebrae39. In a national study40, the optimal diagnostic cut-off value for the amount of bone cement injected to predict the occurrence of adjacent vertebral fracture in a patient after surgery was found to be 3.305 mL by ROC analysis.The restoration of vertebral morphology and stability is dependent on the amount of cement injected, and a small amount of bone cement injected may not provide sufficient support to restore the height of the compressed vertebral body and to correct the posterior convexity. However, over-injection of bone cement can also cause other problems such as cement leakage and spinal nerve compression, and even increase the incidence of re-fracture of adjacent vertebrae. The injection dose of bone cement should take into account the degree of vertebral compression and cortical injury41.

Effect of vertebral fissure features on refracture

Intravertebral vacuum cleft (IVC) is defined as OVCF with ischaemic necrosis of bone and intravertebral instability, which usually requires imaging such as X-ray or CT to confirm the diagnosis.IVC leads to increased stress on the spine in daily life, which increases the risk of vertebral re-fracture42. In addition, IVC may also be related to preoperative preparation and intraoperative manipulation. If the patient is not adequately evaluated and examined preoperatively, IVC may be missed.Meanwhile, if the operation is not performed properly intraoperatively, the vertebral body fissure may be further enlarged, increasing the risk of vertebral body re-fracture43. In this study, we found that the presence of the vertebral body cleft sign is also one of the risk factors for postoperative re-fracture after PVP, which is the same as the results of many previous studies20,44. First, the presence of the vertebral body cleft sign is an imaging manifestation of bone non-healing and osteonecrosis, and there is diseased bone tissue at the edge of the cleft, and these structures severely reduce the strength of the vertebral body, which results in the vertebral body being prone to re-fracture after PVP. Second, the vertebral body cleft sign is related to the distribution of bone cement44–46. The fractured vertebral body cleft is mostly composed of gas or liquid components with a low overall pressure, and when bone cement is injected, it usually collects in the cavity within the vertebral body, resulting in an uneven distribution of bone cement within the vertebral body. It has been shown that the chance of poor contact between the bone cement and the endplate is higher in vertebral bodies accompanied by vertebral body cleft signs than in vertebral bodies not accompanied by vertebral body cleft signs during surgery47. When a fractured vertebra is found to be accompanied by a vertebral body cleft sign, better dispersion of the bone cement should be achieved as much as possible to support the upper and lower endplates and avoid re-fracture by stress.

Effect of time from fracture to surgery on re-fracture

Dong Kai et al.48 demonstrated that both premature and delayed minimally invasive intervention may hinder the natural healing process of fractures, a finding corroborated by Luo Jingling et al.49. Moreover, prolonged fracture healing duration is associated with an increased risk of spinal anterior flexion, osteophyte formation, and refracture. To mitigate vertebral deformation and optimize fracture recovery, clinicians must comprehensively evaluate diagnostic findings to determine the optimal surgical timing50. Dong Kai et al.48 further emphasized that minimally invasive surgery performed 8–21 days after osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) not only enhances functional recovery and accelerates fracture healing but also significantly reduces complications, thereby improving clinical outcomes. Delayed surgical intervention may result in callus or scar tissue formation at the fracture site, complicating reduction procedures, compromising surgical efficacy, and potentially elevating postoperative refracture risk.Particularly for percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP), postoperative refracture is multifactorial, involving low bone density, multiple vertebral fractures, and vertebral height loss. Early surgical intervention—especially during the acute fracture phase—may reduce PVP-related refracture risks. Postoperatively, patients should adhere to standardized anti-osteoporotic regimens, utilize lumbar braces for load reduction, optimize body mass index, and rigorously avoid trauma to minimize refracture probability.

Effect of cement leakage on refracture

The phenomenon of bone cement leakage, which includes disc extravasation, paravertebral extravasation, and epidural leakage, is usually regarded as a common complication of percutaneous vertebroplasty51. Cement leakage increases the vertebral loading stress, especially during vertebral torsion, reducing the biomechanical properties between adjacent vertebrae and predisposing to re-fracture52. Many scholars believe53 that this phenomenon is closely related to postoperative vertebral re-fracture. Although some studies have shown that there is no significant difference in the incidence of adjacent vertebral fracture between the cement leakage group and the non-leakage group in patients who underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty54,55, however, there are also findings that support cement leakage as a potential risk factor for vertebral re-fracture after percutaneous vertebroplasty.Gao et al.56 have pointed out that periarticular, intradural leakage of vertebral body and intravertebral disc cortical leakage are important risk factors for new vertebral compression fractures and recompression fractures, further strengthening the argument for bone cement as a risk factor.ZHOU et al.57 noted that cement leakage is one of the most common complications of percutaneous vertebroplasty, and KOMEMUSHI et al.58 noted that patients with cement leakage had a 4.6-fold increase in the risk of secondary fracture compared with patients who did not have cement leakage, which highlights the importance of effectively controlling cement leakage during the surgical procedure. Similarly, in a previous study, our team found that cement leakage was an independent and significant risk factor for recurrent fracture of the operated vertebra and its adjacent vertebrae in patients with thoracolumbar compression fractures who underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty59. Domestic and international studies have shown56,60,61 that cement leakage is the most common complication of vertebroplasty, among which: periprosthetic leakage is the most common, which usually does not cause symptoms, but some patients can cause pain during muscle contraction. Cement leakage into the intervertebral disc may lead to re-fracture of the adjacent vertebrae18. Bone cement leakage into the spinal canal may lead to neurological dysfunction or even paralysis for those with abnormal volume of the spinal canal or nerve root canal62. Bone cement leakage through the paravertebral venous plexus leads to embolisation of other organs, commonly pulmonary embolism, but also intracardiac embolism and even death63.Objectively speaking, bone cement will go where the pressure is low, so leakage of bone cement is unavoidable. However, the leakage rate of bone cement can be reduced by the following ways64. (1) Preoperative imaging examination should be improved to clarify the type of fracture, and vertebroplasty should be avoided in patients with severe damage to the posterior wall of the vertebral body, as the incidence of cement leakage in such patients is higher than that in other types. Postoperative examinations, including X-ray, CT, and even thin-layer CT scan, should be improved to increase the positive diagnosis rate of bone cement leakage. Bone cement viscosity is a high risk factor for bone cement leakage. Medium- and high-viscosity bone cements have the same efficacy as low-viscosity bone cements and can significantly reduce the incidence of intraoperative bone cement leakage. Read the film carefully before operation, and be prepared for the possible leakage in which direction during operation, when injecting the needle, you can consider to enter into a relatively deeper place, or enter the needle from a relatively intact side; if leakage occurs during operation, you can pause and let the bone cement close for a while, and then continue to inject the bone cement; if you judge that leakage is sure to occur before operation, you can choose to inject it at the time when the cement has a long time to set and the viscosity is high, so that the range of flow will be a little more. In this way the range of flow will be smaller. The surgical method affects the leakage rate of bone cement to a certain extent, and the leakage rate of PKP is lower than that of PVP, but the former has a longer surgical time and is expensive. PKP is recommended for those who are financially supported.Low-viscosity bone cement, when injected into the vertebra, exhibits higher fluidity and is more likely to cause vascular leakage due to blood reflux. High-viscosity bone cement has lower fluidity and is less likely to cause vascular leakage. Clinical studies by Shi Lin et al.65,66 have demonstrated that high-viscosity bone cement can better prevent leakage compared to low-viscosity bone cement, thereby enhancing the safety of percutaneous vertebroplasty.

The nomogram developed in this study demonstrated high sensitivity (0.898–0.906) and moderate specificity (0.607–0.619) in both the training and validation cohorts. It is crucial to emphasize that these performance characteristics indicate the model is better suited for preliminary screening of high-risk populations rather than definitive diagnosis. The high sensitivity minimizes the false-negative rate (missed diagnoses), ensuring that patients at risk of refracture are not overlooked—aligning with the core objective of a screening tool. Meanwhile, the moderate specificity necessitates secondary verification of positive results through diagnostic methods such as imaging examinations to exclude false positives. This design corresponds with the clinical management pathway for osteoporotic vertebral refracture: the screening phase prioritizes identifying all at-risk individuals, followed by radiographic confirmation and intervention.The high sensitivity suggests this model is suitable for the preliminary screening of high-risk patients (e.g., prioritizing postoperative follow-up). However, the moderate specificity necessitates further confirmation of positive predictions using CT or MRI. This aligns with the logical clinical management pathway: non-invasive screening first, followed by radiographic confirmation.

Limitations of this study

(i) Study design bias:This retrospective case study is inherently susceptible to selection bias. Furthermore, the inability to perform radiological screening on all patients with thoracolumbar compression fractures may have influenced the study outcomes. (ii) Medication data limitations: Due to the extended timeframe of medical record review and patient self-adjustment of anti-osteoporosis medication (dosage or regimen), precise data on treatment duration, cumulative dosage, and average medication use were unavailable. Future studies should implement rigorous, real-time documentation of dosage adjustments to improve accuracy. (iii) Limited generalizability: The single-center origin of the study sample may restrict the external validity of the findings. Multicenter, large-scale studies are warranted to enhance the robustness and applicability of the results. (iv) Validation constraints: While internal validation was performed, the absence of external datasets limits the assessment of the nomograms’ predictive performance. Independent cohort validation is needed to confirm their clinical utility. (v) Heterogeneity in fracture analysis: The combined evaluation of secondary phalangeal fractures and adjacent vertebral fractures may obscure distinct pathomechanisms. Additionally, the lack of stratified analysis—particularly regarding cement leakage subtypes (e.g., intervertebral disc leakage) and their association with specific fracture types—represents a critical knowledge gap. (vi) This study did not distinguish between surgical vertebral re-fracture and adjacent vertebral fracture. The existing evidence suggests that the two may have different mechanisms: the former is related to local stability damage caused by cement leakage, while the latter is more closely related to the overall biomechanical changes of the spine. This study did not establish an independent predictive model to distinguish between surgical vertebral fracture and adjacent vertebral refracture, mainly due to the limited sample size. It is suggested that a future multi-center study should be conducted to verify the heterogeneous mechanism of these two types of fractures.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that postoperative re-fracture following percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) is influenced by multiple risk factors. Based on these factors, a nomogram prediction model incorporating six key variables was developed to quantify individualized re-fracture risk in PVP patients. This tool may assist clinicians in stratifying high-risk populations and optimizing postoperative management strategies.
