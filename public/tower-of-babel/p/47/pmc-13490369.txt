Dual-physics programmable metasurfaces for dynamical controls of acoustics and electromagnetics
Authors: Zheng Zhang, Han Wei Tian, Chao Song, Qian Zhu, Lin Bai, Xin Ge Zhang, Tie Jun Cui, Wei Xiang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProgrammable metasurfaces bring new opportunities for modern acoustic and electromagnetic technologies by dynamically tailoring wave-matter interactions at subwavelength scales. Due to differences in physical properties and tuning mechanisms of acoustic and electromagnetic waves, current programmable metasurfaces typically manipulate only one type of wave, while concurrent and programmable controls of both waves remain elusive. Here, we propose a dual-physics programmable metasurface capable of simultaneous, independent and dynamic manipulations of acoustic and electromagnetic waves. The presented metasurface consists of 324 micromotor-driven elements that are individually addressable and wirelessly controlled. Each element can achieve 1-bit reflection phase modulation of both acoustic and electromagnetic waves by dynamic geometric rotation, allowing the metasurface to realize on-demand and decoupled reconfiguration of acoustic and electromagnetic functionalities. The intriguing features of the metasurface are demonstrated by simultaneously achieving local audio enhancement and radio-frequency relay communications, as well as acoustic-electromagnetic dual-channel information camouflage. This work offers significant promise for future metasurface-based multi-physics wireless communications and information security.Subject terms: Metamaterials, Metamaterials

Introduction

Precise and flexible manipulation of acoustic or electromagnetic (EM) waves has long served as a pivotal endeavor, driving many advances in various practical applications ranging from imaging to communications and sensing1–3. On this basis, achieving concurrent controls over both waves can better leverage their physical strengths and further enhance the overall system performance. For example, acoustic-EM multi-modal imaging combines the merits of acoustic deep penetration with EM high sensitivity4–6. Such dual-physics collaborative imaging technologies allow a more comprehensive characterization of the target information, improving the accuracy and reliability of clinical treatments. Additionally, benefitting from the low attenuation of acoustic waves under water and high transmission rate of EM waves in the air7–9, acoustic-EM hybrid communication technologies can facilitate seamless connectivity and efficient information transmission across different media.

At present, several typical technologies have been employed in manipulating acoustic or EM waves. For example, lens can achieve different accumulated phases by designing its internal propagation paths10, thereby realizing a variety of interesting structured acoustic or EM fields, such as vortex waves11, Airy beams12, and focused waves13. In addition, phased arrays can achieve dynamic and flexible acoustic or EM beamforming by independently controlling the amplitude, phase, and other responses of each array element14,15, playing a crucial role in applications such as wireless communications and object detection16,17. Despite these advancements, most of traditional wavefront manipulation technologies can only respond to single acoustic or EM waves, making it difficult to meet the demands for multi-physics collaborative controls in some complex application scenarios. As a result, the current acoustic-EM dual-physics systems are generally constructed by utilizing discrete acoustic and EM devices, suffering from high complexity, low efficiency, poor flexibility, and poor programmability. In view of this, it is of critical importance to develop a simple and highly integrated hardware architecture that can manipulate both acoustic and EM waves simultaneously, independently, and dynamically.

Programmable metasurfaces are two-dimensional (2D) artificial array structures composed of subwavelength active elements18–30. They can dynamically and flexibly adjust various basic physical properties of acoustic or EM waves (e.g., phase18–26, amplitude27,28, and polarization29,30), providing an innovation approach to construct advanced wavefront manipulation platforms with low cost, high degrees of freedom, and easy integration. In the EM domain, the programmable metasurfaces can dynamically control EM functionalities on demand by changing the operating states of integrated electronic components (such as switches and varactor diodes), enabling cutting-edge applications such as information encryption24, target tracking25, omnidirectional communications26, and others27–30. While in the acoustic domain, the programmable metasurfaces can adjust acoustic functionalities in real time by changing their geometric configurations by using mechatronic devices (such as micromotors and piezoelectric sheets), successfully applied in indoor reverberation improvement18, sound channel optimization19, full-space acoustic imaging20, and so on21–23. Owing to the complementary advantage of acoustic and EM waves in many practical applications, developing shared-aperture programmable metasurfaces capable of manipulating both waves are highly desired. Although several previous attempts on metasurfaces have recently been made to achieve both acoustic and EM wave manipulations31–33, the static geometries restrict their application potentials in complex and ever-changing environments with programmable capabilities. Given the fundamental differences in physical properties, wave-matter interaction mechanisms, and tuning methods between the acoustic and EM waves34, programmable metasurfaces capable of simultaneously, independently and dynamically modulating both waves remain elusive.

Here, we report a micromotor-driven reflection-type programmable metasurface that enables independent and dynamic manipulations of acoustic and EM waves at the same time. This acoustic-EM dual-physics programmable metasurface (DPPM) consists of 324 individually rotatable bottle-like elements. Each element contains four well-designed acoustic resonant cavities in its bottle body, and its top cap is driven by a single-actuated micromotor. The top cap can be dynamically rotated to select any acoustic resonant cavity, thereby providing a desired acoustic reflection resonance phase. In addition, specific EM reflection geometric phases can be achieved through the rotation operation. By introducing a joint coding method, each element can achieve simultaneous and decoupled modulations of 1-bit acoustic phase and 1-bit EM phase by using a single micromotor. To illustrate the dual-physics programmable capabilities of the DPPM, a hybrid acoustic-EM modulation system is constructed using the DPPM in an indoor environment, which can achieve local audio enhancement and radio-frequency (RF) relay communications simultaneously, as well as realize dual-physics-enabled dual-channel information camouflage. This DPPM opens up a high-efficiency and cost-effective route for dual-physics concurrent wave manipulations, holding great application potential in the next-generation multi-tasking and secure wireless communications, and beyond.

Results

Principle of the dual-physics programmable metasurface

The principle of the proposed acoustic-EM DPPM is illustrated in Fig. 1. Different from the conventional programmable metasurfaces, DPPM can modulate its acoustic and EM phase responses dynamically and independently through the same geometric rotation, thus realizing specific acoustic and EM functionalities as needed. Moreover, DPPM can receive the phase coding matrices transmitted by a wireless signal transmitter, thereby allowing both acoustic and EM manipulations in a non-contact way. Benefitting from these outstanding features, DPPM is of great application potential in parallel controls of both acoustic and EM signals. For example, DPPM can be deployed in an exhibition hall to focus acoustic message at designated positions, allowing only visitors within the focal zones to hear the audio. At the same time, DPPM can also function as a wireless relay to redirect RF signals for non-line-of-sight (NLOS) wireless transmissions. More importantly, each element of DPPM can be addressed individually to enable the flexible customization of acoustic focal zones and reflected EM beams, thereby providing an innovative approach to reconfigure and reprogram both acoustic and EM environments.

Design and realization of the dual-physics programmable metasurface

To implement DPPM, three key points should be taken into consideration. First, the designed metasurface should be responsive to both incident acoustic and EM waves. Second, it should be able to manipulate the reflection behaviors of both acoustic and EM waves dynamically. Third, the manipulation processes of the metasurface for acoustic and EM waves should not interfere with each other. Herein, we adopt a heterogeneous strategy and incorporate some important technologies to achieve these goals simultaneously. The technologies include acoustic resonance, EM Pancharatnam-Berry (PB) phase, shared-aperture integration, and digital coding representation.

As depicted in the enlarged view of Fig. 1, each element of the DPPM features a bottle-like geometry. The bottle body is made of photosensitive resin and contains four identical bridge-shaped closed-ended cavities arranged in a criss-cross shape. The top cap is made of F4B substrate and contains a bridge-shaped hole that shares the same area as that of four cavities. The cap is driven by a single-actuated micromotor embedded in the bottle body, enabling rapid and precise rotation. Since both photosensitive resin and F4B substrate can be viewed as acoustically rigid walls, the incident acoustic waves can propagate into a certain cavity when the bridge-shaped hole aligns with it. In this case, the inner air channel of the cavity causes a change in the propagation velocity of the acoustic waves, which in turn changes the acoustic reflection phase. Therefore, by elaborately designing the air channel length of each cavity, the desired acoustic reflection phase can be modulated dynamically as the cap rotates. On this basis, we further etch a C-shaped copper ring on the top cap. When the C-shaped copper ring is rotated with the cap by an angle of α, the incident circularly polarized EM waves will be reflected with the same helicity, and the reflected waves carry a PB phase of ±2α35. To enhance the EM reflection efficiency, we set an ultrathin copper sheet at the bottom of the bottle body. This copper sheet can also prevent the direct-current signal generated by the backend micromotor circuit board from deteriorating the reflection EM response. Therefore, this design method and control mechanism enable simultaneous and dynamic modulations of both acoustic resonant phase and EM geometric phase.

To further achieve the decoupled phase modulation of both waves, we introduce a joint coding method that encodes different rotational states of the DPPM element36,37. Specifically, each element comprises a total of four selective coding states, namely “0/0”, “1/0”, “0/1”, and “1/1”. The binary codes before and after the slash symbol correspond to the phase codes of EM and acoustic waves, respectively, where the binary digits “0” and “1” refer to two opposite reflection phase states with a phase difference of 180°. Fig. 2a illustrates the geometric configurations of the DPPM element corresponding to four different coding states. When the cap hole is aligned with the right cavity, the element works in the state “0/0”. As the cap is rotated counterclockwise by 90°, the cap hole is rotated accordingly and aligns with the upper cavity. Since the right and the upper cavities share the identical air channel length, the acoustic reflection phase remains unchanged. On the other hand, the C-shaped copper ring is also rotated counterclockwise by 90°, resulting in a phase shift of 180° for the EM reflection phase. In this case, the element turns to work in the state “1/0”. Furthermore, when the cap hole is turned to align with the left or the lower cavity, the acoustic reflection phase changes by 180° relative to the above two states. Meantime, two opposite EM reflection phases can also be obtained, thereby constructing two states “0/1” and “1/1”. With this joint coding method, each DPPM element can achieve 1-bit phase modulations for both EM and acoustic waves simultaneously and independently.

To demonstrate the above design, we conduct extensive parametric analysis in full-wave numerical simulations (see “Methods” for more details on simulation setup), considering the subwavelength characteristic, fabrication feasibility and ease of tunability. The optimized geometry of the DPPM element is depicted in Fig. 2b, and the key parameters are summarized as follows: the radius and thickness of the cap are r = 13.5 mm and t = 2 mm, respectively; the side length and height of the cuboid are a = 28 mm and d = 14 mm, respectively; the air channel lengths of four cavities are h0 = 11.5 mm and h1 = 9.5 mm, respectively; the width of the C-shaped copper ring is 0.2 mm, and the opening angle is 80°; the arc length and the opening angle of the bridge-shaped hole is 16.7 mm and 80°, respectively.

Figure 2c shows the simulated acoustic reflection responses under normal incidence. In the frequency range from 5.55 to 6.15 kHz, a phase difference of approximately 180° can be observed between the acoustic coding states “0” and “1”, while the reflection phase responses in these acoustic coding states are not influenced as the EM coding state changes. Besides, the acoustic reflection amplitudes in both coding states are close to 1. To investigate the acoustic wave modulation mechanism better, the pressure field distributions in states “0/0” and “1/1” at 5.8 kHz are simulated and depicted in Fig. 2d. The simulation results show that the majority of the incident acoustic energy is coupled into the cavity aligned with the bridge-shaped hole. This phenomenon is attributed to the resonant effect that is occurred when the incident frequency is close to the intrinsic resonant frequency of the metasurface element, leading to a localization of acoustic energy within the cavity. Therefore, fine-tuning the element geometry customizes the resonant frequency for precise phase control. As a result, the DPPM element can modulate acoustic reflection phases independently and efficiently in a broad bandwidth (a relative bandwidth of 10.3%).

Fig. 2e shows the simulated EM reflection responses under the normally incident left-handed circularly polarized (LCP) waves. When the EM coding states are switched between “0” and “1”, a stable phase shift of 180° can be achieved in a broad bandwidth from 2.3 to 4.7 GHz (a relative bandwidth of 68.6%). However, no matter how the acoustic coding states vary, the EM reflection phase responses in these EM coding states are unchanged. In addition, the EM reflection amplitudes of both coding states exceed 0.86, demonstrating good EM reflection performance. To clarify the mechanism of modulating the EM waves further, the surface current distributions induced on the C-shaped ring at 4 GHz are simulated in states “0/0” and “1/1”, as shown in Fig. 2f. When the C-shaped ring is rotated, the surface current at each point is rotated alongside, thereby generating the refection phase shift relevant to the geometry rotation. It is noteworthy that the opposite surface current distributions are generated on the C-shaped ring when the incident EM waves are right-handed circularly polarized, resulting in an inverse refection phase shift38.

To demonstrate the capability of DPPM further, we design and fabricate a DPPM sample composed of 18 × 18 elements, as shown in Fig. 3a, b. The bottle body array and top caps of the sample are manufactured using three-dimensional printing and printed circuit board technologies respectively, and then assembled together to form a complete entity (see “Methods” for more details on sample fabrication and assembly). To achieve real-time reprogrammability, a two-phase four-wire micromotor is installed inside each element to drive the top cap to rotate. Moreover, a micromotor-control circuit board is designed and integrated at the back of the bottle body array to achieve the remote programming of micromotors. The micromotor-control circuit board contains 36 wireless receiving modules based on nRF24L01 chips and 324 micromotor drivers (TMC2009), each connecting with a micromotor by XH2.54 terminal wires. A wireless signal transmitter based on the nRF24L01 chip is designed to remotely transmit the phase coding matrices loaded in the computer in advance. When the transmitted phase coding matrices are acquired by the micromotor-control circuit board, the rotation states of the micromotors can be adjusted automatically, thereby allowing the DPPM to perform the desired acoustic and EM functionalities. To mitigate excessive heat accumulation within the micromotors during continuous rotation and to prevent data loss between the wireless transceiver modules during command updates, the minimum time interval for the DPPM to respond to one phase coding matrix signal is set to 0.5 seconds (see Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2 for more details on control hardware architecture and software interface).

Local audio enhancement and radio-frequency relay communications

Leveraging the prepared DPPM, we construct a hybrid acoustic-EM modulation system in the indoor environment, aiming at achieving the local audio enhancement and RF relay communications simultaneously, as illustrated in Fig. 3c. For the audio enhancement, DPPM can improve the audio signal intensity at particular locations. For the RF relay communications, DPPM can redirect the RF signals towards the target users, thus supporting NLOS signal transmissions. Due to the real-time programmable capability of DPPM, both audio enhancement zones and RF signal transmission directions can be customized as required, as depicted in Fig. 3d.

Without loss of generality, we select two focal zones respectively located at (70 mm, 98 mm, 252 mm) and (70 mm, 406 mm, 252 mm) in front of the DPPM to demonstrate the local audio enhancement performance. As shown in Fig. 3c, a loudspeaker is used to emit a 5.7 kHz continuous pulse signal. The center of the loudspeaker is located at (192 mm, 252 mm, 252 mm) in front of the DPPM. To receive the real-time acoustic signals reflected by the DPPM, two microphones are placed at the preset focal positions. To achieve dynamic local audio enhancements, six groups of different joint phase coding matrices are applied to the DPPM in succession, as illustrated in Fig. 3e. The time-domain curves of sound pressure level detected by two microphones are recorded in Fig. 3f. We clearly observe that four different acoustic functionalities can be realized, including dual-zone suppression, dual-zone enhancement, and two types of single-zone enhancements. It is noted that the curves shown against a white background arise during the switching process of different coding matrices. In this process, the DPPM elements undergo a brief mechanical rotation, producing a time-varying wavefront. Once the DPPM reaches a steady state, this variation ceases and no longer impacts the intended EM or acoustic functionalities. Compared with the dual-zone suppression, the single-zone and dual-zone enhancements improve the sound pressure level by 20 dB and 16 dB, respectively. These results demonstrate that DPPM can control the indoor acoustic fields precisely, achieving efficient acoustic amplification in the desired spatial areas.

In addition, DPPM can work as a relay to support directional RF signal transmission. A 4 GHz RF signal modulated with quadrature phase shift keying (QPSK) is generated by a universal software radio peripheral (USRP), transmitted by a feeding horn antenna and illuminated onto DPPM. Since the designed DPPM is polarization insensitive40, here we only transmit the QPSK signal in the LCP mode for the following functional verification. The feeding horn antenna is aligned parallel to the speaker and separated by a distance of 120 mm. Additionally, two receiving horn antennas are placed at +30° and –30° with respect to the normal direction of the DPPM and are used to act as two different user terminals, as shown in Fig. 3c. Since the two receiving horn antennas are located at the NLOS regions behind the feeding horn antenna, they can only receive the QPSK signal redirected by the DPPM, rather than the QPSK signal directly transmitted from the feeding horn. By applying the joint phase coding matrices shown in Fig. 3e, the receiving horn 1 consistently receives high-quality QPSK signals, as shown in Fig. 3g. In contrast, the receiving horn 2 can hardly receive a valid QPSK signal, as shown in Fig. 3h. The above experimental results show that the transmitted QPSK signal can be redirected to the specified direction and the performance is not affected when the acoustic functionality changes (see Supplementary Movie 1 for details). To demonstrate the RF signal transmission performance quantitatively, relationships between measured error vector magnitude and signal-to-noise ratio are recorded in Supplementary Fig. S3.

We also validate that the QPSK signal can be transmitted alternately between two different user terminals, while the audio local enhancement effect remains almost unchanged. As shown in Fig. 4a, b, the receiving horn 1 or the receiving horn 2 selectively receives the valid QPSK signal by dynamically controlling the joint phase coding matrices applied to the DPPM. While during this process, the sound pressure levels detected by the two microphones remain relatively stable, indicating that the changes in EM functionalities do not cause noticeable perturbation of the acoustic ones, as shown in Fig. 4c. This process can also be observed visually in Supplementary Movie 2. Additional dual-physics functionalities presented in Supplementary Figs. S4 and S5 further demonstrate that the acoustic and EM wave responses are effectively decoupled.

Acoustic-electromagnetic dual-channel information camouflage

Based on the excellent dual-physics wave manipulation capability of DPPM, we directly map the digital information onto different acoustic wavefronts and EM patterns, and further develop an acoustic-EM physical-layer dual-channel information camouflage scheme. To ensure concealment and security of information during transmission, the original information is first translated into two groups of binary data streams. The two data streams are then transmitted synchronously through two independent physical channels: the acoustic channel and EM channel. In this way, the information transmitted in either channel can be deliberately forged to mislead eavesdroppers without destroying the integrity of the original information. When the transmitted data streams in both channels are received simultaneously and synthesized according to the predefined logical criteria, the true information can be restored.

To this end, we utilize the DPPM to generate the time-varying acoustic wavefronts and EM patterns for transmitting the binary information based on amplitude modulation. In the acoustic channel, the focusing and diverging wavefronts are mapped to the binary symbols “1” and “0”, respectively. Likewise, in the EM channel, the directional beam and diffuse scattering patterns are mapped to the binary symbols “1” and “0”, respectively. In this way, the binary amplitude shift keying (BASK) modulation can be implemented in both acoustic and EM channels. At each time point, DPPM can transmit different BASK symbols simultaneously and independently in both channels, as depicted in Fig. 5a. Furthermore, the valid information is hidden within the BASK data streams transmitted in the two physical channels. Only by simultaneously receiving both data streams and performing a logical AND operation can the hidden information be unlocked.

Demonstration of acoustic-electromagnetic dual-channel information camouflage.

a Schematic diagram of information modulation and concealment based on dual-physics wave modulations. b Variations of acoustic wavefronts and electromagnetic patterns versus the time. c Normalized received acoustic intensity signal and electromagnetic amplitude signal. d Camouflaged and hidden messages.

As a demonstration, a microphone is located at the coordinate (140 mm, 252 mm, 150 mm) to receive the modulated acoustic signal, and a receiving horn is placed in front of the DPPM center to receive the modulated EM signal. Under the simultaneous illuminations of a 5.7 kHz acoustic carrier wave and a 4 GHz LCP EM carrier wave, DPPM modulates the digital information directly onto the reflected acoustic and EM waves and transmits them to the desired directions. Figure 5b shows the variation of both acoustic wavefronts at the focal plane (z = 150 mm) and EM patterns in the u-v coordinate versus time. The normalized acoustic intensity signal and EM amplitude signal received respectively by the microphone and the receiving horn are depicted in Fig. 5c. The received signals are then demodulated into binary symbols via the threshold decision, as depicted in Fig. 5d. When the detected acoustic intensity is higher than the threshold value, the demodulated binary symbol is “1”; otherwise, the demodulated binary symbol is “0”. According to this judgment principle, two sets of bit streams “010000110100111101010111” and “010100100100000101010100” are transmitted through the acoustic channel and the EM channel, respectively. After decoding them based on the American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) format, two camouflaged messages “COW” and “RAT” can be obtained respectively. However, the hidden message “BAT” can only be retrieved by performing a logical AND operation on both camouflaged messages. Notably, the camouflaged messages can be changed arbitrarily while keeping the true information unchanged (see Supplementary Fig. S9 for details). This dual-physics collaborative camouflage technique can not only strengthen the information security but also enhance the anti-interference ability of data transmissions.

Although the aforementioned dual-physics applications are demonstrated at fixed acoustic and EM operating frequencies, the presented DPPM can maintain the stability of acoustic and EM functionalities within a certain frequency bandwidth. Here, we further experimentally verify acoustic focusing and EM beam deflection across a broad bandwidth. According to Eqs. 1 and 2, the position of the acoustic focal point and the EM deflection angle are related to the operating wavelengths. To maintain stable acoustic focal position and EM deflection angle, the acoustic and EM coding patterns of the DPPM should be adjusted dynamically as the operating frequencies change. Under the coding pattern shown in Fig. 6a, the reflected LCP EM waves at 2.3 GHz propagate towards −20°, as shown in Fig. 6b. At the same time, the reflected acoustic waves at 5.55 kHz converge at the focal plane located 252 mm in front of the DPPM, forming a distinguishable focal point at (250 mm, 250 mm), as shown in Fig. 6c. As the coding patterns of the DPPM change to Fig. 6d and then to 6 g, the reflected EM waves stably direct towards −20° at 3.1 and 4.7 GHz, as shown in Fig. 6e, h, respectively. At the same time, the acoustic focal points remain at the original position as the operating frequencies change to 5.7 and 6.15 kHz, as shown in Fig. 6f, i. To further evaluate the acoustic focusing performance, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of focal point is measured. Measured results show that the FWHMs maintain stably at approximately 40 mm under these three typical frequencies. Overall, this DPPM has been demonstrated with excellent broadband performance across dual physical fields, maintaining stable acoustic and EM functionalities within the respective ranges of 5.55–6.15 kHz and 2.3–4.7 GHz.

Discussion

We proposed a DPPM capable of programming both acoustic and EM waves independently and simultaneously. The presented DPPM contains 324 independently addressable elements, each driven by a wirelessly actuated micromotor. The DPPM elements adopt a unified physical mapping framework, which facilitates the decoupled modulations of 1-bit acoustic and 1-bit EM reflection phases via a single geometric rotation operation. To demonstrate the intriguing dual-physics wave manipulation functions of DPPM, an indoor hybrid acoustic-EM modulation system is built to achieve two cutting-edge dual-physics applications: simultaneous local audio enhancements and RF relay communications, and high-security dual-channel information camouflage. The experimental results reveal that DPPM provides a simplified dual-physics platform beyond dynamic wavefront shaping, enabling real-time information transmission and processing in acoustic and EM channels. Although the current 1-bit DPPM exhibits relatively low phase resolution and consequently faces certain limitations in wavefront shaping efficiency and beamforming accuracy, such 1-bit DPPM nonetheless demonstrate effective and reliable performance in many scenarios where strict phase continuity is not necessary19,41,42. It is notable that we also investigate the feasibility of constructing an improved DPPM with more available and fully decoupled acoustic and EM phase responses. For example, we constructed a 3-bit DPPM that enables the simultaneous, independent, and dynamic manipulations of both acoustic and EM waves, and have validated its more precise dual-physics wave modulation capabilities, including high-fidelity holography and high-precision beamforming (see Supplementary Figs. S10–S12 for details).

Compared with previous programmable metasurfaces that are typically focused on manipulating only acoustic or EM waves, DPPM innovates an efficient, low-complexity, low-cost, and easy-to-control method for the concurrent manipulations of both wave types. In particular, the co-aperture scheme of DPPM addresses a key technical limitation inherent in conventional dual-physics devices, which typically necessitate configurations involving the stacking or side-by-side placement of distinct physical-response components. This aperture reuse approach is particularly important for highly integrated multifunctional devices with limited usable area and layout constraints. More importantly, it enables the simultaneous modulations of acoustic and EM waves without relying on external coordination, effectively eliminating delay and mismatch problems and being especially critical for applications requiring strict synchronization between acoustic and EM responses. Therefore, DPPM can unlock the potentials of multi-physics programmable metasurfaces in future multi-tasking and multi-scenario information processing, transmission and security applications.

Methods

Numerical simulations

The commercial softwares COMSOL Multiphysics and CST Microwave Studio are used for full-wave simulations in acoustics and EMs, respectively. In the acoustic simulation, the mass density and sound velocity of air, photosensitive resin, and F4B are set as 1.21 kg/m3 and 343 m/s, 1300 kg/m3 and 716 m/s, and 2200 kg/m3 and 1422 m/s, respectively. Periodic boundary conditions are imposed on the structure to simulate the acoustic responses of the DPPM element. Perfectly matched layers are used to absorb the outgoing waves, thereby reducing the near-field interference between the incident and scattered waves. In the EM simulation, the dielectric constant and loss tangent of the F4B substrate are 2.65 and 0.001, respectively. The dielectric constant and loss tangent of the photosensitive resin are 3 and 0.05, respectively. Unit cell boundary conditions are used to simulate the EM responses of the DPPM element. Open (add space) boundary conditions are applied in all dimensions for far-field pattern simulations.

Sample fabrication and assembly

The top caps of the DPPM are fabricated using computer numerical control milling and printed circuit board technologies. The corners of the bridge-shaped hole are rounded with a radius of curvature of 1 mm. A C-shaped copper split ring with a thickness of 0.035 mm is etched on the F4B substrate and is located 0.2 mm away from the edge of the cap. The top cap is connected to the micromotor through a shaft, which can transmit the torque required for the rotation of the cap. A square hole is located at the bottom of the bottle cuboid, allowing stable insertion of the micromotor. The angular accuracy of each micromotor is ±1°. Because each top cap of the DPPM elements is detachable, the acoustic and EM operating frequencies can be adjusted flexibly by replacing different types of caps (see more details in Supplementary Fig. S13). The copper sheet with a thickness of 0.5 mm is fabricated by laser cutting technology. It contains 18 × 18 square holes used for micromotor circuit wiring. The micromotor control circuit boards are fixed onto the copper sheet using double-sided foam tape. The fabricated DPPM sample is stabilized with foam boards.
