Studies on the conservation and sustainability challenges of the wetland resources of the Baro-Akobo River Basin in Gambella and Benishangul-Gumuz regions, Ethiopia
Authors: Brook Lemma, Binyam Tesfaw, Hayal Desta, Bereket Tessema
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, the water quality of the Baro-Akobo River Basin in Ethiopia was comprehensively assessed. Key parameters including temperature (°C), pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), electrical conductivity, total dissolved solids (TDS), and ion concentrations such as K+, Ca2+, Na+, NO3−, NO2−, PO43−, CO32−, HCO3−, and NH4+were measured using standard methods, alongside sampling of phytoplankton, zooplankton, macroinvertebrates, macrophytes, and fish. Phytoplankton and zooplankton were sampled using Hydrobios nets with mesh sizes of 30 μm and 55 μm, respectively, while macroinvertebrates were collected via the kicking method. Results indicated generally alkaline pH levels and elevated turbidity, but acceptable levels of dissolved oxygen and ion concentrations according to WHO and FAO guidelines. Moreover, the study suggests that the wetlands in Gambella and Benishangul-Gumz regions are currently in good condition, providing valuable insights for the conservation and sustainable management of Ethiopia's water resources, ensuring their conservation for both present and future generations. Local authorities can use the study's findings to implement remedial measures to protect water quality and biodiversity in the regions.

Introduction

Water is essential for both rural and urban communities and plays a crucial role in ecosystems [1]. It is fundamental for biodiversity and plays a vital role in supporting the survival of both aquatic and terrestrial life [2,3]. It is essential for life, agriculture, industry, and ecosystems, with rivers serving as critical freshwater sources that support agriculture, transportation, manufacturing, and public water supplies [4]. However, severe stress on water resources is increasing due to various human activities such as urbanization, industrialization, and agriculture [1,3,5], and climate change [[6], [7], [8]], leading to deteriorating water quality. Many regions face water quality degradation from anthropogenic and natural processes, threatening freshwater quality and impacting various uses, with surface waters and rivers near urban and rural areas affected by toxic waste [4]. The growing demand for water is driven by population growth, and increasing needs for different sectors [9].

Water is a fundamental human right and critical for sustainable development, bridging climate systems, human society, and the environment [9]. Contamination of water sources occurring due to natural processes and anthropogenic activities leads to chemical composition alterations [2]. However, human activities significantly increase pollutants, emphasizing the impact of land use practices on water resource degradation [10]. Declining water quality is mainly due to anthropogenic activities, particularly agricultural activities, with irrigation-return flows and uncontrolled chemical fertilizers playing significant roles [11]. Agricultural activities contribute pollutants like fertilizers and pesticides, impacting aquatic ecosystems [12].

Moreover, insufficient environmental protection against these anthropogenic activities can result in soil pollution, which in turn degrades water quality as runoff water carries contaminants from the soil into water bodies [13]. Assessing water quality involves monitoring physicochemical variables, which is essential for conservation efforts that monitor pollutant levels and their impacts on habitats and species [3,4], and for managing environmental risks [12]. Water quality management is therefore not only a crucial aspect of environmental conservation but also the conservation of biotic resources [14].

Assessment of water quality is indispensable for understanding the ecological health of aquatic systems [15]. It involves evaluating multiple parameters to determine suitability for diverse uses amidst escalating pressures from urbanization [16,17] and agricultural activities involving fertilizers, pesticides, and erosion [18]. This assessment is crucial for environmental management, especially in regions facing water resource challenges, as seen in sub-Saharan African countries [19]. underscore its pivotal role in establishing the foundation for ongoing monitoring and management efforts. Various methodologies and tools have been employed to establish baseline data and assess water quality across rivers, lakes, and other water bodies, utilizing physical, chemical, and biological parameters to provide comprehensive insights [20,21].

Effective baseline studies are essential for evaluating the impacts of human activities on water quality [22] and emphasize the necessity of conducting basin-specific assessments for effective water resource management and conservation [20]. These studies offer critical insights into initial water quality conditions, serving as benchmarks for future comparisons and management decisions, thereby guiding ongoing monitoring efforts and resource management strategies [19]. This comprehensive understanding not only informs decision-making but also promotes public awareness and facilitates water resource conservation [20,22,23].

The Gambella and Benishangul regions are situated within the Baro-Akobo River Basin in Ethiopia, with the River Akobo coursing prominently through these areas, sustaining a significant year-round flow supplemented by seasonal tributaries. The basin's ecosystem has undergone considerable changes due to factors like population growth, anthropogenic pressures, and potential climatic fluctuations.

In the expansive regions of Gambella and Benishangul-Gumuz in Ethiopia, water bodies face threats from diverse anthropogenic activities including mining, agricultural expansion, urban growth, and infrastructure development. These activities, driven by ongoing developmental initiatives, pose risks to the delicate wetland ecosystems within the Baro-Akobo River basin. Acknowledging the urgency, this study aims to conduct a thorough baseline assessment to evaluate current water quality conditions in these wetland resources.

The distinctive aspects of this study include its holistic approach, local relevance, rigorous standards evaluation, ecological health assessment, and actionable implications for conservation and management strategies in the Baro-Akobo River Basin in Ethiopia. Together, these factors contribute to advancing knowledge and guiding practical interventions for the conservation and sustainable management of water resources in Ethiopia, ensuring their conservation for the benefit of present and future generations alike.

Methodology

Description of the Baro-Akobo River Basin

The study was conducted within the Baro-Akobo River Basin (Fig. 1), where Gambella and Benishangul Gumz regions are situated in Ethiopia. These regions are characterized by lowlands adorned with towering grasses interspersed with numerous broad-leaf trees, often referred to as bushland [[24], [25], [26]]. Spanning from 5030′ to 10050′ Latitude and 33000′ to 36°18’ longitude in the western part of Ethiopia, the Gambella region encompasses the Akobo River and extends into the Benishangul-Gumuz Region. Elevations within the Baro-Akobo River basin vary from 395 to 3240 m above sea level (masl). The Rivers Akobo and Baro flow prominently through this basin, maintaining a substantial year-round flow, the primary tributaries of the White Nile. Baro River is used for navigation to connect Southern Sudan with Ethiopia. This study focuses on these tributaries, primarily due to ongoing intensive mineral extraction projects, particularly gold mining.Fig. 1Map of the study area.Fig. 1

Map of the study area.

Water protection is essential for sustainable development, ensuring environmental quality and economic security [1]. Sustainable development principles are crucial in balancing biodiversity conservation with human needs [2]. Three of the four UNESCO-registered biodiversity sites in Ethiopia are located within the Baro-Akobo River Basin. These include the Yayu Coffee Forest Biosphere Reserve, the Sheka Forest Biosphere Reserve, and the Kafa Afromontane cloud forests. These areas are renowned for harboring exceptional biodiversity, featuring dense primeval forests, bamboo thickets, grasslands, and wetlands. Particularly, they are recognized as the center of origin for Coffee Arabica, the world's most popular coffee [27]. Extending into South Sudan, the Gambella high forest encompasses national forest priority areas and partly the Gambella National Park [25,26]. The forests boast a wealth of both plant and animal species, comprising over 300 higher plants, 50 mammals, 200 birds, and 20 amphibian species, spanning all habitats. At least 55 plants and 10 birds are endemic to Ethiopia [27]. According to Ref. [25], the area hosts over 38 threatened species, including 5 birds, 3 mammals, and 30 plant species, among them Boswellia spp., primarily found in Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Sudan. Furthermore, the forests in the basin play a crucial role in biodiversity conservation, flood and erosion control, and carbon sequestration, aiding in the mitigation of climate change impacts [27].

In the past few decades, the Baro-Akobo River Basin has gone through various dynamic processes. Population increases, associated anthropogenic pressure, and possible climatic variability have brought visible changes to the ecosystem of the basin. More pressure is coming to the basin in the form of large-scale commercial farming.

The timing of the visit coincided with the peak rainy season, resulting in increased stream volumes. It is noteworthy that many of the streams under investigation, if not the majority, are prone to drying out during the peak of the dry seasons.

Water sampling and quality analyses

First, In situ measurements were made to measure the water (20–30 cm deep) temperature (0C), dissolved oxygen (DO) (mgL−1), electrical conductivity (μScm−1), total dissolved solids (TDS) (mgL−1), pH and percent saturation of the rivers studied at the discrete identified sampling sites using YSI Texas Instruments. Secondly, from each designated sampling site, 1 L of water sample was collected in dark acid-washed plastic bottles rinsed with distilled water. Before collecting the water, each bottle was first rinsed three times with the river water and then the sample was collected. These samples were kept in dark boxes and deep-frozen until transported to the laboratory in Addis Ababa for chemical analyses. Accordingly, water quality analyses were conducted to measure the concentrations of Mg2+, Ca2+, Na+, Cl−, NO3−, NO2−, PO43−, CO32−, HCO3−, NH4+, and a few others using the standard methods described in the Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater, 23rd Edition [28]. The water chemistry analysis was conducted at the Science Faculty laboratory at Addis Ababa University.

Phytoplankton

Phytoplankton communities indicate environmental stress [29]. They are crucial bioindicators due to their sensitivity to environmental changes and rapid response to habitat alterations [30]. They provide valuable insights into the health of aquatic environments by reflecting overall ecosystem conditions [31]. They are good indicators of water quality in aquatic ecosystems [32]. Their sampling in this study was conducted using standard Hydrobios 30 μm mesh size nets fitted with a filtering bucket. Samples were preserved in Lugol's iodine solution for improved visibility under an inverted microscope.

Zooplankton

Zooplanktons like phytoplanktons are reliable indicators of water quality [[33], [34], [35]]. Their sampling in this study was conducted using standard Hydrobios 55 μm mesh size nets fitted with a filtering bucket. Samples were collected and preserved in 5 % formalin solution saturated with sugar for improved visibility and to avoid collapsing of the bodies of the animals, which makes it easier for identification and enumeration [36,37].

Macro-invertebrates

Macroinvertebrates are widely used as valuable bioindicators of water quality [38]. Macro-invertebrates were sampled using the kicking method that disturbs and filters out macro-invertebrates from the shores of the study sites. This method has proved advantageous since it captures the specimens from the sediment up to the surface of the water. There are possibilities of even capturing fish fingerlings sizes that brood in the shore areas.

Macrophytes

Aquatic macrophytes are one of the indicators of water quality for assessing the ecological status of lakes and rivers [39]. These are aquatic vegetation regularly observed in the shallow waters or immediately on the shores of the study wetlands. Those found in the shallow waters could be submerged, floating, or emergent plants. In some rare cases, vegetation growing on rocks and stems found in water is also collected. Once collected the specimens were pressed in hardboard papers and brought to the laboratory for identification.

Fishes

Fish can be used as an indicator of water quality [40] and environmental conditions [41]. Fish samples were collected using nets designed to capture even fingerlings (mesh sizes of 2 cm). The fish samples collected were placed in glass bottles with the addition of 30 % ethanol to avoid bleaching of their colors or damage to their cell structure in cases where molecular identification is needed. Identification of phytoplankton, zooplankton, macro-invertebrates, macrophytes, and fish specimens was carried out at the Science Faculty laboratory at Addis Ababa University.

pH is a key indicator in water quality assessments [48]. Its level in water is essential for assessing water quality and pollution. It is a critical water quality parameter that significantly influences the physicochemical status of aquatic environments alongside parameters such as dissolved oxygen and temperature [49,50]. pH helps maintain stable water conditions [51]. The pH of the water in this study was in the alkaline range which is expected to be the case for such freshwaters. pH levels ranging from 6.5 to 9.5 are generally considered safe for the suitability of water for various purposes [52,53]. WHO recommends a pH range of 6.5–8.5 for optimal water quality [54]. Monitoring pH alongside other water quality parameters like temperature, dissolved oxygen, total suspended solids, etc. is essential for evaluating water quality [55].

The pH level of water can impact various aspects of water quality, including its chemical and microbiological characteristics [56] and fish growth and health, [57]. pH variations significantly impact aquatic organisms [58]. The field measurements of the water temperature showed that it is well above 200C, ranging from 23°C to 27°C in the Benishangul section and from 23°C to 35°C in the Gambella section. This is quite warm for a peak rainy season. In the dry seasons and when the volumes of the running waters decrease to their annual minimum level, the water temperature is likely to go well above 30°C. Elevated temperatures can stimulate bacterial growth, potentially leading to water source contamination [59]. Water temperature is a fundamental indicator of water quality, influencing physical, chemical, and biological processes in aquatic systems [60,61]. It affects parameters like dissolved oxygen and biochemical oxygen demand, especially during warmer months [62]. Water temperature is vital for maintaining aquatic habitats, such as fish farms and freshwater lobster cultivation, where optimal conditions are monitored using temperature sensors [63].

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is a key indicator of water quality [64,65]. It influences aquatic ecosystem health [66,67]. It is critical for the survival of aquatic life and metabolic processes [68,69], and is a fundamental indicator of water quality across various aquatic environments [[70], [71], [72]]. Particularly it is significant in freshwater ecosystems [73,74]. Its measurements are used in rivers to evaluate water quality [72,75,76] and assess aquatic ecosystem health [74,77].

It directly impacts the metabolic processes of aerobic communities in aquatic systems [78]. The waters have sufficient dissolved oxygen in the range of 4–8.5 mgL−1 in the study sections, which again is conducive to supporting freshwater aquatic life forms. The amount of DO in water fluctuates based on water temperature, air pressure, microorganisms, and the presence of oxidizable substances [79].

Electrical conductivity is an indicator of contamination and a decline in water quality [80]. EC indicates the salt concentration in water, and high levels can have ecological impacts on aquatic life [79]. The electrical conductivity of all the waters examined in situ in the Gambella section is in the range of 168–1500μScm−1, while it is in the range of 22–151μScm−1 in the Benishangul section, which is very low. This is a clear indication of the appreciable increase in the volumes of the streams examined by freshly added runoff of the wet season, which is above the average permissible limits of electrical conductivity value of 1.72 mg/l, not aligning with safety recommendations [81]. Typically, water's electrical conductivity rises during dry periods due to evaporation and falls during rainy days due to precipitation [82].

EC is important for assessing water quality as it reflects the presence of dissolved ions [83]. It is influenced by temperature and dissolved solids, making it valuable for water quality monitoring [84,85]. It serves as an accurate indicator of water quality and is sensitive to soluble salts [86,87]. High EC levels can indicate poor water quality, while reductions often signal improvements [88,89]. Consequently, the percent saturations and total dissolved solids (TDS) of the waters were measured to be very low in both sections of this study. The low TDS values can indicate low salt concentrations in water, not affect its overall quality and usability [90].

TDS are critical indicators of water quality, affecting the suitability of water for various uses [90,91]. Its high TDS levels signal poor water quality [92]. TDS, alongside other parameters like electrical conductivity, pH, total solids, and dissolved oxygen is essential for assessing water quality indices and overall water health [93]. In surface water, its levels assess water quality in rivers, affecting uses like fish culture and potability [94]. Its elevated TDS levels stress aquatic life and impact aquatic ecosystems significantly [95].

Turbidity indicates the presence of suspended particles such as clay, silt, organic and inorganic matter, and microorganisms [96]. Turbidity is commonly caused by suspended matter such as clay, silt, organic and inorganic particles, plankton, and microscopic organisms [97]. Its high levels are associated with poor water quality and can indicate microbial contamination [98,99]. Its elevation levels of turbidity in this study suggest the presence of suspended particles in water, affecting its clarity and potentially impacting its safety for various uses [100]. Alkalinity levels in all sample sites in the study were detected below the WHO limit set for alkalinity in water at 200 mg/L. High turbidity is caused by the presence of bacteria, plankton, iron and aluminum hydroxide, sludge, and colloidal matter [79].

Nitrate (NO3−) level was also detected below the WHO guideline value. According to the WHO, the guideline value for nitrate in drinking water is 50 mg/L nitrate units [101]. This is also true for Phosphate (PO43−) and Sulfate (SO42−) whose levels are below the WHO (250 mg/l) and FAO (960 mg/l) standards [102]. This study has shown that Na+, Ca+, and K+ levels were detected within the acceptable limits outlined by WHO and FAO guidelines, indicating suitability for irrigation.

In the Gambella section, the water quality of the selected water systems indicated that soluble reactive phosphorus (SRP), ammonium (NH4+), and total phosphorus (TP) were considerably high, probably because of the disturbance of the wetlands by artisan gold collectors (Fig. 2). Further, the water systems are affected by the rapid expansion of settlements, cultivated land that uses fertilizers and uncontrolled waste disposal in the forests and the water systems. This is a trend that has to be regulated as soon as possible before the wetlands are degraded and the biodiversity therein gets impacted. Nitrogen, phosphorus, and other related ones are within the normal range for a healthy environment. One may look at sodium, calcium, and potassium content which appear to be higher in Gambella than in the Benishangul section. This might be due to the fast-running water of the rainy season washing the rocks and soils of these elements in the study areas.Fig. 2Artisan gold seekers disturbing the rivers and streams by importing soil into the wetland to wash off the dust to collect gold pieces in the streams in the Gambella Region.Fig. 2

Rivers are vital for economic sectors but face increasing anthropogenic pressures that degrade riparian ecosystems [1]. The muddy color of river water (Fig. 2) might be due to increased sediment from natural erosion, agricultural runoff, and mining operations, which may disturb the soil and lead to runoff, especially during heavy rainfall. Meanwhile, the water also looks green in Fig. 2; this might be caused by algal blooms fueled by excess nutrients from fertilizers and higher temperatures in the area, promoting the growth of algae, aquatic plants, and phytoplankton. Human activities such as agriculture, other development activities, and poor soil management contribute significantly to both muddy and green water by increasing sediment and nutrient runoff [103].

Phytoplankton and zooplankton

Phytoplankton are used to assess environmental stress and anthropogenic impacts in water bodies [[104], [105], [106]]. They reflect pollution levels caused by human impacts [107,108], showing direct correlations between their abundance and water quality [109]. They are also essential water quality bioindicators across various aquatic systems for monitoring pollution, eutrophication, and ecological [[110], [111], [112]]. They are valuable bioindicators of ecological conditions [113]. They reflect the health of water bodies, responding rapidly to environmental changes and providing insights into water quality and aquatic ecosystem health [114,115].

Both phytoplankton and zooplankton have been used to reflect the water quality condition of aquatic ecosystems [32,[116], [117], [118]] and can help evaluate changes in water quality [119]. They are reliable bio-indicators of water quality in various environments like temperature, chemical oxygen demand, ammonia, and total nitrogen [[32], [33], [34], [35],120]. Their sensitivity to environmental conditions makes them valuable indicators of changes in water quality parameters [121].

Efforts to gather phytoplankton and zooplankton samples at designated locations using standard nets proved futile due to the excessive turbidity of the water, which obstructed the nets (Fig. 3). The primary factor contributing to this phenomenon was the substantial rainfall experienced during sampling, leading to river inundation and subsequent rapid water flow. Consequently, the elevated siltation and downstream washing of these microscopic organisms occurred. Moreover, the swift water velocity of the River Akobo exacerbated the situation by carrying away these drifting organisms. However, one special attempt was made in a relatively quiet corner of one of the sampling sites, namely, Namqedo (Site 10).Fig. 3Phytoplankton and zooplankton collection at Gambella (A), and Benihangul Gumz (B) sites.Fig. 3

Phytoplankton and zooplankton collection at Gambella (A), and Benihangul Gumz (B) sites.

Brick-colored water in rivers as shown in Fig. 3 might be caused by iron and other minerals, which are released into the water when soils containing iron erode [122]. In the presence of oxygen, iron oxidizes to form iron oxides like rust (ferric oxide), giving the water a reddish or brick hue [123]. Mining activities in the area can release large amounts of iron particles into rivers, intensifying the water's brick-red color [124]. Areas, particularly those prone to erosion during heavy rainfall, contribute suspended iron particles and clay minerals to river water, enhancing its reddish tint [123]. Agricultural practices and soil erosion also play roles by mobilizing iron-rich sediments into water bodies.

In the samples collected in triplicates, traces of some diatoms mixed up with silt, copepodites (larval copepods), and some rotifer genera, namely, Lecane, Hexarthra, and Synchaeta were observed. Copepods are reliable indicators of water quality [[33], [34], [35]]. Nocladocerans were also recorded. These quantities were not sufficient to make the planed biomass estimations of both phytoplankton and zooplankton.

Macroinvertebrates

Aquatic organisms reflect ecosystem health, guiding conservation efforts [128]. Aquatic insects like plant diversity demonstrate ecosystem interconnectivity [20,129]. Macroinvertebrates are crucial biological indicators due to their abundance and sensitivity to environmental changes [130]. They are used globally to assess the impact of human activities on water quality [38,131]. They serve as reliable indicators across various aquatic environments, reflecting ecosystem health and integrity through their abundance, diversity, and sensitivity to environmental changes [132].

Macroinvertebrates like fishes serve as bioindicators, indicating stream water pollution levels [133]. They are valuable bioindicators for water quality assessment due to their sensitivity to environmental changes [38,[134], [135], [136], [137], [138], [139], [140]] and can also effectively reflect the impact of human activities on aquatic ecosystems and provide insights into habitat conditions and water quality [141,142]. Their diversity and abundance correlate with water quality conditions, making them effective for monitoring aquatic ecosystem health [143,144]. Macroinvertebrate's wide distribution, species diversity, and varying pollution tolerance levels support their use as bioindicators [145].

In this study, the macroinvertebrates collected in the Gambella section using the kicking method are detailed below (Table 4; Fig. 4a). The abundance and diversity of macroinvertebrates in water bodies have been linked to the quality of water [146]. In this study, out of the 13 sites surveyed, macroinvertebrates were only collected at four sites, exhibiting poor diversity and quantity, likely due to anthropogenic impacts such as the continued stirring of mud during the sieving of gold particulates. These findings serve as indications of the level of degradation within the Baro-Akobo Basin Gambella section, corroborated by physicochemical results indicating extremely high turbidity (FTU exceeding 360 and transparency measurements not exceeding 2 cm) (Table 2a, Table 2b, Table 2ca–c). Similar observations have been reported by Refs. [147,148] and also in studies conducted in Ethiopia and elsewhere [[149], [150], [151]]. Whereas in the Benishangul section of the Baro-Akobo Basin, all sampled areas were found to be rich in macroinvertebrates, indicating the pristine nature of the Baro-Akobo wetlands (Table 4). Their abundance indicates the health of the studied aquatic ecosystems [146]. The macroinvertebrates collected from the Gambella section were limited in both abundance and diversity (Fig. 4a). The mere absence of the invertebrates from these sites is an indication of frequent disturbance of the aquatic systems by gold-collectors, preventing the re-establishment of the macroinvertebrate communities. The disturbance as observed could be because of two events taking place in the area at the time of the visit, namely, the heavy rains of the season that increased the volumes and velocity of the water flow and the washing of earth in the rivers by gold-collectors.

Macroinvertebrates were abundantly captured, showing remarkable diversity in water bodies in the Benishangul section. The presence and diversity of macroinvertebrates in water bodies indicate the health of the river systems [146,152]. A significant portion of species exhibited resilience to variations in stream flow velocity and turbidity. They were observed clinging to hard surfaces such as rocks, sediments, and gravels, seeking refuge from the swift currents. Employing the kicking method for macroinvertebrate collection proved invaluable, dislodging them from their sheltered positions on hard surfaces. Changes in water quality influence macroinvertebrate composition and distribution, highlighting their role in environmental monitoring [153].

The reappearance of crabs serves as a compelling indicator that the water quality, coupled with warm temperatures and optimal water chemistry, supports the potential for fish, crab, shrimp, and other fishery product farming with minimal additional inputs. This offers a sustainable alternative livelihood for local communities who currently invest significant time in gold panning, inadvertently polluting streams with sediment loads, excess nutrients, and other waste materials (Fig. 5). Severe inundations caused by heavy rains are exacerbated by artisanal gold-seekers who extensively manipulate and wash soil with river water, thereby increasing the movement of sediments. Establishing such livelihoods inherently fosters a market chain encompassing hatchery development, feed processing, fish storage, and transportation facilities. Fishes

Fishes are valuable indicators due to their sensitivity to environmental changes, influenced by various water quality parameters like nitrates, phosphates, pH, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, etc. [154]. Their abundance and diversity are valuable metrics for assessing ecosystem health and the impacts of anthropogenic activities on aquatic environments [155]. Changes in fish abundance and diversity may reflect alterations in water quality following environmental interventions and anthropogenic activities [156]. Fish presence and diversity indicate ecosystem integrity and specific environmental conditions necessary for fish reproduction [157].

By studying fish populations and their interactions with water quality parameters, researchers can better understand the dynamics of aquatic ecosystems and the effects of water quality on fish populations [158].

Fish are valuable indicators of water quality, thus making them crucial for monitoring biological water quality [40,41,159]. The only site where fish could have been surveyed was River Akobo in the Gambella section. However, the high level and velocity of the river made it inaccessible. However, some cyprinid larval fishes were collected while operating the kicking method for macroinvertebrate collection in the shallow sites other than River Akobo (Fig. 6A). This indicated that fishes are breeding somewhere upstream where small tributaries of River Akobo are providing reasonably clearer waters with sufficient dissolved oxygen for fish breeding. Studies have shown that fish communities are influenced by various water quality parameters such as water quality characteristics [160]. Factors such as temperature, pH, and turbidity are crucial parameters affecting water quality in fish culture [161]. Large fishes, as expected in such pristine waters, were not captured. The main reason is the high speed and silt load of the waters due to the high runoffs of the rainy season. In such circumstances, fishes migrate to calmer and clearer waters, or they simply get washed downstream to calmer sections of the water system. Maintaining excellent water quality is essential for fish growth [166]. Based on the attempts made to collect fish specimens from the Benishangul section, some fingerlings were collected (Fig. 6B). Due to the fast-flowing nature of the rivers and streams and very high turbidity, the collection of mature fish was very difficult.

The presence of some larval fishes is a clear indication that fishes of considerable diversity and abundance are likely to be found in stable systems where the silts or sediments settle and the velocity of the waters decreases. Such fish richness and diversity may correlate with water quality in the basin [41]. These larval fishes are apparently on their way downstream washed by the running waters from upstream breeding grounds where the waters are calmer, cleaner, and even richer in dissolved oxygen. It is also typical of most cyprinid fishes to migrate upstream for breeding in wet warm seasons as in the Baro-Akobo system. The study area, the Baro-Akobo Basin, is renowned for its rich aquatic biodiversity, harboring 119 species across 26 families of fishes [[162], [163], [164], [165]]. Consequently, it is highly probable that the wetlands under investigation also host a diverse array of fish species, provided that fish sampling is conducted at the appropriate times. Such species composition provides insights into the environmental conditions of the water bodies in the basin [41]. Monitoring fish communities and their responses to changes in water quality parameters aids in understanding and managing aquatic ecosystems [154].

Macrophytes

Macrophytes provide insights into nutrient enrichment, organic loading, and water quality [167,168]. Aquatic macrophytes are sensitive indicators of water quality influenced by turbidity, nutrient levels, and location [169]. Submerged macrophytes correlate with water quality parameters [39,170]. They absorb excess nutrients, serving as bioindicators of eutrophication and water quality [171,172]. The EU Water Framework Directive recognizes macrophytes for assessing ecological status [171] as aquatic macrophytes are essential for monitoring ecological status and surface water quality [173]. Their presence in aquatic ecosystems can enhance water quality by absorbing excess nutrients from the water body [39]. In this study, numerous macrophytes were collected both from the Gambella and Benishangul sections (Table 5). Many of the wetland plants in the Gambella section have some ecosystem services in some communities. For instance, Typhalatifolia is used for roofing grass houses, or even cottages in some cases; Vitellaria paradoxa contains vital oils used in traditional medicines, Hebiscus spp. Serve as sources of nectars for many ecologically important insects; Panicum maximum is used as fodder to livestock and has some medicinal use in some communities; Sida spp. Produces juicy squashes from its succulent leaves, which is used in many cultures as a fungicide for topical (skin) diseases, including in Ethiopia. In some other cultures, it is used to treat mouth and throat inflammations; Uvaria spp. has edible parts and hence the plant is used as animal fodder; Ricinus spp. Produces oil that is used for many industrial purposes, but it is considered to be poisonous for direct human or animal use; Xanthium stomarium has medicinal uses in different cultures, and Parthenium spp., an aggressive invasive species, is used as a substitute for rubber. It also produces chemicals that are poisonous to plants and humans but can be used as a medicinal plant. In the Benishangul region, however, the macrophytes along the rivers and streams in the selected sites have undergone significant disturbance as a result of heavy rainfall. Smaller macrophytes, whether submerged, floating, or emergent, have either been submerged in the high waters or washed away entirely. These plants typically observed emerging in wetlands, are normally found several meters from the water's edge during dry seasons. The relationship between macrophytes and water quality is influenced by various factors like the extent of macrophyte diversity, coverage, and biomass [169].

Senna didymobotrya, commonly known as the candelabra tree, is an invasive species in Ethiopia that has spread into many wet areas. Setaria megaphylla, the ribbon grass or broad-leafed bristle grass, is tall shade-loving grass commonly found in ephemeral or permanent wetlands. Tephrosia spp., found in warm, wet areas of tropical Africa, has black seeds that are powdered and used for fishing due to its high rotenone content. Rotenone interferes with the breathing system of fishes, resulting in a lack of movement coordination due to oxygen shortage, making them vulnerable to capture by fishers. Phoenix reclinata is also widely found in warm tropical water shores and is utilized for making household, artisanal, or industrial materials. Carex spp. has a worldwide distribution. They are commonly found in the Nile Basin and other Ethiopian wetlands. These plants are utilized as ornamental flowers by horticulturists and industrially as heat insulators for footwear. Locally, they are employed for thatching grass houses and making mats. Hygrophila schulii is found in tropical Eastern Africa wetlands. This herbaceous medicinal plant holds significance within local communities for its anti-inflammatory properties. Also, Syziguum spp. is utilized in some communities to extract juice from its fruits. The presence of Commelina spp., with its unique blooming pattern opening in the early morning and evening and wilting by day, is a common sight in warm tropical wetlands. Sesbania spp. Serves various purposes locally, including as animal feed, as a source of firewood, and for fiber production for household materials. Furthermore, communities harness the water extract of Sesbania spp. to combat external parasites affecting livestock. Especially, its nitrogen-fixing capabilities, similar to leguminous plants, hold promise for soil fertilization applications. Most of the expected typical emergent, submerged, and floating aquatic plants were not present due to the inundation of the rivers by the runoffs of the wet season [175]. Only the emergent vascular plants that could withstand the pressures of the fast-flowing floods were observed. This is because their presence is influenced by various factors including water depth [174].

The relationship between river color and species diversity in river water is influenced by various factors affecting river ecosystems. River color, reflecting the water's physical and chemical properties, correlates with the geomorphology of the river's catchment region [176]. Parameters like turbidity and color can significantly influence the development of flora and fauna in rivers [177]. The thermal regime of rivers is also essential for aquatic ecosystem health, impacting water quality and the distribution of aquatic species [178]. River ecosystems play essential roles in biodiversity maintenance [179].

Conclusion

The research findings highlight significant environmental threats and impacts in the Baro-Akobo Basin due to increasing population growth driven by gold mining and industrial expansion. These activities lead to deforestation, agricultural changes, and heavy metal pollution, endangering biodiversity and water quality. Despite high turbidity, key water quality parameters like dissolved oxygen, conductivity, and nutrient levels generally meet WHO and FAO guidelines. Wetland ecosystems currently support diverse flora and fauna. The study's findings underscore the importance of urgent remedial actions to protect water quality and aquatic biodiversity in the region. To protect these vital resources, the study advocates for sustainable development practices involving local communities, especially artisanal miners, and promoting alternative livelihoods like fisheries. Rigorous regulation and integrated management systems are crucial to balance development with conservation efforts and mitigate environmental degradation in the basin. Local authorities can use the study's findings to implement remedial measures to protect water quality and biodiversity.
