An Unusual Cause of Ataxia in Patient with Sjogren’s Syndrome: Metronidazole Neurotoxicity
Authors: Bahar Say, Mustafa Gungor, Berfin Kardelen Aydin, Yeliz Pekcelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebellar ataxia associated with metronidazole is rare. Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), a chronic autoimmune disease, can also rarely present with neurological symptoms such as cerebellar ataxia. In this report, we aimed to present a 40-year-old female patient diagnosed with SS who developed acute-onset speech disorder and imbalance. The patient’s complaints developed shortly, four days, after taking metronidazole for a vaginal infection. Her neurological examination revealed a broad-based gait, explosive speech, and bilateral dysmetria. Routine blood tests, nerve conduction studies, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis were normal. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed hyperintensity in the dentate nuclei of the cerebellum, consistent with metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity. The patient’s symptoms rapidly improved after discontinuation of the drug, and the lesions had completely resolved on imaging one month later. Apart from neuropathy and cerebellar degeneration, which are the most common etiologies of ataxia in Sjögren’s syndrome, rare metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity was observed in our patient. Metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity may be a potentially reversible cause of cerebellar symptoms, and its recognition based on typical radiological features is important for drug withdrawal and complete recovery.

Metronidazole is a widely used antimicrobial agent. Metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity is rare. The most recognized neurological side effect is neuropathy. Rarely, it can cause cerebellar syndrome, encephalopathy, and seizures.[1]

Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disorder. It is characterized by exocrine gland dysfunction, primarily resulting in dry eyes and mouth.[2] While peripheral neuropathy is the most frequent neurological manifestation, central nervous system (CNS) involvement is less frequently observed. Ataxia in SS is rare and is typically attributed to sensory neuropathy or, less commonly, cerebellar involvement.[3]

Case Report

A 40-year-old woman with a known history of SS was admitted to the emergency department with a two-day history of acute-onset speech disturbance and gait instability. She had been on chloroquine for the past three months and had recently, four days earlier, received doxycycline (100 mg twice daily), metronidazole (500 mg twice daily), and cefuroxime (500 mg twice daily) tablets for a vaginal infection. Vital signs and systemic examination findings were within normal limits. Neurological examination revealed a wide-based gait with scanning speech, bilateral dysmetria, and dysdiadochokinesia. Routine blood tests, folate, vitamin B12, thyroid function tests, and non-contrast brain tomography were normal. Diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was normal, and a nerve conduction study performed one month earlier showed no signs of neuropathy.

Brain MRI with gadolinium was performed (Fig. 1). Axial T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images revealed nonspecific hyperintensities in the bilateral frontoparietal and subcortical white matter (Fig. 1a, b). However, hyperintensities in the dentate nuclei of the cerebellum were remarkable (Fig. 1c). Contrast enhancement was not observed. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was normal, and oligoclonal bands were absent. Upon recognition of these characteristic MRI findings, metronidazole was immediately discontinued. The patient’s symptoms improved significantly following cessation of the drug. A follow-up MRI conducted one month later showed complete resolution of the bilateral dentate nucleus lesions (Fig. 1d).

Discussion

We report a patient with reversible ataxia due to metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity, highlighting the characteristic MRI findings. Neurotoxicity manifesting as cerebellar dysfunction, encephalopathy, or seizures due to metronidazole is less commonly reported.[1] Although the cause of metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity is still unknown, inhibition of postsynaptic central monoaminergic neurotransmission and immunomodulation have been proposed as hypotheses.[4] It has also recently been reported that iron may contribute to neurotoxicity.[5]

Metronidazole-induced ataxia has been reported in the literature in the form of limited case reports.[6] There is only one large case-control (vs. clindamycin), population-based study including adults aged 66 years or older. Over the last 14 years, 12,098 patients, with 1,212 controls, who were admitted to the hospital with newly developed cerebellar syndrome, encephalopathy, and peripheral neuropathy and who had used metronidazole or clindamycin within the previous 100 days were included in the study. As a result, the overall neurological event rate within 100 days was found to be 0.25%; encephalopathy or cerebellar dysfunction was 0.2%, and peripheral neuropathy was 0.05%. In this study, a large number of patients were evaluated retrospectively, and the neurological events associated with metronidazole were found to be quite low. Metronidazole neurotoxicity was reported not to be a dose- or duration-related phenomenon.[7] Prolonged use of this drug may lead to toxicity; however, Kuriyama et al. [8] reported 64 patients with metronidazole-induced neurotoxicity, among whom 26% had taken the drug for less than one week and 11% had taken it for less than three days.[6] In our patient, symptoms started a few days after using metronidazole.

The characteristic MRI finding in ataxia associated with metronidazole is bilateral dentate nucleus hyperintensity. It is observed in up to 90% of affected patients.[9] These nuclei play a crucial role in motor coordination, and their involvement explains the patient’s clinical presentation. Recognition of such imaging findings is crucial for timely diagnosis and management. In our case, discontinuation of metronidazole led to rapid symptom resolution and normalization of follow-up MRI.[9,10]

The prevalence of cerebellar ataxia in SS is estimated to be 1.5%, and cerebellar involvement is more common in women than in men, with a female-to-male ratio of 6:1.[11] Gait ataxia and/or cerebellar speech are related to vermian dysfunction or sensory neuropathy.[3] We excluded neuropathy and cerebellar degeneration through nerve conduction and radiological studies in this patient.

Ataxia encompasses impaired coordination of eye movements, speech, limb dexterity, and gait, usually due to cerebellar involvement. Its causes include nutritional deficiencies, toxins, medications, autoimmune processes, and genetic and neurodegenerative disorders. Clinicians should keep in mind reversible etiologies such as drug-induced toxicity or nutritional deficiencies.[12] On the other hand, detailed history taking and neurological and systemic examination of patients are important for diagnosing the etiology of ataxia.[13]

Conclusion

To our knowledge and based on our literature review, no previous case of coexisting Sjögren’s syndrome and metronidazole-induced cerebellar ataxia has been reported. In patients with autoimmune diseases such as SS, clinicians should include metronidazole toxicity in the differential diagnosis of acute cerebellar symptoms. Prompt recognition and withdrawal of the offending agent can lead to rapid and complete clinical recovery.
