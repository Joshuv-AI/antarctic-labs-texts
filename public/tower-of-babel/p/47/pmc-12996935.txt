Internal target volume reproducibility and dose coverage for lung stereotactic body radiotherapy using spine-based positioning and tracking
Authors: Thomas Opsommer, Mylène Messéant, Florence Le Tinier, Julien Laffarguette, Thomas Lacornerie, Frederik Crop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Internal target volume, Lung tumors, 4D Cone Beam CT, Image-guided Radiotherapy, Stereotactic body radiotherapy

Introduction

Image-guided radiotherapy is essential to ensure accurate target positioning in stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), with soft tissue registration using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) considered a reference for accurate treatment delivery [1]. For lung tumors, an additional challenge arises from deformations, respiratory-induced tumor motion and baseline shifts within the lung [2]. Several motion-management strategies exist for lung SBRT, including internal target volume (ITV)-based planning, breath-hold, and gating, with ITV-based planning being the most frequently employed [3], [4]. This technique involves aligning the ITV, which contains the full gross tumor volume (GTV) breathing motion, as visualized on (4D-)CBCT, with the ITV defined on the planning 4DCT. Other concepts exist such as mid position/ventilation, using reconstructed time-weighted mean position CTs, or internal margins, using movement margins on the GTV instead of the ITV [5], [6]. These result in reduced planning target volumes (PTV), however they are rarely applied.

In robotic lung SBRT, the target must be visible on the 2D X-ray imaging system or be localized using implanted fiducials for tracking. When neither option is feasible, spine-based positioning and tracking is often the only available solution, enabling intrafraction tracking of the spine but without direct visualization of the lesion for positioning and tracking [7], [8]. This occurs also for C-arm linacs when (4D-)CBCT contrast is insufficient due to small lesion size, ground-glass opacities, or irregular movements [9]. Finally, fiducial placement may also be contraindicated.

Next to, of low scientific value, anecdotal cases in practice where fibrosis is sometimes seen next to the target after spine-based lung treatments, there are conflicting results and margins in the literature [[10], [11]]. In the context of lung SBRT, margin recipes are not always valid due to underlying factors and assumptions: e.g. moving target, fractionation, penumbra depending on lung density. Optimization and prescription clearly also plays a role, as PTV-type optimization and prescriptions using algorithms taking into account secondary electron transport tend to lead to variable higher GTV doses and thus may indirectly compensate geometrical variations [12].

Clinical studies showed both equal and lower local control when using 3–10 mm margins, with or without ITV for spine-based lung SBRT [[10], [11], [13], [14]]. Historically, several authors studied the geometric effects of bone-based registration for lung but without detailed 4D-(CB)CT dose calculations as these were not available, resulting for example in 5–10 mm margins [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. More recently, accurate and detailed 4D-(CB)CT dose calculations or robust optimizations were applied, however for lesions positioned using the visible ITV, not spine based registration/tracking, arriving at 3–4 mm margins [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Margins applied to the ITV may be conservative for a moving target as a GTV leaving the PTV will only occur in a small percentage of time, compared to conventional PTV margins applied to a static target [5]. Finally, TG135.B recommends spine-based lung SBRT only when the tumor is located less than 5 cm from the spine, but lacking data [8].

This study aimed to characterize geometric reproducibility for spine-tracking-based lung SBRT and to assess dose implications using per-fraction 4D dose calculations. Specifically, this allowed estimation of the upper-bound risk of severe target underdosage and identification of possible mitigation strategies.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by the local ethics committee (CEC-2025–031). Eighty consecutive patients treated with lung SBRT were included, representing 340 treatment fractions. All patients underwent a planning 4D-CT (Somatom confidence RT pro, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) and prior to each fraction a 4D-CBCT. Treatments were delivered on a TrueBeam accelerator (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Treatment planning and dose calculations were performed using Raystation V24B (Raysearch, Stockholm, Sweden).

Clinical positioning and retrospective spine-based positioning simulation

For clinical treatment, patient positioning was performed using 4D-CBCT with alignment of the ITV. This alignment was considered the reference positioning method. Treatment planning corresponding to homogeneous-fluence optimization was performed on the 30% respiratory phase of the 4D planning CT. A 1 cm water-equivalent virtual bolus was applied around the PTV (=ITV + 5 mm), within lung tissue, to ensure stable fluence optimization in low-density regions during optimization. After optimization, the virtual bolus was removed and the final Collapsed Cone (V5.6) dose calculation was performed. The dose was then normalized to the median dose D50%(GTV), corresponding to the prescribed dose of 3x18 Gy, 5x11 Gy or 8x7.5 Gy. This homogeneous-fluence workflow ensured a consistent, simple and robust prescription in low-density lung tissue, such that the GTV received the prescribed dose for any position within the PTV [[2], [24]].

To simulate spine-based lung SBRT, also called 0-view, a retrospective rigid registration was performed for each fraction using grey-level matching of vertebral structures. The registration was restricted to a cylindrical region of interest centred on the spine with a cranio–caudal length of 7 cm, representing spine-tracking using Exactrac for C-arm accelerators or XSight Spine for Cyberknife [[7], [9]]. Details of prescription and registration methods are shown in Supplementary Figs. S1/S2.

Geometric analysis

The ITV centre obtained from 0-view spine-based positioning was compared with that obtained from per-fraction 4D-CBCT positioning. Additionally, it was assessed whether the new ITV remained within the original PTV. The ITV positioning standard deviation (SD) was first tested for heteroscedasticity with spine distance through a Breush-Pagan test, followed by linear fitting of binned SDs. Differences in ITV positioning error due to lesion localization were analyzed using Games-Howell tests with Holm-Bonferroni adjusted p-values [25], while a Fisher test was applied to evaluate if the top 10% of largest deviations in each direction were related to inferior or superior located lesions. Differences in the mean and positioning variability in each direction were compared using paired t-tests and Levene’s test respectively. 95% confidence intervals of proportions were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson method. All statistical analyses were performed using R for significance at p < 0.05 [26].

Evaluation of dose–volume parameters

Fractions were selected for detailed dose–volume analysis if at least one of the following criteria was met: (a) more than 1% of the 4D-CBCT per-fraction ITV extended beyond the PTV, or (b) the three-dimensional displacement vector exceeded 5 mm. This ensured conservative analysis for fractions with ITV close to the 5 mm PTV margin. The treatment planning method applied is known to be robust as long as the target is within the PTV [[2], [12], [24]]. To verify this assumption, a random subset of 11 fractions with displacement vectors below 5 mm and full internal target volume containment was analysed. 4D per-fraction GTV dose was recalculated over the full respiratory cycle using a virtual CT approach based on deformation of the planning computed tomography [27].

Dose–volume parameters were extracted for the GTV, including the median dose (D50%) and minimum dose to 95% and 98% of the volume (D95% and D98%). ΔD50%>3–5% and ΔD98%>10–20% differences between spine-based and 4D-CBCT positioning were reported descriptively. Fractions resulting in ΔD98%>10% were also optimized and recalculated using 7 mm inferior-superior/posterior-anterior margins to investigate this possible mitigation method.

Results

Lesions were located in the right superior (n = 31), left superior (n = 21), right inferior (n = 16), and left inferior (n = 12) lung lobes. GTVs ranged from 0.1 to 57.2 cm3, with a median of 1.9 cm3 (quartiles [0.75–5.24] cm3). The ITV had a median of 3.5 cm3 (quartiles [1.7, 8.9] cm3). Median GTV motion amplitude during the respiratory cycle was 10 mm (quartiles [6–16] mm) with a between-subject and within-subject SD of 6 and 4 mm. The mean GTV-to-ITV ratio was 52% (SD 19%). The respective between-subject and within-subject GTV-to-ITV ratio SD were 17% and 7%. The median distance between the ITV and centre of spine was 8.1 cm (quartiles [5.5–9.7] cm).

Fig. 1 shows that there was only a small systematic difference between the two registration methods (1 mm in the inferior-superior direction). The 0-view positioning standard deviations were 1.2 mm right-left, 2.7 mm inferior-superior, and 2.5 mm posterior-anterior, with significantly greater variability in inferior-superior/posterior-anterior than in right-left directions. Uncertainty with spine distance showed only heteroscedasticity in posterior-anterior direction, with a σ = 0.5 mm + 0.2 mm/cm*d(center spine) relation, detailed in Supplementary Fig. S3. There was no significant difference in the mean displacement when considering lesion/lobe location. The top 10% differences in inferior-superior direction were statistically significant related to inferior lesions. In 11% of cases (37/340, 95%CI: [8%-15%]), more than 1% of the ITV extended beyond the original 5 mm PTV and 18% (60/340, 95%CI: [14%–22%]) presented displacement vector > 5 mm.

Fig. 2 shows that when using the original 5 mm PTV, nine out of 340 fractions (2.6%, 95%CI: [1.2–5%]) showed ΔD98%>10%, for which three fractions showed higher differences of 35%, 37%, and 45%. Four fractions (1.2%, 95%CI: [0.3–3%]) exhibited a D50% difference exceeding 3% between spine- and 4D-CBCT-based registration. Two of these fractions demonstrated large D50% differences of 6.3% and 7.3%, respectively. When considering the D95%, 16 (4.7%, 95%CI: [3–8%]) of the fractions showed a difference larger than 5%. Fig. 2 further shows that differences in terms of D50% or D98% were minimal for the sampled fractions with ITV displacement < 5 mm and without ITV extension beyond the PTV. Finally, Fig. 2 shows that reoptimized plans (blue) using increased margins reduced considerably, but not completely, dose differences. Further per-fraction details are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4 and Supplementary Table ST1.

Discussion

This study showed that in spine-based lung SBRT using homogeneous-fluence optimization, a 5 mm planning target volume was geometrically insufficient in 11% of fractions. However, respiratory motion largely reduced the dose impact, with ΔD98%>10% observed in 2.6% of fractions. Despite this partial compensation, mitigation strategies are suggested, as occasional larger deviations may result in infrequent but potentially clinically significant underdosage.

The 11% of fractions with the original ITV leaving the 5 mm PTV were not systematically associated with a noticeable degradation in GTV dose coverage which shows that margins, dose distribution, and optimization/prescription methods are linked [[12], [24], [28], [29]]. The geometrical results, but also the dose implications, are relevant for robust optimization/evaluation methods, which often restrict uncertainty sampling to fixed margin distances [23]. The additional uncertainty increase of 0.2 mm/cm distance to spine in only the posterior–anterior direction indicated that spine distance was only a minor contribution [18].

Applying a PTV margin recipe to the ITV is most likely overly conservative as the PTV margin is not independent of the ITV itself, leading to large margins [[5], [18]]. Local control using small margins was both reported equal to tracking [[10], [13]] and inferior [11]. Low frequency occurring events can offer an explanation, combined with an interaction between applied margin, PTV-based optimization and dose calculation algorithm: some methods artificially lead to larger GTV dose [12]. The ΔD98%>10% differences were in a case-by-case basis linked to anatomical deformations, baseline breathing level, respiratory amplitude changes or a combination that affect the spatial relationship between the tumor and the spine. The breathing amplitude lead to an issue for margins: if the breathing amplitude was higher during planning CT then no additional margin was required, but inverse it would, which could lead to higher margins for all patients. For the fractions with ΔD98%>10%, three patients each presented two problematic fractions indicating an issue with the baseline planning 4D-CT with initial very high respiratory amplitude (>2.2 cm) and/or positioning issue. Therefore a second offline 4D planning CT could be advised, as performed in the initial cohort of the SPACE study [13]. This is particularly the case for inferior lobe lesions, where shifts in thoracic-to-abdominal breathing patterns can alter tumor–spine alignment [[30], [31]]. This could also be mitigated with surface-guidance or mechanically assisted ventilation [[32], [33]]. A limitation of the current study is that 4D calculations were based on the 4D-CBCT snapshot while changes may occur during treatment, which could improve or degrade dose also [34]. For superior lobe lesions, suboptimal arm or shoulder positioning occasionally caused local deformations, reducing tumor–spine position reproducibility. In this cohort, 4D-CBCT positioning without a thermoplastic mask mitigated this effect. For 0-view, shoulder immobilization or surface guidance therefore appears recommended [35].

The sampled dataset suggests that when tumor visualization or tracking is unavailable, the following mitigation methods could be applied: additional off-line 4DCT, surface-guidance usage, fractionation and using 5/7/7mm margins as large underdosage risk mitigation if lung adverse effects are not a limiting factor.
