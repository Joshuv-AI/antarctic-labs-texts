Caring beyond the procedure: a qualitative study on thoracic surgery nurses’ perspectives on chronic illness experiences of individuals with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy
Authors: Malene Missel, Pernille Orloff Donsel, Trine Fjællegaard Nielsen, Erik Lilja Secher, Estephani Birkenfeldt Zeballos Medeiros, Katrine Rude, Joan Lilja Sunnleyg Højgaard, Niels-Erik Viby, Simone Birnbaum, Linda Kahr Andersen, Rene Horsleben Petersen, Nanna Witting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore how thoracic surgical nurses perceive and respond to the chronic illness needs of individuals with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy.MethodsData were collected through four focus group interviews using a semi-structured interview guide. The analysis followed a hermeneutic approach, identifying recurring themes through iterative interpretation and critical reflection. Nineteen thoracic surgical nurses from ward and postanaesthesia care unit settings participated, representing a diverse range of clinical experience. The study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for reporting the findings of this study.ResultsThe analysis identified three overarching themes reflecting nurses’ experiences of caring for individuals with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy. First, nurses described how the chronic aspects of the illness often ‘disappeared’ within the surgical care process, with attention focused mainly on procedural recovery, leaving patients’ broader illness needs unaddressed. Second, nurses experienced a dilemma between providing acute surgical care and responding to patients’ chronic illness understanding, highlighting uncertainty due to limited knowledge and lack of clear guidelines. Third, nurses emphasised the difficulty of managing the invisible and unpredictable symptoms of myasthenia gravis, which created a heightened need for vigilance but also uncertainty in symptom assessment and support. Across all themes, the findings suggest that surgical care practices may insufficiently recognise patients’ ongoing illness experiences, emphasising the need for greater knowledge, interdisciplinary collaboration and chronic illness-sensitive care approaches.ConclusionsThe study highlights the need for increased attention to chronic illness needs in surgical care, supporting interdisciplinary collaboration and tailored nursing practices that address the lived experiences of patients with chronic conditions.Keywords: Chronic Disease, Neuromuscular disease, Nursing Care, Qualitative Research, Thoracic surgery

Background

Chronic illness is a growing global health challenge, driven by demographic changes such as population ageing and increased life expectancy.1,3 Chronic illnesses are typically defined as conditions that persist for an extended period and require long-term and systematic approaches to treatment.1 4 This includes a wide range of conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes and autoimmune diseases.5 Autoimmune conditions like multiple sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus and myasthenia gravis (MG) exemplify the complexity of chronic illness trajectories, as they often involve unpredictable symptom fluctuations, complex treatments and significant impacts on daily life.6,11 Living with such chronic illnesses involves continuous adjustments to bodily changes, and thereby altered life trajectories.12,14 The impact also extends beyond the physical domain, affecting emotional, social and existential aspects of life.15,17 Despite their complexity, the healthcare system tends to approach autoimmune conditions primarily within medical specialties, with limited integration across other care settings. This can result in insufficient recognition of their broader impact in surgical and procedural contexts.18 19 In surgical settings in particular, care is typically structured around technical procedures, acute recovery and efficiency targets.20,24 In such fast-paced surgical environments, the broader illness context of patients with autoimmune conditions may become overshadowed by immediate surgical concerns, leaving little space to address the lived experience of chronic illness.25 This creates a risk that surgery-focused care pathways fail to fully recognise or accommodate patients’ complex, fluctuating needs—even when the surgery itself directly targets the underlying disease, as in the case of MG.19 26

MG presents a unique case among chronic autoimmune conditions, as thymectomy—a surgical procedure to remove the thymus gland—is often performed with the goal of improving disease symptoms.27,32 This contrasts with many other autoimmune diseases, where surgery is not part of standard treatment. The dual role of thymectomy as both a technical intervention and a potential means of altering disease trajectory places a particular responsibility on surgical teams to understand and respond to the chronic, fluctuating and often invisible nature of MG. In the perioperative setting, MG is associated with specific clinical concerns, including respiratory muscle weakness, increased sensitivity to anaesthetic agents and a risk of postoperative respiratory complications or myasthenic crisis.33,40 These risks require careful monitoring, timely symptom recognition and close interdisciplinary coordination throughout the surgical pathway, placing particular demands on surgical nursing practice.

Understanding how surgical nurses perceive, interpret and respond to these ongoing illness needs is crucial for enhancing patient-centred care in surgical environments.41 Thoracic surgical nurses play a key role in managing the complex care of patients with MG undergoing thymectomy, navigating both the acute perioperative challenges and the chronic, fluctuating nature of the disease.42 43 However, individuals with MG represent only a small subset of the overall surgical population,28 and thoracic surgical departments often care for diverse patient groups, such as those with lung cancer, trauma and/or other complex thoracic conditions. This diversity can make it challenging for nurses to stay informed about specific diseases and to recognise the unique clinical and existential needs of individuals living with MG. Without sustained awareness and tailored care strategies, there is a risk that patients’ ongoing illness experiences may be overshadowed by procedural priorities, resulting in fragmented and insufficiently responsive care.1944,46

Despite growing recognition of the importance of comprehensive care models,41 47 limited research has explored how chronic illnesses such as MG are understood, experienced and addressed within surgical nursing practice. This also raises broader questions about the role and limits of the healthcare system. To what extent can—and should—surgical teams address the wider, often existential, dimensions of chronic illness? Where does the balance lie between institutional responsibility and the individual’s own adaptation to living with chronic illness? These are essential considerations when evaluating how surgical pathways can support or constrain patients’ ability to manage chronic conditions in the context of acute care. While patient outcomes and surgical techniques for thymectomy have been studied, there is a notable lack of research on how surgical nurses engage with the chronic illness dimensions of MG in clinical practice.

AIM

To explore how thoracic surgical nurses perceive and respond to the chronic illness needs of individuals with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy.

Methods

Design and theoretical framework

This qualitative study was guided by Gadamer’s hermeneutic philosophy, emphasising that human experiences are accessed through interpretation rather than direct observation.48 A hermeneutic approach informed the entire research process, including data collection, analysis and interpretation. To deepen the interpretation of the nurses’ experiences, the analysis was further inspired by phenomenological and narrative understandings of chronic illness. Carel’s concept of bodily disruption49 50 was used to explore how chronic illness unsettles a person’s embodied sense of normality and how it alters their ability to trust their body and navigate everyday life, while Frank’s notion of illness narratives51 52 provided a framework for understanding how patients’ stories of illness and struggle may be disrupted or overlooked within surgical contexts. These theoretical perspectives were integrated into the analysis and offered an additional lens for understanding how nurses perceived and responded to patients’ ongoing illness needs beyond the focus of the surgical care process.

Patient and public involvement

This study incorporated patient perspectives through two avenues.53 54 First, the first author, who is living with MG, contributed an insider perspective throughout the development, analysis and interpretation of the study. Second, an external patient with MG, not otherwise involved in the research team, provided feedback on the conceptualisation and focus of the study, contributed to the interpretation of findings to ensure trustworthiness and approved the final interpretations and conclusions. Their involvement supported the relevance and resonance of the findings for both clinical practice and the patient perspective. No external patients were involved in recruitment or data collection.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at Copenhagen University Hospital—Rigshospitalet, the largest specialised thoracic surgery department in Denmark, with 36 beds for elective and acute thoracic surgery and a dedicated postanaesthesia care unit (PACU). The department serves as the national centre for thymus surgery, performing approximately 60–70 thymectomies annually, with over 80% conducted using minimally invasive techniques (video-assisted thoracic surgery or single port robotic-assisted thoracic surgery). Patients with MG are referred for thymectomy following multidisciplinary evaluation and remain under the joint care of the thoracic surgery and neuromuscular departments throughout the pathway.

Nurses were recruited through an invitation posted in the department, providing information about the study aim and methodology. Interested nurses contacted the department’s clinical nurse specialist (POD), who, together with the senior researcher (MM), coordinated participation and scheduling of the focus groups. No nurses actively declined participation; however, it is possible that some eligible nurses chose not to respond to the invitation. The final sample of 19 nurses represented approximately half of the nursing staff in the department and reflected a broad range of clinical experience across ward and PACU settings.

A purposive sampling strategy55 was applied to ensure variation in clinical experience, roles and exposure to MG care. The composition and size of the focus groups were influenced by practical considerations such as shift schedules. Participants were professionally familiar with the clinical nurse specialist facilitating recruitment and with the senior researcher, who has lived experience of MG and had previously undergone thymectomy at the department. Participants had thoracic surgical nursing experience ranging from 0.5 to 25 years (median 8 years).

Data collection

Focus group interviews55 56 were used to collect data, allowing nurses to share their experiences of caring for individuals with MG undergoing thymectomy. A total of four focus group interviews were conducted between June and July 2024. The focus groups differed in composition. One group consisted primarily of more experienced nurses (n=7), one of less experienced nurses (n=4) and one mixed group (n=4). Nurses from the PACU constituted a separate focus group (n=4) due to their distinct clinical role. Group sizes were determined pragmatically based on availability, clinical workload and shift schedules at the time of data collection. No participants took part in more than one focus group interview. While hierarchical relationships may influence group dynamics, the variations in group composition contributed to different forms of dialogue, ranging from experience-based descriptions to reflective discussions. These differences were considered analytically. The interviews were moderated by researchers with clinical nursing and qualitative research expertise (MM, POD and KR), including the first author, whose lived experience with the condition informed the interview process, particularly in recognising clinically meaningful descriptions that might otherwise be taken for granted, and in formulating follow-up questions grounded in shared clinical understanding.57,59 Participants were aware of the first author’s dual role as researcher and patient prior to the interviews. To manage the dual researcher–patient role during data collection, the interviews were conducted using a semi-structured guide with open-ended questions to facilitate discussions and encourage reflection on clinical experiences (interview guide available in box 1). Moderation was shared among researchers to support reflexivity and balance during the discussions. The interview guide was developed to provide thematic orientation rather than fixed questioning and was used flexibly throughout the focus group interviews. Consistent with a hermeneutic approach, questions were adapted during the interviews to follow participants’ accounts, clarify meanings and explore emerging issues. Minor adjustments were made in phrasing and probing as data collection progressed, informed by ongoing interpretation and reflexive discussions within the research team.

Guiding interview questions

Can you describe how you experience caring for patients with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy, particularly in relation to their ongoing illness needs?

In what ways, if any, does the care of patients with myasthenia gravis differ from the care of other thoracic surgical patients?

Can you describe any particular challenges you encounter when caring for patients living with myasthenia gravis during the surgical pathway, and how you usually respond to these challenges?

Can you share an experience that stands out, either positive or challenging, in caring for a patient with myasthenia gravis?

How would you describe the role and responsibilities of a thoracic surgical nurse when caring for patients with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy?

How does your knowledge, or uncertainty, about myasthenia gravis and its treatment influence the way you assess, support and communicate with these patients?

Interviews lasted between 45 and 60 min, were audio-recorded with permission and transcribed verbatim for analysis. Efforts were made to create a supportive environment to enable nurses to openly reflect on clinical practices that are often performed routinely without conscious articulation. The focus group format encouraged collective reflection, promoting deeper insights into the challenges and considerations involved in nursing care for this patient group. The sample size was guided by the concept of information power, as described by Malterud et al,60 whereby fewer participants are required when the study aim is narrow, the sample is specific and the data are rich and relevant to the research question. The four focus groups included nurses with varying levels of clinical experience and from different care contexts, generating complementary perspectives on caring for individuals with myasthenia gravis undergoing thymectomy. Across the focus groups, recurring patterns and themes were identified, supporting thematic sufficiency for the study aim. In line with the information power framework, data collection was concluded after four focus groups because the study aim was specific, the sample was highly relevant and experienced in the phenomenon under study, and the focus group dialogues were rich, nuanced and analytically informative across different clinical contexts.

Data analysis

The analysis followed a hermeneutic approach inspired by Gadamer,48 focusing on interpretation and understanding rather than mechanical coding. Two researchers (MM and POD) independently and repeatedly read all interview transcripts to gain an in-depth understanding of the content. Through iterative interpretation, patterns and recurring themes across the interviews were identified. Data interpretation moved continuously between the individual parts of the text and the overall emerging understanding, reflecting the hermeneutic circle.61 Themes were developed as interpretive constructions that emerged through movement between parts and whole, informed by the researchers’ preunderstandings and ongoing reflexive dialogue, in line with hermeneutic principles. Interpretive differences between researchers were addressed through critical dialogue until a shared understanding was reached. Participants were not invited to comment on or validate the interpretations, as member checking was not considered consistent with the hermeneutic approach guiding the study. The themes captured the nurses’ perspectives on caring for individuals with MG undergoing thymectomy. Final interpretations were critically discussed and refined in collaboration with all authors, drawing on their diverse clinical and academic backgrounds—including expertise in thoracic surgery and anaesthesiology, neurology, nursing and physiotherapy—as well as input from a patient with lived experience of MG. This collaborative approach enhanced the trustworthiness and depth of the analysis. Interpretative rigour was supported by continuous dialogue among authors, iterative rereading and reflexive engagement with the data, enhancing the depth and credibility of the findings.55

Interviews were conducted in Danish. Selected quotations were translated into English by the first author and checked by coauthors to ensure accuracy and preservation of meaning. No qualitative data analysis software was used in the analytical process.

Results

The analysis identified three overarching themes that collectively illuminate nurses’ experiences of caring for individuals with MG undergoing thymectomy. While analytically distinct, the themes are interrelated and reflect overlapping dimensions of nurses’ experiences—from systemic tendencies to overlook chronic illness to the individual uncertainty and limitations encountered in daily practice. To support the interpretation of nurses’ experiences, theoretical perspectives on illness needs inspired by Carel’s phenomenological philosophy of illness and Frank’s work on illness narratives were used as interpretive lenses. Overview of themes, key findings and theoretical perspectives

Chronic illness ‘disappears’ within the surgical focus

The nurses described how MG, as a chronic illness, tended to be overlooked or even forgotten within the surgical pathway. Their attention was primarily directed towards managing the immediate surgical aspects—chest drains, pain control and rapid discharge—while the patients’ illness history and specific needs were not systematically addressed. As one nurse noted: ‘They’ve just had surgery, and then they are expected to reach out themselves if anything happens—even though they come with a long illness history. “Surely it should be our responsibility while they are with us”’.

Several nurses expressed that they lacked knowledge about MG and therefore hesitated to address it in conversations with patients: ‘I have to admit, for the patients I’ve seen in preoperative consultations, I haven’t really brought up the disease, because I didn’t know what to ask. I just assumed the patient had it under control’. This narrowing of focus to the surgical procedure can be understood through Carel’s49 50 conceptualisation of illness as a disruption of both body and existence, an alteration in a person’s fundamental being-in-the-world. When this deeper meaning of illness is not acknowledged, patients risk feeling existentially negated. Their suffering, vulnerability and lived experiences are dismissed as irrelevant. A PACU nurse reflected how this neglect began as early as admission: ‘When they get to the ward, there are some who don’t even know the patient has myasthenia. They just see that it’s a VATS procedure’.

Frank’s51 theory of illness narratives further elucidates this dynamic, suggesting that patients often navigate both struggle and quest narratives. However, these illness stories tend to be silenced or marginalised when the surgical trajectory dominates. As one nurse reflected: ‘Patients come in here and might actually know more about the disease than we do, but we are only focused on the surgery and the chest drain’. The findings suggest a need for a more integrated approach in which the chronic illness is recognised as central, not peripheral, throughout the surgical pathway. Nurses emphasised the importance of acknowledging MG during preoperative consultations, hospitalisation and at discharge, to better support the patients’ actual needs and foster continuity in care. This oversight highlights the tension between surgical priorities and patients’ broader illness needs.

Nurses’ dilemma between acute surgical care and chronic illness understanding

The nurses described a recurring dilemma; how to meet patients’ immediate postoperative needs while also accounting for their chronic illness. Many expressed that limited knowledge of MG’s disease trajectory, treatment and possible complications led to uncertainty in both care provision and communication. As one nurse put it: ‘I think you need to know a lot about it to feel confident, so as a new nurse, I don’t feel safe taking care of these patients’. This uncertainty was compounded by the absence of shared guidelines and clear protocols for addressing MG within the surgical context: ‘I wouldn’t know what to do if they got worse. I would just have to look it up or call someone’.

According to Carel’s49 50 theory of illness, disease can cause an existential disruption, undermining a person’s confidence in their body and their ability to navigate the world. When nurses are unable to respond to this existential vulnerability, because their focus remains solely on surgical stability, patients may be left alone with the confusing and destabilising effects of their illness. One nurse captured this fragmentation: ‘We focus on the surgery going well, but what about the illness? It’s just forgotten’.

Frank’s51 52 narrative theory further illuminates this challenge; illness narratives allow patients to make sense of uncertainty and reassert control. Yet when these narratives are met with silence or deflection by healthcare professionals, the sense of struggle may be intensified. Another nurse reflected: ‘They ask a lot about their medication, but we can’t really answer. We just tell them to contact their neurologist. It doesn’t feel right’. Thus, the nurses found themselves caught between a procedure-focused practice and a broader understanding of chronic illness. This dilemma points to a deeper structural and professional gap, one that cannot be solved at the individual level alone. This uncertainty is not simply a matter of individual knowledge gaps; it reflects a systemic lack of support for integrating chronic illness care within surgical pathways.

Invisible symptoms and unpredictable illness trajectories

The nurses highlighted how symptoms associated with MG, such as fatigue, muscle weakness and respiratory difficulties, were often invisible and unpredictable. This made them difficult to observe, assess and respond to. As one nurse explained: ‘You don’t know when they might deteriorate. They can suddenly develop breathing problems without any warning’. The invisibility of symptoms created a state of constant vigilance: ‘I’m always keeping an extra eye on them, but it’s difficult because you can’t always see when something worsens’. Several nurses expressed uncertainty when it came to symptom management and medication adjustments: ‘We don’t always know the best way to support them if they get worse. We simply lack knowledge and a clear plan’. The combination of invisible symptoms and a fluctuating disease trajectory fostered a sense of latent insecurity and ongoing preparedness in nursing care. While the nurses remained highly alert, they often lacked the knowledge or tools to act proactively, reinforcing their sense of limitation.

Carel’s49 conceptualisation of illness as an existential disruption is particularly relevant here. For patients with MG, unpredictable bodily changes undermine their sense of autonomy and reinforce their vulnerability. These disruptions affect not only physical functioning but also their broader sense of identity. From Frank’s51 narrative perspective, the unpredictable nature of MG symptoms can be seen as narrative ruptures—breaks in patients’ efforts to construct meaning and maintain coherence in their illness experience. When healthcare professionals overlook these disruptions or fail to respond meaningfully, the patient’s struggle and existential insecurity may be exacerbated. One nurse observed: ‘Some patients are used to having control over their disease, but when they are operated on, they lose some of that control, and we can’t always catch them because we don’t fully know how’.

To better support patients, nurses emphasised the need for more knowledge, clearer clinical guidance and closer interdisciplinary collaboration: ‘We need some sort of guidance; What should we be especially attentive to? What does it mean if they develop more double vision? Or become more fatigued? These are things we need to be able to ask about’. This theme illustrates how the invisible and fluctuating nature of MG places complex and often unacknowledged demands on nurses, both in terms of clinical surveillance and existential care. These invisible symptoms demand a form of clinical and existential vigilance that is often unrecognised in surgical care. Addressing these challenges requires not only improved education and organisational support but also a broader recognition of the existential dimensions of chronic illness within surgical settings.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

This study sheds light on how thoracic surgical nurses perceive and respond to the ongoing illness needs of patients with MG undergoing thymectomy. The findings demonstrate that within the dominant surgical focus on technical success and rapid recovery, patients’ lived experiences of chronic illness—marked by uncertainty, vulnerability and fluctuating symptoms—tend to become marginalised. Nurses expressed dilemmas between addressing immediate postoperative tasks and recognising patients’ broader illness experiences, particularly in the absence of specific knowledge, interdisciplinary guidance and structured support. The study identifies three interconnected challenges: the invisibility of chronic illness in surgical care, the professional uncertainty this creates for nurses and the demands of managing unpredictable symptoms without clear frameworks. These findings highlight the need for an integrated approach in surgical care that bridges the gap between procedural focus and the ongoing needs of patients living with chronic illness.

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

A key strength of this study is its integration of both insider and professional perspectives. The first author’s lived experience with MG contributed to the design, data collection and interpretation of findings, enabling a more nuanced understanding of patient needs.54 57 59 Additionally, the inclusion of an external individual with MG in the interpretive process and authorship enhanced the trustworthiness and relevance of the analysis through patient and public involvement (PPI).53 54 62 However, no patient interview data were collected as part of the empirical material, and the study therefore reflects nurses’ perspectives on caring for individuals with MG undergoing thymectomy. While patient partners contributed through PPI in the interpretation and authorship process, the absence of patient-generated interview data represents a limitation of the study.

The first author’s dual role as both researcher and patient may also introduce a potential for bias, as personal experiences can influence interpretation. This potential for bias was addressed through systematic reflexive dialogue, explicit critical discussions and collaborative analysis within a multidisciplinary author team.63 64 Furthermore, the study is limited by its focus on a single national context and a specific subgroup of nurses working with thoracic surgical patients. While this provides depth, it may limit the transferability of findings to other specialties or healthcare systems.55 Further research involving interdisciplinary teams and broader patient groups is warranted.

Strengths and weaknesses in relation to other studies

While the psychosocial aspects of chronic illness have been extensively examined in long-term care settings such as oncology,65 66 neurology67 68 and palliative care,69 70 few studies have explored how these needs are addressed—or neglected—within surgical pathways. Most existing research in surgical contexts has focused on clinical outcomes, such as postoperative complications, discharge planning or enhanced recovery protocols.71,74 However, our findings and previous research suggest that the effects of chronic illness do not pause during surgical care. Patients continue to experience uncertainty, emotional vulnerability and a need for understanding and support; needs that often remain unrecognised within a fast-paced, surgery-centred framework.19 Studies support this by illustrating how patient concerns and psychological needs are frequently overlooked in surgical settings.75 Similarly, it is reported how nurses across settings experience role strain when emotional and relational needs conflict with institutional priorities.76 77 Our findings support and extend these observations, illustrating how the lived reality of chronic illness—such as fluctuating symptoms, uncertainty and identity disruption—can remain invisible within a highly procedural care model.

The integration of Carel’s49 50 and Frank’s51 52 theories into our analysis adds further nuance by framing these tensions as not only professional, but existential and narrative in nature. Few previous studies have applied such frameworks in surgical care,78,80 and to the best of our knowledge, no prior research has specifically explored nursing experiences related to MG in this context. Our findings thus expand current knowledge by showing how nurses experience chronic illness as both clinically and existentially under-addressed in fast-paced surgical settings.

Meaning of the study: implications for clinicians and policymakers

The findings highlight the importance of recognising and addressing the ongoing needs of patients living with chronic illness during surgical care. This is particularly relevant for individuals with conditions such as MG, where symptoms and challenges extend beyond the immediate surgical event.19 32 38 81 Nurses emphasised the need for greater support through clinical education, clear guidelines and interdisciplinary collaboration to feel confident in recognising and responding to these needs. In practice, this may include MG-focused educational initiatives for surgical nurses, shared neurology–thoracic surgery guidelines, and the use of structured tools such as the MG-Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) scale to support dialogue at admission and discharge. At the organisational level, there is value in considering how surgical pathways can be designed with greater focus on patients’ illness experiences, concerns and vulnerabilities. Acknowledging and integrating these aspects into care planning can strengthen continuity, patient satisfaction and trust in care transitions,82 ultimately improving surgical outcomes for patients living with chronic illness. At the same time, these findings raise broader questions about the role and limits of the healthcare system. While it is crucial that patients feel met and recognised—even in procedure-focused environments—there is also a need to consider what can reasonably be expected of surgical teams whose primary expertise lies in technical care. This underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and role clarity, as well as a broader societal dialogue about the shared responsibility between healthcare systems and individuals in living with chronic illness.

Unanswered questions and future research

Further research is needed to explore how surgical teams can systematically integrate chronic illness perspectives into fast-track surgical pathways. Studies involving patients’ own narratives would provide valuable insight into how their needs evolve and how these can be met more effectively. Moreover, comparative research across different chronic illnesses could help determine how generalisable these challenges are, and whether illness-specific strategies or broader structural changes are required. Finally, studies evaluating interventions aimed at increasing nurses’ preparedness to handle both surgical and chronic care dimensions may help bridge the gap identified in this study. Future research should examine the impact of educational interventions, clinical guidelines and structural changes designed to enhance surgical teams’ capacity to address the complex, ongoing needs of patients living with chronic illness. Additionally, studies could explore how to define realistic and meaningful expectations of surgical care in the context of chronic illness, balancing professional responsibilities with patients’ own role in managing life with long-term conditions.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on how surgical care pathways risk marginalising the complex and personal illness needs of individuals with MG undergoing thymectomy. While the surgical success is often foregrounded, patients’ experiences of vulnerability, uncertainty and the fluctuating nature of their condition tend to remain peripheral in surgical clinical practice. By incorporating theoretical perspectives from Carel and Frank, it becomes visible how illness experiences remain a silent and invisible dimension of care, risking that patients’ vulnerability and existential struggles are not met with sufficient understanding and support.
