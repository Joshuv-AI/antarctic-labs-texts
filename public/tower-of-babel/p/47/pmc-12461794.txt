Persistent Low‐Output Neonatal Chylothorax: How Long Are We Justified to Wait for Pleurodesis in Partial Responders?
Authors: G Rollo, A Zarfati, G Burini, L Valfre, C Iacusso, I Capolupo, B D Iacobelli, P Bagolan, A Conforti, F Fusaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThe aim of our study was to review our experience regarding the management of neonatal chylothorax, with particular focus on outcomes of partial responders.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of neonatal chylothorax cases between January 2015 and December 2023. First‐line management included drainage, fasting, parenteral nutrition, and octreotide. Good responders had a drainage output of <2 ml/kg/day after 1 week; partial responders had 2–10 ml/kg/day, and non‐responders had >10 ml/kg/day or >100 ml/day for 5 days with complications. Partial responders continued conservative management, while non‐responders received second‐line treatment with bedside iodopovidone chemical pleurodesis.ResultsThirty‐five newborns (15 females, 20 males) were diagnosed with chylothorax, 11 congenital and 24 acquired (18 CDH, 5 esophageal atresia, 1 caval thrombosis). After first‐line treatment, 7 (20%) were good responders, 19 (54%) were partial responders, and 9 (26%) were non‐responders. Non‐responders received iodopovidone pleurodesis, which resolved the condition in all cases (median time to resolution was 4 days). Complications during hospitalization included 8 cases of sepsis (1 fatal), 5 of atelectasis (4 related to pleurodesis), and 4 venous thromboses. Partial responders had significantly more sepsis (p = 0.029) but less atelectasis (p = 0.025) compared to non‐responders, with similar resolution times and hospital stays. After a median follow‐up of 3 years, there were no recurrences or thyroid dysfunction in the iodopovidone group.ConclusionsFirst‐line conservative management, although initially appropriate, can lead to serious morbidity if prolonged. Timely second‐line treatment for partial responders with low‐output chylothorax can minimize morbidity and mortality. Bedside chemical pleurodesis with iodopovidone is a safe and effective second‐line treatment for refractory neonatal chylothorax.Keywords: chylothorax, conservative treatment, iodopovidone, pleurodesis, refractory chylothorax

Introduction

Neonatal chylothorax represents a pleomorphic, multifactorial, sometimes life‐threatening condition [1]. The lymphatic leakage in the pleural cavity may cause respiratory distress, hypovolemia, hypoproteinemia, electrolyte imbalance, metabolic acidosis, lymphocyte depletion, hypogammaglobulinemia, immunodeficiency, and coagulation disorders [2, 3, 4]. The diagnosis is confirmed with pleural fluid analysis [2, 5].

First‐line management is based on Medium‐Chain Fatty acids (MCT), skimmed human milk diet, or total parenteral nutrition (TPN) with or without octreotide administration [4, 5]. In the case of medium‐large pleural effusion associated with respiratory symptoms, further procedures such as needle line management, on the basis of lymphatic output, patients can be categorized into three categories: good responders <2 ml/kg/day, partial responders 2–10 ml/kg/day, and non‐responders >10 ml/kg/day or >100 ml/day for at least 5 days in the presence of complications [1]. In our experience, if the management of good and non‐responders is standardized, consisting of the continuation of conservative therapy in the former and treatment in the latter (chemical pleurodesis, thoracic duct ligation, pleuroperitoneal shunt placement, and pleurectomy), the management of partial responders remains challenging [1]. For this group of patients, the literature suggests 3–4 weeks of conservative treatment before considering an invasive approach [1, 6]. However, the persistent lymphatic leak, the prolonged fasting, and the presence of a central venous line and TPN may expose these patients to potential life‐threatening complications, in particular sepsis [3]. At our institution, we have consistently observed that partial responders face a more challenging path than non‐responders, and we have successfully treated them early with bedside chemical pleurodesis using iodopovidone, leading to positive outcomes [7]. Over a decade ago, we implemented iodopovidone for chemical pleurodesis, with favorable results, as documented by other institutions worldwide [6, 8, 9].

Our hypothesis was that this difference between partial responders and non‐responders may be attributed to the different delay and type of approaches after failure of conservative first‐line treatment (3‐4 weeks of bowel rest and TPN, vs. bedside chemical pleurodesis with iodopovidone). However, to this day, this aspect has never been studied in detail.

The aim of our study was to review our experience regarding the management of neonatal chylothorax, with particular focus on outcomes of partial responders.

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed records of newborns affected by chylothorax from January 2015 to December 2023, treated at our tertiary center (Neonatal and Pediatric Surgery, Bambino Gesù Children's Hospital, IRCCS, Rome, Italy). Diagnosis of pleural effusion was made by chest X‐ray, lung ultrasound and thoracentesis/thoracic drainage [10]. Chylothorax was defined as pleural fluid with ≥1000 white blood cells per µL, with ≥70–80% of lymphocytes, and a triglyceride ≥110 mg/dl (or 1.1 mmol/L) [11]. The initial conservative approach consisted of pleural drainage associated with nutritional measures (oral MCT milk or fasting with TPN) and continuous octreotide infusion [1, 12, 13].

We defined a good response as reduction of the total drainage output to <2 ml/kg/day after 1 week of therapy, a partial response as a drainage output of 2–10 ml/kg/day, and nonresponse as drainage >10 ml/kg/day after 1 week of therapy or >100 ml/day for at least 5 days with metabolic or nutritional complications [1]. The patient's response to therapy was assessed using chest X‐ray and/or lung ultrasound (Figure 1).

patient with left sided neonatal chylothorax at presentation. (A) X‐ray. (B) ultrasound.

Chemical povidone‐iodine pleurodesis was used in non‐responders and partial responders when chylous leak persisted more than 3 weeks. Technical details of povidone‐iodine pleurodesis are previously described [7]. This procedure is performed at the bedside without requiring general anesthesia at our institution. The technique was conducted using a saline solution mixed with 4% povidone‐iodine solution. The liquid was administered into the pleural space at a dosage of 2 ml/kg via the pre‐placed chest tube. The chest tube was occluded for 4 h and subsequently reopened to drain the pleural cavity. A transient respiratory distress is expected due to the pleural inflammatory reaction that usually resolves in 24–48 h [7].

The data collected and analyzed were clinical, laboratory, radiological, and surgical, as well as post‐discharge follow‐up and outcomes. In the lack of a validated classification of postoperative complications in pediatric surgical patients, the Clavien–Dindo classification was used [14, 15].

Exclusion criteria included patients with incomplete clinical data, unclear diagnosis, or chylothorax diagnosed beyond the neonatal period.

We performed a pairwise comparative analysis between patients with a good response and the rest of the cohort (partial responders and non‐responders). We realized a further comparative sub‐analysis between partial responders and nonresponder patients. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and continuous variables as median and range. The Chi‐Square test or the Fisher‐exact test (depending on group size) was used to compare categorical variables. For continuous variables, differences were assessed using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. All p‐values were two‐sided, and the value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 37 newborns were treated for chylothorax. Two patients were excluded due to incomplete clinical data, resulting in a total of 35 patients included in the study. Of these, 15 (42%) were female and 20 (58%) were male. The median Gestational Age (GA) was 37 weeks (range: 28–41 weeks); 13/35 (37%) patients were preterm. The median birth weight (BW) was 2950 g (range: 670‐3670 g); 1 patient was ELBW, 1 (3%) VLBW, and 8 (23%) LBW. One patient had EPHB syndrome, one other patient had thrombosis of the superior vena cava, and one patient had micro‐duplication of CHRM1. The chylothorax was left‐sided in 18 (53%) cases, right‐sided in 11 (31%), and bilateral in 6 (16%) (Figure 1). Overall, 11 (32%) patients had congenital chylothorax, 24 (68%) were acquired (23 postoperative and 1 caval thrombosis). In the postoperative group, 18 patients presented chylothorax after Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia (CDH) repair, and 5 (22%) patients after Esophageal Atresia (EA) repair. The CDH were left sided in all cases. After diagnosis, the first week therapeutic approach was conservative in all patients. In 10 cases an MCT‐milk‐based diet alone was used, while 25 were treated with fasting, TPN, and continuous infusion of octreotide. Regarding the volume reduction of lymphatic leak the patients were divided into three groups: 7 (20%) good responders, 19 (54%) partial responders, and 9 (26%) non‐responders. In good responders, complete resolution was obtained after a median of 21 days (range: 2–29 days). Instead, partial responders needed a median of 28 days (range: 18–66 days) for resolution. Non‐responders were treated with iodopovidone after the first‐line management failure. The bedside procedure was performed out at a median age of 36 days (range: 27–67 days). One patient needed a further second iodopovidone treatment. The chemical pleurodesis with iodopovidone was effective on resolution of the effusion for all our patients, with a median time to resolution of 4 days (range: 2–8 days).

Overall, the study population had a global median time for resolution of the chylothorax of 26 days (range: 2–66 days). Furthermore, after diagnosis of the chylothorax, the median hospital stay was 40.5 days (range: 2–97 days). Regarding treatment complications, 8 patients developed sepsis, 5 atelectasis, 4 venous thromboses, and 2 intestinal occlusions. One patient in the partial response group did not survive. The patient developed severe sepsis with septic shock that degenerated into multiple organ failure after 40 days of diagnosis of chylothorax.

After discharge, the patients were followed up to a median age of 3 years (range: 0.8–11.5 years). No thyroid problem occurred during the follow‐up. No documented chylothorax recurrence occurred in our study population during the follow‐up period. However, one patient of the partial responder group experienced thrombosis of the superior vena cava with mild effusion at 70 days from discharge. The patient was treated with anticoagulative therapy without analysis of the pleural effusion.

We carried out an analysis comparing good responders and the rest of our cohort (partial responders and non‐responders), that is detailed in Table 1. The analyzed group had no differences regarding population and baseline data, such as perinatal data (prematurity, GA, BW, etc.), etiology, or side of the chylothorax. Furthermore, we found no differences regarding management and early outcomes, and complications. However, good responders had a significantly shorter duration of chylothorax (21 (2–29) vs. 28 (16–66): p = 0.039) and hospital stay (29 (2–47) vs. 46 (20–97): p = 0.029).

These subgroups were similar regarding baseline data such as gestational age, birth weight, and prematurity. However, in the analysis of etiology, the chylothorax was significantly more congenital in the non‐responders (2 (10%) vs. 5 (55%): p = 0.019) and significantly more postoperative in the partial responders (17 (89%) vs. 3 (33%): p = 0.048). Discussion

We documented a significant cohort of neonatal chylothorax managed at our tertiary center. We provided first‐line conservative treatment upon the confirmation of diagnosis for all patients. The good responders had a favorable and nearly straightforward improvement, resulting in full regression. Conversely, for the others, the journey was more arduous. We identified differences between partial and non‐responders. Indeed, the etiology differed, with a markedly higher incidence of postoperative chylothorax in the partial responders. Furthermore, partial responders exhibited a significantly higher incidence of sepsis. This discrepancy may be ascribed to the varying timeframes and methodologies employed following the ineffectiveness of conservative initial treatment. In fact, we treated partial responders with 3–4 weeks of bowel rest and TPN, while we treat non‐responders with bedside chemical pleurodesis using iodopovidone. In this last subgroup, our approach of chemical pleurodesis was efficient in achieving resolution for all patients, with a median time to resolution of 4 days.

The multidisciplinary treatment of newborn chylothorax presents challenges, even for experienced healthcare providers, especially when it becomes resistant to initial conservative management [2, 12, 16]. In current clinical practice two important aspect are still debated. Firstly, the appropriate timing for a more intervention is still matter of debate. Some clinicians now advocate a more aggressive intervention in cases lasting longer than three to 4 weeks, massive leaks or those with severe nutritional or metabolic alterations [1, 17]. In our study, we observed that more than half of the cohort (54%) had a partial response to conservative therapy. In these patients the reduction of the leak output was not consistent enough to remove the thoracic drainage and/or start refeeding. majority of clinicians recommend a period of three to 4 weeks of conservative therapy [1]. Two recent important systematic review gave raccomendation on the treatment of congenital and acquired neonatal chylothorax. Resch et al. reviewed all the congenital chylothorax and gave raccomendation with an algorithm that poses a strong emphasis on the antenatal treatment of congenital chylothorax. They agree, also, that a more invasive therapy was needed after 4–5 weeks of conservative therapy [6, 12]. Similarly, Rocha et al. wrote another important review that was almost contemporary, focalizing on both congenital and acquired neonatal chylothorax. Rocha et al. divided the newborn in groups based on the response to initial conservative therapy. A more invasive intervention was recommended for newborns with massive chyle leaks or metabolic derangament after 1 week of conservative treatment [1]. These reviews and proposed algorithm already provocated debate, Bellini et al. warned about the risk development of a more invasive posing doubts regarding the safety and long‐term effect of definitive intervention like pleurodesis and thoracic duct logation [6, 12, 13]. Nonetheless, Resch et al. showed that a prolonged cosnervative therapy especially in severely I'll infants with important chylothorax had a mortality rate between 30% and 70% [6, 17]. These reviews gave clear indications for the prenatal treatment and the treatment timing of refractory cases or newborn with a good response to conservative therapy. Our restrospective analysis aimed to better understand the group of newborn that poses the biggest challenge for clinical management, those who have a partial response to conservative treatment and if we are justify to propose a more invasive approach sooner than 4–5 weeks.

Our study findings confirm that keeping a patient with refractory chylothorax on prolonged conservative treatment can lead to severe morbidity and mortality. In fact, all the eight patients who developed sepsis in the cohort were in the partial responder group. This group had significantly more sepsis than the non‐responsive group. One of these sepsis was fatal. Therefore, the only mortality reported in our study was a patient from the group of partial responders. These findings align with the literature, which highlights how failure to resolve chylothorax with conservative management can lead to progressive deterioration in the patient's conditions [12].

Takahashi et al reported their experience with the management of refractory chylothorax in neonates with OK432 to perform pleurodesis with good outcomes. This group advocates for the use of pleurodesis as a first‐line treatment and to be performed 3 weeks or before in case of difficult metabolic derangements [18].

Secondly, the best treatment for refractory chylothorax hasn't been established yet. Thoracic duct ligation has traditionally been considered the main approach for refractory cases [19]. Recently, the use of Indocyanine Green (ICG) guided thoracic duct ligation for refractory chylothorax was recently reported. They also reported that pleurodesis wasn't their preferred method for chylothorax treatment for the adhesions in the thoracic cavity after failed pleurodesis can lead to difficulties in following thoracoscopic surgery [20]. The intranodal lipiodol injection is a promising minimally invasive technique that still has limited diffusion and applicability in newborns [21]. Chemical pleurodesis with iodopovidone has proven to be a safe and effective technique. Indeed, when used iodopovidone was successful in all patients, with a median time to pleurodesis of 4 days. Moreover, no documented chylothorax recurrence occurred in our study population during the hospitalization or the follow‐up period. Furthermore, our technique is a bedside tecnique that does not need for general anesthesia, contrary to the above‐mentioned techniques. This difference is crucial to limit morbidity and possible complications of general anesthesia in neonatal period. Previous studies have reported this use of iodopovidone is effective for resolution of chylothorax [8, 22, 23]. Although our study observed some transient respiratory insufficiency and atelectasis, it resolved without consequences during the follow‐up. The lack of serious complications, combined with the effectiveness of pleurodesis, makes this procedure a valid option in cases of refractory chylothorax.

Basing on our data and findings and the existing literature on the topic, we developed a protocol for the management of neonatal chylothorax with iodopovidone pleurodesisthat is presented in Figure 2. This proposal had three aims. Firstly, we'd like to redefine the timing of treatment in partial responders. Secondly, we advocate the use of bedside pleurodesis with iodopovidone as a preferred first line intervention. Ultimately, our objective was to facilitate debate on management of neonatal chylothorax among specialists from tertiary centers, including neonatologists, pediatricians in neonatal intensive care units, pediatric surgeons, and pediatric radiologists, to minimize mortality and morbidity of our patients.

The Bambino Gesù children's hospital management protocol for Neonatal chylothorax. MCT, Medium‐Chain Fatty; NG, Nasogastric; TPN, Total Parenteral Nutrition.

When a neonatal pleural effusion is suspect for chylothorax pleural fluid analysis is indicated. Thoracentesis or thoracic drainage is performed. White blood cells ≥1000 per µL, with ≥70%–80% lymphocytes, and Triglyceride ≥110 mg/dl (or 1.1 mmol/L) in patients fed through a NG tube are considered diagnostic of neonatal chylothorax. After confirmation of the diagnosis conservative management is started. Conservative treatment consisted in MCT‐milk‐based diet alone or bowel rest, TPN, and continuous infusion of octreotide. The drain is left in place to monitor the output. After 7 days of conservative treatment, good responders had resolution or a drainage output <2 ml/kg/day. Instead, partial responders had an output of 2–10 ml/kg/day. Partial responders are treated conservatively for other 10 days. After 10 further days of conservative treatment, in case of persistence treatment with bedside pleurodesis with iodopovidone is indicated. Finally, we considered nonresponder the patients with an output >10 ml/kg/day or >100 ml/day for 5 days with complications. In this subgroup, the pleurodesis is indicated in second‐line. We must examine and recognize the limitations of our study. The retrospective and monocentric study design stresses the strengths of the current study. We present a relatively large cohort of an extremely uncommon disease. Moreover, we did not perform any randomization. Further studies, possibly prospective and/or multicentric, are necessary to corroborate, validate and generalize our findings.

Conclusions

First‐line conservative management, although initially appropriate, can lead to serious morbidity in some patients if prolonged. Timely second‐line treatment in cases of in partial responders with low‐output chylothorax can minimize morbidity and mortality. Bedside chemical pleurodesis with iodopovidone is a safe and effective second‐line treatment for refractory neonatal chylothorax.
