Temporal dynamics and predictors of carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria in bloodstream infections in hematological patients
Authors: Meyha Sahin, Ali Mert, Ayse Istanbullu Tosun, Ezgi Yilmaz, Hüseyin Saffet Bekoz, Leylagul Kaynar, Bahadir Ceylan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by carbapenem-resistant (CR) Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) represent a critical challenge in febrile neutropenia (FN). This study investigated temporal resistance trends, clinical features, and predictors of CR-GNB BSIs in hematology patients over a 10-year period.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted on GNB BSIs in febrile neutropenic patients hospitalized between 2014 and 2025. Microbiologic, demographic, and clinical data were collected and analyzed.ResultsA total of 604 patients with hematologic malignancies experienced 975 FN episodes with microbiologically confirmed BSIs, yielding 1096 isolates. Of these episodes, 57.5% occurred in males, with a median patient age of 49.5 years (IQR: 34–60.5). GNB accounted for 732 (66.9%) isolates. Among all GN isolates, 22.8% were CR-GNB. CR-GNB BSIs were significantly more common during Period 1 (OR: 0.492; 95% CI: 0.277–0.876 for Period 2; OR: 0.496; 95% CI: 0.285–0.864 for Period 3; OR: 0.455; 95% CI: 0.258–0.804 for Period 4). Independent predictor factors for CR-GNB BSIs included prior CRE colonization or infection within the last year (OR: 3.723; 95% CI: 2.218–6.251), intravenous antibiotic use within the preceding three months (OR: 1.825; 95% CI: 1.076–3.094), and geographic origin from Asia (OR: 2.308; 95% CI: 1.065–5.002).ConclusionsOur findings highlight a reduction in both carbapenem resistance and mortality over time, reflecting improvements in antimicrobial stewardship and infection control. Patients with recent antibiotic exposure or prior CRE colonization at high risk and warrant close monitoring and early targeted therapy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Febrile neutropenia, Carbapenem resistance, Gram-negative bacteria, Bloodstream infection

Background

The global rise in antibiotic resistance among Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) presents a significant therapeutic challenge in managing infections [1]. Inappropriate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics is a well-recognized driver of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Paradoxically, infections caused by resistant pathogens often necessitate the use of multiple and broader-spectrum agents [2].

Carbapenem-resistant (CR) GNB infections now represent one of the most critical challenges in contemporary infection management, particularly in intensive care and hematology/oncology settings [3]. Carbapenems are considered the last line of defense against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens. However, when resistance to carbapenems emerges, treatment options become significantly limited. The available antibiotics are often more toxic (e.g., polymyxins), and combination therapies—associated with increased cost, prolonged treatment, and higher risk of adverse effects—may be required [2].

In Türkiye, ceftazidime-avibactam (CAZ-AVI), which received regulatory approval in 2019, is currently the only first-line agent available for treating CR-GNB infections susceptible to CAZ-AVI. Additionally, systemic fosfomycin and systemic polymyxin B, both considered alternative treatment options, received approval in 2017 and 2020, respectively. Their clinical availability and utilization have progressively increased across the country.

Patients with hematologic malignancies are more susceptible to infections. Among these, bloodstream infections (BSIs), especially in the setting of febrile neutropenia (FN), remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality. Reported mortality rates associated with infections in this patient population range from 17.7% to 23% [4–6]. GNB accounts for more than two-thirds of BSIs in febrile neutropenic patients [7, 8]. Notably, infections caused by CR-GNB are associated with significantly higher mortality rates compared to those caused by carbapenem-susceptible strains [5].

This study aimed to investigate the distribution of GNB isolated from blood cultures of patients who developed FN following the establishment of the hematologic malignancy unit at our hospital, which also serves international patients. Specifically, we analyzed temporal trends in species distribution and CR rates among all GNB. We also looked at factors associated with the development of resistance, aiming to identify patients at risk for CR-GNB bacteremia upon initial presentation. As a secondary objective, we assessed how the availability of antibiotics active against CR-GNB affected local resistance patterns and mortality in FN patients infected with these pathogens.

We conducted a retrospective study on FN patients with hematologic malignancies, starting from the establishment of the hematologic malignancy unit at our institution. Blood culture data were obtained from the hospital information system for patients treated between January 1, 2014, and January 1, 2025.

In addition, all isolated pathogens were analyzed for their antimicrobial susceptibility. For GNB, susceptibility to the following antibiotic classes was recorded: cephalosporins, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems, ceftazidime-avibactam, cefiderocol, quinolones, aminoglycosides, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and colistin.

The definition of neutropenic fever was established by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and is outlined in their published guidelines on febrile neutropenia [9].

Blood cultures obtained from patients were incubated using the BD BACTEC™ FX (Becton Dickinson, USA) blood culture automated system. Samples that signaled positive for growth were subcultured onto BD BBL™ CHROMagar™ Orientation Agar (BD, USA) and chocolate agar plates (Chocolate II Agar combines BD BBL™ IsoVitaleX™ Enrichment Agar, BD, USA) and incubated for 24–48 h. Identification of microorganisms grown on culture media was performed using conventional methods and the BD™ Bruker MALDI Biotyper® CA System (Becton Dickinson, USA). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of the isolated organisms was conducted using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method and the BD Phoenix™ automated identification and susceptibility testing system (Becton Dickinson, USA). The results were interpreted according to EUCAST criteria [10]. For colistin susceptibility, UMIC® Colistin (Bruker Daltonics, USA), the broth microdilution method was employed.

Bacteria exhibiting intrinsic or acquired resistance to carbapenems were classified as CR pathogens. Both ESBL-E and CRE were defined phenotypically.

Enterobacterales species were defined as CRE if they were resistant to at least one of the carbapenems (imipenem, meropenem, or ertapenem) [11]. Enterobacterales species were defined as ESBL-E if they were resistant to at least one of the oxyimino-cephalosporins (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefepime).

Isolation of more than one bacterial species from a single blood culture sample was defined as polymicrobial growth. In cases of recurrent infection episodes in the same patient, growth in subsequent blood cultures was considered a separate episode. However, if the same pathogen was isolated in blood cultures obtained within less than one week, or if source control could not be achieved, the episode was considered part of the initial infection.

The overall rate of carbapenem resistance among all GNB was documented. We also analyzed the association between the presence of CR-GNB and various factors, including study periods, demographic characteristics, type of hematologic malignancy, SCT, GVHD, prior colonization or infection with ESBL-E or CRE, recent IV antibiotic use, and the use of antibiotic prophylaxis during the episode.

Lastly, we assessed mortality attributable to GNB BSIs and analyzed its temporal trends over the study period.

Study periods were defined as follows: Period 1 (2014–2016), Period 2 (2017–2019), Period 3 (2020–2022), and Period 4 (2023–2024). The study period was divided into predefined intervals based on major institutional changes in infection control and antimicrobial stewardship practices. In 2017, just a few years after our institution and hematology department were established, a formal, documented meeting was held between the infection control team and the hematology department to improve the effectiveness of infection prevention measures in hematology patients. Following this meeting, structured training programs focusing on the implementation of isolation precautions were provided to healthcare workers. To enhance training effectiveness, infection control practices were delivered using simulation-based education with mannequins, and individual practice evaluation forms were introduced to objectively assess adherence and competency. In parallel, the institutional antimicrobial treatment guideline was revised and updated in 2017. Given the scope of these interventions, 2017 was considered a rational starting point to evaluate the impact of strengthened infection control and stewardship practices. In 2019, CAZ-AVI became available in our country for the first time and was introduced as a first-line treatment option for infections caused by CRE. To assess the potential impact of this major therapeutic change, 2020 was selected as the starting point for a subsequent study period. The final study period was designed to evaluate the longer-term effects of both fully established infection control practices and the sustained clinical use of antibiotics active against CR GNB, including CAZ-AVI, fosfomycin, and polymyxin B.

Geographic origin was categorized as patients from Türkiye or from abroad, including Asia, Africa, Europe, and Eurasia.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized the demographic and clinical characteristics, expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Comparisons between groups were made using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the t-student test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was performed using variables identified as potentially associated with CR-GNB BSIs in the univariable analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength and direction of associations.

Results

A total of 604 patients with hematologic malignancies who developed FN episodes and had microbiologically confirmed BSIs were included in the study. These patients accounted for a total of 975 FN episodes. Of these episodes, 560 (57.5%) occurred in male patients, with a median age of 49.5 years (IQR 34-60.5). Monomicrobial growth was observed in 866 episodes, while polymicrobial growth occurred in 109 episodes, comprising 98 episodes with two pathogens and 11 with three. In total, 1096 pathogens were isolated. Among these, 732 (66.87%) were GNB, 316 (28.82%) were Gram-positive cocci, and 39 (3.56%) were fungi. Variables showing a potential association with CR-GNB BSIs were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model, and the results are summarized in Table 5. In the multivariable model, CR-GNB BSIs were independently associated with study period, geographic origin, IV antibiotic use within the previous three months, and prior CRE infection or colonization within the last year. Compared with period 1, CR-GNB BSIs were significantly lower in Period 2 (OR: 0.492; 95% CI: 0.277–0.876; p = 0.016), Period 3 (OR: 0.496; 95% CI: 0.285–0.864; p = 0.013), and Period 4 (OR: 0.455; 95% CI: 0.258–0.804; p = 0.007). In regional comparisons, patients from Asia had a higher risk of CR-GNB BSIs compared with those from Türkiye (OR: 2.308; 95% CI: 1.065–5.002; p = 0.034). Additionally, IV antibiotic use within the preceding three months (OR: 1.825; 95% CI: 1.076–3.094; p = 0.026) and prior CRE infection or colonization within the previous year (OR: 3.723; 95% CI: 2.218–6.251; p < 0.001) were independently associated with CR-GNB BSIs. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the effective implementation of infection control measures together with the introduction of antibiotics active against CR-GNB was associated with a decline in resistance rates and a concurrent reduction in seven-day mortality related to GNB BSIs. Consistent with this observation, mortality rates among patients with CR-GNB BSIs progressively declined across the study periods, decreasing from 26.2% in Period 1 to 25.0% in Period 2, 19.6% in Period 3, and 12.2% in Period 4.

Discussion

In this large, retrospective, single-center study of FN patients with hematologic malignancies, we reaffirmed the predominance of GNB as the leading cause of BSIs, accounting for approximately two-thirds of the cases. Moreover, we identify key predictors of carbapenem resistance—most notably a history of CRE colonization or infection within the previous year, and recent IV antibiotic exposure within the past three months—which can aid in risk stratification and guide timely, evidence-based empirical therapy and infection control interventions. Importantly, our findings also demonstrate a notable decline in the rate of CR-GNB BSIs after 2017, with resistance trends plateauing thereafter, potentially reflecting the impact of improved antimicrobial stewardship, infection prevention and control measures, and expanded access to targeted antibiotics.

Despite the global concern over rising CR-GNB, recent studies suggest that this trend may be stabilizing or even reversing in certain healthcare settings, particularly in high-income countries with robust antimicrobial stewardship and infection control programs [1, 12–15]. Similarly, our study demonstrated a decline in resistance rates in recent years. Notably, enhanced infection prevention and control practices following the establishment of our hematologic malignancy unit likely played a pivotal role. In addition, improved access to targeted antimicrobials—such as systemic fosfomycin, CAZ-AVI, and polymyxin B—enabled earlier administration of appropriate therapy, reducing the need for prolonged empirical use of broad-spectrum agents. Such prolonged use, especially of combination regimens involving carbapenems, may exert strong selective pressure favoring the emergence of CRE. These resistance genes, commonly carried on plasmids, are capable of horizontal transfer between bacterial species, thereby accelerating the dissemination of resistance [16]. Moreover, uncontrolled infection foci and extended hospital stay resulting from treatment failures further propagate the cycle of multidrug resistance. Altogether, these observations underscore the need for timely and appropriate antibiotic use in conjunction with effective infection control infrastructure to sustain improvements in resistance trends.

Our study demonstrates that, alongside declining carbapenem resistance, mortality associated with GNB BSIs has also decreased in recent years. This trend highlights the link between antimicrobial resistance and clinical outcomes. While resistant pathogens are known to be associated with higher mortality, the timely administration of appropriate first-line antibiotics significantly improves survival [5, 17, 18]. Therefore, efforts to reduce resistance—through antimicrobial stewardship and improved infection control—may also contribute indirectly to improved patient outcomes.

Colonization of MDR-GNB is a well-established risk factor for subsequent BSIs, especially in FN patients with hematologic malignancies [19, 20]. However, the duration of this risk remains uncertain. Based on our findings, we suggest that prior CRE colonization or infection continues to pose a significant risk for up to one year. This aligns with studies reporting prolonged carriage in immunocompromised hosts, particularly those with repeated and long hospitalizations and broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure [21–23]. Due to the horizontal transfer of plasmid-mediated carbapenemase genes, clearance of CRE colonization may be delayed, particularly in patients with hematologic malignancies who experience prolonged hospitalization and repeated antimicrobial exposure. Therefore, risk stratification and empirical therapy decisions should consider CRE history for at least 12 months. Future studies should clarify risk duration through time-stratified analysis.

Those who had received IV antibiotics within the previous three months were significantly more likely to develop CR-GNB BSIs in our study. This association aligns with previous research showing that prior antimicrobial exposure, especially to broad-spectrum agents, increases the risk of resistant BSIs [24–26]. These findings highlight the need for careful use of antibiotics and strong stewardship programs to reduce resistance in high-risk patients.

Our analysis indicates that patients with febrile neutropenia from Asia were at higher risk for developing CR-GN BSIs. This finding aligns with global surveillance data, which consistently report higher CR-GNB rates in South and East Asia, followed by Eastern Europe, while Sub-Saharan Africa—despite comparatively lower reported mortality—continues to face substantial endemic resistance. In Asia, CR-GNB such as Enterobacterales, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are frequently implicated in nosocomial BSIs, particularly in ICUs and hematology settings. However, these regional estimates should be interpreted with caution, as data collection systems in many low- and middle-income countries may be limited by underreporting, diagnostic gaps, or inconsistent surveillance infrastructure [27–29]. Taken together, these regional data support our finding that geographic origin is an important risk factor for CR-GNB infections. Incorporating geographic risk stratification into empirical treatment decisions may be particularly beneficial in the context of immunocompromised patient populations such as those with hematologic malignancies.

Our study has several strengths, including a large sample size, standardized microbiological testing, and a comprehensive evaluation of both clinical and microbiological risk factors. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study may be associated with incomplete data capture, and although our center serves a geographically diverse patient population, it remains a single-center analysis and may not fully reflect national trends. Furthermore, molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms (e.g., carbapenemase genes) was not performed, which limits our ability to correlate phenotypic resistance with underlying genetic determinants.

In conclusion, CR-GNB BSIs continue to pose a major clinical challenge in patients with hematologic malignancies. Early identification of risk factors—such as recent antibiotic exposure and prior CRE colonization—can guide targeted empiric therapy. The observed reduction in carbapenem resistance rates during the second study period underscores the potential importance of stricter implementation of infection control measures and enhanced infection prevention practices. At the same time, the increasing availability of antibiotics active against CR-GNB may have improved treatment options for these infections. Overall, our findings support the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship strategies and infection control and prevention measures, and monitoring of resistance patterns to optimize patient outcomes in this high-risk population.
