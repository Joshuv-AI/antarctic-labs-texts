Association of healthy lifestyle with incident cardiovascular diseases in individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yaoyao Wang, Junyu Liu, Fang Cao, Yuge Wang, Zhisheng Li, Hanyue Zeng, Mengna Zhou, Qingyue He, Weixi Jiang, Yifeng Li, Junxia Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain a major complication of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM). Although healthy lifestyle behaviors are associated with lower cardiovascular risk in the general population and in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus, evidence specific to T1DM is limited. This study aimed to examine the associations of individual and composite healthy lifestyle factors with incident CVDs among individuals with T1DM.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included participants with T1DM from the UK Biobank who were free of CVDs at baseline. A composite lifestyle score was constructed from six factors: smoking status, alcohol consumption, body mass index (BMI), diet quality, physical activity, and sleep duration. The primary outcomes were incident CVDs, including coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure (HF), and stroke. Associations were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, with subgroup analyses stratified by key demographic and clinical characteristics.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 14.38 years, 455 incident CVD events were documented among 1,390 participants with T1DM. Maintaining a BMI of 25 to < 30 kg/m2 (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.52–0.81) or 18.5 to < 25 kg/m2 (0.59; 0.45–0.78), adhering to a healthy diet (0.83; 0.69–0.99), and obtaining adequate sleep duration (0.77; 0.63–0.92) were independently associated with a reduced risk of CVDs. Compared with participants with a lifestyle score of 0–2, those with a score 4–6 had a significantly lower risk of composite CVDs (0.58; 0.46–0.73), CHD (0.63; 0.49–0.81), and HF (0.49; 0.34–0.70). These associations were generally consistent across subgroups.ConclusionsAmong individuals with T1DM, healthy lifestyle profiles were associated with a lower risk of incident CVDs, particularly through weight management, healthy diet, and adequate sleep duration. These findings support the potential value of incorporating lifestyle assessment and promotion into cardiovascular risk management for individuals with T1DM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02216-x.Keywords: Type 1 diabetes mellitus, Healthy lifestyles, Cardiovascular diseases, UK Biobank, Cohort study

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by pancreatic β-cell destruction, absolute insulin deficiency, and persistent hyperglycemia [1]. According to the Global Diabetes Atlas, approximately 9.15 million people worldwide were living with clinically diagnosed T1DM in 2024, of whom 22.3% (2.04 million) lived in low- and lower-middle-income countries [2]. The global number of individuals with T1DM is projected to reach 13.5 million by 2040 [3]. Although the incidence of T1DM is substantially lower than that of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3, 4], its earlier onset and lifelong course expose patients to decades of hyperglycemia, insulin treatment, and metabolic instability, thereby increasing the cumulative burden of vascular complications [5]. Prevention of chronic complications is therefore a central challenge in the long-term management of T1DM.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure (HF) and stroke, are among the most common and severe vascular complications in individuals with T1DM. In the Pittsburgh Epidemiology of Diabetes Complications Study, the 10-year absolute CVDs risk reached 6.3% among individuals with T1DM aged 30–39 years [6]. With longer disease duration, CVDs become a dominant cause of death, accounting for 25%-50% of deaths after 10–19 years of T1DM and more than half after 20 years [7]. Moreover, a large Swedish cohort showed that excess risks of CVDs and all-cause mortality persist even when conventional cardiovascular risk factors are well controlled [8]. These findings point to the need for additional prevention strategies complementing glycemic control and standard risk factor management.

Healthy lifestyle behaviors, including non-smoking, moderate alcohol consumption, healthy body weight (as reflected by body mass index, BMI), a healthy diet, adequate physical activity, and sufficient sleep, have been consistently associated with lower cardiovascular risk in the general population and in individuals with T2DM [9, 10]. However, evidence from these populations cannot be assumed to apply directly to T1DM, in which lifestyle behaviors are closely intertwined with insulin use, glycemic variability, and hypoglycemia risk [11]. For example, physical activity may have a complex, potentially non-linear relationship with cardiovascular health in T1DM, as both insufficient and excessive activity may be unfavorable, complicating the interpretation of its net cardiovascular relevance [12]. Disease-specific evidence is therefore needed to clarify the role of lifestyle behaviors in cardiovascular risk among individuals with T1DM.

Although T1DM-specific evidence has begun to accumulate regarding the cardiovascular relevance of lifestyle-related factors, several important gaps remain [13–15]. Prior studies have reported that favorable American Heart Association (AHA) cardiovascular health profiles are associated with lower long-term CHD risk or lower preclinical atherosclerotic burden in individuals with T1DM [14]. Other studies have linked individual lifestyle components, including physical activity, Mediterranean diet adherence, and tobacco exposure, to CVD events or carotid atherosclerosis [13]. Nevertheless, much of the existing evidence has focused on single lifestyle factors examined in isolation, or on combined cardiovascular health metrics that incorporate metabolic components (e.g., blood pressure, cholesterol, or glycemic control) or subclinical vascular outcomes. While informative, these scores mix lifestyle and metabolic factors, making it difficult to assess the independent relevance of lifestyle behaviors to cardiovascular risk, because lifestyle behaviors can be directly modified through clinical counseling and patient self-management. What remains unknown is whether such a modifiable-factor lifestyle score is cumulatively associated with lower risks of incident CVDs in T1DM, and whether these associations vary across demographic and clinical subgroups.

Therefore, using data from the UK Biobank, this study aimed to investigate the associations of individual lifestyle factors and a composite modifiable lifestyle score with the risk of incident CVDs and its subtypes, including CHD, HF, and stroke, among individuals with T1DM. We further evaluated whether these associations differed across key demographic and clinical subgroups. Our findings may help establish modifiable, behavior-focused prevention strategies to reduce the residual cardiovascular risk in T1DM beyond glycemic control.

Materials and methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a population-based prospective cohort that recruited more than 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 years between 2006 and 2010. Baseline data were collected through questionnaires, interviews, physical measurements, and biological samples, with subsequent linkage to health outcome records. Details of the UK Biobank design and procedures have been reported previously [16].

In the present study, participants with possible T1DM were first identified using self-reported diabetes type or International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes of E10 for T1DM [17]. Because C-peptide data were unavailable and islet autoantibody measurements were not systematically available in the UK Biobank [18], ICD-10 code definition may cause potential misclassification. To improve the specificity of T1DM identification, we further restricted the analytical population to participants who met the above criteria and reported insulin treatment at baseline. Participants with pre-existing CVDs at baseline were excluded. The final sample included 1,390 individuals with T1DM (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram for participant recruitment in the UK Biobank cohort

The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (reference number: 21/NW/0157; project number: 105139), and all participants provided written informed consent [19].

Definition of healthy lifestyle

In this study, healthy lifestyle was defined based on six modifiable lifestyle factors: smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, diet quality, physical activity, and sleep duration [20]. Each factor was classified as healthy or unhealthy as follows:

Healthy smoking status was defined as never smoking or having only tried smoking once or twice; former regular or occasional smoking and current smoking were classified as unhealthy [20, 21].

Alcohol consumption was assessed based on self-reported average weekly or monthly intake of alcoholic beverages. Moderate alcohol consumption (healthy) was defined as ≤ 14 g/day for women and ≤ 28 g/day for men [22].

Healthy BMI was defined as 18.5 to < 25 kg/m². For analyses examining BMI as an individual factor, participants were categorized as normal weight, overweight, or obese, with obesity as the reference group.

Healthy diet was defined as adherence to ≥ 5 of 9 dietary components according to an updated cardiovascular dietary recommendation [23]. Details were described in Supplementary methods.

Healthy physical activity was defined as ≥ 600 metabolic equivalent task (MET)-min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, assessed using the validated short International Physical Activity Questionnaire [24].

Healthy sleep duration was defined as > 6 to ≤ 8 h/day based on a previous study [25].

For construction of the composite healthy lifestyle score, each factor was categorized as either healthy (assigned 1 point) or unhealthy (0 point) [20]. The total score ranged from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviors. Participants were categorized into three groups: low adherence (0–2 points), intermediate adherence (3 points), and high adherence (4–6 points).

Detailed definitions are provided in Supplemental Methods and Supplementary Table 1.

Ascertainment of CVDs

The primary outcome was the first incident CVDs event during follow-up, ascertained through linkage to primary care, hospital admission, and death registry data. The composite CVD outcomes included coronary heart disease (CHD, including angina and myocardial infarction; ICD-10 codes, I20-I25), heart failure (HF; ICD-10 code, I50), and stroke (including ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke (ICD-10 codes I60-I63). Follow-up extended from baseline until the first occurrence of a CVD event, death, or January 25, 2024, whichever came first.

Assessment of covariates

Baseline covariates were collected through questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. These included age, sex, race, Townsend deprivation index (TDI), family history of diabetes, diabetes duration, history of chronic diabetic complications (including renal [ICD-10: E10.2], ophthalmic [ICD-10: E10.3], neurological [ICD-10: E10.4], and peripheral circulatory [ICD-10: E10.5] complications), antihypertensive medication use, cholesterol-lowering medication use, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), BMI, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Non-HDL cholesterol (non-HDL-C) was calculated as TC minus HDL-C [26]. Detailed definitions are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables, and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Group differences were assessed using ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis, or chi-square tests, as appropriate.

Multiple imputation was used for covariates with less than 20% missing data. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of individual lifestyle factors and the composite healthy lifestyle score with incident CVDs among individuals with T1DM. Two adjustment models were constructed: Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race, and TDI; Model 2 was further adjusted for SBP, DBP, non-HDL-C, HbA1c, antihypertensive drug use, cholesterol-lowering medication use, family history of diabetes, diabetes duration, and chronic diabetic complications. When individual lifestyle factors were analyzed, the models were mutually adjusted for the other five lifestyle factors.

Subgroup analyses were conducted according to sex, age, race, TDI, age at diabetes diagnosis, diabetes duration, HbA1c level, and chronic diabetic complications. Interactions between the healthy lifestyle score and each stratification variables were assessed using likelihood ratio tests that compared models with and without cross-product terms to report the P for interaction.

Sensitivity analyses included: (1) excluding participants who developed CVDs within the first two years of follow-up; (2) excluding individuals with missing data; (3) including participants who were excluded from the main analysis due to missing non-HDL-C data, with Model 2 adjusted for all covariates except non-HDL-C.

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The final cohort included 1,390 adults with T1DM, with a mean age of 56.9 years, and 608 (43.7%) were women. Over a median follow-up of 14.38 years, 455 participants (32.7%) developed incident CVDs, including 363 CHD events, 173 HF events, and 92 stroke events; these CVD subtypes were not mutually exclusive. Association of lifestyle factors with incident CVDs

In the all-covariate-adjusted model (Model 2), BMI in the range of 25 to < 30 kg/m² (HR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.52–0.81) and 18.5 to < 25 kg/m² (0.59; 0.45–0.78), adhering to a healthy diet (0.83; 0.69–0.99), and obtaining adequate sleep duration (0.77; 0.63–0.92) were significantly associated with a lower risk of CVDs. Never smoking (0.88; 0.72–1.07), moderate alcohol consumption (1.06; 0.84–1.34) and adequate physical activity (0.94; 0.77–1.15) were not significantly associated with CVD risks (Table 2).

A grade inverse association was observed between the composite lifestyle score and incident CVDs. Compared with participants in the low-score group (0–2 points), the HRs were 0.74 (95% CI, 0.58–0.93) for the intermediate-score group (3 points) and 0.58 (95% CI, 0.46–0.73) for the high-score group (4–6 points) (Table 2). The association was similar when the lifestyle score was modeled continuously (per 1-point increase: HR, 0.83; 95%CI, 0.77–0.90). Compared with the low-score group, the high-score group also had lower risks of CHD (0.63; 0.49–0.81) and HF (0.49; 0.34–0.70), with a suggestive but non-significant association for stroke (0.71; 0.43–1.17) (Supplementary Tables 3–5).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The inverse association between the lifestyle score and CVDs risk was generally consistent across subgroups defined by sex, age, race, TDI, age at diabetes diagnosis, diabetes duration, HbA1c level, and chronic diabetic complications, with no significant interaction detected (Fig. 2). Similar patterns were observed in analyses of individual lifestyle factors (Supplementary Tables 6–12). Notably, normal BMI (18.5 to < 25 kg/m²) was associated with a lower CVDs risk among participants without baseline chronic diabetic complications (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.45–0.71), whereas no clear association was observed among those with chronic diabetic complications (0.90; 0.60–1.35).

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the main findings. The association persisted after excluding CVD events occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up (Supplementary Table 13); using datasets without multiple imputation (Supplementary Table 14); and including participants with missing non-HDL-C data with Model 2 adjusted for all covariates except non-HDL-C (Supplementary Table 15).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of 1,390 individuals with T1DM, a healthier lifestyle profile was associated with a lower risk of incident CVDs over approximately 15 years. Among individual lifestyle factors, BMI, diet quality, and sleep duration showed significant inverse associations with CVDs risk. The composite healthy lifestyle score also demonstrated a graded inverse association with incident CVDs, particularly CHD and HF, whereas the association with stroke was inverse but not statistically significant. These associations remained generally consistent across major demographic and clinical subgroups.

Previous T1DM studies have linked individual lifestyle components, including physical activity, Mediterranean diet adherence, and tobacco exposure, to incident CVD events or carotid atherosclerotic burden [13, 15, 27, 28]. Two recent studies reported that adherence to (modified) AHA Life’s Simple 7 was associated with lower CHD risk or lower subclinical atherosclerotic burden in individuals with T1DM [14, 29]. However, these cardiovascular health metrics incorporate both lifestyle behaviors and metabolic factors, such as glycemia, blood pressure, and lipid levels. This study extends the previous work by focusing specifically on the cumulative association of modifiable lifestyle behaviors, while accounting for major metabolic and clinical risk factors, and by examining incident CVD outcomes and their subtypes, including CHD, HF, and stroke, in the T1DM population.

The present findings are broadly consistent with evidence from the general population and individuals with T2DM, in whom healthier lifestyle patterns have been associated with lower risks of cardiovascular complications [9, 20, 22, 30]. Extrapolation of such evidence to T1DM is not straightforward because lifestyle behaviors in T1DM are closely linked to insulin treatment, glycemic variability, hypoglycemia risk, and long disease duration [31]. In this context, our results provide disease-specific prospective evidence that multiple healthy lifestyle behaviors, when considered together, are associated with lower cardiovascular risk among individuals with T1DM. Importantly, these findings may strengthen the evidence base for considering healthy lifestyle behaviors as an important component of CVDs prevention and risk management in T1DM.

When CVD subtypes were examined separately, the association of a high lifestyle score was evident for CHD and HF but not statistically significant for stroke. Several factors may explain this pattern. First, the number of stroke events was smaller than that of CHD and HF events, which may have limited statistical power [32]. Second, stroke is a heterogeneous outcome that includes ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes with partly different risk factor profiles [33, 34]. Additionally, lifestyle factors may have differential associations with distinct cardiovascular outcomes [35]. The stronger association observed for HF than for CHD is noteworthy: HF in diabetes is closely related to metabolic disturbances, vascular dysfunction, obesity, blood pressure, and systemic inflammation [36, 37]. Therefore, a lifestyle profile that captures multiple behavioral domains may be particularly relevant to HF risk, however, future studies with larger numbers of specific events are needed to confirm these observations.

Subgroup analyses showed that the inverse association between the lifestyle score and CVDs risk was generally consistent across subgroups. The association appeared stronger among participants aged 60 years or older, but no statistically significant interaction was detected. Overall, these findings suggest that the association between a healthier lifestyle profile and lower CVDs risk was not restricted to a single demographic or clinical subgroup, although further validation in more diverse and larger T1DM populations is warranted.

This study has several strengths. First, it focused specifically on individuals with T1DM, a population in which evidence on combined lifestyle behaviors and cardiovascular outcomes remains limited. Second, the use of a large prospective cohort with long-term follow-up allowed assessment of incident clinical CVD outcomes rather than only surrogate markers or subclinical vascular measures. Additionally, we evaluated both individual lifestyle factors and a composite lifestyle score, examined CVD subtypes and population subgroups, and incorporated chronic diabetic complications into the analysis.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and causal relationships cannot be inferred. Second, lifestyle factors were assessed only at baseline; therefore, changes in lifestyle behaviors during follow-up could not be accounted for. Third, although the prospective design reduced the likelihood of reverse causation, it cannot be fully eliminated. Fourth, the identification of T1DM in the UK Biobank is challenging because C-peptide data were unavailable and islet autoantibody measurements were not systematically available. We therefore adopted a conservative definition by restricting the main analysis to participants who met T1DM criteria and reported insulin treatment at baseline. Additionally, because the number of participants with each specific chronic diabetic complication at baseline was limited, renal, ophthalmic, neurological, and peripheral circulatory complications were combined into a single composite variable. Last, most participants in the UK Biobank were of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to ethnically diverse populations.

Conclusions

In summary, a healthy lifestyle profile was associated with a lower risk of CVDs in individuals with T1DM, particularly CHD and HF. The findings provide prospective observational evidence supporting the potential role of healthy lifestyle behaviors and related assessment in CVDs prevention and risk management for individuals with T1DM. Further interventional and mechanistic studies are warranted to determine whether improving lifestyle behaviors can directly reduce cardiovascular risk in this population.
