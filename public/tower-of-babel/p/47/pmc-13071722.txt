Establishment of a Charlson comorbidity index-based model for predicting the preterm premature rupture of the membrane
Authors: Shu-Xin Zheng, Xun Ren, Qing-Qing Zhou, Wen-Wen Lin, Yu-Ping Cai, Feng-Yan Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) is a tool used to quantitatively assess the severity of comorbidities in patients. This study aimed to explore the predictive value of the CCI in preterm premature rupture of the membrane (PPROM).MethodsData were collected from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database. The continuous variables were analyzed using the T-test/one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were performed using the chi-square test. Colinearity analysis was used to exclude collinear variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to assess the prediction efficiency. The prediction model was established by the extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), logistic, and Random Forest on the independent impact factors, and the areas under the curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, specificity, and F1 were used to assess the prediction efficiency of the model.ResultsCCI was significantly higher in the PPROM group than in the term premature rupture of membranes (TPROM) group. The ROC (AUC: 0.535) and DCA indicated that CCI was limited in predicting the PPROM in clinical practice. The results of the logistic analysis showed that the CCI, platelet, age, red blood cell distribution width (RDW), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were the independent influencing factors of PPROM. The ROC results showed that the CCI-based model had a better prediction efficiency in predicting the occurrence of PPROM (AUC: 0.627).ConclusionsCCI was a risk factor for the PPROM. In addition, a risk prediction model based on the CCI and clinical features had a good prediction efficiency for PPROM.

Introduction

Fetal membranes, composed of an outer chorion and an inner amnion, constitute the amniotic sac that encloses the fetus and the amniotic fluid. Their primary functions are to maintain the structural integrity of the amniotic cavity, provide mechanical cushioning and immune protection, and regulate the amniotic fluid environment, thereby ensuring normal fetal development (1,2). Preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) refers to the rupture of fetal membranes that occurs before 37 weeks of gestation, with an incidence rate of 3–4% (3). The pathogenesis of PPROM involves complex interactions among maternal, fetal, and environmental factors, along with dysregulation at the maternal-fetal interface (4,5). The disease process often encompasses an extended subclinical stage and may exhibit a reciprocal causal relationship with infection. PPROM can lead to complications such as preterm birth, sepsis, umbilical cord prolapse, and pulmonary hypoplasia, significantly increasing neonatal morbidity and mortality (6). It directly increases the probability of premature birth to as high as 40–50%, posing a significant clinical risk and a major threat to the health of both the mother and the fetus (3). Despite improvements in prenatal care, the incidence of PPROM and the resulting premature birth rate have not decreased due to its complexities (7).

The etiology of PPROM is complex, with maternal chronic diseases considered significant risk factors. For instance, diabetes can lead to the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products, which downregulate the expression of key collagen genes in the fetal membranes and trigger local inflammation, thereby compromising their structural integrity (8,9). The chronic inflammatory state associated with autoimmune diseases can directly compromise the integrity of the fetal membranes (10). All of these factors may increase the risk of PPROM. Existing studies have predominantly focused on the association between individual chronic conditions and PPROM. However, this focus fails to reflect the clinical reality where patients often present with multiple coexisting chronic diseases, and synergistic effects may exist among these conditions. To provide a more comprehensive and integrated assessment of the impact of maternal underlying disease burden on PPROM, this study introduces the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI).

CCI was developed in 1987 by Mary E. Charlson and became the most widely used index, and is often considered to be the gold-standard measure to assess comorbidity in clinical research (11). The index calculates a total score to measure patients’ burden of disease by assigning different weights to 17 specific diseases. A higher score indicates a poorer health baseline for the patient and a significantly increased risk of future mortality due to comorbidities (12,13). The diagnostic criteria for its 17 constituent chronic conditions are unaffected by pregnancy, and these comorbidities typically predate gestation. Consequently, pregnancy-induced physiological changes minimally influence the CCI score. Thus, the CCI represents an appropriate and validated instrument for assessing the comorbidity burden of pregnant women in our study. This study investigated the relationship between CCI and PPROM. A predictive model for PPROM was subsequently developed based on CCI. This model may assist in clinical risk assessment for PPROM and inform individualized delivery planning, thereby potentially reducing the incidence of preterm birth. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-aw-745/rc).

Methods

Data source and study population

In this retrospective case-control study, all the data were collected from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database. The MIMIC-IV database spans from 2008 to 2019. The database comprises a relational structure and encompasses authentic records of hospitalizations for patients admitted to a tertiary academic medical center located in Boston, MA, USA. It is also a large publicly available single-center critical care database. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Patients with PROM and hospitalization were selected for analysis in this study. Specific inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients who met the PROM criteria; (II) age ≥18 years. Exclusion criteria: (I) patients without CCI data; (II) patients without the blood routine index data. In addition, for patients with multiple hospitalization records, only the first hospitalization record will be included.

Outcomes and covariables

In this study, the primary outcome was that the patient was diagnosed with PPROM [International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-code: 65811, 65813, O4200, O42012, O42013, O42112, O42113, O4290, O42912, O42913, O42919] vs. term premature rupture of membranes (TPROM) (ICD: O4202, O4212, O4292) (ICD-code was originated from ICD-9 and ICD-10, https://www.who.int/). The main independent variable was the CCI. The baseline characteristics include age, race, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), marital status, smoking history (yes vs. no), drinking history (yes vs. no), chorioamnionitis (yes vs. no), nulliparity (yes vs. no), hematocrit, red blood cell distribution width (RDW, %), hemoglobin (g/dL), platelet (K/µL), red blood cells (m/µL), white blood cells (K/µL), D-dimer (ng/mL), partial thromboplastin time (PTT, s), prothrombin time (PT, s), international normalized ratio (INR, %), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), creatinine (mg/dL), and folic acid (ng/mL). The laboratory indicators data were obtained from the first test within 24 hours after admission.

Statistical analysis

Before data analysis, the multiple imputation method was used for missing data. The baseline characteristics of patients were divided into two groups: the PPROM group and the TPROM group. Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using a t-test (in two groups) or using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (in more than two groups) with post hoc Bonferroni and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test (in two groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (in more than two groups) with post hoc Bonferroni and expressed as median [interquartile range (IQR)]. Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were performed using the chi-square test and expressed as N (%). The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check the multicollinearity between variables. The variable was dropped when its VIF was >10 (14). The multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to explore the relationship between the variables and the outcome. Results were reported as odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Sensitivity analyses were performed in those without chorioamnionitis (n=1,343), those without infection (excluding 14 pregnant women with positive for high-risk pathogens in blood, 13 pregnant women with positive for group b beta streptococci in the vaginal, and 47 pregnant women with positive for the main pathogenic bacteria in urine), and those who were no nulliparity (excluding 82 nulliparity), respectively to validate the robustness of the results. All participants were divided into the training set and the validation set at a 7:3 ratio. Three prediction models were established by the extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) (parameter: learning rate: 0.3; maximum tree depth: 1; L2 regularization coefficient: 1), logistic (parameter: regularization factor: 1; convergence metric: 0.0001; number of iterations: 100; regularization type: L2), and Random Forest (parameter: maximum tree depth: 1; splitting criterion: gini impurity; number of trees: 100), separately. The 10-fold cross-validation using resampling techniques was used to improve the robustness and reliability of predictive performance. The area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1 score were used to assess the prediction efficiency of the three models and to select the optimal model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to assess the prediction efficiency. A calibration curve was constructed, and the consistency between the model’s predicted probabilities and the actual event occurrence was assessed using the Brier score.

Results

The importance of the CCI in the PPROM

A statistically significant disparity in CCI (P<0.001) was observed between the PPROM and TPROM groups (Figure 1A). In the population of patients with PPROM, our analysis revealed no statistically significant disparities across the three BMI groups (<25, 25 to <30, and ≥30 kg/m2) in CCI (Figure 1B). However, there was a statistically significant difference across the three age groups (≤24, 25 to <30, and ≥30 years) in terms of CCI for patients with PPROM (Figure 1C). In contrast, there was no significant difference in CCI across the five race groups (Black, White, others, Asian, and Hispanic) (Figure 1D) or between single/divorced and married individuals (Figure 1E). The efficacy of CCI in predicting PPROM was assessed via the ROC curve and DCA analysis. The results of DCA showed that CCI had the net benefit when the risk threshold was between 65–75% (Figure 1F). The ROC results showed that the AUC for CCI prediction of PPROM is 0.535 (Figure 1G).

Screening of the other risk factors for PPROM

Due to the limited value of a single CCI, we further explored the other risk factors associated with the PPROM to establish an optimal CCI-based model for predicting PPROM. First, we compared the clinical characteristics between the TPROM and PPROM groups. A total of 1,475 patients with rupture of the membrane were included, among whom 495 patients had TPROM and 980 patients had PPROM. The median age of patients was 30 years old, which was younger in the TPROM group than in the PPROM group with significant differences [29.0 (IQR, 25.0–33.0) vs. 30.0 (IQR, 26.0–34.0) years, P<0.001]. The patients had a hematocrit of 32.5 (IQR, 31.099–35.900) g/dL, which was higher in the TPROM group than in the PPROM group with significant differences [33.700 (IQR, 31.099–36.500) vs. 31.800 (IQR, 31.099–35.400) g/dL, P<0.001]. Uniformly, hemoglobin, red blood cells, and CRP in the TPROM were higher than in the PPROM group with significant differences (P<0.05). However, levels of RDW, white blood cells, and CCI were notably lower in the TPROM group than in the PPROM group (all P<0.05) (Table 2). The proportion of chorioamnionitis was significantly higher in the TPROM group than in the PPROM group (20.000% vs. 3.400%, P<0.001). The proportion of primiparous women was significantly lower in the TPROM group compared to the PPROM group (2.626% vs. 7.041%).

Construction of CCI-based prediction model for PPROM

The model for PPROM prediction was constructed based on five independent influencing factors via XGBoost, logistic, and random forest. The AUCs of the three models were 0.687 (95% CI: 0.684–0.690), 0.612 (95% CI: 0.608–0.615), and 0.631 (95% CI: 0.628–0.635) in the training set, respectively; while they were 0.587 (95% CI: 0.556–0.617), 0.591 (95% CI: 0.560–0.623), and 0.569 (95% CI: 0.527–0.611) in the validation set, suggesting that the logistic model had the best stability, compared with the other two models (Table 6). Therefore, the logistic regression model was selected as the optimal model, and its predictive power was evaluated in all population groups. The DCA plot showed that the logistic model had a net benefit, especially in the threshold range of 0.6–0.9 (Figure 3A). In addition, the AUC of ROC was 0.627, showing a modest prediction efficiency (Figure 3B). The calibration curve showed a consistency between the predicted risk probability from the model and the actual risk (Brier score =0.226, Figure 3C). These results suggested that the CCI-based model exhibited a better prediction ability than the single CCI (AUC: 0.535, Figure 1G).

Discussion

The fetal membranes serve as an essential physical barrier, and their breach will compromise this barrier integrity and create a direct pathway for ascending pathogen infiltration from the vagina into the intrauterine cavity (15). This invasion can trigger an inflammatory response in the body, causing the accumulation of immune cells such as neutrophils in the chorion and amnion. Retrograde infection can result in intrauterine infection, leading to serious consequences such as preterm birth, stillbirth, neonatal infection, and brain damage (16).

There are multiple risk factors influencing the occurrence of PPROM, such as maternal reproductive tract infections, substance abuse, parity, and prior cervical surgery (3). Studies have reported that these risk factors primarily lead to PPROM through two mechanisms. Firstly, maternal reproductive tract infections can result in PPROM. Pathogens produced abundant hydrolytic enzymes after infection, leading to the degradation of the extracellular matrix and collagen in fetal membrane cells. Additionally, surface tension decreases, inducing high intrauterine pressure and uterine contractions, ultimately causing preterm PPROM. Secondly, inflammatory cascade reactions triggered by diseases or lifestyle behaviors can place the patient’s body in an inflammatory state, leading to PPROM. Lifestyle behaviors such as smoking and drug abuse are examples (17,18). Relevant diseases include chronic maternal diseases, diabetes, pulmonary disorders, periodontal disease, nutritional deficiencies, and pyelonephritis (7,19,20). Many diseases have an impact on PPROM, highlighting the importance of investigating the relationship between CCI and PPROM.

Initially utilized for assessing the 1-year mortality of critically ill patients, the application of CCI has evolved to encompass predicting the prognosis and occurrence of a wide range of diseases (21,22). Nevertheless, there is limited research available regarding the effects of CCI on PPROM. Therefore, based on real data from the MIMIC database and strict inclusion and exclusion criteria to control for data bias to the greatest extent possible, this study investigated the relationship between CCI and the occurrence of PPROM. In addition, a predictive model for the occurrence of PPROM was constructed based on CCI combined with clinical characteristics. This model holds significance in evaluating the likelihood of pregnant women experiencing PPROM.

In this study, the relationship between CCI and PPROM was assessed. The results indicate that CCI can predict the occurrence of PPROM. It was found that certain components of the CCI, including cerebrovascular disease, diabetes without complication or comorbidity, chronic pulmonary disease, and age score, play a significant role in the occurrence of PPROM. This may be attributed to genetic changes occurring between these diseases, as Ilona et al. demonstrated that cardiovascular disease-related microRNA biomarkers, such as miR-16-5p, miR-20b-5p, miR-24-3p, miR-26a-5p, among others, are dysregulated in PPROM. These biomarkers can be used to predict the probability of PPROM in pregnant women (23).

We further found that CCI was an independent risk factor for PPROM. In addition, we also found that the independent influencing factors of PPROM included age, RDW, platelet, and CRP. PPROM is associated with high levels of oxidative stress and lower levels of antioxidant enzymes. Oxidative stress leads to the generation of reactive oxygen species, activating the main transcription factor nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway within the fetal membranes. This activation results in the production of various pro-inflammatory factors, which are the main contributors to PPROM (3). Most studies have shown that CRP is a marker used to assess the severity of inflammation (24,25). Generally, CRP is considered a risk factor for PPROM (26), however, our results indicate that it acts as a protective factor. We suspect that the key lies in interpreting the clinical context and the test time of CRP. The CRP level in this study was obtained at hospital admission within 24 h. CRP primarily reflects the intensity of the initial systemic stress response to tissue damage and acute infection, etc. (27). In this study, it may reflect the body’s stress response to ruptured membranes, rather than the burden of chronic subclinical inflammation that may have contributed to preterm birth. Consequently, a rapid and significant rise in CRP may indicate a more robust and vigilant maternal innate immune system capable of rapidly mobilizing defensive resources. This could facilitate the early containment of focal infection during the latent period and create a more favorable window for clinical intervention. Another pivotal finding of this study—patients with TPROM generally exhibited higher admission CRP levels than those with PPROM—provides strong corroborative evidence for this interpretation. This reflects inherent differences in maternal immunophysiological status at different gestational ages. At term, the maternal immune system undergoes physiological reprogramming in preparation for parturition, characterized by a systemically lowered threshold for inflammatory responses and a significantly enhanced capacity for the synthesis and release of acute-phase proteins, manifesting a pro-inflammatory preparedness state (28). Therefore, when TPROM occurs, the body can immediately mount a more intense systemic inflammatory response. In contrast, PPROM often occurs against a pathological background involving potential immune dysregulation at the maternal-fetal interface, chronic subclinical infection, or increased tissue fragility. These underlying conditions may have already depleted or suppressed the body’s acute response reserve (29). The protective association of admission CRP in PPROM essentially reflects the counteracting effect of a preserved capacity for acute immunostress against adverse outcomes. RDW is a parameter reflecting the heterogeneity of red blood cell volume in peripheral blood, indicating the degree of consistency in red blood cell size and shape within a sample. Numerous studies have demonstrated a correlation between RDW and inflammation in various diseases, indicating it as a predictive factor. For instance, He et al. showed that RDW can serve as a potential parameter for detecting inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis (30). Ahmed et al. confirmed a strong relation between diabetics with vascular complications and RDW, indicating RDW as a marker of inflammation in type 2 diabetes mellitus (31).

In addition, we constructed a comprehensive predictive model based on those factors to enhance the prediction of PPROM. However, the CCI-based logistic model demonstrates only moderate predictive power, suggesting its limited clinical utility as a diagnostic tool. This indicates the difficulty of predicting multifactorial PPROM using a model constructed solely from single-time-point clinical indicators. Despite its general AUC, the model may still be able to differentiate the population into relatively high- and low-risk subgroups. Given resource constraints, it may serve as a preliminary screening or risk assessment instrument rather than a definitive diagnostic one. The model’s primary clinical value lies in enabling risk stratification and guiding precise interventions. Quantifying an individual pregnant woman’s risk, it provides an objective foundation for clinical decisions. For high-risk patients, clinicians can intensify prenatal monitoring, schedule glucocorticoid administration to promote fetal lung maturation in advance, and plan delivery at facilities equipped for neonatal resuscitation. For low-risk patients, the model helps avoid excessive medical intervention, thereby reducing unnecessary hospitalization and associated economic burdens. Applying this prediction tool could optimize the allocation of obstetric resources and shift the diagnostic paradigm from passive treatment to active prevention, ultimately improving pregnancy outcomes for both mother and infant. Future research should integrate additional indicators, including genomic and proteomic data, lifestyle factors, and dietary outcomes, to improve its predictive performance for PPROM.

This study has the following strengths: (I) this study constructed a model based on CCI to distinguish high-risk PPROM populations. It elucidated the new application of CCI in predicting adverse obstetric outcomes. (II) A multi-model strategy incorporating logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost was implemented to ensure methodological thoroughness and permit comparative assessment of predictive accuracy. (III) This study systematically transforms readily available clinical comorbidity data into a quantitative risk assessment tool, thereby establishing a practical and cost-effective strategy for the early identification of high-risk pregnancies. However, there were also some limitations. First, this study is limited by the absence of some data in the database, including potential confounding factors (e.g., dietary patterns and social circumstances) as well as specific information on pregnant women (such as parity, gestational age at blood sampling, and birth outcomes) and diseases (e.g., the time and location of membrane rupture). Second, the causal relationship between CCI and PPROM could not be determined due to a cross-sectional study. Third, the study population was collected from the US MIMIC single-center database, which is subject to population bias and cannot be generalized to a global population. Fourth, the model’s generalizability remains uncertain without validation using an independent cohort or temporal split.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CCI is closely associated with PPROM. The CCI-based logistic regression model was selected as the optimal model for predicting PPROM. Despite its moderate prediction efficiency, it can distinguish between high- and low-risk groups for PPROM for early intervention, improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. Future prospective studies with more comprehensive indicators in a multi-center cohort will be conducted to validate the findings.
