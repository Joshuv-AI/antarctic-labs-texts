Pilonidal sinus beyond the sacrococcygeal region: A penile case report
Authors: Fadi Sawaqed, Omar Hamdan, Anas Taleb Albudairat, Malik Ayyad, Abdalrhman Albassomi, Lana Alzayadneh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPilonidal sinus disease (PSD) is an acquired pathological illness that typically manifests in the natal cleft of the sacrococcygeal region, but it can rarely occur in other locations, including the penis. We report a 30-year-old circumcised man with a painless, slowly enlarging penile shaft mass. Excision under local anesthesia revealed a hair-containing sinus with chronic granulomatous inflammation on histology, confirming pilonidal disease. Recovery was uneventful. Although uncommon, penile pilonidal sinus should be considered in the differential diagnosis of chronic penile lesions. Histopathology is essential for confirmation, and complete surgical excision provides a definitive cure.Keywords: Pilonidal sinus, Penis, Mass, Histopathology

Background

The pilonidal sinus is a chronic inflammatory condition that results from the subcutaneous entrapment of hair.1 Most commonly, it occurs in the sacrococcygeal region or natal cleft, but rarely in the penis, with less than 30 cases reported worldwide.2,3 Awareness of this atypical location is important because misdiagnosis may delay treatment and increase morbidity.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old circumcised Mediterranean man, medically free and otherwise healthy, presented to the urology clinic with a one-year history of a slowly enlarging, painless mid-shaft penile mass. Four months earlier, a general practitioner had prescribed doxycycline and azithromycin for presumed infection without improvement. He denied discharge, dysuria, any history of local trauma, or other urologic symptoms and reported sexual activity with multiple partners. His body mass index (BMI) was 22.4 kg/m2, within the normal range. A comprehensive sexually transmitted infection (STI) screen, including HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B/C, chlamydia, and gonorrhea, was negative.

On physical examination, a mobile, red, well-demarcated lump (0.7 × 0.7 × 1 cm) was observed on the shaft of the penis, without any tenderness, and there was no evidence of palpable inguinal lymphadenopathy (Fig. 1). The lesion was excised under local anesthesia through a small skin incision and closed with 4–0 undyed Vicryl. Histopathology demonstrated squamous epithelium with dense mixed acute and chronic inflammatory infiltrates and foreign-body giant-cell reaction surrounding hair shafts, with no dysplasia or malignancy (Fig. 2). Cultures for bacterial, fungal, and actinomycotic organisms were negative.

Follow-up at two weeks showed complete healing, and no recurrence was observed at six months. Written informed consent for publication was obtained.

Discussion

Pilonidal sinus disease (PSD) is a chronic dermatological condition most commonly observed in the sacrococcygeal area. However, it has been reported in the nose, abdominal wall, groin, axilla, intramammary region, feet, scalp, and, rarely, the penis.4 Penile pilonidal sinus is a rare entity, almost exclusively described in isolated case reports, with fewer than thirty cases documented worldwide.3,5 Its pathogenesis parallels that of sacrococcygeal disease: loose hairs gather in the coronal sulcus or preputial fold, and repetitive mechanical friction drives these hairs into the epidermis, inciting a chronic foreign-body granulomatous reaction.4,6,7

Multiple risk factors have been implicated in pilonidal sinus development and pathogenesis, including male sex, obesity, poor hygiene, sedentary lifestyle, trauma, and hirsutism.8, 9, 10 Although most penile cases have been reported in uncircumcised men,3 our patient was circumcised and otherwise healthy, highlighting that circumcision does not confer absolute protection. His normal BMI, lack of systemic illness, and absence of any traumatic history further underscore that penile pilonidal sinus can occur without the usual predisposing factors.

Clinically, penile pilonidal sinus could present with numerous manifestations, which include abscess formation, discharge, dyspareunia, penile swelling, phimosis, and, in rare cases, erectile dysfunction.5,7,11, 12, 13 The overlapping clinical features with other penile conditions, such as balanoposthitis, epidermoid cyst, penile abscess, and even malignancy, can contribute to diagnostic delay.5 Further distinction from other penile lesions is essential to avoid misdiagnosis. Benign cystic lesions, such as epidermoid or median raphe cysts, are typically smooth, well-circumscribed, and asymptomatic subcutaneous nodules that lack sinus openings or visible hair shafts14. Epidermoid cysts arise within the dermis and are lined by mature stratified squamous epithelium with a granular layer and laminated keratin but show no hair shafts, foreign-body reaction, or urethral connection14,15. Median raphe cysts, by contrast, are midline, translucent, and generally asymptomatic lesions lined by pseudostratified columnar or mucinous epithelium16. In comparison, pilonidal sinus lesions display sinus tracts containing multiple hair shafts within inflamed granulation tissue, surrounded by multinucleated foreign-body giant cells and fibrosis11. Clinically, penile pilonidal sinus may mimic balanoposthitis, dorsal vein thrombosis, inclusion cysts, or even malignancy; however, a history of chronic irritation, hair entrapment, or prior ulceration helps distinguish it from congenital or neoplastic processes17. Malignant tumors such as squamous cell carcinoma or Kaposi's sarcoma typically present as irregular, ulcerative, or exophytic lesions with induration or bleeding, most often in older or immunocompromised patients14.

Preoperative imaging, such as ultrasound, can sometimes demonstrate sinus tracts, hair-like echogenic structures, or associated abscesses3,18; however, definitive diagnosis still relies on histopathologic examination of the excised tissue.11,12 In this case, a negative STI panel and sterile cultures confirmed the absence of an infectious etiology and supported purely hair-driven pathogenesis.

Management of penile pilonidal sinus is primarily surgical, with complete excision and primary closure, along with circumcision when indicated, providing a definitive cure and an almost negligible risk of recurrence.3,12 Two reported cases treated with drainage alone developed recurrence, underscoring the need for complete excision.3,13 One recurrent case associated with Actinomyces israelii was successfully managed with a 10-day course of crystallized penicillin G followed by excision and primary closure.13

Delayed diagnosis can lead to abscess formation, ulceration, and secondary actinomycotic infection.6 Although uncommon, malignant transformation, most often to squamous cell carcinoma, has been reported in pilonidal sinuses at other anatomical sites,19,20 but never in penile lesions.

Conclusion

Penile pilonidal sinus is a rare but clinically significant condition that can mimic more common penile lesions. This case shows that it may occur in healthy, circumcised men with normal BMI, no history of trauma, and no systemic risk factors. The absence of pathogens in the excised tissue supports a purely hair-driven inflammatory process. Early recognition, histopathologic confirmation, and complete surgical excision are essential for successful outcomes. Continued reporting of similar cases will aid in establishing standardized management and follow-up strategies.
