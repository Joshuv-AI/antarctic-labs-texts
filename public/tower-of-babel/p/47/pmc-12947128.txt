From morphine to buprenorphine - Modeling opioid use disorder and its treatment during pregnancy: Effects on maternal care and offspring neurodevelopment in a translational rodent model
Authors: Lauren M Richardson, Abigail M Myers, Jecenia Duran, Deep K Patel, Kit L Tran, Ella R Walsh, Shane A Perrine, Scott E Bowen, Susanne Brummelte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpioid use during pregnancy has increased drastically in the last two decades. Pregnant women who use opioids are often prescribed Medications for Opioid Use Disorder (MOUDs), including buprenorphine (BUP), to mitigate negative effects on the fetus. However, BUP exposure during pregnancy may still negatively impact maternal care behavior and offspring neurodevelopment. In the current study, we used a translational rodent model to investigate the effects of continued morphine or BUP use from preconception (7 days prior to mating) to the early postpartum period, as well as the transition from morphine to BUP during early pregnancy (gestational day (GD) 5), on both maternal care behaviors and acute offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes. Our results reveal that exposure to BUP beginning before pregnancy or on GD5 resulted in decreased nesting quality, maternal motivation, and pup-directed care behaviors as compared to controls. For the offspring, BUP-exposure resulted in increased pup mortality, more neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome-like (NOWS) symptoms, altered norepinephrine levels in the brain, and decreased offspring weight, body length, and presence of milk bands compared to vehicle pups. Importantly, maternal care behavior was significantly correlated with offspring mortality, physical maturation, and NOWS-like scores, suggesting that at least some of the adverse effects were driven by impairments in maternal care. Morphine-exposed dams and pups showed overall fewer impairments compared to BUP-exposed dams and pups. This highlights that more research is needed to further understand the unique impact of BUP on the maternal brain and subsequent infant outcomes to mitigate potential adverse effects in pregnant women with MOUD prescriptions.Keywords: Opioids, Medication for opioid use disorders (MOUD), Gestation, Maternal care, Early life adversity, Prenatal opioid exposure, Neonatal opioid withdrawal symptoms

Introduction

Opioid Use Disorder (OUD) diagnoses during pregnancy increased 4-fold from 1999 to 2014 (Haight et al., 2018; Krans and Patrick, 2016) and was associated with increases in maternal and infant complications, such as preterm labor, stillbirths, congenital malformations, and Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS), along with long-term cognitive and behavioral issues in prenatally exposed children (Baldacchino et al., 2014; Larson et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2020; Lind et al., 2017; Whiteman et al., 2014). Women with an opioid use disorder have been recommended to transition to a Medication for Opioid Use Disorder (MOUD), such as Buprenorphine (BUP), to mitigate the negative outcomes associated with opioid use during pregnancy. Buprenorphine results in more favorable infant outcomes than methadone or continuing opioid use (Brogly et al., 2014; Gaalema et al., 2012; Hall et al., 2016; Jones et al., 2010; Welle-Strand et al., 2013). However, despite evidence of preferable infant outcomes, the effect of MOUD use during pregnancy on maternal-infant bonding and attachment is critically understudied. Opioid use (i.e., morphine or BUP) during pregnancy is associated with alterations and/or impairments in maternal-infant dyad attachment and interactions, decreased parental involvement, and reduced maternal responsiveness (Mirick and Steenrod, 2016), which in turn may impact child development and behavioral outcomes (Romanowicz et al., 2019). Importantly, evidence suggests that infant outcomes are improved when maternal-infant bonding and attachment is prioritized (Newman et al., 2022). Thus, research on maternal outcomes in the context of opioid use during pregnancy is crucial, especially when considering the fact that opioid-using mothers may face stigma from society and in hospital settings during and after birth (Banwell and Bammer, 2006; Haycraft, 2018). It is imperative that this topic is handled with care with the goal of de-stigmatization in the quest for improved outcomes for both mother and child.

The transition from a nulliparous brain to maternal brain is a delicate, precise process that has been studied extensively in the preclinical literature. This transformation is regulated by endogenous opioids (endorphins, dynorphins, enkephalins), which bind to the three main opioid receptors: mu, kappa, and delta (Brunton, 2019; Mansour et al., 1994). The widespread distribution of opioid receptors within the maternal brain network (MBN) makes this delicate system vulnerable to exogenous opioid exposure (see reviews by: Brunton, 2019; Wallin et al., 2021). Animal studies have demonstrated that exposure to exogenous opioids before, during, and after pregnancy has the potential to dysregulate the MBN and maternal care behaviors, subsequently impacting offspring outcomes (Chen et al., 2015; Grimm and Bridges, 1983; Myers et al., 2024; Sobor et al., 2010; Wallin et al., 2019). Indeed, deficits in appropriate maternal care behaviors, particularly nursing and licking/grooming behaviors, have been shown following administration of morphine (Bridges and Grimm, 1982; Chen et al., 2015; Slamberova et al., 2003; Sobor et al., 2010) or BUP during pregnancy (Chen et al., 2015; Myers et al., 2024; Wallin et al., 2019). However, many studies fail to examine the impact of maternal care behavior on offspring outcomes in the context of opioid use during pregnancy (see Myers et al., 2023 for review).

The switch from an opioid of abuse (e.g., morphine) to a MOUD (e.g., BUP) during pregnancy is drastically understudied, particularly in terms of the effects on maternal care behaviors and offspring outcomes compared to the outcomes after either continued opioid exposure (opioid of abuse or MOUD), or a switch to a vehicle administration. To our knowledge, only one other study has investigated a switch from an illicit opioid to a MOUD during pregnancy (Schlagal et al., 2021). This study was done in mice and results showed that the transition from oxycodone (10.0 mg/kg) before pregnancy to BUP (0.5–4.0 mg/kg) immediately following pregnancy confirmation resulted in impairment of fetal brain development and slight modifications in maternal care behaviors (Schlagal et al., 2021). However, there was a lack of sufficient control groups in that study to account for oxycodone or BUP exposure by themselves. Further, expecting mothers typically do not find out about their pregnancy until several weeks after conception during which time they may continue using an illicit opioid before being able to transition to BUP/MOUD treatment. Thus, we aimed to mimic this change from illicit opioid to MOUD in our animal model by switching drugs in the early stages of pregnancy (i.e. on gestational day (GD) 5, to roughly mimic the time in humans when a woman would discover she is pregnant (i.e., ~ 6–8 weeks of pregnancy). Additionally, previous studies initiated opioid administration around GD5 because it is prior to the development of the endogenous opioid system.

BUP was chosen due to the lack of literature on maternal care behaviors and subsequent offspring outcomes following administration/exposure to this MOUD. As a semi-synthetic MOUD, BUP acts as a partial mu-agonist, a kappa- and delta-opioid antagonist, and an OLR1 agonist (Lutfy and Cowan, 2004). Its unique pharmacological profile, with slow dissociation kinetics at mu receptors, may result in outcomes for both dams and pups that are distinct compared to abuse opioids, including morphine. Previous studies from our laboratory revealed higher mortality and neurodevelopmental deficits, increased NOW-like symptoms, and decreased maternal care behaviors and nest building following once-daily gestational exposure to 1.0 mg/kg BUP compared to controls (Myers et al., 2024; Wallin et al., 2019).

In the current study, we investigated perinatal opioid exposure (Preconception Day (PC) 1 to Postnatal Day (PND) 2) to better mimic typical human patterns of opioid use and encompass the full spectrum of opioid effects preconception through early postpartum. We used 1.0 mg/kg BUP to replicate previous findings from our lab and others (Robinson and Wallace, 2001; Sanchez et al., 2008; Wallin et al., 2019) and to represent an approximate human dose equivalent (see method section for detailed calculation). Rats develop a rapid tolerance to morphine, so we used an increasing dose of morphine (3–10.0 mg/kg) as this range/these doses have been utilized in previous literature and have produced deficits in maternal care and offspring development (Ahmadalipour et al., 2015; Chiang et al., 2010; Slamberova et al., 2005; Smith et al., 2023).

Adult female rats were pseudo-randomly assigned to one of five experimental groups: saline-vehicle (VEH), BUP continuous (BC), morphine continuous (MC), morphine to BUP (MB), or morphine to vehicle (MV). The VEH, BC, and MC groups were administered saline or drugs 7 days before pregnancy and throughout gestation until PND2 which accounts for the effects of only BUP or morphine exposure before, during, and after pregnancy, as may be seen in mothers who are already maintained on a MOUD when they get pregnant (BC) or who continue to use illicit opioids throughout pregnancy (MC). The MB group was administered morphine until GD4, after which they were switched to BUP starting on GD5. This group was designed to mimic the human situation, in which an opioid-using woman finds out she is pregnant and decides to start a MOUD. The MV group received morphine until GD4 and then switched to saline for the rest of the experiment. While the MV group may not be very clinically or translationally relevant, as abrupt discontinuation of opioids during gestation is not typically recommended, it serves as a necessary control for the MB group to account for pregestational and early gestational morphine-exposure.

These exposure paradigms allowed us to examine how distinct patterns of opioid exposure impact maternal and offspring physiological and behavioral outcomes. We analyzed maternal nest building, maternal care and motivation, as well as the dams’ nociceptive and stress responses. Paw-withdrawal latencies in the hot-plate test for nociceptive behavior can reveal changes in opioid efficacy during pregnancy and the postpartum, while measuring corticosterone in response to a restraint stress test will shed further light on opioids’ interference with HPA axis function. Previous studies suggest that chronic opioid and/or MOUD use can lead to adrenal insufficiency (Coluzzi et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2008), which in turn could influence maternal behaviors and offspring outcomes.

For the offspring, we aimed to analyze survival rates, body weights and lengths, surface righting, withdrawal symptoms and monoamine concentrations in the offspring’s brains. Dopaminergic, serotonergic, and norepinephrine systems play major roles in early brain development including aiding in synaptogenesis, neuronal proliferation, and migration (Gaspar et al., 2003; Herlenius and Lagercrantz, 2004; Robertson et al., 2013) and given that the timing of the development of these neurotransmitter systems corresponds with the development of the endogenous opioid system (Clendeninn et al., 1976; Olson and Seiger, 1972; Tan et al., 2018), they may be particularly sensitive to exogenous opioid exposure. Therefore, measuring levels of these monoamines in the neonatal brains of opioid-exposed offspring may give us an important insight into opioid-related changes to early brain development.

Our previous data revealed that discontinuing morphine administration 3 days before birth rescued maternal care and offspring developmental outcomes (Myers et al., 2024). Thus, we hypothesized that the MV group would have better outcomes than our MB, MC, and BC groups, and similar outcomes as our VEH group. Due to the higher dose of morphine compared to our previous study (Myers et al., 2024), we expected the MC group to exhibit worse maternal care and offspring outcomes compared to the BC, MB, MV, or VEH groups, though we still expected impairments in BUP-exposed dams given our previous findings (Myers et al., 2024). Taken together, this project investigated the effects of morphine, BUP, and the shift from an opioid of abuse to BUP on maternal care behavior and subsequent offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes using a translational rodent model in hopes of improving treatment for pregnant women dependent on opioids.

Methods

Subjects

A total of N = 50 female and N = 10 male Sprague-Dawley rats, weighing approximately 200–250 g, were purchased from Charles River Laboratory, Wilmington MA. Rats were kept in a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 06:00 h), 55 % humidity, 22.2 °C. Food (Purina Rodent Chow) and water were provided ad libitum. Rats were housed according to sex (3–4 per cage upon arrival) and were given 4–5 days to habituate before the start of experiments. All procedures were approved by the Wayne State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol #22–05-4630) and were in accordance with the principles and guidelines established by the National Institute of Health for preclinical research (NIH, 2015).

Drug preparation and administration

BUP (Chemical Abstracts Service Registry Number (CASRN): 161772–95–8) and morphine (CASRN: 80573–75–7) were obtained from the NIDA Drug Supply Program (https://nida.nih.gov/research/research-data-measures-resources/nida-drug-supply-program). Liquid solutions were prepared in-house using a serial dilution method to obtain drug concentrations of 1.0 mg/ml for each drug and dose. Fresh solutions were made approximately once a week and were stored at room temperature. Solutions were given as subcutaneous (s.c.) injections just above the hip on the dorsal posterior surface of the rat using 1-ml sterile plastic syringes and 26-gauge needles. The injection site was alternated between the right and left hip for each injection. Solution volumes were adjusted every day according to individual weight gain throughout pregnancy. Volumes were adjusted again after birth until sacrifice to account for weight loss.

Rats receiving BUP (1.0 mg/kg) were administered the drug once daily in the morning, and saline was administered in the evening to equalize handling effects. Since morphine (3–10.0 mg/kg) has a shorter half-life than BUP (Elkader and Sproule, 2005; Iwamoto and Klaassen, 1977), rats receiving morphine were administered drug twice daily (once in the early morning (~7:00), and once in the late afternoon (~15:45)). Due to quick tolerance build-up, morphine doses were increased by 1.0 mg/kg every 3–4 days. Virgin female rats started with 3.0 mg/kg seven days prior to mating and were then maintained at 4.0 mg/kg of morphine until they became pregnant. The dose was then increased throughout pregnancy with the final dose of 10.0 mg/kg being reached on GD21 (see Fig. 1A). The chosen BUP and morphine doses are similar to doses used in the literature and have been shown to elicit maternal care deficits, offspring mortality, and offspring developmental delays (Ahmadalipour et al., 2015; Hung et al., 2013; Myers et al., 2024; Slamberova et al., 2005; Smith et al., 2023; Wallin et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2014). Further, the BUP dose was calculated to closely mimic human exposure levels, as pregnant women receive an average of 15.0 mg/day of BUP (with ranges from 1 to 24.0 mg) (Martin et al., 2020) which equals ~0.2 mg/kg (based on 70 kg body weight of pregnant person). If multiplied by 6 to account for increased metabolism in rats (Nair and Jacob, 2016), this results in a dose of 1.2 mg/kg for a rat. Thus, our 1.0 mg/kg dose is within the desired equivalent range for human expectant mothers. Control females received 1.0 ml/kg of saline twice daily. All behavioral testing (except maternal care observations which were spread across the day) was conducted during the period of acute opioid effect (i.e., within 3–4 h after drug administration, see Fig. 1B).

Adult female rats were pseudo-randomly assigned to one of the following five experimental groups based on weight at the start of the experiment: BUP continuous (BC, 1.0 mg/kg q.a.m., n = 10), morphine continuous (MC, 3–10.0 mg/kg, b.i.d, n = 10), morphine to BUP (MB; morphine: 3–5.0 mg/kg, b.i.d.; BUP: 1.0 mg/kg; q.a.m.; n = 10), morphine to saline vehicle (MV; morphine: 3–5.0 mg/kg, b.i.d; saline: 1.0 mg/kg, b.i.d; n = 10) or saline-vehicle (VEH, 1.0 ml/kg, b.i.d, n = 10). The BC, MC, and VEH groups began administration 7 days prior to mating and continued administration until PND2. The MB group began morphine administration 7 days prior to mating, then switched to BUP administration on GD5 and continued BUP administration until PND2. The MV group began morphine administration 7 days prior to mating and then switched to saline on GD5 and continued saline administration until PND2.

Breeding, culling, and postnatal procedure

Rats were bred in-house starting on the 7th day of drug administration. One female was paired with one male overnight, and mating continued until sperm was identified via vaginal lavage, which was indicated as GD0. Females were checked for sperm via vaginal lavage each morning. Females continued to be housed overnight with the same male until sperm was observed, if a female was not pregnant within 4 days, she was switched to a new male. The maximum breeding period was 8 days total before the rat was removed from the study (n = 1).

The day of parturition was indicated as PND0. In the morning of PND1, litters were culled to 4 males and 4 females where possible. After completion of all behavioral testing on PND2, dams and pups were sacrificed via live decapitation, and truck blood and brains were collected for further analyses.

Maternal characteristics and behavior

Nesting behavior and maternal care observations

Dams were weighed and assessed daily, beginning on GD0, for gestational length, well-being, and birthing and litter outcomes were documented. Dams were video monitored from GD19-PND2, and additionally their maternal care behaviors were live-scored daily from PND0–PND2. Nesting behavior and nest scores were evaluated from GD20-PND2. Scoring (0–4) indicated the level of nesting proficiency as defined by the following criteria: “0” - nesting material was not opened, “1”- a flat nest, “2” - localization of nesting materials, “3” - both localization of nesting materials and a definition of nest edge, and “4” - large and complex nest (Brancato and Cannizzaro, 2018). Beginning on PND0, dams were monitored for timing of birth, signs of caregiving initiation (cleaning pups), or consumption of deceased pups. The following behaviors were evaluated until PND2 - time on/off nest, licking/grooming pups, active/passive nursing, and sleeping off nest.

Live observations were scored by a researcher during 30-min observation sessions, 4-times per day (between 08:00–16:00 h) from PND0-PND1, and 2×/day on PND2 (10 total sessions for each dam). Each dams’ behavior was coded once per minute throughout each session (N = 120 observations per day (PND0-PND1), N = 60 observations per day (PND2)). At least 60 min were allotted between each session. The frequency total for each behavior was summed and divided by the total number of observations to calculate the percentage of time a dam spent engaged in a certain behavior. Maternal care behavior was then further organized by percentage of time spent partaking in pup-directed behaviors (average of on nest, licking/grooming pups, arched back nursing, and passive/blanket nursing) and non-pup directed behaviors (average of off nest and sleeping off nest).

Nociception: Hot-plate test

Animals were evaluated for a nociceptive response to a Hot-Plate (Life Science, IITC Hot Plate Analgesia Meter) test before conception (PC7), during gestation (GD7) and in the postpartum (PND1) to assess opioid-induced analgesia. This test was included as a crude measure of opioid (morphine) efficacy during the different reproductive stages. Each rat was placed onto the Hot-Plate (55 °C) and latency to nociceptive response (hind or forepaw withdrawal/licking and/or stamping) was recorded (Espejo and Mir, 1993), with a cutoff of 60-s. One day prior to baseline testing, female rodents were acclimated to the Hot-Plate apparatus to limit novelty stress.

Olfaction preference test

On PND1, dams were placed into the center division of a two-choice apparatus with a plastic filter top. One side was filled with clean bedding and the other side held pup-soiled bedding from their home cage. Bedding type was counterbalanced for each side of the apparatus between dams (Kinsley and Bridges, 1990). Time spent on each side, total number of median crossings, and percent difference from chance (i.e., chance being defined as 50 % on each side) were documented for a total of 5-min. Dams underwent habituation to the apparatus prior to testing (GD19) to prevent novelty stress.

Pup retrieval test

On PND1 and PND2, dams were temporarily removed from their home cage and pups were scattered equidistantly from the nest. The dam was then reintroduced and the percentage of pups a dam returned to her nest and the latency (seconds) to return each pup was measured using a 600-s cut-off time. On PND2, this assay was conducted immediately following the stress restraint test (described below).

Stress-restraint test

One day prior to testing (PND1), the rats’ hind legs were shaved in preparation for blood collection. On PND2, rats were placed in a rodent-specific clear acrylic restrainer (Plas Labs, 554 BSRR) for 30 min in order to assess hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function in response to stress by analyzing corticosterone (CORT) levels. Using a 23-gauge needle, three blood samples of 100–200 μl were collected from the saphenous vein at 3 different time points: baseline (immediately prior to being placed in the restrainer tube), peak (immediately following removal from the tube), and recovery (60-min after return to the home cage). Blood-draws were kept under 3 min to limit handling effects. The site of blood collection was alternated between each hind leg on each time-point of interest. One hour after collection, blood was centrifuged (2500G for 15-min) and the serum was extracted and frozen at −20 °C until analyzed.

Corticosterone analysis

Serum from the stress-restraint test was analyzed for CORT levels using an Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA K014, Ann Arbor Assays) following manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were run in duplicate and inter-assay coefficients were set to <10 %.

Opioid concentration analysis

Serum samples from trunk blood collected on PND2 (dams and pups) were sent to the Lumigen Center at Wayne State University for opioid concentration analysis using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The average concentrations per group were calculated after subtracting the detection limit from each value (BUP detection limit = 0.05 ppb; MS detection limit = 0.10 ppb).

Offspring measurements

Litter assessment

Litters were assessed for maturation, development, and mortality from PND0-PND2. On PND1, pups were sexed via anogenital distance assessment. Percent missing pups (i.e., percent of pups per group that were missing from the cage on PND1) for each group was documented. On PND1 and PND2, pups were individually weighed (g) and body length was measured (cm; from the tip of the nose to the base of the tail).

Milk band assessment

On the mornings of PND0-PND2, individual pups were inspected for the presence of milk bands (“0” indicated no milk band present and “1” indicated the presence of a milk band) and the percentage of pups per litter to have milk bands was calculated.

Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome

In the morning on PND1, remaining pups (after culling) were observed for NOWS symptoms (~90–200 min after drug administration to the dam). Pups were placed in a plastic holding bin on top of a heating pad with separate compartments filled with bedding. Pups were videotaped for 15 min, and behaviors were recorded in increments of 30 s. Behaviors included hyperactivity, tremors, vocalization, mouth movements, stretching, and “face-washing” (Robinson and Wallace, 2001). Pups were given a score of “0” (no) or “1” (yes) if they exhibited the withdrawal behavior within the 30-s window. To ensure inter-rater reliability, one researcher live-coded the session, and a different researcher re-coded the videos. Scores were then compared, and any discrepancies were revisited and discussed until both researchers came to an agreed upon consensus. Withdrawal scores were summed for each individual behavior (0–15), and these scores were summed together to create a composite score for all NOWS symptoms (0–105 per sex group; 0–210 per whole litter). The data were corrected using the control group (VEH) as the baseline measurement and scores were calculated to reflect percent difference from baseline.

Surface righting

On PND2, pups underwent a surface righting task as a measure of neuromuscular development (Heyser, 2004). Pups were placed in a supine position (i.e., on their back) and the latency to right themselves to a prone position (i.e., upright on all four paws) was recorded with a 60-s cut-off.

Offspring monoamine and metabolite concentration analysis

PND2 pups were euthanized via live decapitation, and brains were removed and immediately frozen on dry ice until further processing. Two-millimeter brain slices were obtained using a neonatal rat brain matrix, and 1 mm diameter bilateral punches were taken from the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and striatum using the Atlas of the Developing Rat Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates: PND2 (Khazipov et al., 2015) as a guide. Brain punches were weighed and stored at −80 °C freezer until analysis. Pup brain samples were analyzed for the following monoamine and metabolite concentrations using High-Pressure Liquid Chromatography (HPLC): dopamine (DA) and its metabolites 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC), 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT), and homovanillic acid (HVA); serotonin (5HT) and its metabolite 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA); and norepinephrine (NE) as previously described in (Davidson et al., 2022). The samples were analyzed using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 HPLC system’s autosampler at 5 °C (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Standards for all monoamines and metabolites described above were prepared using serial dilution and run before and after each tissue analysis. A detection threshold of 2 times baseline was set, and standard curve equations were created for all monoamines and metabolites. Tissue levels of monoamines and metabolites were expressed as ng (neurochemical)/mg (wet tissue weight).

Data analysis

Continuous data (e.g., body weight, pup retrieval percentage and latency, etc.) was analyzed using a one-way ANOVA, repeated measures ANOVA (RM), or covariate ANOVA (ANCOVA) when appropriate (i.e., controlling for litter size). Frequency counts or ordinal scoring (e.g., nest scores, etc.) were analyzed using Kruskal Wallis tests (for comparison between 3+ groups) or a Mann-Whitney U Test (for comparison between 2 groups). Percentage data was transformed using arcsine transformation before being analyzed. If the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated, the Welch or Brown-Forsythe correction was applied. If the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was utilized. Data was screened for outliers and extreme scores (more than 3 standard deviations from the group mean) were excluded from further analyses.

Male and female offspring data were averaged per sex for each measurement and all offspring analyses included sex as a between subjects factor. Treatment Group was used as the initial between subjects factor for the entirety of the conducted analyses after GD5 (i.e., BC, MC, MB, MV, and VEH). For all analyses prior to GD5, Opioid Type (Morphine vs. BUP vs. VEH) was used as the initial between subjects factor because MB, MV and MC all received morphine up until GD5. Due to our a priori hypotheses, for all analysis past GD5, if a significant result was not identified by Treatment Group, we repeated the same tests with Opioid Type at Birth (i.e. Morphine vs. BUP (MB + BC) vs. MV vs. VEH) as a follow-up analysis. MC and MV were not combined for the Opioid Type at Birth analysis due to MV acting as a control group for the MB group. If Opioid Type at Birth was non-significant, a final follow-up analysis was conducted comparing all opioid groups (“Drug”, including MV) to VEH. Significant main or interaction effects were followed by Tukey’s or LSD (for repeated measure analyses with covariates) post hoc analyses. No direct comparisons were made between the BC and MV groups during follow-up analysis as they varied on more than one factor.

Correlations between maternal care behavior and offspring outcomes were analyzed using either Spearman Rho’s for frequency data (mortality) or Pearson’s correlation for continuous data. All data was analyzed using SPSS (IBM Corp.) version 28 and results were considered significant if p < 0.05.

Results

Litter exclusion and reassignment

Two litters did not have enough of one sex to cull down to a 4:4 sex ratio (males: females), and therefore had a different sex ratio after the culling procedure on PND1. Additionally, a total of 8 litters had less than 8 pups and therefore could not be culled down to a 4:4 sex ratio but were still included in the analysis.

A total of 8 dams were excluded from analysis due to failure to become pregnant (n = 1, VEH group, no sperm detected after 8 mating days) or failure to give birth (n = 7). Of the seven rats that failed to give birth [MB: n = 3, MV: n = 2, MC: n = 1, VEH: n = 1], all had clear evidence of sperm on their lavage samples. Further, the three MB rats exhibited clear weight increases in early pregnancy but then plateaued following the switch to BUP. However, the other 4 rats did not show the expected pregnancy-related weight gain. None of the seven rats gave birth which led to their exclusion from the study.

Additionally, we observed one unexpected dam death following birth (MV: n = 1), and several litters with high pup mortality rates (MB: n = 2, MC: n = 1), which led to missing data for those cases for some PD1 and PD2 measures. Lastly, one rat that was originally assigned to the MB group had no clear sperm on her lavage slides, so her morphine treatment was discontinued after 8 days of mating. However, her weight gain suggested she had an undetected pregnancy, so we started administering the vehicle again 6 days later. She eventually gave birth, so we backwards calculated that her opioid administration had been discontinued on either her GD5 or GD6. Thus, we assigned her to the MV group. This resulted in the following final sample sizes for each group: BC (n = 10), MB (n = 6 on PND0, n = 5 on PND1, n = 4 PND2), MC (n = 9 on PND0 and 1, n = 8 on PND2), MV (n = 8 on PND0, n = 7 on PND1 and 2), and VEH (n = 8).

Maternal physiology and behavior data

Preconception and early gestation characteristics

An analysis of weight during preconception and early gestation (PC1-GD4) was conducted utilizing RM ANOVA with a between-subjects factor of Opioid Type and PC1 weight as a significant covariate [F (1,34) = 143.407, p < 0.001]. No main effects or interactions were observed between Day or Opioid Type, indicating that drug exposure did not significantly impact weight change during this period (p’s > 0.05, depicted in Fig. 2A). Days to conception was not significant at the level of Opioid Type or Drug (all p’s > 0.05).

Discussion

This study investigated the effects of perinatal (PC1-PND2) opioid exposure on maternal care, behavior, and physiology, and how these factors relate to and potentially influence offspring outcomes. The findings reveal that BUP exposure initiated prior to pregnancy or in early gestation and sustained throughout the postpartum period significantly disrupted maternal care and offspring outcomes. Dams exposed to BUP exhibited reduced gestational weight gain, impaired nesting proficiency, and diminished maternal behaviors, including pup directed care and retrieval compared to controls. Offspring prenatally exposed to BUP displayed higher rates of mortality, increased NOWS-like symptoms, and decreased weight, body length, and presence of milk bands. Notably, these offspring outcomes were negatively correlated with maternal care levels, suggesting that at least some of the adverse effects were driven by the impairments in maternal care. Interestingly, transitioning from morphine to BUP during pregnancy did not mitigate maternal or offspring deficits compared to continuous opioid exposure; rather, it exacerbated certain outcomes, producing some of the most severe effects observed. Continuous morphine exposure reduced late-gestational weight gain and maternal care behaviors on PND2 in dams. Similar to BUP, offspring continuously exposed to morphine displayed reductions in body weight and length. Further, pups exposed to any opioid in utero (including the MV pups) had lower NE levels in the PFC and higher NE levels in the striatum compared to VEH, suggesting that some offspring outcomes may be a direct result of the drug exposure during gestation (and not related to maternal care). Interestingly, morphine exposure that was discontinued in early pregnancy resulted in no differences from the controls in maternal care or offspring development on any measurements. Overall, the study highlights that continuous BUP exposure from pre-pregnancy through the postpartum period, as well as transitioning from morphine to BUP during pregnancy, poses significant risks to both the dam and offspring, and that these effects appear not to be attributable to early pregnancy morphine exposure alone (see Table 3 for overview of main findings).

Maternal physiology

Noteworthy, three dams in the MB group may have spontaneously aborted their litters following the transition to BUP on GD5. These dams exhibited clear sperm on their lavage slides and indicated initial weight gain during gestation; however, their weight gain plateaued mid-gestation and they never gave birth. In addition, two MV rats also had clear sperm, but failed to give birth, though these rats did not display obvious weight gain (although weight gain is subtle during the first week of pregnancy). This may be attributable to the consequence of morphine withdrawal during the implantation phase of gestation, as morphine has been shown to impair embryo implantation in mice (Tang et al., 2015). However, because necropsy was not performed, spontaneous abortions cannot be confirmed in this study. Future studies should further investigate this potential effect on implantation issues with opioid/morphine exposure.

Our finding of lower body weights in the BUP group compared to controls beginning on GD11 and reduced body weight in late gestation in the morphine-continued group is in line with previous findings of reduced weight in BUP exposed dams around parturition (Chiang et al., 2010; Myers et al., 2024; Wallin et al., 2019) and studies reporting decreased gestational weight gain and food and water intake in morphine exposed dams (Chiang et al., 2010; Smith et al., 2023). This anticipated impact on weight gain is likely due to several factors playing an influential role such as reduced food intake, withdrawal symptoms and pharmacokinetic changes during pregnancy.

Opioid treatment in the present study produced no significant effect on hot-plate withdrawal latency across groups during the preconception, gestation, or postpartum periods, indicating that the chosen doses for the opioids did not appear to change pain perception in our rats. Though the lack of a significant nociceptive effects for these opioids may seem counterintuitive, it is important to note that our preconception morphine dose (4.0 mg/kg) was lower than the standard analgesic dose of at least 5.0 mg/kg (Gardmark et al., 1998) and we saw a lot of variability in our vehicle group, which may have overshadowed a morphine effect on nociception during preconception. Further, in the postpartum period, dams were on drug treatment for approximately 30 days (from 3.0 to 10.0 mg/kg) and chronic morphine administration has been shown to induce hyperalgesia in both rats (Kayan et al., 1971) and humans (Mao et al., 1995) meaning rats develop a heightened nociceptive response despite morphine being onboard. This hyperalgesia may partially explain why our morphine dams exhibited a significantly increased nociceptive response (i.e. faster paw withdrawal) on PND1 compared to PC7.

Importantly, serum opioid levels in dams confirmed that our doses were appropriate for mimicking MOUD levels (for BUP) and abuse ranges (for morphine), see Table 2. We also measured opioid concentration in pups and morphine levels in the serum at PND2 were significantly and positively correlated with levels in dams, while BUP serum levels were positively correlated albeit not significantly. However, it is important to note that the small sample sizes for some groups (MB) and high variability within groups, which might be due to variations in time between last injection and time of sacrifice (that varied slightly based on the number of dams that needed to be tested and/or sacrificed that day).

Litter characteristics

BUP exposure during gestation significantly increased offspring mortality, whether administration began before pregnancy or early in gestation. This finding builds on our previous research and is consistent with earlier studies using the same BUP dose (1.0 mg/kg) despite varied administration routes (Chen et al., 2015; Chiang et al., 2010; Hutchings et al., 1996; Robinson and Wallace, 2001). However, other studies have reported no differences in offspring mortality following BUP exposure, though they utilized lower doses or different administration routes and paradigms (Hutchings et al., 1995; Kongstorp et al., 2019; Schlagal et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2023). It is crucial for future research to report postnatal mortality rates, maternal care behaviors, and/or whether cross-fostering was implemented. Although offspring mortality rates may partly result from direct opioid exposure in utero, the role of maternal care cannot be dismissed. Since most previous studies have not reported on maternal care behaviors, it remains unclear whether pup survival rates in these studies were influenced by variations in maternal care.

Continuous morphine exposure during pregnancy did not result in significant differences in offspring mortality compared to controls in our study, which aligns with previous studies using similar doses (Chen et al., 2015; Chiang et al., 2010; Myers et al., 2024). However, older studies using higher doses of morphine (10–40 mg/kg, s.c.) indicate high mortality rates in offspring of exposed dams (Eriksson and Ronnback, 1989; Sobrian, 1977), suggesting a strong dose-dependent effect for morphine on offspring vitality.

Litter size did not differ across groups; however, the MV and BC groups had significantly more females than the MC and VEH groups. This finding contrasts with our previous work, where BUP (1.0 mg/kg) exposure resulted in fewer females compared to a lower dose of BUP (0.3 mg/kg) (Wallin et al., 2019). High rates of pup consumption (i.e., missing pups) between PND0 and PND1 in this study (discussed below) limited our ability to determine the sex of all missing pups, potentially skewing the reported sex ratio data for PND1. Consequently, these results should be interpreted with caution, as they may not accurately reflect the true male-to-female ratio. While sex ratio differences are rarely reported in preclinical studies, the mixed findings from our lab highlight the need for further research to clarify whether gestational opioid exposure influences the number of females born.

Offspring development

Prenatal opioid exposure (i.e., morphine or BUP) has been shown to reduce nursing behaviors in rodents (Cruz Ade et al., 2010; Dye et al., 2025; Grimm and Bridges, 1983; Rubin and Bridges, 1984; Wallin et al., 2019). BUP exposed litters exhibited fewer milk bands on PND0–2 than their non-exposed counterparts, consistent with our previous findings of fewer milk bands found on PND2 in litters exposed to 1.0 mg/kg BUP (s. c.) throughout gestation and early postpartum (Myers et al., 2024). Additionally, BUP administration (0.18 mg/kg, i.p.) has been linked to decreased milk-ejection responses in lactating rats as compared to their saline exposed counterparts (Clarke and Wright, 1984), suggesting that BUP may interfere with milk letdown, potentially contributing to the higher mortality rates observed in BUP exposed offspring. However, it is noteworthy that some BUP litters had adequate milk bands, but some pups died despite the presence of milk bands. Thus, while lactation and nursing difficulties may play a role, they cannot solely explain the elevated mortality rates.

The current study found that repeated administration of BUP or morphine throughout pregnancy resulted in lower pup body weight and length on PND1 and PND2 compared to the MV or VEH groups. This aligns with previous findings of reduced offspring body weight following gestational BUP exposure at doses ranging from 0.3 to 3.0 mg/kg (Gildawie et al., 2025; Robinson and Wallace, 2001; Sahid et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2014). However, discrepancies in the literature remain, with other studies reporting no changes in body weight after gestational opioid exposure (Alaee et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2015; Chiang et al., 2010; Hutchings et al., 1996; Kongstorp et al., 2020; Schlagal et al., 2021). These inconsistencies may result from differences in dosage, administration routes, and exposure duration, or lack of maternal care considerations. Additionally, external stressors endured by the dam during pregnancy, due to factors including daily injections or behavioral testing, has been shown to negatively impact the physical maturation and neurodevelopment of newborns in both clinical and preclinical studies (Brunton, 2013; Kentner et al., 2019; Lazinski et al., 2008; Maccari et al., 2014). In our study, dams participated in a multitude of behavioral tests and blood draws before and during gestation, which may have influenced the observed outcome. Nonetheless, our results align with decades of clinical evidence linking prenatal opioid exposure (abuse or MOUD opioids) to reduced birth weight, shorter body length, and smaller head circumferences (Graeve et al., 2022), though the literature on BUP exposure in utero poses some discrepancies (Kakko et al., 2008; O’Connor et al., 2021). Future studies should address these contributing factors to better understand the interplay between direct drug exposure and maternal influences on birth outcomes.

We found no significant differences among groups in surface righting latency on PND2, potentially due to the high variability observed within each group (Table 1). Additionally, the size of the milk band may influence a pup’s ability to right itself, as controls with full milk bands may take longer to right themselves compared to opioid exposed pups (Myers et al., 2024). The mixed findings reported in the literature may result from factors such as variability within treatment groups, differences in milk band size, and time since the last feeding.

BUP exposed pups (BC and MB) exhibited significantly more withdrawal symptoms compared to the VEH group. Surprisingly, and in contrast with our previous findings, the morphine group (MC) did not show significantly more withdrawal symptoms than controls, despite the higher morphine dose used in this study (6–10.0 mg/kg compared to 3–6.0 mg/kg in prior research) (Myers et al., 2024). Importantly, NOWS assessments were conducted 90–200 min after the dam received drug administration, without precipitating withdrawal. As a result, MC pups may have been less irritable due to drug transfer through breast milk at the time of measurement. This interpretation aligns with our correlational analysis, which revealed a significant negative relationship between the amount of time spent in pup-directed maternal behaviors and NOWS symptoms across all groups (see Fig. 7H).

HPLC analysis of monoamine and metabolite concentrations in PND2 pup brains revealed significantly lower NE levels in the PFC and higher levels in the striatum among all the opioid exposed pups (i.e., BC, MB, MC, and MV) compared to non-exposed controls. Unexpectedly, the MV group exhibited altered NE levels despite morphine being discontinued early in pregnancy and showing no differences from the VEH group in any other measurement. This suggests that even early gestational exposure to morphine may exert a lasting neurochemical effect directly on the fetal brain. These findings are consistent with our previous study, which reported reduced DOPAC levels in the PFC of PND2 offspring prenatally exposed to BUP or morphine. Interestingly, neonatal neurotransmitter levels were among the few outcomes not significantly correlated with maternal care behaviors, further supporting the notion that these alterations may result directly from gestational opioid exposure. However, these effects may be transient, as studies by Vathy and colleagues revealed no differences in NE levels in the PFC or striatum of adult males or females after prenatal exposure to morphine (5–10.0 mg/kg) from GD11–18 (Vathy et al., 1994; Vathy et al., 1995). In line with this, one possible explanation for the increased NE levels in the striatum and decreased levels in the PFC in opioid-exposed pups could be that opioids may interfere with and delay maturation along the caudal-rostral maturation gradient of the NE system. Caudal areas reach their mature noradrenergic innervation pattern and functionality earlier than forebrain areas such as the PFC; thus, the lower levels in the PFC may indicate that these areas are ‘behind’ in their NE maturation. Though the striatum is not technically a caudal area, it is ‘more caudal’ than the PFC, and increased levels in the striatum may suggest a temporary imbalance or shift to increased release of NE in other areas while the PFC is still maturing or suppressed (Busche et al., 2004). Of note, the NE system maturation seems to be slower than other neurotransmitter systems such as serotonin which might explain why we only saw changes in NE, but not the other monoamines (Murrin et al., 2007).

Maternal behaviors

Similar to our previous studies, BUP exposed dams (both BC and MB) exhibited delayed nesting initiation and formed flatter, sparser nests before parturition. Although BC and MB dams began to form more localized nests on PND0, they did not achieve brood-like nest height until PND2 and consistently demonstrated less nesting behavior compared to VEH, MV, and even MC dams. This impairment in nesting behavior is somewhat paradoxical, given that BUP acts as a KOR antagonist, and Jacobson et al. (2020) discovered that KOR agonists inhibit nest building, which is reversed by KOR antagonists in both pregnant and non-pregnant mice. However, stress is known to decrease nesting behavior (Otabi et al., 2016), and BUP’s unique pharmacodynamics and interactions with various opioid receptors, may complicate this expectation. Overall, the impairment or delay in nesting behavior, apparent even before parturition, suggests that BUP interferes with the initiation of maternal care behavior prior to birth. This aligns with other significant reductions in pup-directed maternal care behavior, particularly within the MB group, however BC and MC groups also showed drastic reductions in maternal care behaviors compared to controls. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance, potentially due to considerable variation within the opioid groups. For instance, while some BC dams displayed normal behaviors, others spent 0 % of their time with their pups on PND1 during the observation period (see Fig. 3C). In line with the notion of impaired maternal care and processing of infant cues, we expected that in the olfaction test BUP exposed dams would spend more time on the clean side compared to the pup-soiled side. However, our results from this test showed no statistically significant differences, despite a trend suggesting that BUP exposed dams may spend more time on the clean side. Most VEH dams, by contrast, tended to spend more time on the pup-soiled side. The variability observed within the BUP groups, and the small sample size within the MB group, likely contributed to the lack of statistical significance. Future research is needed to explore whether opioid exposure disrupts normal olfactory processing related to maternal behaviors, as drug-naive dams are generally expected to prefer the pup-soiled side of the chamber.

BUP treatment, particularly in the MB group, negatively impacted pup retrieval behavior in dams, consistent with our previous studies (Myers et al., 2024; Wallin et al., 2019). Surprisingly, in this study, many VEH administered dams also failed to retrieve many of their pups, which is atypical. Normally, drug-naïve dams are expected to retrieve most, if not all their pups back to the nest within the allotted time window (Alsina-Llanes et al., 2015; Mattson et al., 2003). However, pup retrieval on PND2 was assessed immediately after a stressor (restraint stress test), which may have contributed to the poor performance of VEH administered dams, as stressed dams are less likely to attenuate to their pups (Kim, 2021; Li, 2022; Orso et al., 2019; Sanson et al., 2024). Interestingly, VEH exposed dams had higher baseline (prior to stressor) CORT levels compared to all opioid exposed dams, averaging ~200 ng/ml, which is at the upper range (50–200 ng/ml) for non-pregnant Sprague Dawley females (Bekhbat et al., 2018), but in line with ‘normal’ CORT levels during the early postpartum period (Li et al., 2021). The low baseline levels in opioid-exposed dams are in line with previous studies (Ali et al., 2016; de Vries et al., 2020) suggesting that hypercortisolism is a common consequence of long-term opioid exposure and more research is needed to investigate the influence of this altered HPA axis function on maternal care and offspring outcomes.

Of note, our opioid exposed groups exhibited high rates of missing pups and thus potential filial cannibalism during the early postpartum period. Though we did not observe active cannibalism for all cases, we did witness some dams consuming live pups. However, we do not know for sure whether most dams consumed healthy, moribund, or already dead pups as this happened mostly at nighttime. Notably, one dam in the MB group (MB7) consumed her entire litter, while another MB dam (MB6) and one MC dam (MC6) consumed the majority of their litters, with MB6 consuming ~50 %, and MC6 consuming ~60 % of her litter. The underlying cause of this elevated rate is unclear but may be related to opioid-induced changes in the MBN (Swain et al., 2019; Wallin et al., 2021), including increased maternal aggression, or irritability stemming from withdrawal symptoms. However, it is well known that animals may eat offspring with low survival chance, so this may also have been a natural response to dealing with unhealthy pups. Either way, this phenomenon emphasizes the importance of considering maternal care and well-being when evaluating offspring outcomes. Opioids may disrupt neural circuits critical for maternal behaviors, potentially leading to increased aggression and higher pup mortality. Future research should investigate the functional connectivity of the maternal brain at various gestational stages to better understand the effects of opioid exposure and the transition from opioids of abuse to BUP on maternal care and pup survival.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to statistically analyze correlations between maternal care behavior and offspring outcomes following opioid administration during pregnancy. Pup-directed maternal care behaviors were significantly positively correlated with offspring weight, body length, and milk band presence, and significantly negatively correlated with PND1 mortality and NOWS-like symptoms. A recent study by Nyberg et al. (2024) further supports the importance of maternal care by demonstrating that gestational BUP exposure (1 mg/kg) increased anxiety-like behavior in young adult offspring, but only when they were continuously kept with their dams. Interestingly, offspring that experienced 3 h of maternal separation during the neonatal period did not exhibit these anxiety-like behaviors. These findings bolster our hypothesis that deficits in maternal care due to opioid exposure during pregnancy are associated with adverse offspring outcomes. The quality of maternal care in rodents, such as licking, grooming, and nursing behaviors is well-documented to influence both short- and long-term offspring neurodevelopment (for review see: Champagne et al., 2003; Myers et al., 2023; Wijenayake et al., 2023). Similarly, clinical research has shown that maternal physical contact, such as skin-to-skin care and mother-infant synchrony, have enduring benefits on children’s physiological and cognitive development, extending into adulthood (Feldman et al., 2014; Ulmer-Yaniv et al., 2023). It is therefore plausible that the reduced licking and grooming behaviors observed in our opioid exposed dams contributed to the developmental deficits that we observed in the offspring (Myers et al., 2023; Swain et al., 2019). Together with our previous findings of significant reductions in maternal care in BUP exposed groups (Myers et al., 2024; Wallin et al., 2019), and clinical reports of altered functional connectivity in the MBN and higher maternal aggression scores in postpartum mothers treated with BUP during pregnancy (Swain and Ho, 2019; Swain and Ho, 2023), these results highlight BUP’s unique and concerning impact on maternal behaviors. Notably, many studies investigating prenatal opioid exposure either fail to assess maternal care or bypass its influence by cross fostering the offspring to drug naive dams. This significant gap in the literature underscores the need for further research to better understand the variability in these effects, both within exposed groups and across studies in order to optimize treatment strategies and provide effective maternal support for mothers with OUD being treated with BUP.

Conclusions

Our study utilized translationally relevant doses of BUP and morphine aimed to mimic clinical and abuse levels and revealed that BUP, despite its role as a MOUD, had significant adverse effects on both dams and offspring that raises concerns about its use during pregnancy. While animal models cannot perfectly replicate the human condition, they provide a controlled and ethically sound means to explore complex biological processes. By incorporating a transitional group (MB) and an appropriate control (MV), our study achieved a clearer understanding of the factors driving the detrimental effects often observed in BUP exposed dams, thereby offering a more precise model of the human condition. Our findings emphasize the profound impact of gestational opioid exposure on dams and underscore the role of impaired maternal care in adverse offspring outcomes, beyond the direct effects of fetal drug exposure. While our results must be cautiously translated to human conditions, particularly abrupt morphine discontinuation, as used in our MV group, is not recommended. As of now, MOUDs remain the gold standard for pharmacological treatment of OUD during pregnancy. Importantly, our data highlights the critical need for comprehensive support for mothers with OUD to promote the well-being of both mother and child. Clinical research has shown that MOUD use during pregnancy is associated with reduced maternal responsiveness and impaired mother-infant interactions (Konijnenberg et al., 2016; Salo et al., 2010). These impairments in mother-infant interactions may negatively influence offspring neurodevelopmental outcomes. Therefore, additional support to enhance breastfeeding, bonding, and other maternal-infant interactions should be a key component of postpartum care for these mothers to ensure optimal development and well-being of their infant.
