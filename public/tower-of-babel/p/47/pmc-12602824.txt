Characterization, expression, and mutational analysis of endosomal protein lethal giant disc (Lgd/CC2D1A) as a biomarker in patients with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Sandeep Pandey, Ranjana Singh, Nimra Habib, Rashmi Kushwaha, Abbas Ali Mahdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe endosomal pathway plays a crucial role in eukaryotes, and its conservation across evolution underscores its importance in fundamental processes, such as endocytosis, cytokinesis, and autophagy. Dysregulation of the Notch signaling system, essential for growth and development, contributes to cancers like leukemia. In Drosophila melanogaster (DM), specific endosomal genes function as tumor suppressors, regulating Notch signaling, either through promoting Notch degradation or directing its recycling. Nevertheless, it is still unanswered how endosomal genes specifically, lethal giant discs (lgd/CC2D1A), affect Notch-1-mediated transduction in human malignancies, such as T-cell acute lymphoblastic (T-ALL).AimThis study aimed to comprehensively characterize the (lgd/CC2D1A) CC2D1A/lgd in human T-ALL by performing mutational analysis and sequencing and evaluating its gene and protein expression levels. These analyses were conducted to elucidate better the role of (lgd/CC2D1A)CC2D1A in the pathophysiology of T-ALL and to explore its potential association with the Notch-1 signaling pathway.Materials and methodsIn this study, we sequenced the lgd and investigated its expression in peripheral blood samples of human T-ALL samples. We used molecular approaches to analyse the Lgd/CC2D1A gene for mutations and characterize its gene and protein expression across genomic and proteomic datasets.ResultsSequencing data revealed the presence of the lgd variant (CC2D1A) in T-ALL patients and healthy individuals. Lgd and Notch-1 expression were significantly upregulated in T-ALL patients, demonstrating a strong positive correlation at both the genomic and proteomic levels.ConclusionsThrough this analysis, we reported the presence of Lgd with an altered nucleotide sequence in T-ALL patients. We also found its elevated expression compared with healthy controls, which might affect disease progression. Introduction

T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) is a serious and potentially fatal hematological malignancy that establishes itself in vital blood-forming organs, including the lymphatic system and bone marrow, and is frequently linked to immunosuppression. Frequent noncoding genomic alterations causing oncogenes to become dysregulated render it challenging to characterise and identify the underlying genetic drivers [1]. It arises from the thymus-derived clonal proliferation of T-cell precursors that invade the bone marrow and peripheral circulation [2, 3].

In children, acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is the most prevalent kind of leukemia. T-ALL makes up just 10–15% of paediatric ALL cases [4, 5]. Fewer than 1% of adult cancer diagnoses are attributable to ALL, with T-ALL accounting for around 25% of adult ALL cases [6, 7]. Survival rates for relapsed T-ALL are abysmal, < 10% of patients surviving, regardless of age. The intricate pathophysiology of T-ALL includes cellular pathways and molecular processes linked to signaling that control thymocyte survival, differentiation, proliferation, and metabolism [8]. T-ALL has been linked to the uncontrollable activation of the Notch signaling pathway [9]. Due to the aggressive nature of the disease and poor outcomes in relapsed cases, there is a need for ongoing research and treatment advancements. Lethal (2) Giant Disc (Lgd) is a gene conserved across all metazoans, encoding a protein of 816 amino acids. The Lgd protein features four sequential DM14 domains—hallmarks of the Drosophila melanogaster 14 family—and a C-terminal C2 domain that can bind phospholipids. This C2 domain plays a key role in ensuring the correct localization within the cell and maintaining the stability of the Lgd protein. However, further functional analysis of this domain has been hindered by the instability of deletion variants lacking the C2 region [10–12]. Mammals possess two homologous variants of the DM lethal (2) giant disc (Lgd) gene, known as LGD1 (CC2D1B) and LGD2 (CC2D1A) [13–15].

A crucial part of signal transduction in several signaling pathways, including Notch, is played by the endosomal pathway. Recent studies suggest that Lethal giant discs (Lgd) have an additional essential function in preventing ligand-independent activation of the Notch signaling pathway during the endosomal trafficking of the Notch receptor toward lysosomal degradation. In Drosophila, Lgd acts as a tumor suppressor, and when this gene is lost, the disc cells proliferate excessively, forming enormous imaginal discs [16]. The mammalian counterparts of Lgd remain largely unidentified; however, the human homolog CC2D1A (also known as hlgd1) was initially linked to autosomal recessive non-syndromic mental retardation (NSMR) [17, 18]. In addition to its involvement in neurological disorders, CC2D1A has been linked to human heterotaxy, impaired ciliary function, and resistance to chemotherapy in ovarian cancer [19]. A homozygous and haploidentical area of 2.4 Mb to 0.9 Mb on chromosome 19p13.12 was discovered using homozygosity mapping of people with NSMR who belonged to the same family [20]. Different kinds of mutations were discovered when the cDNA was sequenced using different primers. The first mapped mutation in hlgd was a 3,589-nucleotide deletion, spanning from intron 13 to intron 16. Due to a frameshift brought on by this loss, the mutant protein had a premature stop codon at amino acid position 438, followed by a nonsense peptide of 30 amino acids. The mutation resulted in the loss of the DM14 and C2 domains of the gene. To assess protein expression, Western blot analysis was conducted on lymphoblastoid cells derived from patients, using an antibody targeting the N-terminus of CC2D1A; in contrast, the antibody successfully detected the protein in cells from healthy individuals, but no signal was observed in patient-derived cells [17].

This study aimed to characterize the human Lgd sequence in T-ALL patients and healthy controls, and to analyze gene and protein expression levels of Notch and Lgd. We employed a standardized approach to analyze gene sequencing data and assess gene/protein expression changes in the peripheral blood of individuals with T-ALL. By utilizing gene sequencing technology, we analyzed the Lgd sequence and performed mutation analysis to compare differences between T-ALL patients and healthy controls. Furthermore, we examined variations in RNA and protein expression levels between the two groups. This is the very first study to explore the incidence and characteristics of Lgd mutations in T-ALL patients, and the findings could help to establish the lgd/CC2D1A as a promising candidate biomarker for early identification of human T-ALL.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

The sample size was determined based on the reported prevalence of T-ALL in India (2–3% as per ICMR data) [7]. A total of 65 samples (cases and controls) were included in the study. This comprised 42 newly diagnosed, untreated T-ALL patients (25 males, 17 females) and 23 healthy individuals as controls. Peripheral blood and bone marrow aspirates were collected from T-ALL patients, while only peripheral blood samples were obtained from healthy controls. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of King George’s Medical University, Lucknow, India, and all procedures adhered to institutional and national ethical guidelines. The clinical diagnosis of T-ALL was established through a combination of patient clinical presentation, morphological evaluation of peripheral blood smears and bone marrow aspirates (stained with Leishman stain), and immunophenotyping by flow cytometry. The antibody panel used for flow cytometry included markers for T-lymphocytes (CD3, CD2, CD5, CD7, CD4, CD8), immaturity markers (TdT, CD34), B-lymphocytes (CD10, CD19, CD79a, CD20, CD22), myeloid lineage (CD13, CD33, CD14, CD64, HLA-DR, CD117, cyt MPO), and other markers like CD1a, CD99. The pan-leukocyte (CD45) was used as a gating marker. A summary of baseline clinical and laboratory characteristics of the study population is provided in Table 1.

Bone marrow and peripheral blood smear staining

Leishman stain was formulated by solubilizing 0.2 g of the staining agent in 100 mL of methanol. Thin blood smears were then prepared on slides and air-dried. The dried smears were then stained using the prepared dye for two minutes, giving the background a pink hue. This was followed by a gentle wash with buffered water for an additional two minutes. When T-ALL was suspected, staining was carried out on smears derived specifically from the buffy coat layer of the blood sample.

Assessment of bone marrow aspirates using multi-parameter flow cytometry

This technique enables an in-depth, single-cell analysis of multiple cellular features, as well as the selective isolation of defined cell subsets. As a result, it serves as a robust reference standard for benchmarking the diagnostic accuracy of our novel microfluidic assays against this well-established multi-parameter flow cytometry method. Bone marrow samples were analyzed using the BD FACSLyric™ ten-color clinical flow cytometry system. Although the instrument supports up to ten colors, an eight-color leukemia panel was specifically chosen to optimize staining sensitivity and minimize spectral overlap for accurate detection of leukemic cells.

Sample preparation followed the stain–lyse–wash protocol. Briefly, 100 µL of either whole blood or bone marrow aspirate was added to FACS tubes pre-labeled with patient identifiers and the designated antibody cocktail (20 µL). Tubes were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10–15 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of diluted FACS lysing solution was added, followed by centrifugation at 200–300 g for 3–5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended and washed twice with sheath fluid. For studying the cytoplasmic markers, cell cell-permeabilizing agent was used. Finally, cells were suspended in ~ 0.5 mL of sheath fluid and acquired on a calibrated BD FACSLyric™ flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using BD FACSuite™ Software, enabling the identification of T-ALL cells based on their characteristic immunophenotypic profile.

For immunophenotypic confirmation, a comprehensive panel of fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies was employed, each labeled with distinct dyes including FITC, PE, PerCP, PE-Cy7, APC, APC-H7, V450, V500-C, and PerCP-Cy5.5. Harvested cells were incubated with a tailored cocktail of fluorescently labeled antibodies against selected CD markers for B-lymphocytes (CD19, CD20, CD79a, CD10), T-lymphocytes (CD3, CD5, CD2, CD7, CD4, CD8), Myeloid cells (CD13, CD33, CD117, CD64, CD14, cyt MPO, CD117), immaturity markers (CD34, TdT) and other markers like CD1a, CD99. After incubation, the labeled cells were loaded into the multi-parameter flow cytometer for acquisition and analysis using standardized instrument settings. The BD FACSuite™ Software processed the data, enabling the leukemia cells to be distinguished based on their unique immunophenotypic profile.

Flow cytometric confirmation of T-ALL cells

T acute lymphoblastic leukemia cells were positive for Cyt CD3 along with variable expression of CD2, CD5, CD7, CD4, CD8, and immaturity markers CD34/TdT. Other markers, like CD13/CD33 expression, were seen in ETP-ALL. Once identified, these leukemic cells were sorted into separate containers for subsequent downstream analyses. All reagents were procured from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA).

Primer designing, PCR, and sanger sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood of sixteen individuals (comprising one healthy control and fifteen T-ALL patients) using the classical phenol–chloroform method. The purified DNA was then analyzed using Sanger sequencing to detect relevant genetic variants. Five sets of primers were designed and synthesized using Primer-Blast (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/index.cgi?LINK_LOC=BlastHome) to target specific regions of the gene, enhancing PCR amplification specificity and sensitivity. PCR experiments were conducted using cDNA from case and control samples. Using the amplified PCR products, Sanger sequencing was performed for a genetic variation study in case and control samples. As recommended by the manufacturer, genomic DNA has been isolated from whole blood samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, catalogue number 51304). Before being kept for later use at −20 °C, the extracted DNA was evaluated both qualitatively and quantitatively. Five sets of primers (listed in Table 1 of the supplementary file) were used to amplify the exonic regions of lgd. Three sets of primers and 20 ng/µl of extracted DNA from each sample were sent to GCC Biotech in Kolkata, India, for bidirectional Sanger sequencing after PCR amplification of the targeted DNA regions. Using a 100 bp DNA ladder as a reference, the PCR products were electrophoresed on a 2% agarose gel for analysis, and bands were seen using a gel documentation system.

Bioinformatics

The three-dimensional structures of both the wild-type and mutant forms of Lgd/CC2D1A were predicted using the Iterative Threading ASSEmbly Refinement (I-TASSER) platform, which predicts full-length protein conformations via template threading, fragment assembly, and iterative refinement (https://zhanggroup.org/I-TASSER/).

Sequence analysis

The sequenced templates were analyzed from patients, healthy controls, and the published reference sequence. Each patient’s sequence was compared to the reference sequence to identify any discrepancies or mismatched regions. The alignment was performed using GeneDoc Software (Version 2.7.000), a powerful bioinformatics tool widely used for sequence alignment and annotation. The software was configured with the BLOSUM62 matrix, a gold-standard scoring system for sequence alignment that evaluates evolutionary relationships by assigning scores based on the likelihood of substitutions between amino acids.

Analysis of the gene expression

Using the TRIzol reagent, total RNA was extracted from blood samples, followed by assessment of RNA purity and concentration with the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer to ensure suitability for downstream molecular analysis. Only samples with an A260/280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.0 were considered suitable for downstream applications. In line with the manufacturer’s instructions, mRNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat. No. 4368814). Gene expression was measured using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix, and gene expression was measured by qRT-PCR. β-actin was implemented as the endogenous control, and DataAssist software was used to interpret the outcome. The ΔΔCt method was applied for relative quantification. Melting curve analysis was used to confirm primer specificity, and 2.2% agarose gel electrophoresis was used to validate product size. PCR was conducted for 40 cycles, each comprising denaturation at 95 °C, annealing at 60 °C, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s per step; all reactions were performed in duplicate and independently repeated a minimum of three times to ensure reproducibility.

Protein expression by western blotting

Peripheral blood samples were collected from both patients and healthy controls, and proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% (w/v) phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific) was employed to determine protein concentration. 15 µg of protein per sample (equal quantities) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked and then probed with custom primary antibodies: Lgd human antibody (1:20,000, Cat No. abc1027; Abcam/customized from Abcam), Anti-Notch1 (1:20,000, Cat No. ab65297), and beta-actin (1:5000, Cat No. ab8227; Abcam). Primary antibodies were incubated with the membranes overnight at 4 °C to produce protein-antibody complexes. Post-wash, membranes were incubated for one hour with HRP-linked secondary antibodies. Protein bands exhibiting immunoreactivity were detected and visualized using the Odyssey Scanning System (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA). Deploying the Odyssey Scanning System (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA), the immunoreactive protein bands have been detected and visualised.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used to conduct statistical analyses (San Diego, CA, USA). An independent samples t-test was used to assess the significance of group differences, and results are presented as mean ± SD. Indicative of statistical significance was a value of p < 0.05. ****p < 0.0001, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and *p < 0.05.

We examined peripheral blood and bone marrow specimens from patients as part of the diagnostic process for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). Blast cell morphology was assessed through Leishman-stained specimens comprising peripheral blood smears, trephine biopsy sections, and bone marrow aspirates. Flow cytometry was employed for immunophenotypic profiling, utilizing a panel of biomarkers to classify blasts based on lineage and maturation status. Key markers analyzed included those for myeloid (e.g., CD13, CD33), B-lineage (e.g., CD19, CD20), T-lineage (e.g., CD2, CD3), and immaturity (e.g., CD34, TdT). Blasts were gated primarily in the dim CD45 region, with flow cytometry confirming immature blast populations characteristic of T-cell ALL (T-ALL). Protein levels

Western blot analysis was performed using a customized antibody (details are provided in Fig. 10 of the supplementary data) targeting the N-terminus of the Lgd protein (human-abc1027, Abcam) in T-ALL cells. A 60 kDa band corresponding to the Lgd protein was detected. Furthermore, a 100 kDa band indicative of cleaved Notch-1 was observed using the anti-Notch-1 antibody (ab65297, Abcam) in samples from both T-ALL patients and healthy individuals, consistent with prior studies [23]. Given the lack of commercially available murine antibodies for the Lgd protein, it would be valuable to detect this protein in human samples using western blotting. Previous studies have reported that elevated levels of Lgd/CC2D1A mRNA are associated with early recurrence in ovarian cancer [24]. To investigate whether the CC2D1A protein is detectable in both malignant and non-malignant ovarian tissues, researchers analyzed both human ovarian cancer cell lines and tissue samples. They observed a 100 kDa CC2D1A band across multiple ovarian cancer cell lines. Furthermore, CC2D1A was localized to both the cytoplasmic and nuclear compartments of ovarian adenocarcinoma cells, consistent with its known roles in transcriptional regulation and as a scaffold protein in Akt signaling pathways [25]. Diagnostic evaluation of lgd and Notch pathway-related genes using receiver operating characteristic analysis

To assess the diagnostic value of selected genes in differentiating between healthy individuals and patients with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted. The genes examined in this study included Notch-1, Notch-2, Notch-4, lgd/CC2D1A, and MAML-1. ROC curves illustrate the trade-off between sensitivity and 1-specificity, reflecting each gene’s effectiveness in distinguishing disease presence from absence. The area under the curve (AUC) provides a comprehensive metric of diagnostic accuracy, where values approaching 1 signify greater ability to correctly classify individuals.

The ROC analysis, as shown in Fig. 8, highlighted significant variations in the diagnostic capabilities of the individual genes examined. Notch-1 showed outstanding diagnostic accuracy, achieving an AUC of 0.956, which reflects its strong ability to distinguish T-ALL patients from healthy controls with high sensitivity and specificity. The ROC curve’s sharp rise near the y-axis indicates a low false-positive rate combined with effective true-positive identification. Among all genes assessed, the lgd/CC2D1A gene demonstrated the highest diagnostic precision, with an AUC of 0.972. Its nearly vertical ROC curve suggests excellent sensitivity and specificity, making lgd a particularly promising biomarker for T-ALL detection. Notch-4 exhibited a fairly strong diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.903, showing good potential in differentiating between cases and controls, although slightly less effective than Notch-1 and lgd. In comparison, Notch-2 showed only moderate diagnostic usefulness, with an AUC of 0.707, indicating a less reliable ability to discriminate T-ALL patients from healthy individuals. Likewise, MAML-1 had relatively poor discriminative power, reflected by an AUC of 0.674, suggesting it is unlikely to be a reliable biomarker for T-ALL when used alone.

Discussion

The gene Lethal (2) giant discs 1[L(2)gd1] in DM has the domain featuring Coiled-coil and C2 domain-containing protein 1-like, involved in targeting Notch for endosomal degradation, regulating the Notch signaling pathway. We perform the blast analysis using the sequence of gene L(2)gd1 in DM to find out the homolog of this gene in Homo sapiens. Homology refers to the similarity in sequences of genes or proteins among different species that arises from a common evolutionary ancestor. Therefore, we analyzed the L(2)gd1 from DM and its human homolog, coiled-coil, and C2 domain-containing protein 1 A. Our study revealed an 85% homology between human and Drosophila for lgd. This 85% homology suggests that the lgd is highly conserved across species, suggesting an essential cellular role. This degree of conservation suggests that lgd may play a crucial, evolutionarily conserved biological role. Some diversion may be due to mutation, insertion, or deletion over the years. The BLAST alignment presented in Supplementary Fig. 2 demonstrates protein-level similarity, suggesting that despite minor variations in the DNA sequences, the encoded proteins remain functionally similar. In Drosophila, lgd loss-of-function disrupts the endosomal activity of the ESCRT machinery, causing aberrant ligand-independent activation of the Notch signaling pathway. Many malignancies, including T-ALL and other solid tumors, have been linked to uncontrollably activated Notch signaling [9]. Notch-1 is an important receptor involved in cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival. When the notch receptor attaches to its ligand on the neighbouring cell, the highly conserved signalling pathway is triggered, resulting in the intracellular domain (NICD) being released. When this NICD moves to the nucleus, it triggers transcription factors that modulate the expression of several genes involved in cell division and differentiation.

Previous studies have shown that human Lgd can functionally replace the DM Lgd protein [26]. The Lgd proteins are implicated in various brain disorders, including autism, intellectual disabilities, and dementia [27]. The discovery of two LGD2-associated alleles implicated in both autism and intellectual disability has raised questions about whether these variants result in a loss of function or act through a dominant-negative mechanism. The original allele, termed LGD2MR, contains a frameshift mutation and encodes a variant that consists of the first three DM14 domains fused to a 30-amino acid nonsense peptide generated by the frameshift. A subsequent analysis of two other CC2D1A alleles, which produce even smaller proteins, further supports the idea that the loss of function in CC2D1A contributes to mental impairments [28, 29]. The findings from our previous study on chronic myeloid leukemia patients reveal that both lgd and Notch-1 expression were significantly downregulated, behaving as a tumor suppressor [30]. The sequence analysis of the case and control samples revealed several genetic variations compared with each other and reference sequences. The observed genetic alterations include one insertion (TT insertion observed in T-ALL) and multiple point mutations (G >T; T >C; G >C; A >C) and substitutions (dinucleotide mutations GC >AA; and GC >CA, trinucleotide mutations GAT >TGA; and GGT >ACC, and tetranucleotide mutations TGTG >CTCA). These mutations highlight significant genetic alterations within the lgd in the T-ALL sample. The presence of these variations could have functional implications and may be indicative of underlying genetic or pathological conditions. The insertion and point mutations might affect the gene’s expression or the structure and function of the encoded protein, potentially leading to altered phenotypic outcomes. This suggests that mutations, including nonsense, missense, or single-nucleotide polymorphisms, may cause amino acid substitutions or disrupt exon splicing, ultimately impairing protein function. These mutations might have a role in the transformation of Lgd from a tumor suppressor to an oncogene.

Although interactions between Lgd and the notch signaling have been observed, their precise relationship, particularly in T-ALL, has not been fully elucidated. Additionally, MAML-1, a critical coactivator of the Notch transcription complex, is essential for Notch-1-mediated gene activation and leukemic cell survival in T-ALL [31–33]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the coactivation function of MAML-1 are still not well explored [34]. Previously published research demonstrated that MAML-1 depletion reduces Notch target gene expression, similar to gamma-secretase inhibition, suggesting its role in recruiting transcriptional coregulators [35, 36]. In our previous study involving CML patients [30], we found that elevated HIF1α levels suppress MAML-1 expression and impair Notch signaling, thereby contributing to the progression of blast crisis. These findings highlight MAML-1 as a key regulator of leukemogenesis. Our results showed that the control sample showed smooth, well-defined, consistent peaks, suggesting normal sequence alignment.

In contrast, when we compared it with the case, there was an apparent variation in the peak intensity, overlapping peaks were present, and no normal sequence alignment, indicating the presence of genetic mutations and variation in T-ALL patients. Analysis of the electropherogram showed nucleotide substitutions, mixed signals, and peak shifts in exons 16–23 of lgd, a region that most likely encodes a crucial protein domain. The structural integrity of this region may be compromised by the detected mutations, such as missense, nonsense, or frameshift alterations, which might lead to abnormal or defective protein production. Mechanistically, these changes could disrupt essential mechanisms, including apoptosis, transcriptional regulation, or signaling, which would lead to leukemogenesis in T-ALL. When combined, the lgd nucleotide alterations show that it may be a pathogenic factor in T-ALL. Further, our findings support genetic screening to identify persons at risk and improve a personalized patient care approach. Our results show upregulation of Notch-1, Notch-4, and Lgd/CC2D1A, while Notch-2 and MAML-1 genes were found to be downregulated, which agrees with previous findings [23, 35]. A hallmark of cancer cells is their ability to evade apoptosis, and Notch-1 supports this process by promoting the expression of anti-apoptotic genes, thereby enhancing cell survival in the leukemic microenvironment.

Our data argue that Notch-1 and Lgd drive T-ALL oncogenesis might be through similar pathways, as assessed both by changes in gene expression and by proteomic analysis [20]. Endosomal pathways appear to be essential for the control and activation of the oncogenic Notch-1 signaling pathway, according to emerging data [37]. DM lgd has also been shown to regulate the notch pathway during the development of wings and brittleness [12]. The lgd, when overexpressed in T-ALL, might be involved in regulating pathways that promote tumorigenesis. In T-ALL, genetic mutations or alterations in the regulation of such genes can contribute to the development and maintenance of leukemia. The expression of Lgd might be upregulated because of Notch-1 activation, leading to increased survival and proliferation of leukemia cells. The specific mechanisms by which lgd expression increases could involve transcriptional activation by the NICD (Notch intracellular domain), or it might be part of an adaptive response to increased oncogenic stress in the leukemia cells. Therefore, the results of our study draw attention to the fact that an increase in lgd expression in T-ALL, in conjunction with Notch-1 activation, might represent a cooperative mechanism to sustain leukemic cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis. Notch inhibitors or modulators, which block Notch-1 signaling, are being explored in clinical trials as potential treatments for T-ALL. The overexpression of the lgd in T-ALL suggests that targeting the Notch signaling pathway could be a therapeutic strategy. So, understanding how the lgd interacts with Notch-1 and other oncogenic pathways opens additional therapeutic avenues for tackling the pathogenesis of T-ALL.

The ROC analysis highlights the strong diagnostic potential of Notch-1 and lgd for detecting T-ALL. Their notably high AUC values (above 0.95) indicate that these genes could serve as dependable biomarkers for early diagnosis or clinical evaluation of the disease. In contrast, Notch-2 and MAML-1 show relatively low discriminative ability, suggesting that their expression alone may not provide sufficient accuracy for diagnosis. Notch-4 demonstrates moderate diagnostic performance and could be used alongside Notch-1 or lgd in a combined gene panel to improve overall predictive accuracy. These results emphasize the value of ROC analysis in validating biomarkers, confirming that Notch-1 and lgd exhibit strong sensitivity and specificity, which are essential for effectively differentiating T-ALL patients from healthy individuals.

Conclusion

Our sequencing analysis confirmed the presence of the lgd homolog (CC2D1A) in both healthy controls and T-ALL patients, with notable sequence variations observed in the patient group. The elevated expression of lgd in T-ALL may contribute to leukemogenesis, potentially through Notch-1–mediated pathways that promote cell proliferation and survival. While the precise functional role of lgd remains to be clarified, our findings suggest that Lgd represents a promising candidate for diagnostic biomarkers in T-ALL. Future studies with larger cohorts are essential to determine the functional consequences of these mutations and their possible association with disease phenotypes.
