End-inspiratory pause enhances ventilation efficiency without increasing autoPEEP in patients with COPD during controlled ventilation
Authors: Joaquin Pérez, Javier H Dorado, Ramiro Bragagnolo, Ana Rivera, Matias Accoce, Laurent Brochard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEnd-inspiratory pause (EIP) is traditionally discouraged in mechanically ventilated patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) because it shortens expiratory time (TE) and may worsen hyperinflation. Previous data in hypoxemic patients suggest that, at equal total inspiratory time (TI), shorter insufflations followed by EIP can improve CO₂ elimination. Whether this strategy is effective and safe in obstructive lung disease remains unknown.ObjectiveTo evaluate whether a ventilation with high inspiratory flow and EIP reduces arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO₂) without increasing hyperinflation in mechanically ventilated patients with COPD undergoing controlled hypoventilation.MethodsProspective, single-center, randomized, cross-over physiological study including sedated, intubated adult patients with acute exacerbation of COPD, PaCO₂ ≥45 mmHg, and no spontaneous respiratory effort. Patients were randomized to two 30-minute ventilation strategies in volume-controlled mode: conventional ventilation without EIP (VentNO−PAUSE) and ventilation with EIP (VentPAUSE), in which inspiratory flow was increased and 40–50% of TI was replaced by an EIP. Total TI and TE were identical in both strategies. Arterial blood gases, respiratory mechanics, and hemodynamics were recorded.ResultsVentPAUSE significantly reduced PaCO₂ (58.5 ± 9.4 to 52.6 ± 10.4 mmHg; P < 0.001) and increased arterial pH (7.31 ± 0.09 to 7.34 ± 0.08; P < 0.001). Ventilatory ratio decreased (P < 0.001), indicating improved ventilatory efficiency. Oxygenation improved (PaO₂ and PaO₂/FiO₂; P < 0.05), while the alveolar–to–arterial oxygen difference was unchanged (p = 0.813). Total PEEP and auto-PEEP were not affected (P > 0.999). PaCO₂ reduction was greater in patients with higher respiratory system elastance.ConclusionsIn sedated, mechanically ventilated patients with COPD, a strategy using high inspiratory flow with a short inspiratory pause reduces PaCO₂ and improves oxygenation without increasing auto-PEEP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05930-0.Keywords: Pulmonary disease, Chronic obstructive, Respiration, Artificial, Mechanical ventilation, End-inspiratory pause, Hypercapnia

Introduction

In patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) receiving mechanical ventilation, an important objective is to reduce lung hyperinflation while preserving ventilation and oxygenation [1]. However, maintaining an acceptable gas exchange is challenging, as major reductions in minute ventilation (VE) raise carbon dioxide (CO2), cause respiratory acidosis and hypoxemia.

Relatively prolonged end-inspiratory pause (EIP) has been shown to increase CO2 removal in hypoxemic mechanically ventilated patients [2, 3]. However, adding EIP is classically discouraged in COPD because – at constant respiratory rate (RR) – prolonging inspiration reduces expiratory time (TE), and worsen hyperinflation [4, 5]. Previous data suggests that, at equivalent total inspiratory time (TI), shorter insufflations followed by EIP can enhance CO2 elimination in acute lung injury [3]. However, whether this strategy is effective and safe in obstructive lung disease remains unknown.

In this study, we assessed whether ventilation with high inspiratory flow (V’) plus EIP could reduce arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) without inducing hyperinflation in patients with COPD undergoing controlled hypoventilation.

Methods

We performed a prospective, single-center, cross-over, randomized trial including sedated and intubated adults with COPD exacerbation, PaCO2 ≥ 45mmHg and no signs of respiratory effort. Persistent air-leaks, hemodynamic instability, or intracranial hypertension were exclusion criteria. The study was approved by the institutional review board (number #10/2024) and patient’s next of kin signed the informed consent.

We collected demographic characteristics and baseline respiratory variables. A pulmonologist (A.R.) calculated a computed tomography (CT) emphysema score [6]: each lung was divided into 3 regions (superior, medium and inferior) based on anatomical references and graded as no emphysema (score = 0), ≤ 25% (score = 1), ≤ 50% (score = 2), ≤ 75% (score = 3) and > 75% (score = 4), giving a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 24 points. Total scores < 2 denote no emphysema, according to values observed in control subjects.

At enrollment, patients were ventilated with a Servo-I ventilator (Maquet, Solna, Sweden) in square-flow volume-control using a controlled hypoventilation strategy: tidal volume of predicted body weight (VtPBW) was set to 6–8 ml/kg, TI 0.6–0.8 s and RR 10–16 breaths per minute (bpm). External positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEPext) was adjusted to the maximum value that did not increase plateau pressure (Pplat) > 1cmH2O, and FiO2 to oxygen saturation 88–92%.

Two ventilation strategies with equivalent total TI and TE, each applied for 30 minutes, were randomly evaluated (Fig. 1A): a) ventilation without EIP, using initial ventilator settings (VentNO−PAUSE); b) ventilation with EIP (VentPAUSE), in which V’ was increased and 40–50% of TI replaced by EIP; the remaining setting were equal to VentNO−PAUSE. We collected arterial blood gases, respiratory mechanics and basic hemodynamics. Total PEEP and quasistatic Pplat were assessed in both study phases with 5-second end-expiratory, and 2-second end-inspiratory occlusions, respectively. Driving airway pressure (ΔP) was quasistatic Pplat - PEEPtot, normalized elastance (ERS−n) was ΔP/VtPBW and inspiratory airway resistance (Raw) was Peak pressure (Ppeak) – quasistatic Pplat divided by V’. The alveolar pressure of oxygen (PAO2) was calculated as [(760–47) FiO2 – (PaCO2/0.8)]. The alveolar-to-arterial O2 difference (PA−aO2) was calculated [7].

Statistical analysis

Based on previous studies showing that EIP reduced PaCO2 by 4–6 mmHg [2, 10], we hypothesized that a sample size of 14 patients would allow us to detect a 5-mmHg reduction of PaCO2 during VentPAUSE, with a power = 0.80 and α error = 0.05.

Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (25th -75th quartile) according to the distribution tested by Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in physiological parameters between ventilation strategies are reported as mean difference and 95% confidence intervals. Paired comparisons were performed with paired t-test or Wilcoxon sign-rank test, while unpaired comparisons were evaluated with unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney-U test. Associations were tested by simple linear regressions. The differential response of PaCO2 to EIP was studied in groups divided according to the median ERS−n and Raw at baseline. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (R software).

Results

We enrolled 14 patients within 1.0 [0.0–1.0] day of intubation (9 male; 75 [66–81] years; Simplified Acute Physiology Score II: 49.0 [36.0–55.0]). Five patients (35.7%) had long-term domiciliary oxygen therapy. CT-emphysema score was 11.0 [3.0–18.0]. Six patients died (42.8%) in ICU.

At enrollment, patients had an endotracheal tube size of 8.0 [8.0-8.5] mm and were ventilated with a set VtPBW of 7.0 [6.7–7.1] ml/kg, RR of 15.0 [14.0–16.0] bpm, PEEP of 8.0 [5.0–8.0] cmH2O, FiO2 of 0.30 [0.25–0.40], TI of 0.80 [0.70–0.80] seconds and I/E relationship of 1:4 [1:4 − 1:5]. These settings were kept constant throughout the study. With this clinical ventilation strategy, most of the patients presented moderate to severe respiratory acidosis (pH < 7.30) with PaCO2 values ranging from 45 to 80 mmHg. Six out of 14 patients (43%) were receiving noradrenaline at the time of study enrollment.

During the study protocol, V’ was increased from 33.5 [30.4–37.1] to 61.0 [54.3–71.6] L/min and an EIP of 0.30 [0.30–0.40] seconds was applied during VentPAUSE, keeping total TI and I/E ratio constant.

Physiological effects of EIP setting

Compared with ventilation without pause, PaCO2 was reduced from 58.5 ± 9.4 to 52.6 ± 10.4 mmHg (P < 0.001) and arterial pH was increased from 7.31 ± 0.09 to 7.34 ± 0.08 (P < 0.001) with EIP (Fig. 1B).

As a result of more efficient ventilation, the VR decreased (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Accordingly, we calculated that RR could have been reduced by -1.5 ± 1.3 bpm, TE prolonged by 0.50 ± 0.43 s and VE decreased by -657 ± 512 ml/min for the same PaCO2 obtained during VentNO−PAUSE (Fig. 1C), indicating that a deeper hypoventilation could have been achieved.

Gas exchange, respiratory mechanics and basic hemodynamic collected during both randomized study phases

The arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) (P = 0.018) and PaO2/FiO2 ratio (P = 0.027) also improved during VentPAUSE. These changes were explained by the increase in PAO2, as PA−aO2 (P = 0.813) remained unchanged (Fig. 1C).

During VentPAUSE, Ppeak markedly increased (P < 0.001), while Raw only slightly increased (P < 0.001) due to higher set V’. A marginal reduction of Pplat (P = 0.026), ΔP (P = 0.048) and ERS−n (P = 0.059) was observed (Table 1). PEEPtot (8.0 [6.0–9.0] vs. 8.0 [6.0–9.0] cmH2O; P > 0.999) and autoPEEP (1.0 [0.0–1.0] vs. 1.0 [0.0–1.0] cmH2O; P > 0.999) were unaffected by EIP setting. At individual patient level, PEEPtot increased by 1 cmH2O in 2 patients, decreased by 1 cmH2O in 2 patients, and remained unchanged in 10 patients during VentPAUSE. Hemodynamics remained stable (Table 1).

Factors influencing the changes in CO2

VentPAUSE had larger impact on PaCO2 reduction in patients with higher ERS−n (ΔPaCO2–High-ERS−n group: -7.8 ± 3.1 vs. Low-ERS−n group: -3.2 ± 3.5 mmHg; P = 0.030). Baseline ERS−n and ΔP negatively correlated with CT-emphysema score (R2 = 0.60, and R2 = 0.61, respectively; P < 0.001) (Additional file 1). ΔPaCO2 was not related to baseline Raw (P = 0.870).

Discussion

We found that ventilation with high V’ plus EIP improved ventilation efficiency without inducing autoPEEP in patients with COPD during controlled ventilation. This could help reduce RR and prolong TE, favoring the expiratory lung emptying. This also improved oxygenation through a higher alveolar oxygen partial pressure [7].

Previous studies have suggested that EIP can worsen hyperinflation in patients with COPD [4]. Consequently, EIP is not recommended in this population [1]. However, this is true when EIP is added to the TI and reduces the TE. In contrast, we did not modify the I/E relationship, adjusted the RR to obtain a long TE, and subsequently replaced part of the TI with EIP.

The reduction of PaCO2 during VentPAUSE could be explained by several mechanisms. First, during inspiration airways VD/VT is reinhaled first, followed by fresh gas; thus, the average time that fresh gas is available for diffusion within respiratory zone (i.e., mean distribution time, MDT) is lower than the total TI [3]. Shorter insufflations followed by EIP enhance CO2 elimination by increasing the MDT. Second, the sudden interruption of a high end-inspiratory flow may induce turbulences that are transmitted down and enhance gas mixing in the interface between the conduction airways and alveoli, facilitating CO2 diffusion [2, 3].

The increase in PaO2 during VentPAUSE appears to be explained by an increase in PAO2 secondary to a reduction in alveolar CO2. This interpretation is supported by the principles of Dalton’s law and the alveolar gas equation, and the observation that PA−aO2 did not improve with EIP setting [7].

Patients with higher ERS−n (corresponding to a lower emphysema score) had more benefits from VentPAUSE. It is possible that higher emphysema indicates more destruction and less capacity to improve CO2 clearance.

Our study present limitations. First, we evaluated only square-flow waveforms during volume-controlled ventilation, and the baseline V’ was relatively low. Second, our physiological study may provide a proof-of-concept demonstration not designed to study clinical outcomes. The potential reduction in RR with EIP intends to give a clinical estimate of a possible impact in situations where ventilation could be more challenging, such as more severe hypercapnia, associated metabolic acidosis or more severe obstruction. Third, measuring respiratory mechanics (i.e., elastance and resistance) using 2-seconds inspiratory occlusions may not fully reflect static conditions in patients with lung heterogeneity. Also, our calculated Raw does not allow to differentiate changes in the frictional (ohmic resistance) or viscoelastic (differential resistance) component [11]. Therefore, our encouraging physiological results should not be directly extrapolated to daily clinical practice. High V’ may increase the alveolar stress on units with faster time constants, possibly resulting in local overdistension. In our study, VentPAUSE reduced PaCO2 without affecting hemodynamics, with no visible short-term physiological consequences. However, the true long-term clinical impact of VentPAUSE remains to be established.

Conclusions

In sedated and intubated patients with airflow obstruction during controlled ventilation, using a high inspiratory flow plus a short inspiratory pause is useful to reduce arterial CO2 without increasing autoPEEP and improves oxygenation.
