Altered venous flow drives endothelial to mesenchymal transition in varicose veins by suppressing PIEZO1-KLF2 signaling
Authors: S. Ahalya, C. L. Karthika, B. J. Sreelakshmi, S. R. Kalpana, S. Sumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Varicose veins are characterized by disturbed hemodynamics due to blood reflux. We previously identified altered flow-driven endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in varicose veins. Mechanosensors such as Piezo1 provide a molecular bridge between hemodynamic forces and cellular mechanoregulation in vasculature. We hence hypothesized that depletion of blood flow-sensing Piezo1 channels induces EndMT process during venous remodeling. Here, we analyzed 20 human varicose veins and 22 healthy saphenous veins for a comprehensive view of Piezo1 expression using qRT-PCR, western blot and immunohistostaining techniques. Our study reveals a significant loss of Piezo1 expression in varicose veins at both mRNA and protein levels. Our in vitro experiments using microfluidic flow channels showed that Piezo1 expression and calcium influx function are drastically affected in endothelial cells (ECs) exposed to oscillatory venous shear stress. Piezo1 depletion reduces KLF2, which triggers the EndMT program in venous ECs. Kinase inhibitor assays indicated that Piezo1-based calcium influx promotes CAMKII autophosphorylation and induces KLF2 expression via both MEK5/ERK5 and PI3K-AKT-FOXO pathways in venous flow. Piezo1 agonist Yoda1, at low doses mimicking venous flow, augmented KLF2 expression and prevented aberrant molecular EndMT reprogramming in cells exposed to oscillatory shear stress. Yoda1 was also effective in restoring calcium influx and stimulating angiogenesis by promoting endothelial tube formation hindered by Piezo1 depletion. Taken together, the endothelial-protective role of Piezo1 ion channels due to uniform hemodynamic flow is coupled to KLF2, a downstream mediator in the venous circulation. The Piezo1-KLF2 pathway can be utilized for identifying targeted therapeutic options in patients with varicose veins.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05854-y.

Introduction

Chronic venous diseases, commonly known as varicose veins, are the most prevalent venous disease worldwide [1]. We still lack a fundamental understanding of the etiopathogenesis of this disease. The lack of insight into the molecular pathogenesis of varicose veins significantly impacts the non-invasive pharmacological strategies. Varicose vein treatment heavily relies on various remedial interventions that are often followed by disease relapse [2]. Our earlier studies to delineate the molecular pathogenesis of human varicose veins have demonstrated that there is a molecular switch towards a mesenchymal phenotype, that drives endothelial dysfunction and venous wall remodeling [3]. However, the molecular players that drive endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) in varicose veins have not been fully characterized.

In our previous study, we identified the role of disturbed fluid shear stress in the occurrence of EndMT in venous systems [3]. The molecular signaling in luminal endothelial cells (ECs) of blood vessels is at least partially modulated by hemodynamic forces. Mechanosensors that act as molecular bridges connect the alterations in vascular hemodynamics such as fluid shear stress and stiffness with endothelial signaling [4]. Against this backdrop, we hypothesized that mechanosensing proteins on luminal ECs might be involved in the venous biomechanical signaling cascade and that venous diseases are caused by their dysregulation.

Piezo1 ion channels are prominent mechanosensors that detect and respond to various external stimuli. They are cation channels that control the mechanotransduction pathways induced by blood flow and govern vascular development [5]. Piezo1 proteins are one of the initial classes of mechanosensing channels that were identified to be associated with disease pathogenesis due to altered biomechanical forces [6]. Piezo1 expression is very high in many cancer types [7, 8]. Molecular genetic studies demonstrated variants in FAM38A, the gene encoding for Piezo1, to be associated with lymphatic dysplasia, where varicose veins are a secondary implication [9]. Recently, various exome analysis projects reported the presence of rare loss-of-function risk-causing variants of FAM38A in large varicose vein patient cohorts [10, 11]. Contrary to loss-of-function variants, Backman et al. in 2021 identified missense variants in the Piezo1 gene associated with a lower risk of varicose veins [12]. These findings indicate that regardless of the type of genetic variants, dysregulated Piezo1 expression is associated with varicose veins.

The Piezo1-KLF2 signaling in response to arterial high shear stress was earlier described by Zheng et al. in 2022 [13]. Nevertheless, there is currently no research on Piezo1 and its downstream signaling in luminal ECs exposed to low-magnitude hemodynamic systems such as venous flow. Hence, we elucidated the molecular relationship between Piezo1 and the transcription factor KLF2, a zinc finger DNA-binding Kruppel-like family protein in venous flow as well as demonstrated the dysfunctional Piezo1-KLF2 axis in human varicose veins. We further extended the experimental outcome towards utilizing Yoda1-based Piezo1 activation as an approach to prevent aberrant vascular remodeling.

Materials and methods

Research subjects and sample collection

20 varicose veins were collected from the remnants of vein-stripping surgical procedures conducted in patients with chronic venous disease CEAP class 3 at Kempegowda Institute of Medical Sciences, Bengaluru, India. Physical examinations and duplex ultrasound were used to confirm the presence of chronic venous pathology, with a venous reflux time of more than 1 s serving as the diagnostic criterion. Healthy veins to be used as controls for this study were collected from 22 saphenous vein conduits used for coronary artery bypass grafting at Sri Jayadeva Institute of Cardiovascular Sciences and Research, Bengaluru, India. The demographic details of study participants are given in Table S1.

The study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Rajiv Gandhi Centre for Biotechnology and all study subjects gave their written consent for sample collection. A vascular surgeon reviewed the inclusion and exclusion criteria and patients with varicosities as secondary complications to malignancies, deep vein thrombosis, as well as minors and pregnant women were excluded.

Histochemistry

Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed in 4 μm-thick formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) vein sections for histological evaluation and to negate any abnormal neointima formation in control veins.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Six each of FFPE varicose and control veins were used for IHC as per our earlier studies [14]. Antibodies are listed in Table S2. Imaging of stained sections was performed by Nikon, Japan. Histoscoring was done based on signal intensity produced by each antibody and as measured by ImageJ software.

Tissue immunofluorescent staining

FFPE control saphenous (n = 3) and varicose vein sections (n = 3) were used for double immunofluorescence assay to colocalize CD31 and Piezo1 in the intima, as described earlier [15].

Cell culture

Piezo1 was expressed in both ECs and SMCs in saphenous veins, we still focused on ECs for our investigations since shear stress sensing is prominent in ECs lining the vascular lumen. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), were grown in HiEndoXL endothelial cell expansion medium (Himedia, India) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. As a selective agonist of Piezo1, Yoda1 was used in various assays with HUVEC at 1µM final concentration.

Microfluidic in vitro shear stress experiments

The pump system from Ibidi-Integrated BioDiagnostics, Germany was utilized to simulate the normal and pathological blood flow patterns in vitro. Shear stress and fluid flow rates were controlled by the IBIDI pump control software version 1.5.4. Ibidi channel slides, specifically µ-slide I 0.4 Luer ibiTreat and µ-slide Y-shaped ibiTreat, were seeded with 1 × 106 HUVECs for flow experiments. We exposed cells seeded in µ-slide I 0.4 Luer to a laminar shear stress (LSS) pattern of 6 dyn/cm² for 3 h to simulate venous physiological flow, with the pressure progressively increasing from 2 dyn/cm². The µ-slide Y-shaped ibiTreat was used to create altered oscillating shear stress (OSS) at 3 dyn/cm² to mimic non-uniform low shear stress conditions as seen in chronic venous diseases. These flow conditions were selected for the study based on preliminary dyne-hour standardization experiments using Piezo1 protein expression as the criteria. Cells seeded on channel slides without exposure to fluid flow were used as static flow controls.

Quantitative RT-PCR

RNA from vein specimens and cells subjected to various treatments was isolated using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and 500 ng of total RNA was used for the cDNA synthesis. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using QuantStudio™ 5 real-time PCR System, 384-well as described earlier [16]. Primers were designed incorporating spanning exon-exon junctions using Primer Premier 5 software (PREMIER Biosoft International, USA), and the sequences are listed in Table S3. The fold ratio was calculated with the 2−ΔΔCT formula by normalizing it to GAPDH.

Western blotting

Total protein was isolated from six varicose veins and six controls as well as treated cells by RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and Western blotting was performed [17]. The chemiluminescent bands were captured by ImageQuant LAS 500 imaging system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, USA) and densitometry analysis was performed. Table S2 lists the antibodies that were utilized along with their dilutions.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and further non-specific binding was blocked by incubating with 3% BSA-PBS for 1 h before an overnight incubation with primary antibodies (Table S2) at 4 °C. The sections were subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h, and the nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 5 min. Olympus cellSens standard software was used to assess the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Knockdown and rescue assays

30 nM siRNA to Piezo1 (siPiezo1) from Eurogentech was used to silence Piezo1 in ECs. For KLF2 knockdown, 50 nM Mission esiRNA KLF2 (siKLF2) from Sigma-Aldrich was used. Mission siRNA Universal negative control #1 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was used at the same concentration as siRNAs for transfection controls. 2 × 10⁵ HUVECs were seeded and after 24 h, cells were transfected with specific siRNA using JetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus, USA) and incubated for 72 h. Piezo1 and KLF2 KD cells with and without various treatments were further assessed by qRT-PCR and western blot analysis.

Additionally, to assess the effects of flow on silenced cells, cells were seeded onto an µ-slide I 0.4 Luer ibiTreat, followed by transfection with siPiezo1. After 72 h of transfection, the cells were exposed to LSS for 3 h and the expression of EndMT markers (SNAI1, & αSMA) as well as KLF2 was assessed. As controls, cells treated with universal scrambled siRNA and exposed to LSS were used.

Rescue assays were performed by co-transfecting hPiezo1- tdt plasmid (gift from Prof Ardem Patapoutian) and Piezo1 siRNA using JetPrime. Briefly, 2 × 105 cells were seeded in a 30 mm dish to attain approximately 80% confluence during transfection. Media was replaced with fresh 2% serum - containing EGM without antibiotics and the transfection mix containing 1 µg of plasmid DNA and, 50 nm Piezo1 siRNA and 6ul of JetPrime reagent was added dropwise to the cells. Cells were incubated for 6 h and later replenished with fresh 2% serum containing-media without antibiotics for 72 h.

Intracellular calcium imaging

Cells were seeded in µ slides and were loaded with 5 µM Fluo- 4AM dye in combination with Pluronic F-127 and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C under dark conditions followed by washing with PBS to remove excess dye. The cells were exposed to LSS or OSS, and the Ca2+ influx was measured (Olympus, Japan). Static cells were used as the control.

Additionally, Ca2 + influx assays were performed to evaluate the changes in intracellular Ca2+ levels upon EC Piezo1 knockdown. Ca2+ influx was recorded at time points ranging from 1 to 20 min by Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Piezo1 KD cells were further loaded with Fluo- 4AM indicator for an hour, followed by exposure to LSS, and the fluorescence images were captured (Olympus, Japan). To investigate the potential of Piezo1 activation in reversing OSS-induced cellular alterations, Yoda1-pretreated cells were loaded with Fluo-4 AM dye, followed by exposure to OSS for 1 h.

Cell viability analysis

Prior to performing flow-based shear stress analysis on ECs, MTT assays were done to ensure effective inhibition without adverse effects on cell viability. Yoda1 as well as the kinase inhibitors (Ly294002, U0126, BIX02189, and SB203580) were used at concentrations ranging from 0.25 µM to 20 µM for 24 h, and an assay was performed as described earlier [18]. Optical density at 570 nm was recorded using an ELISA Microplate Reader (Robonik, India).

Inhibitor studies

2 × 105 HUVECs were seeded in a 30 mm dish and incubated overnight and treated with a specific inhibitor to achieve the desired final concentrations (10 μm U0126, 10 μm SB203580, 5 uM of BIX02189, and 5 uM LY294002) for 24 h. For control studies, the vehicle, DMSO was used. Cell pellets were collected to study the gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR.

To confirm the KLF2 regulation by Piezo1, HUVECs were treated with 5 µM of BIX02189 and then treated with Yoda1 for 1.5 h and KLF2 expression was measured. LY294002 (5 µM) was also used to treat cells and mRNA fold expression of KLF2 was calculated.

Rhodamine phalloidin staining

Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 12 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After blocking the cells with 1% BSA in PBS, Rhodamine Phalloidin (Invitrogen, USA) staining was performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by nuclear staining with Hoescht 33,342.

Transwell migration assay

Approximately 40,000 ECs were allowed to migrate for 24 h through an 8 μm pores of cell insert. 20% FBS-containing media served as the chemoattractant in the lower chamber. After incubation, the unmigrated cells in the upper chamber were removed by wiping and the cells were fixed in 4% PFA and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. The insert was further stained with Crystal violet, and the assay was repeated with Hoechst stain to confirm live cell nuclei. The unbound stain from the insert was rinsed off and after air drying, a fluorescent microscope was used to visualize the migrated cells.

In vitro angiogenesis assay

The capacity of ECs with Piezo1 KD to develop capillary-like tubular structures was examined by growing cells on Matrigel. Cells (1 × 10³) were trypsinized and seeded onto a 15-well Ibidi 3D µ-Slide pre-coated with 10 µL of Matrigel (Corning™, USA). After 6 h, 5 µM Calcein AM was added to the ECs in media and incubated at 37˚C for 30 min. Dye-containing media was replaced with fresh 2% serum- containing media and cells were kept in a CO2 incubator for 60 min for AM ester removal. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus microscope, Japan). The effect of Yoda1 treatment on Piezo1 KD HUVEC was also studied.

Statistical analysis

All data from this study were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8 (San Diego, CA, USA). The qRT-PCR results from tissue specimens were shown as scatter plots. Cell-based experiments were done in biological triplicates. The mean ± standard deviation of biological triplicates was used to prepare bar graphs. Bar graphs depict the histoscores, mRNA folds, and MFI values. Error bars in graphs show the standard deviation. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Piezo1 is depleted in varicose veins and ECs subjected to altered venous shear stress

To detect the mRNA expression levels of the Piezo1 (FAM38A) gene, we chose 20 varicose and 22 saphenous veins for qRT-PCR. The fold change in Piezo1 gene expression relative to the endogenous control gene, GAPDH was studied. Figure 1A represents a scatter plot of Piezo1 expression in all study subjects. The results indicate that Piezo1 mRNA is significantly lower in varicose veins compared to healthy veins (p-value < 0.0001). Piezo1 protein-level expression was assessed by western blot in varicose and control veins. Densitometry analysis of immunoblots indicates that Piezo1 protein was significantly reduced by 0.55- folds in varicose veins compared to healthy veins (Fig. 1B, C). We examined Piezo1 localization in varicose as well as control veins by histochemical staining. Based on H & E, neointima formation and irregular wall patterns were evident in all varicose veins (Fig. S1). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that Piezo1 was significantly expressed in both the intima and media of control veins, whereas Piezo1 stained weakly in varicose veins (Fig. 1D, Fig. S2). The quantitative histoscore analysis of DAB staining was performed using ImageJ and the downregulation of Piezo1 in varicose veins was found significant (p < 0.0001, Fig. 1E). Moreover, double immunofluorescence in tissues demonstrated Piezo1-specific staining in CD31-positive ECs (Fig. 1F) in control veins which were lost in varicose veins.

The effect of fluid flow is initially sensed by mechanosensors in luminal endothelium [19] and hence the cellular mechanistic studies were performed in venous ECs. To assess the causal relation between the disturbances in fluid shear stress and altered Piezo1 expression, HUVECs were exposed to venous LSS at 6 dyn/cm2 and compared to static cultured cells. To simulate the hemodynamic alterations in varicose veins where blood reflux is common, the cells were subjected to OSS at a reduced flow rate of 3 dyn/cm2. These values were selected based on preliminary analysis (Fig. S3). Piezo1 mRNA levels were 2.43-fold upregulated in ECs exposed to LSS compared to static culture. This study demonstrated that even low venous magnitude in uniform direction induces Piezo1 expression (Fig. 1G). In line with the findings in varicose veins, Piezo1 mRNA expression was reduced in cells in response to OSS compared with other flow conditions. Piezo1 protein was also significantly less in HUVECs subjected to OSS compared to both LSS and static conditions (Fig. 1H, I). The results were confirmed by cell immunofluorescence assay in cells exposed to various flow conditions. Piezo1 expression was very low in cells under OSS, whereas a very high expression of Piezo1 was noted in cells subjected to LSS compared to OSS after short term (3 h) as well as long term (24 h) exposure (Fig. 1J, Fig. S4). Figure 1K represents the mean value of MFI of five random microscopic fields.

Earlier studies have reported that a low magnitude of shear stress per se causes impaired mechanosensing [20]. Mechanosensing by ion channels such as Piezo1 is critically dependent on Ca2+ influx [13]. To assess the effect of flow at venous magnitude on endothelial Ca2+ influx, HUVEC were stained with Fluo-4AM prior to exposure to fluid shear stress. As seen in Fig. 1L and Fig. S5a, venous LSS exposure caused increased intracellular Ca2+ levels compared to static cells. However, endothelial Ca2+ influx was significantly inhibited due to OSS. This study indicates that flow disturbances rather than magnitude are linked to altered mechanosensing. To further identify the role of Piezo1 in venous Ca2+ influx, we used Piezo1 agonist Yoda1. An earlier study reported that 1 µM Yoda1 gave the closest results to the venous shear stress magnitudes [21]. MTT assay was done to negate the toxicity associated with various concentrations of Yoda1 (Fig. S6). Cells were pre-treated with Yoda1, and exposed to OSS, and we found that Ca2+ influx augmented compared to OSS with vehicle alone (Fig. 1M, Fig. S5b).

Our earlier studies in human varicose veins showed that active EndMT is associated with the disease pathogenesis [3]. To examine the potential association of Piezo1 depletion with EndMT, Piezo1 was knockdown (KD) in primary HUVECs using siPiezo1 after a prior comparison of silencing efficiency with two sets of commercial siRNAs (Fig.S7). The effectiveness of silencing was verified at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2A-C). Time course analysis of the Fluo-4AM indicator showed that the Ca2+ influx rate of the Piezo1 KD cells was reduced compared to siNC-treated cells (Fig. 2D). Ca2+ influx was significantly lower by approximately 49% in Piezo1 KD cells exposed to LSS compared to siNC-transfected cells under LSS (Fig. 2G, H). This finding shows the significant role of Piezo1 in venous flow-based endothelial Ca2+ signaling.

Piezo1 silencing led to elevated expression of EndMT-associated transcription factor SNAI1 in HUVECs (Fig. 2E, F) giving direct proof of an anti-EndMT role of Piezo1. The next question was if EndMT prevails under LSS albeit the loss of endothelial Piezo1. When Piezo1-gene depleted cells were exposed to LSS for 3 h, EndMT markers such as αSMA and SNAI1 were upregulated by 1.84 and 2.23-folds respectively (Fig. 2I, J). Nuclear localization of transcription factor SNAI1 was prominent in Piezo1 silenced cells.

To confirm if, Piezo1 channels are indeed important for endothelial function and to prevent EndMT, we further exposed to Piezo1 KD ECs rescued with hPiezo1-tdt overexpression plasmids (Fig. S8). HUVECs were co-transfected with the siRNA or siNC and plasmids encoding Piezo1tdt. The rescue experiment was confirmed by evaluating the expression of Piezo1 and KLF2 in KD and rescued cells (Fig. S9). A rescue experiment with overexpression plasmid caused an upregulation of both Piezo1 and KLF2 compared to Piezo1 KD cells. Further, a qRT-PCR-based analysis of αSMA, TWIST1, and SNAI1 was performed in mRNA extracted from Piezo1 KD as well as Piezo1-rescued cells. We found that the reconstitution of Piezo1 expression prevented EndMT marker expression in ECs at mRNA levels (Fig. 2K) and protein levels (Fig. S10).

Piezo1-KLF2 signaling prevents EndMT in altered venous flow

Previous studies have reported that KLF2 is essential for vascular hemodynamic regulation [19]. A recent study has shed light on the Piezo1-KLF4/2 dynamics [13]. However, the exact cellular functions of KLF2 in the venous system remain poorly understood.

To explore whether Piezo1 is essential for shear stress-induced cellular KLF2 expression, we examined Piezo1 KD HUVEC cells. KLF2 mRNA expression was notably reduced in the Piezo1 KD cells compared to siNC-treated cells (Fig. 3A). To corroborate these cellular RNA interference findings in patient samples, we quantified KLF2 gene expression in varicose and control veins. The downregulation of KLF2 mRNA was highly significant in varicose veins (Fig. 3D). Moreover, the downregulation of KLF2 in varicose veins was also confirmed at the protein level by western blot analysis in six each of varicose and healthy veins (Fig. 3E, F). IHC staining was used to visualize KLF2 protein expression and localization in six FFPE varicose and control veins. KLF2 protein was localized in the media and intimal lining of control veins and was significantly low in varicose veins (Fig. 3G, H). KLF4 was also studied in this setting, but we could not find any significant differential expression at mRNA levels between varicose and control veins (Fig.S11).

KLF2 is a major shear stress-responsive gene, hence we analyzed the mRNA-level expression of KLF2 in ECs exposed to various flow regimes. KLF2 mRNA expression was significantly reduced to 0.45-folds in cells subjected to OSS compared to cells exposed to static flow (Fig. 3I). Correspondingly, KLF2 protein expression was significantly downregulated in cells subjected to OSS compared with other flow conditions. (Fig. 3J, K). The results were confirmed by immunofluorescence, and KLF2 expression was very low in cells under OSS, whereas a very high nuclear expression of KLF2 was noted in cells subjected to LSS (Fig. 3L, Fig. S12).

When Piezo1 KD cells were exposed to LSS for 3 h, we found that the KLF2 expression was drastically reduced by 0.35-folds (Fig. 3N, Fig. S13). Even uniform flow as in the case of LSS, could not effectively prevent KLF2 depletion in the absence of Piezo1. This indicates the prominent role of Piezo1 in KLF2 regulation. To confirm if, Piezo1 channels are essential for endothelial function and KLF2 expression, we further exposed ECs to OSS in the presence of Yoda1 for 3 h. Yoda1 could induce KLF2 under ensuing altered flow (Fig. 3O, Fig. S14).

To determine if Piezo1 indeed works through Kruppel-like factors, especially KLF2, we prepared KLF2 KD cells using eMission SiRNA to KLF2 (Fig. 3M). KLF2 KD cells were found to have expressed SNAI1 and α SMA by 2.08 and 1.7-folds respectively. In the presence of Piezo1-agonist Yoda1, EndMT marker expression was slightly downregulated in KLF2 KD cells, however, the reduction was not remarkable. This may indicate a KLF2-dependant mechanism of action of Piezo1 (Fig. 3M). A Phalloidin staining further demonstrated a spindle-like morphology in KLF2 KD cells that was found to revert with Yoda1 treatment (Fig. S15).

KLF2 expression is regulated by both MEK5/ERK5 and PI3K pathways in venous flow

KLF2 expression is associated with enhanced Calcium/calmodulin-dependent kinase II (CaMKII)-ERK5 [13], as well as phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways [22]. Moreover, there is a lots of ambiguity regarding the roles of p38 MAPK and ERK1/2 kinases in KLF2 regulation [23, 24]. An earlier study using arterial shear stress values of 16 dyn/cm2 as well as high (5 µM) Yoda1 concentrations that induce very high Piezo1 activity confirm the role of Piezo1- CaMKII-MEK5 pathway in KLF2/4 induction [13]. To examine if the CAMKII-MEK5 pathway is associated with KLF2 expression in low shear venous flow as well, we studied the effect of specific kinase inhibitors, such as BIX02189, U0126, SB203580 and LY294002 targeting MEK5/ERK5, MEK/ERK1/2, P38 MAPK and PI3K kinases respectively. After the initial MTT assay (Fig. S16), these inhibitors were used to treat HUVECs. Interestingly, all inhibitors had a repressive role on KLF2 expression, with BIX02189 and LY294002 having the highest inhibitory action on endothelial KLF2 mRNA expression, highlighting the importance of MEK5/ERK5 as well as PI3K signaling in venous flow (Fig. 4A). Further, ECs were primed with Yoda1 and later checked the effect on KLF2 expression of MEK5 and PI3K pathway inhibitors (Fig. 4B). As myocyte enhancer factor 2 C (MEF2C) transcription factors act upstream of KLF2 in anti-inflammatory signaling [25], we inhibited MEK5 and PI3K kinases and studied the expression of phosphoMEF2C (pMEF2C) and KLF2 in vein ECs by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4C). We observed that Mitogen-activated protein kinase (pMEKK3) and pMEK5 were low in cells exposed to OSS compared to LSS and static conditions (Fig. 4E-G).

An immunofluorescence assay was performed to understand the expression pattern of pCAMKII, the calcium-regulated kinase as well as the pMEF2C transcription factor. There was a higher expression of pCAMKII and pMEF2C in cells exposed to physiological LSS (Fig. 4H, I). OSS flow significantly reduced the pCAMKII and pMEF2C in ECs.

We further studied the Piezo1 KD phenotype by vasculogenic endothelial tube formation assays in Piezo1 KD cells. We found that Piezo1 KD significantly diminished the capability of HUVECs to form tube-like structures in vitro (Fig. 6C). Tube formation remained unaltered by siNC transfection. When Piezo1 silenced cells were treated with 1 µM Yoda1, the endothelial tube formation significantly improved (Fig. 6C, D). Further, to identify the migratory behavior of Piezo1 silenced cells, we performed a transwell migration assay (Fig. S19). Even though Piezo1 KD cells did not display any spectacular migratory capacity, they migrated more compared to control cells, and Yoda1 was effective in reducing this migration.

As CD31 and vWF are vital in maintaining endothelial integrity, we assessed their expression in Piezo1 KD ECs. There was a marked downregulation of CD31 and vWF in Piezo1-depleted ECs compared to control cells (Fig. S20). Yoda1 treatment marginally improved CD31 (p = 0.0203) and vWF (p = 0.3042) expression in Piezo1 silenced cells.

To further analyze if the phosphorylation of MEK5 and FOXO1 is associated with Piezo1, we silenced Piezo1 in vein ECs and performed a western blot. Phosphorylated MEK5 as well as pFOXO1 in Piezo1 KD cells was reduced compared to siNC-treated cells (Fig. 6E, Fig S21) Similarly, their downstream molecule KLF2 was also found downregulated. This loss of signaling molecules was restored in the presence of Yoda1. Likewise, OSS reduced pMEK5/total MEK5 and pFOXO1/total FOXO1 as well as KLF2 in ECs, which was restored by Yoda1 (Fig. 6F, Fig S22). Yoda1, as expected, reduced the EndMT markers in the presence of ongoing OSS (Fig. 6G, H). Expression of αSMA was drastically reduced in the presence of Yoda1. SNAI1 expression was reduced as well as a very few cells lost SNAI1 nuclear localization in the presence of Yoda1 even under OSS conditions.

Discussion

We demonstrated that Piezo1 is significantly downregulated in human varicose veins compared to normal saphenous veins. Furthermore, our studies using microfluidic shear stress-exposure chambers demonstrated a similar loss of Piezo1 expression in venous ECs exposed to OSS. A key finding of our study was that inhibition of Piezo1 using in vitro KD models overexpressed EndMT-associated markers in cells exposed to even protective uniform flow. Our research further demonstrates a dysregulated Piezo1-KLF2 signaling axis in both human varicose veins and vein ECs exposed to OSS. Piezo1 mediated-KLF2 expression is dependent on both MEK5/ERK5 and PI3K-AKT-FOXO1 pathways. Interestingly, treatment with the molecular agonist of Piezo1, Yoda1 prevents the endothelial aberrant tube formation caused by Piezo1 depletion and augments KLF2 expression even in the presence of OSS which is an advanced form of disturbed flow. Yoda1 treatment reduced EndMT marker expression significantly in the face of continuing altered flow.

Disturbances in the magnitude and flow direction of blood flow have been associated with the pathogenesis of various vascular diseases [3, 4]. The molecular signaling downstream to different patterns of blood flow is conveyed to nuclear machinery and regulates downstream gene expression either inducing or disrupting them [4]. Piezo1, a multipass transmembrane-domain cation channel, is a key hemodynamic mechanosensor [5]. Piezo1 differential expression is associated with various diseases such as cancers [7, 8]. However, in the last decade, a large body of evidence implicated Piezo1 depletion in various lymphedemas [9]. In very recent years, data from exome sequencing research indicated that people with varicose veins have missense and loss-of-function variations in the Piezo1 gene [10–12]. Loss-of-function and protein-truncating variants were associated with disease risk while missense variants reduced the risk of varicose veins [10, 28]. The loss of Piezo1 channels causes a dearth in Ca2+ influx whereas the gain of these ion channels causes a Ca2+ influx surge into cells. Both these aspects result in altered downstream signaling pathways and can disrupt vascular development. Nevertheless, the role of these channels in the venous system, flow magnitude and venous pathology is much less studied.

Piezo1 is a prominent mechanosensor required for venous valve formation [29]. It is plausible that regardless of the mutations and their functional proteins, heavy disturbances in blood flow as observed in vascular disorders such as varicose veins would have a significant impact on luminal EC mechanosensor expression. We hence studied the Piezo1 expression in human varicose veins irrespective of their genetic landscape. In healthy saphenous veins, Piezo1 was expressed in the intimal area which is predominantly formed of ECs and media consisting of SMCs. In varicose veins, however, both EC and SMCs lose Piezo1 expression. In a very recent study, Zhao et al. reported a 2-fold increase of Piezo1 mRNA in 8 varicosed regions of veins in comparison to the relatively normal adjacent tissue of the same veins [30]. Our cohort comprised 20 varicose veins from CEAP class 3 subjects and 22 histologically normal saphenous veins. To prevent any additional disease parameters from influencing the gene expression, we decided to focus on the same class of disease severity. CEAP class 3 is a comparatively early stage of disease pathogenesis where surgical sections can be obtained. Piezo1 downregulation was checked at the mRNA and substantiated at protein levels, as well as through immunostaining on tissue specimens. We assume that the larger number of samples, as well as the use of histologically normal control, contributed to our findings.

We previously observed the existence of EndMT during the development of varicose veins [3]. Even though we showed the altered signaling in cells exposed to altered flow, the involvement of mechanosensors in this process remains unclear. Earlier observations in cancer cells indicate high Piezo1-induced EMT [7]. We, however, investigated if Piezo1 depletion causes EndMT in the presence of protective flow. When Piezo1 is silenced, cells express EndMT-associated markers including SNAI1 and αSMA, even in the presence of cell-protective LSS flow. Mature ECs are associated with enhanced angiogenesis [31]. The number of branches and tubes formed from venous ECs was markedly reduced when Piezo1 is silenced. Also, EC markers such as CD31 and vWF were found to be reduced in Piezo1 KD cells. Taken together, Piezo1 is a prominent mechanosensory ion channel required for the luminal endothelial integrity.

High Piezo1 expression is known to increase KLF2 in vascular endothelium. Zheng et al. reported that augmented Piezo1 activity due to Yoda1 induces KLF2/4 expression in ECs [13]. Earlier studies have shown that Kruppel factors induce the expression of inhibitory SMAD7, and thus prevent EndMT progression [32]. In the current study, we found a lower expression of KLF2 in Piezo1 KD cells. Zheng and colleagues further reported that higher LSS at a magnitude of 16 dyn/cm2 corresponding to arterial vasculature activates Piezo1 and thereby induces KLF2/4 [13]. They also utilized a rotating orbital shaker for shear stress studies, and simulated disturbed flow by exposing the cells at the center of the well to ~ 5 dyn/cm2 shear stress. The cells exposed to this disturbed flow condition showed a lower expression of KLF2. However, the venous system always functions at physiologically low shear stress such as 1–6 dyn/cm2. The low expression of KLF2 in this earlier study at 5 dyn/cm2, led us to examine if the KLF2 pathway is significant in low shear stress systems like venous circulation. We initially checked for the KLF2 expression in human veins. Our data demonstrates that KLF2 is profoundly expressed in normal saphenous veins whereas it is drastically less in varicose veins. Vein ECs exposed to LSS at 6 dyn/cm2 also expressed KLF2, while its expression was lost in cells exposed to OSS. This clearly substantiates our finding that more than flow magnitude, flow disturbances cause hemodynamics-based pathologies. We also evaluated the expression pattern of KLF4 in our patient cohort. Unexpectedly, we did not find differential expression of KLF4 in varicose versus control specimens. Hence, we focused on KLF2 as the downstream mediator of Piezo1 in the venous system.

The EndMT preventive role of KLF2 has been explored in several previous studies [33]. To further ascertain if Piezo1-based altered mechanoregulation of EndMT is primarily happening via loss of KLF2, we silenced KLF2 and treated these cells with Piezo1 agonist Yoda1. Yoda1 was not too successful in preventing EndMT in the absence of KLF2.

Finding out how Piezo1 depletion impacts KLF2 expression under different flow patterns was the next step. It was earlier reported that shear stress at an arterial magnitude activates Piezo1 and induces Ca2+ influx that subsequently phosphorylates CaMKII and promotes MEKK3/MEK5/ERK5 signaling assisting the transcription of KLF2/4 genes [13]. We found that the MEK5/ERK5 as well as PI3K-AKT/FOXO1 pathways regulate KLF2 expression in uniform venous flow magnitude. Fascinatingly, the activated phosphorylated proteins from these pathways as well as CAMKII were found to be less in varicose veins as well as cells exposed to OSS, coinciding with the Piezo1 depletion.

We hence proposed that Piezo1 activates CaMKII signaling as one of the initial steps in fluid mechanotransduction. Several studies document that MEKK3 and PI3K phosphorylation are mediated by autophosphorylated CAMKII [13, 34]. Whether MEKK3/MEK5/ERK5 and PI3K pathways lead to KLF2 was not elucidated in venous flow. Lu et al. experimentally demonstrated that the MEF2 transcription factor is associated with 90% of the expression of KLF2 and KLF4 [25]. MEF2C, is a member of the MEF2 family of transcription factors that regulate angiogenesis in ECs. pMEF2C expression was significantly decreased in varicose veins than in control veins, as in cells subjected to OSS compared to LSS and static flow. Moreover, low pMEF2C in the presence of MEK5 inhibitor BIX02189 as well as PI3K inhibitor LY294002 indicate that the two pathways converge at the phosphorylation of MEF2C.

MEF2c is a known substrate for MEK5/ERK5 kinases as ERK5 phosphorylates Ser386/7 in MEF2C [35]. We presumed that the PI3K-AKT pathway exerts its effect on MEF2C via FOXO1 phosphorylation. KLF2 expression is known to be negatively regulated by FOXO1. FOXO1 transcription factor is a member of the forkhead box family and has been demonstrated to negatively influence MEF2C [27]. FOXO1 translocates to the cytoplasm after getting phosphorylated by PI3K/AKT kinases, facilitating MEF2C expression and thus promoting KLF2 transcription. We found that phosphorylated FOXO was downregulated in varicose veins and cells exposed to altered oscillatory flow inhibiting KLF2 expression, while normal saphenous veins and cells exposed to uniform venous flow have high levels of pFOXO1 abetting KLF2 expression.

Mimicking in vivo hemodynamic conditions such as shear stress and circumferential stretch using ECs in vitro is an attractive strategy in vascular drug discovery. The small molecule activator of Piezo-1, Yoda-1, is known to imitate the effect of uniform flow by acting like a ‘molecular wedge’ and causing conformational changes that reduce the mechanical threshold for Piezo1 activation [36]. Piezo2, a homologous protein that is likewise expressed in ECs, is not activated by Yoda-1 [37]. We found that Yoda1 at small concentrations of 1µM that simulate venous flow force is beneficial to prevent altered flow-induced cellular pathologies such as suboptimal Ca2+ influx as well as abnormal tube formation and EndMT. As with any drug compound, meticulous calibration of optimum concentration is required with Yoda1 administration. For instance, studies using 30 µM Yoda1 in vascular SMCs have shown high levels of apoptosis due to excessive Ca2 + influx and ROS generation [38]. Even 5 µM Yoda1 has been shown to reduce VE-cadherin expression and affect endothelial integrity [39].

The main drawback of our study is that, while this data considerably expands our understanding of how Piezo1 modulates endothelial function in the setting of uniform fluid flow, we did not conduct an in vivo assessment of Yoda-1 in reversing venous remodeling.

Our study reveals a crucial role of Piezo1 in the regulation of hemodynamics-driven venous remodeling using human vein samples and in vitro microfluidic experiments. We identified a malfunctioning Piezo1-KLF2 axis due to altered venous flow that promotes EndMT and limits angiogenic potential in venous ECs. Rectifying Piezo1 depletion by agonists such as Yoda1 would be an effective strategy for the pharmacological management of patients with varicose veins.
