A post-discharge homecare journey map for patients with colorectal cancer living with a permanent stoma: A qualitative study
Authors: Jia Qiao, Shenghua Jia, Yu Zhang, Songmei Cao, Haijing Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a stage-based post-discharge homecare journey map for patients with colorectal cancer living with a permanent stoma, integrating patient–caregiver dynamics to inform digitally supported nursing interventions.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 15 patients aged 18–59 years, 6 informal caregivers, and 4 stoma nurses. Patient diaries and photo-elicitation were used as supplementary data sources. Data were analyzed using a framework approach informed by the Chronic Care Model and the Theoretical Domains Framework. Two rounds of Delphi consultation with 8 multidisciplinary experts were undertaken to refine the structure and content of the journey map.ResultsFive sequential phases of the post-discharge homecare journey were identified: Home Transition, Early Adaptation, Late Adjustment, Chronic Challenge, and Stabilization. Across phases, participants described fluctuating emotional responses, strong bidirectional influences between patients and caregivers, variable engagement with digital resources, and discrepancies between perceived and actual self-management capacity. Key critical events—such as stoma leakage, concerns about public exposure, and caregiver–patient conflict—frequently triggered setbacks in recovery and reduced self-care confidence.ConclusionsThe proposed journey map provides a structured, stage-based framework for understanding post-discharge homecare experiences. It offers a conceptual foundation for developing personalized, digitally supported nursing interventions that address emotional needs, dyadic interactions, and disparities in health and digital literacy.

Introduction

Adults living with a permanent ostomy following colorectal cancer surgery face complex and evolving challenges after hospital discharge. These challenges extend beyond technical stoma management to include psychosocial adjustment, shifts in family roles, and the development of effective self-management strategies in everyday life. Previous qualitative and mixed-methods studies have documented persistent difficulties such as stoma leakage, peristomal skin complications, altered body image, stigma, and barriers to social reintegration, alongside fluctuating emotional recovery and variable confidence in self-care.1,2 Standardized, time-limited discharge education—commonly implemented in China and internationally—often proves insufficient to address these dynamic and context-dependent needs, particularly during the early post-discharge period (1–3 months), when formal support declines while home-based demands intensify.3,4

Patient journey mapping has increasingly been used as a structured approach to capture longitudinal care experiences, identify unmet needs, and locate opportunities for intervention across critical touchpoints.5, 6, 7 However, in the field of ostomy care, such approaches remain limited and have largely focused on hospital-based episodes or generalized survivorship pathways. Comparatively little attention has been paid to the homecare phase, where self-management responsibilities and caregiver involvement are most pronounced. Evidence from Asian settings, including China, further suggests that culturally embedded factors—such as stigma-related concealment, family role expectations, and variation in digital literacy—may substantially shape adaptation processes. Yet these factors are rarely incorporated into stage-based frameworks intended to guide intervention development.

Importantly, these sociocultural influences are not merely contextual but actively shape patterns of care engagement. Concealment and stigma may delay help-seeking and normalize trial-and-error approaches to self-management, thereby increasing the risk of technique drift and recurrent complications.8 Family role expectations can concentrate caregiving responsibilities and complicate the restoration of patient autonomy, rendering caregiver involvement both essential and potentially conflict-laden as roles evolve.9 In parallel, disparities in digital literacy and reliance on clinician-mediated trust influence access to and use of digital health resources, raising concerns about inequitable uptake if interventions are not designed to be accessible and minimally burdensome.10,11

Against this background, three interrelated gaps remain insufficiently addressed. First, self-management trajectories are often non-linear and prone to relapse. Patients’ perceived competence may diverge from observed performance, with diary and photographic data revealing technique errors or avoidance behaviours despite high self-efficacy scores.12,13 Second, patient–caregiver relationships exert bidirectional influences over time. While caregivers may initially support skill acquisition and emotional adjustment, later phases may be marked by caregiver burden, conflict, and ambiguity in role boundaries, which in turn affect patient coping—a pattern highlighted in recent dyadic syntheses.14,15 Third, engagement with digital support remains uneven. Although mobile health education, peer platforms, and asynchronous follow-up have shown promise, their uptake depends on usability, trust, and digital health literacy, with older and less-educated populations in China at particular risk of exclusion.16, 17, 18

These challenges are not independent but dynamically interconnected. For example, a setback in self-management—such as leakage or skin complications—may increase anxiety and uncertainty, prompting intensified caregiver involvement. While such involvement may enhance safety, it may also heighten tension, role conflict, and ambiguity as autonomy is renegotiated.13 In turn, dyadic strain can influence communication patterns and help-seeking behaviors, shaping engagement with digital resources—for instance, reluctance to share symptoms or reliance on unverified information sources.19 Conversely, well-timed and low-burden digital interventions, such as photo-based feedback or shared care tools, may simultaneously support skill development, facilitate communication, and reduce interpersonal tension.20 These reciprocal dynamics suggest that single-component interventions may have limited or short-lived effects, underscoring the need for an integrated, stage-sensitive framework to guide intervention design.

To address these complexities, this study integrates the Chronic Care Model (CCM) and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) as complementary conceptual approaches. The CCM offers a system-level perspective on continuity of care and proactive support, while the TDF provides a behavioral lens to examine modifiable determinants, including knowledge, skills, beliefs, emotions, and social influences. Together, these frameworks enable a coherent analytical structure linking individual experiences, interpersonal dynamics, and care delivery processes, while also identifying opportunities for embedding low-burden digital support within routine care pathways.21,22

The aim of this study was to develop a post-discharge homecare journey map for adults living with a permanent colorectal ostomy. Specifically, the objectives were to: (a) characterize the evolving self-management challenges experienced over time; (b) elucidate the dynamic and bidirectional relationships between patients and informal caregivers during recovery; and (c) identify stage-specific opportunities for digital support that are aligned with real-world clinical and sociocultural contexts in China.

Research questions

This study was guided by the following research questions:

RQ1: What time-structured yet psychologically dynamic self-management challenges and emotional trajectories do patients experience after discharge while living with an ostomy?

RQ2: How do informal caregivers influence, and in turn become influenced by, patients’ adaptation across recovery stages, including facilitators, tensions, and renegotiation of care boundaries?

RQ3: Which stage-specific digital support opportunities offer the greatest potential for impact, and what barriers—such as health literacy, trust, and usability—must be addressed to promote equitable uptake in the Chinese context?

By addressing these questions through an integrated focus on self-management, caregiver interaction, and digital support, this study advances a stage-sensitive and dyadic understanding of post-discharge recovery. The resulting journey map is intended to inform person-centred, nurse-led interventions that are both clinically actionable and culturally responsive.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative exploratory study used a multi-source journey-mapping approach, integrating semi-structured interviews, patient diaries, and photo-elicitation to examine post-discharge self-management among young and middle-aged colorectal cancer survivors with permanent ostomies. Reporting followed SRQR and COREQ guidelines to ensure transparency and rigor.23,24 The study was guided by continuity-of-care principles, with a focus on how patients and families manage recovery after discharge. We examined modifiable factors—such as knowledge, skills, self-efficacy, emotional responses, social context, and environmental constraints—that influence daily management and engagement with follow-up support.21,22 The study was conducted at Qingdao Municipal Hospital (Qingdao, China), with recruitment and data collection between January and June 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; additional consent was obtained for diary and photo data, including use, de-identification, and withdrawal of images prior to analysis. Participants included patients, informal caregivers, and stoma nurses/enterostomal therapists. Eligibility criteria and recruitment procedures are described below.

Participant groups and eligibility

Eligibility criteria were defined a priori for each group. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18–59 years, had a diagnosis of colorectal cancer, had undergone permanent colostomy or ileostomy, and had been discharged home after surgery at the time of recruitment. To capture experiences across different post-discharge stages, patients were purposively recruited across time since discharge, including within the first month, 1–2 months, 2–3 months, and ≥ 3 months after discharge. Cognitive and communication ability was assessed pragmatically—through clinician confirmation of no documented impairment and a brief screening conversation by the research assistant to ensure participants could engage in an interview in Mandarin. No formal cognitive screening tool was used. Caregivers were unpaid primary caregivers aged ≥18 years who met at least one of the following criteria: (1) living with the patient or (2) providing ≥14 hours of care per week over the past month. The ≥14-h/week threshold was selected to identify caregivers with sustained involvement and has been used in prior caregiving research.25 Paid caregivers and those unable to provide informed consent were excluded. Nurses were registered nurses with at least five years of experience in ostomy care and/or enterostomal therapist certification. All were actively involved in discharge education or follow-up care. Administrative-only staff and trainees were excluded.

Sampling

We used purposive maximum-variation sampling to capture a range of post-discharge experiences.26 For patients, variation was sought across sex, ostomy type (colostomy/ileostomy), time since surgery (< 1 month, 1–2 months, 2–3 months), education level, and the presence of peristomal skin complications. For caregivers, we considered relationship to the patient, co-residence, and caregiving intensity (< 3, 3–6, > 6 hours/day or ≥ 14 hours/week). For nurses, variation included years of experience, specialist certification, and practice setting (ward, clinic, community). Patients and caregivers were not recruited as complete matched dyads. Accordingly, patient–caregiver comparisons were used to explore complementary perspectives across respondent groups rather than to conduct paired dyadic analysis.

Recruitment procedures

Participants were recruited using a standardised process. A research assistant not involved in clinical care contacted eligible individuals within seven days, provided study information, and allowed at least 24 hours for consideration. Written consent was obtained in person or via an institutional e-consent system. Interviews were conducted in person, by telephone, or via secure video according to participant preference. Patients were identified through the hospital EMR system and approached in clinic or by phone. Caregivers were recruited via patient nomination, with participation clearly voluntary and independent; interviews were conducted separately where appropriate. Nurses were recruited through departmental lists and invitations distributed via email or WeChat, with consent obtained outside working hours to minimise perceived coercion. Participants who opted into the diary/photo component received guidance on privacy and handling of identifiable content. All participants received a small token of appreciation and transport reimbursement in line with institutional policy. Reasons for non-participation were recorded where available.

Use of CCM and TDF in analysis

Coding approach

Analysis was informed by the TDF and CCM, which were used to guide coding and interpretation rather than as rigid frameworks. An initial codebook was developed based on key TDF domains (e.g., knowledge, skills, beliefs about capability, emotion, social influences, environmental context). Segments of text were coded to these domains only when clearly aligned with their definitions. For example, anxiety about leakage was coded under emotion, while practical difficulties with appliance use were coded under skills. At the same time, data were considered in relation to care delivery (e.g., self-management support, follow-up systems), allowing links to be made between patient experience and potential service responses. Two analysts coded the data independently. Differences were discussed and resolved through consensus, with decisions recorded in analytic memos.

Temporal structuring

To support a longitudinal view of recovery, approximate time windows were defined before analysis (days 1–3, 4–14, 15–30, months 2–3, and ≥ 3 months post-discharge). These were used as flexible reference points rather than strict categories. Where timing and experience did not align—for example, when a participant showed a clear shift in challenges despite being within an earlier time window—interpretation was guided by the nature of the experience rather than by time alone.

From codes to stages

Codes were grouped into broader themes and examined across time. Stages were defined when consistent patterns in tasks, emotions, and contextual factors emerged across participants. Early stages were typically dominated by practical and emotional challenges, while later stages reflected social adjustment and longer-term management. Negative cases were examined throughout. When reported confidence did not match observed behaviours, interpretation was based on what participants described doing rather than what they reported feeling. These decisions were documented in analytic memos.

Identifying intervention opportunities

Potential digital support points were identified where three conditions were met: (1) a clear and recurring challenge; (2) a feasible way to address it within existing care structures; and (3) no compromise to patient safety or clinical responsibility.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews

Interview guides were developed separately for patients, caregivers, and nurses, while retaining a shared structure to support comparison. Guide development was iterative and informed by prior work, relevant literature, and clinical input. Drafts were tested with two individuals from each group and refined for clarity and flow. Minor adjustments were made during data collection where needed. Interviews were conducted in Mandarin, audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Where required, transcripts were translated into English. Interviews lasted 35–65 minutes. All interviews covered core topics including self-care, emotional adjustment, caregiver involvement, and use of digital support. Additional questions addressed role-specific experiences. Full guides are available in Supplementary File 1 (S1–S3).

Diaries and photo-elicitation

Five patients completed a 7-day diary and provided photographs documenting daily routines and challenges. These data added context to interview findings and helped capture day-to-day variation. Diaries and images were analysed alongside transcripts. Images were interpreted only in conjunction with participant explanations to avoid over-interpretation.

Expert validation

An expert panel (n = 8) reviewed the emerging findings, including stage structure and potential intervention points. The panel included colorectal surgeons, stoma nurses, and specialists in psycho-oncology and digital health.

An initial NGT session was followed by two Delphi rounds conducted online. Experts rated items on relevance, clarity, and feasibility. Consensus criteria were defined a priori (Item Content Validity Index [I-CVI] ≥ 0.78; ≥ 75% agreement).27, 28, 29

All experts completed both rounds. Only items supported by qualitative data were retained. Full details are provided in Supplementary File 1 (S4).

Data analysis and journey-map construction

Analytic approach

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis with both inductive and deductive elements.30,31

Data preparation

All data were transcribed, anonymised, and managed in NVivo 14. Visual data were linked to text and handled according to established ethical guidance.32

Stepwise analytic pipeline

Data analysis followed a structured, iterative process. First, two nurse-researcher analysts (A1/A2), who were not involved in participants’ clinical care, independently conducted open coding on an initial 20% stratified sample (patients, caregivers, nurses) to develop Codebook v1. The remaining data were then coded iteratively with memoing. Discrepancies were discussed in weekly meetings and resolved by consensus, with input from a health-services methodologist when needed.

Codes were subsequently grouped into broader thematic categories, including behavioural tasks/errors, emotional responses, dyadic dynamics, environmental context, and digital engagement. Code definitions and inclusion/exclusion criteria were maintained in a living codebook with version control.

Temporal structuring was guided by participant-reported markers (e.g., discharge, first independent appliance change, first leakage episode, return to work) and diary timestamps. Candidate stages were identified when consistent shifts in themes were observed across cases, either through inflection across multiple domains or recurring configurations of experiences. Five stages were retained after team review, with final labels confirmed during validation (reported in Results).

For each stage, key touchpoints were mapped across setting (home, clinic, digital), actor (patient, caregiver, nurse), and task (e.g., pouch change, skin care, supply management). Each touchpoint was linked to supporting data (interview excerpts, diary entries, or images) and annotated with challenges, facilitators, and potential digital supports.

To support interpretation, composite personas were generated based on recurring patterns (e.g., role, stoma type, caregiving intensity, confidence–performance gaps, digital literacy). These were used for representation only; all findings remained grounded in the data.

Findings were organised into a structured matrix (Stage × Touchpoint × Emotion × Need × Opportunity). Priority scores (0–3) were assigned independently by A1/A2 and reconciled through discussion. Mapping rules are detailed in Supplementary File 2.

Several validation steps were undertaken. Interpretive summaries were shared with a subset of participants to confirm stage boundaries and key challenges.33 A senior stoma nurse reviewed the clinical plausibility of touchpoints. In addition, a modified Delphi process with NGT elements was conducted over two rounds (four-week interval), involving experts in colorectal surgery, stoma care, psycho-oncology, and digital health. Consensus was defined a priori (≥ 75% agreement),34, 35, 36 and all revisions were documented.

Finally, the matrix was translated into a five-stage journey map using UXPressia and refined in Adobe Illustrator. Visual elements were limited to abstract representations, and accessibility (alt-text) was incorporated. The full analytic workflow is provided in Supplementary File 1 (S5).

Handling of visual and diary data

Photographs were coded in relation to both what they showed and what participants described. Only interpretations supported by accompanying text were used. Diary entries were time-stamped and used to support temporal analysis.

Reflexivity and audit trail

Analytic decisions were documented throughout. Codebooks, memos, and data matrices were versioned and archived to allow traceability.

Cross-group analysis

Data from patients, caregivers, and nurses were analysed together while retaining group identity. A matrix was used to compare perspectives across stages and domains. Patterns were classified as agreement, complementarity, or divergence. Differences were discussed and explored through further analysis. Representative quotations are provided in Supplementary File 1 (S6).

Data security and withdrawal

Participants were assigned unique study IDs, with identifiers stored separately from research data on encrypted drives. Audio and image data were de-identified upon upload; any identifiable elements in photos were removed or obscured prior to analysis. Participants could withdraw at any time before data anonymization or final analysis without consequence.

Research team, reflexivity, and trustworthiness

Research team and interviewer characteristics

Data were collected by two nurse-researchers (A/B) with oncology and stoma-care experience and formal training in qualitative methods. Prior to fieldwork, both completed refresher training in semi-structured interviewing, management of sensitive topics (e.g., body image, intimacy), and ethical handling of diary/photo data. Neither interviewer had prior clinical relationships with participants. The wider team included a qualitative methodologist (analytic oversight) and a senior stoma-nurse advisor (clinical review). Throughout the study, researchers maintained reflexive memos on their clinical background, assumptions (e.g., expectations regarding digital follow-up), and potential influence on data collection and interpretation. These reflections were discussed in regular team meetings and documented in the audit trail.

Coding procedures and analytic rigour

Two analysts independently coded an initial stratified subset (∼20%) of transcripts (patients, caregivers, nurses) to develop Codebook v1, followed by iterative coding of the full dataset with regular consensus meetings. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with methodologist input when required. Emphasis was placed on transparent memoing and analytic consistency rather than formal inter-rater statistics; a calibration exercise demonstrated high agreement prior to full coding.

The coding framework encompassed behavioural tasks/errors, emotional responses, dyadic dynamics, contextual factors, and digital engagement/trust. Framework tags were applied as attributes to support temporal analysis and identification of intervention opportunities. The full codebook (definitions, rules, and examples) is available in Supplementary File 1 (S7).

Sampling adequacy and saturation

Sampling was guided by information power, with ongoing assessment of meaning saturation. After every three interviews, emergent findings were reviewed; recruitment ceased when no new major codes emerged across two consecutive rounds and themes were sufficiently developed. A detailed saturation log is provided in Supplementary File 1 (S8).

Strategies to enhance trustworthiness

Multiple strategies were used to ensure credibility and rigour. Data triangulation was achieved by integrating interviews (patients, caregivers, nurses), diaries, and photo-elicitation. Interpretive member checking was conducted with a subset of participants to validate stage boundaries and key findings. Clinical plausibility was reviewed by a senior stoma nurse, and discrepancies prompted targeted re-analysis. An audit trail documenting coding decisions, matrices, and analytic iterations was maintained (Supplementary File 1, S9). Reflexivity was embedded throughout via ongoing memoing and team discussion.

Reporting standards

The study was reported in accordance with SRQR and COREQ guidelines. Completed checklists, including item-to-manuscript mapping, are provided in Supplementary File 3 (SRQR) and Supplementary File 4 (COREQ).

Interviews were conducted in person or remotely (telephone/video) according to participant preference. A subset of patients (n = 5) completed 7-day diaries with contextual photographs. No participants withdrew. Phase 1 — Home Transition (Day 1–3): disorientation and perceived threat

The initial post-discharge period was characterised by leakage anxiety, sleep disruption, and dependence on immediate support. Patients described hyper-vigilance and repeated checking, while caregivers provided continuous monitoring. Nurses prioritised rapid reassurance and stepwise guidance.

Illustrative evidence.

Patient (P03, F, 38): “I didn't dare sleep—I worried the bag would leak. My mother stayed by my side every night that week.”

Patient (P09, M, 44): “I checked the edge every hour. I kept thinking I smelled something.”

Caregiver (CG01, mother, co-resident): “We took turns at night because she was afraid of a leak. I watched while she slept.”

Caregiver (CG02, spouse, co-resident): “I set alarms for 2 a.m. and 4 a.m. to help him check and calm down.”

Nurse (N01, ET nurse, 10y): “First 72 hours are leak-anxiety heavy; we coach step-by-step by phone when needed.”

Nurse (N02, stoma nurse, 6y): “Early calls focus on fitting basics and what to do if a seal lifts.”

Diary/photo. “Set alarm 2 a.m. to check seal.” (D-P03-d2-l5); bathroom counter ‘lay-out tools in order’ workaround (Ph-P03-d2-p1).

Micro-findings. Rapid reassurance + concrete “what to do if” steps; household sleep disruption common; early, trusted access reduces avoidant behaviours and error spirals.

Phase 2 — Early Adaptation (Day 4–14): fragile confidence

Confidence increased but remained unstable. Patients experimented with appliances, while minor symptoms (e.g., skin irritation, odour) triggered disproportionate concern. Caregivers reinforced technique and emotional coping; nurses focused on standardised care routines.

Illustrative evidence.

Patient (P12, M, 46): “I kept switching pouch brands. Some days I felt confident, then the next day something went wrong again.”

Patient (P06, F, 35): “The ring helped, but I wasn't sure if I placed it right.”

Caregiver (CG03, spouse, co-resident): “Any redness makes him panic; I remind him about the skin-care steps.”

Caregiver (CG02, spouse, co-resident): “Odour made him want to avoid eating; we followed the nurse's list.”

Nurse (N02, stoma nurse, 6y): “Skin care and odour dominate early calls; caregivers co-manage practice at home.”

Nurse (N04, stoma nurse, 12y): “Short, targeted tips work best—like ‘clean, dry, warm the skin, then ring.’”

Diary. “Tried barrier ring → less itching.” (D-P12-d5-l3); “Kept food diary to check odour triggers.” (D-P06-d8-l2)

Expert validation and framework refinement

A modified Delphi process, preceded by a structured NGT workshop, was used to refine stages, needs, and digital intervention opportunities. Eight experts participated (100% response rate), representing colorectal surgery, stoma nursing, psycho-oncology, and digital health (median experience 9 years). Across two Delphi rounds, consensus thresholds were achieved for all domains (agreement ≥ 75%). Stage definitions demonstrated high content validity (Scale Content Validity Index [S-CVI]/Ave > 0.90), and refinements focused primarily on clarifying boundaries and improving feasibility of digital components. Full results and item-level data are provided in Supplementary File 2. All retained items were required to meet predefined evidence criteria (triangulation across participant groups and/or diary/photo data), as specified in Supplementary File 2.

Digital intervention opportunities

Nurse-endorsed photo triage with escalation rules

In the early post-discharge period (Phases 1–2), uncertainty around leakage and appliance management was a dominant concern. Patients described difficulty sleeping and persistent vigilance, often supported by caregivers who provided continuous monitoring. As one patient noted, “I didn't dare sleep—I worried the bag would leak. My mother stayed by my side every night that week” (P03), echoed by a caregiver who described taking turns overnight. Nurses similarly characterised the first 72 hours as “leak-anxiety heavy,” requiring step-by-step remote guidance (N01). Diary entries further illustrated these patterns, including repeated nocturnal checks and improvised routines. These accounts point to the value of timely reassurance and technique correction when in-person access is limited. Photo-based triage, when endorsed by nurses and supported by clear escalation criteria, offers a feasible way to provide feedback while maintaining safety.

Just-in-time skin-care micro-lessons

During Early Adaptation (Phase 2), participants frequently described fluctuating confidence linked to minor technical issues. Patients reported repeated adjustments to appliances and uncertainty around correct technique, while caregivers often acted as intermediaries in reinforcing care routines. Nurses emphasised that early contacts were dominated by skin care and odour concerns, typically involving small but consequential errors. Diary data captured iterative learning processes, such as experimenting with barrier rings or tracking dietary triggers. In this context, brief, targeted inputs delivered at the point of need—rather than general education—appeared better aligned with participants’ experiences, particularly for reinforcing consistent technique.

Communication support for disclosure and stigma-sensitive contexts

By Phase 3, challenges shifted toward social participation. Although some patients engaged with peer support online, disclosure in workplace settings remained limited. One participant noted that, despite gaining confidence, they had “still not told colleagues anything” (P07). Caregivers, meanwhile, described increasing emotional strain as expectations around independence evolved. Nurses identified this stage as one where relational tensions often emerged, requiring support not only for practical management but also for communication. Avoidance behaviours—such as withdrawing from shared spaces—were also evident in diary accounts. These findings suggest a role for structured communication support to facilitate disclosure and reduce stigma-related stress.

Return-to-work planning and scenario-based preparation

In Phase 4, attention shifted toward managing stoma care in more complex, real-world contexts. Participants described adapting clothing, planning routines around work schedules, and preparing for situations outside the home. For example, one patient reported wearing specific garments to increase confidence during meetings (P11), while caregivers described ongoing concern despite reduced involvement. Nurses highlighted the importance of anticipatory guidance at this stage, particularly in relation to work and social scenarios. Practical planning—such as timing, access to facilities, and carrying supplies—appeared to support alignment between perceived and actual capability.

Intimacy communication support

Alongside practical adaptation, issues related to intimacy and identity remained salient in Phase 4. Although patients often reported being able to manage self-care, both patients and caregivers described lingering concerns about confidence in private and social contexts. Nurses noted that discussions around intimacy typically emerged at this stage, often requiring careful timing and sensitive communication. These findings indicate that support needs extend beyond technical competence, with a role for guidance that addresses expectations, communication, and relational adjustment.

Asynchronous nurse-supported follow-up

In the Stabilization phase (Phase 5), participants described a sense of routine and independence, but not complete resolution of uncertainty. Patients expressed a desire for occasional reassurance—“someone following up like a coach” (P05)—while caregivers noted persistent, though less visible, concerns about long-term outcomes. Nurses described asynchronous communication, including photo-based review, as a practical way to maintain support without increasing service burden. Diary entries illustrated how such interactions could resolve minor issues without escalation. Taken together, these accounts support the value of low-intensity, nurse-linked follow-up that remains available as needed, particularly when combined with clear criteria for escalation. Full mappings of stage, opportunity, and supporting evidence are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Cross-group integration of perspectives

Across patients, caregivers, and nurses, several patterned alignments and mismatches were observed, which together point to key mechanisms underlying post-discharge adaptation. First, there was clear convergence around leakage as an early turning point. The initial leakage episode consistently triggered heightened vigilance, emotional distress, and increased caregiver involvement, while prompting nurses to re-emphasise core techniques. As one patient described, “I didn't dare sleep—I worried the bag would leak. My mother stayed by my side every night that week” (P03). This suggests that early experiences function less as isolated events and more as behavioural anchors shaping subsequent adaptation trajectories.

Second, perspectives were complementary in relation to confidence development. Rather than progressing linearly, patient confidence fluctuated, with setbacks common even after initial gains. Caregivers played a buffering role during these periods, while nurses framed these fluctuations in terms of correctable technique issues. Taken together, this indicates that self-efficacy is dynamically co-regulated within patient–caregiver dyads, rather than individually stabilised. Third, a consistent dissonance was observed between perceived readiness and demonstrated performance. Although many patients reported feeling ready by late month 1, diary and photo data, alongside nurse assessments, revealed persistent errors and avoidance behaviours. This gap suggests that subjective readiness may overestimate actual competence, highlighting the limits of self-report in capturing adaptation.

Fourth, social reintegration was characterised by complementary but distinct concerns. Patients emphasised stigma management and selective disclosure—“I still haven't told my colleagues anything” (P07)—whereas caregivers described role strain as responsibilities shifted. Nurses, in turn, focused on structured preparation for real-world contexts. These combined accounts indicate that adaptation involves parallel negotiation of technical, social, and relational domains. Finally, all groups converged on the importance of accessible, trusted follow-up, albeit with different emphases. Patients expressed a desire for ongoing reassurance, while nurses prioritised scalable models such as asynchronous communication and photo-based review. This supports the view that sustained adaptation depends on low-burden, responsive support systems rather than episodic care alone. Additional supporting quotations are provided in Supplementary File 1 (S6).

Discussion

Main findings

Theory-integrated five-phase recovery trajectory after stoma surgery

This study develops a theory-informed and empirically grounded framework that captures the dynamic, emotionally fluid, and socially embedded nature of post-discharge recovery in young and middle-aged colorectal cancer patients with permanent stomas. By integrating the Chronic Care Model (CCM) and the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), we delineated a five-phase trajectory—Home Transition, Early Adaptation, Late Adjustment, Chronic Challenge, and Stabilization. Each phase is characterised by distinct challenges, behavioural shifts, emotional inflection points, and evolving support needs. These findings extend survivorship care beyond technical self-management to encompass psychosocial and relational adaptation. Cross-phase alignment and supporting excerpts are provided in Supplementary File 1 (S6).

Phase-specific dynamics in post-discharge adaptation

The trajectory observed here contrasts with linear rehabilitation models, highlighting the cyclical and non-linear nature of recovery. In the Home Transition and Early Adaptation phases, patients experienced psychological disorientation, strong caregiver dependence, and uncertainty in appliance management despite standard discharge education. This aligns with prior work showing that cognitive overload and low self-efficacy hinder effective self-care.13 The Late Adjustment phase emerged as a critical but under-recognised period marked by emotional vulnerability, driven by treatment fatigue, social isolation, and identity disruption. Even with technical competence, patients reported psychological strain, consistent with survivorship literature describing distress during the transition back to “normal life”.37 In the Chinese context, stigma and concealment may further intensify this burden, particularly during return-to-work and public reintegration.8 In the Chronic Challenge and Stabilization phases, patients gradually developed routines and greater autonomy. However, concerns related to body image, intimacy, recurrence, and social participation persisted. This reinforces evidence that psychosocial challenges remain long after initial recovery and require sustained support.1,37

The bidirectional influence of informal caregivers

Caregivers functioned as both facilitators and, at times, amplifiers of stress. Early in recovery, active involvement helped compensate for patients’ inexperience and emotional distress. Over time, however, increasing patient autonomy often led to role renegotiation and relational strain. These patterns are consistent with dyadic coping theory, which emphasises the interdependence of patient and caregiver adjustment9,14,15. Prior qualitative studies similarly report caregiver fatigue and uncertainty, particularly in mid-recovery phases.38 Despite this, caregiver needs remain under-addressed in many oncology care models.39 In familism-oriented contexts such as China, caregiving is often expected, which may intensify early over-involvement and later tensions around autonomy and boundaries. These findings highlight the need for dyadic interventions that support both skill development and relational adjustment.

Disparities in digital engagement and implications for eHealth design

Although participants expressed general openness to digital tools, engagement varied by age, education, and digital literacy. Younger and more educated patients reported greater use of apps and online resources, whereas older participants often faced usability barriers or expressed distrust, consistent with eHealth literature.16 Engagement was notably higher when digital tools were introduced or endorsed by trusted clinicians, particularly specialist nurses.20 This underscores the central role of relational trust in digital uptake, suggesting that nurse-led onboarding and follow-up are critical for sustained engagement.17 To ensure equitable access, digital interventions should prioritise user-centred design, including simplified interfaces, visual feedback, and adaptability across recovery stages. Cultural factors further shape these patterns. Familism may increase caregiver involvement, stigma may limit disclosure, and reliance on trusted clinical sources may explain preferences for nurse-endorsed platforms.8,14,39 Together, these findings support digital models that are family-inclusive, culturally sensitive, and embedded within ongoing clinical relationships.

Mismatch between perceived competence and actual self-care ability

A consistent finding was the gap between self-reported confidence and observed performance. While patients often expressed confidence in stoma care, diary and photo data revealed frequent technical errors, hygiene lapses, and avoidance behaviours such as fasting. This aligns with evidence that self-report may overestimate competence in chronic illness management.40 Standard assessment tools may not capture the practical complexity of self-care behaviours.41 Integrating visual monitoring, tele-coaching, or future studies could examine whether AI-assisted or nurse-supported visual feedback improves skill acquisition and calibration.42

Underutilization of professional support despite ongoing needs

Despite available services, many participants hesitated to seek help due to logistical barriers, fear of judgement, or uncertainty about symptom significance. Notably, this persisted even in later phases, when patients appeared independent but continued to experience unresolved concerns.18 This suggests a gap between service availability and accessibility. Asynchronous, low-threshold support models—such as secure messaging, symptom-checking tools, and AI-assisted triage—may offer timely reassurance without increasing burden.43

Implications for nursing practice

This study provides a trajectory-informed framework for post-discharge oncology nursing. The five-phase model supports stage-specific interventions tailored to patients’ evolving emotional and self-management needs, rather than uniform education approaches. A dyadic perspective is essential. Nurses should assess and support both patients and caregivers, recognising the influence of relational dynamics on recovery outcomes. The findings also highlight the role of nurses in facilitating digitally supported care. Active involvement in introducing and guiding digital tools can enhance trust, engagement, and continuity of care. Finally, sustained psychosocial support is needed across the survivorship continuum. Flexible, nurse-led follow-up—via telehealth or digital platforms—may help address fluctuating needs, reinforce self-care, and support long-term adaptation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in China, and the findings should therefore be interpreted within this specific institutional and sociocultural context. The transferability of the journey map to other regions, health care systems, and community-based ostomy care settings may be limited. Second, although maximum-variation sampling was used, the overall sample size was modest, particularly for informal caregivers and stoma nurses. Their perspectives enriched the analysis but may not fully represent the diversity of caregiver roles and nursing practices across different settings. Third, patients and caregivers were not recruited as complete matched dyads; therefore, the findings should be understood as complementary perspectives rather than paired dyadic accounts.

Fourth, diary and photo-elicitation data were available from only five patients. These materials were used to enrich and contextualize the interview findings, but they did not independently define the journey stages. Fifth, the digital health intervention opportunities identified in this study were proposed based on qualitative findings and expert consultation; they were not implemented or evaluated in clinical practice. Future studies should examine the feasibility, acceptability, safety, and effectiveness of these proposed digital components. Sixth, digital literacy was not formally measured. Education level was used only as a pragmatic proxy and may not fully capture participants’ actual digital skills, confidence, access, or readiness to use digital health tools. Finally, the five journey phases were analytically derived to organize recurring patterns in the post-discharge homecare experience. Individual trajectories may vary, and movement across phases may be non-linear or overlapping rather than strictly sequential.

Conclusions

This study presents a homecare trajectory for colorectal cancer patients with a permanent ostomy, outlining five distinct post-discharge phases. The model captures evolving needs, barriers, and emotional transitions, providing a structured understanding of recovery. By integrating the CCM and TDF, the framework links behaviours with contextual and psychological drivers. Expert consultation supported the face validity and clinical plausibility of the proposed journey map. These findings provide a foundation for developing phase-specific digital nursing interventions and personalised support strategies in ostomy care.
