Evaluating intra‐ and inter‐life stage density‐dependent dynamics for management of perennial amphidromous fish
Authors: Ben R. J. Crichton, Michael J. H. Hickford, Angus R. McIntosh, David R. Schiel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCompensatory density‐dependent (DD) processes play an integral role in fisheries management by underpinning fundamental population demographics. However, DD processes are often assessed only for specific life stages, likely resulting in misleading evaluations of population limitations. Here, we assessed the relative roles of intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD interactions in shaping the population dynamics of perennial freshwater fish with demographically open populations. Specifically, we monitored populations of amphidromous banded kōkopu ( Galaxias fasciatus ), giant kōkopu ( Galaxias argenteus ), and shortjaw kōkopu ( Galaxias postvectis ) in five streams where migratory post‐larvae are fished and in three no‐take (“closed”) streams located on New Zealand's South Island for two years. Using mark‐recapture data, we investigated whether fishing altered densities of “small” (non‐territorial recruits ≤1‐year‐old) and “large” (territorial fish >1‐year‐old) kōkopu size classes, and how subsequent density shifts affected the apparent survival and growth of each class while controlling for other confounding factors (e.g., habitat characteristics). We found that closed areas had substantially greater biomass of small kōkopu, particularly following the two‐month fishing season. Despite this greater influx of recruits, there was no difference in the biomass of large kōkopu at the species level, or as a combined assemblage between stream types. This indicated that although fishing of post‐larvae reduced recruit influxes into adult habitats, there was no subsequent evidence of recruitment‐limitation within adult populations. Instead, kōkopu demographics were underpinned by intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD competition and predation. Greater large fish densities played a key role in regulating the survival, growth, and/or presence of various kōkopu classes. In contrast, greater small fish densities had positive effects on the growth of opportunistic and insectivorous congeners, likely due to cannibalism and altered foraging behaviors, respectively. Our study details the prominent role of intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD interactions in regulating the population dynamics of perennial migratory freshwater fishes, even in populations with inhibited recruit and juvenile availability. We emphasize the importance for fisheries management to implement recruitment dependencies and complex interactions between distinct life stages to avoid deleterious DD responses and ensure population persistence.

INTRODUCTION

Compensatory density‐dependent (DD) processes are paramount in the management of fisheries due to their fundamental role in population regulation (Lorenzen & Camp, 2019; Lorenzen & Enberg, 2002). However, DD processes (e.g., recruitment dependence or competition) are often assessed only within specific life stages, likely leading to inaccurate evaluations when there are explicit intra‐life stage interactions (Grossman & Simon, 2019; Halpern et al., 2005; Lobón‐Cerviá, 2022). The relationship between intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD interactions and population demographics is difficult to quantify in natural systems, particularly in species with a multistage life cycle where adults produce larvae (i.e., potential recruits) that disperse to distinct habitats (Johnson, 2008). Consequently, empirical research is needed to assess the relative roles of DD and recruitment processes in controlling the population dynamics of species with demographically open populations (Atlas et al., 2015; Lobón‐Cerviá, 2022). This knowledge is essential for formulating effective fishery and conservation goals, especially for fish populations with exploited or stocked juveniles, degraded nursery habitats, or with migratory life stages (Lobón‐Cerviá, 2009b; Oele et al., 2018).

The relative role of DD regulation in controlling population dynamics is often interconnected with “recruitment”—the annual number of juveniles that enter the population to initiate a new cohort (Lobón‐Cerviá, 2009a). Insufficient juvenile availability can constrain recruitment (“recruitment‐limitation”), resulting in partial occupation of available habitat by older cohorts, reduced population size, and weakened DD processes (Lobón‐Cerviá, 2022). For migratory species, recruitment‐limitations can be exacerbated by anthropogenic factors including juvenile fishing, nursery habitat degradation, and dispersal barriers (Harris et al., 2012; Lorenzen, 2005; Oele et al., 2018). Conversely, in environments with greater recruitment (e.g., no‐take reserves), DD processes may have a more dominant role in moderating populations as they approach local carrying capacity, the maximum population size that can be supported from a particular level of resources (Ayllón et al., 2012; Lobón‐Cerviá, 2007). However, even with low recruitment, carrying capacities and regulatory DD process may largely control population dynamics if adult individuals are long‐lived and the turnover of available positions within adult habitats is slow (Halpern et al., 2005). Although limited juvenile availability can restrict the abundance of adult populations, the extent to which recruit dependency and adult habitat capacities interact to regulate overall population size through DD processes is still poorly understood (Halpern et al., 2005; Wilson et al., 2016).

In addition to assessing recruit dependency, understanding how territorial behaviors underpin resource use and DD processes is essential for accurately evaluating and directing management efforts (Ayllón et al., 2012; Nicola et al., 2016). This is especially relevant in organisms that compete via exploitation and interference, because energetically costly aggressive interactions and resource monopolization typically result in lower carrying capacities than what would be due to resource availability alone (Adams, 2001; Begon et al., 2006). Moreover, the energetic costs of foraging and territorial defense escalate proportionally with density because of heightened encounter rates with competitors (Post et al., 1999; Rosenfeld & Boss, 2001). Nondominant individuals are forced to occupy suboptimal habitats, alter foraging behaviors, emigrate, or die (Adams, 2001; Lo'pez‐Sepulcre & Kooko, 2005). Despite these potential implications, territoriality is often omitted in evaluations of population dynamics (Nicola et al., 2016). Consequently, management practices such as stocking, which often increases fish densities above local carrying capacities, can drive over‐compensatory DD feedbacks with resulting mortality and long‐term reductions in population size (Ayllón et al., 2012).

The extent to which DD pressures act on the demographics of life stages within populations is generally underpinned by body size, which often determines an individual's susceptibility to predation, gape‐limitation (i.e., prey availability), metabolic efficiency, and competitive ability (Hasegawa & Fukui, 2022; Huntsman et al., 2021). Generally, the demographics of large‐bodied life stages, typically mature adults, are driven by DD growth because they are competitively superior, too large to be eaten, and have greater energy stores that permit survival during periods of low food availability (Lorenzen & Enberg, 2002; Post et al., 1999). Alternatively, the demographics of small‐bodied life stages are generally driven by a combination of DD mortality and growth, because they are more prone to starvation and predation while being competitively excluded from key feeding habitats, which inherently slows growth and prolongs these risks (Lorenzen, 2008). Despite being competitively inferior, densities of small‐bodied fish can influence the demographics of larger fish through increased exploitative competition for shared prey resources or by acting as an important prey themselves (Kaspersson & Hojesjo, 2009; Nordwall et al., 2001; Pereira et al., 2017). Therefore, identifying and incorporating intra‐ and inter‐life stage interactions into conservation management will help ensure that beneficial DD feedbacks continue, available positions within the population are filled, and that regulatory DD processes are not exacerbated.

Here, we quantitatively assessed amphidromous galaxiid populations for two years within five streams where migratory post‐larvae were fished and within three streams located in no‐take reserves. The aim was to evaluate the relative importance of recruitment and DD processes in controlling population dynamics. We examined how the survival and growth of “small” (non‐territorial recruits ≤1‐year‐old) and “large” (territorial fish >1‐year‐old) size classes of banded kōkopu (Galaxias fasciatus), giant kōkopu (Galaxias argenteus), and shortjaw kōkopu (Galaxias postvectis) responded to intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD processes, while controlling for other confounding factors (e.g., habitat characteristics). Kōkopu are long‐lived (>9 year lifespan), territorial, iteroparous fish that are endemic to New Zealand (Hopkins, 1979). Each Austral spring, migratory galaxiid populations receive enormous pulses of immigrating post‐larvae (“whitebait”) after 4–6 months of marine development (McDowall, 1965). Adult individuals are not fished. However, whitebait are caught around coastal river mouths, forming a culturally, commercially, and recreationally important fishery (McDowall, 1996). To obviate the potential for overexploitation, a network of 22 no‐take (i.e., “closed”) areas was introduced along the west coast of the South Island, beginning in 1964 (McDowall, 1996; Reddy, 2023). However, there is little conclusive research on how closed areas or fishing of post‐larvae affect kōkopu populations. In this regard, the closed area network provides a suitable experimental design to investigate the conservation benefits of reserves for kōkopu and to assess the relative role of recruitment and DD processes in shaping the population dynamics of iteroparous fish in general.

We evaluated the role of recruitment in controlling population dynamics of kōkopu by testing whether closed streams received greater influxes of migratory recruits within adult habitats and, if so, whether this increased adult kōkopu densities. Specifically, if recruitment‐limitation were limiting population size, we would expect to see increases in adult densities in response to increased recruit availability. However, if adult carrying capacities were limiting population size, there would be no discernible change in adult densities with increased recruit availability. Beyond comprehending recruit dependency, it remains crucial to understand how intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD processes regulate survival, growth, and size structuring within populations to guide effective management. Therefore, we also explored the role of recruitment as a DD factor by testing how juvenile densities influenced the growth and survival of small and large kōkopu. Specifically, for large kōkopu, we would expect growth and survival to increase if recruits were viable prey resources, or decrease if recruits were additional competitors. For small kōkopu, however, recruits are likely too big to be eaten by individuals in the same size class, so we expected growth and survival to exclusively decrease through increased competition. Finally, we assessed the relative role of competitively dominant large kōkopu in regulating the demographics of local kōkopu populations, with the expectation that increased large kōkopu densities would reduce growth and survival of small and large kōkopu through increased predation and competition, respectively.

METHODS

Study sites

To investigate how kōkopu population dynamics are influenced by fishing of post‐larvae, three 50‐m reaches were sampled bimonthly within each of three closed (unfished) and five open (fished) streams (hereafter “sites”) on the West Coast of New Zealand's South Island between January 2020 and December 2021 (Appendix S1: Figure S1). The three closed rivers have been protected since at least 1994. Local topographic maps, site visits, and databases, such as Freshwater Ecosystems of New Zealand (FENZ; Leathwick et al., 2010) and the New Zealand Freshwater Fish Database (NZFFD; National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research, 2021), were used to select sites that were within the natural distribution of each kōkopu species and that had no fish passage barriers or introduced species (e.g., trout). Selected sites were in minimally degraded catchments, within 17 km of the coast, and <100 m in elevation to control for landscape‐scale influences.

Kōkopu survey

Study reaches were set between riffles in areas with minimal surface turbulence, or natural visual‐obstructions, and were <1.5 m deep. Fish sampling began >1 h after sunset when kōkopu are active. Sampling consisted of using a high‐powered spotlight to scan the reach for fish (Joy et al., 2013), a method that has been used effectively for sampling kōkopu within wadeable streams at night (David et al., 2002). Alternative fish sampling methods such as electrofishing and trapping are less effective for surveying kōkopu because these species sink when stunned, occupy deep bank cover during the day, and may not encounter traps due to having high fidelity to pools at night (Hickey & Closs, 2006; West et al., 2005). The one‐hour delay after sunset ensured that resident kōkopu had left their daytime refuges and moved into nocturnal foraging areas where they could be seen and captured. When spotlighted, kōkopu generally remained stationary (Video S1) and were able to be caught using hand nets. On the rare occasions when kōkopu were seen but not caught, their length and species identity were estimated. Reaches were sampled using successive depletion passes until fish were no longer observed. Each 50‐m reach required up to five passes and took around 1.5 h to complete.

Captured kōkopu were anesthetized in 20 mg/L of AQUI‐S water‐dispersible liquid anesthetic to facilitate handling. Fish were identified to species level, measured (total length ± 1 mm), and weighed (± 0.01 g). In the first year of sampling, all captured kōkopu with a total length >50 mm were given a unique visual implant elastomer (VIE) tag to monitor individual growth rates and apparent survival. VIE tagging consisted of injecting a small strip (3–5 mm) of visual elastomer directly under the skin, which remained clearly visible for several years. Importantly, VIE tagging does not alter growth, relative condition, or survival in galaxiid fishes (O'Brien & Dunn, 2018). After measurement, fish were returned to their area of capture. All sampling procedures were approved by the University of Canterbury Animal Ethics Committee (Permit Number 2020/06R).

Habitat survey

To isolate the effect of whitebait fishing, we controlled for local habitat characteristics that were differentially associated with the local biomass of kōkopu, using the methods in Crichton et al. (2023). Habitat surveys involved measuring pool volume, in‐stream bank cover, percentage forest cover, and average substrate size within each reach. Habitat surveys were completed every 8 months or after reach morphology was altered significantly by flood disturbances. This ensured that temporal habitat‐biomass relationships included in analyses were accurate. Temperature loggers (Hobo logger, Onset Instruments Inc) were placed in each site to record water temperature every hour.

Data analysis

A size class breakpoint of 90 mm (total length) was used to be consistent with the methods set in Crichton et al. (2023) and to investigate how small and large kōkopu size classes were independently influenced by post‐larvae fishing and DD processes. This breakpoint was selected because at this size, kōkopu begin to compete for territory and are approximately 1 year old (Hopkins, 1979; Jellyman, 1979). Small kōkopu size classes, therefore, should represent the most recent year‐class to be targeted by fishers as post‐larvae.

Total biomass is used as a proxy for fish density within each small and large size class because it accounts for differences in individual size and competitive ability (Hansen & Closs, 2009). This approach also provides a comprehensive size class‐specific indication of the total resources used, ultimately leading to a better understanding of the overall contribution of each size class in controlling population dynamics.

To account for differences in abiotic habitat characteristics between stream reaches that were differentially associated with the local biomass of small and large kōkopu of each species, relevant species‐ and size‐specific linear mixed‐effects “habitat‐biomass” models were extracted from (Crichton et al. 2023; summarized in Table 1). In summary, these habitat‐biomass models were developed by sampling fifty‐seven 50‐m reaches across 19 open streams in May and June of 2021, and parameters included in models were identified using Akaike's information criterion (see Crichton et al., 2023 for further information). Habitat‐biomass models incorporate the availability of influential habitat characteristics within each 50‐m stream reach and predict the biomass of a respective kōkopu class based on those local conditions. By subtracting the predicted biomass from the observed biomass of the associated kōkopu size class for each 50‐m reach, a measurement of biomass deviation (g/50 m) was acquired. When habitat‐biomass models were applied to closed and open reaches within the same month, the variation in biomass deviation between reaches of different stream types should be predominantly due to fishing effects. This is because these models were developed using open sites and other variables such as local habitat differences, downstream barriers, and introduced fish were accounted for. Similarly, monthly variation in kōkopu biomass can be detected when habitat‐biomass models are applied to reaches throughout the year and the within‐group trends in biomass deviation are compared. Biomass deviation estimates provide a more accurate standardization of kōkopu biomass than variables like stream area because they account for local habitat characteristics that are differentially associated with the biomass of each kōkopu size class. Although biomass deviance analyses do not incorporate the unexplained variation of the foundational habitat‐biomass models, a more conservative test of the effect of fishing is gained by accounting for habitat‐induced variation first because any overlap in explanatory power is attributed to habitat instead of fishing pressure.

A single habitat‐biomass model was used to predict the composite biomass deviation (CBD) of small kōkopu size classes because Crichton et al. (2023) were unable to make specific models for small giant kōkopu and shortjaw kōkopu due to their natural scarcity. However, it is likely that all small kōkopu size classes use similar habitats due to being competitively displaced by large congeners. A measure of large kōkopu CBD, calculated as the sum of the biomass deviation of each species' large size class within a reach, was also used to examine overall size‐specific trends. Additionally, this procedure accounted for the naturally rare (i.e., patchy) distribution of adult giant kōkopu and shortjaw kōkopu, which may impede the ability to discern temporal or fishing effects on respective species‐specific size class biomass.

A permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA), using Euclidean distance coefficient and 999 permutations, with fishing status (open or closed) and sampling month as fixed factors, and a random effect for site was used to test for temporal effects of fishing on the reach‐scale biomass deviation of large banded kōkopu, giant kōkopu, shortjaw kōkopu, composite large kōkopu, and composite small kōkopu size classes. A random effect for site was included so that each of the three 50‐m reaches nested within a site could be tested independently. By using the reach‐scale, more accurate and informative localized population characteristics could be obtained.

To examine how shifts in compensatory DD processes affected kōkopu mortality, we used the RMark interface (Laake, 2013) for Program Mark (White & Burnham, 1999) to do Cormack‐Jolly‐Seber (CJS) analyses on recapture histories of all tagged individuals and estimate per capita rates of apparent survival (φ). We refer to φ as apparent survival because the disappearance of tagged fish could represent either mortality or emigration. To test the effect of intra‐ and inter‐size class DD feedback on kōkopu mortality, we calculated the densities of small and large congeners experienced by each individual fish. This density was the average CBD of small (or large) kōkopu within the same 50‐m reach between the fish's first and last capture.

The apparent survival probability for each species' small and large size class was estimated using a single candidate model that included mean small kōkopu CBD, mean large kōkopu CBD, and fish length at first capture as fixed effects, and a constant recapture probability parameter in RMark. A constant recapture probability parameter was included because we had no a priori reason to suspect individual recapture ability varied with time or space. By extracting the mean slope estimate and 95% CIs of each fixed effect, we could obtain the independent effect of small or large kōkopu biomass on the survival of individuals of each kōkopu size class while accounting for variation caused by densities of the opposing size class and fish body size. Initially, we explored the need to control for site as a fixed effect because RMark does not allow for specification of random effects, but it was removed due to low sample counts when grouped (Table 2). The goodness‐of‐fit for each RMark model was evaluated using the Fletcher ĉ method (Fletcher, 2011), whereby a ĉ > 1 indicates potential overdispersion and ĉ ≤ 3 being an acceptable maximum limit (Lebreton et al., 1992). Because all candidate models had ĉ < 1.2, no models were adjusted for overdispersion.

In addition to survival estimates, we tested how intra‐ and inter‐class densities affected the specific growth rates of small and large kōkopu while accounting for stream temperature, which is known to influence fish growth (Watson et al., 2022). Specific growth rates (SGR), given in percentage increase per day, were calculated using: (1) SGR%day−1i=logTLir−logTLic∆daysi×100, where “TL” is the total length at the time of initial or previous capture (c) and recapture (r), and “Δdays” is the number of days between readings for each individual fish (i) (Lugert et al., 2016). Total length was used because weight was confounded by spawning condition. For each fish, we calculated the mean daily stream temperature and mean CBD of small and large kōkopu across the same 50‐m reach between c and r. Prior to SGR analyses, data were checked using quantile‐quantile plots to identify potential errors. The SGR of individuals that were not recaptured within three sampling events (6 months) were not included because they gave inaccurate indications of growth in response to model predictors.

Partial dependence plots, which show the independent effect of a single variable on the response by accounting for the average effects of all other variables in a model, were used to show the independent effect of mean CBD of small and large kōkopu on the specific growth rate of individuals within each size class. Using the “effects” package (Fox, 2003), partial dependence plots were developed for each kōkopu species' small and large size class by extracting the independent effects of each variable within a linear mixed‐effects model that included mean small kōkopu CBD, mean large kōkopu CBD, total fish length upon prior capture, mean stream temperature, and a random factor for unique fish ID. Total fish length was included to account for difference in growth rates associated with body size, whereas mean stream temperature accounted for the influence of stream temperature, and fish ID accounted for multiple growth rates being taken from the same individual over time. A random variable for site was initially included to account for any site‐related variation, but this could not be included due to model complexity (i.e., insufficient replication across all fixed effects when site was included as a random effect). However, the variables already included within the model, particularly stream temperature and fish ID, should account for variation in SGR between sites. Linear mixed‐effects models were constructed using the “lmer” function (Package “lme4”; Bates et al., 2015) in R version 4.3.0 (R Development Core Team, 2023).

RESULTS

Estimates of biomass deviation

After accounting for environmental variation, there were no significant differences in the biomass of large banded kōkopu (Pseudo‐F 1,6 = 0.06, p = 0.795), giant kōkopu (Pseudo‐F 1,6 = 2.47, p = 0.173), shortjaw kōkopu (Pseudo‐F 1,6 = 0.37, p = 0.575), or composite large kōkopu biomass (Pseudo‐F 1,6 = 1.83, p = 0.227) between closed and open reaches (Figure 1). There was significant monthly variation in large banded kōkopu biomass (Pseudo‐F 11,66 = 8.23, p = 0.001; Figure 1a) and composite large kōkopu biomass (Pseudo‐F 11,66 = 3.58, p = 0.001; Figure 1d), with biomass peaking in Austral summer when individual fish were increasing in condition for spawning. No temporal trends were detected in the biomass of large giant kōkopu (Pseudo‐F 11,66 = 0.49, p = 0.506; Figure 1b) or shortjaw kōkopu (Pseudo‐F 11,66 = 0.15, p = 0.999; Figure 1c). In contrast to larger congeners, there was greater composite small kōkopu biomass in closed areas, particularly during summer months when new recruits were immigrating into adult habitats, as indicated by a significant interaction between fishing status and month (Pseudo‐F 11,66 = 3.33, p = 0.001; Figure 1e). Therefore, the absence of fishing resulted in a greater biomass of small kōkopu, although this did not equate to significant differences in large kōkopu biomass at any time.

Survival

Fishing‐induced shifts in small kōkopu biomass had no effect on the survival of small or large banded kōkopu (Figure 2a,b), giant kōkopu (Figure 2e,f), or shortjaw kōkopu (Figure 2i,j), as indicated by CIs overlapping zero (Table 3). Conversely, large kōkopu biomass had a more regulatory role, being negatively associated with the survival of large banded kōkopu (Figure 2d) and small giant kōkopu (Figure 2g). No association was observed between large kōkopu biomass and the survival of small banded kōkopu (Figure 2c), large giant kōkopu (Figure 2h), small shortjaw kōkopu (Figure 2k), or large shortjaw kōkopu (Figure 2l). However, it is important to note that shortjaw kōkopu were found only in reaches with a low biomass of large kōkopu, suggesting that they may be competitively excluded at higher densities. Therefore, small kōkopu density had no detectable effect on kōkopu survival, whereas large kōkopu density was likely a key factor in regulating the survival and presence of various kōkopu size classes.

Growth

Although small kōkopu biomass had no detectable effect on individual survival, greater small kōkopu biomass had various positive effects on individual growth rates among kōkopu size classes and species (Figure 3). Specifically, greater small kōkopu biomass, characteristic of closed areas, was associated with increased growth rates of small giant kōkopu (F 1,61.42 = 4.33, p = 0.042; Figure 3e), large giant kōkopu (F 1,107.58 = 5.44, p = 0.022; Figure 3f), and small shortjaw kōkopu (F 1,62 = 17.35, p < 0.001; Figure 3i). No association was detected between small kōkopu biomass and the growth of small banded kōkopu (F 1,549 = 2.65, p = 0.104; Figure 3a), large banded kōkopu (F 1,426.45 = 0.01, p = 0.944; Figure 3b), or large shortjaw kōkopu (F 1,85.23 = 0.15, p = 0.701; Figure 3j). Conversely, greater large kōkopu biomass was negatively associated with the growth of large banded kōkopu (F 1,669.91 = 18.00, p < 0.001; Figure 3d). No association was detected between large kōkopu biomass and the growth of small banded kōkopu (F 1,549 = 2.54, p = 0.111; Figure 3c), small giant kōkopu (F 1,76.71 = 0.40, p = 0.531; Figure 3g), large giant kōkopu (F 1,126.25 = 0.15, p = 0.700; Figure 3h), small shortjaw kōkopu (F 1,62 = 3.46, p = 0.068; Figure 3k), or large shortjaw kōkopu (F 1,87.53 = 0.15, p = 0.701; Figure 3l). Therefore, increased juvenile availability can be an important DD mechanism for facilitating the growth of select individuals, whereas greater large fish densities played an exclusively regulatory role on kōkopu demographics overall.

DISCUSSION

This study presents evidence of size‐specific DD growth and survival among distinct life stages of diadromous species and provides important insights into the drivers of population dynamics in perennial freshwater fish with open populations. After accounting for environmental variation, we found that areas closed to whitebait fishing had substantially greater biomasses of small kōkopu, particularly following the fishing season. This indicated that, as hypothesized, fishing does reduce the number of juveniles reaching and settling in adult habitats. Despite the greater influx of recruits into closed areas, there was no difference in the biomass of large kōkopu at the species level, or as a combined assemblage between open and closed rivers, suggesting that fishing did not cause recruitment‐limitation in adult kōkopu populations. Instead, kōkopu populations were likely near their carrying capacity, with local population sizes being regulated by intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD effects. Greater large fish densities were influential in regulating the survival, growth, and/or presence of various kōkopu classes, whereas greater small fish densities, characteristic of closed areas, had exclusively positive effects on kōkopu growth. Overall, these results emphasize the significant role of post‐recruitment DD processes in underpinning the population dynamics of long‐lived migratory freshwater fishes, even in populations with inhibited recruit and juvenile availability.

Adult longevity, or residence time within adult habitats, is a fundamental life history component that often determines the extent to which recruitment and ontogenetic habitat requirements drive DD processes and control population dynamics (Halpern et al., 2005). This longevity and slow turnover of resident adults likely explains why adult kōkopu populations are maintained near their carrying capacity despite differences in recruit availability due to fishing. Life stage structured population models predict that populations of long‐lived taxa are limited by adult habitat, either due to habitat quality or resource availability, while recruitment is only limiting when adults are short‐lived and/or when recruitment is low (Halpern et al., 2005). This is because greater longevity results in a higher accumulation of recruitment pulses during the typical adult lifespan, thereby minimizing the effect of perturbations in recruitment availability on adult densities (Warner & Hughes, 1988).

While adult populations of long‐lived fish generally have relatively low recruit dependencies, shifts in population size structuring can intricately influence DD processes that shape population demographics. This is particularly true for species like kōkopu, where younger life stages may also serve as prey. Consequently, positive DD outcomes via cannibalism and intraguild predation may offset the negative effects of competition for shared resources (Hasegawa & Fukui, 2022; Kestemont et al., 2003). Giant kōkopu are generalist feeders and are known to target smaller congeners (Bonnett & Lambert, 2002), which likely explains their enhanced growth rates in reaches with greater availability of small fish. Similar cannibalism‐driven growth rate enhancement has also been documented in other freshwater species, including pikeperch (Cortay et al., 2019) and smallmouth bass (Carlander, 1977). While enhanced juvenile shortjaw kōkopu growth would not be a result of piscivory because they are largely insectivorous (McDowall et al., 1996), greater densities of small fish may alleviate individual predation risk through predator satiation or distraction, permitting increased foraging. Therefore, juvenile densities likely have multifaceted effects on local kōkopu populations by either indirectly altering foraging behaviors or by directly acting as prey for opportunistic congeners.

In contrast to the positive DD outcomes driven by increased recruitment, the regulatory role of greater adult kōkopu densities on the growth of individuals and overall population size is likely attributed to exploitative and interference competition. Such negative DD regulation is often underpinned by increased metabolic demands associated with vigilance and defense of territories and/or exploitative competition for food that restricts the availability of resources and positions within adult habitats (Ayllón et al., 2012; Matte et al., 2021; Nordwall et al., 2001). Given that this regulation was largely underpinned by the demand for shared resources within adult habitats, it becomes imperative that restoration efforts aimed at improving population productivity account for size‐related social inertia (Grant et al., 2017). For example, in territorial riverine drift‐feeding fish, including kōkopu, grayling (Thymallus spp.), charr (Salvelinus spp.), and trout (Salmo spp.), typically only the most dominant individuals are able to benefit from increased food supply (Cullen & Grossman, 2019; Grossman & Simon, 2019; Hansen & Closs, 2009). Specifically, it is estimated that a 2‐fold increase in food supply would be required to increase salmonid densities by 15%, and a 20‐fold increase to double local fish densities (Grant et al., 2017). However, simple habitat features like woody debris and large boulders weaken DD interactions and increase local salmonid densities by providing visual isolation and shelter while lessening the interference and prey monopolization of dominant fish (Matte et al., 2020; Rosenfeld, 2014; Whiteway et al., 2010).

The creation of vacancies within the adult population, either through habitat‐based restoration initiatives or disturbance, will also consequently increase the demand for otherwise non‐limite life stages (Rosenfeld & Hatfield, 2006). Therefore, even in long‐lived species, long‐term population viability can be jeopardized by perturbations to otherwise non‐limiting life stages (Lobón‐Cerviá, 2009a). For example, through thermal and hydrological perturbations in spawning and nursery habitats, long‐lived migratory shovelnose sturgeon (Scaphirhynchus platorynchus) are predicted to lose spawning ground availability (by 51%), leading to increased DD in remaining habitats, declines in recruit availability (by 46%–95%), and reduced population size (by 21%–74%; Goto et al., 2018). Therefore, a holistic management approach encompassing the requirements of all life stages, regardless of their presumed contemporary availability, will provide populations with the resilience required to respond securely to environmental change and perturbations.

It is important to note that kōkopu population dynamics will likely vary depending on the extent of catchment modification or with regionally variable recruit influxes (Yungnickel et al., 2020). However, with our closed sites located in reserves >20 years old and all sites being in minimally impacted catchments, this study provides a unique “healthy” reference system that can be used to evaluate kōkopu population states and management outcomes more widely. Ideally, more than three closed areas would have been included in this study, but no additional areas were located that were accessible or not impaired by migratory barriers or habitat degradation. Nevertheless, the long‐term, frequent, and thorough sampling of our sites enabled a robust representation of temporal trends in local kōkopu population dynamics, thus providing a globally applicable case of DD control within open populations of long‐lived freshwater fish. More areas closed to fishing across catchment modification gradients and regions will be integral to determine when recruitment‐limitation, driven by fishing or other causes, becomes problematic.

In conclusion, we provided strong empirical evidence that intra‐ and inter‐life stage DD processes are primary forces determining population size and growth in long‐lived kōkopu populations, even in catchments with fishing of post‐larvae. The prominence of DD relationships and their consequent, compensatory responses, indicate that these populations will have high per capita productivity when below carrying capacity, enabling populations to withstand fishing pressure and recover following disturbances (Rose et al., 2001). However, identifying and maintaining the environmental requirements of all life stages will be essential to safeguard long‐term population viability. In this respect, the implementation of species‐wide management approaches is imperative for the development of sustainable practices and targeted conservation strategies that work toward addressing the critical requirements of limiting life stages (Rose et al., 2001; Vincenzi et al., 2011).
