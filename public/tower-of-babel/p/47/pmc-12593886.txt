From dry eye to depression: a machine learning-based framework for predicting adolescent mental health
Authors: Le Han, Ying Liu, Peng Xian, Xiao Liu, Kai Cao, Li Ren, Yue Chang, Zhangfang Ma, Lei Tian, Shijing Deng, Xuejiao Liu, Yunshuang Liu, Ying Jie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdolescent depression is a major public health concern. Physical health indicators are rarely included in risk tools. We examined whether adding dry eye disease (DED) to psychosocial and behavioral factors improves prediction of depressive symptoms.MethodsWe analyzed 2,076 adolescent questionnaires (94.5% response) covering ocular health, sleep, electronic device use, social support, and demographics. Five machine-learning classifiers were trained with cross-validation and evaluated for discrimination and calibration.ResultsModels that included DED achieved strong discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.84) and good calibration, with highest accuracy for no and severe depression and lower performance for mild/moderate categories.ConclusionsIntegrating ocular health with psychosocial factors enhances machine-learning prediction of adolescent depression and may support earlier, school-based identification and referral. Given the low-cost, questionnaire-based inputs and favorable calibration, this approach shows promise for population screening and targeted prevention, pending external validation and prospective testing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03246-7.

Introduction

Adolescent depression is a pressing global public health challenge, with an estimated prevalence of 10–20% worldwide, leading to profound impacts on academic achievement, social functioning, and long-term mental health outcomes [1, 2]. Depression during adolescence is particularly concerning given its association with increased risks of suicide, substance abuse, and recurrent depressive disorders into adulthood [3, 4]. Its etiology is multifactorial, involving genetic, neurobiological, psychosocial, and environmental determinants [5]. Accordingly, comprehensive predictive models that integrate both physical and behavioral health indicators are essential for early identification and intervention.

Recent studies have highlighted the bidirectional links between physical health and mental disorders in youth [6–8]. One emerging area of interest is ocular surface disorders, particularly dry eye disease (DED), which causes discomfort, visual disturbances, and tear film instability with inflammatory components [9]. The chronic burden of DED can impair quality of life and increase vulnerability to psychological distress [10]. While visual impairment has long been associated with depression [11], the role of DED—especially among adolescents—remains underexplored, despite evidence that prolonged screen use and social media exposure exacerbate both ocular symptoms and mental health risks [12–14]. Sleep disturbances are another critical pathway, with consistent evidence linking poor sleep to both DED and depression [15–17]. For instance, recent meta-analyses and cohort studies highlight the bidirectional relationship between sleep disruption and depressive symptoms in adolescents [18, 19]. Moreover, psychosocial factors such as social support, academic pressure, and family environment strongly influence both ocular and emotional health, underscoring the value of integrating psychological theories into predictive frameworks [20, 21].

Machine learning (ML) offers a promising approach to capture nonlinear, high-dimensional relationships between demographic, clinical, and behavioral predictors of adolescent depression [22, 23]. Applications of ML have already shown potential advantages over traditional models in mental health screening [24, 25]. However, methodological concerns—including overfitting, inadequate external validation, and lack of calibration reporting—remain major barriers to clinical translation [26, 27]. To address these issues, best-practice guidelines emphasize the use of nested cross-validation, transparent reporting, and robust evaluation metrics in biomedical ML studies [28, 29].

From a translational perspective, digital health interventions are increasingly viewed as scalable avenues to promote adolescent mental well-being [30], while behavioral exposures such as social media use continue to shape youth mental health trajectories [31]. In this context, the present study develops and compares ML models for predicting depressive symptoms in adolescents, integrating ocular (DED), behavioral, sleep, demographic, and psychosocial features. This study contributes to the literature by exploring dry eye disease (DED) as a potential predictor of adolescent depression and by implementing nested cross-validation with representative algorithms to obtain more reliable performance estimates. Moreover, the analysis is informed by psychological theory and public health practice, providing preliminary evidence for an integrative perspective on adolescent mental health.

Research methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association between dry eye disease (DED) and depressive symptoms in adolescents and to develop predictive models for classifying depression severity using machine learning (ML) techniques. Participants were middle school students aged 12–18 years, recruited using cluster sampling from 20 randomly selected middle schools in Beijing, China. This sampling approach ensured demographic representativeness across age, gender, and geographic region, enhancing the generalizability of the findings.

Prior to data collection, informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the institutional review board of Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, Beijing, China (Ethics Approval Number: TRECKY2021-204) and all procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were collected anonymously through a standardized electronic questionnaire administered between March and June 2023.

Data preprocessing

A total of 2,197 questionnaires were distributed (see Table 1). After excluding responses with missing information in key variables or implausible values, 2,076 valid questionnaires remained for analysis (valid response rate: 94.5%). Missing and implausible responses were removed prior to analysis, and no imputation algorithms were applied, ensuring that only complete and valid cases were included. We chose not to impute because doing so would require unverifiable missing-at-random assumptions for several psychosocial and behavioral predictors, and imputation outside the resampling loop can inflate apparent performance via information leakage. To gauge whether complete-case exclusion altered the composition of the analytic sample relative to the original survey frame, we compared overall distributions of sex and location between the initial dataset (n = 2,197) and the retained complete-case dataset (n = 2,076) using percentage-point differences. The complete-case sample showed minimal shifts: female 52.39% → 51.83% (Δ = −0.56 pp); location: loc = 1 53.07% → 52.41% (Δ = −0.66 pp), loc = 2 11.52% → 11.18% (Δ = −0.34 pp), loc = 3 35.86% → 36.37% (Δ = +0.51 pp). Continuous variables were standardized (z-score normalization) within training folds of nested cross-validation to avoid data leakage. To address class imbalance across depression-severity categories, SMOTE was applied only within training folds. Finally, data were split using stratified sampling (80% training, 20% testing), with all preprocessing performed inside the training pipeline of nested cross-validation.

Feature selection

To ensure robust predictor inclusion, we first assessed multicollinearity by removing variables with pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients exceeding |ρ| >0.80, followed by exclusion of variables with variance inflation factor (VIF) > 5. For residual collinearity among sleep-related variables, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) to derive a composite sleep score. For embedded feature selection, we used LASSO (L1-regularized logistic regression) to shrink non-informative coefficients to zero. To improve interpretability and quantify global and local effects, we computed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values for the trained models.

Representative algorithms for nested cross-validation

For rigorous yet computationally feasible evaluation, we restricted the nested cross-validation analysis to five representative algorithms. These were chosen to capture the principal paradigms of machine learning while ensuring interpretability and efficiency: logistic regression with elastic net regularization (linear baseline), support vector classifier with RBF kernel (kernel-based learner), random forest (bagging-based ensemble), gradient boosting (boosting-based ensemble), and multilayer perceptron (MLP, neural network). This representative selection balanced methodological diversity and computational feasibility, given that all experiments were conducted in a Google Colab environment with limited computing resources. By spanning linear vs. non-linear, bagging vs. boosting, and kernel vs. neural approaches, these five models provided broad methodological coverage while allowing efficient implementation of nested cross-validation (5 outer folds × 5 inner folds). Such representative selection strategies are consistent with prior recommendations for methodological diversity in biomedical machine learning research [26–28].

Hyperparameter tuning and overfitting prevention

All models were trained and evaluated within a stratified 5 × 5 nested cross-validation framework. Hyperparameters were optimized in the inner loop using RandomizedSearchCV (50 iterations, optimizing macro-F1), with preprocessing steps (scaling, SMOTE, feature selection) performed strictly inside the inner loop to prevent data leakage. Model-specific regularization and constraints were applied to mitigate overfitting. Class imbalance was handled via class weighting in training folds, while outer test folds remained untouched. Performance was summarized from outer folds (macro-AUC, macro-F1, balanced accuracy, Brier score, ECE). Full hyperparameter ranges, optimal configurations, and pre- versus post-tuning results are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

Demographic characteristics analysis

This analysis included 2,076 adolescents with a balanced gender distribution (48.2% male; 51.8% female) and a younger age profile (63.9% aged 12–14; 36.1% aged 15–17). Parental education skewed higher (~ 63% university or above; see Table 1 for the full breakdown). Regarding ocular symptoms, about half had no DED symptoms, while ~ 17% had moderate–severe DED. Sleep quality was generally suboptimal (~ 82% poor or average). Daily electronic device use clustered at 1–3 h/day for most participants, although ~ 20% reported ≥ 4 h/day. Detailed counts and percentages for all categories are provided in Table 1 (sex, age groups, parental education sublevels, DED severity, sleep quality, and device-use strata).

Machine learning model performance

Exploratory comparisons across all models

Figure 4 summarizes performance across 15 machine-learning algorithms. MLP, elastic-net logistic regression, and ELM achieved the most balanced results (F1 ≈ 0.61–0.62), with CatBoost and AdaBoost also competitive; decision trees and bagging lagged (F1 < 0.55). Figure 5 shows the ROC curves. LightGBM, XGBoost, and CatBoost attained the highest AUCs (~ 0.94), followed by Random Forest and Gradient Boosting (~ 0.92). MLP also performed strongly, while logistic regression and naïve Bayes had moderate discrimination (AUC 0.82–0.86). ELM was close to chance on AUC.

Rigorous evaluation of representative models (5 × 5 nested cross-validation)

To ensure robust and unbiased estimation of generalization performance, we conducted a 5 × 5 nested cross-validation for five representative models (SVC-RBF, Gradient Boosting, MLP, Random Forest, and Elastic-net Logistic Regression). The averaged results across outer folds are reported in Table 2. Among these, SVC (RBF) achieved the highest discrimination (AUC = 0.842, 95% CI 0.825–0.859), closely followed by Gradient Boosting (AUC = 0.840, 95% CI 0.821–0.858). Macro-F1 values were similar across models (~ 0.50–0.52), with MLP performing best (F1 = 0.515, 95% CI 0.472–0.557). Balanced accuracy was highest for Logistic Regression with elastic net (BAcc = 0.535, 95% CI 0.496–0.574).

Representative model diagnostics

As the best-performing representative model, SVC (RBF) was further examined. Figure 6 shows its one-vs-rest ROC curves, yielding a macro-AUC of 0.842 [0.825–0.859] with class-specific AUCs ranging from 0.744 to 0.922. Figure 7 presents calibration analysis, with ECE = 0.038 [0.026–0.050] and Brier score = 0.462 [0.433–0.491], indicating good probability alignment. Gradient Boosting and Random Forest also displayed favorable calibration, while MLP and Logistic Regression exhibited higher miscalibration.

Per-class performance

Detailed classification outcomes across depression severity levels (No, Mild, Moderate, Severe) are reported in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Figures S1–S4). In summary, advanced models such as MLP, ELM, and ensemble methods (CatBoost, LightGBM, XGBoost) performed best in identifying No and Severe depression, whereas Mild and particularly Moderate depression were more challenging, with substantially lower F1 scores across models. Simpler classifiers, including decision tree and naïve Bayes, consistently underperformed in these categories.

Discussion

The present study developed and compared multiple machine learning (ML) models to predict depressive symptoms in adolescents by integrating ocular surface health, behavioral, demographic, and psychosocial variables. To our knowledge, this is among the first investigations to incorporate dry eye disease (DED) into predictive frameworks for adolescent depression, extending prior research that has focused primarily on psychosocial and lifestyle determinants [6, 8, 25, 34]. Our findings highlight that the inclusion of DED-related variables improved classification performance, supporting the hypothesis that ocular health may meaningfully contribute to adolescent mental well-being.

The observed association between DED and depression is consistent with clinical and neurobiological evidence linking chronic ocular discomfort with negative emotional states [27, 35]. Mechanistically, persistent irritation, visual impairment, and reduced daily functioning may exacerbate psychological vulnerability, while inflammatory pathways may underlie both ocular and psychiatric symptoms [29]. These results underscore the importance of adopting a biopsychosocial model of adolescent depression that explicitly considers interactions between physical health conditions and psychosocial stressors.

From a methodological perspective, our nested cross-validation framework provided a more rigorous and unbiased assessment of model performance than conventional k-fold CV, with representative models such as SVC (RBF) and Gradient Boosting achieving stable discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.84) and good calibration (ECE < 0.05). Nonetheless, per-class analyses revealed that while models performed well in detecting adolescents with no or severe depression, classification of mild and moderate depression remained challenging, yielding substantially lower F1 scores. This difficulty reflects the clinical reality that early or moderate symptoms are more heterogeneous and harder to differentiate. Advanced ML techniques, particularly neural networks and ensemble learners, showed advantages in these complex categories, consistent with prior evidence that such models can capture non-linear interactions [10, 23, 24, 36].

Comparison with recent literature suggests that our framework offers performance comparable to other state-of-the-art ML approaches in public health prediction, while remaining preliminary in scope. For example, studies in Computers in Biology and Medicine have demonstrated the value of boosting and deep learning for clinical risk prediction [23, 24], and recent reviews highlight the promise of ML in digital mental health interventions [37]. Similarly, work in Nature Human Behaviour emphasizes the role of social media use in adolescent mental health [25, 38]. As our sample was drawn from Beijing secondary schools, the findings should be interpreted in that educational and urban context. This setting—including intensive study schedules, widespread use of digital homework platforms, and seasonally dry winters—provides context—not causal evidence—for the observed co-variation among DED, sleep, device use, and depressive symptoms. External validation in non-Chinese settings, with measurement invariance checks and recalibration as needed, is required to establish generalizability. In this context, our study adds novelty by integrating ocular surface health into predictive modeling while aligning with broader interdisciplinary evidence on behavioral and environmental determinants.

These findings point toward several promising directions. Future work should integrate additional behavioral and digital health indicators—such as social media activity, lifestyle patterns, and circadian rhythm metrics—into predictive models to better capture the complexity of adolescent depression [39–43]. Validation in geographically and culturally diverse populations, ideally using longitudinal cohorts, will be essential to establish generalizability. Moreover, translating these models into clinical and public health practice will require integration with real-world health data platforms, evaluation of computational scalability, and benchmarking against other emerging ML-based mental health prediction tools.

Conclusion

This study shows that combining ocular surface health with behavioral and psychosocial variables enables effective machine learning–based prediction of adolescent depressive symptoms. By demonstrating stable discrimination and calibration across representative models, the work underscores the added value of incorporating ophthalmic indicators into mental health research. These insights provide a foundation for developing practical tools to support early risk detection and proactive adolescent mental health care.

Limitation

Several limitations should be noted. First, this study used a single regional sample and self-reported measures, which may introduce reporting bias and limit generalizability beyond Beijing secondary schools. Second, although we included ocular, demographic, and psychosocial factors, other potentially informative behaviors (e.g., detailed social media use, lifestyle patterns, circadian metrics) were unavailable. Third, while internal validity was strengthened by 5 × 5 nested cross-validation, external validation across other regions, school types, and time periods is still needed; prospective testing and possible recalibration would help assess transportability. Finally, real-world deployment will require attention to data protection for minors, interpretability for non-technical users, and basic fairness monitoring across subgroups, as well as practical considerations for scalability and integration into school workflows.
