Inferior STEMI in a Patient With Cardiac Metastasis
Authors: Scott Vella Sorensen, Colby Fischer, Roberto Herrera, Kerrilynn Hennessey, Natalia Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac metastases occur more commonly than primary cardiac tumors but are often clinically silent. ST-segment elevation mimicking acute myocardial infarction because of cardiac metastasis is rare.Case SummaryWe present the case of an 80-year-old woman with advanced pulmonary squamous cell carcinoma and intracardiac metastasis presenting with ST-segment elevations on electrocardiogram. Coronary angiography demonstrated nonobstructive coronary disease. Presentation was thought to be secondary to myocardial infiltration leading to myocyte necrosis. Management focused on symptom control, and the patient ultimately transitioned to hospice care and passed away.DiscussionCardiac metastases can cause ischemic electrocardiographic changes via myocardial invasion, coronary artery compression, or embolization. This case highlights a rare mimic of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.Take-Home MessagesCardiac metastases should be considered in cancer patients with findings of ST-segment elevation and nonobstructive coronary disease. Early recognition can guide prognosis, avoid futile interventions, and support timely goals of care discussions.Key words: acute coronary syndrome, coronary angiography, electrocardiogram, echocardiography, myocardial ischemia

Timeline of Presentation

Patient was diagnosed with squamous cell lung cancer 1 year before presentation and was found to have an intracardiac mass on imaging in the months leading to presentation. At presentation, she was found to have STEMI findings without signs of obstruction on coronary catheterization. Throughout her hospital course, she developed intermittent symptomatic atrial fibrillation and eventually elected for a comfort-focused approach with symptom improvement. She passed away peacefully 12 days after presentation. History of Presentation

An 80-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset dyspnea at rest, lightheadedness, and weakness. She denied chest pain, coughing, wheezing, or loss of consciousness. On arrival, initial vitals noted a temperature of 36.4 °C, heart rate of 63 beats/min, blood pressure of 97/48 mm Hg, and 93% oxygen saturation on her baseline home 3 L of oxygen. Physical examination noted diminished right breath sounds.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a peripheral leukocytosis (white blood cells: 18.28 × 103/μL) and anemia (hemoglobin: 8.8 g/dL). Electrolytes (including sodium, potassium, magnesium, and calcium) were all within normal limits. Cardiac biomarkers were notable for a pro–B-type natriuretic peptide level of 21,493 pg/mL and high-sensitivity troponin level of 80 ng/L that trended to 79 ng/L at 3 hours. Electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed sinus bradycardia with ST-segment elevations in the inferior leads with reciprocal depressions in the lateral leads, raising concern for acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (Figure 1). Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a large echodensity attached to the inferior septum.

Admission Electrocardiogram

Electrocardiogram on admission showing sinus bradycardia; 2-mm ST-segment elevations in leads II, III, aVF, V3, and V4; and 1-mm ST-segment elevations in V5 and V6 with ST-segment depressions in aVL.

Past Medical History

The patient's past medical history was significant for sarcoidosis, right-sided breast cancer diagnosed 30 years ago treated with mastectomy and chemotherapy, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hypothyroidism, mild diffuse coronary artery disease noted on coronary catheterization 9 years prior, essential thrombocythemia, and a 60 pack-year smoking history.

One year before presentation, she had been diagnosed with poorly differentiated squamous cell lung cancer. She underwent lobectomy and systemic chemotherapy (carboplatin and docetaxel) and was maintained on immunotherapy, with pembrolizumab started 3 months before presentation. Additionally, an indwelling right-sided pleural drain was placed for persistent pleural effusion.

Four months before presentation, she had been hospitalized for acute hypoxic respiratory failure secondary to COVID-19 pneumonia and subsegmental pulmonary emboli and was started on apixaban. During that admission, TTE revealed a mobile echodensity (Figure 2, Video 1), not seen on the TTE 3 months prior. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging 2 months later showed a large intracardiac mass within the left ventricle along the inferoseptum (Figure 3, Video 2), and a full-body positron emission tomography scan showed an enlarging left ventricular mass (Figure 4). There was no evidence of myopericarditis at this time.

Differential Diagnosis

Considerations included atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion with superimposed thrombosis versus myocardial infiltration and necrosis from intracardiac mass versus transient thromboembolism from cancer-associated hypercoagulability versus immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis.

Investigations

After aspirin loading and heparin infusion, the patient underwent emergent coronary angiography, which revealed a codominant system with nonobstructive coronary artery disease, unchanged from 9 years earlier. There was TIMI flow grade III in the right coronary artery and no obvious etiology for the inferior ST-segment elevations (Figure 5, Video 3). End-diastolic pressure was not obtained given known left ventricular mass.

Coronary Angiography Findings

Imaging of RCA during coronary angiography showing mild diffuse (≤25% stenosis) disease of the entire segment. (A) Left anterior oblique view, (B) right anterior oblique view. RCA = right coronary artery.

TTE redemonstrated a large mass measuring 4.4 × 2.2 cm embedded within the basal to mid left ventricular inferoseptum and inferior wall, protruding partially into the left ventricular cavity (Figure 6, Video 4). Left ventricular size and systolic function were preserved with an ejection fraction of 66% with no regional wall motion abnormalities. The left atrium was severely dilated with mild to moderate mitral regurgitation. There was no evidence of pericardial effusion.

Echocardiogram Findings

Echocardiogram apical 4-chamber view showing a large echodense structure measuring 4.4 × 2.2 cm affixed to the inferior septal wall from the base to the midsegments, protruding into the left ventricular cavity. The red arrow denotes the location of the structure.

Computed tomography (CT) of the head to further evaluate lightheadedness and lethargy showed tiny lytic foci scattered throughout the calvarium, consistent with metastatic disease.

Management

On hospital day 2, the patient developed symptomatic atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, with heart rate in the 140s beats/min (Figure 7). She received metoprolol and self-converted to sinus rhythm. Amiodarone was started for rhythm maintenance.

Serial electrocardiograms taken throughout the hospital course. (A) Hospital day 2 showing atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (ventricular rate: 144 beats/min) with premature ventricular or aberrantly conducted complexes and persistent ST-segment elevations in the inferior lateral leads. (B) Hospital day 6 with increased ST-segment elevations in the inferior lateral leads in V3 and V4 with ongoing changes in V5 and V6. (C) Hospital day 8 showing atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response and a ventricular rate of 133 beats/min.

Oncology recommended restaging CT scan of chest, abdomen, and pelvis, which compared to 1 month prior, showed new and enlarging metastases involving the lungs/pleura, myocardium, lymph nodes, chest wall and bones, liver, kidneys, pancreas, bowel, and peritoneum. Cardiac evaluation revealed multiple enlarging metastases throughout the left ventricle (Figure 8).

Full-Body Computed Tomography Scan Findings

Hospital day 8 CT scan showing extent of cardiac metastases, denoted by red arrows. (A) Enlarging metastasis centered on the intraventricular septum measuring 5 × 6 × 7 cm, previously up to 5 cm. (B) Second metastasis along left ventricular free wall measuring 1.5 cm. (C) An additional metastasis on the left ventricular apex adjacent to the mitral annulus and left ventricular outflow track measuring 2.6 cm. CT = computed tomography.

On hospital day 5, after discussion with the palliative care team, the patient chose “do not resuscitate/do not intubate” status without further escalation of care, and anticoagulation was held preemptively for pleural drain removal.

On hospital day 6, the pleural drain was removed. The patient again developed chest pain, and troponins remained elevated in the 100s serially. Repeat ECG showed new/increased ST-segment elevations in V3 and V4 with ongoing changes in V5 and V6 (Figure 7). The interventional cardiology team did not recommend repeat coronary catheterization. Although findings were initially thought to be due to coronary artery compression, further imaging review showed that the mass locations were unlikely to cause compression in that distribution. Further management was focused on pain control and comfort, with significant symptomatic improvement with opioids. Anticoagulation was resumed.

On hospital day 8, the patient again went into atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response (Figure 7). She continued to have alternating episodes of atrial fibrillation and sinus bradycardia with persistent ST-segment elevations throughout her hospital course. No ventricular arrhythmias were observed while under cardiac monitoring.

Outcome

On hospital day 12, after multidisciplinary team discussions about the advanced nature of her disease and limited therapeutic options, she elected for comfort measures only. She was transferred to inpatient hospice and passed away several days later. An autopsy was not performed.

Discussion

Cardiac metastases are more common than primary cardiac tumors, with an incidence ranging from 2.3% to 18.3% in autopsy studies.1 Most cardiac metastases are clinically silent or present with nonspecific findings such as arrhythmias.2 Cardiac involvement should be suspected in cancer patients with new cardiovascular symptoms or signs. This case highlights an unusual, but life-threatening cause of ST-segment elevation with normal coronary angiography, broadening the differential in patients with advanced cancer and informing management, prognosis, and goals of care.

The most common cancers to metastasize to the heart are pleural mesothelioma, melanoma, lung adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated carcinomas, and lung squamous cell carcinomas.1 These can spread to the heart via direct extension, through the bloodstream or lymphatic system, and via intracavitary diffusion through the inferior vena cava or the pulmonary veins.3 Once metastasized to the heart, myocardial infarction can result from direct myocardial invasion causing necrosis, coronary artery compression, and tumor embolization to coronary arteries.3

Common signs and symptoms from cardiac metastases include tachycardia, shortness of breath, syncopal episodes, and chest pain.3 However, cancers that commonly metastasize to the heart, like thoracic cancers, often present with similar symptoms, making cardiac metastases difficult to diagnose. In a study evaluating ECG changes, new atrial arrhythmias and low-voltage ECGs were associated with cardiac metastases, whereas normal ECGs were unlikely to have cardiac involvement.4 An observational study of cardiac metastases with ST-segment elevations found a specific coronary artery territory in nearly all cases and a correlation between metastasis location and ST-segment elevation lead distribution.5 Inferior lead elevations were less common and typically from metastases to the left ventricle inferior, posterolateral wall, and apex, though with a limited sample size.5

The exact cause of the ST-segment elevations in our case is uncertain. Myocardial infiltration by the mass resulting in inflammation and myocyte necrosis with subsequent disruption of action potentials between healthy cells, remains the most likely explanation for the ST-segment elevations on ECG.5 However, other possible explanations include transient thromboembolic events involving coronary arteries and intramyocardial microvasculature from a malignancy-driven hypercoagulable state or from tumor particles. Another possibility, though less likely, includes immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis secondary to her pembrolizumab, which she had received 3 months before presentation. A systemic review of case reports showed pembrolizumab myocarditis presenting with ST-segment elevations in 21.7% of patients and typically presented 1 to 3 months after initiation.6

There is currently an absence of standardized guidelines regarding management of cardiac metastases.2 Treatment strategies and median survival time after diagnosis of cardiac metastases depends on the type of primary tumor and the extent of cardiac involvement, with median mortality of 6 months after diagnosis.7 A retrospective analysis found a median survival of 6.6 months in patients who did not undergo treatment compared with 27.33 months in patients who were treated.8 Surgery is generally reserved for patients who have a favorable prognosis and good overall functional status, and resection is favored when it is possible to remove the tumor completely or in cases of intracardiac obstruction.9 Although limited, studies of surgical intervention have shown worse outcomes when ventricular function has sustained irreversible damage, either before or after surgery.2 Radiation and chemotherapy can also be effective treatments, and palliative radiotherapy can be considered; however, technical challenges often limit the amount of effective radiation doses that can be delivered to the heart.2

Patients with known cardiac metastases benefit from a multidisciplinary care team. Knowledge about their presentations can guide diagnostic decision-making and goals-of-care discussions. Finally, new ECG changes in patients with known cancer should prompt discussion about metastases to the heart and consideration of imaging evaluation with either ultrasound, CT, or magnetic resonance imaging.

Conclusions

An 80-year-old woman with known poorly differentiated squamous cell lung cancer and known cardiac metastases presented with acute dyspnea and was found to have ST-segment elevations, elevated troponins, and nonobstructive coronary artery disease. Her presentation was not completely explained, but it was felt to be due to myocardial infiltration leading to necrosis from rapidly progressing metastatic burden, which was ultimately treated with a comfort-focused approach.
