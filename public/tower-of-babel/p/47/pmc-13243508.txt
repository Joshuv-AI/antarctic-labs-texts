Association of healthy lifestyle and dietary practices with prediabetes among young Korean adults: sex-specific findings from KNHANES 2020–2024
Authors: Youngmi Lee, Sooyoun Kwon, Heejung Park, Jean Kyung Paik, Hee-Sook Lim, Yoo Kyoung Park, Yun-Jung Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND/OBJECTIVESDespite the increasing prevalence of prediabetes among young Korean adults, evidence regarding modifiable lifestyle and dietary factors in this population remains limited. This study investigated the association of healthy lifestyle and dietary practices with prediabetes among Korean adults aged 19–34 yrs using sex-specific analyses.SUBJECTS/METHODSThis cross-sectional study examined data from 3,865 participants (1,805 men and 2,060 women) in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2020–2024. Prediabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level of 100–125 mg/dL or a glycated hemoglobin level of 5.7–6.4% without diagnosed diabetes. Healthy lifestyle practices included nonsmoking, moderating drinking, and walking. Healthy dietary practices comprised appropriate fat intake (15–30% of energy), sodium intake of ≤ 2,300 mg/day, fruit and vegetable intake of ≥ 500 g/day, and nutrition label use. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).RESULTSIn this study, 15.9% of participants had prediabetes. Participants with prediabetes had higher body mass index and lower engagement in muscle-strengthening exercise in both sexes. Among men, prediabetes was associated with more frequent eating out and lower nutrition label use, and among women, it was associated with higher dietary fat density and lower fiber intake density. In men, adherence to healthy dietary practices was significantly associated with lower odds of prediabetes after multivariable adjustment (adjusted OR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.54–0.96; P-value = 0.025). No significant association was detected between healthy lifestyle practices and prediabetes in either sex.CONCLUSIONHealthy dietary practices were inversely associated with prediabetes among young Korean men, whereas healthy lifestyle practice indicators showed no significant correlation. These findings suggest that early, sex-specific dietary strategies may be relevant for metabolic risk management among young adults.Keywords: Healthy lifestyle practices, healthy dietary practices, prediabetes, young adults

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus is a major chronic disease affecting more than 1 in 10 adults worldwide, and its associated health and economic burdens continue to intensify. It has been estimated that the global economic cost of diabetes would reach INT$ 78.8 trillion between 2020 and 2050 when informal caregiving is included [1]. The situation in Korea is similarly serious. As of 2024, ~3% of all deaths were attributable to diabetes and its complications, with a mortality rate of 21.7 per 100,000 population [2].

In recent years, the age at onset of type 2 diabetes in Korea has progressively decreased, accompanied by a sharp increase in the prevalence of prediabetes among young adults in their 20s and 30s. According to a report by the Korean Diabetes Association [3], approximately 2.2% of the young adult population (aged 19–39 yrs) in Korea are estimated to have diabetes, with a markedly higher prevalence in men than in women. By age group, the prevalence was 1.1% among those aged 19–29 yrs and 3.4% among those aged 30–39 yrs, indicating that individuals in their 30s had approximately 3 times higher prevalence than those in their 20s. Furthermore, almost 1 in 5 young adults—approximately 3 million individuals—are classified as having prediabetes. The prevalence is approximately 2-fold higher in those in their 30s than in those in their 20s and is also higher among men. Individuals with prediabetes have a significantly increased risk of progression to type 2 diabetes mellitus and of developing cardiovascular disease compared with those with normoglycemia [4]. Therefore, early detection of abnormal glycemic status and the implementation of lifestyle modifications during young adulthood are crucial for establishing long-term metabolic health across the life course.

Early adulthood is a critical developmental period during which lifelong health behaviors are established along with increasing social and economic independence. Nevertheless, young adults at this stage are frequently exposed to health risk factors—such as irregular eating patterns, insufficient physical activity, alcohol consumption, and smoking—largely due to academic demands, employment pressures, and social stress [5]. According to the 2024 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES), the breakfast skipping rate among individuals aged 19–29 yrs was 62.1%, the highest across all age groups. Furthermore, adherence to healthy lifestyle practices, including moderate alcohol consumption and regular physical activity, remained lower in this age group than in others [6]. The cumulative impact of these unhealthy behaviors constitutes a central determinant in the subsequent development of chronic diseases.

Adherence to healthy lifestyle practices is widely emphasized as a cornerstone in the prevention of metabolic diseases, including diabetes [7]. For instance, higher composite scores reflecting healthy behaviors—such as balanced dietary intake, regular physical activity, and weight management—are significantly associated with a reduced risk of developing diabetes [8]. In particular, appropriate nutritional intake and adherence to healthy eating practices are essential for attenuating glycemic variability and maintaining metabolic regulation. Studies have shown that improvements in dietary patterns and healthy eating behaviors significantly enhance insulin sensitivity [9], and an inverse association has been identified between the extent of healthy lifestyle adherence and the risk of metabolic syndrome [10]. Lifestyle modification has also been demonstrated to reduce the risk of developing diabetes by up to 43% [11]. Consistent with these data, a domestic study involving adults with prediabetes reported that interventions targeting dietary improvement, alcohol moderation, and physical activity were effective in increasing the probability of reverting to normoglycemia [12]. Nonetheless, most existing studies have primarily focused on middle-aged and older populations, resulting in limited evidence comprehensively investigating the relationship between health behaviors and glycemic control among young adults. Moreover, although individuals in their 20s and 30s represent a life stage during which foundational health behaviors are established, there is a scarcity of domestic research specifically exploring how healthy lifestyle and dietary practices in this age group are associated with glucose metabolism.

Recent socioenvironmental changes in Korea have also adversely affected the health of young adults. For instance, the rapid expansion of online food delivery services, increased overtime and shift work, and heightened social stress have contributed to irregular eating patterns and greater consumption of high-calorie and high-sodium foods, which may cause energy imbalance and metabolic abnormalities [13,14]. Moreover, after the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, the widespread adoption of remote work and non–face-to-face activities has been associated with an overall decrease in physical activity levels [15,16]. Hence, a comprehensive evaluation of healthy lifestyle practices among young adults and an analysis of their impact on prediabetes are of significant importance from a public health policy perspective.

Therefore, the present study was conducted to examine the prevalence of prediabetes and its association with healthy lifestyle practices and healthy dietary behaviors among Korean young adults aged 19–34 yrs using raw data from the KNHANES conducted between 2020 and 2024. The findings of this study may serve as foundational evidence for developing tailored strategies to prevent chronic diseases in young adults and for formulating national health promotion policies.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Study population

Raw data from the KNHANES collected over the most recent 5 yrs (2020–2024) were analyzed to investigate the association of prediabetes with healthy lifestyle practices and healthy dietary practices among young Korean adults. Among 30,531 participants who completed both the health examination and nutrition surveys during 2020–2024, 4310 individuals aged 19–34 yrs were initially selected. Among 4,310 individuals aged 19–34 yrs, the following were sequentially excluded: 62 pregnant or lactating women; 67 individuals with diagnosed diabetes; 175 individuals without complete data on health behavior indicators (nonsmoking, alcohol consumption, and walking); and 175 individuals with a fasting duration of < 8 h or missing data on fasting plasma glucose or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). As 34 individuals met more than one exclusion criterion, they were counted only once to avoid double exclusion, resulting in a final analytic sample of 3,865 participants (1,805 men and 2,060 women). The number of participants may vary slightly across analyses due to missing values in specific variables, as indicated in the respective table footnotes. Prediabetes was defined as not having diabetes and having either a fasting plasma glucose level of 100–125 mg/dL or an HbA1c level of 5.7–6.4% [17]. The normal group comprised individuals without diabetes or prediabetes, with a fasting plasma glucose level of < 100 mg/dL and an HbA1c level of < 5.7%. The KNHANES used in this study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before participation. This study was conducted following exemption from review by the Korea National University of Transportation Institutional Review Board (KNUT-2026-HR-13-05).

General measurements

Information regarding age and sex was obtained from the health interview survey. General characteristics, including household income, household size, marital status, educational level, physical activity, alcohol intake, and smoking status, were also derived from the health interview data. Household income was categorized as low, lower-middle, middle, or upper-middle. Living alone was determined based on household size. Marital status was classified as married/living with a partner or single. Educational level was categorized as middle school graduate or less, high school graduate, or college graduate or higher. Alcohol drinkers were defined as individuals who consumed at least one alcoholic drink per month during the past year. Physical activity was evaluated using the following 3 indicators: walking practice (walking ≥ 30 min per session on ≥ 5 days during the past week), muscle exercise (performed on ≥ 2 days during the past week), and aerobic physical activity (≥ 150 min of moderate-intensity activity, ≥ 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination per week, where 1 min of vigorous activity was considered equivalent to 2 min of moderate activity). Details regarding body mass index (BMI), fasting plasma glucose, and HbA1c were obtained from the health examination survey. BMI was classified as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), or obese (≥ 25.0 kg/m2) [18].

Dietary habits and intake

Dietary habit variables were obtained from the dietary behavior survey of the KNHANES and included breakfast frequency (weekly frequency over the past year), frequency of eating out (average frequency over the past year), and dietary supplement use (use for ≥ 2 weeks during the past year). Furthermore, individual nutrient intake and food group intake data were obtained from a single 24-h dietary recall conducted as part of the dietary intake survey [19]. Total daily energy and food intake were analyzed. To minimize the impact of total energy intake on nutrient intake when evaluating nutrient intake, nutrient densities per 1,000 kcal were calculated. The percentage of energy intake derived from carbohydrates, protein, and fat was also calculated.

Healthy lifestyle practices and healthy dietary practices

Healthy lifestyle behaviors—nonsmoking, no high-risk alcohol consumption, and walking—are included in the “Healthy Lifestyle Practice” domain of the National Health Plan 2030 [20]. In the Community Health Survey, a nationwide community-based health behavior survey, individuals who adhere to all 3 components are classified as practicing a healthy lifestyle [21]. Accordingly, healthy lifestyle practice in this study consisted of these 3 components, defined in accordance with the operational criteria of the Community Health Survey conducted by the Korean government [21]. Muscle-strengthening and aerobic exercise were not included, as these components are not part of the nationally standardized healthy lifestyle indicator. Nonsmoking was defined as never having smoked or having quit smoking. Moderate drinking was defined as abstinence or alcohol consumption no more than once per week, with fewer than 7 drinks per occasion for men and fewer than 5 drinks per occasion for women during the past year. Walking practice was defined as walking for at least 30 min/day on ≥ 5 days during the past week [6].

Healthy dietary practice is an indicator within the nutrition domain of the National Health Plan 2030 and consists of the following 4 components: 1) appropriate energy intake from fat (15–30% of total energy intake for adults aged ≥ 19 yrs) (fat indicator), 2) sodium intake of ≤ 2,300 mg/day (sodium indicator), 3) combined fruit and vegetable intake of ≥ 500 g/day (fruit and vegetable indicator), and 4) reading nutrition labels when selecting processed foods (nutrition label indicator). Each component was classified as achieved or not achieved, the definition of healthy dietary practice, including the 4 components and the threshold of meeting at least 2 criteria, was adopted directly from the operational definition established by the Korean government for national health monitoring in the KNHANES [6]. The fat, sodium, and fruit and vegetable indicators were evaluated using 24-h dietary recall data, and the nutrition label indicator was evaluated based on responses to the dietary behavior survey.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Complex sample analyses were performed to account for the sampling design of the KNHANES by incorporating sampling weights, stratification variables, and cluster variables. General characteristics, diabetes-related blood indicators, nutrient and food group intake variables, dietary habit variables, healthy lifestyle practices, and healthy dietary practices were compared between the normal and prediabetes groups. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the Rao–Scott χ2 test to determine group differences. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and SE and compared using survey-weighted regression models. Covariates were selected based on prior knowledge and established associations with prediabetes and dietary or lifestyle behaviors in the literature. All analyses were stratified by sex. Depending on the analysis, models were adjusted for combinations of the following covariates: age, BMI, household income, living alone, marital status, education level, alcohol consumption frequency, aerobic physical activity, muscle strengthening physical activity, and energy intake. Specific covariates included in each analysis are described in the respective table footnotes. The associations of healthy lifestyle practices and healthy dietary practices with prediabetes were examined using multivariable logistic regression analyses, with BMI additionally adjusted for in Model 3 to assess potential overadjustment. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Prediabetes was associated with marital status in both men and women, with a higher proportion of married individuals in the prediabetes group (P = 0.002 for men, P = 0.001 for women). The prevalence of alcohol consumption was lower among women with prediabetes (P = 0.001), whereas it was higher among men with prediabetes, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.095). As anticipated, fasting blood glucose and HbA1c levels were significantly higher in the prediabetes group in both men and women (P < 0.0001).

Dietary supplement use showed no difference according to prediabetes status in either sex.

Lifestyle and dietary practices and prediabetes

Table 5 summarizes the healthy lifestyle and dietary practice factors. Current smoking prevalence was significantly higher among individuals with prediabetes in both men and women (P = 0.007 and 0.022, respectively). However, there were no significant differences in moderate drinking or walking activity according to prediabetes status in either sex. DISCUSSION

This study investigated the association between prediabetes and healthy lifestyle as well as healthy dietary practices among young adults using data from the KNHANES collected over the past 5 yrs. Results showed that young adults with prediabetes performed significantly less engagement in muscle-strengthening exercise than individuals with normal glucose status, whereas obesity prevalence was significantly higher. Among men, those with prediabetes reported more frequent eating out than their normoglycemic counterparts. Among women, the prediabetes group showed a higher dietary fat density but lower dietary fiber and magnesium intake densities than the normal glucose group. Regarding healthy lifestyle indicators, the proportion of nonsmokers was significantly lower in the prediabetes group in both men and women; however, the overall healthy lifestyle practice score showed no significant association with prediabetes. In contrast, the association between healthy dietary practice and prediabetes differed according to sex. After adjusting for potential confounders, adherence to healthy dietary practices was associated with 28% lower odds of prediabetes among young men. Furthermore, among men, the prediabetes group showed a significantly lower rate of nutrition label use, an indicator of healthy dietary practice.

Young adulthood is a transitional life stage characterized by substantial changes in socioeconomic circumstances, academic demands, employment, and occupational stress, all of which may affect lifestyle and behavioral patterns [22]. These changes can result in health-risk behaviors—such as physical inactivity, increased alcohol and tobacco use, and unhealthy dietary habits—associated with metabolic dysfunction and impaired glycemic regulation [23,24,25]. Consequently, there have been increasing reports on metabolic abnormalities among young adults [26,27]. In Korea, the prevalence of diabetes among individuals aged 19–39 yrs was 2.2% in 2022, similar to that in 2012, indicating no reduction over the past decade [3]. Advancing age is associated with a progressive decline in pancreatic β-cell function [28], and age-related loss of skeletal muscle—the primary site of glucose disposal—may contribute to increased insulin resistance [29]. Considering that aging itself is accompanied by an increased risk of developing diabetes, the presence of diabetes-related risk factors during adolescence and young adulthood may result in long-term health complications and substantial economic burden over the life course [30,31]. In addition, even among young adults in their 20s and 30s, increasing age has been identified as a significant predictor of both prediabetes and diabetes [24]. In the present study as well, young adults aged 19–34 yrs with prediabetes were older than their normoglycemic counterparts, consistent with previous studies [32,33].

Obesity is a well-established risk factor for insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus, and the relationship between excess adiposity and impaired glucose metabolism is supported by multiple biological mechanisms [34,35]. For instance, adipose tissue in individuals with obesity produces proinflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6, which interfere with insulin signaling pathways and promote systemic insulin resistance [36]. Obesity also downregulates glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) expression in the adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, thereby impairing glucose uptake and contributing to increased plasma glucose levels [37]. In the present study, young adults with prediabetes showed significantly higher obesity prevalence and BMI than those with normoglycemia, consistent with previous studies that reported a strong association between obesity and prediabetes [38,39]. These findings emphasize the importance of promoting weight management and maintaining an appropriate body weight among young adults to reduce the risk of progression to diabetes.

Recommended lifestyle behaviors for health promotion include increased physical activity, moderation of alcohol consumption, smoking cessation, stress management, and adequate sleep [40,41,42,43]. Among these, regular physical activity improves glycemic control, reduces cardiovascular risk, and contributes to weight reduction [44]. Moreover, in individuals at high risk for diabetes, exercise prevents the onset of the disease [45]. Both aerobic and resistance exercises improve glycemic control in individuals with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes through multiple physiological mechanisms [46]. For instance, during exercise, glucose is used as a primary energy substrate, thereby improving glucose metabolism. Exercise reduces visceral adiposity and also increases lean body mass, improves insulin sensitivity, and upregulates GLUT4 expression in the skeletal muscle, thereby facilitating glucose uptake and ultimately improving glycemic regulation [47,48]. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials involving adults with prediabetes (mean age, 56.1 yrs) reported that aerobic exercise significantly improved fasting plasma glucose and 2-h postprandial glucose levels compared with those in control groups, although the magnitude of the improvement varied according to the duration of the intervention [49]. Furthermore, an intervention study among women with prediabetes (mean age, 60.8 yrs) that combined high-intensity interval training with walking reported that walking alone did not produce significant metabolic improvements [50]. In that study, achieving 10,000 steps/day was insufficient to elicit significant differences in peripheral insulin sensitivity or skeletal muscle mitochondrial oxidative capacity compared with that in the control group. Hence, these data suggest that low-intensity physical activity alone does not provide a sufficient stimulus to induce significant metabolic adaptations at the muscular level.

In the present study, physical activity levels during the previous week were evaluated among young adults aged 19–34 yrs, and the prevalence of walking, muscle-strengthening exercise, and aerobic activity was examined. The rate of involvement in muscle-strengthening exercise was found to be significantly lower in the prediabetes group than in the normoglycemic group. Because this study focused on a relatively young population and investigated recent physical activity levels, direct comparisons with previous studies are limited. Nonetheless, the most recent Korean clinical practice guidelines for diabetes recommend that, in the absence of contraindications, individuals perform at least 30 min of moderate- to vigorous-intensity exercise daily whenever possible and that aerobic exercise be combined with resistance training [17]. Furthermore, international health guidelines similarly underscore the importance of regular and sufficiently intensive physical activity for metabolic health. Overall, these findings suggest that, among young adults aged 19–34 yrs, intervention strategies that consider not only the frequency but also the intensity and type of physical activity May be important for metabolic health, particularly for improving glycemic control.

Ethanol induces mitochondrial dysfunction and promotes the apoptosis of pancreatic β-cells, a process associated with increased production of reactive oxygen species [51]. Moreover, ethanol suppresses insulin-induced phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase activity and downregulates GLUT4 expression in the skeletal muscle, thereby contributing to insulin resistance [52]. On the basis of these mechanisms, alcohol consumption has been recognized as a factor associated with diabetes risk [53]. Nevertheless, the relationship between alcohol consumption and diabetes appears to vary according to the amount consumed, with several studies reporting a J-shaped association with the risk of developing type 2 diabetes [54]. In particular, moderate alcohol consumption is associated with a reduced risk of developing diabetes [55], whereas heavy alcohol consumption tends to be related to an increased risk [56]. The association between alcohol consumption and diabetes or prediabetes may also differ according to sex, obesity status, type of alcoholic beverage, and the overall level of alcohol intake [54,57,58].

In the present study, rather than quantifying alcohol consumption based on the absolute amount of ethanol (in grams) according to beverage type, we used an indicator of high-risk drinking calculated from total alcohol consumption and drinking frequency. After adjusting for potential confounders, high-risk drinking—a healthy lifestyle practice indicator—showed no significant association with the odds of having prediabetes in either men or women. These results may, partially, be attributable to methodological differences from previous studies that quantified alcohol consumption using absolute ethanol intake (g/day). Furthermore, the prevalence of high-risk drinking in our study population was ~10%, representing the lowest level among all adult age groups [6]. This relatively low prevalence may have limited the statistical power to detect a significant association between high-risk drinking and prediabetes.

Smoking contributes to abnormalities in glucose metabolism by increasing insulin resistance and impairing insulin secretory function [59]. Nicotine stimulates the release of insulin-antagonistic hormones, including cortisol, catecholamines, and growth hormone, which in turn increase insulin demand and inhibit peripheral insulin action [60]. A meta-analysis that investigated the risk factors associated with progression from prediabetes to diabetes reported that smoking was associated with a 1.31-fold higher risk of developing diabetes [61]. Consistent with these reports, the present study identified a significantly higher proportion of current smokers in the prediabetes group than in the normoglycemic group among both men and women.

In the present study, healthy lifestyle practice was evaluated using 3 indicators, viz., smoking cessation, moderation of alcohol consumption, and walking practice, and the results showed that neither the healthy lifestyle score nor adherence to healthy lifestyle practices was significantly associated with the odds of having prediabetes. Although each of these lifestyle factors is closely associated with the pathophysiological mechanisms of diabetes, the lack of significant associations in this study may be attributable to differences in the definition and operationalization of the indicators compared with that in previous research. In particular, the measures used in this study may not have completely captured the intensity, duration, or cumulative exposure of lifestyle behaviors. Conversely, a longitudinal study of adults aged 30–64 yrs that defined healthy lifestyle based on 5 factors—smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, sleep duration, and weight control—and followed up participants for ~8 yrs reported that maintaining or improving healthy lifestyle behaviors was significantly associated with a reduced risk of developing prediabetes [62]. Similarly, a 14-yr prospective study among adults with prediabetes found that lower healthy lifestyle scores—based on 6 factors, including nonsmoking, moderate alcohol consumption, regular physical activity, healthy diet, maintenance of normal body weight, and adequate sleep—were significantly associated with an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes [63]. These data suggest that both composition and sustained adherence to healthy lifestyle factors are essential in preventing dysglycemia. Therefore, although no significant association was found between healthy lifestyle practices and prediabetes in the present study, continuous maintenance and reinforcement of healthy behaviors remain essential for reducing diabetes risk. Future studies should move beyond the simple dichotomous measures of adherence and incorporate the intensity, frequency, duration, and combined patterns of lifestyle behaviors to develop more tailored and effective intervention strategies.

Inappropriate dietary habits are closely associated with dysglycemia [64,65], among which frequent eating out is a major risk factor for metabolic disorders, as it tends to increase the consumption of high-calorie and high-sodium foods along with potentially reducing micronutrient intake [66,67]. A study conducted among Chinese workers also reported that a higher frequency of eating out was associated with an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, with the strongest association found in individuals aged < 45 yrs [65]. In the present study, young men with prediabetes were significantly more likely to eat out than their normoglycemic counterparts, demonstrating a pattern consistent with previous findings. However, there was no significant difference in the frequency of eating out between the prediabetes and normoglycemic groups among women, which may be related to evidence suggesting that women generally report lower frequencies of eating out than men and tend to make healthier food choices when dining out [68]. Moreover, women with prediabetes in this study exhibited significantly lower dietary fiber and magnesium intake densities than those with normoglycemia. Considering that dietary fiber and magnesium play beneficial roles in improving insulin sensitivity and glycemic control [69,70], these results emphasize the need for educational interventions targeting young adults to promote healthier food choices when eating out. In addition, nutritional strategies that encourage adequate intake of fiber- and magnesium-rich foods may be essential to prevent dysglycemia in this population.

The present study also examined the association between healthy dietary practices—comprising 4 indicators (percentage of energy from fat, sodium intake, fruit and vegetable consumption, and nutrition label use)—and prediabetes. Results showed that among men, individuals with prediabetes had a significantly lower rate of nutrition label use than those with normoglycemia. Furthermore, after adjusting for potential confounders, adherence to healthy dietary practices was associated with 28% lower odds of having prediabetes. Nutrition label use promotes healthier food choices, as evidenced by reports that individuals who read nutrition labels tend to consume lower amounts of energy, fat, and sugar and higher amounts of fruits and vegetables and generally exhibit better overall diet quality [71]. Conversely, those who do not read nutrition labels are more likely to consume energy-dense diets high in saturated fatty acids and of lower overall quality [72,73]. Such dietary patterns are closely linked to reduced insulin sensitivity, emphasizing the importance of continued and proactive education on nutrition label use. Previous community-based intervention research on patients with type 2 diabetes has reported that promoting nutrition label use contributed to not only improvements in diet quality but also reductions in HbA1c levels [74]. These data suggest that encouraging nutrition label use among young adults, particularly those at risk of developing dysglycemia, serves as an effective strategy for early metabolic risk management.

This study has several limitations. First, being a cross-sectional analysis, it cannot be interpreted as causal relationships between healthy lifestyle or dietary practices and prediabetes. Second, because secondary data were used, it was not possible to comprehensively account for all potential factors affecting healthy lifestyle and dietary behaviors, thereby limiting the scope of interpretation. Third, although the association between healthy dietary practices and prediabetes was observed in men but not in women, a formal statistical test for sex-based interaction was not performed. Therefore, the sex difference in this association should be interpreted with caution, and future studies are warranted to examine whether sex modifies the relationship between dietary practices and prediabetes risk. Fourth, another limitation is that dietary intake was assessed using a single 24-h dietary recall, which may not adequately reflect individuals' usual dietary intake. This is particularly relevant for nutrients with high day-to-day variability, such as sodium, fat, and fruit and vegetable intake [75]. Consequently, the associations observed in this study may be subject to measurement error due to within-person variability, and the true associations between dietary practices and prediabetes risk may have been underestimated or overestimated. Future studies employing multiple dietary assessments or validated food frequency questionnaires are warranted to confirm our findings. Fifth, the healthy lifestyle practice score was limited to 3 components—nonsmoking, moderate alcohol consumption, and walking—as defined by the Community Health Survey [21]. The exclusion of muscle-strengthening and aerobic exercise may underestimate the full scope of lifestyle factors associated with prediabetes, and future studies incorporating a broader range of physical activity measures are warranted.

Nonetheless, this study has remarkable strengths. It focused on young adults, a population in which the risk for dysglycemia has been progressively increasing. By targeting prediabetes rather than overt diabetes, the study could identify modifiable lifestyle and dietary factors that may be associated with progression to disease. These findings may serve as important intervention points for preventing the onset of chronic diseases later in adulthood. Moreover, because early adulthood is a stage in which individuals may not entirely recognize the importance of maintaining healthy dietary and lifestyle habits, this study provides significant evidence to support educational and preventive efforts targeting this age group. Using key health indicators aligned with national health objectives, the findings also provide valuable evidence to inform the direction and prioritization of public health policies.

To summarize, this study demonstrates that adherence to healthy dietary practices is significantly associated with lower odds of having prediabetes among young Korean men, emphasizing the significance of dietary quality during early adulthood. Although overall healthy lifestyle practice indicators were not significantly associated with prediabetes, individual components—such as smoking status and walking—exhibited significant differences between normoglycemic and prediabetic groups. These results highlight that early adulthood represents a critical window for preventing metabolic risk. Public health strategies must prioritize sex-specific, diet-focused interventions, promote nutrition label use, and encourage muscle-strengthening activities to mitigate early dysglycemia. Longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify the temporal relationships and further refine tailored prevention strategies for young adults at metabolic risk.
