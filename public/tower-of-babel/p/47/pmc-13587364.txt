Full-term delivery in pregnancy complicated with suspected residual cervical alveolar soft part sarcoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yan-Ran Sheng, Xi-Rong Xiao, Yun-Zhen Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) is an exceptionally rare soft tissue malignancy, with limited data available on fertility preservation and pregnancy management. Managing ASPS during pregnancy poses unique challenges, particularly when the condition is incidentally discovered following surgery initially performed for presumed benign conditions.Case presentationWe present the case of a young woman who underwent a transvaginal clamp procedure for a cervical myoma and a curettage due to irregular vaginal bleeding. Histopathological examination confirmed the presence of cervical ASPS. Shortly thereafter, an unexpected pregnancy was identified, prior to the completion of a comprehensive staging assessment and evaluation of residual lesions. The patient expressed a strong desire to continue the pregnancy. Under the guidance of a multidisciplinary team (MDT), she underwent close surveillance throughout her pregnancy. Histopathology revealed a low Ki-67 proliferation index, and a series of pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans showed no evidence of mass lesions. The patient successfully carried the pregnancy to term and delivered a healthy infant via cesarean section. During the cesarean section, a suspicious lesion was identified; however, biopsies and placental pathology confirmed the absence of residual tumor or metastasis. Follow-up at 42 days postpartum revealed no signs of tumor recurrence.ConclusionOur case provides valuable evidence supporting the safety of fertility preservation in patients with cervical ASPS in the short term. Additionally, we outline a management approach for pregnancies complicated by rare, low-grade malignancies through multidisciplinary evaluation. Our experience suggests that, with rigorous and dynamic evaluations, it may be possible for these patients to safely navigate pregnancy.

Introduction

Alveolar soft-part sarcoma (ASPS) is a rare and aggressive soft tissue malignancy, accounting for 0.4% to 1% of all soft tissue sarcomas [1]. When it occurs in the cervix, it is exceptionally rare, posing significant challenges for both diagnosis and treatment. The cornerstone of ASPS treatment is complete surgical resection (R0), which can be achieved through standard hysterectomy or, in select cases, fertility-sparing surgery [1]. However, the incidental discovery of ASPS following surgery for presumed benign conditions, especially when complicated by concurrent pregnancy, presents a unique clinical dilemma. This scenario requires a careful balance between oncological management and pregnancy care, making it particularly complex.

The diagnosis of ASPS is based on distinctive histopathological features and molecular confirmation. Histologically, ASPS is characterized by organoid nests of large polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, separated by fibrovascular septa. Molecularly, it is confirmed by the presence of the ASPSCR1-TFE3 fusion gene, which results from the t(X;17)(p11.2;q25) translocation [2]. When ASPS is discovered incidentally after surgery, particularly in cases where complete staging and assessment of residual disease are constrained by pregnancy, management becomes exceptionally challenging. The limited available literature on concurrent ASPS and pregnancy has created substantial uncertainty regarding optimal treatment strategies and the timing of interventions [3].

Here, we present a case of cervical ASPS incidentally discovered in a young woman during early pregnancy. Through multidisciplinary management, the patient successfully carried the pregnancy to term and delivered a healthy infant via cesarean section. Close surveillance throughout pregnancy and the early postpartum period revealed no evidence of disease recurrence. This case highlights the critical role of multidisciplinary management in achieving positive outcomes when dealing with rare gynecologic malignancies during pregnancy.

Case report

A 24-year-old woman presented with irregular vaginal bleeding lasting 15 days per cycle since 2022. Initial evaluation revealed a 3 cm cervical tumor, suspected to be a submucosal fibroid. On March 28, 2024, she underwent transvaginal clamping and curettage, which identified a vascular, spherical tumor. During the procedure, the tumor shattered. Pathology confirmed uterine alveolar soft-part sarcoma (ASPS) with immunohistochemical positivity for TFE3 and a Ki-67 proliferation index of 5% (Fig. 1). The tumor cells were negative for CD10, S-100, CK, Desmin, α-inhibin, MyoD1, P16, CD34, and SMA (Fig. 2). The patient was subsequently referred to Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center. Pelvic MRI with plain scan and contrast enhancement revealed an abnormal signal in the uterine cavity (47 × 36 mm), raising suspicion of pregnancy. Routine prenatal visits revealed no abnormalities, except for a positive Group B Streptococcus (GBS) test and hypothyroidism, which was managed with levothyroxine. At 38 weeks and 6 days, a planned cesarean section was performed. Suspected lesions were identified in the lower uterine segment and the right ovary, and biopsies were taken. Intraoperative frozen pathology and postoperative histopathology showed no residual tumor. Placental and ovarian biopsies revealed no signs of metastasis. The newborn had an Apgar score of 9–9, and the patient recovered well, breastfeeding postpartum. At 42 days postpartum, follow-up tumor markers, ultrasound, and pelvic MRI showed no evidence of recurrence (Fig. 3). Discussion

This case represents the first documented instance of a pregnancy complicated by cervical ASPS. Thanks to the vigilant monitoring and evidence-based management provided by a multidisciplinary team, the patient was able to navigate both pregnancy and the postpartum period successfully, resulting in excellent outcomes for both mother and newborn. This experience offers valuable insights for the clinical management of pregnancies complicated by residual malignancy risk.

Comparison and interpretation

ASPS of the female genital tract is exceedingly rare and represents a malignancy with unclear differentiation. Given its rarity and unclear differentiation, the standard treatment for localized ASPS typically involves total hysterectomy with or without bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy [1, 5]. However, in younger patients with cervical ASPS, local excision that preserves fertility may be an option [6, 7]. There have been reports of patients remaining recurrence-free for up to 155 months after local excision [8]. Notably, fertility-sparing approaches have also been successful in cases of uterine sarcomas [9–11]. Andrea Etrusco and colleagues conducted a comprehensive search across multiple databases to identify studies on fertility-sparing treatments for uterine sarcomas in the United States. And we have re-reviewed and updated the literature retrieved from PubMed up to April 30, 2026, and refreshed the number of pregnancy cases of rare malignant tumors in (Table 1). (Table 1) [3, 12–15]. The histological subtypes included leiomyosarcoma (LMS), rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS), adenosarcoma (AS), and low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (LGMS). Among the 156 patients, 19 (12.18%) of the women achieved pregnancy [3]. Notably, the single patient with LGMS had two successful deliveries but later experienced tumor recurrence after childbirth, necessitating radical surgery [10]. Pregnancy success rates varied significantly across different subtypes, with relatively higher success rates observed in patients with LMS compared to those with RMS and AS. This underscores the potential for fertility preservation in carefully selected cases of uterine sarcomas, although outcomes are dependent on the histological subtype. In this particular case, the lesion was primarily located in the cervix, and radical surgery was not performed. During the initial procedure, the tumor detached and fragmented, which could potentially leave residual lesions. Pregnancy was discovered almost simultaneously with the identification of ASPS. Given the patient’s strong desire to preserve fertility, the low Ki-67 expression (Ki-67+, 5%) on immunohistochemistry, and the favorable outcomes reported in prior cases of uterine malignancy, we boldly proposed a plan to continue the pregnancy, with close surveillance and multidisciplinary evaluation throughout the gestational period. No signs of tumor recurrence were detected, further corroborating our decision.

The obstetric management of this patient, including optimal timing and route of delivery, was collaboratively determined through multidisciplinary team evaluation. In addition to standard prenatal surveillance, serial MRI examinations of the upper abdomen and pelvis were performed at three-month intervals to monitor disease progression, with predetermined plans for pregnancy termination if concerning abnormalities emerged. At 38 weeks’ gestation, comprehensive pelvic MRI reassessment and tumor marker profiling were completed prior to the onset of spontaneous labor. Current literature analysis reveals that while all four documented AS cases resulted in vaginal deliveries, current clinical practice predominantly favors cesarean delivery for most uterine sarcoma-associated pregnancies to mitigate oncological risks [3]. This strategy facilitates comprehensive intraoperative assessment and immediate management of suspected pathological findings. In the current case, despite documented risks of residual neoplastic tissue, rigorous antenatal monitoring demonstrated no radiographic or biochemical evidence of disease recurrence during gestation. Consequently, a scheduled cesarean delivery with uterine conservation was coordinated at 39 weeks’ gestation following multidisciplinary consensus. A contingency protocol mandated intraoperative gynecologic oncology consultation for resection of any macroscopic lesions identified during the procedure, with subsequent histopathological analysis of excised tissues and placental specimens. To mitigate the risk of neoplastic cell dissemination, standard uterine packing techniques were deliberately omitted during the lower uterine segment closure of the cesarean procedure. The delivery course was uncomplicated, culminating in the birth of a phenotypically normal neonate with Apgar scores within normal parameters (9 at 1 min, 9 at 5 min). Mother-infant dyad cohabitation was initiated within two hours post-delivery, with exclusive breastfeeding successfully established and sustained through the postpartum period. Histopathological evaluation of intraoperative biopsy specimens coupled with systematic placental examination revealed no evidence of malignant transformation or metastatic deposits. Comprehensive neonatal evaluation including metabolic screening and ultrasonographic assessment confirmed physiological adaptation, with all parameters aligning with gestational age-specific norms. The puerperium was characterized by unremarkable recovery. At the 6-week postpartum evaluation, comprehensive surveillance including transvaginal ultrasonography, pelvic MRI, and serum tumor marker panels revealed no evidence of disease recurrence. Given the diagnostic rarity of the primary condition and its established propensity for delayed recurrence or metastatic progression, the gynecologic oncology team implemented a structured surveillance protocol. This evidence-based strategy incorporates serial multimodal imaging and biomarker monitoring to optimize early detection sensitivity for occult recurrence, while enabling preemptive therapeutic escalation through multidisciplinary tumor board coordination should disease resurgence occur.

Concerns about radiation exposure

At 8 + 4 weeks gestation, the patient underwent contrast-enhanced upper abdominal computed tomography (CT) with concurrent pelvic MRI utilizing intravenous contrast administration without knowledge of her pregnancy. Contemporary evidence establishes a dose-dependent teratogenic risk continuum: high-dose ionizing radiation (> 1000 mGy) carries significant risks of embryonic demise and severe fetal sequelae including growth restriction, microcephaly, and neurocognitive impairment, with deterministic effects typically manifesting above 50–200 mGy thresholds [16, 17]. Standard diagnostic imaging protocols—notably abdominal CT (fetal radiation exposure range: 1.3–35 mGy)—generally maintain exposures below 50 mGy, though epidemiological studies suggest a potential 0.1–0.2% excess relative risk of childhood leukemia per 10–20 mGy first-trimester exposure [18]. Importantly, no causal relationship exists between single-exposure diagnostic radiography/CT and adverse fetal outcomes, with current guidelines explicitly advising against pregnancy termination following accidental low-dose exposure [19]. While iodinated contrast agents (FDA pregnancy category B) demonstrate theoretical transplacental thyroid effects, clinical studies have not substantiated neonatal hypothyroidism or developmental abnormalities [20]. MRI remains the preferred modality for pregnancy-associated diagnostic imaging when clinically indicated, though safety data regarding ultrahigh-field systems (> 3.0T) remain inconclusive [21]. Gadolinium-based contrast agents (FDA category C) require judicious risk-benefit analysis due to animal model evidence of teratogenicity at supratherapeutic doses and observational studies associating prenatal exposure with elevated risks of stillbirth (adjusted HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.09–1.70) and childhood inflammatory conditions [22]. Longitudinal pediatric surveillance has been initiated to monitor potential neurodevelopmental, endocrine, and oncological sequelae in this case, including biennial developmental assessments and thyroid function tracking until adolescence.

Roles of MDT

Accumulating evidence underscores the pivotal role of multidisciplinary team (MDT) collaboration in fertility-preserving management of rare gynecologic malignancies [23, 24]. This cervical ASPS case with residual lesions during pregnancy demonstrates three critical success factors of MDT management in rare gestational malignancies: histopathological risk stratification, fetal-protective imaging protocols, and precision surgical planning. Central to this approach was the diagnostic alliance between gynecologic oncologists and specialized pathologists, whose integrated analysis of tumor biology, quantified by a 5% Ki-67 proliferative index, provided prognostic validation for expectant management through documented indolent tumor kinetics. This quantitative risk profiling enabled evidence-based equipoise between immediate intervention and pregnancy continuation, particularly crucial given ASPS’s status as an ultra-rare mesenchymal malignancy with limited gestational outcome data. Radiologists were instrumental in conducting a focused assessment of the patient. By employing targeted imaging studies, they were able to monitor the lesion closely while minimizing fetal exposure to radiation. This meticulous imaging approach ensured that any potential changes in the tumor’s behavior were promptly detected, allowing for timely adjustments to the management plan. The obstetric team played a crucial role, providing detailed antenatal management and evaluation to ensure the health of both the mother and fetus throughout the pregnancy. Their expertise was especially vital in determining the optimal timing and mode of delivery. Given the potential risks associated with pregnancy in this context, the decision to proceed with a cesarean section was made to minimize any intraoperative risks to both the mother and the newborn. Additionally, strict adherence to tumor-free principles during surgical exploration, including the avoidance of using gauze rolls during the cesarean section, was essential to prevent any potential dissemination of tumor cells. The care of the neonate by neonatologists, along with the assessment of potential tumor transmission from mother to child, was also of critical importance. The coordinated efforts of the multidisciplinary team enabled a watchful waiting approach, which ultimately led to an excellent obstetric outcome. The patient successfully navigated both the pregnancy and the puerperal period, with no signs of tumor recurrence detected during close surveillance. This case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing complex cases, especially when they involve rare conditions like cervical ASPS. The successful collaboration between different specialties not only ensured the best possible outcomes for the patient but also provided valuable insights for future cases.

Roles of hysteroscopy in cervical malignant tumors and iatrogenic dissemination risk

Considering the special pathological type of this tumor (alveolar soft part sarcoma is a rare mesenchymal tumor, different from common epithelial-derived cervical cancer), the main follow-up indicators recommended in current clinical guidelines for alveolar soft part sarcoma are imaging examinations (such as pelvic CT/MRI) and tumor marker detection, to monitor the occurrence of local recurrence and distant metastasis. In addition, the patient had no obvious clinical symptoms (such as irregular vaginal bleeding, abnormal discharge) during the follow-up period, and the results of pelvic imaging examinations and tumor markers were all within the normal range. Therefore, we did not perform hysteroscopy in the short-term follow-up. However, hysteroscopy plays an irreplaceable role in the diagnosis and management of cervical masses and malignant tumors [25]. It enables direct visualization of lesions, facilitates targeted biopsy, and helps improve the detection rate of early cervical malignant tumors. This diagnostic advantage is particularly important for hidden endocervical lesions that are difficult to detect by imaging alone. Standardized hysteroscopic operation, following the principle of tumor-free manipulation, will not increase the risk of iatrogenic dissemination. For rare cervical mesenchymal tumors, such as the case in this study, imaging examinations (ultrasound, MRI) are sufficient for follw-up monitoring, and routine hysteroscopic examination is not required to avoid unnecessary risks.

In clinical practice, the rational application of hysteroscopy should balance diagnostic value and safety risks. For patients with cervical malignant tumors, the application of hysteroscopy should follow standardized operating procedures to avoid iatrogenic dissemination caused by improper operation. This not only ensures the accuracy of diagnosis and treatment but also maximizes the protection of patients’ prognosis.

Advantages and limitations

To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of successful pregnancy continuation and term delivery in a patient with suspected residual cervical ASPS, providing a seminal reference for fertility-preserving management of this ultra-rare malignancy. While this single-institution experience offers critical preliminary data on perinatal ASPS behavior, particularly the observed tumor indolence despite pregnancy-associated hormonal stimulation, its inherent limitations as an isolated case report underscore the need for international registry studies to establish evidence-based protocols. Further research is needed to better assess disease status, establish optimal surgical and chemoradiation strategies, and improve perinatal management for patients who become pregnant after treatment.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that with careful patient selection, a comprehensive MDT approach, and close monitoring, positive pregnancy outcomes can be achieved after a cervical ASPS diagnosis. The insights gained offer useful guidance for managing similar cases in the future.
