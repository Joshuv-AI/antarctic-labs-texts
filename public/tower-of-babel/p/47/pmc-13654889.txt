Multidisciplinary approach to managing GPRC5D-related adverse events in relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma patients
Authors: Nicolas Albrecht, Eline Boeckx, Jo Caers, Sophie Delaunois, Michel Delforge, Dorien Deneve, Nicolas Kint, Nathalie Meuleman, Louise Sarrazyn, Rik Schots, Anneleen Vanhellemont, Marie-Christiane Vekemans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The introduction of talquetamab, a G protein-coupled receptor family C group 5 member D (GPRC5D)-targeting bispecific antibody, represents a novel therapy for patients with relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM). Despite its promising efficacy, talquetamab is associated with distinctive adverse events (AEs), such as oral (dysgeusia, dysphagia, dry mouth) and dermatologic AEs (rashes, other skin-related symptoms, nail toxicities). These recommendations aim to develop a multidisciplinary framework for managing GPRC5D-related AEs, tailored for a wide audience that includes hematologists, oncology nurses, dieticians, and patients. A comprehensive literature review was conducted to identify current evidence and guidelines on GPRC5D-related AEs. Clinical experience from 12 Belgian healthcare professionals (6 hematologists, 4 nurses, 2 dieticians) was integrated and discussed to inform the development of practical recommendations. Data were synthesized to develop practical recommendations for managing common talquetamab-related AEs. Key AEs included dysgeusia, dry mouth, dysphagia, skin toxicities, and nail toxicities. These AEs, though mostly low-grade, impacted patient quality of life and nutritional status. Management strategies focused on dose modifications, oral care, dermatologic support, and early dietician referral. Patient education and multidisciplinary collaboration were identified as essential components of optimal supportive care. This is the first expert consensus-based set of multidisciplinary recommendations in Belgium addressing GPRC5D-related AEs. It provides practical, role-specific recommendations to support talquetamab-treated patients, improve tolerability, and optimize treatment adherence and outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00277-026-07110-0.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) represents approximately 1% of all cancers and around 10% of hematologic malignancies [1] and is characterized by uncontrolled proliferation of malignant plasma cells. Despite recent therapeutic advancements, MM remains largely incurable, with most patients relapsing or developing resistance to multiple lines of standard therapies [2, 3], including immunomodulatory agents (IMiDs), proteasome inhibitors (PIs), anti-CD38 antibodies, and increasingly to B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-directed therapies [4, 5], often accompanied by a progressive decline in quality of life in later treatment lines [6]. These challenges in managing relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) emphasize the need for treatments with novel mechanisms of action [5].

The G protein-coupled receptor family C group 5 member D (GPRC5D) has emerged as a promising target for MM due to its elevated expression on myeloma cells [7]. Talquetamab, a first-in-class, off-the-shelf, T-cell-redirecting bispecific antibody (BsAb) targets GPRC5D on MM cells and CD3 on T-cells, activating T-cells to kill GPRC5D-expressing cells [7–10].

Talquetamab was approved by the European Medicines Agency in August 2023 [11] as a single agent and demonstrated overall response rates of 74.1% and 69.5% for weekly and biweekly talquetamab dosing respectively, and 66.7% in patients previously treated with other T-cell redirection therapies [5, 8] in the supporting MonumenTAL-1 trial (NCT03399799, NCT04634552). These effects were durable and comparable to those of anti-BCMA BsAbs [12–15]. T-cell redirection therapies, including chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T-cell) therapy and BCMA-directed BsAbs, are associated with both hematologic (anemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia) and nonhematologic (cytokine release syndrome [CRS], immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome [ICANS], and infections) adverse events (AEs) [4, 5].

Additionally, on-target, off-tumor activity results in class-specific AEs. GPRC5D-targeting therapies are particularly linked to oral cavity, skin, and nail AEs [16–19]. Although expression of GPRC5D in healthy tissues is limited, it has been observed in areas such as the tongue, skin, and glands [20–22]. Taste disturbances can cause weight loss, malnutrition, and decreased quality of life [23–26], necessitating diligent monitoring and proactive management [4, 5]. Beyond talquetamab, other GPRC5D directed immunotherapies such as forimtamig and GPRC5D targeted CAR T-cells, have shown similar on-target, off-tumor toxicity profiles in early phase studies [27, 28]. Nevertheless, talquetamab demonstrated both T-cell redirection- and GPRC5D-associated AEs, with lower rates of grade 3 and 4 infections and neutropenia compared to BCMA-directed BsAbs [15, 22, 29].

In addition to already published management guidelines specific to clinical trials [5] and nursing practice [4], this consensus adopts a multidisciplinary approach to consolidate strategies for managing GPRC5D-related AEs for a broader audience, including hematologists, (oncology) nurses, dermatologists, dieticians, and patients. These recommendations synthesize data from the MonumenTAL-1 trial [5], relevant literature and guidelines [20, 30, 31], and the authors’ multidisciplinary clinical experience in Belgian practice.

Methodology

An initial targeted literature review identified existing guidelines and published best practices on managing GPRC5D-related AEs, with a focus on oral AEs (dysgeusia, dry mouth, dysphagia), weight loss, and dermatological AEs (skin and nail toxicities) but also other toxicities (infections, CRS and ICANS) to identify existing guidelines [20, 30, 31] and best practices reported in the literature.

Following this review, multidisciplinary clinical experience from 12 Belgian healthcare professionals (6 hematologists, 4 nurses, and 2 dieticians) was incorporated. The healthcare professionals were selected based on their direct clinical involvement in the day-to-day management of patients receiving GPRC5D-targeting BsAbs, to capture real-world practices.

Key clinical considerations were discussed during a multidisciplinary expert meeting on 8 October 2024 with 9 healthcare professionals (5 hematologists, 3 oncology nurses, and 1 oncology dietician). In instances where available evidence was inconclusive, consensus was defined as a majority (> 50%) of panel votes.

This manuscript integrates a targeted, non-systematic literature review with consensus-based input from a multidisciplinary clinical expertise to provide Belgian guidance on GPRC5D‑related AEs.

Comprehensive management strategies for GPR5CD-related AEs

In the MonumenTAL-1 trial, the most common nonhematologic AEs (any grade) were CRS, oral AEs (dysgeusia, dry mouth, dysphagia), and dermatologic AEs (skin and nail toxicities), consistent with other GPRC5D-targeting therapies [15–19]. This article will focus on the management of oral and dermatologic AEs related to talquetamab.

Oral AEs

Oral talquetamab-related AEs included dysgeusia (altered taste perception, affecting 72% of patients in MonumenTAL-1), xerostomia (subjective dry mouth, 36%) [32], and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing, 24%), occasionally accompanied by reduced appetite and subsequent weight loss [5].

Published evidence

Published literature indicates that dose modifications may reduce oral AE severity [20] in patients who had achieved at least a partial response (PR) [5, 8], while maintaining efficacy. Other supportive strategies for dysgeusia (e.g. adequate hydration, salivary stimulants, and flavor enhancers) showed inconsistent efficacy. Local treatments (oral steroids and antifungals) and zinc supplementation had equally limited or inconclusive efficacy [5, 33].

Management strategies for dry mouth and dysphagia underscore the key role of saliva in oral health. Salivary substitutes, sugar-free gum, and good dental hygiene aid in reducing discomfort and oral infection risk. From an oncodietary standpoint, patients are advised to ensure adequate hydration, eat smaller bites, food with varied structures, or moist food (e.g. with sauces and broths). For stomatitis and dry mouth, sodium lauryl sulphate-free toothpaste can be considered. Routine use of corticosteroid mouthwashes is discouraged due to limited evidence of benefit and the potential risk of secondary infections [4, 5]. Despite these supportive options, there is currently no consensus on the optimal management of oral AEs, and they remain a challenging AE to address.

Oral AEs can lead to dietary changes and subsequent weight loss. Clinically significant weight loss is defined as a ≥ 10% weight decrease from baseline and occurred in 40% of the patients in the MonumenTAL-1 trial. Early dietician involvement is recommended (see “Oncodietary Measures”).

Clinical recommendations

General recommendations for oral AEs

As there is currently no single therapeutic measure for oral AEs, a multifaceted and supportive approach is recommended. For all oral AEs, dose modifications (e.g. dose reductions, dosing interval extensions, or skipping a dose) should be considered, but only after an adequate treatment response (i.e. at least a partial response or better) [34]. It is currently unclear whether deeper responses are required, with dose reduction generally favored in patients achieving a very good partial response or complete response. Interval extension is generally favored over dose reductions, although both approaches are currently considered equivalent strategies in terms of efficacy. If oral toxicities persist, restarting on a modified schedule can be considered (0.4 mg/kg every 2 weeks [Q2W] instead of 0.4 mg/kg every week [QW], or 0.8 mg/kg once every 4 weeks [Q4W] instead of 0.8 mg/kg Q2W) [11].

Patient education regarding oral toxicities is described in “Patient Communication”. Physicians should evaluate for underlying infectious or inflammatory causes, such as candidiasis or esophagitis, which are infrequent, and treat accordingly.

Based on patients’ needs, referral to additional specialists on an as-needed basis, rather than systematically, is also recommended. These may include psychologists, pharmacists, stomatologists, gastrointestinal (GI) specialists, speech therapists, and otolaryngologists, to support the overall well-being and provide appropriate treatment.

There remains a need for specialized training and clear guidelines for oncology dieticians to manage the side effects of anticancer treatments with significant oral AE. In addition to existing international initiatives, such as European Specialist Dietetic Network for Oncology (ESDN), an initiative under the European Federation of the Associations of Dietitians (EFAD) or Exercise Oncology/Oncology Nutrition (EON), national networks are still in development in Belgium.

Dysgeusia

Dysgeusia represents the most common and impactful long-term oral side effect, often having a greater effect on patients than dry mouth or dysphagia. Although fluctuations in dysgeusia during cycles have been observed, the quality of life (QoL) impact of these AEs remained impactful, contributing to significant weight loss in the first cycles of treatment.

There are no standardized preventive measures for dysgeusia; management focuses on preventing associated weight loss especially during the first treatment cycles.

Few patients discontinue talquetamab due to dysgeusia alone; symptoms are expected to improve over time with continued treatment and appropriate supportive care [5, 20]. However, treatment interruption or cessation may be considered when oral AEs contribute to clinically significant or progressive weight loss. Oncodietary follow-up should be initiated early (see “Oncodietary Measures”).

In case of dysgeusia, rinsing the mouth regularly with water or baking soda is recommended. For dysgeusia specifically, routine use of steroid- or lidocaine‑containing mouthwashes is discouraged, as efficacy has not been demonstrated and might facilitate fungal infections, which are uncommon. However, their use may be considered in selected patients with persistent or severe symptoms.

For patients with persistent taste disturbances, there are limited data regarding the use of zinc gluconate at a dose of 140 mg daily in idiopathic dysgeusia [31]. In this regard, zinc supplementation may be considered a therapeutic approach in cases of persistent symptomatology.

Dry mouth/dysphagia

Swallowing difficulties may result from dysgeusia, reduced saliva, or food texture, rather than pain; not all cases qualify as true dysphagia. Although these symptoms are encountered in practice, the need for clearer, standardized clinical pathways to support consistent assessment and management across centers was highlighted.

Early dietician evaluation is recommended (see “Oncodietary Measures”). If dry mouth is the main cause of swallowing difficulty without pain, further investigation is typically not required. For more severe dysphagia (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [CTCAE] grade 2 or higher, Supplementary Table 1) or painful symptoms, a GI specialist consultation is advised for potential endoscopy or biopsy.

Supportive measures (e.g. saliva stimulation/substitutes; sauces/broths) are discussed in “Oncodietary Measures”.

Weight loss

Weight loss is common during the first 3–4 months of talquetamab treatment, even with nutritional support, followed by a period of stabilization [5]. Subsequent weight gain is not consistently seen, but may occur over time in patients with sustained treatment response and adequate management of side effects. This weight loss is strongly associated with oral toxicities, particularly dysgeusia. Preventing weight loss is critical in this high-risk population, and any unexplained weight loss should prompt an evaluation for possible dental problems or oral inflammation. Physicians or nurses should monitor weight on a regular basis, and patients should be encouraged to self‑monitor their weight between visits.

In patients with significant weight loss, dose adjustments to weight-based medications may be necessary.

Weight should be checked at each routine visit, at least monthly, and patients should be encouraged to monitor their weight regularly at home to allow early detection of changes. Any unexplained weight loss prompts assessment and treatment for other underlying causes, such as nausea or gastrointestinal issues. CTCAE grade 1 weight loss requires monitoring, grade 2 intervention, and grade 3 may warrant talquetamab dose reduction or cessation over starting enteral or parenteral nutrition (Supplementary Table 1).

Oncodietary measures in GPRC5D directed treatment

Dieticians can play an important role by providing dietary support where available. A comprehensive dietary assessment is recommended prior to initiating talquetamab treatment. In general, patients are encouraged to experiment with different foods, flavors and textures; eat smaller, more frequent meals; stay well hydrated; and practice good oral hygiene, i.e., using a soft toothpaste and regular dental check-ups essential to prevent complications. Maintaining a food diary is advised.

Dysgeusia

Dieticians highlight the individuality of food enjoyment in preventing weight loss. An individualized approach to experiment with food flavors (e.g. sour, salty, fresh), textures (liquid, crunchy) and temperatures (hot and cold within same meal) is therefore advised, supporting nutritional intake with smaller, frequent meals.

Dry mouth/dysphagia

Dieticians are important in helping patients manage dry mouth and dysphagia by recommending soft, moist foods and avoiding dry, crumbly items like bread and meat. Sauces or broths, liquid or pureed foods, and upright eating can further alleviate swallowing difficulties. Monitoring dietary intake is essential to ensure nutritional needs are met.

Weight loss

For patients experiencing moderate weight loss, the inclusion of high-calorie and high-protein foods, such as milkshakes or oily fish, is recommended. If further nutritional intake is required, dieticians may propose further strategies, including high-calorie shakes or protein powders.

Nutritional supplements, such as a combination of vitamin B/C or vitamin D3/calcium, minerals, and high-calorie shakes are routinely prescribed, although the use of these supplements is currently not supported by clear evidence.

Dermatologic AEs

Dermatologic AEs include skin toxicities such as rashes, reported in 35% of patients in the MonumenTAL-1 trial, and non-rash manifestations (including skin exfoliation, dry skin, pruritus, and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome) in 65% of patients, and nail toxicities, in 56% of patients [5].

Published evidence

In MonumenTAL-1 [5], skin and nail toxicities were managed according to institutional guidelines prioritizing early intervention. For widespread or persistent rashes not responding to topical treatments, short courses of oral corticosteroids were recommended, and dermatology consultations were advised for refractory or severe cases (defined as CTCAE grade ≥ 2 or persistent [> 2 months] grade 1) [4, 5].

Nurse recommendations [4] highlight the importance of early and regular skin hydration (e.g. petrolatum-based emollients), avoiding hot showers, and using sensitive skin soaps.

Nail toxicities were managed with nail soaks, moisturizers, corticosteroids, emollients, vitamin E oil, biotin supplements, and protective coverings such as socks and gloves worn overnight [4, 5].

For persistent or high-grade toxicities (Supplementary Table 2), particularly after cycle 2, dermatology consultation was recommended for rashes unresponsive to emollients or low-potency corticosteroids, and to rule out alternative explanations such as Grover’s disease, a transient acantholytic dermatosis characterized by pruritic papulovesicular eruptions, typically on the trunk [4, 5]. For nail-related AEs, podiatry referral is recommended for toenail hygiene issues, infections of compromised nail beds, or footwear-related discomfort [5]. Pre-emptive podiatric consultation was not advised during this consensus.

Clinical recommendations

Skin and nail toxicities associated with talquetamab, and other cancer therapies are most often graded as CTCAE grade 1–2, but may be frequent, persistent, and clinically burdensome for patients, even when not leading to treatment discontinuation. Nail-related events tend to have a low-grade but important esthetical impact. Preventive measures, appropriate topical care, and close collaboration with dermatologists are central to managing skin and nail toxicities; podology support may be helpful for nail‑related complications when self‑care is insufficient (see “Nail Toxicities”).

Skin toxicities

Grade 1 skin toxicities (excluding dry skin or exfoliation) can typically be managed with topical steroids such as clobetasol or mometasone furoate. Grade 2 or higher toxicities warrant dermatologist referral. Oncology nurses with skin care experience may also be beneficial. Specific emollients, such as urea-based creams for desquamation on hands or feet, or general moisturizers (containing e.g. niacinamide, shea butter, glycerin, glycerol, liquid paraffin) can be used for overall skin hydration. Dose modifications may be considered (as discussed above).

Early dermatology referral is advised particularly if topical or systemic corticosteroids are needed. Systemic steroids should be reserved for severe cases due to side effects. While Chari et al. [5] recommend dermatology referral primarily for persistent grade 3 or 4 skin toxicities, earlier dermatologic intervention is considered appropriate for grade 2 or higher. This recommendation does not reflect a formal consensus in the literature; rather, it is based on clinical experience in the Belgian healthcare setting. In healthcare settings with smaller patient volumes per clinician and broad availability of dermatologic care, earlier specialist involvement was considered practical and beneficial to support proactive management.

Preventive skin hydration should be initiated proactively from the start of treatment, with routine application of emollients and topical agents. Patients should avoid hot showers, use mild fragrance-free soap, wear protective clothing (e.g. for gardening), avoid tight footwear, and limit sun exposure using high-SPF sunscreen.

Nail toxicities

Patients are encouraged to ensure good nail hygiene before treatment, such as addressing calluses or trimming nails short, to reduce complication risks. Prolonged water exposure (e.g. dishwashing) should be avoided, and hand/feet protected with gloves or socks during gardening or overnight. Tight footwear is discouraged.

If patients are unable to manage nail care independently, referral for supportive nail care may be recommended, preferably under dermatologic or podiatric guidance. Dose modifications may also be considered (as discussed above). Nail health should be monitored, ideally with photographic documentation. If atypical or unexplained nail changes occur, further diagnostic evaluation may be considered.

Dermatologist consultations should focus on preserving nail health, including preventive and supportive measures such as nail polish, hardeners, vitamin E products, or biotin supplements. Routine systemic corticosteroids are generally avoided. Referral for specialized supportive nail care may benefit patients with painful or malformed nails and may also be considered proactively in patients at risk.

Other toxicities of interest

Infections, CRS, and ICANS are common AEs in MM patients. Preventive measures and management strategies for these events are outlined in the European Myeloma Network (EMN) [30] and International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) [20] guidelines, and by Chari et al. [5]. These guidelines are routinely applied in clinical practice; therefore, this section focuses on practical aspects relevant to talquetamab treatment.

Infections

Infections, reported in 64% of MonumenTAL-1 patients, remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in MM, influenced by disease-, treatment-, and patient-specific factors [5, 35, 36].

Preventive strategies follow current guidelines,, including vaccination, antibacterial/antiviral prophylaxis, and intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIg) use [20, 30]. Antifungal prophylaxis can be considered for persistent grade 4 neutropenia (absolute neutrophil count [ANC] < 500/µL). SARS-CoV-2 precautions, such as social distancing and mask-wearing, are no longer routinely advised, but could be considered for patients with long-lasting lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count [ALC] < 500/µL or CD4 count < 200/µL).

Although BsAbs and CAR-T-cell therapies carry elevated infection risk, talquetamab demonstrated a lower incidence of high-grade infections compared to BCMA-targeting BsAbs like teclistamab or elranatamab [5, 20]. IVIg prophylaxis should be considered by immunoglobulin IgG ≤ 4 g/L, recurrent infections, or rapidly declining IgG levels, recognizing that many patients may require ongoing support during treatment, in order to ensure appropriate and judicious use of IVIg in the context of periodic supply constraints.

If dose modifications are necessary, extending dosing intervals is preferred (as discussed above). However, no clinical evidence has demonstrated that one approach is superior to the other in terms of response effectiveness.

CRS

CRS, an acute systemic inflammatory syndrome associated with CAR T-cell therapies and BsAbs, occurred in 77% of MonumenTAL-1 patients [5], with rates consistent with other T-cell-redirecting BsAbs.

Close monitoring for CRS symptoms, according to the established American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) grading framework, and prompt medical intervention are essential for patients with high tumor burden (e.g. high bone marrow infiltration, extramedullary disease, or elevated paraprotein levels), comorbidities, or frailty [37]. Management typically includes tocilizumab and corticosteroids (e.g. dexamethasone), particularly for grade ≥ 2 CRS or earlier in clinically vulnerable patients. Treatment should be paused until CRS has fully resolved, with discontinuation considered only in cases of severe CRS, although such events were rare with talquetamab and generally resolved with timely and appropriate management in the MonumenTAL 1 trial [5].

Neurotoxicity/ICANS

ICANS, a central nervous disorder (CNS) caused by the activation or engagement of endogenous or infused T-cells and/or other immune effector cells following immunotherapy [38], was reported in 7.7% of MonumenTAL-1 patients [5], consistent with other T-cell-redirecting therapies.

Similar to CRS, ICANS assessment and management should follow the established ASTCT grading framework, with corticosteroids (e.g. dexamethasone) commonly used from grade 1 ICANS [37].

Pseudoprogression

Pseudoprogression, a rare phenomenon in which apparent disease progression (e.g. increase in bone or extramedullary lesions) occurs due to immune cell infiltration following T-cell activation, has been reported with bispecific antibodies [5, 39–41]. Although its incidence and risk factors remain unclear, suggestive signs of pseudoprogression involve suspicion of rapid clinical progression of bone lesions or extramedullary lesions during step-up, in spite of demonstration of signs of T-cell activation (CRS/ICANS); monitoring laboratory parameters such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and serum free light chains may support clinical assessment. Crucially, treatment should not be discontinued prematurely, as patients may still experience significant clinical responses, provided that careful clinical and laboratory monitoring is ensured.

Cytopenias

Cytopenia AEs (neutropenia, lymphopenia and thrombocytopenia) were commonly observed in the MonumenTAL-1 trial patients, but in were in general reversible and limited to the first cycle [5]. Management of these AEs falls under routine hematological treatment and was therefore not included in the panel discussion.

Patient communication

Providing patients with pretreatment education on talquetamab, including realistic expectations regarding potential alterations in taste and skin or nail changes, supports emotional coping [4]. Reassurance about talquetamab’s favorable overall response rate and progression-free survival (PFS), alongside the generally transient and reversible nature of AEs, is essential.

Patients are ideally informed about potential GPRC5D-related adverse effects prior to initiating treatment and encouraged to promptly report new or worsening symptoms. This counseling is primarily provided by the treating hematologist, with support from the multidisciplinary team where appropriate (nurse or hospital pharmacist). Before treatment start, clinicians should explain the possibility of weight loss resulting from reduced food intake or oral toxicities and emphasize early monitoring and reporting of reduced intake or oral toxicities². Similarly, patients are typically informed about the risk of skin and nail toxicities associated with talquetamab and encouraged to promptly report any new or worsening symptoms.

In addition to the informed consent form, written educational materials (including available patient brochures) can support patient understanding at home. Accessible, patient‑friendly educational resources developed by sponsors, such as illustrated brochures or multilingual videos, are available and may further support understanding.

Nurses in oncology, hematology, and MM play an important supportive role follow-up care. Training on skin and nail toxicities and nutritional support is vital. While trial staff are typically knowledgeable in talquetamab, continued education and clear guidance remain important to support consistent patient care. Comprehensive training across all healthcare settings, from academic centers to regional hospitals, is vital to improve access and optimal patient care.

Discussion

The multidisciplinary approach to managing GPRC5D-related AEs with talquetamab highlights the importance of collaboration among hematologists, nurses, dieticians, and dermatologists. MonumenTAL-1 trial data and expert recommendations are closely aligned with existing literature.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The recommendations were developed through a panel-based process supported by a focused, non-systematic literature review, without formal methodologies such as GRADE or a modified Delphi technique. Consequently, some guidance relies on expert opinion, particularly in areas where evidence is emerging. Although other disciplines such as clinical pharmacists or dermatologists were not part of the interview panel, this reflects the intended focus on professionals with the closest and most continuous patient interaction. Future work could benefit from broader multidisciplinary inclusion. External validity may be influenced by the Belgian healthcare context, where access to specialist care is generally good and patient volumes per clinician are relatively low. Adaptations may be required in settings with different resource levels.

Early patient education is key. Proactively informing patients about potential GPRC5D-associated side effects, particularly oral and dermatologic AEs, promotes engagement, adherence, and timely reporting of symptoms - ultimately improving treatment tolerability and outcomes.

Access to specialized oncology dieticians or dermatologists is not universal, which may create care gaps. Therefore, this guideline provides practical recommendations for nutritional and dermatologic management that can be implemented even in the absence of specialist support. Strengthening and scaling a national network of oncology dieticians could improve nutritional guidance and knowledge sharing across treatment centers. Similarly, while specialist MM teams rapidly build familiarity with talquetamab, experience can vary across broader care settings; targeted training and peer exchange can help ensure consistent supportive care expertise.

Conclusion

Talquetamab, with its novel mechanism of action targeting GPRC5D, represents an important treatment option for RRMM, with a distinct adverse event profile that requires proactive and informed management. In later lines of therapy, where QoL may deteriorate in the absence of effective treatment, maintaining therapy with appropriate supportive care and dose adaptation is critical. This multidisciplinary, Belgium-based set of recommendations complements existing guidance on GPRC5D-related AEs and provides practical support for clinical management, synthesizing existing literature and clinical trial data with multidisciplinary clinical expertise.
