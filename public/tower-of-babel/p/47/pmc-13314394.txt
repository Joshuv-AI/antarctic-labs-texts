Associations between Meal Timing and Frequency and Obesity among Adults in Jeju, Republic of Korea
Authors: Youjeong Jang, Hyunji Ham, Sumin Kim, Hanbin Ko, Insuk Chai, Kyungho Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Irregular eating patterns, including skipping breakfast and/or late-night eating, have gained frequency and may contribute to rising obesity in South Korea. The prevalence of obesity is high, and breakfast consumption is low in Jeju, yet research on meal timing and frequency is limited. This study examined the association between meal timing/frequency and overweight/obesity among adults in Jeju based on the 2022 Jeju Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey data (n=947). Meal timing and frequency were assessed via a 1-d 24-h dietary recall, while late-night eating behaviors were evaluated employing a separate questionnaire. Breakfast consumers were divided into tertiles based on the proportion of total energy intake from breakfast. Compared with breakfast skippers, participants in the highest tertile of the proportion of energy from breakfast had significantly lower odds of overweight/obesity after adjusting for age, sex, total energy intake, and lifestyle and socioeconomic factors (odds ratio, 0.47; 95% confidence interval, 0.24－0.92; P for trend=0.0140). Breakfast skippers consumed more energy at lunch and dinner than breakfast consumers (P<0.0001). Compared with breakfast skippers, breakfast consumers had a lower prevalence of vitamin and mineral intakes below the estimated average requirement and had a higher adherence to the acceptable macronutrient distribution range for fat (P<0.05). Overall, breakfast consumption was positively associated with nutrient adequacy and adherence to appropriate fat intake levels. These findings suggest that breakfast consumption may help maintain healthy nutritional status and is inversely associated with overweight/obesity prevalence among adults in Jeju.

INTRODUCTION

Korea has adhered to a traditional pattern of three meals a day (Ji et al., 2022). However, rapid socioeconomic changes, including an increase in single-person and dual-income households (Jin et al., 2020) and day/night shift work (Clark et al., 2023), have disrupted eating patterns (Jung et al., 2017). Irregular eating patterns have been shown to be associated with a higher risk of metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance (Sierra-Johnson et al., 2008). Based on the 2016－2020 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data suggest that one-third of Korean adults skip at least one meal－usually breakfast－and meal skipping enhances susceptibility to metabolic syndrome and/or its components (Park et al., 2023). The breakfast consumption rate in Korea, defined as the proportion of individuals who consumed breakfast at least five days a week during the preceding year, has steadily declined, from 53.4% in 2019 to 47.0% in 2023 (KDCA, 2024). Skipping breakfast increases the risk of chronic diseases, such as obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular conditions, as well as poor nutritional status, including protein, vitamin, and mineral deficiencies (Jang et al., 2015; Kubota et al., 2016; Jeong et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2020).

In addition, the proportion of late-night eating－meals or snacks－has increased, contributing to breakfast skipping and the elevated prevalence of overweight and obesity (Okada et al., 2019). A study reported that 34% of Korean adults consumed snacks after dinner (Suh et al., 2012), while 48.4% consumed foods after 9 PM (Ha and Song, 2019). Although definitions of late-night eating vary, these findings indicate an increasing trend in late-night eating. Late-night eating is associated with an elevated risk of metabolic syndrome and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol in Korean males (Ha and Song, 2019); its persistence may lead to obesity, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and sleep disorders (Shim and Lee, 2022). According to a recent cross-sectional study, the risk of diabetes in Koreans was higher if the last meal of the day was consumed after 9 PM (Kwak et al., 2023).

Obesity is a worldwide public health concern; an analysis of global underweight and obesity trends from 1990 to 2022 revealed that over a billion people are affected by obesity, with the prevalence among adults more than doubling since 1990 (NCD-RisC, 2024). Obesity elevates the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (Kivimäki et al., 2017), and cancer (Choi et al., 2018). The obesity rate among Korean adults increased from 25.4% in 2014 to 33.7% in 2023, and was the highest in Jeju, the largest island in Korean, with a prevalence of 36.1% in 2023 (KDCA, 2024). Furthermore, the breakfast consumption rate in Jeju was 44.4%, ranked 3rd lowest in Korea (KDCA, 2024).

From a chrononutrition perspective, not only the amount but also the timing and temporal distribution of daily energy intake play a vital role in metabolic regulation. Metabolic efficiency, insulin sensitivity, and diet-induced thermogenesis are generally higher in the morning, suggesting that consuming a greater proportion of daily energy early during the day may be more favorable for body weight (BW) regulation (Peters et al., 2021). In this context, the proportion of energy intake at breakfast has emerged as a relevant indicator of meal timing in relation to obesity-related outcomes. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the association between meal timing and frequency, including breakfast and dinner intake, adherence to a “three-meal-a-day” pattern, and late-night eating, and their relationship with the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adults in Jeju.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This study used data from the 2022 Jeju Nutrition and Physical Activity Survey, which evaluated the dietary and physical activity status of Jeju residents and contributed to the development of health promotion policies. The survey included a health questionnaire, nutrition and physical activity assessments, and body measurements. The study sample comprised ∼1,300 free-living residents of Jeju Province, aged ≥1 year, recruited using probability sampling. The study methods are detailed elsewhere (Ham et al., 2024). Initial subjects were adults aged ≥19 years who participated in a 1-d 24-h dietary recall survey (n=974). After excluding individuals without height and weight data (n=2), those who consumed total energy <500 or >5,000 kcal (n=15), pregnant or lactating females (n=6), and those with unclear residential status (n=4), 947 participants were included in the final analysis. As the 2022 Survey was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jeju National University, Jeju City, South Korea (approval number: JJNU-IRB-2022-040), this study was exempted from further review by the same board (exemption number: JJNU-IRB-2024-077). Informed written consent was obtained from each participant.

Nutritional intake assessment

To evaluate meal timing and frequency, trained staff conducted 1-d 24-h dietary recalls at each participant’s household, assessing total meal frequency, adherence to a three-meal-a-day pattern, breakfast/dinner skipping, and energy intake per meal. The participants self-identified the meal type－breakfast, lunch, dinner, or snack－based on subjective judgment and their daily routines. Trained reviewers analyzed all interview responses to ensure completeness and logical consistency within each participant’s dietary record. The daily meal frequency ranged from 1 to 5. Based on adherence to three regular meals, breakfast, lunch, and dinner, participants were categorized as “consumers” if they consumed all three meals, or “non-consumers” if they skipped at least one meal. In addition, for breakfast and dinner, participants were categorized as ‘skippers’ and ‘consumers.’ Consumers were further classified into tertiles based on energy intake from each meal (kcal/d and % of total energy). Energy intake distribution from three regular meals and macronutrient intake distribution for each meal were also examined. Late-night eating behavior was assessed via a separate structured questionnaire.

Energy and nutrient intakes were evaluated using the 2020 Korean Dietary Reference Intakes to examine whether nutritional status differed per meal timing and frequency. Energy intake was compared with 75% of the estimated energy requirement (EER), and consumption of carbohydrate, protein, vitamin A, thiamin, riboflavin, vitamin C, niacin, calcium, phosphorus, and iron were compared with the estimated average requirements (EARs). The intake of dietary fiber, sodium, and potassium was evaluated based on the adequate intake (AI) levels; cholesterol consumption was assessed considering the intake goals of <300 mg. Additionally, macronutrient intake was ascertained following the acceptable macronutrient distribution range (AMDR).

Dietary behavior assessment

Dietary behaviors over the previous year were evaluated employing the responses to a structured questionnaire. Usual breakfast consumption frequency was categorized as “5－7 times per week,” “3－4 times per week,” “1－2 times per week,” and “less than once a week.” Dinner timing was classified as “before 6 PM,” “6－7 PM,” “7－8 PM,” “8－9 PM,” and “after 9 PM.” Late-night eating consumption frequency was categorized as “5－7 times per week,” “3－4 times per week,” “1－2 times per week,” and “less than once a week.” Consuming late-night meals ≥3 times per week categorized a participant as a consumer (Takeshita et al., 2018; Jaffar et al., 2024). Eating-out frequency was classified as “more than once a day,” “3－6 times per week,” “1－2 times per week,” and “less than once a month.” Vegetable intake frequency was assessed separately for raw and pickled vegetables, categorized as “3 or more times a day,” “1－2 times a day,” “2－6 times a week,” and “once a week or less.” Fruit intake frequency was categorized as “once or more a day,” “2－6 times a week,” and “once a month or less.” Vegetable and fruit intake was also analyzed because these dietary behaviors are known to influence energy intake and BW, and may play a role in the pathogenesis or prevention of overweight and obesity (Ledoux et al., 2011; Mytton et al., 2014).

Anthropometric measurements

Height was measured with an InLabS50 portable stadiometer (InBody Co.), and weighed using an InbodyDial H20N body composition analyzer (InBody Co.). Waist circumference was determined with a standardized tape, Seca 203 (Seca). All measurements were conducted by trained staff who followed standardized protocols. Self-reported data were considered for 28.1% of participants, who refused such measurements. Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated by dividing the BW (kg) by the square of the height (m2). According to the guidelines of the Korean Society of Obesity (Kim et al., 2023), overweight is defined as a BMI of 23.0－24.9 kg/m2, obesity as a BMI ≥25 kg/m2, and abdominal obesity as a waist circumference of ≥90 or 85 cm for males and females, respectively.

Sociodemographic and lifestyle variables

Sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex, residential area, education, household income, marital status, and occupational status. Lifestyle factors included alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, and sleep duration. Education was sub-divided as elementary school or less, middle school, high school, and college graduate or higher. Household income was sub-classified as lower, lower-middle, upper-middle, and upper using quartiles of monthly household income. Marital status was categorized as unmarried, married, separated, widowed, or divorced. Occupational status was classified as daytime worker, shift worker, or other (including the unemployed). Alcohol consumption was defined as “yes” if a participant drank alcoholic beverages more than once per month during the preceding year. Current smoking was defined as “yes” if the participant had smoked more than 100 cigarettes during their lifetime and was currently smoking. Physical activity was defined as “yes” if the participant performed at least 150 min of moderate-intensity activity per week or at least 75 min of vigorous-intensity activity per week. Sleep duration was categorized into less than 7, 7－8, and >8 h (Ock et al., 2008).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute). To reflect the complex sampling design of the survey, strata, clusters, and weights were used in the PROC SURVEY procedures. Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as means±standard error or as numbers (%), respectively. Differences in general characteristics and dietary behaviors or BMI－regarding meal timing and frequency－were examined using a chi-square test and a general linear model, respectively. Multiple logistic regression was performed to estimate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for overweight/obesity and abdominal obesity, with breakfast or dinner skippers as the reference group. Age, sex, total energy intake, alcohol consumption, current smoking, education level, household income, and physical activity were included as confounders. Differences in nutritional status based on breakfast consumption were analyzed with a chi-square test. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P<0.05 considered significant.

RESULTS

Meal timing and frequency according to general characteristics are presented in Table 1. The average age of participants was 49.8 years; 58.6% were females. On average, participants consumed 3.7 meals per day. Meal frequency was greater in females, the elderly, rural inhabitants, married individuals (including separated, widowed, or divorced), and those who consumed alcohol less than once a month (P<0.05). About 64.0% consumed three meals: breakfast, lunch, and dinner. This proportion was higher in older or married (including separated, widowed, or divorced) participants, less educated, and non-smokers (P<0.05). The breakfast skipping rate of the total participants was 28.1%; this rate was significantly higher among participants who were male, younger, unmarried, with higher education and income, monthly alcohol consumers, smokers, and shift workers (P<0.05).

The differences in BMI according to meal type are shown in Table 2. Compared to the BMI of the breakfast energy (kcal/d) Tertile 3 (25.0 kg/m2), that of breakfast skippers (25.8 kg/m2) was significantly higher (P=0.0231). While BMI tended to be lower among those who did not follow a three-meal-a-day pattern, and among breakfast/dinner consumers and those avoiding late-night snacking, these differences were statistically insignificant.

Table 3 presents the association between breakfast and dinner consumption and the odds of overweight/obesity and abdominal obesity. Compared with breakfast skippers, the tertile group with the highest breakfast energy intake had lower odds of overweight/obesity (OR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.27－0.99; P for trend=0.0200). A similar association was observed for the proportion of energy from breakfast in total energy intake (OR for tertile 3, 0.47; 95% CI, 0.24－0.92; P for trend=0.0140). In sex-stratified analyses, females with the greatest proportion of energy from breakfast had 63% lower odds of overweight/obesity (OR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.16－0.87; P for trend=0.0630) and 72% lower odds of abdominal obesity (OR, 0.28; 95% CI, 0.10－0.76; P for trend=0.0978), compared to skippers. No significant association was seen in males and for dinner consumption.

Table 4 presents the energy intake per meal according to the proportion of energy consumed at breakfast. As the proportion of breakfast energy increased, energy intake at all other meals decreased (P<0.0001). Breakfast skippers consumed approximately 270 kcal more at lunch (breakfast skippers: 674.7 kcal, Tertile 3: 400.2 kcal) and about twice the number of calories at dinner (breakfast skippers: 901.2 kcal, Tertile 3: 453.4 kcal) compared to－Tertile 3－the group with the highest proportion of energy derived from breakfast (P<0.0001).

The energy and nutrient intake status according to breakfast consumption is presented in Table 5. The proportion of participants consuming <75% of the EER was greatest among breakfast skippers (51.3%), followed by those in the highest tertile of proportion of energy obtained from breakfast (46.2%) (P<0.0001). As the portion of energy derived from breakfast increased, adherence to the AMDR for carbohydrate and fat also increased (P<0.0001). Compared to breakfast consumers, breakfast skippers exhibited a higher prevalence of consuming less than the EAR and AI levels recommended for dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals (P<0.05). Notably, among breakfast consumers, those in the highest tertile of breakfast-derived energy intake exhibited a higher prevalence of nutrient inadequacies compared to those in the lowest tertile; however, these levels remained generally lower than those observed in breakfast skippers, except for niacin.

Dietary behaviors per breakfast consumption are presented in Table 6. In the breakfast-consumption group, dinner was usually between 6 and 7 PM, while it was between 7 and 8 PM in the breakfast-skipping group (P=0.0028). Additionally, the tertile with the highest proportion of energy obtained from breakfast also comprised the greatest number of participants consuming vegetables ≥3 times per day. In contrast, breakfast skippers were likely to consume fruits less than once a week (P<0.0001 for all).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the associations between meal frequency and timing and overweight/obesity, as well as dietary behaviors among adults in Jeju, Republic of Korea. The results showed significant differences in BMI based on the proportion of energy derived from breakfast, with lower BMI observed in Tertile 3 compared to breakfast skippers. Among all participants, 71.9% and 28.1% consumed or skipped breakfast, respectively. In particular, compared to breakfast skippers, females who obtained most of their energy from breakfast had lower odds of overweight/obesity and abdominal obesity.

This study assessed meal timing and frequency in various ways: consumption of each meal, meal frequency, and late-night eating. However, only breakfast consumption was significantly associated with overweight/obesity. The group in the highest tertile of breakfast intake, measured as kcal/d and the proportion of energy derived from breakfast relative to total energy, had 49% and 53% lower odds of overweight and obesity, respectively, compared to breakfast skippers. In a UK cohort study, an increase in the proportion of daily energy consumed at breakfast was associated with relatively lower weight gain (Purslow et al., 2008). Additionally, among Korean adults, a lower energy intake at breakfast was associated with a higher risk of obesity (Jang et al., 2015). The same association was also identified in evaluations of breakfast consumption status and frequency, suggesting that despite differences in definitions, the results are similar and underscore the importance of breakfast consumption (Odegaard et al., 2013; Goon and Islam, 2014; Rong et al., 2019).

When stratified by sex, males did not display any significant associations; however, females in the tertile with the highest proportion of energy from breakfast had 63% lower odds of overweight and obesity than breakfast skippers. This result is in line with the findings from studies conducted in Japan (Okada et al., 2019) and Bangladesh (Goon and Islam, 2014), which reported an increased risk of obesity among females who skipped breakfast. In contrast, in a cohort study of Japanese males aged 20－49 years, the incidence of being overweight was 1.18 times higher in breakfast skippers than consumers (Kito et al., 2019). This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in study populations, such as the exclusion of individuals with a BMI of ≥25 kg/m2, as well as gender-specific variations in dietary and lifestyle habits across different regions.

These sex-specific associations may stem from biological and lifestyle differences. Women generally exhibit higher insulin sensitivity and more efficient glucose homeostasis, which may enhance their metabolic responsiveness to early-day energy intake (Mauvais-Jarvis, 2015; Tramunt et al., 2020). Conversely, in men, the potential benefits of breakfast might be masked by competing lifestyle factors. In our study population, males exhibited markedly elevated rates of alcohol consumption and current smoking, which are established risk factors for adiposity that could potentially offset the positive effects of regular breakfast consumption (Kim et al., 2016; AlKalbani and Murrin, 2023).

Although this study did not identify any significant association between breakfast consumption and abdominal obesity in the total participants, an inverse association was observed only in females. This finding is consistent with those from Iran, where females aged 18－28 years who consumed breakfast had an abdominal obesity prevalence that was half that of those who skipped breakfast (Azadbakht et al., 2013). Lower breakfast energy intake levels were associated with a reduced risk of abdominal obesity in Korean adults (Jang et al., 2015). The prevalence of abdominal obesity was 1.5 times higher among Spanish adults who skipped breakfast than those who consumed breakfast, results that contrast with those of this study (Navia et al., 2017). However, no significant association was observed between breakfast consumption and waist circumference or waist-to-hip ratio in Puerto Rican adults (Tamez et al., 2020), aligning with the findings of our study.

The findings of our study indicate a multifaceted relationship between breakfast consumption and nutritional adequacy. Consistent with earlier studies (Lee and Song, 2017; Uzhova et al., 2018; Giménez-Legarre et al., 2020; Hwang, 2020), compared to skippers, breakfast consumers generally maintained a better nutritional status, characterized by a lower proportion of individuals with vitamin and mineral intake below the EAR.

However, a closer look at the proportion of energy derived from breakfast reveals a more nuanced association. Beyond the caloric quantity of breakfast, nutritional quality must be considered while interpreting these findings. As the proportion of energy from breakfast declined, the prevalence of individuals consuming carbohydrates below the AMDR and fats exceeding the AMDR increased. This pattern aligned with observations suggesting that skipping or minimizing breakfast is associated with reduced carbohydrate and increased fat consumption (Jang et al., 2015; Hwang, 2020), likely due to compensatory overeating. Indeed, as breakfast-derived energy was reduced, more calories were consumed from lunch, dinner, and afternoon snacks, consistent with findings of prior studies on young Japanese females (Yoshimura et al., 2017) and U.S. adults (Fanelli et al., 2021). This pattern may support a mechanism in which breakfast consumption enhances satiety and reduces the likelihood of excessive energy intake at subsequent meals (Yoshimura et al., 2017; Ruddick-Collins et al., 2022).

Conversely, a high energy contribution of breakfast does not necessarily reflect nutritional adequacy. As indicated in Supplementary Table 1, participants in the highest tertile of breakfast-derived energy proportion (Tertile 3) exhibited a grain-based, high-carbohydrate breakfast pattern. While this effect contributed to greater energy intake at breakfast, it was coupled with restricted dietary diversity. Specifically, a higher intake of grains along with lower consumption of fruits and meats in this group may explain the widespread vitamin and mineral inadequacy. Nevertheless, this high-energy breakfast group consumed the lowest total daily energy and had lower odds of being overweight and obese. Although these results suggest an association between a higher proportion of energy obtained from breakfast and favorable weight management, or a reduced risk of compensatory overeating, further longitudinal studies are required. These findings underscore the importance of balancing caloric quantity and food composition to support overall metabolic health.

This study found that individuals who skip breakfast or consume it less frequently tend to have late-night dinners, dine out more often, and exhibit poor dietary habits, such as inadequate intake of fruits and vegetables. These findings are consistent with previous research reporting that breakfast skipping is related to late-night eating (Kito et al., 2019; Okada et al., 2019), frequent dining out (Koo and Park, 2013), and an enhanced likelihood of inadequate fruit and vegetable intake (Fayet-Moore et al., 2019; Pengpid and Peltzer, 2020). Collectively, these findings underscore that “breakfast skipping” may be a marker of a cluster of unhealthy eating habits that could increase obesity risk. Therefore, to better understand the impact of breakfast on overall nutrient intake, studies are needed to evaluate the qualitative aspects of breakfast, such as food group diversity, timing, and portion size. In addition, longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the long-term effects of breakfast consumption on health.

The common prevalence of breakfast skipping among Jeju inhabitants must be analyzed in the context of this island’s unique socioeconomic and industrial landscape. First, Jeju has the greatest proportion of dual-income households in South Korea (Statistics Korea, 2024), which often limits time available for morning meal preparation. Furthermore, this study found that 51.5% of shift workers skipped breakfast, a rate that was higher than that of non-shift workers. This finding is particularly relevant to Jeju’s industrial structure, which relies on tourism, services, and agriculture. In these sectors, irregular shift schedules and early-morning labor demands often disrupt conventional meal times. Thus, targeted interventions, such as workplace-based programs for shift workers and community-led nutritional support for the agriculture workforce, are essential to address such occupational challenges. Nevertheless, further research is required to explore the multifaceted determinants of breakfast skipping in Jeju.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design makes it difficult to examine causal relationships between meal frequency and type and overweight/obesity. In particular, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be excluded, as overweight or obese individuals may have intentionally modified their eating behaviors, such as skipping meals or altering breakfast intake. Such adjustments may mask or confound the associations between habitual dietary patterns and metabolic outcomes. Second, dietary intake in a single-day 24-h recall may not accurately reflect the usual diet. Third, breakfast was defined based on participants’ responses, and not based on the accurate time of consumption. Fourth, although most anthropometric measurements were direct, 28.1% of the data were self-reported, which may lead to underestimation of obesity; this potential bias was not specifically adjusted for in the present analysis. Lastly, despite adjustment for various confounding variables, potential residual biases may still exist. Despite these drawbacks, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that evaluated meal frequency and timing in various ways and examined its associations with nutritional status, dietary behaviors, and obesity among adults in Jeju, which has the highest obesity rate in South Korea.

These findings suggest that breakfast consumption is related to better nutritional quality, healthier dietary behavior, and lower odds of overweight and obesity among adults in Jeju. Consequently, these results provide foundational data for developing community-based nutritional policies and programs aimed at promoting healthy eating patterns and supporting obesity prevention strategies.
