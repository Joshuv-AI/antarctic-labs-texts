The effects of boriding on kinetic, microstructure and corrosive behavior of Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels
Authors: Hasan Onur Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effects of boriding parameters on kinetics, microstructure, and corrosion behavior of Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels were investigated. Despite their wide use in defense, there is a lack of scientific data on the surface modification of Ramor armor steels. While other steels are well-known, the boron diffusion and bilayer formation in these specific alloys are not yet fully understood. This makes it difficult to predict how boriding affects their performance in harsh conditions. Our study fills this gap by providing a precise kinetic dataset, which allows for regression-based modeling to optimize the boriding process for better durability. The steels were borided at 900 °C, 950 °C, and 1000 °C for 2, 4, and 6 h using pack boriding with Ekabor II powder. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were used to characterize the microstructure and elemental composition of boride layers. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed the presence of FeB and Fe2B phases in the boride layers. A bilayer boride structure, consisting of an outer FeB layer and inner Fe2B layer, was observed in all borided samples. The boride layer thickness increased with boriding temperature and time, following a parabolic growth law. Activation energies for boron diffusion were calculated as 120.54 kJmol-1 and 126.21 kJmol-1 for Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels, respectively. Microhardness measurements showed a gradual decrease from surface to substrate, with maximum values between 1800 and 2000 HV. Electrochemical tests in 3% NaCl solution demonstrated that boriding improved corrosion resistance by forming a dense boride barrier, with no significant differences between the steels. The study provides quantitative insights into optimizing boriding parameters by achieving activation energies of 120.54–126.21 kJmol-1 surface hardness up to 2000 HV, and improved corrosion resistance in 3% NaCl solution for Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels widely used in military and industrial applications.Keywords: Boriding, Diffusion kinetics, Ramor 500, Ramor 550, CorrosionSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Boriding is an advanced chemical heat-treatment technique that significantly enhances the surface properties of metallic materials. Its versatility and effectiveness have led to widespread use across various industries for treating components that require improved wear and corrosion resistance. This treatment is particularly beneficial for materials such as steels, titanium alloys and aluminum alloys1–5. The process involves exposing the material to a boron-rich medium at a specific temperature, either through heating or electrolysis. During this process, highly reactive boron atoms diffuse deeply into the substrate, creating a durable boride layer that consists of one or more intermetallic compound phases1,6,7, such as FeB and Fe2B, providing an alternative method for enhancing the mechanical properties of commercial steels8–10. This boride layer significantly increases the hardness and wear resistance of the substrate, resulting in an extended operational lifespan for the treated components9,11–20. Consequently, boriding has become an essential technique for boosting the performance and durability of critical components in numerous applications, ranging from the automotive and aerospace industries to the manufacturing and energy sectors.

Boriding treatment is widely employed to improve augment the surface hardness, wear characteristics, and corrosion resistance of various metallic substrates. Among the various boriding processes, pack-boriding is extensively employed due to its cost-effectiveness, simplicity, and non-toxic nature11–14. Moreover, this process does not require complex machinery. Consequently, boriding serves as an effective surface treatment to protect the steel from wear and corrosion. Studies indicate that boriding enhances the corrosion resistance of AISI 304 steel in acidic environments by roughly 4.3 times15. Another study indicated that the boriding of steels and ferrous alloys is a distinctive surface treatment for enhancing the wear and corrosion resistance in large, long, and complex-shaped tubular components within the petroleum chemical industry16. According to Medvedovski and Antonov17 orided tubing and equipment have been shown to perform successfully under mineral processing, downhole oil production, and other engineering application conditions. Additionally, boriding significantly improves the corrosion-erosion resistance of ferrous materials in weakly acidic solutions and alkaline media, regardless of the evaluation method used (i.e., immersion corrosion testing or polarization method). The corrosion behavior of borided steels in acidic or alkaline media is mainly influenced by the boride layer characteristics, including its porosity and thickness, in addition to the steel’s chemical composition.

Ramor armor steels were primarily developed for military applications, specifically for defense and attack purposes, such as in armored vehicles where blast protection or high ballistic resistance is paramount. The superior mechanical properties of Ramor 500 and 550—specifically their refined grain structure, hardness, and toughness—are attributed to the thermomechanical processing involving hot rolling and specialized quenching stages18. The exceptional ballistic properties of Ramor steels are attributed to their hardness and strength18,19. The preference of Ramor steels in armor applications, such as in military hospitals and armored vehicles where explosion protection or high ballistic resistance is critical, is due to its low cost, ease of fabrication and supply, high strength, and hardness coupled with ductility18,20–22. An experimental and numerical investigation was conducted to evaluate the ballistic performance of Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 armor steel plates under various conditions20. It was determined that using Ramor 550 as a facing plate and Ramor 500 as a backing plate provided superior ballistic resistance compared to the reverse layering order, with the primary failure modes being plugging and bulging20. Ramor 500 armor steel may be favored over Ramor 550 due to its ease of machining and lower cost. However, Ramor 550 offers a weight advantage by achieving the same level of ballistic protection at a reduced plate thickness18. Additionally, these steels are employed in the automotive industry for vehicle lighting applications. For instance, equipment installed in a concrete mixer can be manufactured using Ramor 500 steel with a thinner plate than structural steel, resulting in enhanced dynamic efficiency and fuel savings23. Furthermore, Ramor steels are utilized in various security and structural applications, including doors, window frames, bank counters and vaults, currency exchange booths, security containers, shooting range equipment, and naval vessels18.

As mentioned above, the utilization of Ramor steels across various industries is progressively increasing due to their superior mechanical properties. Extending the service life of components is of paramount importance in these sectors. The objective of the present study is to examine the effect of boriding time and temperature on Ramor steels and to ascertain whether boriding enhances the corrosion resistance of Ramor steels under the aforementioned harsh service conditions.

The surfaces of the test specimens were meticulously polished using a series of SiC abrasive papers with grit sizes of 80, 150, 360, 500, 800, 1200, and 2000, respectively, to eliminate any oxides and residues. Post-sanding, the samples were initially cleansed with acetone to remove residual oil and surface impurities. The boriding process was conducted utilizing commercial Ekabor II powder. The specimens, along with the powder, were placed in a cylindrical stainless steel crucible. The furnace was preheated to temperatures of 900 °C, 950 °C, and 1000 °C prior to the placement of the crucibles. The samples underwent boriding for durations of 2, 4, and 6 h in the preheated furnace, followed by cooling to ambient temperature. The sample designations are detailed in Table 3. Although nine boriding treatments were executed, only four samples (S1, S3, S7, and S9) were selected for characterization. Following the boriding process, the cross-sectional areas of the samples exhibiting the borided layer were polished with SiC abrasive papers and 3 μm and 1 μm diamond suspensions, and subsequently etched with a 5% natal etchant for microstructural examination. The etched samples were scrutinized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Thermo Fischer Versa 3D) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV to ascertain the thickness of the boride layer. Furthermore, the elemental compositions of the samples were analyzed via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS/EDX). The phase structures of the borided samples were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (GNR Europe 600 XRD) over a range of 30°–90°. The XRD analyses were conducted with parameters of 40 kV and 15 mA, employing CuKα radiation with a wavelength of 1.542 Å to identify the phases. Microhardness measurements were performed using a Vickers microhardness tester (Laihua LH-MHV-1000) under a load of 100 g for 10 s at intervals of 20 μm. A minimum of five measurements were taken, and the average value was calculated.

The corrosion behavior of the borided Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steel samples was evaluated through electrochemical methods in a 3 wt% NaCl aqueous solution, simulating seawater conditions. The electrolyte was prepared using NaCl and deionized water and was used at room temperature under naturally aerated conditions. Prior to testing, the samples were rinsed with deionized water, cleaned in ethanol and dried in air to remove any surface contaminants that could affect the electrochemical response.

Electrochemical measurements were performed using a conventional three-electrode cell configuration (Ametek VERSASTAT 3, USA) in a glass beaker. The borided steel sample served as the working electrode, a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode (3 M KCl) was used as the reference electrode, and a Ti-coated Ru mesh acted as the counter electrode. Electrical contact to the working electrode was established through a copper wire, and the exposed surface area of each sample was fixed at 1 cm² by masking the remaining regions with an inert insulating coating. Before polarization measurements, the samples were immersed in the electrolyte and allowed to stabilize at the open-circuit potential (OCP) for approximately 20 min to reach a quasi-steady electrochemical state.

Potentiodynamic polarization scans were then performed starting from − 0.3 V below the OCP and extending to + 0.5 V at a controlled scan rate. During testing, the current response as a function of applied potential was continuously recorded. The obtained polarization curves exhibited distinct anodic and cathodic branches. The linear regions of the Tafel plots were extrapolated to determine key corrosion parameters, including the corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (icorr), which were used to evaluate the corrosion behavior and relative corrosion resistance of the borided samples.

Results and discussion

Figures 1 and 2 show the etched cross-sectional microstructures of boride layers formed on the surfaces of Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels at different temperatures and for various treatment times respectively. The images clearly show the formation of a continuous FeB/Fe₂B bilayer structure, consisting of an outer FeB layer and an inner Fe₂B layer with a characteristic saw-tooth morphology at the layer–substrate interface. The increase in boride layer thickness with increasing boriding temperature and treatment time is evident, indicating diffusion-controlled growth behavior. Figures 3 and 4 shows the cross-sectional SEM images of the borided samples along with the EDS analysis results. The maps confirm a high boron concentration within the boride layer region, with a gradual decrease toward the substrate, supporting the formation of a diffusion-controlled FeB/Fe₂B bilayer structure. As illustrated in Figs. 1, 2 and 3, the boride layers exhibit a continuous FeB/Fe₂B bilayer structure whose thickness and morphology depend on boriding temperature and time. The thickness of this boride layer, along with the adjacent transition zone, is directly controlled by the key process parameters of time and temperature27. The morphology of the boride layers formed on the steel surfaces exhibits a characteristic tooth-like structure, similar to that observed in low-alloy steels28–30. Consistent with previous studies, the observed layers originate from a preferred diffusion direction31–34.

The formation of boride layers during the boriding of Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels is mainly controlled by the boron potential at the steel surface and a diffusion-controlled growth mechanism. The formation and growth of boride layers on Ramor 500 and 550 steels follow the “diffusion channel theory” as proposed by Kunst and Schaaber35. In this thermochemical process, active boron atoms first diffuse into the steel surface, with the diffusion rate being only slightly affected by the lattice structure (FCC or BCC). The growth of the boride layer follows Fick’s diffusion laws, which explains the experimentally observed parabolic relationship between layer thickness and treatment time. When the boron concentration at the surface reaches a critical level (approx. 8.83 wt.%B), Fe₂B nuclei form first at the substrate interface due to its lower boron requirement and higher thermodynamic stability. As the boron potential increases with temperature and treatment time (approximately 16.23 wt% B), FeB nucleates on the outer surface, resulting in the characteristic FeB/Fe₂B bilayer structure observed in this study.

The characteristic saw-tooth or needle-like morphology of the boride layers is related to crystallographic orientation and preferential growth directions, with pronounced toothing commonly observed in low-carbon steels36. Boron atoms migrate toward the interior of the substrate primarily through the [001] crystallographic direction of the tetragonal Fe2B crystals. Boron diffusion mainly occurs along the [001] crystallographic direction of tetragonal Fe₂B crystals, which results in the observed saw-tooth morphology in the SEM images of Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steels. The presence of alloying elements in Ramor steels (such as Cr, Ni, and Mo) significantly influences the diffusion rate. These elements tend to accumulate at the boride/substrate interface, acting as a diffusion barrier13,37. This explains why Ramor 550, with its specific alloy composition, exhibited a slightly higher activation energy (126.21 kJmol-1) compared to Ramor 500 (120.54 kJmol-1).

The present results are consistent with the literature, in which the formation of saw-tooth boride layers on steel surfaces has been explained in detail31,38–42. Microcracks and porosity were identified within specific regions of the boride layers. The evolution of these surface pores is primarily linked to several factors: atmospheric oxidation during treatment, the Kirkendall effect and high boron activity—mechanisms that are extensively discussed in the literature43,44. Moreover, the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients at the layer-substrate interface promotes the accumulation of thermal stresses, thereby triggering crack formation within the boride layer44,45. The rapid cooling of specimens to ambient temperature following boriding may promote microcrack formation.

The formation of the FeB phase requires the boron concentration in the outer Fe2B layer to exceed a critical threshold. This condition is governed by: (1) the initial boron potential in the boriding agent, and (2) the progressive enrichment of boron in the medium as diffusion resistance increases during processing. Notably, elevated concentrations of alloying elements in the substrate material further impede boron diffusion44. As boron diffusion into the substrate decelerates, the resulting accumulation of boron in the boriding medium promotes FeB layer formation atop the Fe2B layer43,44. This phenomenon enhanced by two interdependent factors: (1) increasing boriding temperatures and (2) extended process durations, which collectively contribute to greater boride layer thickness while simultaneously impeding boron diffusion. The consequent rise in surface boron concentration drives the transformation of Fe2B to FeB through additional boron incorporation at the phase interface.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the steels presented in Fig. 5. XRD analysis was conducted to elucidate the phase composition of the boride layers in the treated samples. The identified phases, whether mono or multiphase, are crucial in determining the material’s performance characteristics, particularly its tribological behavior44, adhesion properties, and mechanical response44,46. Microstructural characterization indicated that the boride layers predominantly consisted of two distinct intermetallic phases, FeB and Fe2B, which formed through the thermally activated diffusion of boron atoms from the boriding medium into the metallic substrate. This phase evolution was strongly influenced by both the processing temperature and duration. Cross-sectional analysis revealed a distinct phase distribution: the FeB phase preferentially formed in the near-surface region due to the higher boron concentration, whereas the Fe2B phase developed at the interface between the FeB layer and the steel matrix. With increasing boriding time and temperature, a decrease in the intensity of the Fe₂B phase and a corresponding increase in the FeB phase intensity were observed. These results are in good agreement with the micrographs and previously reported studies28–30,47. The analysis revealed no diffraction peaks corresponding to secondary phases. This absence suggests that any minor phases present either had peaks overlapping with the dominant boride phases or intensities below the detection limit. This lack of distinct secondary phases can likely be attributed to a high-entropy effect within the alloy system48–50.

The temperature and duration of the boriding process are pivotal kinetic parameters influencing the diffusion mechanism. The growth kinetics of the boride layers are dictated by the diffusion of boron into the substrate27,51–54. Figure 6 illustrates the boride layer thicknesses in relation to the boriding parameters. To ensure measurement accuracy, it is imperative to conduct comprehensive characterization involving multiple measurements across various regions of the layers. Kaouka et al.55 advocate for the acquisition of at least 20 measurements distributed throughout the boride layers. Statistical analysis of the mean thickness values reveals a clear correlation between layer growth and processing parameters, with both elevated temperatures and prolonged durations resulting in thicker boride layers. An increase in both boriding temperature and duration results in a thicker boride layer. This behavior can be explained by Fick’s laws of diffusion and the parabolic growth law, as reported in the literature47,56,57. According to this law, the growth of the boride layer follows a parabolic relationship (x2 = kt), indicating that the number of atoms diffusing into the material increases with diffusion time, while the layer thickness grows proportionally to the square root of the boriding time58,59. The thickness of the boride layer demonstrates a parabolic variation with process time. The subsurface penetration of boron atoms into the metallic matrix was successfully established; notably, the elemental profile of the substrate was found to significantly moderate the growth kinetics and final thickness of the boride layer. Figure 9 presents the microhardness profiles of samples S1, S3, S7, and S9 as a function of depth from the surface. The boride layer microstructure typically consists of an outer FeB layer and an inner Fe₂B layer. Due to the higher hardness of FeB compared to Fe2B, the microhardness values are elevated near the surface and exhibit a gradual decline with increasing depth. This trend reflects the phase distribution, as the hardness profile is governed by (1) the relative proportions of FeB and Fe2B phases and (2) the total thickness of the boride layer. Although the FeB phase possesses superior hardness, it is also inherently more brittle. Consequently, the formation of a boride layer predominantly composed of Fe₂B is generally preferred for achieving a more favorable combination of hardness and toughness. It is evident that both increased boriding time and temperature contribute to enhanced hardness values across the boride layer. Consistent with findings in the literature, the boriding process typically yields boride layers with surface microhardness values ranging between 1200 and 2000 HV, depending on the treatment parameters and substrate characteristics1,11,12,44,60–62.

The corrosion resistance of the prepared Ramor 500 and Ramor 550 steel samples was investigated to evaluate their stability in simulated seawater conditions. Electrochemical measurements were carried out in 3% NaCl solution, which mimics a marine environment commonly used for accelerated corrosion testing of structural and protective materials. Figure 10 presents the Tafel polarization curves for both samples. The potentiodynamic scans were initiated at a potential approximately 0.3 V below the open-circuit potential (OCP) and extended up to 0.5 V above the reference potential (against Ag-AgCl reference electrode) to capture the anodic and cathodic responses.

Linear sweep voltammetry curves of borided (a) Ramor 500 and (b) Ramor 550 steel samples in 3% NaCl solution.

The Tafel plots display the logarithmic relationship between current density and applied potential, where the linear portions of the anodic and cathodic branches are extrapolated to their intersection to determine the corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (icorr). The corrosion rate can be calculated directly from icorr using Faraday’s law and standard electrochemical conversion factors, providing a quantitative measure of material degradation in the environment.

Once the samples are exposed to the NaCl solution, the corrosion process is initiated by electrochemical reactions at the interface. The cathodic reaction involves the reduction of oxygen (O2+2H2​O + 4e–→4OH–), while the anodic reaction is dominated by the dissolution of iron (Fe→Fe2++2e–). In addition to the primary anodic and cathodic reactions, intermediate processes such as the formation of Fe(II) hydroxide (Fe+2H2O→Fe(OH)2 + 2e– + 2 H+), may occur on the steel surface in NaCl solution63. This hydroxide could oxidize to form iron oxides (Fe(OH)2 → Fe2O3 + H2O + 2e⁻ + 2 H⁺), contributing to the development of corrosion products64.

Upon immersion in the NaCl solution, no dramatic surface degradation or localized corrosion features were visually detected over the testing period, indicating that the borided surfaces provided a degree of inherent protection. Potentiodynamic polarization analysis revealed that the corrosion potentials and icorr values of the differently treated samples were nearly identical. This similarity suggests that the boriding treatment dominated the electrochemical response, resulting in comparable corrosion behaviors regardless of minor variations in bulk properties or microstructure between Ramor 500 and Ramor 550.

The relatively unchanged corrosion performance can be explained by the formation of a dense borided surface layer, which acts as an effective barrier to chloride ion penetration and inhibits the initiation of localized attack. Since electrochemical corrosion processes are primarily governed by surface characteristics rather than the bulk material, the presence of a uniform boride layer minimizes the exposure of the steel substrate to the corrosive environment. This surface modification not only reduces the active sites available for pitting but also promotes a more uniform dissolution pattern, thereby decreasing the likelihood of localized damage.

The borided Ramor steels exhibited stable corrosion behavior in simulated seawater, with no significant differences between the treatments. These findings highlight the potential of boriding treatments to enhance surface durability and suggest that the prepared materials are suitable for applications where prolonged exposure to marine or chloride-rich environments is anticipated.

The comparison in Table 5 shows that Ramor 500 and 550 steels have lower activation energies (120.54 and 126.21 kJmol− 1) than high-alloy steels like AISI 304 (234.641 kJmol− 1) and AISI H13 (186.2 kJmol− 1). This is because Ramor steels contain fewer alloying elements (like high Cr) that normally block boron diffusion. The FeB/Fe2B bilayer structure in Ramor steels has a characteristic sawtooth morphology. This morphology provides a strong mechanical bond between the coating and the base steel. Even though the activation energy is lower, the presence of the hard FeB phase on the outer surface allows Ramor steels to reach very high hardness levels (> 2000 HV), matching or even exceeding the hardness of more expensive tool steels. While high-alloy steels show slower growth due to their complex chemical composition, the boride layer in Ramor steels grows more efficiently, creating a dense and thick protective barrier that enhances surface performance.

Conclusions

In this study, the boriding kinetics, microstructural evolution, and corrosion performance of Ramor 500 and 550 armor steels were comprehensively analyzed. The main conclusions and their broader implications are summarized as follows:

For the first time, the boron diffusion kinetics and bilayer (FeB/Fe₂B) formation mechanisms were quantified specifically for Ramor 500 and 550 steels. It was scientifically established that despite their complex alloy design, these steels exhibit lower activation energies (120.54–126.21 kJmol-1) compared to high-alloy tool steels, enabling efficient surface hardening without sacrificing the bulk properties of the armor.

The boriding process increased the surface hardness by 4 to 5 times, reaching up to 2000 HV. The growth of the boride layer followed a predictable mathematical model which helps in choosing the right temperature and time for production. Boriding significantly improved the corrosion resistance of these steels in 3% NaCl solution. The dense boride layer acts as a strong protective shield against environmental damage.

These results are directly applicable to the protection of armored vehicle parts, such as hull plates, hatches, and naval defense components, where both extreme hardness and saltwater resistance are required. Future research should investigate how boriding affects the ballistic impact resistance and crack behavior of these steels under high-velocity combat conditions.
