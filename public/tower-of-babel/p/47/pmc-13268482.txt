A bibliometric analysis of cardioembolic stroke utilizing HistCite Pro, CiteSpace, and VOSviewer
Authors: Dandan Liu, Changyong Fu, Shiyao Zhang, Fuling Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cardioembolic stroke (CES) often results in poorer outcomes than other ischemic stroke subtypes. Recent years have seen a marked increase in the scientific output of researchers, highlighting the need for a comprehensive bibliometric analysis to clarify the developmental trends, key research hotspots, and primary contributors in this research field.To perform a bibliometric analysis of CES utilizing HistCite Pro, CiteSpace, and VOSviewer.Methods:Data were sourced from the Web of Science Core Collection, encompassing publications from January 1, 2011, to January 29, 2024. Publication characteristics were analyzed using HistCite Pro and the Web of Science, whereas VOSviewer and CiteSpace facilitated keyword and collaborative network analyses.Results:In total, 5498 publications were identified, originating from 7916 institutions across 112 countries/regions, published in 897 journals, and authored by 29,620 researchers. The annual number of publications surged from 2014 to 2022, with the United States and the Journal of Stroke & Cerebrovascular Diseases leading in contributions. The keyword network revealed 6 key clusters, emphasizing biomarkers, causes of CES, and randomized clinical trials evaluating direct oral anticoagulants for treating embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS), CES, and cryptogenic stroke. Recent hotspots included direct oral anticoagulants, ESUS, Mendelian randomization, instruments, and atrial cardiopathy.Conclusion:Research on CES has progressed rapidly, and research development and collaboration frequently occur across countries, regions, and institutions. Further strengthening these partnerships may advance the field. The recent focus on ESUS, Mendelian randomization, and atrial cardiopathy highlights the need for refined etiological phenotyping and individualized secondary prevention.

1. Introduction

Stroke ranks as the second most common cause of death and the third most prevalent cause of disability globally.[1,2] Cardioembolic stroke (CES) accounts for about 25% of all ischemic strokes (ISs), which generally have worse outcomes and greater severity compared with strokes caused by other factors.[3] The complexity of CES highlights the importance of searching for biomarkers in both clinical practice and research. CES is primarily caused by atrial fibrillation (AF),[4] though other rare causes, such as atrial cardiomyopathy and aortic arch atheroma, can also lead to embolic strokes.[5] The use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) is effective in preventing strokes in patients with AF,[6] yet the necessity of anticoagulant therapy for patients with CES without AF is still uncertain.

In the management of stroke, a critical challenge exists in identifying the etiology of stroke. Despite a non-CES diagnosis, patients still have the potential for future cardioembolic events. Strokes with unknown causes, such as cryptogenic stroke (CS), as well as embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS), pose a significant challenge to global healthcare systems.[7] CS and ESUS are distinct, as CS may involve patients with various causes or incomplete diagnostic assessments.[8] This difference emphasizes the need for advanced diagnostic techniques to identify hidden etiologies. The diagnosis and treatment of CES are urgent clinical issues that require attention. Therefore, adopting innovative testing methods and advanced technologies in clinical practice and research is crucial for effectively screening for and treating the causes of CES. Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as a tool to examine causal relationships between modifiable factors and health outcomes.[9] Thus, using MR to uncover the causes of CS or ESUS can aid in identifying patients for whom anticoagulant therapy may be beneficial.

Bibliometrics analyzes publications quantitatively using methods based on mathematics and statistics.[10] In this method, development trends and distribution structures are evaluated, as well as interrelationships within a particular area, by analyzing publication characteristics. This includes publications, authorship, and journal output.[11] The purpose of this study was to apply bibliometric analysis to comprehensively analyze CES research and summarize the current state of the field. This study also identified key hotspots and trends to inform future research directions.

2. Methods

2.1. Sources of data and search strategy

This study utilized publications obtained from the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), spanning from January 1, 2011, to January 29, 2024. Our search strategy was developed together with a review of previously identified relevant literature. We employed the following keywords: Topic Search = (cardioembolic stroke) OR (cardioembolic strokes) OR (stroke cardioembolic) OR (strokes cardioembolic) OR (cardio-embolic stroke) OR (cardio embolic stroke) OR (cardio-embolic strokes) OR (stroke cardio-embolic) OR (strokes cardio-embolic) OR (embolic stroke) OR (embolic strokes) OR (stroke embolic) OR (strokes embolic). Articles and reviews were the only document types included. The language was limited to English. After the initial search, 2 researchers independently screened the literature for relevance, consulting a third investigator in cases of disagreement.

2.3. Analyses of data

Several external characteristics were summarized using the Web of Science database analysis function. These included the number of publications and the average number of citations. We downloaded the literature records and cited references in text format and renamed them as download_*.txt. Keywords with similar meaning with different spellings were consolidated (for instance, “cardioembolic” and “cardioembolic stroke”), whereas general and irrelevant keywords were removed (for instance, “non-cardioembolic stroke”). Data cleaning was followed by internal structure analysis using the software mentioned above.

3. Ethical consideration

Data used in the study were obtained from the WoSCC, with no involvement of patients or the public.

4. Results

4.1. Evaluation of publication outputs and their corresponding local citation scores

After data cleaning, 5498 of the 6763 papers retrieved from the WoSCC were included in the analysis, representing 81.30% of the total. The process of literature screening and research framework development is illustrated in Figure 1, whereas the number of publications per year is depicted in Figure 2. Research related to CES increased steadily, rising from 282 papers in 2011 to 555 in 2022, with a cumulative total of 4536 publications between 2014 and 2022. However, the total local citation score (TLCS) exhibited variability, as more recent publications generally receive lower citation counts. A higher TLCS indicates greater significance within this field. Three peaks in the TLCS were observed during the study period (in 2014, 2017, and 2019), suggesting that these years were pivotal for seminal contributions to the literature.

Publication output and the TLCS over time. TLCS = total local citation score.

Our findings suggest a link between the TLCS peaks and the publication of influential literature. In 2014, the concept of ESUS was first introduced by Hart et al,[16] who described it as a brain infarction that does not appear to cause lacunar or vascular stenosis in the proximal artery or identifiable sources of cardioembolism, highlighting a need for anticoagulation. They proposed that future randomized controlled trials (RCTs) should evaluate the efficacy of DOACs for the secondary prevention of ESUS.

In 2017, Hart et al[17] performed an analysis of systematic reviews evaluating the efficacy of anticoagulation compared with antiplatelet therapy for secondary stroke prevention in patients with ESUS. An analysis of 5 studies involving 1605 patients with ESUS showed that 86% received antiplatelet therapy. Over a mean follow-up period of 2.7 years, recurrent strokes occurred at a rate of 4.5% per year. The authors emphasized the necessity of improving antithrombotic prophylaxis for patients with ESUS.

In 2019, Diener et al[18] performed a multicenter, randomized, double-blind trial evaluating dabigatran’s effectiveness in preventing recurrent stroke after ESUS. The results indicated that there was no difference between dabigatran and aspirin in terms of preventing recurrent stroke. Both groups experienced similar rates of major bleeding; however, dabigatran was associated with a greater percentage of clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding events.

4.6. Keyword co-occurrence and burst detection

Keywords are crucial for revealing the structure of academic knowledge in bibliometrics, enabling the identification of emerging research trends.[21] This study analyzed 5498 papers, focusing on keywords that appeared at least 4 times. The red cluster (cluster 1) corresponds to the biomarkers of CES, featuring keywords such as “cardioembolic stroke,” “biomarker,” “risk factors,” and “brain natriuretic peptide.” Cluster 2 (green) focuses on aortic arch atheroma, encompassing keywords such as “aortic arch,” “atheroma,” “plaque,” and “atherosclerosis.” Cluster 3 (blue) focuses on CS and ESUS, including words such as “cryptogenic stroke,” “undetermined source,” “atrial fibrillation,” and “patent foramen ovale.” Clusters 2 and 3 are collectively involved in the causes of CES. Cluster 4 (yellow) discusses anticoagulation treatments for CES, highlighting keywords such as “dabigatran,” “rivaroxaban,” “anticoagulants,” “warfarin,” and “aspirin.” Cluster 5 (violet) examines bleeding outcomes in CES, with essential terms such as “hemorrhagic transformation,” “intracerebral hemorrhage,” and “hemorrhage.” Cluster 6 (light blue) analyzes outcomes related to CES, emphasizing keywords such as “outcomes,” “complications,” “predictors,” and “high-risk patients.” Together, these 6 clusters encompass the core academic literature on CES, with clusters 4, 5, and 6 specifically addressing treatment-related topics.

Keywords that undergo sudden increases in usage and are frequently cited within a short period are termed burst keywords.[22] These keywords identified by CiteSpace serve as important indicators of emerging research hotspots. Figure 6 presents the top 25 burst keywords between 2011 and 2024, with the red line representing the outbreak period for each keyword. “Embolic stroke of undetermined source” recorded the highest burst strength (28.21), while “thrombolytic therapy” and “paradoxical embolism” demonstrated the longest burst duration, lasting 6 years. The strong burst of “embolic stroke of undetermined source” is clinically meaningful because it reflects the persistent challenge of assigning a precise embolic source in many patients with IS. Rather than treating ESUS as a homogeneous entity, recent studies increasingly emphasize refined phenotyping to distinguish occult cardioembolism from other mechanisms. This trend suggests that future clinical research should integrate prolonged rhythm monitoring, atrial cardiopathy markers, neurovascular imaging, and circulating biomarkers to improve risk stratification and guide individualized secondary prevention.

5. Discussion

5.1. Principal findings

Our study revealed a gradual increase in research on CES over the past 14 years, indicating rapid development and significant research activity. However, this progress has been uneven across countries and regions, with limited collaboration in less developed areas. Such disparities may be linked to the levels of economic development and the size of the aging population in each country. The World Stroke Organization projected that stroke-related deaths are expected to increase by 50%, from 6.6 million in 2020 to 9.7 million by 2050.[23] CES is an important subtype, with AF as a common risk factor, exhibiting a global prevalence of 60 million people.[24] Additionally, cardioembolic etiology accounts for approximately 25% of IS cases.[25] Accurate diagnosis of CES is essential, as secondary prevention strategies differ from those for other stroke subtypes.[26]

This study sought to explore the research hotspots and development trends of CES. Our examination of highly cited studies highlighted key research focuses: identifying biomarkers of CES, understanding its causes, and investigating the use of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants in patients with ESUS. These findings were further validated through cluster analysis of keywords and visualized in a keyword burst map.

5.2. Research on biomarkers of CES is a research hotspot in this area

Several parameters are associated with biomarkers of CES. These parameters include brain imaging, electrocardiograms, echocardiograms, advanced cardiac imaging, and blood markers. In addition to these parameters, rapid bedside neurovascular evaluations are crucial for stroke management. Ağaçkiran et al reported that transcranial Doppler ultrasonography is a fast, noninvasive, and reproducible method in the emergency department, demonstrating a high negative predictive value of 96.5% for excluding acute middle cerebral artery occlusions, which may aid rapid etiology classification.[27] Their primary applications include risk stratification and rapid etiology classification, thereby enhancing treatment strategies. Additionally, these biomarkers strengthen the identification of causal relationships with specific etiologies. In cases of undetermined stroke etiology, biomarkers can justify more extensive evaluations, such as prolonged cardiac monitoring. Certain biomarkers have also been incorporated into randomized secondary prevention trials for high-risk CES populations, including patients with presumed atrial myopathy, indicating their potential to inform future secondary stroke prevention decisions. The potential biomarkers for CES are categorized by different mechanisms, such as markers of myocardial stress, which include B-type natriuretic peptide, N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide,[28,29] midregional proatrial natriuretic peptide,[30,31] cardiac troponin,[32–34] and creatine kinase-MB[35] that are valuable for identifying candidates for anticoagulation and prolonged cardiac monitoring. Markers of inflammation: Key indicators include C-reactive protein,[36] interleukin-1 beta,[37] and tumor necrosis factor alpha.[37,38] Markers of coagulation: Notable markers consist of D-dimer,[39–43] fibrin monomer complex,[40] soluble fibrin,[40] fibrin degradation products,[40] and soluble thrombomodulin.[44] Markers of endothelial dysfunction: This category includes the receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) and its soluble forms.[39] Echocardiographic findings include P-terminal force in lead V1,[45,46] interatrial block,[47] and excessive supraventricular ectopic activity.[48] Electrocardiographic findings include left atrial volume index.[49–51] Advanced cardiac imaging techniques include left atrial strain[52] and delayed-enhancement magnetic resonance imaging.[53]

5.3. Research on the causes of CES is another hotspot in this area

Currently, the etiology of CES cannot be determined by a gold standard. TOAST (Trial of Organization 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) is the most widely used classification system, established in the 1990s.[54] According to this system, approximately 30% of ISs are classified as “of undetermined source,” commonly termed CS.[55] A major challenge is that the etiology of stroke changes over time. Later, a new proposal, ESUS, introduced in 2014, represents a notable advancement in classifying IS etiology.[16] While the CS includes 3 distinct patient groups: patients with incomplete diagnostic evaluations, patients with multiple potential stroke causes, and patients who have completed the recommended diagnostic work-up without identifying a specific cause. Therefore, ESUS is not synonymous with CS.[8] ESUS accounts for about 17% of IS cases, predominantly affecting younger patients with mild strokes, and has a recurrence rate of 4% to 5% each year.[17,56]

Atrial cardiopathy may precede AF or independently contribute to the formation of thrombi in the atrium and the subsequent stroke.[57–59] Atrial cardiopathy is often diagnosed using various biomarkers, despite the lack of fully established diagnostic criteria. Indicators of atrial disease include echocardiograms showing an enlarged left atrium, electrocardiograms demonstrating increased P-wave terminal force in lead V1, and elevated levels of N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide in the serum, all of which are associated with an increased risk of stroke and are promising biomarkers of atrial cardiopathy.[57,60] It is important to consider atrial cardiopathy as a mechanism underlying ESUS. The ARCADIA trial specifically examined ESUS patients with atrial cardiopathy, offering insights into an important therapeutic consideration for stroke.[61]

Promptly diagnosing the underlying cause is critical for improving secondary prevention strategies in CES. Diseases such as AF and aortic arch atheroma are key causes.[62] Aortic arch atheromas with large or mobile segments have been linked to IS through artery-to-artery embolism.[63] Atheromas larger than 4 mm are associated with an increased risk of initial as well as recurrent strokes,[64] especially in patients with ascending, mobile, or ulcerated atheromas, and are more frequently observed in CSs.[65] This highlights the potential causal role of aortic arch pathology in stroke, which should be thoroughly evaluated in stroke patients with unexplained causes. Aortic arch atherosclerosis not only poses a significant stroke risk but also provides opportunities for targeted therapeutic interventions and preventive strategies.

In recent years, MR has become a prominent research tool for exploring the relationships between risk factors and stroke incidence. For example, greater adult height is causally linked to an increased risk of CES while correlating with a lower risk of other IS subtypes.[66] Additionally, a 2023 study by Wang et al using a two-sample MR approach indicated that rises in plasma prekallikrein levels were associated with stroke risk, including CES, highlighting prekallikrein’s potential role in stroke development.[67] MR analyses also suggest that systemic iron status, as a modifiable risk factor, adversely affects IS risk, particularly CES.[68] A two-sample MR study by Georgakis et al indicated that monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 levels are strongly related to IS risk, particularly large artery stroke and CES.[69] Furthermore, a systematic MR screening by Chong et al identified scavenger receptor class A member 5 as a promising therapeutic target in CES, without predicted adverse effects.[70] Overall, MR serves as a valuable method for utilizing genetic variants to establish causal relationships in observational studies,[71,72] aiding in the identification of potential therapeutic targets for preventing CES.

5.4. RCTs on evaluating DOACs for the treatment of ESUS, CES, or CS

ESUS patients in 2 RCTs, NAVIGATE ESUS[56] and RE-SPECT ESUS,[18] did not demonstrate that DOACs, including rivaroxaban and dabigatran, were more effective than aspirin in preventing the recurrence of strokes. Subgroup analyses from RE-SPECT ESUS suggested that taking low doses of dabigatran may benefit patients aged 75 years or older; however, there is still a need for more randomized trials to validate this finding. Additionally, the trials highlighted that the diagnosis of ESUS decreases with advancing age, whereas renal dysfunction is correlated with heightened risks of mortality, adverse functional outcomes, and major bleeding events.[18]

Martinez-Majander et al conducted a substudy of NAVIGATE ESUS, finding that patients with ESUS and a history of cancer had similar outcomes regarding IS recurrence and all-cause mortality when treated with aspirin or rivaroxaban; however, aspirin appeared to be safer in terms of major bleeding.[73] Similarly, Sebastian F Ameriso et al[74] also analyzed NAVIGATE ESUS and reported that patients without stenotic intracranial atherosclerosis did not show significant differences in outcomes between rivaroxaban and aspirin treatment for recurrent IS.

According to the NAVIGATE ESUS substudy with a prospective design, Sharma et al[75] found that patients with ESUS exhibited a higher incidence of silent brain infarcts compared with clinically significant ISs. Additionally, the use of rivaroxaban in patients with ESUS did not significantly affect the rate of covert brain infarcts or increase the occurrence of microbleeds compared with aspirin, although the results were accompanied by wider confidence intervals.[75]

The ATTICUS study indicated that apixaban did not demonstrate superiority over aspirin, as guided by cardiac monitoring, for the prevention of new occurrences of IS in a population enriched with ESUS.[76] Limitations of ATTICUS included its open-label design, reliance on magnetic resonance imaging-detected new ischemic lesions as an endpoint, and a relatively short follow-up period of 12 months, along with a small sample size that may have contributed to insufficient statistical power.

Rong et al conducted a study revealing that tirofiban treatment did not lower disability levels and was associated with an increase in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage within 48 hours post-endovascular therapy in patients with CES.[77] This study was a secondary analysis of the RESCUE BT trial.[77]

Kamel et al published the results of the ARCADIA trial, which evaluated the effectiveness of anticoagulant treatment in reducing the incidence of stroke recurrence among individuals diagnosed with atrial cardiopathy without evidence of AF.[78] The study found that apixaban did not demonstrate a significant reduction in the risk of recurrent stroke compared with aspirin in patients with CS and comorbid atrial cardiopathy.[78]

Taken together, the recent burst keywords point to several clinically relevant research gaps. First, MR studies can help distinguish causal risk factors and prioritize potential therapeutic targets for CES, but their findings require validation in prospective cohorts and mechanistic studies. Second, atrial cardiopathy remains an unresolved clinical entity. Standardized diagnostic criteria integrating electrocardiography, echocardiography, cardiac imaging, and serum biomarkers are needed to identify patients with atrial substrate-related embolism before AF is documented. Third, the ESUS literature suggests that future trials should move beyond broad anticoagulation strategies and toward phenotype-guided designs that combine neurovascular imaging, prolonged rhythm monitoring, transcranial Doppler assessment, and blood or cardiac biomarkers to select patients most likely to benefit from targeted secondary prevention.

6. Limitations

There are a few notable limitations to this study. First, we analyzed only data from the Web of Science database, a key multidisciplinary index with more than 12,400 high-impact journals. While this source is valuable for academic evaluation, focusing solely on it may have led to the exclusion of significant research findings.[79] Specifically, reliance on a single database may omit relevant studies indexed exclusively in Scopus or PubMed/MEDLINE. Such an omission could potentially bias collaboration network metrics – including publication output, network density, and centrality – by underrepresenting regions or authors more prevalent in alternative databases. Future bibliometric research should consider multi-database retrieval, combined with rigorous deduplication, to construct a more comprehensive map of global collaboration in CES research. Second, our self-designed keyword-cleaning process and statistical methods may reveal limitations in our expertise. Future research should aim to enhance study quality and prediction accuracy by broadening data sources and standardizing keywords.

7. Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis examined research trends and collaboration networks in CES research over the past 14 years. Our findings show that research development and collaboration frequently occur across countries, regions, and institutions, although further strengthening international and inter-institutional cooperation may accelerate progress. Current research is concentrated on biomarkers for CES, etiological classification, and RCTs evaluating DOACs for ESUS, CES, and CS. The recent burst keywords, especially ESUS, MR, and atrial cardiopathy, indicate that future studies should focus on refined phenotyping, causal inference, and individualized secondary prevention strategies.
