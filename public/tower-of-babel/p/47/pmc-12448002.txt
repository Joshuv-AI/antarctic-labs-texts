Gut microbiota composition and dietary interventions modulate abdominal fat deposition in poultry: Mechanisms and applications
Authors: Bahareldin Ali Abdalla Gibril, Xutang Tu, Xuewen Chai, Jiguo Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExcessive abdominal fat deposition (AFD) in poultry reduces meat yield and efficiency. The gut microbiota regulates AFD through shifts in microbial composition and the production of metabolites. Reduced microbial diversity and fat-promoting taxa (e.g., Methanobrevibacter, Escherichia-Shigella) elevate AFD, while lean-linked bacteria (e.g., Bacteroides, Oscillospira) promote leanness. Dietary interventions, including botanical ingredients (e.g., honeycomb flavonoids elevating short-chain fatty acid producers), fermented feeds (e.g., cottonseed meal enriching butyrogenic taxa), probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus johnsonii downregulating PPARγ/FAS), and additives (bile acids activating FXR/PPARα), modulate gut microbiota to reduce AFD by enhancing barrier function, suppressing pathogens, and regulating lipid metabolism. Fecal microbiota transplantation confirms microbiota-driven AFD reduction but faces donor-matching challenges. Breed-specific microbial signatures and context-dependent outcomes inform precision strategies to improve feed efficiency and lean yield.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Poultry, Abdominal fat deposition, Host-microbiota interactions, Diet composition

Introduction

Excessive abdominal fat deposition (AFD) in poultry is a major global challenge for the industry (Shen et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2019). It reduces usable meat yield, increases feed waste, and raises processing costs (Siegel, 2014). Beyond economics, AFD significantly harms bird health (Siegel, 2014; Zhang et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2024). Traditionally, AFD has been managed through genetic selection and diet control. While breeding has gradually reduced fat levels, high costs and slow implementation have limited progress (Siegel, 2014). Dietary approaches often compromise growth rates, creating a balance between leanness and productivity. Antibiotics, once used for growth promotion, now face global bans due to the risks of resistance (Ashayerizadeh et al., 2023).

Poultry, like humans and pigs, host trillions of gut microbes that aid in nutrient digestion and absorption (Zhang et al., 2025, 2021a; Waite and Taylor, 2014). Modulating these gut microorganisms could improve growth and overall health. The gut microbiome has emerged as an essential regulator of AFD, specifically through lower microbial diversity correlating with higher AFD. Importantly, key bacteria play distinct roles, as fat-promoting microbes (e.g., Methanobrevibacter) enhance fat storage, while lean-linked bacteria (e.g., Oscillospira) suppress it (Wen et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2024). Furthermore, bacterial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) regulate fat metabolism genes (Liu et al., 2023d).

Importantly, gut microbiota responds rapidly to dietary changes, offering a practical lever for AFD control. For example, plant extracts reduce AFD while enriching lean-associated bacteria (Liu et al., 2023a; Ashayerizadeh et al., 2023). Probiotics and fermented feeds lower AFD by altering microbial balance (Nan et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2017a). Fecal transplants prove that gut bacteria directly cause fat changes (Zhang et al., 2021b). Breed-specific differences add complexity. Indigenous chickens harbor distinct microbiomes linked to lower fat than commercial breeds (Paul et al., 2021). Even within species, Beijing-You chickens develop less fat than Arbor Acres (AA) broilers under identical conditions due to microbial differences (Lei et al., 2022).

While previous reviews have explored the gut microbiota's role in poultry health, this review provides a unique and updated synthesis by: (1) integrating recent evidence on spatial (e.g., intestinal niche-specific) and temporal microbiota dynamics in AFD; (2) critically evaluating the context-dependency of microbial functions across different breeds and diets; (3) offering a comprehensive mechanistic framework linking dietary interventions to microbiota-mediated changes in host lipid metabolism; and (4) translating these findings into practical, breed-specific strategies for reducing AFD. We specifically address the gap between laboratory findings and commercial application, discussing the challenges and future directions for implementing microbiota-targeted solutions in sustainable poultry production.

To achieve this, we begin by outlining AFD in poultry, followed by a discussion of standard methods to analyze gut microbiota and its role as a dynamic regulator of AFD. Next, we examine dietary interventions that modulate the gut microbiota to reduce AFD, as well as the underlying mechanisms linking the microbiota to AFD. Finally, we review breed-specific microbiota signatures and their potential applications in breed-specific strategies.

Overview of abdominal fat deposition in poultry

Economic implications

Modern broiler production relies on genetic selection and high-energy diets to maximize growth rates and feed conversion efficiency (Tallentire et al., 2016). However, this approach has inadvertently promoted AFD, the accumulation of visceral adipose tissue (Fig. 1). AFD poses severe economic consequences: it reduces carcass yield (edible meat), increases processing waste, and lowers feed efficiency (Siegel, 2014; Chen et al., 2021; Baéza et al., 2022). Globally, the poultry industry discards approximately 3 million tons of abdominal fat annually from broilers, generating >$2.7 billion in economic losses (Wen et al., 2019).

Health and productivity implications

Beyond economic impacts, excessive AFD is associated with metabolic disorders, including obesity and hepatic steatosis (Siegel, 2014; Sun et al., 2024; Dunnington and Siegel, 1996). Significantly, AFD severely compromises breeder performance: broilers genetically selected for high AFD exhibit delayed sexual maturity, reduced egg production, poorer semen quality, and diminished fertility/hatchability (Zhang et al., 2018). Notably, broiler breeder lines with high egg-laying capacity show reduced adiposity linked to specific gut microbiota profiles (Yang et al., 2020b), pointing to the gut-fat axis as a key modulator of reproductive efficiency.

Genetic drivers of AFD

Genetic selection has progressively mitigated AFD in commercial stocks, with documented reductions (e.g., 2 % to 1.5 % in abdominal fat percentage) between 1985 and 2010 (Siegel, 2014). Divergent selection has established lean and fat chicken lines (e.g., NEAUHLF broilers), revealing AFD heritability greater than 0.8 and differential expression of adipogenic genes (e.g., fat mass and obesity-associated, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)) between lines (Abdalla et al., 2018). Breed-specific fat partitioning strategies further show genetic influences: Tianfu meat geese prioritize hepatic lipid storage over subcutaneous fat during overfeeding, whereas Gang geese exhibit the opposite pattern (Wei et al., 2021). Importantly, multi-omics analyses confirm that genetic selection directly shapes host-microbiota interactions, driving AFD (Jing et al., 2021).

Physiological and dietary drivers

Nutritional factors, including energy density, protein levels, and fatty acid profiles, directly regulate AFD by modulating hepatic lipogenesis, lipoprotein lipase activity, and adipocyte differentiation (Fouad and El-Senousey, 2014). Dietary components engage in microbiota crosstalk: high iron intake (5 g/kg, 28-day(d)) counters high-fat diet (HFD)-induced AFD in silky fowl by altering microbial ecology and host metabolism (Yang et al., 2024). Conversely, antibiotics (e.g., chlortetracycline, tylosin at 50 g/1000 Kg feed, 42-d) increase AFD in AA broilers by enhancing intestinal fatty acid absorption and hepatic lipogenesis, upregulating PPARγ, diacylglycerol-acyltransferase-2, and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) and fatty acid-binding protein-4 (FABP4) expression (Li et al., 2020). Probiotic interventions effectively reduce AFD across various poultry species, including ducks (Huang et al., 2015; Hrnčár et al., 2013) and chickens (Kalavathy et al., 2003), demonstrating conserved microbiota-mediated mechanisms.

Standard methods to analyze gut microbiota

Sampling considerations for microbiome experiments

Birds possess a monogastric digestive system (Fig. 2), meaning they have a single-chambered stomach (unlike ruminants like cows and sheep) (Nesheim et al., 1979). This system efficiently processes feed through specialized compartments. The esophagus primarily acts as a wide, expandable tube designed to handle large pieces of unchewed food and provides lubrication via mucous glands to facilitate the swallowing process. Crop: Temporarily stores and moistens food. Proventriculus: True stomach where acid/enzyme digestion begins. Gizzard: Muscular pouch that grinds food (replaces chewing). Small intestine: Nutrient absorption hub, comprising: Proximal and distal duodenum (bile/enzyme secretion) and Jejunum/Ileum (nutrient uptake). Cecum: Twin pouches ferment fiber into energy (e.g., SCFAs). Colon/Cloaca: Water reabsorption and waste excretion. Pathway: The food passage from the esophagus to the crop, proventriculus, isthmus, gizzard, next to the duodenum to the Jejunum, then to the ileum, cecum (ceca), colon, cloaca, and finally exits (vent) as feces (Fig. 2) (Reece et al., 2015).

Chicken digestive tract (nonruminant), showing key compartments.

Why sampling site and other considerations are matters: Multi-compartment sampling (duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, feces) in broilers reveals distinct microbial ecosystems at each site (Wen et al., 2019). For example, cecal microbiota drive fermentation but are diluted 10–100 × in fecal samples. Fecal analysis fails to capture small-intestinal communities (Wen et al., 2019).

In a global meta-analysis of broiler chickens, Burrows et al. also demonstrated that microbial composition varies significantly across gastrointestinal tract sites (e.g., cecum vs. feces), breeds (e.g., Ross 308 vs. Tibetan chickens), and ages (Burrows et al., 2025). Their study, which utilized 16S rRNA (V3-V4) gene sequencing of 602 datasets, demonstrates the critical importance of standardizing sampling sites and host variables in poultry microbiome research to ensure reproducible insights related to AFD. Thus, site-specific sampling (e.g., cecal luminal scrapings), as well as breed and age, are essential for establishing careful and accurate links between microbiota and function.

Complementary techniques to dissect microbial influences

16S rRNA gene sequencing, metagenomics, and metabolomics

Gut microbiota analysis employs complementary techniques to dissect microbial influences on AFD (Fig. 3). 16S rRNA gene sequencing (also known as 16S rDNA sequencing) amplifies hypervariable regions (e.g., V3–V5) of bacterial 16S genes, clustering sequences into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) or Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) for taxonomic profiling (e.g., Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio) and identification of AFD-associated taxa like Methanobrevibacter (Wen et al., 2019). However, primer bias limits the detection of archaeal and eukaryotic organisms, as well as the acquisition of functional insights. This method sequences the gene DNA that encodes the 16S rRNA (Zhang et al., 2020). High-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Illumina MiSeq) of commercial turkey ileal communities (Danzeisen et al., 2013) identified taxa linked to weight gain. 16S rRNA gene sequencing (V4 or V3-4 region; Illumina HiSeq 4000/MiSeq) has also been used for microbiota analysis in geese (He et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023) and ducks (Xie et al., 2025). For example, 16S rRNA gene sequencing of cecal microbiota in geese revealed breed-specific disparities in Ruminococcus and Methanobrevibacter abundance, aligning with divergent fat deposition phenotypes (Ni et al., 2022). It's important to note that 16S rRNA transcript analysis or sequencing is also used in studying metabolically active communities. Unlike rDNA (which targets genomic DNA and reflects potential membership), rRNA transcript sequencing analyzes the transcribed RNA molecule, requiring reverse transcription to cDNA before amplification (Wang et al., 2017b). This captures organisms with high ribosomal activity, offering insights into functional states and responses to environmental changes, but with greater technical complexity due to the instability of RNA.

Metagenomics overcomes this via shotgun sequencing of total DNA, reconstructing genomes to annotate metabolic pathways (e.g., starch degradation in Luqin chickens; (Liu et al., 2022)) and mobile genetic elements, albeit with computational intensity and risks of host DNA contamination. Shotgun metagenomics enables functional annotation of microbial pathways (e.g., lipid metabolism, SCFA production) and identifies obesity-associated taxa (e.g., Alistipes, Ruminococcaceae) in site-specific intestinal compartments of NEAUHLF broilers (Jing et al., 2021). Large-scale metagenomic sequencing in ducks (24 million genes; 4437 metagenome-assembled genomes) revealed intestinal region-specific “carbohydrate-active enzymes database” (CAZyme) functions and temporal microbiome maturation, demonstrating the utility of MAGs for revealing gut microbial ecology in poultry (Ma et al., 2024).

Metabolomics quantifies microbial metabolites (SCFAs, bile acids (BAs)) through targeted Liquid Chromatography (LC)/Gas Chromatography (GC)-Mass Spectrometry (MS) or untargeted Nuclear Magnetic Resonance, linking compounds like butyrate or long-chain acylcarnitines to host fat metabolism (Liu et al., 2023c, 2023a, 2022). GC effectively quantifies SCFAs in gut luminal contents, as demonstrated in duck cecal samples where butyrate, propionate, and branched-chain fatty acids (isobutyrate and isovalerate) were found to be the most abundant (Yang et al., 2020a). However, metabolomics faces challenges in attributing the origins of metabolites and accounting for their dynamic half-lives.

Combined 16S rRNA gene sequencing and shotgun metagenomics in Tiannong Partridge chickens revealed genus/species-level taxa (e.g., Methanobrevibacter woesei) and functional pathways (e.g., lipid metabolism) driving AFD (Xiang et al., 2021). Integrated microbiome (cecal microbiota)-metabolomic (serum) analysis has also been reported in ducks to show how microbial shifts alter host lipid metabolism (PPAR pathway) (Xie et al., 2025). In addition, integrated metagenomic-metabolomic approaches have elucidated functional pathways in AFD. For example, in Pekin ducks, metagenomics identified enriched fat-linked genera (e.g., Methanobrevibacter ), while metabolomics revealed correlated changes in amino acid metabolism and energy homeostasis (Chen et al., 2024). Moreover, Liu et al. use 16S rDNA sequencing (cecal microbiota DNA with Ion S5™ XL platform) and metabolomics (LC-MS/MS) to link LJPS (Laminaria japonica polysaccharide)-driven microbiota shifts (increased Bacteroides, decreased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio) to AFD reduction in ducks (Liu et al., 2023a). Collectively, these methods map the taxonomic shifts, functional pathways, and metabolite interactions that drive AFD, despite inherent technical constraints.

While these powerful techniques provide invaluable insights, cross-study comparisons and the definitive identification of AFD-associated taxa are complicated by significant methodological variability. Differences in DNA extraction protocols, the selection of 16S rRNA gene hypervariable regions (e.g., V3-V4 vs. V4), and bioinformatic processing choices (e.g., OTU clustering versus ASV algorithms) can profoundly influence the resulting microbial community structure and diversity metrics. Furthermore, metagenomic and metabolomic studies face challenges with host DNA contamination and the dynamic turnover of metabolites, respectively. Consequently, findings from individual studies must be interpreted with caution, and the field would benefit from increased standardization in analytical pipelines to better reconcile data across different research groups.

Fecal microbiota transplantation applications

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is a functional tool to establish causal links between gut microbiota and AFD. It involves transferring microbiota from donor to recipient poultry, directly testing microbial influences on fat metabolism (Fig. 3). For example, FMT from high-growth Turpan × Leghorn chickens to 1-d-old chicks enhanced hepatic fat catabolism (e.g., increased carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1 (CPT1), PPARα expression), confirming microbiota-driven AFD regulation (Zhang et al., 2021b). This approach validates microbial taxa (e.g., Sphingomonas, Microbacterium) as targets for lean phenotypes. Conversely, FMT from native Beijing-You chickens to commercial AA broilers increased AFD and body weight, demonstrating the transmissible role of gut microbiota in modulating host metabolism (Lei et al., 2022). Similarly, FMT from high-growth-performance donors to 1-day-old Yellow-feathered chickens increased Lactobacillus abundance, reduced jejunal inflammation via Th17/Treg cell balance, and improved growth metrics, as demonstrated using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, qPCR, and metabolomics (Ma et al., 2023). In another study, FMT from high-abdominal fat Qingyuan partridge chickens to 1-day-old chicks significantly reduced variation in fat deposition traits and altered cecal microbiota composition/metabolic functions (e.g., enhanced fatty acid biosynthesis, reduced vitamin metabolism), as revealed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and UHPLC-Q/TOF-MS metabolomics (Song et al., 2024).

As shown in Fig. 4, FMT consistently reduced abdominal adipocyte size and/or weight in chickens. For example, FMT from low-AFD adult chickens to 1-day-old white feather broilers via daily oral gavage (1 mL fecal microbiota for 28-d) significantly reduced abdominal fat weight (AFW) and adipocyte diameter while enriching lean-linked Sphaerochaeta and decreasing fat-related genes (fatty acid synthase (FAS), LPL) via qPCR analysis (Chen et al., 2023b). Notably, FMT from normal-fat diet (NFD) chickens increased gut microbes (Megasphaera, Flavonifractor, Merdibacter, Enterococcus), increased butyric acid, and reduced fat deposition via the butyric acid/miR-204/acyl-coA synthetase short-chain-2 (ACSS2) axis (Chen et al., 2023a). Further supporting this, FMT from folic acid-supplemented donors to recipient broilers replicated fat-reduction effects (decreased adipocyte diameter, decreased abdominal fat% accompanied by increased forkhead box-O1 (FoxO1) while decreased PPARγ&FABP4 protein expression) via increased e.g., Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Oscillospira, confirming gut microbiota's causal role in mediating dietary interventions (Liu et al., 2024). Together, these findings confirm that microbial regulation of fat metabolism is a reality. While FMT establishes causal links between specific microbiota and host phenotypes, results can be influenced by donor-recipient compatibility and fail to isolate individual bacterial contributions within complex communities.

Gut microbiota as a dynamic regulator of AFD

The gut microbiota is a critical regulator of AFD in poultry. Reduced α-diversity consistently correlates with increased AFD, as demonstrated by antibiotic-induced dysbiosis in chickens (Zhang et al., 2021a) and force-feeding in geese (Wei et al., 2021). Host genetics significantly shapes microbiota composition: lean-line chickens enrich Lactobacillus and Fusobacteria, while fat-line chickens show higher Proteobacteria (Ding et al., 2016; Gao et al., 2024). Spatial heterogeneity is crucial, as cecal microbiota explain the variance in AFD in broilers (Wen et al., 2019), and jejunal enrichment of Escherichia coli promotes adiposity in broilers (Jing et al., 2021). Temporal dynamics reveal that cecal diversity stabilizes after 21-d, dominated by Firmicutes in broilers (Zhou et al., 2021).

Analyses reveal that AFD is linked to specific lean-associated or fat-promoting microbes (Table 1). Pro-adipogenic microbial taxa drive AFD via enhanced energy harvest and lipogenesis. Methanobrevibacter is the most consistent fat-promoting archaeon across poultry species (Zheng et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2019; Ni et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024). Key fat-promoting bacteria include the Ruminococcus torques group, Escherichia-Shigella/Klebsiella, Desulfovibrio, Campylobacter coli, and Rikenellaceae RC9_gut_group (Table 1). A higher Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio generally promotes AFD, although exceptions exist.

Anti-adipogenic taxa protect against AFD through the production of SCFAs, bile acid metabolism, and the suppression of lipogenesis. Core lean-associated microbes include Lactobacillus/Ligilactobacillus, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia, Oscillospira, and Mucispirillum schaeclieri (Table 1). SCFA producers, such as Butyricicoccus and Faecalibacterium, are generally protective, although this protection is context-dependent.

Together, these data indicate that AFD is associated with reduced gut microbiome diversity and shifts in the dominant bacterial groups. Fat-promoting microbes like Methanobrevibacter, pathogenic Proteobacteria (e.g., Escherichia-Shigella), and Ruminococcus torques are linked to increased fat deposition, while lean-promoting bacteria such as Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia, and Oscillospira help reduce it. However, some bacteria have dual roles: Butyricicoccus and Faecalibacterium promote leanness in chickens but fat storage in ducks, and Firmicutes can either increase or decrease AFD depending on diet. Current evidence for these divergent microbial effects (e.g., Butyricicoccus promoting leanness in chickens but adiposity in ducks, Firmicutes exhibiting diet-dependent roles) derives primarily from isolated single-species studies under varying experimental conditions. Direct comparative trials co-housing ducks and chickens under identical diets are needed to determine whether these inversions stem from: (i) Fundamental host physiological differences (e.g., bile acid metabolism, PPAR isoform expression), or (ii) Methodological variability (e.g., sampling sites, sequencing platforms). Until such studies validate these patterns, interpretations of microbial roles should be context-specifically qualified.

Importantly, the pro- or anti-adipogenic roles of microbial taxa are not absolute but are highly context-dependent. The effects of core taxa, such as Firmicutes or Butyricicoccus, can vary dramatically and even appear contradictory across studies due to differences in host genetics (breed), age, basal diet composition, and environmental conditions. This underscores the necessity of interpreting any microbial signature within the specific experimental context of the study and avoiding broad generalizations.

Interventions consistently alter the gut microbiota by suppressing fat-associated bacteria and promoting lean-linked communities.

Botanical ingredients

Botanical ingredients reduce AFD by remodeling gut microbiota and regulating host lipid metabolism, primarily through enrichment of SCFA-producing bacteria and enhanced intestinal barrier function. Examples include: Honeycomb flavonoids (geese) and phytosterols (broilers) elevate butyrogenic taxa (e.g., Clostridiales, Lachnoclostridium), upregulate tight-junction proteins (ZO-1, claudins), aationress inflammation (He et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2023). Plant essential oils (ducks) enrich Subdoligranulum (SCFA producer), inhibit pathogens (e.g., Alistipes), and improve barrier function (Ge et al., 2023). Turmeric/black pepper in Japanese quails reduces AFD, decreases Coliforms, and enhances villus morphology and Lactobacillus proliferation (Ashayerizadeh et al., 2023).

Alterations in bile acid metabolism and microbial metabolites further modulate AFD: Seaweed polysaccharides (ducks) suppress bile acid synthesis while enriching Bacteroides and vitamin B6 pathways (Liu et al., 2023a). Acer truncatum extract (broilers) elevates propionate via Bacteroidota enrichment (Liu et al., 2023b). Purslane enriches carbohydrate-metabolizing bacteria (Wang et al., 2021a). Asiaticoside in Yellow-feathered chickens increases Bacteroidetes and Lactobacillus, suppresses fat-promoting Ruminococcus, and upregulates β-oxidation genes (e.g., CPT1, PPARα), reducing AFD (Fu et al., 2023). Anoectochilus and walnut husk extracts reduce the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and enrich lean-linked genera (e.g., Candidatus Arthromitus, Bacteroides) (Wu et al., 2023, 2024).

Direct suppression of lipogenic genes and pathogens complements microbiota-mediated effects: Polysaccharides (dietary Achyranthes bidentata; ducks), herbal powders (e.g., dietary chicory, ginger, in broilers), and dietary echinocystic acid (broilers) inhibit pathogens (e.g., E. coli), elevate Lactobacillus, and downregulate lipogenic genes (e.g., FAS, ACC) or activate PPARα (Ao and Kim, 2020; Xiao et al., 2025; Khoobani et al., 2019; Qorbanpour et al., 2018). Fiber-rich botanicals (e.g., alfalfa meal) reduce AFD, enriching Bacteroides/Lactobacillus and inhibiting Clostridium (Zheng et al., 2019). Relative contributions of specific SCFAs to barrier integrity, causal links between microbial metabolites (e.g., B vitamins) and host lipid genes, and functional roles of lean-associated genera (e.g., Candidatus Arthromitus) remain unquantified. Germ-free studies are needed to distinguish direct host effects from microbiota-dependent actions (e.g., for phytosterols).

Fermented feeds

As shown in Fig. 5, fermented feeds, including fermented cottonseed meal (FCSM), fermented grape seed meal (FGSM), and fermented corncob meal (FCM), reduce AFD in chicken broilers through SCFA production and microbiota remodeling. For example, FCSM increases lactic acid bacteria, suppresses coliforms, and reduces AFD (Jazi et al., 2017). FGSM elevates butyric acid and reduces AFD (Nan et al., 2022). FCM enriches Akkermansia (butyrate producer) and downregulates lipogenic genes (FAS, PPARγ), reducing AFD (Cui et al., 2022). Optimal fermentation protocols (strains, duration) for maximizing SCFA yield remain unresolved.

Probiotics & synbiotics

Probiotics & synbiotics exhibit strain-/dose-dependent effects. For example, Lactobacillus johnsonii BS15 (broilers) downregulates PPARγ/SREBP-1c, upregulates CPT1, and reduces AFD (Wang et al., 2017b). Bacillus coagulans (broilers) suppresses coliforms and reduces AFD (Khajeh Bami et al., 2020). Synbiotics (xylooligosaccharide, Bacillus subtilis, and Clostridium butyricum) reduced AFD 23.8 % in ducks via lipid metabolism modulation (Chen et al., 2018). A critical research gap remains in understanding context-dependent effects, like findings that Lactobacillus increases abdominal fat in overfed geese through hepatic lipid redirection (Wen et al., 2022).

Direct additives, SCFAs & fiber

Sodium propionate (0.05 %, 42-d) lowers the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and reduces AFD in broilers (Li et al., 2021). Butyric acid (broilers) suppresses E. coli and reduces AFD (Panda et al., 2009). Inulin (25 g/kg, 18-d) enriches Alistipes/Streptococcus and reduces adipocyte area in ducks (Li et al., 2025). Ryegrass (75 % diet) reduces goose AFD via Ruminococcaceae enrichment (Guo et al., 2019). Unresolved efficacy thresholds for additives (e.g., phenyllactic acid modulates microbiota without reducing AFD; (Wang et al., 2010)).

Amino acids, metabolites, and associated compounds

Amino acids and derivatives reprogram microbiota and bile acid metabolism, as demonstrated by γ-aminobutyric acid (0.1 g/kg; broilers) increases Bacteroidetes (e.g., Barnesiella), activates PPARα/CPT1α, and reduces hepatic lipids (Chen et al., 2022). Similarly, β‑hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate (HMB; layer chickens) upregulates bile acid synthesis (Cyp7a1) and reduces AFD (Liao et al., 2022), whereas methionine (0.38–0.47 %) enriches Lactobacillus and reduces AFD but lowers Faecalibacterium (SCFA producer), requiring a balance between fat reduction and gut health (Ma et al., 2021). Notably, low-protein diets increase AFD and alter BAs (Yuan et al., 2024).

Vitamins (such as folic acid) and minerals (including chromium and iron) reduce AFD via microbiota-mediated gene regulation. For example, folic acid (0.013 g/kg; broilers) downregulates PPARγ/FABP4 and reduces AFD (Liu et al., 2023d, 2024), while chromium picolinate (6 × 10⁻⁴ g/kg) alters tryptophan metabolism and reduces duck fat deposition (Xie et al., 2025). Similarly, iron (0.5 g/kg) lowers AFD via Firmicutes enrichment in silky fowl (Yang et al., 2024). However, optimal dosing remains undefined due to the confounding effects of basal diets.

BAs modulate microbiota-host crosstalk to reduce adiposity across poultry species. In late-phase Hy-Line Grey laying hens, dietary porcine BAs (0.06–0.09 g/kg, 168-d) reduced AFD and serum lipids (total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-C) while enriching cecal Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Turicibacter and enhancing bile acid biotransformation (Yang et al., 2022a). Similarly, in geese supplemented with BAs (0.15 g/kg, 28-d), AFD decreased via enriched SCFA-producing taxa (e.g., Blautia, Clostridium) and activated hepatic FXR/PPARα signaling, suppressing lipogenesis (Li et al., 2023). A separate study found BAs (0.06 g/kg, 42-d) reduced hepatic fat and AFD by increasing Akkermansia (lean diet) or Bifidobacterium/Lactobacillus (HFD), altering BA profiles (Wang et al., 2024a). Most recently, Zhijiang ducks fed HFD + 0.25 g/kg porcine BAs (73.2 % hyodeoxycholic acid) showed a 51.4 % decrease in AFW. This was mediated by enrichment of lean-associated genera (e.g., Rikenellaceae RC9-gut group, Eubacterium nodatum group), which elevated serum total BAs and upregulated breast muscle expression of FXR and PPARα. Consequently, fatty acid oxidation increased via the FXR/PPARα-CPT1α pathway (Chen et al., 2025).

The collective evidence from a diverse array of dietary strategies, ranging from botanical ingredients and fermented feeds to probiotics, specific additives, and bile acids, consistently demonstrates their potent efficacy in reshaping the gut microbiota to reduce AFD in poultry. These interventions primarily act by enriching lean-associated and SCFA-producing bacteria, enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, and directly modulating host lipogenic and lipolytic pathways. However, the interpretation of these promising results and their application are subject to important limitations. Much of the evidence is derived from studies assessed at a single time point, capturing a snapshot that misses microbial community succession, and relies on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, which precludes firm functional assignments. Furthermore, while sequencing reveals strong correlations, establishing definitive causation requires complementary techniques like germ-free models. The efficacy of an intervention is also highly context-dependent, influenced by interactions with the basal diet, host genetics, age, and other factors, making it challenging to define universal optimal dosages.

Consequently, the translation of these findings from controlled research to commercial practice necessitates careful consideration not only of these scientific challenges but also of cost-effectiveness, regulatory approval, and potential side effects. Therefore, future research must prioritize longitudinal designs, multi-omics integration, and dose-response studies in standardized models to move from correlation to causation. Ultimately, large-scale trials under real-world farming conditions are essential to validate the economic viability and practical integration of these strategies into sustainable poultry production systems.

Mechanisms linking gut microbiota to AFD

The gut microbiota reduces AFD through metabolite-driven signaling, epigenetic regulation, and immune-endocrine crosstalk. As summarized in Table 3, key mechanisms include: SCFAs (e.g., butyrate from Butyricicoccus) activate PPARα-mediated β-oxidation in the cecum of aging broilers (Zhou et al., 2021). BAs (e.g., hyodeoxycholic acid) bind FXR/PPARα to suppress lipogenesis in ducks (Chen et al., 2025). Epigenetic reprogramming, where microbiota dysbiosis downregulates miR-204 (reducing butyrate), enabling lipogenic ACSS2 expression, is reversed by FMT from lean donors (Chen et al., 2023a). Immune-endocrine crosstalk, such as reduced pro-inflammatory taxa (Escherichia-Shigella), mitigating inflammation-driven lipid accumulation in geese (He et al., 2021). Key research gaps include synergy between SCFA/BA ratios, microbial consortia effects, and gut-niche-specific impacts (e.g., ileal vs. cecal SCFAs; (Liu et al., 2023d, 2023c).

Breed-specific microbiota signatures

Host genetics shape distinct gut microbiota signatures across poultry breeds, directly modulating AFD. Indigenous and commercial breeds maintain divergent core microbiota even under controlled conditions. Pandit et al. identified 42 breed-specific microbial biomarkers, with higher OTU richness in commercial broilers (Cobb 400, Ross 308) versus Indian indigenous breeds (Aseel, Kadaknath), confirming genetic regulation of microbiota assembly (Pandit et al., 2018). Similarly, Paul et al. documented elevated Bacteroidetes and diversity in native Indian chickens (Nicobari, Ghagus), correlating with reduced adipogenesis (Paul et al., 2021). Critically, whether these signatures directly regulate AFD or reflect host-microbe coevolution remains unknown.

Artificial selection for adiposity systematically alters microbial ecology. Divergently selected lean/fat lines (≥15 generations) exhibit stark contrasts: lean birds enrich Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria, and butyrate producers (e.g., Anaeromassilibacillus), while fat lines show higher Proteobacteria and pathogens like Enterococcus (Hou et al., 2016; Jing et al., 2021; Ding et al., 2016). Metagenomics reveals concurrent shifts in lipid metabolism pathways (e.g., PPAR signaling) (Ding et al., 2016), often coinciding with host genomic adaptations (e.g., APOB, PPARγ SNPs) (Gao et al., 2024). However, the mechanistic links driving adipogenesis require elucidation.

Microbiota frequently outweighs host genetics in explaining AFD variance. In broilers, gut taxa show minimal heritability (≤3.46 %), yet duodenal/cecal microbiota explain >20 % of AFD variability, exceeding AFW heritability (h²=0.33) (Wen et al., 2019). Breed-specific AFD susceptibility (e.g., Beijing-You vs. AA chickens) correlates with cecal microbiota profiles, with environmental standardization eliminating differences and directly impacting fat deposition (Lei et al., 2022). This dominance raises a key question: Can microbiota profiles serve as biomarkers for genetic selection of leanness?

Functionally, breed-specific signatures alter nutrient harvesting and the production of metabolites. Fatty Luqin chickens enrich Erysipelatoclostridium and starch/lactose degradation pathways, elevating pro-adipogenic long-chain acylcarnitines (Liu et al., 2022). Lean Xianghai geese show high Faecalibacterium (SCFA producer), contrasting with fat-prone Zi geese, which favor pro-adipogenic Ruminococcus torques (Ni et al., 2022). Pekin (high-fat) versus Shaoxing (lean) ducks differentially enrich Fusobacterium and Butyricicoccus, altering lipid metabolites (Chen et al., 2024). Despite robust associations, causal validation (e.g., via FMT) is needed.

Cross-species conservation of genetic-microbiota-adiposity relationships is evident. Comparative metagenomics of 10 duck breeds identified signature taxa (e.g., Actinobacteria in high-fat Sansui vs. Proteobacteria in lean Mawang) (Shen et al., 2025). Lean Pekin ducks enrich Bacteroidetes, while fatty lines elevate Firmicutes and Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratios (Yang et al., 2022b). Collectively, this prompts a translational question: Can lean-associated consortia be engineered across species via dietary interventions?

Conclusions

The gut microbiota composition has a critical influence on AFD in poultry. Reduced microbial diversity and certain fat-promoting microbes contribute to elevated lipid storage, while lean-linked bacteria mitigate adiposity. Dietary interventions, such as probiotics, plant extracts (e.g., flavonoids), fermented feeds, bile acids, and folic acid, consistently reduce AFD by enriching beneficial taxa, enhancing gut barrier function, and suppressing pathogens. FMT validates microbiota-driven AFD reduction but requires optimization for donor compatibility. Integrating microbiota-targeted strategies with precision nutrition will advance leaner, healthier poultry production.

Future research priorities should focus on both mechanistic and applied questions. Adjusting diets to improve the distinct microbial signatures of different poultry breeds is a key goal. Using gnotobiotic models will help isolate the causal microbes and mechemeolites that control fat metabolism, while optimizing additives through precision dosing is needed to balance AFD reduction with gut health. Identifying microbial biomarkers will aid genetic selection for low-AFD poultry.

However, translating these interventions from research to commercial practice requires careful consideration of cost-effectiveness, regulatory status, and potential side effects. For instance, while probiotics and fermented feeds show great promise, their cost-benefit ratio must be favorable for producers. Botanical extracts face regulatory hurdles that vary by region and require strict standardization of active compounds. Furthermore, high doses of certain additives (e.g., bile acids, essential oils) may potentially disrupt gut homeostasis or impart undesirable flavors to meat. Therefore, the final and essential step must be large-scale trials under real-world commercial farming conditions to validate efficacy, economic viability, and practical integration into sustainable poultry production systems.
