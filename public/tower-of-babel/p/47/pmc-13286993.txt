Mediators and moderators of successful population-level weight management in primary care: results from the PATHWEIGH cluster-randomized pragmatic trial
Authors: Leigh Perreault, Carlos Rodriguez, Peter C Smith, E Seth Kramer, Lauren Tolle, Johnny Williams, Qing Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPATHWEIGH is the first intervention scaled to 274,182 patients to mitigate population weight gain. In a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized pragmatic trial in Colorado, USA, 56 primary care clinics were randomly assigned to three clusters with staggered start dates for a one-way crossover from usual care to the intervention phase. The intervention (PATHWEIGH) included: health system primary care leadership endorsement, an electronic health record (EHR)-driven care process designed to prioritize, facilitate and expedite weight management, and implementation strategies to support use of the care process and educate clinicians on obesity treatment. The objective of the current analysis was to identify mediators and moderators associated with successful population-level weight management in primary care. The majority of subgroups (moderators) benefited from the intervention because they either lost more weight, gained less weight or switched from weight gain to weight loss compared to usual care. Patient and/or provider use of an EHR component of the intervention mediated 37% of additional patient weight loss and use of an anti-obesity medication mediated 4% over 18 months (p < 0.001 for all comparisons). Altogether, the intervention had favorable effects on patient weight across the subgroups, particularly when a patient or provider used ≥1 EHR component of PATHWEIGH.Subject terms: Weight management, Health care

Introduction

The steady rise in the prevalence of obesity has been repeatedly attributed to an average population weight gain of 0.5 kg per year [1, 2]. Despite this seemingly small absolute gain, no widescale strategy has been able to reduce the prevalence of obesity [3]. Recently, PATHWEIGH became the first intervention scaled to 274,182 patients to mitigate population weight gain (−0.10 kg loss vs. 0.47 kg gain in usual care over 18 months) [4]. Its success likely lies in its divergence from traditional weight loss programs. Importantly, PATHWEIGH is not a standardized, fee-for-service, duration-limited weight loss program, but rather a care process to manage obesity long-term in primary care [4]. The current exploratory analysis sought to examine heterogeneity in the treatment effect to inform future research.

Methods

Trial design

An effectiveness-implementation hybrid type 1 stepped-wedge, cluster-randomized pragmatic trial [5] was conducted in 56 primary care clinics in a single health care system in Colorado, USA. The protocol and trial design have been previously published [6].

Trial population

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 and ≥2 measured weights in one of the clinics between March 17, 2020, and March 16, 2024. BMI values were excluded if they were suspected to be erroneous (height <135 cm, >225 cm, weight >273 kg). Patients were assigned unique encoded identifiers and all data were de-identified. The study was exempt from informed consent and approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB).

Trial intervention

The trial intervention included: (1) health system primary care leadership-endorsement to prioritize weight management, (2) customization of the EHR with a specialized weight-prioritized visit type, a pre-visit weight management intake questionnaire, consolidation of any potential treatment the clinician may recommend (i.e., instructions for diet, exercise and behavior modification, referrals for weight-related care, prescription of anti-obesity medication [AOM] or performance of a bariatric procedure), as well as prompts to maximize billing and follow-up, and (3) implementation strategies to support use of the care process as well as educate clinicians on obesity treatment [4]. The intervention was intended to facilitate obesity care, but its use was voluntary and non-prescriptive.

Trial outcomes

The current analysis examined pre-specified factors as potential mediators or moderators of successful population-level weight management in primary care over 18 months. Weights for all adult patients with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and ≥2 measured weights in the clinics from March 17, 2020 to March 16, 2024 were extracted from the electronic health record. Data collection was opportunistic.

Candidate moderators included patient age categories (< 45, 45–60, >60 years old), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic white, Asian, African American, Hispanic), insurance type (Medicare, Medicaid, self-pay, commercial), and baseline BMI ≥ 30 vs. <30 kg/m2. Candidate mediators included any referral for weight-related care, prescription and use of anti-obesity medicine (yes/no at any time during the trial), and patient and/or provider use of one or more EHR component of the PATHWEIGH care process.

Statistical analysis

Linear mixed models assessed weight trajectories as a continuous piecewise linear function and were adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and calendar year of the index visit and assumed a constant rate of weight change from the first weight measurement in usual care with a change in rate after the patient was weighed in a clinic that had received the intervention.

Candidate moderators of weight change in were examined by adding interaction terms between covariates and number of months after the initial visit for usual care and between number of months post-intervention start and a visit in the intervention. The effect of the intervention was measured as the weight change from the usual care to the intervention using all observed weight in an interrupted time series framework. A stringent threshold p-value of <0.001 was used to adjust for the multiple hypotheses.

Candidate mediators of weight change during the intervention were examined as time-varying covariates on average causal and percent mediated effects over total effects of the intervention [7]. The mediator analysis was restricted to patients exposed to the mediators and compared the weight of the same patient before and after the mediator event, reflecting the mediator effects on weight change, minimizing reverse causation. Again, a p-value < 0.001 threshold was employed.

Results

Baseline demographics and health metrics of the patients at their first weight in usual care and intervention phases have been published [4]. Briefly, patients were ~54 years old, ~54% women, 78% Non-Hispanic white, 61% commercially insured with baseline body mass index (BMI) of 31.3 kg/m2 in both trial phases.

Moderators

The intervention had a favorable, but distinct, impact on patient weight with three specific trajectories observed (Table 1). First, patients lost 0.03–0.05 kg/mo more weight during the intervention compared to usual care for those >60 years, insured by Medicare, and/or with a baseline BMI > 30 kg/m2. Second, patients gained 0.03–0.05 kg/mo less weight during the intervention compared to usual care for those <45 years, self-pay or insured by Medicaid, and/or with a baseline BMI 25–30 kg/m2. Third, 0.002–0.04 kg/mo weight gain during usual care changed to 0.01–0.08 kg/mo weight loss during the intervention for those 45–60 years, in both sexes, most race/ethnic groups, and whether or not a patient received weight-related care (p < 0.001 for all; Table 1).

Mediators

Patient and/or provider use of at least one of the EHR components of PATHWEIGH during the intervention resulted in a weight change of −0.20 kg/mo more than usual care, 37.2% of which was mediated by use of the EHR tools. Prescription of AOM also favorably impacted weight during the intervention, with 4% of the 0.15 kg/mo extra weight loss mediated by use of the medication (p < 0.001 for all; Table 2). A referral for any weight-related service (e.g., to a dietician, endocrinology or bariatric surgery) did not significantly mediate patient weight during the intervention for those who received a referral.

Discussion

Weight management is central to the treatment of obesity [8], but definitions of success can differ. PATHWEIGH favorably impacted patient population weight with three distinct trajectories observed: (1) accentuation of weight loss compared to usual care, (2) altering the weight trajectory from gain to loss, and (3) mitigation of weight gain observed during usual care. In patient subgroups stratified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type, and baseline BMI, 26 of the 34 subgroups exhibited one of these favorable weight trends. Collectively, these data shed light on how PATHWEIGH may positively impact public health. Further, results from the current analysis provide insight into the magnitude of benefit from weight-related care beyond lifestyle advice. Interestingly, referrals did not impact patient weight for patients who received a referral for weight-related care (i.e., to a dietician or endocrinology), and, although the prescription and use of AOM was associated with weight loss in the 15% of patients ever exposed to an AOM over the course of the trial, the overall proportion of weight loss attributable to medication was a mere 4%, likely due to short-term use of generic medications. Patient weight was most impacted when a patient and/or provider used a EHR component of PATHWEIGH. Motivating change in medical practice is challenging [9, 10], but perhaps holds the greatest promise to alter trends in population weight gain.

The current results should be viewed in light of their imitations. Candidate moderators and mediators were pre-specified. Patient exposure to the mediators was unlikely to be random. Unmeasured factors cannot be accounted for in the analyses—including details of the lifestyle advice that was provided - nor can reverse causality be excluded.

In conclusion, the current analyses demonstrate the favorable, but distinct, impact of PATHWEIGH on patient weight trajectories in subpopulations. Improving patient and provider uptake of EHR-based tools has the potential to magnify these benefits.
