The first South Korean data challenge for drug discovery using human and mouse liver microsomal stability data
Authors: Nam-Chul Cho, SeongEun Hong, Jin Sook Song, EuiJu Yeo, SoI Jung, Yuno Lee, Seul Gee Hwang, Su Min Kang, JaeSung Hwang, Tae-Eun Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Korea Chemical Bank (KCB) has generated a dataset containing metabolic stability data for approximately 4,000 compounds that have been tested on human and mouse liver microsomes. The first South Korea Data Challenge, named the Jump AI Challenge for Drug Discovery (JUMP AI 2023), was opened using the metabolic stability data of KCB in 2023. The objective of the JUMP AI 2023 was to promote and encourage the development of new drugs using artificial intelligence (AI) technology in South Korea. A total of 1254 teams participated in the competition, developing algorithms to estimate the remaining percentage of compounds after 30 min of incubation with human and mouse liver microsomes. The data set comprised training and test sets of 3498 and 483 compounds, respectively. This paper provides an overview of the JUMP AI 2023 and its outcomes, highlighting the diverse range of algorithms and artificial intelligence technologies employed by the competing teams. Among these, five teams stood out by utilizing GNN-based approaches winning awards. This competition was the first AI competition for drug discovery in South Korea, attracting numerous researchers and playing a key role in promoting drug research through the application of artificial intelligence technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-025-01047-8.Keywords: Korea data challenge, JUMP AI 2023, Metabolic stability

Introduction

In recent years, the incorporation of advanced technologies such as data science, informatics, and big data-driven artificial intelligence (AI) has led to a paradigm shift in the field of drug discovery [1]. AI has emerged as a transformative tool in drug discovery, offering the potential to increase efficiency, reduce costs, and minimize reliance on animal testing. Through the use of machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), generative chemical structures, knowledge graphs, and multi-property optimization techniques, AI has facilitated advances in key drivers of drug discovery: disease understanding, target identification, and therapeutic modality development [2]. Numerous initiatives and competitions have been launched to foster innovation and collaboration in AI-driven drug discovery in recognition of this potential.

A prominent example is the DREAM (Dialogue for Reverse Engineering Assessments and Methods) Challenge, an international community effort to address fundamental questions in field of systems biology and translational medicine [3]. The DREAM Challenges often collaborate with large-scale consortia such as NIH-CTD2 to explore the effective algorithms for predicting drug targets and drug sensitivity. Kaggle, a prominent platform for data science and machine learning, hosts various AI challenges in the pharmaceutical domain [4]. For instance, the “AI Drug Discovery Workshop and Coding Challenge” provides participants with an opportunity to develop AI programming skills for drug discovery. These competitions offer a platform for researchers to work with real-world data and develop innovative algorithms that can potentially revolutionize the drug discovery process.

Metabolic stability of compound emerges as a pivotal parameter in the initial phases of drug discovery, exerting a direct influence on the pharmacokinetics of a compound, encompassing its absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) profiles [5]. A lack of sufficient metabolic stability can expedite the degradation of a drug candidate, diminishing its therapeutic efficacy and augmenting the probability of toxicity. This aspect stands as a critical factor in the failure of compounds during the early stages of drug discovery. Accordingly, it is essential to prioritize compounds with favorable metabolic profiles at the preclinical stage.

The Korea Chemical Bank (KCB) serves as a national repository of chemical compounds, collecting, managing, and distributing diverse compound resources to support pharmaceutical and biotechnological research. Notably, KCB has independently generated data to secure a versatile resource for drug discovery research and to provide domestic pharmaceutical and biotech researchers with valuable information. In this context, KCB organized a data challenge providing a newly generated metabolic stability dataset comprising in vitro human and mouse liver microsomal stability data for a diverse set of compounds. The dataset was constructed using compounds sourced from the Korea Chemical Bank, ensuring broad structural diversity. This diversity was intentionally incorporated to enhance applicability and generalizability of AI models for metabolic stability prediction across various chemical scaffolds. The primary objective of the challenge was to enable participants to refine their predictive models and benchmark their algorithms against this scientifically curated dataset. Ultimately, this initiative aimed to encourage the development of robust in silico tools for early metabolic stability assessment and effective compound prioritization in drug discovery pipelines.

The 2023 AI Drug Discovery Challenge (JUMP AI 2023) a collaborative initiative between the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology (KRICT) and the Korea Pharmaceutical and Bio-Pharma Manufacturers Association (KPBMA), aimed to advance the application of AI technologies in pharmaceutical innovation. The competition facilitated the development of novel AI models by providing researchers, developers, and students with access to real-world datasets and emerging pharmaceutical challenges. This initiative enabled participants to evaluate and demonstrate the performance of their algorithms within a practical drug discovery context. Furthermore, it fostered collaboration among academic, biotech, and pharmaceutical sectors, encouraging the growth of a diverse and interdisciplinary research environment.

This paper presents an overview of the competition results and provides a brief introduction to the algorithms used by the winning participants. By examining how the competition progressed and analyzing the strategies used by the highest-ranking teams to address the challenge, we aim to highlight the key approaches and models that contributed to their success. Additionally, we discuss the implications of these findings for the future of AI-driven drug discovery.

Material and methods

Metabolic stability data

The dataset used in the JUMP AI 2023 challenge includes metabolic stability measurements for approximately 4,000 compounds, carefully selected from the representative library of the Korea Chemical Bank with consideration of structural diversity. All experiments were performed in a single laboratory under consistent conditions to ensure data homogeneity and high reliability. The oxidative metabolic stability of the compound used was evaluated using a reaction mixture containing NADPH regenerating solution and human or mouse liver microsomes. The compound (final concentration of 2 μM) was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by the addition of ice-cold acetonitrile. The percentage of parent compound remaining after the 30-min incubation was determined by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Experimental outcomes represented the percentage of the parent compound remaining after 30 min. Compounds with more than 50% remaining after 30 min reaction were classified as metabolically stable [6].

Training and test sets

The data set was divided into training and test sets using splitting methods based on chemical properties and molecular fingerprints to ensure optimal representation. To characterize the ligands, a comprehensive set of molecular descriptors was calculated using Pipeline Pilot software, including Extended-Connectivity Fingerprint 6 (ECFP6), AlogP, molecular weight, the number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, and the number of rotatable bonds. Clustering was performed using specific parameters to optimize the grouping of ligands. A total of 20 clusters were generated with an average number of ligands per cluster determined by the clustering algorithm. The maximum allowable inter-ligand distance within a cluster was set to 0.4. Cluster centers were selected using the “Nearest Outside Maximum” method, which identifies centers based on the maximum distance to the nearest neighbor outside each cluster to ensure adequate cluster separation, and the clusters were then refined using a re-centering algorithm (“To Minimize Average Distance”) repeated twice for improved accuracy.

To finalize the challenge problem design, the dataset was carefully split to achieve balanced representation across chemical clusters and metabolic stability ranges, while ensuring suitable complexity for AI model development. Based on this refined distribution, the overall dataset was divided into a training set comprising 3,498 compounds and a test set of 483 compounds. Detailed clustering information with number of data point of training and test sets is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The training and test datasets were provided to participants in CSV format, containing SMILES representations of each compound alongside their corresponding in vitro human and mouse liver microsomal stability values. No 3D structure files (e.g., SDF) were provided. To enhance reproducibility and support future research, the complete dataset, including SMILES and metabolic stability values, is provided as Supplementary Data 2 (Excel file). A t-SNE visualization (Fig. 1) confirms that the test set is well distributed within the training set, ensuring representative coverage. Furthermore, the metabolic stability distributions in human (Fig. 2A) and mouse (Fig. 2B) liver microsomes show that the test set was sampled proportionally while maintaining adequate coverage across all stability ranges.

Evaluation

The scoring criteria for the JUMP AI 2023 competition are divided into two main categories: Quantitative Evaluation (60%) and Qualitative Evaluation (40%). The quantitative evaluation focuses primarily on the predictive accuracy of the model, while the qualitative evaluation emphasizes the methodological rigor and broader applicability of the approach. The quantitative evaluation (60%) is driven by the RMSE score of remaining percentage in human or mouse liver microsomes, which measures the prediction accuracy on the private test set. This score, which represents the leaderboard (https://dacon.io/competitions/official/236127/leaderboard), is central to assessing how well the model generalizes to unseen data during the competition period. The score was calculated using the following formula:

As lower RMSE values show better model performance, the raw RMSE scores were converted into relative scores for ranking. For the quantitative evaluation (60%), the submission with the lowest (best) RMSE was assigned a score of 60 points. The scores for all the other submissions were then calculated by dividing the best RMSE value of the top team by each participant's RMSE value and multiplying by 60. This scoring method ensured that better-performing models, which had lower RMSE values, received higher standardized scores. The best model got 60 points.

In addition, the qualitative evaluation (40%) considers key factors beyond numerical performance. Modeling Strategy (10%) evaluates the sophistication and relevance of the approach, focusing on the integration of domain-specific knowledge. Data Preprocessing and Validation Strategy (10%) reviews how effectively participants prepare and validate the data, ensuring robustness. Experimental Management (10%) assesses how systematically participants explore different techniques and hyperparameters. Finally, Applicability and Generalizability (10%) evaluates the potential for the model to be applied in broader contexts beyond the competition.

Overview of top five teams’ approaches

All five award-winning teams employed graph neural network (GNN)-based architectures [8] as the core component of their predictive models. Common properties across all approaches included the use of molecular features such as AlogP, molecular weight and hydrogen bond donors/acceptors. These varied modeling strategies highlight the importance of tailoring architectures to address the inherent complexity of ADME-related prediction tasks. Furthermore, the results demonstrate that integrating advanced techniques such as contrastive learning, attention-based pooling, and multi-task learning can deliver superior predictive performance compared to conventional GNN-based approaches. Datu enhanced existing GNN architectures by incorporating chemical ring information into their molecular graph representations. They applied node masking prediction and contrastive learning-based pretraining to improve molecular representation robustness, particularly in handling interspecies variability. The team emphasized species-specific differences by incorporating distinct human and mouse metabolic stability features into their model. Notably, they referred to MetaboGNN [9] and addressed limitations in conventional GNNs when dealing with flexible metabolic mechanisms and interspecies variation. Interestingly, the team also noted that species-specific differences in CYP2E1 expression levels influence compound metabolism between human and mouse microsomes [10].

Suleezard and Silryeokeuro Malhae both adopted the Directed Message Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN) [11] as their base architecture, recognizing its ability to capture bond-directional reactivity information critical for metabolic pathways. Suleezard enhanced their model’s interpretability and predictive power by integrating extensive molecular and atomic feature engineering, including custom descriptor generation and a unique sample weighting strategy. They additionally utilized ChatGPT (https://chatgpt.com/) to analyze functional group patterns, and combined AlogP and LogD values at a 7:3 ratio as a custom feature for improved prediction performance Table 5.

Meanwhile, Silryeokeuro Malhae extended the D-MPNN architecture with a Global Multi-Head Attention Pooling (GMHAP) mechanism to improve global feature aggregation and context awareness. The team further applied multi-task learning to simultaneously predict human and mouse liver microsomal stability values. For pretraining, they utilized publicly available data from the Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) [12], including metabolic data for five CYP isoforms (16,364 data points) and additional human and rat stability data (9,773 data points) from a 2022 study [13]. Model weights pretrained on this combined dataset were subsequently transferred and fine-tuned on the competition dataset.

Dimer employed a Graph Transformer model, GraphGPS [14], to address the locality limitations of standard GNNs by capturing long-range dependencies and complex molecular graph structures. Their strategy involved extensive data pruning based on Monte Carlo test loss estimation, as well as feature space linear transformations to improve model generalization and training efficiency. In their multi-task learning approach, the team incorporated eight external datasets, including 22 TDC tasks [12] and NCATS ADME datasets, alongside the competition’s human and mouse microsomal stability data. Notably, they reported that data pruning effectively eliminated outliers and led to significant improvements in predictive performance.

Finally, Yakgwa Donut implemented MolCLR [15], a contrastive pretraining method for molecular graph representations, to enhance model performance in data-scarce scenarios. They transferred the pretrained molecular embeddings into their downstream predictive model and incorporated a comprehensive set of domain-specific descriptors, including physicochemical properties (e.g., logP, solvation energy predicted via C3Net), SMARTS-based functional group patterns associated with known metabolic reactions, and over 30 electronic structure descriptors derived from quantum chemical simulations (e.g., ionization potential, electrophilicity, dipole moment, and electrostatic potential surfaces).

Example of good and bad test compounds

To further evaluate model performance beyond aggregate metrics, we conducted a case-based analysis by comparing the prediction results of Datu and Suleezard, the two highest-scoring teams. Specifically, we identified the top 10 compounds with the highest and lowest prediction errors from each model. From this comparison, two compounds consistently exhibited high prediction accuracy, while one compound showed poor predictive performance in both models.

Interestingly, the two well-predicted compounds, Test 222 and Test 261, displayed notably different metabolic stability values between human and mouse liver microsomes, yet both models accurately captured these interspecies differences. This indicates that well-trained AI models can effectively handle variations even when the same chemical structure yields different outcomes across species. In contrast, Test 323 demonstrated poor prediction accuracy across both models. Structural analysis suggested that this compound was likely an outlier, with limited similarity to other compounds within the training and test datasets. Despite being clustered alongside 121 other compounds, its highest 2D similarity to any test set compound was relatively low (maximum similarity score: 0.645, data not shown). These results highlight a common challenge in AI-driven prediction tasks: reduced performance for structurally unique or underrepresented compounds in the training data.

These case analyses highlight the complexity of ADME-related prediction tasks. They also underscore that, while well-optimized models can effectively handle compounds with clear metabolic patterns, prediction accuracy tends to decline for structurally unique compounds not adequately represented in the training set.

Conclusion

The JUMP AI 2023 drug discovery competition successfully demonstrated the growing role of AI-driven approaches in pharmaceutical research in South Korea. With participation from over 1,400 individuals, the event reflected the emergence of a strong data-centric research ecosystem, even within a field that has traditionally faced a shortage of AI specialists. The quality and diversity of the submitted models indicate significant progress in the application of AI technologies for drug discovery, positioning South Korea as a promising contributor to the global AI-enabled pharmaceutical research community.

This competition also emphasized the practical challenges and opportunities associated with predictive modeling for metabolic stability and highlighted the importance of fostering interdisciplinary expertise in bio-health convergence research. As the pharmaceutical sector increasingly adopts AI technologies, such collaborative competitions play a valuable role in promoting innovation, providing researchers with access to real-world datasets, and accelerating the development and validation of AI methodologies.

In alignment with the national strategy for digital bio-innovation, the open sharing of high-quality domestic datasets and active engagement from researchers will help cultivate a dynamic and sustainable research environment. Continued support for initiatives like JUMP AI 2023 is expected to strengthen South Korea’s international competitiveness and contribute to future advancements in AI-driven drug discovery and development.
