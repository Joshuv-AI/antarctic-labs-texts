The following fictional case is intended as a learning tool within the Pathology Competencies for Medical Education (PCME), a set of national standards for teaching pathology. These are divided into three basic competencies: Disease Mechanisms and Processes, Organ System Pathology, and Diagnostic Medicine and Therapeutic Pathology. For additional information, and a full list of learning objectives for all three competencies, see https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acpath.2023.100086.1

Primary objective

Objective NSC1.2: Classification of Brain Tumors. Compare and contrast the common types of brain tumors with respect to their location, age, incidence, pathogenesis, molecular basis, morphology, and prognosis.

Competency 2: Organ System Pathology; Topic: Nervous System-Central Nervous System (NSC); Learning Goal 1: CNS Neoplasia.

Patient presentation

A 61-year-old woman presents to the emergency department for evaluation of a new-onset seizure. The patient is accompanied by her daughter, who witnessed the patient's seizure. The patient's daughter states she was walking with her mother when her mother appeared confused and had twitching movements in her left arm. Subsequently, her mother collapsed. She summoned emergency medical services. The daughter states that the seizure lasted for several minutes, whereby the patient was unconscious, and that this was the first time her mother had experienced a seizure. The daughter states that her mother has no significant health issues. Other than the birth of her children, she has never been hospitalized. She is not on any medications. She states her mother is allergic to shellfish, and her social history is unremarkable for recreational drugs, tobacco, or alcohol use. She states there is no family history of seizure disorders or other pertinent medical conditions.

Diagnostic findings, Part 1

Vital signs on physical examination are as follows: blood pressure of 108/66 mm Hg, heart rate of 72 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 17 breaths per minute, temperature of 98.2 °F, and oxygen saturation of 98%. Body mass index is 21.8 kg/m2. Cardiac examination reveals a normal S1 and S2 with regular rate and rhythm without murmurs, heaves, or gallops. Lungs are clear to auscultation. The abdomen is nondistended with normoactive bowel sounds. Neurological examination is remarkable for postictal symptoms, predominantly confusion and drowsiness. No focal deficits are present.

Questions/discussion points, Part 1

What disorders are in the differential diagnosis based on the clinical presentation?

Seizures are defined by periods of sudden outbursts of uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain. They are divided into two main types, generalized and focal. The clinical presentation varies depending on the type of seizure, its progression, and severity. Focal seizures may evolve into generalized seizures.2, 3, 4

Seizures are also classified based on etiology as provoked (nonepileptic) and unprovoked (epileptic). Symptomatic seizures (e.g. provoked as a consequences of a brain lesion) are seen in the setting of a wide array of etiologies, including ischemia, genetic abnormalities, central nervous system (CNS) infections, neoplasms, trauma, metabolic disturbances, medications, illicit drug use and withdrawal, and from unknown causes (idiopathic) (Table 1).2, 3, 4 New onset epilepsy is a consideration for unprovoked seizures where no obvious immediate cause is identified.5 Epilepsy diagnosis requires a minimum of two unprovoked seizure events.2,4

What additional workup is needed to establish a diagnosis?

Additional workup needed to establish the diagnosis for the patient's seizure includes a complete blood count (CBC), a comprehensive metabolic profile (CMP), and imaging, (computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), of the brain. If no cause is immediately discovered from the above laboratory tests and imaging, electroencephalography (EEG) should be performed within 24 hours of the seizure.

Diagnostic findings, Part 2

Based on the clinical presentation, a CBC, CMP, and initial MRI scan are ordered.5, 6, 7, 8 The CBC and CMP are unremarkable.

How do the MRI findings narrow the differential diagnosis?

The presence of the well-circumscribed, extra-axial, dural-based mass is consistent with a meningioma. Meningiomas generally compress, but do not invade, adjacent brain parenchyma. They usually appear as homogenous, isodense/hyperdense, extra-axial, dural-based masses on noncontrast CT with or without associated hyperostosis of the underlying calvarium. They demonstrate avid postcontrast enhancement on postcontrast CT. On MRI, typical meningioma signal characteristics include T1 signal isointense to gray matter, T2 signal isointense to hyperintense to gray matter, and intense, homogenous postcontrast enhancement. Diffusion restriction can sometimes be seen with higher-grade meningiomas.5,6,9 Other pathologies to consider in the imaging differential diagnosis of a dural-based mass are solitary fibrous tumor of the dura, dural metastasis, and other sarcomas.6,9,10

Included in the imaging differential diagnosis for an intra-axial brain mass are primary brain tumors and metastases. In adults, the most common brain tumors are metastatic tumors. Metastatic tumors are secondary tumors that originate most frequently from lung, breast, skin, kidney, and gastrointestinal tract neoplasms. Involvement of the meninges is common.10 Imaging typically shows a mass located on the gray-white matter junction, sharp demarcation, and local peritumoral edema.

What is the incidence of intracranial tumors?

The CNS tumors are distinguished by age, sex, anatomic location, clinical course, imaging features, diagnosis, grade, and molecular features. The CNS tumors are also often associated with several hereditary syndromes that include tuberous sclerosis, von Hippel-Lindau Disease, Li-Fraumeni Syndrome, Gorlin Syndrome, and neurofibromatosis.10

The World Health Organization (WHO) divides CNS tumors into several categories based on morphology and molecular features. The more common categories are outlined in Table 2.11 Primary brain tumors can be benign or malignant.10, 11, 12, 13 Primary brain tumors are categorized as glial or nonglial. Nonglial tumors can be derived from various tissue types, including nerves, blood vessels, and the meninges, while glial tumors are primarily derived from glial cells. In the most recent Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States (CBTRUS) review of CNS tumors diagnosed between 2017 and 2021, the average annual age-adjusted incidence rate of primary adult intracranial tumors was 25.34 per 100,000 individuals.13 Approximately 27.1% of all CNS tumors were malignant and 72.9% were nonmalignant, which makes nonmalignant tumors more than twice as common as malignant tumors. Gliomas (i.e. astrocytoma, ependymoma, and oligodendroglioma) accounted for 22.9% of all CNS tumors. Using the 2016 WHO Classification of Tumors, the CBTRUS stated the most commonly occurring malignant CNS tumor was glioblastoma (14.0% of all tumors and 51.5% of all malignant tumors) and the most common nonmalignant tumor was meningioma (41.4% of all tumors and 56.8% of all nonmalignant tumors). Meningiomas are typically more common in women.13

What are some clinicopathological features of adult CNS tumors?

Clinical features are divided into 1) generalized nonspecific symptoms (fatigue, focal or generalized seizures, altered mental status, and symptoms related to increased intracranial pressure (headache, nausea, vomiting, and papilledema) and 2) focal neurologic deficits based on the location of the tumor. Nonspecific symptoms tend to be more prevalent. Endocrine and visual problems may be seen with pituitary tumors. Hearing loss is associated with vestibular schwannomas. Schaff and Mellinghoff provide a detailed outline of symptoms based on location.12 For example, frontal lobe tumors, which account for 25% of CNS tumors, may demonstrate contralateral motor deficits, personality changes, cognitive decline, and expressive aphasia if the lesion is in the dominant hemisphere. Patients with temporal lobe tumors, which account for 18% of CNS tumors, may demonstrate auditory perception disturbances, impaired memory, seizures, contralateral homonymous superior quadrantanopia, and receptive aphasia if the dominant hemisphere is involved.12

Brain tumors are usually diagnosed based on clinical presentation and medical imaging (CT or MRI). Based on these findings, tissue may be obtained by craniotomy to establish a diagnosis and guide further management. Pathologic diagnosis and grade of CNS tumors are based on morphology and molecular features. Gliomas, as stated, can be further categorized into astrocytomas, glioblastoma, oligodenrogliomas, and pilocytic astrocytomas (Table 3). Except for pilocytic astrocytomas ,which classically affect the cerebellum, astrocytomas, glioblastomas, and oligodendrogliomas can present anywhere within the cerebral hemispheres. The latter three primary brain tumors predominantly affect the adult population, while pilocytic astrocytomas are often seen within the pediatric population. Astrocytomas, derived from neoplastic astrocytes, often harbor mutations in the TP53 tumor suppressor gene, CDKN2A/B, and IDH (isocitrate dehydrogenase) mutations, which are implicated in abnormal cellular metabolism. Glioblastomas, notably the IDH wild-type variants, are aggressive and malignant glial CNS neoplasms; they are often observed harboring aberrations in crucial regulatory cell cycle genes such as telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). Alterations in the BRAF gene, which is implicated in uncontrolled cell division in multiple malignancies, are frequently seen in pilocytic astrocytomas.10

Ependymomas, which are predominantly located along the ependymal-lined ventricular system, may exhibit ZFTA or YAP1 fusions, or MYCN amplifications that are implicated in abnormalities in DNA transcription and synthesis. Among glioneuronal tumors, dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors usually occur in the pediatric population and involve the superficial temporal lobe. Dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumors often harbor FGFR1 gene alterations that are implicated in cell division processes. Gangliogliomas are rare CNS tumors that affect children and young adults and are also categorized as glioneuronal tumors. This type of CNS lesion often presents within the temporal lobe and is also often seen harboring BRAF gene mutations.10

Outline common brain tumors that affect children

The CNS tumors account for 20% of childhood tumors and are the most common solid tumor in children.17,18 The average annual age-adjusted incidence of brain tumors is 5.65/100,000 population.18 The WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System integrates morphology with molecular features.6,11,16,18 Well-documented brain tumors that affect children include, but are not limited to, pilocytic astrocytoma, high-grade astrocytoma, medulloblastoma, pituitary tumors, and ependymal tumors.

What pathologic features are observed in meningiomas and how are they classified?

Meningiomas are diverse. Macroscopically, meningiomas vary in consistency and are usually firm on sectioning. They tend to be discrete, smooth-surfaced lesions of variable size that compress the adjacent brain parenchyma. The WHO recognizes several histologic subtypes based on morphology.6,10,11,13, 14, 15, 16,19, 20, 21 The three most common subtypes are meningothelial, fibrous, and transitional.10,14,15,19, 20, 21 According to the WHO classification, meningiomas are divided into three distinctive histological grades with varying outcomes as shown in Table 4.10,11,14,15,19, 20, 21 Eighty percent of meningiomas are benign and classified as grade 1. The WHO grade 2 meningiomas are classified as atypical, higher-grade lesions and account for 5–15% of meningiomas. The WHO grade 3 meningiomas are classified as malignant, higher-grade lesions, accounting for 1–3% of meningiomas.6,10,22

In the absence of brain invasion and aggressive histologic features, the rhabdoid and papillary subtypes may behave as a CNS WHO Grade 1 meningioma.11,14,19

Grading is based on several factors that include mitotic index, accounting for genetic changes, aggressive histologic features, and histologic subtype.14 Using mitotic index, tumors with less than four mitoses per 10 consecutive high power fields are grade 1. Tumors with 4–19 mitoses per 10 consecutive high power fields are grade 2 tumors, and tumors with 20 or more mitoses per 10 consecutive high-power fields are grade 3 tumors. Tumors with any three of the following five histologic features (prominent nucleoli, necrosis, sheet-like growth, small cell formation and hypercellularity) are classified as grade 2.14,19 Tumors with invasion of brain parenchyma are also classified as grade 2. The chordoid and clear-cell histologic subtypes have been classified as grade 2, and the rhabdoid and papillary histologic subtypes as grade 3 lesions (Table 4). The 2021 WHO Classification of Tumors of the CNS comments that, in the absence of aggressive histologic features and increased mitotic activity, the papillary and rhabdoid subtypes should not be considered high-grade based on histologic subtype alone.11,14,16 Specific genetic changes to categorize tumors as grade 3 are discussed in the subsequent paragraph.

Molecular biomarkers (SMARCE1, BAP1, KLF4/TRAF7 mutations, TERT promoter mutation and/or homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B) have been involved in grading and classifying meningiomas.11,19,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29 SMARCE1 is a gene that encodes a protein required for transcriptional activation of genes. It is associated with the clear cell-meningioma subtype. BAP1, a tumor suppressor gene involved in DNA damage, signaling, and repair, is associated with the rhabdoid and papillary subtypes. TRAF7 encodes the TRAF7 protein–seen in approximately 25% of meningiomas that lack the NF2 mutation. TRAF7 is involved in transmitting signals within the TNF receptor family. It, along with KLF4, a gene that encodes a protein that belongs to a family of transcription factors involved in the cell cycle, is associated with the secretory subtype.11 The TERT is a gene encoding for a polymerase that maintains telomere ends and plays a role in cellular senescence. The TERT mutations and homozygous CDKN2A gene deletions, a tumor suppressor gene that encodes the proteins p16/INK4a and p14/ARF, have been reported in high-grade meningiomas (grade 3) regardless of subtype and are associated with early recurrence.10,16,20,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27

Halabi et al. provide an excellent overview and outline many of the molecular markers based on tumor location.22 More research is needed to determine the predictive value of each of the meningioma-related biomarkers listed above. The use of biomarkers will have a significant role in future classifications and patient management6,10,16,22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29

What is the underlying pathogenesis of meningiomas?

Meningiomas are the most common primary brain tumor in adults and more common in women.6,12,13,20,30 The majority are benign (CNS WHO grade 1) and are slow-growing with an excellent prognosis.9,19,20 Meningiomas originate from meningothelial cells in the arachnoid, which are themselves are thought to be derived from mesenchymal pluripotent progenitor stem cells.6,10,31 They are dural-based, extra-axial lesions. Common locations include the vicinity of the superior sagittal sinus, cerebral convexities, the wing of the sphenoid, olfactory groove, sella turcica, and foramen magnum.10 Most meningiomas arise sporadically.6,10,20 The development of meningiomas is twice as likely in women as in men, suggesting a role of hormonal involvement in tumor formation.6,10, 31-33Ionizing radiation exposure, especially when exposure occurs during childhood, and neurofibromatosis gene (NF2) mutations are well-established in the predisposition for meningioma development.8,10,20,32,34 Tumor predisposition disorders are another factor.8,10,16,20,26 The finding of multiple meningiomas associated with glial tumors, vestibular schwannomas, or ependymal tumors of the cervical spinal cord suggests neurofibromatosis type 2.10,15 Roughly half of patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 have multiple meningiomas.10,15 For sporadic meningiomas not associated with neurofibromatosis type 2, the somatic loss-of-function mutation in the NF2 tumor suppressor gene on chromosome 22 (22q) is commonplace. Of the remainder, mutations in the Hedgehog pathway, DNA repair genes, and transcription factors have been identified. 10,33 In individuals without NF2 mutations, mutations involving TRAF7, KLF4, AKT1, and SMO are observed.24,26

How do patients with grade 1 meningiomas present?

Presentation is based on tumor location.6 Symptomatic patients present with vague nonlocalizing symptoms such as headache. Besides headaches, paresis and altered mental status are findings. Seizures are the presenting symptom in approximately 30% of patients with meningiomas in some studies, especially with meningiomas arising at parasagittal sites.6,8,17,20,21,30,33,34 Seizures may be focal with or without loss of awareness or may progress to a generalized seizure. Risk factors for seizures are peritumoral edema >1 cm, location of the tumor adjacent to the cortex of the temporal, parietal, or frontal lobes, male sex (more aggressive than female) and age younger than 55.34 Some patients with small meningiomas are asymptomatic, with the tumor discovered incidentally at autopsy or by imaging studies for a head or neck lesion (Fig. 5).8, 9, 10,17,21,34 In certain patients, neurologic deficits on physical examination are due to compression of the brain parenchyma.8, 9, 10,20,21

How are meningiomas treated?

The prognosis for meningiomas varies depending on the size and location of the tumor, histological grade, and surgical accessibility.6,8,12,17 In general, meningiomas are thought to have a good prognosis. Therefore, most meningioma cases are monitored, especially if a meningioma is not compressing a vital brain structure, is small and relatively asymptomatic, or is found in elderly patients. The first-line treatment for meningiomas is surgical excision and can be curative if the tumor is resected in totality.8,12,17,33 Craniotomy with tumor excision, however, is not without risk. Possible complications from tumor resection include cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, bleeding, infection, and damage to nearby brain tissue. General surgical complications that include infection, pneumonia, deep vein thromboses (DVTs), pulmonary emboli, and anesthesia complications are concerns worth considering with respect to surgical management. In circumstances where surgery is not feasible, first-line treatment fails, the tumor recurs, or residual tumor remains after resection, radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy can be implemented. Whether patients with meningiomas choose to undergo surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, or observation is based on various factors, including individual patient preferences and values.8,12,17,33

What are recommendations for patients diagnosed with a meningioma?

Based on the clinical presentation, imaging, and pathologic findings, patients with suspected meningiomas should follow-up with a neurosurgeon to discuss treatment options and decide whether surgical excision, radiation or chemotherapy, or supportive measures are most appropriate. Furthermore, adjunctive therapies and treatments can be considered for a patient experiencing seizures or other neurological deficits.

Patients with prior seizure activity need to carefully consider whether they are fit for driving and/or operating heavy machinery.33 Patients with meningiomas experiencing neurological focal deficits or seizures should further discuss with their healthcare provider on what types of daily activities they can perform and whether they need accommodations to perform specific daily tasks. Additionally, each state regulates driver's license eligibility. Patients need to comply with their states' driving requirements postseizure.
