Number Needed to Teach (N2T2): Bringing Additional Context to the Evaluation of CME Outcomes
Authors: Derek T. Dietze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNumber needed to treat is a well-established concept in medicine that distils the benefit of a clinical intervention into a single, intuitive value. This paper discusses practical applications of number needed to teach (N2T2), a parallel metric for continuing medical education that quantifies the number of learners who must complete an activity and evaluation for one to achieve a meaningful educational benefit. While the concept has been considered in graduate education of future healthcare professionals, this paper describes the rationale and practical application of N2T2 using a hypothetical example focused on glycaemic status monitoring in type 2 diabetes for CE/CME professionals and participants. By applying pre- and post-activity assessment data to a prespecified improvement threshold, educators can calculate N2T2 to summarise learner gains in knowledge, competence, or performance. Methodological considerations such as paired versus unpaired responses, activity context, learner baseline proficiency and alignment with SMART, Link-SMART and TACT objectives are discussed. It is important to emphasise that N2T2 should be interpreted within the specific context of each activity’s goals, audience and outcomes. In the absence of systematic use or established benchmarks for N2T2, early adoption and shared learning within the community are encouraged. N2T2 offers a complementary tool for expressing educational impact clearly and consistently, particularly when applied to longitudinal programmes or multi-activity outcomes. As with number needed to treat in medicine, N2T2 has the potential to bring greater clarity and practicality to how we understand and communicate the value of education. N2T2 is a timely and accessible innovation as we continue to refine educational outcomes measurement together.

Introduction

The concept of “number needed to treat” is used in medicine to quantify how many patients need to receive an intervention to prevent a disease occurrence or outcome in one patient [1]. It was introduced in 1988 and further developed by Cook and Sackett in 1995 to help healthcare providers contextualise clinical trial results with their calculus of risk-benefit in real-world treatment decisions [1,2]. The intention is to summarise treatment effects into a single, reasonably intuitive number that can better support the clinician and their discussion with a patient. Briefly, if there is a 5% difference in the occurrence of outcome “X” with an intervention versus placebo over a certain time period, we can say the intervention prevented 5 events for every 100 patients who were treated, or “1 out of 20”. We can then invert this proportion to say that 20 patients would need to receive the intervention to prevent 1 occurrence of outcome “X” over the same period. The value of “number needed to treat” is in putting an aggregate result into the context of a larger population, and for this reason its use has endured and evolved for more than 30 years.

As in medicine, the process of measuring the effectiveness of interventions is robust and ever evolving in continuing medical education (CME). The prevalent application of Moore’s educational outcomes framework (2009) [3], which was elaborated with actionable suggestions in 2018 [4], has been indispensable in advancing the thoughtful, structural assessment of educational value. In addition to assessing changes in knowledge, we are collectively and routinely working to evaluate changes in confidence, competence, performance and patient- and population-level outcomes. We are simultaneously evaluating learner needs and preferences while applying the principles of instructional design and seeking best practices in educational delivery strategies and formats – all contributing to a pipeline of research about the conduct and outcomes of CME itself [5–7].

With such a healthy, evidence-based discussion thriving in the CME community, it is tempting to ask how we can further support the application of our learnings into real-world decisions. A worthwhile effort may be to translate the intention of number needed to treat in medicine to our use of Number Needed To Teach (N2T2) in CME. Our work may be further supported by the distillation of outcomes into a simple, intuitive summary metric as N2T2. For example, N2T2 may help us to apply pre/post evaluation responses into uniquely concise measures of educational impact across CME activities, and to lend additional context to our learnings about learning as medicine has done with learnings about treatment effect. If two CME activities produce similar improvements in learner outcomes, N2T2 can help quantify how many participants are required for one learner to achieve a meaningful benefit in each programme, helping planners or supporters compare the relative educational efficiency of activities when prioritising programme design, scale or resource allocation, or when communicating programme impact to organisational leadership. Just as number needed to treat is measured at the level of individual cases and clinical outcomes, so can N2T2 be applied at the level of individual educational objectives and outcomes.

A conceptual commentary considering N2T2 in the realm of academic health profession education has been described by Low and colleagues [8], building on work by Dolan et al (2015) that explored “number needed to educate” for medical residents to impact bone health screening [9]. These contributions highlight the potential for such metrics in an educational context. However, to date, N2T2 has not been translated into common practice for accredited CME programmes where practical tools for interpreting changes in educational outcomes are an area of concerted attention and effort. The purpose of this Brief Report is not to assert the conceptual novelty of N2T2, but to support its practical application in CE/CME settings where educational outcome data are routinely collected and may benefit from further synthesis into interpretable summary metrics. Therefore, this paper is focused on the practical use of N2T2 in CE/CME, including rationale, calculation and reporting using theoretical applications and examples of direct relevance to the CE/CME practitioner.

Calculating Number Needed to Teach (N2T2)

The potential applications of N2T2 in CME are numerous, but first we will walk through a practical example of how N2T2 can be used in a CME activity evaluation. Our goal is to determine the educational benefit of an intervention. That is, the percentage of participants with an improvement in educational outcome “X” that is attributable to the CME activity. This will allow us to calculate N2T2, to see how many activity and evaluation completers are needed for one to take away a meaningful educational benefit. In this context, “completers” refers to learners who responded to outcome-related questions, typically knowledge or competence in pre/post assessments or confidence and intent-to-change in evaluations, which depends on how the programme designer structures the measurement of educational outcomes. As with the use of “treat” in “number needed to treat”, the term “teach” in N2T2 refers not to the delivery of content alone, but to the number of learners required for one to achieve a measurable improvement in an educational outcome. It is also worth noting that pre- and post-activity questions may themselves reinforce learning, a phenomenon described in educational research as the testing effect [10]; in this context, such questions are considered part of the instructional design rather than a separate source of bias.

In this exercise, we will consider a hypothetical CME activity concept entitled Effective Monitoring of Glycemic Status in Adults with Type 2 Diabetes. This hypothetical example is intentionally simplified to illustrate the calculation and interpretation of N2T2 and is not intended to represent a comprehensive or validated educational outcomes assessment. We will focus on one of the learning objectives for this activity which is a fundamental practice point aligned with common quality measures. In this case, a quality measure published by the US National Committee for Quality Assurance (NCQA) based on clinical practice recommendations from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [11,12]. Learning objective #2 for the activity is to “identify the recommended glycemic status thresholds for adult patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus”. We are using pre- and post-activity questions to assess changes in competence and knowledge where activity completion includes completion of the educational content, in line with the conventions of the Outcomes Standardisation Project [13], but also, for this activity, completion of the post-activity evaluation. There are 3 questions evaluating achievement of this specific learning objective (Appendix Figure A1). While we do not have a control group in our example activity, calculating N2T2 can be done by comparing responses between groups, as discussed in Appendix A2.

After the CME activity, we have pre- and post-activity responses from 100 of the learners. We used an audience response system, so we can connect each participant’s (anonymous) pre- and post-activity responses, known as “paired” responses. Paired responses are good because they are a direct measure of individual-level change. We can still calculate N2T2 without paired responses by using scores from the entire audience, known as “unpaired” responses. Using unpaired responses is a valid approach but will likely have more variability in the results. Please see Appendix A2 for more detail on calculating N2T2 with unpaired responses.

Our analysis spreadsheet has 100 rows and 3 columns: Learner ID#, pre-activity score and post-activity score. However, the calculation of N2T2 requires a frequency count (occurrences of outcome “X” out of the total population), and changes in pre/post-activity responses are not frequency counts. Therefore, we count the number of participants whose changes in pre- and post-activity scores met or exceeded a prespecified improvement threshold. Improvement thresholds are commonly used in medicine to interpret the clinical significance of changes in outcomes, particularly with patient-reported outcomes. For example, survey-based research indicates that a 24.5-point improvement in the 100-point Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) Physical Functioning domain is considered a meaningful improvement for people with rheumatoid arthritis, which is used to identify meaningful improvement for each patient [14]. There are several ways to establish a relevant improvement threshold, which are summarised in Appendix A3. In our hypothetical activity, learning objective #2 is evaluated over 3 questions. Statistics can help us find an improvement threshold that is not too lenient or too strict based on the standard deviation of pre-activity scores. Our spreadsheet software calculates the standard deviation of pre-activity scores to be 0.93. Following a common standard, we use half of the standard deviation (0.93/2 = 0.47 in this case) as a benchmark. Since we need to see improvement in a full question (not “0.47” of a question), and 1 whole question is larger than 0.47 of a question, then we know that getting 1 more question correct on the post-activity evaluation is a reasonable threshold for improvement.

From learning objectives to N2T2.

While the illustrative example used here focuses on changes in competence and knowledge, N2T2 can be used for any educational outcome with clear linkage of objectives, content and evaluation across a variety of frameworks and models of behaviour change. For example, the educational outcomes may be applicable to changes in confidence or changes in clinical performance. If we had evaluated confidence rather than knowledge, we may have asked participants “How confident are you in identifying the most appropriate glycemic control target for adult patients with type 2 diabetes based on individual clinical factors, such as age, comorbidities, and lifestyle considerations?” with 5 response options ranging from “1, Not at all confident” to “4, Very confident” and “5, Extremely confident”. Then we could determine the percentage of responders who reported being “very confident” or “extremely confident” before and after the activity. N2T2 in Practice: Key Considerations for Interpretation and Reporting

There are several important considerations when including N2T2 in educational outcomes measurement. Most importantly, as with any evaluation of educational effectiveness, we need to have high-quality objectives and evaluation questions that are directly aligned with the activity content. This linkage is well facilitated by the use of structured frameworks such as SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound) and TACT (target, action, context, and time) [15–17]. Taking it one step further, the LINK-SMART framework was designed specifically to support the creation of CME learning objectives and outcomes questions that are directly connected to the gaps identified in the needs assessment [18]. In our first example activity, the evaluation questions about measuring glycaemic status can be traced directly through learning objective #2 to the published NCQA quality measures and ADA clinical practice recommendations [11,12]. When we have a direct line of sight through the learning objectives, content, and evaluation questions, we can apply measures like N2T2 with confidence to one activity and to all the activities using the same linked objectives, content, and questions. In this way, the inherent simplicity of N2T2 can help us to look across variations in educational design, format and delivery with greater efficiency and fairness when learning objectives are used longitudinally and in multi-activity programmes.

Once we have completed our calculations and are ready to report our N2T2 findings, it is important to note that N2T2 should be reported and interpreted as a function of important contextual factors, including: • Learner population, baseline proficiency• Instructional design• Disease state• Outcome measurement methods and instruments• Learning objectives and domain (e.g. knowledge, confidence)• Type of response data, improvement threshold and rationale• Activity format and duration• Other defining aspects of the activity

For example, we might report the N2T2 for our type 2 diabetes activity by saying: “We estimated an N2T2 of 3.7 for this 1-hour, didactic online activity for general practitioners managing adults with type 2 diabetes. One of every 3.7 learners who completed the activity and evaluation achieved a meaningful educational benefit by providing 1 additional correct response on the post-activity assessment aligned with the learning objective for identifying recommended glycemic thresholds for adults with type 2 diabetes”.

N2T2 is impacted by the level of effort required by a clinician to make a change in the educational outcome, which can vary tremendously if we are aiming to increase knowledge of a common topic or to change the process of care and clinical outcomes in a health system. We might expect knowledge-related N2T2 scores to be relatively low, reflecting the shorter-term nature of the objective and activity, and those for performance-related N2T2 scores to be much higher, reflecting the process-oriented efforts required to impart meaningful, measurable change at this level.

Pre-activity proficiency in the educational topic is a particularly important consideration when reporting and interpreting N2T2, just as patients’ baseline risk influences how we interpret number needed to treat for clinical outcomes. Failing to account for differences in baseline proficiency could lead to misleading comparisons of N2T2 across different audiences and other factors. For our type 2 diabetes activity, if participants had a high pre-activity knowledge level, there may not have been much improvement in post-activity responses because there may not have been much room for improvement. This would make our N2T2 higher and could lead us to wonder if the educational activity itself was suboptimal, which may not have been the case at all. For example, an N2T2 of 2.0 may be good if our reason for conducting the activity is to familiarise younger general practitioners with the latest glycaemic status guidelines and quality measures. Alternatively, an N2T2 of 20 may be good if we are trying to approach 100% correct responses among nurse practitioners working in diabetes speciality clinics. Both scores can tell us something useful. The lower N2T2 value may indicate a healthy appetite for improving knowledge among the younger generalists, while the higher value may indicate a strong baseline familiarity that would take near individual-level learning and evaluation to show improvement.

While the illustrative example used here is purposefully straightforward, using a simple knowledge assessment to illustrate the N2T2 calculation, the same approach can be applied to more complex CME outcomes, including competence, performance or longitudinal measures when appropriate data are available. Detailed exploration of these applications is beyond the scope of this manuscript.

Looking Ahead

Our profession sustains a healthy spirit of active development which is not surprising given our focus on productive learning and improvement. As we continue to refine our approaches to educational outcomes measurement, N2T2 can add a concise, practical measure to further contextualise our evaluations of educational value. While it may be tempting to judge an N2T2 value quickly, we should remember that N2T2 is specific to the learning objective, content, and context from which it was derived. This concept has been used to interpret clinical outcomes for decades, but it is relatively new to professional medical education and, as in medicine, we can add it to our existing approaches with care. In time, and with shared experience, we can establish meaningful, credible expectations to help interpret N2T2 results in the context of different clinicians, therapeutic areas, educational outcomes, instructional designs and cost-effectiveness. Indeed, N2T2 may be a powerful tool for professionals seeking to quantify the value of educational impact through cost-effectiveness analysis, which is shown to be substantially underserved in the ever-growing body of literature exploring ways to optimise best practices and outcomes in CME [19].

Our openness to using new metrics represents our continued efforts to investigate and improve educational benefit and value. Just as number needed to treat is calculated for a specific clinical outcome rather than an entire clinical trial, so should N2T2 be calculated for a specific educational outcome and viewed as a summary measure rather than a universal indicator of impact. The current lack of benchmarks should not discourage early adopters from publishing their N2T2 findings and sharing them with the community. Whether N2T2 becomes a widely adopted tool for comparing educational formats or tracking impact over time will depend on its utility in practice. This paper aims to lower the barrier to entry for its use by CME professionals and to encourage implementation and shared learning. Only our collective experience can help us to establish expectations of N2T2 for different objectives and outcomes. Ultimately, the number needed to implement N2T2 in an upcoming activity is only 1, which is the perfect place to start!
