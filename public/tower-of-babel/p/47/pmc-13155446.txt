The Effect of Transcranial Near-infrared Laser Therapy on Chronic Ischemic Stroke Patients: A Randomized, Single-blind, Sham-controlled Trial
Authors: Merna Hassan, Osama Al Balah, Ahmed Hegazy, El Sayed Satour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscranial photobiomodulation (TPBM) using near-infrared (NIR) light is a novel, non-invasive neuromodulation strategy with potential to enhance neurorecovery. While preclinical studies are promising, clinical evidence in chronic stroke remains limited and inconsistent.ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of 980 nm transcranial NIR laser therapy as an adjunct to standard rehabilitation in improving neurological function in patients with chronic ischemic stroke.MethodsIn this randomized, single-blind, sham-controlled trial, 40 chronic ischemic stroke patients (>6 months post-onset, NIHSS 7–22) were allocated to active treatment (n = 20) or sham control (n = 20). The active group received 980 nm laser therapy (6W, 20 min/session, 3 sessions/week for 8 weeks) applied to the forehead. The sham group underwent identical procedures with the laser inactivated. Both groups received concurrent standard rehabilitation. The primary outcome was the change in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score from baseline to one-week post-treatment. Secondary outcomes included the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS), Barthel Index (BI), and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). The trial was registered with the Cairo University Postgraduate Office (NILES-MAL-CU 24/05/21), no AI or large language model tools were used in the preparation of this manuscript.ResultsNo statistically significant between-group differences were observed in post-treatment NIHSS scores. After adjusting for baseline NIHSS scores using ANCOVA, the adjusted mean difference between groups was 0.6 points (95 % CI: −1.8 to 3.0, p = 0.620; Cohen's d = 0.15). Both groups showed minimal, equivalent improvements from baseline (active: −0.6 ± 2.1 vs. sham: −0.6 ± 1.9; p = 1.000). Secondary outcomes (mRS, BI, MMSE) also revealed no significant differences. Post-hoc power analysis indicated the study was underpowered (12 %) for the observed small effect size. No intervention-related adverse events were reported.ConclusionTranscranial 980 nm NIR laser therapy did not demonstrate superior efficacy over sham treatment when added to standard rehabilitation for chronic ischemic stroke recovery. The intervention was safe and well-tolerated but provided no additional clinical benefit. These findings do not support the clinical use of this specific TPBM protocol in chronic stroke populations, though results are specific to the 980 nm wavelength and chronic phase, and should not be extrapolated to other parameters or acute stroke.

Introduction

Stroke persists as a foremost global cause of mortality and long-term disability, with ischemic stroke constituting approximately 87 % of cases [1]. Although acute interventions like thrombolysis and thrombectomy have advanced, a substantial proportion of survivors endure chronic neurological deficits, severely compromising quality of life [2]. Current chronic-phase management relies heavily on multidisciplinary rehabilitation, yet recovery trajectories often plateau, underscoring an urgent need for innovative, plasticity-enhancing adjunctive therapies [3].

Transcranial photobiomodulation (TPBM), the application of red or near-infrared (NIR) light to the head, has emerged as a promising non-invasive neuromodulation technique. The proposed mechanism involves photon absorption by mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase [30], leading to enhanced cellular metabolism, increased adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, and modulation of reactive oxygen species [4,5,14,17]. Longer NIR wavelengths (e.g., 980 nm) may also activate transient receptor potential (TRP) ion channels, initiating neuroprotective and pro-plasticity signaling cascades [6,19].

We selected 980 nm wavelength for this study based on several theoretical considerations: (1) Tissue penetration: 980 nm light falls within the optical window where absorption by melanin and hemoglobin is reduced, potentially allowing deeper penetration than shorter wavelengths [7]; (2) TRP channel activation: Recent evidence suggests 980 nm may activate transient receptor potential (TRP) ion channels, particularly TRPV4, which could initiate neuroprotective signaling cascades independent of cytochrome c oxidase [6]; and (3) Clinical availability: 980 nm laser devices are widely available for therapeutic applications. However, we acknowledge that most positive preclinical evidence involves 808–810 nm wavelengths that more directly target cytochrome c oxidase absorption peaks. The relationship between wavelength selection and clinical efficacy remains an important research question.

Preclinical models of stroke and traumatic brain injury have consistently reported neuroprotective and cognitive-enhancing effects of TPBM [8,9,23–25]. However, clinical translation has yielded mixed results. While some pilot studies in acute stroke reported positive signals [10,11], larger pivotal trials like NEST-3 failed to demonstrate efficacy [12,13]. Crucially, evidence is particularly sparse for the chronic stroke phase (>6 months), where the brain environment is characterized by completed remodeling and potentially reduced neuroplastic capacity.

1.1. Study rationale and objective

This randomized controlled trial (RCT) was designed to rigorously evaluate the efficacy of a structured 980 nm TPBM protocol, adjunctive to standard care, in patients with chronic ischemic stroke. We hypothesized that active TPBM would yield superior functional outcomes compared to sham treatment.

Methods

2.1. Study design and oversight

This was a single-center, randomized, single-blind, sham-controlled, parallel-group trial conducted at Cairo University. The protocol was approved by the NILES Ethics Committee, Cairo University (Approval Number: NILES-EC-CU 24/7/17) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered as an institutional thesis with the Cairo University Postgraduate Office (NILES-MAL-CU 24/05/21). We acknowledge that prospective registration in an international clinical trials registry (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) was not completed, representing a deviation from contemporary reporting standards. This manuscript adheres to CONSORT 2010 guidelines for reporting randomized controlled trials.

2.2. Participants

Patients were recruited from the outpatient rehabilitation clinic between March and September 2024. Key inclusion criteria were: age 40–85 years; clinical and radiological diagnosis of ischemic stroke >6 months prior; baseline NIHSS score between 7 and 22; medical stability; and capacity to provide consent. Major exclusion criteria included uncontrolled hypertension (>185/110 mmHg) or hypotension (<80/50 mmHg), severe dementia, other major neurological diseases (e.g., advanced multiple sclerosis), seizure disorders, pregnancy, and concurrent participation in another interventional trial.

2.3. Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly allocated (1:1) to active or sham groups using a computer-generated sequence created by an independent statistician. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent statistician not involved in participant enrollment or assessment. Envelopes were stored in a locked cabinet and opened sequentially only after baseline assessments were completed and participant eligibility confirmed. Participants were blinded through identical treatment setups. The treating physician was necessarily unblinded to administer the correct protocol. All outcome assessors and data analysts remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

2.4. Interventions

Active TPBM

Treatment was delivered using a Class IV continuous-wave laser device (980 nm ± 5 nm). The laser probe was placed in direct contact with the skin over the forehead (approximating Brodmann areas 9–10). Each session consisted of 20 min of irradiation at a power output of 6 Watts, resulting in a calculated energy delivery of 7.2 kJ per session. Treatments were administered three times per week for eight consecutive weeks (total 24 sessions).

Sham Control

The identical procedure was followed, including device placement and timing, but the laser emitter was inactive.

Standard Rehabilitation (Both Groups)

All participants continued their prescribed, individualized standard stroke rehabilitation throughout the study period. This included physical therapy, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), strengthening exercises, and standard pharmacological management.

2.5. Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the change in NIHSS total score from baseline to one week after the 8-week intervention period (week 9). The primary endpoint was assessed at one week post-intervention rather than immediately post-treatment to allow for: (1) consolidation of any treatment-induced neuroplastic changes, (2) stabilization of transient physiological effects, and (3) alignment with clinical decision-making timeframes. While this timeframe may not capture delayed effects that emerge over months, it represents a pragmatic balance between detecting meaningful short-term benefit and minimizing loss to follow-up in this chronic stroke population. This approach is consistent with prior TPBM trials in stroke. The NIHSS is a validated 11-item scale assessing stroke severity across consciousness, motor, sensory, and language domains (scores range from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating greater impairment).

Secondary outcomes assessed at the same time-points included: 1) Modified Rankin Scale (mRS; 0–6, measuring global disability), 2) Barthel Index (BI; 0–100, assessing activities of daily living), and 3) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; 0–30, screening cognitive function). All assessments were performed by trained, blinded evaluators.

2.6. Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated based on a clinically meaningful difference of 2.5 points on the NIHSS scale, representing the minimal clinically important difference reported in prior stroke trials. Using a standard deviation of 3.5 points (derived from Lampl et al., 2007 [10] and Zivin et al., 2009 [11]), with two-sided α = 0.05 and 80 % power, we calculated a requirement of 18 participants per group. Accounting for a 10 % dropout rate, we aimed to recruit 20 participants per group (total N = 40). This corresponds to a standardized effect size of Cohen's d = 0.71, representing a moderate-to-large effect.

2.7. Statistical analysis

Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. Baseline characteristics were compared descriptively between groups. Continuous variables were compared using independent t-tests and categorical variables using chi-square tests (or Fisher's exact test when appropriate). Between-group differences in post-treatment scores were analyzed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA), with baseline scores as the covariate. Effect sizes were expressed as Cohen's d with 95 % confidence intervals (CI). Secondary outcomes were analyzed similarly.

Change scores were calculated as post-treatment minus baseline values; thus, negative values indicate improvement (reduction in neurological impairment).

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 to determine the achieved power for detecting the observed effect size (Cohen's d = 0.15) with our sample of 40 participants, α = 0.05, two-tailed. Additional sensitivity analyses included: (1) per-protocol analysis excluding any protocol deviations, and (2) ANCOVA models adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, and time since stroke onset. Exploratory subgroup analyses examined potential treatment effect modification by age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), baseline stroke severity (NIHSS 7–14 vs. 15–22), time since stroke (<12 vs. ≥12 months), and sex, using interaction terms within ANCOVA models. Given their exploratory nature, these subgroup analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). All p-values are reported to three decimal places or as p < 0.001 for very small values. All continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0.

Results

3.1. Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Of 52 patients screened, 40 were randomized (20 per group) and all completed the intervention and follow-up assessments (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were well-balanced between groups (Table 1). The mean age was approximately 63 years, mean time since stroke was approximately 15 months, and the majority had middle cerebral artery territory infarcts (70 %). Baseline NIHSS, mRS, and BI scores were comparable.

3.2. Primary outcome: NIHSS

There was no significant difference in post-treatment NIHSS scores between the active and sham groups. After adjusting for baseline NIHSS scores using ANCOVA, the adjusted mean difference between groups was 0.6 points (95 % CI: −1.8 to 3.0, p = 0.620). The change from baseline was minimal and identical in both groups (−0.6 points, p = 1.000) (Table 2). The effect size was negligible (Cohen's d = 0.15).

3.4. Additional and safety analyses

Exploratory analysis of individual NIHSS domains revealed no differential improvement in any specific domain (Table 3). Post-hoc power analysis for the primary outcome indicated only 12 % power for the observed small effect size. Sensitivity and per-protocol analyses confirmed the robustness of the null findings. Subgroup analyses showed no significant interaction effects for age, stroke severity, time since stroke, or gender. Treatment compliance was excellent (>95 % sessions completed). No device- or procedure-related adverse events were reported.

Discussion

This rigorously conducted RCT found that an 8-week course of transcranial 980 nm NIR laser therapy provided no additional functional benefit over sham treatment when combined with standard rehabilitation for chronic ischemic stroke patients. The intervention was feasible and safe but ineffective for improving neurological status, disability, activities of daily living, or global cognition.

Our null result aligns with the negative findings of the large NEST-3 trial in acute stroke [12] but contrasts with some smaller, positive pilot studies [10,11]. Several factors may explain the lack of efficacy.

First, the chronic phase of stroke may represent a less responsive biological environment. Our patient population, with a mean time since stroke of approximately 15 months, was characterized by completed gliosis, established glial scarring, chronic inflammation, and substantially diminished endogenous neuroplasticity compared to the acute phase [23–25]. The therapeutic time window is a critical determinant of TPBM efficacy: preclinical studies demonstrating neuroprotective effects typically involve treatment within hours to days of injury, when mechanisms such as mitochondrial rescue, reduction of excitotoxicity, and modulation of acute inflammation are most relevant. The biological targets of TPBM—including ATP synthesis enhancement, reduction of oxidative stress, and anti-inflammatory effects—may have limited impact once tissue reorganization is complete and the acute injury cascade has resolved [20]. Conversely, the large negative NEST-3 trial was conducted in acute stroke (within 24 h), suggesting the therapeutic window question is complex and may interact with other factors such as wavelength, dosing, and patient selection. Future research should systematically investigate whether TPBM efficacy differs across stroke phases (hyperacute, acute, subacute, and chronic) and should prioritize earlier intervention when neurobiological targets are most accessible [26].

Second, wavelength selection may be critical. Most positive preclinical and clinical signals involve 808–810 nm light targeting cytochrome c oxidase, whereas our use of 980 nm, chosen for its potential TRP channel effects and tissue penetration [6,7], may not optimally engage the relevant recovery mechanisms [28]. While 980 nm offers theoretical advantages in tissue penetration and alternative mechanistic pathways, the lack of efficacy in our study may reflect insufficient engagement with the primary mitochondrial targets that appear critical for neuroprotection and neurorecovery. We cannot determine whether 808–810 nm wavelengths would have yielded different results in this population [22].

Third, dosimetry and delivery pose challenges. While we used parameters within reported ranges, the optimal energy density, pulse parameters, and treatment frequency for human chronic stroke are unknown [13,17,18]. Furthermore, superficial forehead application may not effectively deliver sufficient photon density to deeper or more posterior lesion sites relevant to our predominantly MCA stroke cohort [15,16]. Recent discoveries regarding meningeal lymphatic vessels and their role in brain waste clearance and neuroinflammation suggest novel targeting strategies for TPBM. Application of photobiomodulation at anatomical sites enriched with meningeal lymphatic vessels—such as along the superior sagittal sinus, transverse sinus, or skull base regions—may enhance clearance of toxic metabolites and modulate neuroinflammatory responses. Emerging evidence suggests that photobiomodulation [21] can enhance meningeal lymphatic function and promote clearance of amyloid-beta and other pathological proteins [31–33]. Future stroke trials should explore whether anatomically targeted TPBM protocols focusing on lymphatic-enriched regions yield superior outcomes compared to standard forehead application. This approach may be particularly relevant in the chronic phase, where persistent neuroinflammation and impaired clearance mechanisms contribute to secondary neurodegeneration.

4.1. Clinical implications

Based on this evidence, clinicians should not adopt 980 nm TPBM for chronic stroke recovery. Resources should continue to be directed toward rehabilitation interventions with proven efficacy. However, our findings are specific to one protocol and population and should not preclude further research. Our null findings in chronic stroke should not be extrapolated to earlier stroke phases, where the biological substrate may be more amenable to photobiomodulation.

Conclusion

This trial demonstrated that adjunctive transcranial 980 nm near-infrared laser therapy was not superior to sham treatment for improving functional outcomes in patients with chronic ischemic stroke undergoing standard rehabilitation. The intervention was safe but ineffective within the tested protocol. These results do not support the clinical use of this specific TPBM approach in the chronic stroke population. Further research is warranted to optimize parameters, identify potential responder subgroups, clarify the role of therapeutic timing across different stroke phases, and explore novel targeting strategies including meningeal lymphatic-enriched sites.
