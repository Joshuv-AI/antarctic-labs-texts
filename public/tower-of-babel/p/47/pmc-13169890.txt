Factors associated with the chronicization of psychological responses and intention to stay among nurses 18 months after the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Reiko Hashimoto, Hisao Nakai, Yusuke Nitta, Yumeno Furukawa, Takashi Uehara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake caused extensive infrastructure damage and increased the burden on human resources in an already depopulated and aging region. In major hospitals in the Oku-Noto area, approximately 18% of nursing staff left, which led to a near-collapse of the regional healthcare delivery system. Although many studies have documented the immediate mental health effects of disasters, there is a lack of evidence on the factors that affect nurses’ intention to stay in their current workplace during the recovery phase (18 months post-disaster). This study aimed to identify the predictors of intention to stay among hospital nurses in the earthquake-affected regions of Noto, Japan.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey from August to September 2025 among staff nurses working at disaster-affected medical institutions in the Noto Peninsula. Participants were recruited through all 106 medical and long-term care facilities in the affected municipalities. Using “intention to stay in the current workplace” as the dependent variable and adjusting for confounding variables, we performed binary logistic regression analysis (stepwise method) to identify associations between intention to stay and individual attributes (e.g., age, sex), geographical factors, psychosocial burden, and perceived organizational support.ResultsAmong the 578 total respondents (response rate: 48.4%), 358 staff nurses working in hospitals were analyzed (mean age: 43.2 years). No statistically significant associations were found between intention to stay in the current workplace and birth origin, workplace location, or personal disaster-related damage. Conversely, logistic regression analysis identified two significant predictors: “absence of anxiety or depressive symptoms” (odds ratio [OR]: 1.66, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.03–2.69) and “perception of a well-established psychological support system within the workplace” (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.39–3.81).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that internal organizational support systems are more important for nurse retention in the Noto region than individual disaster status or geographical factors. During the recovery phase, external aid provision should shift from direct individual interventions to supportive roles that strengthen local institutional support frameworks. Furthermore, establishing mechanisms to identify and monitor staff with chronic psychological burdens is important for maintaining healthcare delivery systems in depopulated and aging regions.

Introduction

On January 1, 2024, a magnitude 7.6 earthquake, known as the Noto Peninsula earthquake (NPE), struck the northern part of the Noto Peninsula (Oku-Noto) in Ishikawa Prefecture, Japan. The earthquake triggered intense seismic motion, massive crustal deformation, and a major tsunami, causing extensive damage across the region [1]. In the immediate aftermath (1.5 months after the disaster), the number of evacuees exceeded 50,000, and more than 1,500 evacuation centers were established to accommodate them [2]. As of April 2024, the total population of Ishikawa Prefecture was approximately 1.1 million, with approximately 56,000 residents living in the heavily affected Oku-Noto area. Because of the subsequent rapid post-disaster outflow of residents, the population of this area had declined to approximately 52,000 by 2025 [3].

The NPE highlighted the substantial vulnerability of a region already characterized by super-aging and depopulation, factors exacerbated by its geographical location as a peninsula on the Sea of Japan and the fact that the earthquake occurred during a harsh winter. These constraints, which meant that land-based aid was restricted to a single direction and adverse weather hampered aerial and maritime relief efforts, severely impeded the initial disaster response [4]. An analysis of population movement data in Wajima City before and after the disaster showed that, despite an influx of external support personnel, there was a net outflow of over 1,000 residents within 6 months of the earthquake, threatening the stability of the local community [5]. This outflow included essential healthcare professionals and nurses who maintain regional hospitals. A major driver of this migration was the prolonged evacuation necessitated by extensive infrastructure destruction [6]. Previous research has shown that the damage caused by the NPE transcended physical destruction, as it divested healthcare workers of their basic livelihood and accelerated the drain of human resources from the region [7]. Indeed, there were numerous resignations from four public hospitals in the Oku-Noto area, with the number of nurses decreasing by approximately 18% post-disaster, leading to a severe labor shortage [8].

Nurses who remained in the disaster-affected areas had to quickly adopt dual roles. Despite being victims themselves, and having to cope with personal and familial losses, they simultaneously served as frontline responders and were expected to provide continuous care [9, 10]. According to reports from the Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Team following the NPE, healthcare workers in the affected areas experienced considerable physical and mental exhaustion owing to overwhelming workloads. The Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Team emphasized that these professionals should not be viewed merely as service providers, but as individuals exposed to extreme fatigue and distress who must be recognized as legitimate recipients of care [11]. Previous literature on disaster nursing indicates that nurses tend to prioritize the needs of others while repressing their own suffering. This pattern of self-sacrifice often leads to the chronicization of psychosomatic symptoms such as depression, insomnia, and various physical ailments [12].

Burnout in such demanding environments is an important issue for healthcare professionals. The risk of burnout increases rapidly during crises such as disasters and pandemics. For instance, post-2019 reports on the COVID-19 pandemic show that many healthcare workers experienced moderate to high levels of stress and burnout [13–15]. Previous research has established that burnout among nurses is a powerful predictor of job turnover [16]. Furthermore, a scoping review of the effects of disasters in Japan reported that labor shortages, long working hours, and high role demands lead to psychological stress, burnout, and turnover intention among nurses and municipal employees [17]. A study conducted 8 months after the 2024 NPE found that frontline hospital nurses experienced severe and persistent psychological stress because of the dual burden of their personal disaster experiences and difficult working environments [18]. Workers who experience daily inconveniences, concerns for family safety, and anxiety about their children’s education can develop chronic psychological distress. These factors are important issues that can reduce the motivation to continue working. In fact, many nurses who decided to leave the Oku-Noto region did so for personal reasons, such as the destruction of their homes and substantial changes in their children’s educational environment [8].

Many studies have reported on mental health in the immediate aftermath of disasters. However, there is a lack of detailed information about the intention of healthcare workers to stay in the current workplace during the mid-to-long-term recovery phase. In particular, research focusing on the connection between geographical factors and organizational support systems remains scarce. Emergency external support is typically withdrawn approximately 18 months after a disaster. During this period, local healthcare professionals face both chronic fatigue and an increasing burden of regional reconstruction. Previous studies have indicated that psychological distress can persist or become chronic long after the acute phase of a disaster [19, 20]. Considering this, investigating the specific timing of the development of psychological distress is essential for planning the long-term maintenance of the healthcare system. The Noto Peninsula is characterized by severe geographical constraints and a rapidly aging population. In such a vulnerable disaster-affected area, identifying the factors that encourage nurses to remain in their workplaces is an urgent challenge for regional recovery.

The objective of this study was to identify the factors associated with psychosocial burden and the intention to stay in the current workplace among nurses employed at hospitals in the Noto Peninsula. Our findings could contribute to the development of specific retention strategies and the optimization of both internal and external mental health support measures for nursing professionals experiencing critical labor shortages. Ultimately, the study aim is to provide empirical evidence to support the sustainable recovery and maintenance of the healthcare delivery system in the Noto region.

Methods and materials

Data collection

We conducted a cross-sectional survey from August 1 to September 30, 2025, targeting nurses working at medical and long-term care facilities in the Noto Peninsula. The survey targeted municipalities that recorded a seismic intensity of 6-lower or higher. To comprehensively assess the regional situation, we requested cooperation from all 106 relevant facilities, comprising 11 hospitals, 78 clinics, 15 home-visit nursing stations, and 2 integrated facilities for medical and long-term care. From the overall survey data, this study focused on data from a subset of frontline hospital nurses. The analytic sample was limited to staff nurses in non-managerial positions currently working in hospitals. Nurses from clinics, home-visit nursing stations, and integrated facilities were excluded. Additionally, within the participating hospitals, managerial staff (e.g., directors of nursing and head nurses) were excluded to ensure a homogeneous sample of frontline staff; such staff are most susceptible to the physical and mental strain caused by staffing shortages [21, 22].

Data were collected using a hybrid method to increase participant convenience and response rates. We used anonymous web-based surveys via Microsoft Forms, as well as anonymous self-administered paper questionnaires, which allowed participants to choose their preferred format according to their work environment. For both formats, we ensured that all responses remained completely anonymous and that no individuals could be identified.

The survey instrument was an original questionnaire that was developed following a comprehensive literature review [23–25]. We opted to develop specific items rather than using existing standardized scales, as we considered that pre-existing instruments might not fully capture the unique and severe stressors experienced by nurses in the Noto region following the earthquake. Furthermore, our objective was to identify specific, actionable factors that could directly inform support strategies. After identifying candidate items from previous studies, the research team, which comprised specialists in disaster nursing and public health, held several discussions to confirm the face validity and content relevance of the items. These discussions focused on ensuring that the items accurately reflected the unique stressors of nurses in the Noto region, such as the dual role of being both a victim and a responder, which are often not captured by general workplace scales. To test the clarity and practical utility of the instrument, we conducted a pilot test with 10 participants before the main survey. Although the sample size of the pilot test was insufficient to statistically evaluate internal consistency (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha), we qualitatively assessed the participants’ feedback to ensure that each item was interpreted as intended. Based on the participants’ feedback, we improved the wording and structure of the questions to finalize the instrument.

Survey items

Demographic characteristics

We collected data on sex, age, years of nursing experience, and nurses’ birthplace (1: Oku-Noto, 2: Naka-Noto, 3: Outside Oku-Noto and Naka-Noto). This variable was used as a proxy for regional attachment, long-standing community ties, and proximity to family, which may influence the intention to stay. Living arrangements were categorized into four options (1: Living alone, 2: Living with family, 3: Living with friends, 4: Others). Work location included seven areas (1: Shika Town, 2: Suzu City, 3: Wajima City, 4: Anamizu Town, 5: Noto Town, 6: Nanao City, 7: Nakanoto Town).

The seven target municipalities represent a range of demographic and medical contexts within the Noto Peninsula. The Oku-Noto region (Suzu City, Wajima City, Noto Town, and Anamizu Town) is a rural, mountainous area characterized by a super-aging society, with aging rates exceeding 50% and a total population of approximately 52,000 as of 2025 [3]. Medical resources in Oku-Noto are limited, consisting mainly of small to medium-sized public hospitals [26]. In contrast, Nanao City and the surrounding Naka-Noto region serve as the medical and population hub of the peninsula; Nanao City has several large-scale regional medical centers and a higher population density compared with the northern regions [27]. Psychosocial burden and workplace environment

To assess psychosocial burden and workplace environment, the following 14 items were used: increased burden of housework, increased burden of daily living, increased psychological stress, frequent feelings of anxiety or depression, loss of hope for the future, psychological burden of being both a victim and a responder, difficulty sharing emotions with others, increased workload, reduced workplace staffing, workload reaching its limit, sufficient rest and time off, well-established psychological support system in the workplace, mutual support among colleagues, and confidence in responding appropriately through personal judgment (in nursing practice). Each item was rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1: Strongly disagree, 2: Disagree, 3: Agree, 4: Strongly agree).

Support required for job retention

Support required for job retention was assessed using a multiple-choice format with five items: (1) Mental health support, (2) Financial support, (3) Increased staffing, (4) Improved leave systems, and (5) Support for family members.

Intention to stay in the current workplace

The intention to stay in the current workplace was measured by asking participants whether they wished to continue working at their current workplace. Responses were on a 4-point Likert scale (1: Strongly disagree, 2: Disagree, 3: Agree, 4: Strongly agree).

Analytic methods

Descriptive statistics were calculated for participant characteristics, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables (age and years of experience) and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Because many healthcare workers left the Noto Peninsula following the 2024 NPE and the regional medical system is still recovering [28, 29], we set “intention to stay in the current workplace” as the primary dependent variable. First, the associations between the dependent variable and nurses’ birthplace (1: Oku-Noto, 2: Naka-Noto, 3: Outside Oku-Noto and Naka-Noto) and work location (the seven municipalities) were evaluated using chi-square tests and adjusted standardized residual analysis. Next, bivariate analyses were performed to examine associations between the dependent variable and the independent variables. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test while categorical variables were converted into binary variables and evaluated using chi-square tests.

The binary classifications were established based on previous research and the characteristics of the current dataset. Age was dichotomized into two groups (< 43 and ≥ 43 years) based on the mean age reported in a study of a single hospital in the Noto Peninsula 8 months post-disaster [29] and the mean age of the current participants. Years of nursing experience were divided into two groups (< 18 and ≥ 18 years) based on the median. The 18-year threshold corresponds to the main working-age group for nurses, which has an approximate age of 40 years. According to the Ishikawa Prefecture Medical Plan (2024) [30], more than half of the nursing staff in northern Noto are aged ≥ 50 years. Although the main working group of nurses aged < 50 years plays a central role in the workplace, these staff also experience substantial family and community responsibilities, making them susceptible to the cumulative stress of the disaster. Thus, this threshold was set as a critical analytical boundary. Birthplace was categorized into two groups (1: Oku-Noto/Naka-Noto, 2: Outside Oku-Noto and Naka-Noto) to identify those whose living arrangements were directly and severely affected. For psychosocial burden, the 4-point Likert scale responses were dichotomized into “Agree” (3 and 4) and “Disagree” (1 and 2). Each item for support required for retention (multiple-choice) was also treated as a binary variable.

Finally, a binomial logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors independently associated with the intention to stay in the current workplace. Sex, age, years of experience [31, 32], living arrangements [33, 34], and birthplace [35, 36] were entered as potential confounders using the forced entry method. Subsequently, all variables that were statistically significant in the bivariate analyses were entered as candidates using the stepwise backward elimination method. This stepwise approach was chosen for the psychosocial and workplace variables because of the exploratory nature of this study, and to identify the most salient predictors among multiple correlated stressors in the unique post-disaster context. Before analysis, multicollinearity among all candidate variables, including the various psychosocial and workplace environment items, was assessed using the variance inflation factor. All variance inflation factor values were below 2.0 (ranging from 1.1 to 1.7), confirming the absence of substantial multicollinearity and indicating the stability of the multivariable model. Listwise deletion was used to handle missing data; therefore, the number of valid responses could vary by item in the bivariate analyses, while the sample size (N = 357) remained consistent for all variables in the final logistic regression model. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in Fortaleza, 2013) and was approved by the institutional review board of the authors’ affiliated university (approval number: C200; 250079). All participants were provided with a document explaining the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, the assurance of anonymity, and the protection of privacy.

This was a completely anonymous survey, and thus no personally identifiable information, such as names or contact details, was collected. Therefore, the process of obtaining informed consent was simplified. Participants were considered to have consented to the study by submitting their responses via the online form or by mailing back the completed paper questionnaire.

Participants retained the right to withdraw from the study at any time by discontinuing their responses before submission. However, because of the strictly anonymous nature of the data, specific responses could not be matched with participants once they had been submitted. Therefore, the explanatory document clearly stated that data withdrawal after submission would not be possible. This procedure for informed consent and the limitations regarding data withdrawal were reviewed and formally approved by the aforementioned institutional review board.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 578 nurses working in Noto Peninsula responded to the survey. By administrative method, 169 responses were collected via self-administered paper questionnaires, and 409 were collected via the web-based survey. Based on the 1,193 questionnaires and recruitment flyers distributed, the overall response rate was 48.4%. Of the 358 staff nurses working in hospitals (61.9%), 356 responded to the question about sex, comprising 34 males (9.6%) and 322 females (90.4%). The mean age (± standard deviation) was 43.2 ± 13.3 years and the mean length of nursing experience was 20.0 ± 13.1 years (Table 1). Regarding birthplace, the largest group was from Naka-Noto (Nanao City, Hakui City, Shika Town, Hodatsushimizu Town, and Nakanoto Town) with 241 participants (67.3%), followed by Oku-Noto (Suzu City, Wajima City, Noto Town, and Anamizu Town) with 92 participants (25.7%), and 25 participants (7.0%) from outside these areas (Table 2). In terms of living arrangements, most participants lived with family (n = 304, 84.9%), while 49 (13.8%) lived alone and 2 (0.6%) lived with friends. The most frequent work locations were Nanao City (n = 252, 70.4%), followed by Suzu City (n = 30, 8.4%) and Wajima City (n = 23, 6.4%) (Table 2).

Associations between intention to stay in the current workplace and birthplace/work location

Chi-square tests and adjusted standardized residual analysis were performed to evaluate the associations between the intention to stay in the current workplace and geographical factors. No statistically significant difference in intention to stay was observed based on birthplace (Oku-Noto, Naka-Noto, or outside Oku-Noto and Naka-Noto): χ2(2) = 0.038, p = 0.981. Similarly, there was no significant variation in the distribution of intention to stay across different work locations (Shika Town, Suzu City, Wajima City, Anamizu Town, Noto Town, Nanao City, and Nakanoto Town): χ2(6) = 4.760, p = 0.575) (Table 2).

Factors associated with the intention to stay in the current workplace 18 months post-disaster

Bivariate analyses identified several significant associations between participant characteristics and intention to stay in the current workplace. Specifically, age showed a significant difference between groups (43.16 ± 13.3; p = 0.010). Analysis of age and nursing experience as categorical variables showed that participants in the ≥ 43 years age group (n = 99, 58.2%; p = 0.022) and those with ≥ 18 years of nursing experience (n = 102, 57.0%; p = 0.043) were significantly more likely to indicate an intention to stay (Table 1).

Regarding psychosocial burden and workplace environment, several items showed significant associations with intention to stay (Table 3). Participants were significantly more likely to indicate an intention to stay if they responded “Disagree” to the following items: frequent feelings of anxiety or depression (n = 101, 58.4%; p = 0.019), loss of hope for the future (n = 117, 58.2%; p = 0.007), workload reaching its limit (n = 109, 57.4%; p = 0.022), and confidence in responding appropriately through personal judgment (n = 107, 54.9%; p = 0.005). Additionally, a significant intention to stay was associated with an “Agree” response to the psychological burden of being both a victim and a responder (n = 110, 53.9%; p = 0.010), sufficient rest and time off (n = 108, 57.8%; p = 0.019), and mutual support among colleagues (n = 167, 54.4%; p = 0.023). Interestingly, a significant association was also found for those who responded “Disagree” to having a well-established psychological support system in the workplace (n = 123, 58.3%; p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated factors associated with the intention to stay in the current workplace among staff nurses working in hospitals in disaster-affected areas 1.5 years after the 2024 NPE. The primary finding was that geographical factors, such as birthplace and work location, as well as the specific disaster conditions of those areas, were not significantly associated with intention to stay. Conversely, the binomial logistic regression analysis identified two key predictors of the intention to stay: the absence of frequent feelings of anxiety or depression and the perception of a well-established psychological support system in the workplace.

The lack of significant differences based on birthplace or current work location suggests a widespread risk of nursing staff turnover across the entire Noto Peninsula, regardless of specific regional backgrounds. Following the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake, studies reported a substantial outflow of young individuals from the general labor force who did not return even after reconstruction [37]. In the aftermath of the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant accident, younger physicians tended to leave because of concerns about their children’s health and their own careers, whereas more experienced physicians remained [35]. Similarly, younger nurses in Fukushima reported a stronger intention to leave, often citing health concerns for their children [38]. Indeed, at a core hospital in Wajima City, which sustained catastrophic damage, 30 out of approximately 120 nurses expressed an intention to resign, citing deteriorating living environments and concerns over their children’s education as reasons for relocation [39]. These findings suggest that there is a uniform and severe risk of labor outflow in hospital nurses throughout the Noto Peninsula that transcends localized geographical differences.

Several factors may explain why more than half of the nurses maintained their intention to stay despite the severe post-disaster staffing shortages. First, their strong professional ethics as healthcare providers likely played a key role. Previous disaster research indicates that first responders in medical fields often remain onsite owing to a powerful sense of ethical obligation, even when facing high-risk conditions; however, this can lead to situations of extreme self-sacrifice [40]. Specifically, reports on nurses have shown that although they experience profound conflict between the safety of themselves and their families and their professional duties, they tend to continue disaster response efforts out of a strong ethical awareness of their roles as specialists [41]. Second, the relatively high mean age of the participants (43.2 years) suggests that socioenvironmental barriers to resignation are important. According to a meta-analysis of nursing turnover, factors such as greater age, years of experience, and changes in social attributes (e.g., marriage) strengthen multilayered ties with organizations and communities. This phenomenon, known as “job embeddedness,” increases the relative psychological and economic costs of leaving, thereby raising the barrier to resignation [42]. For mid-career and senior nurses in particular, the socioeconomic risks of abandoning long-established living arrangements are substantially higher than for their younger counterparts. In the unique geographical context of the Noto Peninsula, this sense of being “embedded” in the community may be a powerful factor influencing nurses to remain in their current workplaces, even under challenging circumstances.

The most important finding of this study is that a “well-established psychological support system in the workplace” showed the highest odds ratio (2.30) for the intention to stay. This suggests that, for mid-to-long-term staff retention following a large-scale disaster, perceived organizational support was more strongly associated with the intention to stay, suggesting that establishing internal mechanisms may play a more important role in retention than relying solely on individual efforts or external psychological interventions. Previous research has indicated that a higher perceived level of organizational support increases nurses’ psychological resilience, which in turn strengthens their intention to remain in the workplace [43]. Studies of intensive care unit nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic also highlighted that excessive workload and lack of organizational support were primary stressors, emphasizing that systemic improvements to the work environment are essential for maintaining healthcare workers’ resilience [44, 45]. Furthermore, reports on Japanese workers have demonstrated that social support in the workplace is an important factor in protecting mental health [46]. These findings, established during the COVID-19 pandemic and in routine work environments, are equally applicable to vulnerable regions such as the Noto Peninsula. As 1.5 years have passed since the disaster, and the traumatic experiences of nurses have become chronic and entrenched, the situation has transitioned into a phase where nurse resilience depends heavily on the organizational environment. In other words, the highest priority for medical institutions is to establish support systems during normal times so that the organization can continue to protect its staff across all phases of a disaster or crisis.

The present findings have important implications for the role of external mental health support during disasters. Particularly in the recovery phase, 1.5 years post-disaster, direct interventions by external, temporary supporters may not necessarily lead to long-term staff retention. Previous studies on post-disaster support for children have emphasized that although direct external support is effective immediately after a disaster, the primary focus should shift over time toward local services and infrastructure [47]. Some researchers have pointed out that following the Great East Japan Earthquake, the role of external teams in Japan later in the support phase should transition from direct intervention to “rear-support” (logistic and structural support) aimed at bolstering the existing activities of local public health nurses and organizational foundations [48]. Considering these perspectives, the most important role of external supporters may be to strengthen the internal support systems of organizations. Improving such organizational support structures is likely to contribute substantially to the long-term retention of nursing staff in disaster-affected areas.

Another key finding is that individuals who did not experience frequent feelings of anxiety or depression had a higher intention to stay (odds ratio: 1.66). This suggests that individuals experiencing persistent anxiety or depression could represent a “high-risk group” for potential resignation associated with chronic mental health deterioration. In other words, even 1.5 years after the disaster, the psychological impact of the event continues to affect staff. Previous studies have reported that healthcare workers, including nurses, who experience prolonged psychological distress such as anxiety and depression, following large-scale crises are at a substantially higher risk of leaving their jobs [49, 50]. In the Noto region, where reconstruction of the physical infrastructure has been delayed and depopulation and aging are accelerating [51], recruitment and retention of nursing staff are vital to maintain the medical system and ensure steady regional recovery. To achieve this, it may be important to identify individuals with chronic psychological burdens and to consider providing continuous monitoring and appropriate interventions.

This study has several limitations. First, because this was a cross-sectional study, causal relationships could not be established. Second, there was a geographical sampling bias, with approximately 70% of respondents working in hospitals in Nanao City. The reason for this bias is that Nanao City is the population and medical center of the Noto Peninsula, with the highest population density and the largest medical facilities. Consequently, the findings may disproportionately represent the perspectives of nurses in large-scale hospitals in densely populated areas. The intention to stay may differ among nurses in northern regions, such as Suzu City and Wajima City, where infrastructure damage was more extensive and reconstruction has been considerably delayed. Third, the participants were limited to nurses who remained in their hospitals 1.5 years after the disaster. Because data from nurses who had already resigned or relocated were not included, these results may not represent the psychological state of all nurses in the Noto region before their departure. Furthermore, the selection of respondents, especially those with an intention to stay, may have been biased toward those with greater organizational commitment or relatively more stable living conditions. Fourth, the use of both web-based and paper-based surveys may have introduced potential mode effects or differences in response patterns. For instance, younger nurses might have preferred the web format, whereas more experienced nurses might have chosen the paper format. Additionally, regarding the web survey, it should be noted that multiple individuals may have accessed the survey using the same QR code, as the instructions were shared within hospital departments rather than distributed individually. Fifth, this study used an original questionnaire tailored to the specific context of the Noto Peninsula earthquake rather than established standardized instruments. Although this approach provided high contextual utility by capturing specific stressors unique to this disaster, we acknowledge that the lack of a standardized, validated instrument is a limitation. Specifically, aspects of the psychometric rigor of our questionnaire, such as construct validity and internal consistency, may not be as robust as for established scales, which may have affected the precision with which the hypothesized constructs were captured. This also limits the direct comparability of our findings with other studies. Sixth, there were unmeasured confounding factors that were not included in the logistic regression analysis, such as individual economic status, the presence of children or caregiving responsibilities, salary levels, participants’ own health and mental health conditions, and the extent of personal disaster damage. Additionally, whereas we used the forced entry method for demographic confounders, we used the stepwise method for psychosocial variables. Stepwise selection is associated with certain limitations; specifically, relying on bivariate screening and automated selection may lead to model instability or the inadvertent exclusion of significant predictors that are highly correlated with other variables in the model. Seventh, because multiple bivariate analyses were performed to explore various psychosocial and demographic factors, there is an inherent risk of Type I error because of multiple comparisons. Although these analyses informed our multivariable model, the p-values in the bivariate analysis should be interpreted with caution. Finally, this study focused on the unique geographical and social environment of the Noto Peninsula, a depopulated area with an aging population. Therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to nurses affected by disasters in regions with different demographic structures.

Practical and policy implications

The findings suggest that prioritizing the retention of nursing professionals may be an important consideration in the reconstruction of the Noto region. More consideration by hospital organizations of the development of sustainable strategies to protect and care for their staff is warranted. Furthermore, such strategies should be considered in the development of public policies, including regional planning and budgetary support, to address the loss of essential healthcare workers.

Our finding that a well-established internal organizational support system is a more significant predictor of retention than individual factors has important implications for broader support strategies. Regarding future disaster mental health support, the role of external organizations in the recovery phase should ideally transition from direct individual intervention toward empowering local organizations. Additionally, establishing a framework to identify and support individuals facing chronic psychological distress may be beneficial for the local healthcare workforce. The insights gained regarding organizational support structures may provide a basis for disaster management strategies in other regions facing similar challenges, potentially supporting the resilience of communities and the professionals who serve them.

Conclusion

This study showed that the perception of well-established internal organizational support systems and the absence of anxiety or depressive symptoms were more strongly associated with intention to stay among hospital staff nurses than their geographical background or personal disaster damage. These findings suggest that organizational factors may play an important role in nurse retention during the chronic recovery phase. Ultimately, fostering such systems may contribute to maintaining healthcare delivery in depopulated and aging regions facing long-term disaster recovery.
