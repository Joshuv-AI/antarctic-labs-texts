Programmable electric hysteresis in graphite/MoS2 heterojunctions through twisting
Authors: Zhaokuan Yu, Juntai Wu, Yuqing He, Wei Cao, Xin Lu, Ni Zhong, Ming Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Van der Waals devices have recently been shown to enable remarkable field-effect control over electronic orders, including sliding ferroelectricity. In this work, we report robust electric hysteresis in graphite/MoS2 heterojunction devices. The hysteretic behavior is programmable via interlayer twisting, with the memory window sharply decreasing near 30°, confirming strong angle-dependent modulation. Owing to the superlubric nature of the interface, such manipulation can be performed rapidly and with minimal energy cost. The underlying mechanism is further supported by the observation of a finite out-of-plane piezoelectric response in the graphite/MoS2 heterojunction, with an effective piezoelectric coefficient of d33 = 3.8 pm/V. Density functional theory calculations reveal that the electric response originates from a combination of interfacial charge transfer and moiré potential effects, without requiring interlayer sliding to explain the observed hysteresis. This work shows that adjusting the twist-angle in heterojunctions can control ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties, enabling better nanoelectronic devices.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) materials have garnered significant attention in recent years due to their atomically thin nature and unique physical properties, which enable a multitude of novel electronic and optoelectronic applications. Among these properties, ferroelectric and piezoelectric responses are particularly attractive for ultrathin nonvolatile memory, low-power logic devices, and nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMS)1. Conventionally, ferroelectricity in bulk materials stems from structural distortions that break inversion symmetry, such as in perovskite oxides2. However, atomically thin ferroelectricity is difficult to achieve in these oxide materials due to the intrinsic thickness limit3. Ferroelectrics in van der Waals (vdW) layered structures with sub-nanometer thickness have been reported and attract particular interest over the past decades4. Realizing robust ferroelectricity is challenging due to the inherently low-dimensional nature and the presence (or near-presence) of inversion symmetry in many 2D crystals.

Recent discoveries of ferroelectricity in ultrathin layered materials such as In2Se35, WTe26, and CuInP2S67 have spurred intense research on manipulating ferroelectric domains, designing vdW heterojunctions, and exploring novel device concepts8. Unlike their bulk counterparts, 2D ferroelectrics can exhibit unique thickness-dependent phase transitions and domain structures, which open up unprecedented possibilities for engineering ferroelectric properties via external stimuli such as strain9, electric gating10, and interlayer twisting11. The concept of “twistronics”, originally explored in twisted bilayer graphene12, has shown that moiré superlattices arising from controlled twist angles can drastically alter the mechanical13 and electronic response14. This approach can break or restore symmetries at will, making the stacking angle a powerful degree of freedom to tune various phenomena, including structural superlubrication15, and correlation effects16. For example, the twist between MoS2 bilayers can modulate both the interlayer shear force and the domain topologies to reach a superlubric state17, and induce a transition from ferroelectricity to antiferroelectricity18, respectively. However, controlling the twist angle in homojunctions presents significant challenges, as the layers tend to thermodynamically relax back to the energetically favored aligned stacking in the absence of external forces or applied strain19. In contrast, heterojunctions with lattice mismatch exhibit much lower rotational energy barriers. In such systems, the angular friction can be effectively eliminated20, enabling facile manipulation of the twist angle and the associated electronic ordering.

In this work, we demonstrate a programmable electric hysteresis in a graphite/MoS2 heterojunction. The system has been shown to exhibit structural superlubricity21. By systematically varying the twist angle, we show that hysteresis and the corresponding memory window can be modulated, indicating a pronounced influence of interlayer registry on the electronic response. Furthermore, we reveal a large piezoelectric coefficient, with a d33 value of 3.8 pm/V, approximately 14.6 times higher than that of monolayer In2Se35,22.

Results

Unconventional hysteresis in graphite/MoS2 ferroelectric transistor

To fabricate the graphite/MoS2 ferroelectric transistor, a graphite flake is transferred onto a monolayer MoS2 flake to form a graphite/MoS2 heterojunction, followed by the integration of Au top electrodes. Prior to the heterojunction assembly, MoS2/SiO2/Si samples are prepared. A monolayer MoS2 flake is mechanically exfoliated and transferred onto the SiO2/Si substrate under ambient conditions. To eliminate residual surface contaminants, the as-prepared MoS2/SiO2/Si samples are annealed in an ultra-high vacuum (UHV) environment (see Part 1 in Methods for details).

After annealing, the graphite flake is transferred onto the MoS2/SiO2/Si samples. The corresponding optical images and schematic illustrations of each step are shown in Fig. 1a. As shown in Fig. 1a(i), the graphite flake is prepared from a graphite mesa capped with Au, with a lateral dimension of 4 μm × 4 μm. Details about the preparation of the graphite mesa are provided in Part 2 of the Methods. A tungsten probe with a radius of curvature of approximately 500 nm is controlled by a micro-manipulator (Kleindiek, MM-3A) to prepare graphite/MoS2 heterojunctions (Fig. 1a(i)). As shown in Fig. 1a(ii), the upper section of the graphite mesa is laterally sheared for a distance of 2 μm. After the shear force release, the samples with a self-retracting motion (SRM) property retract back to their initial position to minimize the surface energy23 (Fig. 1a(iii)). The upper graphite section showing SRM is then completely sheared out, lifted up by the tungsten probe, and selected as the top graphite flake for subsequent transfer onto the MoS2/SiO2/Si substrate (Fig. 1a(iv)). The self-retraction process serves as a reliable indicator of high-quality heterojunction contact, as only such interfaces correspond to atomically smooth, single-crystalline, and incommensurate contacts23,24, thereby eliminating measurement uncertainties introduced by defects such as steps, wrinkles, and polycrystalline domains. Since the adhesion force between the tungsten probe and the graphite flake is much smaller than that between the graphite flake and the MoS2 substrate, the graphite flake can be directly placed on the MoS2 substrate by manipulating the micro-manipulator. Notably, all procedures shown in Fig. 1a(i)-(iv) are carried out in a home-built chamber filled with dry nitrogen gas (N2) to ensure a clean graphite/MoS2 interface. The optical image of the resulting graphite/MoS2 heterojunctions is shown in Fig. 1a(v), while a more detailed AFM characterization is presented in Fig. 1c. Both the top surface of the MoS2 flake and the bottom surface of the graphite flake are atomically smooth, single-crystalline, and superlubric23,24, which is essential for achieving a well-defined and controllable interfacial twisting process. A self-developed measurement system is built for the hysteresis measurement of graphite/MoS2 ferroelectric transistor. The schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 1b. In this system, a bias voltage, Vbias, is applied between the tungsten probe and the MoS2 substrate using a source meter (Keithley, 2450), and the resulting current is recorded simultaneously, corresponding to the out-of-plane transport current through the graphite/MoS2 device. In addition, a gate voltage, Vgate, is applied to the highly doped Si substrate to modulate the transport characteristics of graphite/MoS2 interface. Specifically, the graphite/MoS2 device is mounted on a rotation stage, by which the relative twist angle between the graphite and MoS2 is precisely tuned during the subsequent angle-dependent measurements. All hysteresis measurements are performed under ambient conditions, with a temperature of 25 °C and a relative humidity of 20%. Compared with commercial AFM-based measurement systems, the self-developed system is free from intrinsic current limiting, enabling stable and reproducible hysteresis measurements over a wider current range with improved control of the out-of-plane electric field.

The results in Fig. 1d display the current-voltage (I-Vgate) characteristics of the graphite/MoS2 device under varying gate voltages, indicative of a prominent ferroelectric hysteresis loop. In contrast, devices with only MoS2 show no hysteresis. The observed electrical response in graphite/MoS2 device originates from the interface between graphite and MoS2. This is supported by a systematic analysis of the interfacial resistances, which demonstrates that the dominant contribution arises from the graphite/MoS2 junction rather than from other interfaces in the measurement circuit, including the tungsten probe/Au cap, Au cap/graphite flake, and graphite flake/MoS2 substrate interfaces (Supplementary Note S1 of the Supporting Information (SI)). The observed clockwise (inverted) hysteresis originates from interfacial polarization that modulates carrier transport via bottom-surface band bending in MoS2 (Supplementary Note S2 of the SI)25. Importantly, edge-related effects are strongly suppressed in this measurement because microscale vdW contacts in an area of 4 μm × 4 μm are employed, for which the interfacial area is much larger than the edge region. As a result, the electrical response is governed by the bulk graphite/MoS2 interface, whereas random edge adsorption or local defects cannot account for the systematic hysteresis observed across varying gate voltages. Notably, to suppress the influence of airborne contaminants that may adsorb during the measurement process, an electric-shock-assisted cleaning procedure is employed prior to each characterization26. The robustness of the hysteresis behavior is further confirmed by reexamining the device after replacing the SiO2/Si substrate with hexagonal boron nitride, where a similarly pronounced hysteresis loop is observed (Supplementary Note S3 of SI), ruling out substrate-induced artifacts.

Twist-angle dependence of electric hysteresis

The twist-angle dependence of the electric hysteresis in the graphite/MoS2 device is investigated using the self-developed measurement system (Fig. 1b). In these measurements, the accessible twist-angle range spans 180°. High angular resolution is achieved by employing a fine step size of less than 0.2° near the 30° regime (or 90° and 150°), while a coarser increment of approximately 10° is utilized for other ranges. During the twisting procedure, the MoS2 flake is rotated by the rotation stage, while the graphite flake is held stationary by the tungsten probe in contact with it, thereby generating a relative twist between the two flakes. The accuracy of the rotation angles ±0.2°. The relative twist-angle, θ is defined as the in-plane rotation angle between the crystallographic orientation of the graphite flake and that of the underlying MoS2 flake, as indicated in Fig. 2a(i) and (ii). The gate-dependent transfer characteristics are recorded as I-Vgate curves over a sweep from –80 V to +80 V to ensure that the entire hysteresis loop is fully captured within the experimentally accessible window. Three representative hysteretic I-Vgate curves are shown in Fig. 2a(iv), corresponding to relative twist angles of approximately 0°, 30°, and 60°, respectively. The hysteretic I-Vgate curves at more twist angles are shown in Supplementary Note S3 of the SI. The results show that at a twist angle of θ≈30°, the current switching during the retrace sweep occurs at a positive gate voltage. In contrast, at θ≈0° and θ≈60°, switching occurs at negative gate voltages. This suggests that interlayer charge transfer and the resulted electron doping is enhanced at θ≈0° and θ≈60° but suppressed at θ≈30°. Furthermore, the device at θ≈30° exhibits a distinctly different gate-dependent behavior. The transfer curve at θ≈30° displays two pronounced hysteresis loops during a full gate voltage sweep, whereas only a single hysteresis loop is observed at angles away from θ≈30°, such as θ≈0° and 60°. This dual-hysteresis feature at θ≈30° is attributed to the substantially reduced doping level in the MoS2 channel. It leads to a weaker intrinsic electron doping in MoS2, making the channel more susceptible to carrier depletion under negative gate voltage. When a sufficiently large negative gate voltage is applied, electrons in MoS2 are first depleted and subsequently hole carriers begin to accumulate. As a result, during a negative-to-positive gate sweep, the first hysteresis loop originates from hole-dominated transport at large negative gate voltages, while the second hysteresis loop corresponds to electron-dominated conduction when the gate voltage is swept back toward positive values. These two carrier regimes give rise to two distinct switching events in the transfer curve, producing the observed double-hysteresis behavior. This carrier conversion is only accessible when the intrinsic electron density is sufficiently low, further confirming that the interlayer-induced electron doping at θ≈30° is much weaker than that at θ≈0° and θ≈60°.

The ON-state and OFF-state of the graphite/MoS2 device are defined directly from the hysteretic switching behavior. Specifically, the ON-state corresponds to the high-conductance state, while the OFF-state corresponds to the low-conductance state. The boundary of the ON-state and the OFF-state is 10 nA. Accordingly, memory-window voltage (VMW) is defined as the voltage window between the forward (ON-state) and backward (OFF-state) switching voltages, as indicated by the red arrow in the Fig. 2a(iv). Notably, electric hysteresis loops are observed at all angles, but their VMW varies strongly with θ, as shown in Fig. 2b. The polar representation of the data demonstrates the anisotropy and six-fold symmetry of the measured VMW. The error bars of VMW represent the range of VMW values obtained by varying the ON/OFF-state boundary, reflecting the sensitivity of VMW to the choice of the current threshold (i.e., 10 nA), which primarily arises from the determination of the switching voltages.

With this dense angular sampling, three pronounced and well-resolved dips are clearly observed at twist angles near ~30°, ~90°, and ~150°, respectively. These dips are not isolated points; instead, each dip consists of a cluster of consecutive data points extending over several degrees, indicating that they are robust and reproducible features rather than artifacts arising from measurement uncertainty. Away from the dip regions, VMW remains at a relatively stable background level of approximately 40 V, showing only small fluctuations within the experimental uncertainty. In contrast, at the dip positions near ~30°, ~90°, and ~150°, it drops sharply to values of approximately 10 V, corresponding to a reduction by a factor of about 4 compared with the neighboring angles. Importantly, the depth of each dip is significantly larger than the associated error bars, confirming that the observed reductions in VMW are intrinsic and not caused by measurement noise.

A close-up view of the dip near 150° is shown in Fig. 2c, where the relative twist-angle is varied within a very narrow range, and VMW exhibits an exceptionally sharp variation. Specifically, when θ increases from 148.0° to 149.1°, VMW drops dramatically from 32.95 V to a minimum value of 14.01 V. Conversely, when θ increases from 149.2° to 150.3°, VMW rises steeply from 13.20 V to 43.03 V. Overall, these variations correspond to an absolute change of approximately 30 V, or a relative modulation by a factor of about 3, induced by an angular variation of only ~1°. Such a steep dependence demonstrates an exceptionally high sensitivity of VMW on θ in the vicinity of the dip. The dip is quantitatively defined by a nonlinear fitting procedure described in Supplementary Note S5 of the SI, as shown in Fig. 2c. The fitting parameters provide a unified and quantitative framework for assessing their relative angular sensitivity.

Piezoelectric response from PFM measurements

To validate the piezoelectric functionality of the graphite/MoS2 device, we employ piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) measurements to support the transport hysteresis observed in electrical measurements. An out-of-plane mapping of the heterojunction area is performed under an AC excitation of VAC = 1 V using an AFM under ambient conditions (25 °C, relative humidity of 20%) (see Part 3 in Methods for details). As shown in Fig. 3a, a clear contrast is observed between the heterojunction region and the MoS2-only region, indicative of a vertical polarization response at the interface. To benchmark the reliability of the PFM setup, a periodically poled LiNbO3 (PPLN) standard is measured in parallel. The extracted piezoelectric coefficient of the PPLN reference, which is extracted from the slope of the PFM amplitude as a function of the applied AC excitation voltage, is d33 = 10.1 pm/V (black squares in the inset of Fig. 3b), consistent with reported values27 and confirming the accuracy of the instrumentation. Applying the same VAC sweep to the graphite/MoS2 device yields a linear ∆Z-VAC relationship, from which an effective out-of-plane piezoelectric coefficient of d33 = 3.8 pm/V is extracted (Fig. 3b). Using graphene instead of graphite, we measured a piezoelectric coefficient of d33 = 5.2 pm/V (Fig. S3). This slightly higher value compared to the graphite-based system is attributed to graphene’s increased elasticity. The measured piezoelectric coefficient is remarkably larger than those reported for other two-dimensional ferroelectric systems, such as In2Se3 (0.3 pm/V)22 or 3R-like MoS2/WS2 (2.0 pm/V)28.Fig. 3Out-of-plane piezoelectric response of the graphite/MoS₂ heterojunction.a Out-of-plane PFM amplitude map of the heterojunction under VAC = 1 V. b ∆Z versus VAC for the heterojunction; linear fits yield d33 = 3.8 pm/V.

The existence of a finite and reversible out-of-plane piezoelectric response in the graphite/MoS2 heterojunction provides independent microscopic evidence for electrically switchable interfacial polarization, which naturally explains the gate-voltage-dependent hysteresis observed in the transport measurements through polarization-induced modulation of the interfacial potential landscape.

Discussion

Ferroelectricity and hysteretic behavior in van der Waals (vdW) heterojunctions have been extensively explored, particularly in graphene/hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) systems. Switchable ferroelectricity in Bernal-stacked bilayer graphene encapsulated by h-BN was first reported through transport measurements29. The underlying mechanism was attributed to interlayer charge transfer between the graphene layers, mediated by correlated electrons in the moiré band structure29. However, this interpretation has been challenged: subsequent studies on Bernal-stacked bilayer graphene moiré superlattices encapsulated with 30°-twisted h-BN showed that the polarized charge density remains stable even when the Fermi level lies outside the moiré bands, suggesting an alternative mechanism30. Ferroelectricity has also been observed in monolayer graphene moiré superlattices, where it is explained by the localized injection and extraction of carriers modulated by the moiré potential under varying electric fields31. This contrasts with the earlier interlayer charge transfer hypothesis. Furthermore, recent studies have identified interlayer sliding as a key mechanism, termed sliding ferroelectricity, in which spontaneous polarization arises from broken inversion symmetry due to relative shifts between layers32. In multilayer heterostructures, this sliding leads to stable polarization states that are switchable by coordinated lateral displacements of the constituent layers33. Intriguingly, the insertion of WSe2 into graphene/h-BN heterostructures preserves the ferroelectric response, suggesting that graphene may function primarily as a passive probe34. In this configuration, the encapsulation by WSe2 suppresses moiré superlattice formation and electronic correlations in graphene, thereby implicating broken inversion symmetry in h-BN as the primary origin of ferroelectricity35,36.

In this study, the graphite shown in Fig. 1 is composed of highly ordered pyrolytic graphite (HOPG), which is characterized by arbitrary stacking configurations and significant thickness. Due to this structural disorder, the probability of forming a spontaneously polarized stacking order, such as the ABCB configuration known to exhibit ferroelectricity in tetralayer graphene37,38, is negligible. Additionally, monolayer MoS2 in the common 2H phase does not exhibit ferroelectricity39, and no phase transformation to ferroelectric polymorphs (e.g., 1 T or 3 R) is observed in our measurements. Therefore, the observed anomalous hysteresis can be attributed exclusively to the graphene/MoS2 interface. Our DFT calculations reveal that the graphene/MoS2 heterobilayer exhibits a spontaneous out-of-plane polarization (Fig. 4a–c), arising from interfacial electron transfer from graphene to MoS2 and generating a polarization vector pointing from graphene to MoS2. The experimental data in Fig. 1 further demonstrate that this polarization can be modulated by an external electric field. We also increase the thickness of graphene and found that the out-of-plane polarization increases slightly and saturates with thickness (Fig. S4), consistent with our previous report40. The physical mechanism of the hysteresis is discussed. First, the change of polarization is not due to electron correlation in the moiré band, since the moiré potential is weak in our small-angle devices. Next, we examine sliding ferroelectricity, which requires registry-dependent polarization associated with relative interlayer displacement. Our DFT calculations of interlayer sliding along both armchair and zigzag directions reveal that the polarization is hardly registry-independent (Fig. 4d), effectively ruling out sliding ferroelectricity in this system. Furthermore, due to the large exfoliation energy of HOPG42, interlayer shifts within the bulk graphite are energetically prohibited, restricting any possible displacement to the graphene/MoS2 interface, which is known to be structurally superlubric.

The above considerations suggest that charge trapping is the dominant mechanism responsible for the observed ferroelectric-like hysteresis. Similar to phenomena observed in graphene/h-BN heterostructures31, the moiré potential in graphene/MoS2, although weaker due to shorter moiré wavelengths, can still localize a small number of charge carriers. This is supported by the transistor characteristics shown in Fig. 2, where device performance, such as the on/off current ratio and memory window, strongly depends on the twist angle. For example, as the twist angle increases from 0° to 30°, both the moiré potential and interlayer charge transfer decrease, leading to reduced polarization and degraded device performance.

DFT calculations of the heterostructure without moiré modulations show a near-monotonic dependence of polarization on electronic doping (Fig. 4e). The effect of doping arises from the band alignment of the hetero bilayer (Fig. 4f): electron doping predominantly populates the graphene layer first, followed by MoS2, thereby reducing the interfacial polarization, whereas hole doping enhances it. When the moiré potential is present, carrier localization is expected to amplify these doping-induced effects, further increasing polarization under hole doping and reducing it under electron doping. The injection and extraction of these localized carriers during an electric field sweep led to the observed hysteresis. Notably, the polarization electric field hysteresis loop differs from that of graphene/h-BN, exhibiting asymmetry due to the asymmetric polarization response (Fig. 1d). A schematic hysteresis loop is illustrated (dashed curves in Fig. 4e) to qualitatively capture this behavior, supporting our experimental observations.

In summary, we report unconventional electric hysteresis in graphite/MoS2 vdW heterojunctions, highlighting their potential for ferroelectric device applications. Unlike previously studied systems relying on low-dimensional graphene, our devices feature micrometer-scale contact areas, underscoring their robustness and suitability for scalable integration into electronic platforms. The electric hysteresis is tunable via the interlayer twist angle; notably, the dual-hysteresis appears at a twist angle of 30°, where the memory window decreased by about 75%. This hysteretic behavior is further corroborated by PFM measurements at the graphite/MoS2 interface, which reveal a relatively large piezoelectric coefficient, exceeding those of several reported 2D ferroelectrics. First-principles DFT calculations attribute the polarization switching under an electric field to a synergistic effect between interfacial charge transfer and moiré potential, rather than requiring interlayer sliding. This work presents a straightforward and effective strategy for engineering ferroelectricity in vdW heterostructures through twist-angle modulation. By precisely tuning the rotational alignment between 2D layers, one can achieve controllable polarization and tailor device functionalities. The demonstrated tunable ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties open avenues for next-generation nanoelectronics devices, including high-performance non-volatile memories, sensors, and actuators.

Methods

Part 1: The preparation of monolayer MoS2 flake on SiO2/Si

We clarify that the MoS2 flakes used in this work are mechanically exfoliated and are monolayer. The process of preparing MoS2 on SiO2/Si is shown as follows: firstly, to remove impurities, the SiO2/Si substrates are rinsed with acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and deionized water, heated in air (25 °C, 40% relative humidity) at an elevated temperature of 150 °C for 30 min, and plasma treated at 300 sccm and 500 W for 20 min. The working gas of the plasma treatment is O2 which is 99.999% pure. Secondly, the MoS2 sheets are mechanically exfoliated using the Scotch tape method and transferred onto the SiO2/Si substrates. Specifically, an ordinary water-soluble tape (3 M) is used to peel small MoS2 flakes from a natural MoS2 block. Then, the tape, with MoS2 flakes adhered to it, is attached to the SiO2/Si substrates and annealed at 150 °C for 30 min. Finally, the tape is peeled, leaving MoS2 sheets on the SiO2/Si substrates. The above mechanical exfoliation processes are operated under ambient conditions. To further eliminate residual surface contaminants, the as-prepared MoS2/SiO2/Si samples are annealed in an ultra-high vacuum (UHV) environment at a pressure of 1 × 10−10 mbar and a temperature of 500 K for 1 h. After this treatment, the samples are transferred into a home-built chamber filled with dry nitrogen gas (N2) prior to the subsequent transfer of graphite flakes. Notably, monolayer regions of MoS₂ are selected for the next fabrication step.

Part 2: The fabrication of the graphite mesas

The fabrication process of the microscale graphite mesas is as follows: firstly, a double layer photoresist LOR 1 A (100 nm)/ZEP (400 nm) is spun on the fresh cleavage surface of the highly ordered pyrolytic graphite (HOPG, ZYB grade), and then is removed by electron beam lithography with a pattern of square arrays in size of 4 μm × 4 μm. Secondly, an Au cap with a thickness of 100 nm is grown through electron beam evaporation and then lifted off. Finally, the reactive ion is operated with an etching depth of 2 μm, and the graphite mesas with an Au cap is obtained.

Part 3: The PFM characterization

Piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) measurements are performed on an NT-MDT system in AC Sample mode. A conductive AFM tip (ASYELEC.01-R2; Ir-coated, spring constant k = 2.8 N/m) is used with a signal gain of 10×, and the tip calibration yielded a conversion factor of 0.1176 nm/pA. An AC excitation voltage VAC is swept from 0.1 V to 1.0 V in 0.1 V increments to record the out-of-plane piezoresponse. A periodically poled LiNbO3 (PPLN) standard (d33 = 10.1 pm/V) is measured under the same conditions to validate the quantitative accuracy of our setup.

Part 4: Theoretical method

The state-of-the-art density functional theory (DFT) is performed by the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package43. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) generalizes gradient exchange correlation functional approximation44 augmented by the Grimme-D2 dispersion correction45 are employed. The core electrons of the atoms are treated via the projector augmented wave (PAW) and pseudopotential approach. The initial graphene/MoS2 structure is built from two relaxed monolayers and the bilayer model is further optimized, during which the coordinates of the ions are allowed to relax. The initial graphene/MoS2 structure is constructed by a graphene supercell of 5 × 5 and a MoS2 supercell of 4 × 4. The coordinates of the ions are optimized. The lattice constant is 12.46 Å and the relaxed interlayer distance (defined by the normal direction of the adjacent S ions and C ions within each layer) is 3.4 Å, which is consistent to the experimental observation46. A single point calculation is then performed to obtain the converged electron density. A plane wave energy cutoff of 650 eV and a k-mesh of 6 × 6 × 1 are used. The vacuum size is set to 2 nm to avoid interactions between adjacent bilayer images. A vacuum layer of 2 nm prevented interactions between periodic images, and dipole corrections are applied to evaluate out-of-plane polarization via the potential difference across the vacuum.

Doping simulations are carried out with Quantum ESPRESSO47 using RRKJ PAW pseudopotentials48. A Gaussian smearing of 0.0019 Ry is used for electron density construction, with Fermi–Dirac smearing for convergence. The vacuum and dipole corrections mirror the VASP setup. Dopant effects are modeled via the pseudoatom method49, generating fractional nuclear charge PAWs using the ld1.x tool50. Full methodological details follow the reported work51.
