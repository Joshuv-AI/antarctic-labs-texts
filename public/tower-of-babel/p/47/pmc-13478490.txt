Neural mechanisms of time-forward predictions for naturalistic auditory tone sequences
Authors: Thomas J Baumgarten, Lua Koenig, Richard Hardstone, Adeen Flinker, Sasha Devore, Daniel Friedman, Patricia Dugan, Orrin Devinsky, Biyu J He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrediction of future events is essential for guiding effective actions in dynamic environments. Studies on the neural mechanisms for time-forward predictions have typically used stimuli with relatively simple statistical regularities. Here, we investigated time-forward predictions using stimuli containing statistical regularities similar to those found in natural auditory stimuli. Using intracranial EEG recordings in neurosurgical patients, we found that prediction signals were primarily carried by low-frequency activity across widespread cortical regions, including sensory, parietal, and frontal areas. Prediction-error (PE) signals were found in both low- and high-frequency activity, with high-frequency components localized mainly to sensory areas. Contrary to previous hypotheses, prediction and PE signals did not show a clear spatial or spectral segregation. Directed connectivity between brain regions decreased over the course of the stimulus sequence as predictability increased, except for pathways originating from the parietal cortex. These results reveal integrative and distributed predictive processing and highlight the dorsal auditory pathway in time-forward prediction.Subject terms: Perception, Cortex

Introduction

Predicting future events is crucial for organisms to maximize effective action and survival. In dynamic environments, forming valid predictions relies on continuously updated sensory information and its accumulation over time. For example, animals anticipate future positions of a prey or predator based on its past locations and trajectories, even if it is currently out of view, and human listeners anticipate upcoming words based on a discussion’s prior course to follow a conversation in noisy surroundings1.

The predictive coding theory is a prominent framework for considering the neural implementation of this process2–4. It posits that the brain constantly generates predictions about future sensory inputs, which are then compared against the actual sensory evidence. Following a reciprocal and hierarchical loop, higher-level cortical areas generate internal models of the organism’s world, using top-down connections to predict sensory inputs received by lower-level areas. In turn, prediction error (PE) signals resulting from this comparison update the predictive models via bottom-up connections from lower- to higher-level areas. If successful, such predictions guide neural information processing, facilitating adaptive behavior and response preparation. In addition to a spatial segregation of prediction and prediction error signals, an influential proposal within the predictive coding framework suggests a spectral segregation whereby top-down predictions are carried by low-frequency (e.g., alpha/beta) signals and bottom-up PEs are carried by high-frequency (gamma) signals4,5.

In accordance with this proposal, invasive recordings in non-human primates showed that prediction and PE signals appear to largely follow the sensory processing hierarchy, with a spectral distinction between brain signals containing prediction and PE information: low-frequency signals from higher-order brain regions carry predictive information and high-frequency signals from sensory areas carry PE information6–8. However, recent work has failed to replicate these findings9,10, showing instead that PE information was carried by broadband activity instead of band-limited oscillations and, moreover, PE signals were distributed across the cortical hierarchy. It remains unclear how to reconcile these discrepant findings from primate neurophysiology, obtained using variations of the auditory oddball paradigm (see ref. 11 for one perspective). Resolving this debate has significant implications for the predictive coding theory, as a central prediction of the theory is that prediction and PE signals are distinct across frequency domains and cortical hierarchy levels4.

To probe predictions of upcoming sensory information, prior studies employed variations of the oddball paradigm and examined the brain responses to predicted stimuli and violations of these regularities. Despite relying on the extrapolation of temporal regularities to form predictions, such paradigms have limited ecological validity, as they fail to capture the dynamic variability of natural sensory inputs12. In the natural environment, sensory stimuli are dynamic and variable, and predictions are typically formed on the basis of complex patterns.

In this study, we use intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings in human patients combined with a naturalistic tone sequence prediction task to probe the neural mechanisms of time-forward prediction and examine the spatial and frequency-domain localization of prediction and PE signals. Our task paradigm represents a key departure from studies using simple repetitions of items or sequences to induce prediction. In the natural environment, predictive information is rarely conveyed by repetition or fixed transition probabilities such as those used by variations of the oddball paradigm12. Instead, predictive information in natural settings unfolds dynamically across space and time in the form of complex statistical regularities, which can often be captured by a 1/f-type power spectrum13. In this sense, we note that we use the term “naturalistic” to describe statistically naturalistic properties, with 1/f statistics, rather than directly ecologically derived. Such temporal fluctuations are prominent in music, speech, and natural soundscapes14–16. Here, slow, gradual fluctuations appear more often than large, abrupt changes, increasing the chance that future stimulus features are similar to recent stimulus features. This temporal autocorrelation allows predictions of future stimuli based on statistical trends rather than exact repetitions of past stimuli.

Importantly, advances in signal processing have enabled precise construction and manipulation of sequences exhibiting statistical regularities similar to natural stimuli17, which have been successfully applied in cognitive neuroscience experiments18–20. These studies showed that humans exploit naturalistic time-varying information to predict upcoming sensory input. Using noninvasive magnetoencephalography (MEG), they also revealed neural markers of prediction and temporal integration of past sensory evidence. These observations support an earlier proposal that the brain, through evolutionary pressure, is well-suited to extracting the statistical regularities prevalent in nature21. However, due to the limited spatial resolution of noninvasive recordings, it remained unclear how these mechanisms are implemented in neural networks and the sensory processing hierarchy.

In addition, effectively predicting future sensory input must rely on integrating past sensory input and extracting its temporal statistical regularities. How brain activity integrates sensory information from an evolving tone sequence, a process termed “sensory history integration” (SHI), is still poorly understood. Previous MEG work on this question revealed widespread SHI effects but had limited spatial resolution20, and it remains unclear how this process is distributed across brain networks involved in predictive computation.

To anticipate our results, we found that while prediction signals are mainly carried by low-frequency activity, PE signals are present in both low and high-frequency activity. Contrary to the predictive coding framework, there was no cortical hierarchy separation between prediction and PE signals, and directed connectivity between electrodes showing PE effects did not increase for tones that violated the established regularity. Instead, over the course of a sequence, as prediction becomes established, inter-regional directed connectivity gradually decreased across widespread regions, except for the parietal-to-frontal pathway, which showed increased connectivity towards the end of the sequence. Together, these findings reveal an integrated and distributed neural processing system for time-forward prediction based on naturalistic temporal regularities and support a re-examination of our conceptualization of how sensory predictions are generated.

Results

Paradigm and experimental design

We presented nine epilepsy patients (age: 22–55, five females), who were undergoing invasive surgical monitoring, with auditory tone sequences following naturalistic temporal regularities prevalent in natural acoustic stimuli (e.g., speech, music, and natural soundscapes). Specifically, each sequence consisted of 34 pure tones (frequency range: 220–880 Hz) presented consecutively with no gap, and the pitch fluctuation of the first 33 tones in the sequence followed a 1/f type power spectrum (P = A/f β, where A is amplitude and β the power-law exponent). Following previous work18–20, we kept the amplitude, A, constant across the sequence and adopted a β value between 0.5 and 1.5 to investigate predictive information in 1/f sequences that mimic natural stimuli’s temporal regularities. To keep the task length manageable for the patients, in this study, we used a constant β value of 1.5, which induces a relatively high autocorrelation that nonetheless remains within the range of dynamical natural stimuli.

Using previously established methods19, we generated fifteen unique, 33-tone sequences (Fig. 1A, black circles and lines). Importantly, all sequences converged on the same pitch value for the 33rd tone, at 440 Hz, at which time point the optimally predicted upcoming tone pitches (p34*) would fall into the low, medium, and high ranges (Fig. 1A, colored arrows). This constraint was introduced to allow the disentangling of sensory processing from predictive processing: during the 33rd tone of each sequence, the sensory input was identical across all sequences, while the predictive information conferred by the sequence history received up to that point varied across sequences. This manipulation allowed us to identify neural activity carrying predictive information that was unrelated to the processing of the current sensory input19,20. Dissociating prediction, PE, and sensory processing signals is a well-known challenge in the investigation of time-forward prediction and previous studies adopting repetition-based paradigms have addressed this issue creatively9,22. Here, by using mathematically constructed sequences that are precisely controlled, we were able to accomplish the same goal in the context of dynamical stimuli containing natural variation and statistical regularity.

The optimally predicted final tone pitch (p34*) was calculated based on the entire past sequence history, given the 1/f power spectral statistics [for details, see ref. 19]. To probe whether participants could form valid predictions based on the sequence history, instead of presenting the optimally predicted pitch, the 33rd tone was followed by a probe tone whose pitch was randomly drawn from four values evenly distributed around 440 Hz (Fig. 1A, open squares) (all pitch values were evaluated on a log(frequency) scale according to human psychoacoustics). On each trial, participants rated how well the final (probe) tone fits with the preceding sequence on a scale of 1–5 (with 1 indicating a low likelihood and 5 a high likelihood), yielding a final tone pitch likelihood (FTPL) rating (Fig. 1B). They then received visual feedback on their rating. For instance, if a participant heard a sequence whose statistics would result in an expectation of a high-pitched predicted final tone (p34*) but, instead, the participant heard a low-pitched final tone (p34), then a reported FTPL rating of 1 or 2 (low likelihood) would constitute a successfully predicted trial.

To assess the participants’ ability to form valid predictions under different rates of sensory information arrival, we manipulated the sequence speed by adopting two different tone durations: 200 and 400 ms (Fig. 1A). In a previous noninvasive MEG study, we found that neuromagnetic signals supporting prediction integrated a fixed number of tones regardless of sequence speed, suggesting a flexible time window for integrating stimulus history involving an informational bottleneck20. However, it remained unknown which specific neural circuits are involved and whether there is heterogeneity across brain regions in either the length (how many tones are integrated) or mode (informational vs. temporal bottleneck) of sequence history integration.

Here, we leveraged intracranial recordings with fine spatial and temporal resolution to examine the neural signatures of prediction and PE, as well as sensory history integration (SHI) underlying predictive computation, that subserve time-forward prediction for naturalistic stimuli sequences.

Predictive behavior based on naturalistic tone sequences

If participants can gain valid predictive information based on the sequence history, their judgments of the likelihood of the final probe tone should depend not only on its actual pitch (p34), or its pitch distance to the previous tone (p33), but also on the optimally predicted final tone pitch based on the preceding sequence (p34*). To test this, we entered subjects’ FTPL ratings into a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with p34 and p34* being the two independent factors. While we found no significant main effects for either p34 or p34* (all p > 0.2), there was a significant interaction effect, with data pooled across both tone duration conditions (F6,42 = 4.39, p = 0.0016; Fig. 2A). Analyzing each tone-duration condition separately (Fig. 2B), the interaction effect was significant for the 400 ms tone duration condition (F6,42 = 4.35, p = 0.0017) and marginally significant for the 200 ms tone duration condition (F6,42 = 2.29, p = 0.053) (one participant’s data were excluded from this analysis due to uneven trial numbers across conditions; for details, see Methods). Specifically, when the presented final tone pitch (p34) was high (880 Hz, shown on the x-axis), participants rated it as more likely if the optimally predicted final tone pitch (p34*) was also high (orange line). Similarly, when the presented final tone pitch was low (220 Hz on the x-axis), participants rated it as more likely if the predicted final tone pitch was also low (dark blue line).

To ensure these effects were not dependent on assumptions of normality, we also performed a permutation-based, nonparametric version of the two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (5000 within-subject shuffles per condition). The pattern of results was unchanged: no significant main effects of p34 or p34* were observed (all p > 0.25), and the interaction effect remained significant for the 400 ms tone duration condition (F6,42 = 0.16, p = 0.032) and for the pooled data (F6,42 = 0.17, p = 0.014), with a marginal effect for the 200 ms condition (F6,42 = 0.13, p = 0.1).

These results confirm that, as with healthy volunteers19,20, epilepsy patients undergoing surgical monitoring could also perform this auditory prediction task correctly and gain valid predictive information by extrapolating from naturalistic, 1/f-type temporal statistical regularity. We conducted careful screening of patients and iEEG data to minimize any potential contribution of pathological activity to the analyzed data (for details, see Methods). On average, ~150 electrodes were recorded in each patient, covering extensive healthy brain tissue outside the seizure onset zone (Supplementary Table 1).

Neural correlates of tone sequence tracking

In total, 990 cortical surface grid/strip electrodes with electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings were recorded and analyzed across the nine patients (for electrode coverage and detailed patient information, see Fig. 1C, Fig. S1, and Table S1). Of these 990 electrodes, 863 were included after preprocessing for further analyses. Each patient performed a task including ten blocks and 120 trials total, lasting 45–50 min. Each unique sequence as shown in Fig. 1A, at each sequence speed, was presented four times total, once with each possible probe tone value.

Since ECoG signals cover a wide range of frequencies and diverse types of neural activity, we first sought to identify the components of the ECoG signals that provide reliable tracking of a given sequence. Any predictive processing based on sequence history requires reliable tracking of the specific sequence—i.e., neural activity involved in predictive computation must exhibit similar modulation over time when the same sequence is repeatedly presented. In other words, neural signals that exhibit reliable tracking of sequences are potential candidates for different components of predictive processing, such as sensory processing (which feeds into predictive processing), sequence history integration, and prediction and PE signals.

To identify such signals, we followed the approach used in a previous MEG study19. For each neural signal of interest (e.g., a specific frequency component from a given electrode), we compared the correlations of its activity time courses across repeated presentations of an identical sequence with the correlations observed across statistically similar sequences (defined by a similar p34*, i.e., sequences within each column of Fig. 1A; for analysis schematic, see Fig. S2A). Thus, this analysis identifies neural signals that have reliable tracking of a given unique sequence, above and beyond statistically similar sequences. The earlier MEG study showed that for the raw (i.e., non-rectified) fluctuations of neuromagnetic activity, the event-related field (ERF)-range activity, especially the low-frequency (<15 Hz) component, reliably tracked a given sequence. Given this, and considering the higher signal sensitivity of iEEG as compared to MEG, we investigated the filtered ECoG activity in the event-related potential (ERP) range (0.5–30 Hz). In addition, given the sensitivity of ECoG to high-frequency activity, especially high-gamma power which correlates with population neuronal firing rates23–25, we additionally investigated the amplitude envelopes of alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (15–30 Hz), low-gamma (30–70 Hz), and high-gamma (70–150 Hz) band activity.

As expected, we found robust sequence tracking in the low-frequency (0.5–30 Hz) filtered signal (Fig. S2B) and high-gamma (70–150 Hz) amplitude fluctuations (Fig. S2C), with 234 (27.1% of all analyzed electrodes) and 149 (17.3%) electrodes showing significant sequence tracking (p < 0.05, one-sided t-test on Fisher-z-transformed correlation values), respectively. Sequence tracking was moderate in the low-gamma (30–70 Hz) band (125 electrodes, 14.5%) and lowest in the alpha (102 electrodes, 11.8%) and beta (113 electrodes, 13.1%) bands.

These results showed that stimulus sequence tracking was most robust in low-frequency filtered activity and in the high-gamma amplitude envelope. Because reliable sequence tracking is a prerequisite for predictive processing based on sequence history information, in the following analyses on the neural substrates of predictive processing, we focus on these components of the ECoG signals.

Neural signals carrying predictive information

We first probed the neural correlates of prediction. In previous studies, isolating the neural signals for prediction was often challenging, due to it being entangled with sensory processing and prediction-error signals. As a result, previous studies using variations of the auditory oddball task typically focused on PE signals, because in these paradigms the sensory information typically correlated with predictive information (e.g., the standard tone or tone sequence was also the predicted tone/tone sequence). Omission trials8 and the pre-stimulus period22 were sometimes investigated to identify prediction signals; however, omission generates its own PE and prediction might not be fully developed in the pre-stimulus period.

In our task, because the penultimate (33rd) tone of each sequence always had a pitch value of 440 Hz, we could examine the neural correlates of predictive information—built up from the sequence history—without confounds introduced by the processing of the current auditory input. In addition, because the 33rd tone fits into the statistical trend of the preceding sequence, it is not associated with elevated PE. During the 33rd tone, stimulus history information as well as the predictive information varied across sequences, whereas the instantaneous sensory input was constant across sequences. We therefore identified the neural correlates of prediction by regressing neural activity during the 33rd tone onto the optimally predicted upcoming final tone pitch (p34*) (see Fig. 3A and Methods).

Specifically, we tested whether low frequency (0.5–30 Hz) neural activity and high-gamma (70–150 Hz) amplitude during the penultimate tone (p33) contained information about the optimally predicted final tone pitch (p34*), and, if so, where in the brain such predictive information resided. To this end, we computed a linear regression of neural activity during the 33rd tone as a function of p34* at each electrode and time point, using cluster-based permutation tests to correct for multiple comparisons across time. Results were pooled across participants and tone duration conditions, and presented using both a conservative threshold based on FDR correction across all electrodes (Fig. 3B, C, large yellow spheres) and a relatively liberal threshold without FDR correction (Fig. 3B, C, all colored spheres).

For the low-frequency activity (Fig. 3B), 13 FDR-corrected (88 uncorrected) prediction electrodes were found, located mainly in auditory areas (eight electrodes) and frontal regions (five electrodes). Less prominent effects were found for the high-gamma amplitude (Fig. 3C), with four FDR-corrected (76 uncorrected) prediction electrodes located in auditory areas (two electrodes) and frontal regions (two electrodes).

Prediction effects for the low-frequency activity started comparatively early. For electrodes reaching FDR-corrected significance, the onset time of predictive information was 27.3 ± 3.2 (mean ± SEM) ms after tone onset for the 200 ms tone-duration condition, and 103.7 ± 7.6 s ms for the 400 ms tone-duration condition (see “Pred” bars in Fig. 4C). This result indicates a relatively early onset of prediction signals, consistent with the gradual build-up of predictive information throughout the course of sequence presentation20.

A potential concern is that even though the 33rd tone had a constant pitch value across trials, sensory-evoked responses driven by the 32nd tone might linger during the presentation of the 33rd tone and confound our analysis of prediction-related neural activity. We addressed this potential caveat in a control analysis. We asked whether, at any of the prediction electrodes shown in Fig. 3B, C, the sensory-evoked activity driven by the 32nd tone correlated with the optimally predicted final tone pitch (p34*) (for details, see Methods). None of the electrodes with significant prediction-related activity (13 for low-frequency activity; 4 for high-gamma amplitude) had any significant time clusters in this analysis. Thus, even if residual sensory-evoked activity from p32 persists into the presentation of the 33rd tone, it does not contain information about the predicted upcoming tone pitch and therefore cannot contribute to the observed prediction effects.

In the alpha band, we also found four FDR-corrected electrodes (116 uncorrected) with a prediction effect, located near the Sylvian fissure (three electrodes) and in the superior parietal area (one electrode). These results are shown in Fig. S4. In the gamma band, we found three FDR-corrected electrodes (84 uncorrected) with a prediction effect, located in the superior temporal gyrus, the precentral gyrus, and the supramarginal gyrus. No significant prediction effects were found in the beta band.

In an additional control analysis, we tested whether neural signals at the frequency of the tone presentation (5 Hz for the 200 ms tone sequence, 2.5 Hz for the 400 ms tone sequence) showed significant prediction effects. No electrodes survived FDR correction in this analysis, suggesting that the neural signal at the tone-presentation frequency does not carry predictive information. This null finding is consistent with an earlier MEG study, which found that the neural oscillation entrained by the tone presentation frequency does not have consistent phase modulation over the course of the stimulus sequence19.

Prediction-error information is present in both low- and high-frequency activity

We next investigated the neural correlates of prediction error (PE). In this task, the final, 34th tone was randomly drawn from four possible values and was thus associated with a large and variable PE. We identified the neural correlates of PE information by analyzing neural activity during the presentation of the final tone. Specifically, we examined which components of this activity correlated with the absolute pitch difference between the actually presented final tone (p34) and the optimally predicted final tone based on the preceding sequence (p34*) (for details, see Fig. 4A and Methods). To this end, we regressed low-frequency (0.5–30 Hz) neural activity and high-gamma (70–150 Hz) amplitude during the final tone of each sequence against ∣p34−p34*∣ across trials. We note that this corresponds to an unsigned prediction error, where the direction of the error (either above or below the predicted tone) is not considered, similar to previous studies using oddball-type paradigms. This analysis, by modeling neural activity as a function of the magnitude of the prediction error, inherently tests whether larger prediction errors elicit stronger neural responses. To control for instantaneous sensory processing, we also included the actual pitch value of the final tone (p34) as a term in the linear regression. This analysis was carried out for each time point during the final tone presentation and each electrode separately. As with the prediction analysis, results were cluster-corrected across time and FDR-corrected across electrodes.

For the low-frequency neural activity, 13 FDR-corrected (80 uncorrected) electrodes with significant PE information were found, with seven electrodes located in the peri-sylvian auditory region, three electrodes located in the frontal cortex, and three electrodes located in the broader temporal/parietal cortex (Fig. 4B). For high-gamma amplitude, 15 FDR-corrected (81 uncorrected) electrodes with significant PE information were found, with 14 electrodes residing around the auditory processing area, and one electrode in the frontal cortex (Fig. 4D). Thus, while the neural correlates of PE in low-frequency signals spread across wider temporal and frontal regions, high-gamma correlates of PE information were concentrated around auditory areas. These results are suggestive of a potential cortical hierarchy in PE processing.

In contrast to the prediction effects, PE effects onset later, with electrodes reaching FDR-corrected significance exhibiting significant PE information at 94.2 ± 4.9 ms after tone onset for the 200 ms tone-duration condition and 126 ± 6.4 ms for the 400 ms tone-duration condition (see “PE” bars in Fig. 4C; calculated using low-frequency neural activity). These results show that predictive information emerges earlier than PE information. This is likely because prediction can build up over the course of the sequence (p1−33), whereas prediction error results from the comparison of predicted (p34*) and currently received (p34) sensory input.

In the alpha band, only one FDR-corrected (115 uncorrected) electrode with significant PE was found, located near Sylvian fissure. In the beta band, only one FDR-corrected (91 uncorrected) electrode was found in the superior frontal region. In the gamma band, only one FDR-corrected (98 uncorrected) electrode was found in the superior parietal region.

In a control analysis, we tested whether neural signals at the frequency of the tone sequence (5 Hz for the 200 ms tone sequence, 2.5 Hz for the 400 ms tone sequence) showed significant PE effects. No electrodes survived FDR correction in this analysis, suggesting that the neural signal at the tone presentation frequency does not carry PE signals.

Finally, we also tested whether neural activity during the presentation of p34 was linearly correlated to the trialwise subject likelihood ratings. For the low-frequency activity, four FDR-corrected (104 uncorrected) electrodes with significant information related to the likelihood rating were found (Fig. S6), with a spatial distribution similar to the original analysis using PE magnitudes calculated from the tone sequence (Fig. 4B). For the high gamma amplitude, 1 FDR-corrected (109 uncorrected) electrode was found (Fig. S6). These results suggest that PE-related neural signals are also correlated with the behaviorally reported likelihood rating, i.e., subjects’ judgment of how surprising the final tone is given the preceding sequence.

Integration of past sensory information in widespread cortices

To shed further light on the neural mechanisms underlying time-forward prediction, we analyzed how brain activity integrates sensory information from an evolving tone sequence, a process termed “sensory history integration” (SHI)20. In naturalistic sensory environments, effective prediction of future sensory input must rely on the integration of past sensory information.

To assess SHI, we analyzed how neural activity during the second half of a tone sequence depends on the pitches of preceding tones. We linearly regressed the neural activity at each electrode and time point during the presentation of a current tone (using 50-ms non-overlapping windows) onto the pitches of the current tone and k′ previous tones (see Methods for details). This analysis was carried out separately for each tone duration. Thus, k′ indexes how many previous tones influence neural activity, above and beyond the current sensory input. We determined the best fitting k′ using a cross-validation procedure and assessed statistical significance by comparing the empirical k′ against a null distribution obtained by shuffling the pitch sequence. Multiple comparisons across electrodes were corrected using FDR.

We had two key questions for this analysis: (1) Does the timescale of SHI increase along the cortical hierarchy? Previous studies have revealed a hierarchy of time scales across the cortex, with progressively longer time scales for stimulus processing as one ascends the cortical hierarchy26–28. (2) Is SHI based on a fixed temporal window (“temporal bottleneck” hypothesis, motivated by fixed time constants in neurons and neuronal circuits) or a fixed amount of information (“information bottleneck” hypothesis, motivated by the need for flexible computation to form optimal predictions)? A previous MEG study20 found that neuromagnetic activity carrying out SHI to support prediction integrated a fixed amount of information. Here, we investigated whether this held true for invasively recorded brain signals and whether there was variability in whether SHI follows a temporal or informational bottleneck across cortical areas involved in SHI.

We first calculated the k′ values using the low-frequency (0.5–30 Hz) neural activity. There were 62 significant electrodes in the 200 ms tone-duration condition (k′ = 6.58 ± 0.75 (mean ± SD)), and 93 significant electrodes in the 400 ms tone-duration condition (k′ = 5.85 ± 0.57). Although significant electrodes were spread throughout the cortex, the majority clustered around auditory sensory regions and frontal regions, and no clear spatial hierarchy in k′ values was observed.

Comparison of k′-values between tone durations in the form of a ratio (k200ms′/k400ms′; Fig. 5B) revealed different modes of integration across electrodes. The scatter plot in Fig. 5B, right, plots k200ms′ against k400ms′, with two colored lines indicating the predictions of the “informational bottleneck” hypothesis (green line; k200ms′=k400ms′) and the “temporal bottleneck” hypothesis (red line; k200ms′=2k400ms′), respectively. Some electrodes integrated more tones in the 400 ms tone-duration condition (i.e., ratio <1; blue electrodes in Fig. 5B), some electrodes integrated a similar number of tones in both tone-duration conditions (ratio ~1; green electrodes), yet other electrodes integrated more tones in the 200 ms tone-duration condition (i.e., ratio >1; red-yellow electrodes in Fig. 5B). A small number of electrodes integrated twice as many tones in the 200 ms tone-duration condition as in the 400 ms tone-duration condition (ratio ~2; red electrodes), suggested that they had a fixed time window for integration. However, no clear spatial hierarchy between these integration modes was found; instead, electrodes following different modes of integration appear to be mixed within each cortical region.

Averaged across all significant electrodes, the number of tones integrated was similar across tone-duration conditions (k200ms′/k400ms′ = 1.14 ± 0.6) and was around 5 (green diamond in Fig. 5B, right). This suggests that, on average, the brain performs sensory history integration mainly using a fixed amount of information (i.e., a fixed number of tones), rather than across a fixed time duration. While this population-level result is consistent with the conclusion from earlier observation using MEG20, the intracranial data reveal a much richer and more heterogeneous picture across electrodes, with different modes of SHI (Fig. 5B) and different lengths of integration (Fig. 5A) intermixed.

We obtained similar SHI results in the high-gamma amplitude (Fig. S3A). Overall, a larger number of electrodes had significant SHI in the high-gamma band, with 146 significant electrodes in the 200 ms tone-duration condition (k′ = 6.00 ± 1.17), and 124 significant electrodes in the 400 ms tone-duration condition (k′ = 5.34 ± 0.97). Across all significant electrodes, the number of integrated tones in each tone-duration condition was around 5 (Fig. S3B, right, green diamond), strikingly similar to the low-frequency activity.

Overall, these results depart from previous studies, revealing a hierarchy of time scales. These earlier studies used a pre-stimulus waiting period27 or long, semantically meaningful stimuli (e.g., stories and movies)26,28. Instead, we found that short and long integration windows are intermixed at each level of the cortical hierarchy. In addition, although integration based on an informational bottleneck was the dominant mode of SHI, individual electrodes exhibited a high degree of variability, with integration based on a temporal bottleneck and integration based on an informational bottleneck intermixed in each cortical region. These results provide fresh perspectives and new constraints for future studies aiming to understand the cortical circuit algorithms for sensory history integration and prediction.

Changes in directed connectivity during sequence processing and sensory prediction

Complex perceptual processes such as sensory history integration and prediction of future sensory input can be expected to extend beyond single brain regions and rely on the interaction of multiple, spatially distributed brain regions. Indeed, our earlier analyses on prediction- and PE-related neural signals revealed neural activity distributed across auditory, frontal, and parietal regions. To understand the interactions and information flow across this distributed set of brain regions, we made use of the high spatial specificity and temporal precision of ECoG recordings, by computing directed connectivity using Granger causality (GC) between different brain regions (see Fig. 6A and Methods for details). Because predictive processes are not static but evolve as regularities in the stimulus sequence are learned, we reasoned that changes in connectivity across the sequence may reveal how brain regions dynamically coordinate predictive processing. This approach tests whether prediction- or PE-related networks strengthen or reorganize as the sequence unfolds, reflecting the gradual formation or updating of internal models. To this end, we selected electrodes demonstrating a prediction (P) or prediction error (PE) effect in either tone-duration condition. To be inclusive in electrode selection, any electrode showing a significant (p < 0.05, uncorrected) P or PE effect in the low-frequency activity or high-gamma amplitude was included. After sorting the electrodes based on their lobe-specific locations, we defined electrode pairs for each combination of effects (i.e., P→P, PE→PE, P→PE, and PE→P) and each pair of anatomical regions (frontal-parietal, frontal-temporal, and parietal-temporal) within each participant. Table S2 lists the number of electrodes and electrode pairs for each effect type and anatomical location pairing. For each electrode pair, we computed pairwise GC using neural activity in the broadband (0.5–150 Hz) range, following the well-established time-domain GC analysis approach29,30. Importantly, this approach is not subjected to the potential spectral power confound that might influence frequency-domain GC analysis11.

We first investigated if GC estimates—which index the strength of directed connectivity—linearly changed over the course of tone sequence presentation and if such changes varied depending on brain region or effect combination. Examining linear trends across tones allowed us to test whether directed information flow systematically increases or decreases as predictive models become stronger. To this end, for each combination of spatial regions and P/PE effects, we selected two subsets of seven evenly spaced tones covering the entire length of the tone sequence from the 1st to the 32nd tone (p33 and p34 were excluded due to their special properties) and computed GC for each electrode pair. GC values were averaged across the two subsets of evenly spaced tones and across the tone duration conditions. Next, we fit a linear trend across the seven averaged GC values and compared the resulting slope estimates across electrode pairs via a two-sided t-test against 0. For the majority of spatial connections and effect combinations, connectivity strength gradually decreased over the course of the tone sequence (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Table 3). However, for all types of connections from parietal to frontal regions (P→P, PE→PE, P→PE, and PE→P), connectivity strength increased toward the end of the sequence (Fig. 6B, magenta lines; also see Table S3). In addition, a similar effect existed in the parietal to temporal pathway, reaching significance in the PE→PE and PE→P connections (Table S3). This increased connectivity towards the end of the sequence for connections originating from the parietal cortex might reflect strengthened predictive computation, because towards the end of the sequence, enough tones have occurred for the statistical trend to be processed explicitly. The parietal cortex is situated within the dorsal auditory pathway, which transmits information from auditory regions in the temporal cortex to the frontal cortex via the parietal lobe. Therefore, our results show that both putatively bottom-up (parietal→frontal) and putatively top-down (parietal→temporal) connections (based on the auditory hierarchy31) in this pathway may be involved in predictive processing.

Since p33 and p34 followed unique properties compared to the preceding tones of each sequence, we also investigated how GC estimates changed for these tones. For each effect and lobe combination, we compared the Granger Causality estimates for p33 or p34 against the average of the immediately preceding tones (i.e., p30, p31, p32), using a Wilcoxon sign-rank test across lobe-specific electrode pairs. The results followed the dominant trend of decreasing GC estimates across the tone sequence, with all significant results indicating lower GC estimates for p33 or p34 compared to the preceding tones (Fig. 6C, D and Supplementary Table 4).

To directly test whether the observed parietal→frontal GC increase at the end of the sequence reflects predictive processing rather than general attentional effects, we examined parietal→frontal GC strength during the last tone as a function of participants’ trial-by-trial FTPL ratings. Since the last tone, p34, was randomly chosen from four probe tones, we reasoned that prediction effects and attentional effects are dissociated here. Since more surprising stimuli tend to attract attention, attentional effects should negatively correlate with FTPL rating across trials (because high FTPL means less surprise), while prediction effects should positively correlate with FTPL rating across trials. We therefore performed a median split of all trials for each subject based on FTPL ratings. For each subset of trials, we separately computed GC strength for each connectivity type (P → P, P → PE, PE → PE, and PE → P) in the parietal→frontal pathway. For all connectivity types, GC values were significantly higher on trials with high FTPL ratings compared to low FTPL ratings (all p < 0.014; Fig. 6E). This positive association between parietal→frontal GC and behavioral rating of prediction supports the interpretation that the increase in GC from parietal to frontal cortex toward the end of the sequence is specifically linked to predictive processes.

The above analyses used a lobar-based parcellation29, which provided more statistical power since only simultaneously recorded electrodes could be analyzed for connectivity (see Table S2, Part B, for detailed counts of electrode pairs). In order to address the limitation that lobe-based parcellation is relatively coarse, we performed an additional analysis using a finer-grained parcellation of electrode pairs between regions of the auditory hierarchy. To this end, we adopted an established framework of auditory hierarchy involving two auditory pathways31 (Fig. S7A), with a dorsal pathway being: auditory cortex (AC; Brodmann area 41/42) → superior temporal sulcus (STS; area 22) → inferior parietal lobule (IPL; area 40) → dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC; area 8) and premotor cortex (PMC, area 6). In parallel, a ventral auditory pathway is defined as: AC → inferior frontal cortex (IFC, dorsal: areas 44; ventral: area 45) → dlPFC and PMC. Throughout the course of the tone sequence, we found that connectivity strength gradually decreased between the dorsal IFC and STS, and between dorsal and ventral IFC (Fig. S7C). However, connectivity strength from STS and dorsal IFC to dlPFC increased. Unfortunately, in this finer-grained parcellation, there were not a sufficient number of electrode pairs between IPL and frontal regions (Fig. S7B), precluding a direct comparison with the parietal-to-frontal GC increase observed in Fig. 6B. When comparing GC during p33 and p34 to the preceding tones, we found a pattern highly similar to Fig. 6C, D, with globally reduced GC (Fig. S7D, E). The only exception was an increase in GC from ventral IFC to PMC during p33, likely reflecting motor preparation as the tone sequence nears the end and an upcoming response is expected. In sum, while this analysis based on a fine-grained parcellation should be taken with caveats given the comparatively lower number of electrode pairs analyzed, the results are broadly consistent with our analysis using a lobar-based parcellation.

Together, these results do not concord with the predictive coding framework, which postulates that bottom-up prediction error signals should increase for the final tone in each sequence that did not fit with the preceding sequence autocorrelation statistics. Further, the predictive coding framework predicts a clear segregation of spectral and anatomical signatures for prediction and PE signals, which we did not observe here. Our findings highlight the potential role that the dorsal auditory pathway plays in time-forward predictive computation, as connectivity in this pathway (between electrodes showing significant predictive or PE information) uniquely increased towards the end of the sequence, when predictive computation becomes more established.

Discussion

The brain’s ability to form valid predictions about sensory environments is a key function essential for an organism’s survival in the natural world. Natural sensory stimuli are dynamic, with complex temporal statistical regularities that can be used to predict upcoming sensory inputs. Yet, prediction of upcoming sensory information (“time-forward prediction”) is typically studied using paradigms that rely on simple repetitions or fixed transition probabilities. Here, we employed a recently developed paradigm involving auditory tone sequences that follow naturalistic 1/f-type temporal regularities to investigate brain mechanisms of time-forward prediction in a more ecologically valid setting. Combining this paradigm with large-scale iEEG recordings in human patients—providing millisecond temporal resolution and precise spatial localization unmatched by current noninvasive methods—we identified the neural signatures of prediction and prediction-error signals, their inter-regional communication, as well as sensory history integration computation underlying predictive processing. Our findings reveal an integrated and nuanced picture of distributed predictive processing and call for a re-evaluation of theoretical frameworks in the context of time-forward prediction.

In addition to being more ecologically valid, our current paradigm allows a relatively clean separation of prediction and PE signals from sensory processing, which has often been a challenge in past studies. Several creative solutions had been proposed9,22,32, but all were based on variants of the auditory oddball paradigm. Given our experimental design, prediction-related neural activity found in our analysis cannot be attributed to instantaneous stimulus processing at p33, as this tone was held constant across trials. Although sensory-evoked activity from the 32nd tone might linger during the presentation of the 33rd tone, our control analysis ruled out the possibility that any such residual sensory-evoked activity could have contributed to prediction-related neural activity identified by our analysis. This conclusion echoes a previous finding from an MEG study using a similar paradigm, which suggested that instantaneous sensory-evoked responses do not carry prediction signals related to the processing of the evolving sequence (see Fig. 3 in ref. 20). Taken together, our experimental design aims to control for low-level confounds while elucidating how the brain extrapolates from past sensory history to anticipate future sensory inputs33,34.

We found that both prediction and PE signals involve both low-frequency signals in the classic ERP range (<30 Hz) and high-frequency gamma-band (70–150 Hz) amplitude, with prediction signals being more prominent in the low-frequency activity. In addition, both prediction and PE signals were distributed across both auditory and frontal regions. These findings challenge a strong claim of the predictive coding framework, positing that prediction signals reside in higher-order brain regions and lower frequencies and, conversely, PE signals reside in sensory regions and higher frequencies4. By contrast, our results revealed a spatial distinction based not on function but on frequency components: low-frequency effects (for both prediction and PE information) were more widespread, whereas high-frequency effects were more localized, with clusters in sensory regions. This pattern aligns with previous findings showing that high-frequency effects tend to be more spatially focal than low-frequency signals35. These results were obtained following an initial frequency-domain “localizer” analysis to evaluate reliable tracking of a given stimulus sequence in different frequency bands, which was found to be strongest in these two signal components. Consistent with this finding, we did not find strong prediction or PE signals in other frequency bands, including alpha, beta, and low gamma.

Early electrophysiological studies supporting the predictive coding framework found that predictions were conveyed by low-frequency signals in the top-down direction and PEs were conveyed by high-frequency signals in the bottom-up direction5–7,36,37. However, some of these studies investigated only top-down and bottom-up information flow without using a paradigm that induces time-forward predictions5,36,37. In addition, the data interpretation in some of these studies has been recently challenged11. Several studies have reported findings at odds with this standard framework. Similar to our findings, early iEEG studies on auditory prediction reported PE effects in low-frequency event-related potentials and high-frequency signals, and in both temporal and frontal cortices38,39. Further, recent intracranial recordings of auditory oddball tasks in primates revealed PE information in broadband signals, while no rhythmic components could be linked to any auditory statistical irregularities9,10. Finally, our targeted investigation of prediction-related effects in the alpha and beta bands—previously postulated to convey top-down predictions by the predictive coding framework—revealed weak or absent effects. In sum, our present findings add to the accumulating evidence that a strict hierarchical and spectral separation of prediction and PE signals proposed by the predictive coding framework warrants revision.

Interestingly, we found that PE signals in the high-gamma band were concentrated around the auditory region, whereas PE signals in low-frequency activity were more broadly distributed (Fig. 4). This suggests a potential functional difference between PE signals in lower and higher levels of the cortical hierarchy, which remains to be investigated.

In this work, we investigated “unsigned” PE signals, where deviations from the optimal prediction in both the “too high” and “too low” directions are treated similarly (see Eq. 3 in Methods). This approach follows previous human and animal work investigating sequence prediction using variations of the oddball paradigm or sequence repetitions, e.g., refs. 6,10,40. However, signed PE signals have also been discovered, most notably in the subcortical dopamine system, where they appear to co-exist with unsigned PE signals41. Thus, an interesting question for future investigation is whether both signed and unsigned PE signals co-exist in the cortical hierarchy.

For prediction signals, we found that both low-frequency and high-gamma correlates were distributed across both auditory and frontal regions, suggesting that the computation of prediction might involve coordinated activity across regions at different levels of the hierarchy. The prediction- and PE-related signals we observed in low-frequency activity and high-gamma activity likely reflect distinct but complementary neural processes in these different frequency bands. Since the spatial distributions of prediction- and PE-related signals are broader in low-frequency activity than in the high-gamma band, these results might indicate interactions between distributed modulatory signals and local spiking activity, consistent with findings linking high-gamma amplitude with population firing rates24,25.

We also found that directed influences across the network mostly decreased over the course of the sequence, except for directed influences from parietal cortex to frontal and temporal cortices, which increased toward the end of the sequence. For the parietal→frontal pathway, this increased influence was evident for all pairs of electrode combinations (P→P, PE→PE, P→PE, and PE→P); for the parietal→temporal pathway, the increased influence toward the end of the sequence came from PE electrodes in parietal regions and was targeted to both P and PE electrodes in temporal cortex (Table S3). As the sequence progresses, the statistical trend in the sequence becomes more evident and, as a result, predictability increases. Therefore, the overall decreasing trend of directed influences across the cortex (frontal↔temporal, frontal→parietal, temporal→parietal) might reflect decreasing task demand as the sequence is processed and predictability becomes established. By contrast, the robust increase of directed influences out of the parietal cortex toward the end of the sequence might reflect the accumulation of predictive information in preparation for the response prompt. Although the detailed computation remains to be elucidated, these results provide strong evidence for a special role of the parietal cortex in predictive processing. The parietal cortex is part of the dorsal auditory pathway, which carries information from the auditory cortex to frontal regions via the parietal cortex and has been proposed to carry spatial and temporal processing and an internal model for sensorimotor control31. Since prediction of upcoming sensory input is important for guiding actions, our result suggests that the dorsal visual pathway might play a central role in time-forward prediction in the service of guiding actions.

We did not observe increased bottom-up PE signaling during the processing of the final probe tone, which was randomly drawn from four possible values and did not follow the statistical trends of the preceding sequence. It is possible that our connectivity analysis, focused on inter-lobe long-distance connections, may have failed to identify local PE signaling (e.g., between primary auditory cortex and auditory association cortex within the temporal lobe) that could have increased during the final tone. In addition, although we used time-domain GC29,42, which is not subject to the spectral power confounds that might influence frequency-domain GC11, other directed influence measures such as nonlinear metrics could be explored in future work, e.g., refs. 10,43.

Finally, using the manipulation of sequence presentation speed, our SHI analysis confirmed an earlier observation using noninvasive MEG showing that sensory history integration is limited by an informational bottleneck20. On average, electrodes with significant SHI effects integrated pitch information over the prior ~6 tones, and had a similar number of integrated tones even when sequence speed doubled, suggesting that a similar amount of information was integrated regardless of the rate of sensory information arrival. Yet, our present results obtained with iEEG also revealed substantial heterogeneity across electrodes, with short and long integration effects mixed within each cortical region and no apparent hierarchy across regions. In addition, although integration based on an informational bottleneck was dominant, a small number of electrodes exhibited a pattern more consistent with a temporal bottleneck, suggesting heterogeneity in SHI computation within a cortical region. Finally, the increased sensitivity of ECoG to high frequencies44 allowed us to establish robust SHI effects in high-gamma amplitudes, which was not assessed in the previous MEG study.

Although the optimal predictor of a 1/f-type time-varying signal formally requires an integration window of infinite length, biological systems cannot maintain arbitrarily long histories. Instead, our estimated neural integration window of k’ ≈6 tones likely reflects a resource-efficient compromise, capturing the most behaviorally informative portion of the stimulus history while avoiding the computational burden of tracking the full long-range dependencies of the sequence. Further, the match with the integration window length reported in prior work using a different recording modality and participant groups19,20 suggests that a relatively short, approximately five-to-six-item temporal memory may represent a consistent feature of human auditory sequence prediction.

Our study shares common limitations of iEEG research in clinical populations. Although iEEG in patients with epilepsy can yield valuable insights into normal physiology, pathological factors must be carefully controlled. We mitigated these influences by recruiting patients with intact cognitive function (WAIS-IV screening), excluding electrodes with prevalent interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) from analyses, and by screening the data carefully for any pathological or artifactual activity and removing such activity from the analyzed data (see Methods and Fig. S10). Patients were typically off anti-epileptic medication during recordings due to clinical seizure monitoring, reducing medication-related confounds. Moreover, participants exhibited behavioral signatures of auditory prediction that align with findings in healthy individuals20, supporting the view that the reported neural effects are not epiphenomena of pathology. In this study, we focused on superficial cortical areas relevant to auditory prediction accessed by ECoG electrodes, similar to previous iEEG studies6,38. Although depth (SEEG) electrodes were available in our patient sample (see Table S1), they mostly covered mesial and deep brain structures that were not our target areas of interest—e.g., there was no sampling of primary auditory cortex (A1); hence, we did not analyze the SEEG data in this study. Future studies leveraging research electrodes with more targeted electrode coverage, e.g., refs. 45,46 could potentially provide a more fine-grained anatomical view.

A further limitation concerns across-subject inference and statistical modeling. We pooled electrodes across participants for group-level analyses, an approach supported by current iEEG recommendations47, as mixed-effects frameworks are not easily applied to the nested and unbalanced structure of iEEG data with variable electrode coverage across subjects. This challenge is greater when there are no pre-defined regions of interest, as is the case here for the prediction, PE, and SHI analyses. We verified that significant results were contributed by multiple or most participants (See Part C of Table S2 and Figs. S8 and S9), ensuring that reported findings were not driven by outlier subjects. Additionally, grouping electrodes by lobe, while reducing dimensionality and aiding interpretability, may obscure finer-grained connectivity patterns. To address this, we have also grouped electrodes according to regions of the auditory hierarchy (Fig. S7), which yielded broadly consistent results. Finally, technical limitations, such as minor imprecision in electrode localization (due to coregistration of post-implant CT/MRI with standard atlases) and potential volume conduction, further constrain anatomical specificity.

In sum, our results demonstrate that time-forward prediction of naturalistic sequences is a neural process that is spatially and spectrally distributed. These findings do not fit neatly with the existing predictive coding framework, and suggest the need for a re-evaluation and update of our understanding of how the brain constructs time-forward prediction of future sensory input. Our results further highlight the role of the dorsal auditory pathway in constructing time-forward prediction. These findings invite future investigation to further elucidate the underlying circuit-level and computational mechanisms supporting this critical brain function.

Methods

Participants

Nine epilepsy patients (age range: 22–55, mean: 30.9; five females, eight right-handed) implanted with electrode strips and/or grids performed a task involving auditory tone sequences at bedside while undergoing surgical evaluation with intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) monitoring at New York University Langone Health (NYULH) Comprehensive Epilepsy Center. The study protocol (s14-02101) was approved by the NYULMH Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to study participation. All participants had intact or corrected-to-normal visual and auditory perception. Patients with cognitive impairment as assessed by Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-IV), brain lesion, or contraindication to MRI were excluded from the study. In addition, any patients who had an electrographic seizure within 1 h prior to the intended task onset were excluded. Demographic, clinical and electrode coverage information for each patient is included in Supplementary Table 1. Individual electrode placement plotted on an MNI template brain is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Experimental stimuli

We presented participants with naturalistic auditory tone sequences with pitch fluctuations exhibiting statistical regularities similar to those prevalent in natural stimuli14,15. We created tone sequences following a procedure described in detail in ref. 19,20. Specifically, each tone sequence consisted of 34 concatenated pure tones presented without temporal overlap or gap. Within the same sequence, tone pitches were autocorrelated over time (i.e., temporally dependent on each other), allowing for the prediction of future tone pitches based on the information provided by previously presented tones. Within each sequence, the degree of autocorrelation was determined by β, which defines the temporal power spectrum of the pitch fluctuation over time, such that P ∝ 1/f β. A β of 0 means that pitch values between any two time points are uncorrelated, while a positive β implies that tone pitches are temporally dependent on one another, with a larger β corresponding to a stronger autocorrelation. In the present study, we presented auditory tone sequences featuring a constant autocorrelation strength, with β = 1.5. This was chosen to fall within the range of natural acoustic stimuli, yet have a relatively strong autocorrelation. In previous MEG work19,20, we had tested β values ranging between 0.5 and 1.5, and showed that the results were similar.

Following previous work19, we scaled each tone series such that their pitches ranged from 220 to 880 Hz, covering two octaves (A3–A5). In this frequency range, the iso-loudness curve of human hearing is flat; that is, with constant amplitude, loudness stays roughly constant. Next, we discretized all tone series so that each tone was assigned to one of 25 values evenly spaced on the log scale with semitone distance. A circulant embedding algorithm17 was used to create 15 unique 33-element long series:

All tone sequences converged on an identical penultimate (33rd) tone pitch (p33) of 440 Hz. During the presentation of the 33rd tone, sensory input was constant across trials (a pure tone of 440 Hz), but sequence history and, therefore, the predicted upcoming tone pitch varied across sequences. This allowed us to disentangle neural processing of the current sensory input from neural processing of sequency history and time-forward prediction.

For each tone sequence, we computed the theoretically predicted final (34th) tone pitch (p34*) based on the preceding sequence history (see ref. 19 for details). p34* represents the optimally fitting final tone pitch given the pitch information provided by the first 33 tones. We selected five unique sequences for each level of predicted final tone pitch (p34*: low [370 Hz], medium [440 Hz], high [523 Hz]; corresponding to the columns in Fig. 1A).

To probe participants’ ability to predict p34*, we independently selected the actual 34th tone of each sequence (p34) from one of four possible pitches. These pitches were either six [at 311 or 622 Hz] or 12 [at 220 or 880 Hz] semitone steps below or above 440 Hz, the pitch of the 33rd tone (note that pitch is evaluated on a log(frequency) scale). Consequently, for a listener who can extract the predictive information provided by the temporal autocorrelation within a given tone sequence, the tone pitch distance between p34 (i.e., the presented final tone) and p34* (i.e., the theoretically predicted final tone) should influence if p34 is considered likely or unlikely following the previous sequence information (p1−33).

We presented identical tone sequences in two different tone duration (TD) conditions, with 200 ms per tone (corresponding to 6.8 s per sequence) or 400 ms per tone (13.6 s per sequence). In total, 15 unique sequences (5 exemplars × 3 p34*) × 2 TD constituted 30 distinct auditory sequences. Each distinct sequence was presented four times at randomized time points within ten blocks, each time paired with a unique probe tone pitch (p34), resulting in a total of 120 trials per participant.

Paradigm and experimental setup

Participants performed the task (Fig. 1B) while sitting upright in their hospital bed with a laptop placed in front of them on a bedside table. We presented auditory tone sequences with a sampling frequency of 44,100 Hz using the PsychPortAudio function of the Psychophysics Toolbox48 in MATLAB (The Mathworks Inc., Natick/MA, USA). Auditory tone sequences were presented through a speaker placed in front of the patients.

Each trial began with a blank screen (duration: 0.5 s), followed by the central presentation of a fixation point (0.7 s). Next, we presented tone sequences (duration: 6.8/13.6 s) while the fixation point remained on the screen. We instructed participants to fixate on the fixation point whenever it is present to minimize eye movements. Next, we presented a blank screen for 0.4 s, after which participants rated how likely the final tone pitch p34 was given the preceding tone sequence. Thus, participants rated how well p34 fitted with the overall pattern of tone pitches in the preceding tone sequence. Participants rated final tone pitch likelihood (FTPL) on a scale of 1 (very unlikely) to 5 (very likely) within a response window of 5 s. Participants entered responses using the number keys on the laptop keyboard using their left hand. After providing a response, participants were provided with feedback for 2 s, after which the next trial began. During the main task, the feedback screen simply displayed the response given by the participant as well as its interpretation (e.g., “Response = 4; relatively likely.”); this helped to reduce button press mistakes. Feedback about the correctness of the response was only given during pretask training.

Prior to the task, participants performed a short training session to familiarize themselves with the task. For each theoretically predicted final tone pitch (p34*: low, medium, high), three exemplary tone sequences not used in the main task were presented. Feedback about how close the respective presented final tone pitch (p34) was to the predicted final tone pitch (p34*) was provided after each given response. Feedback was graded based on the distance between the predicted tone (p34*) and the presented tone (p34). For larger mismatches, participants were informed that a response of 1 or 2 would be appropriate. If a participant selected 3 (unsure), the task and example sequence were re-explained. Subsequently, participants repeated the training session with these training sequences in randomized order without further feedback, until they reported that they were familiar with the task.

Across the entire experiment, we split trials into ten blocks of 12 trials each, and participants were given the option to take self-terminated rest periods after each block. The entire experiment lasted for ~50 min, including setup and training time.

Behavioral analysis

Participants’ FTPL ratings allowed us to investigate if participants could extract the information present in the preceding tone sequence (p1−33) to predict the upcoming final tone’s pitch. If this is the case, FTPL ratings should be a function of both p34 and p34*, since p34* represented the most likely final tone pitch given the preceding tone sequence and the statistical regularity embedded therein. At the group level, we analyzed FTPL ratings with a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (factors: p34*, p34), separately for each TD condition, as well as for the combined data across both TD conditions. For this analysis, we included participants #2–9 (N = 8), since participant #1 performed an older task version featuring uneven trial numbers across p34*-p34 combinations, but the behavioral pattern from patient #1 was qualitatively similar to the others.

To ensure our results were robust to potential violations of normality given the small sample size, we also implemented a nonparametric permutation version of the two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. For each tone-duration condition and for the combined data, condition labels (p34 and p34*) were shuffled within participants 5000 times to generate null distributions of the F-statistics, from which empirical p values were computed. This approach directly tests the significance of main effects and interactions without assuming normally distributed residuals.

iEEG recordings

We recorded iEEG from implanted subdural platinum-iridium electrodes embedded in silastic sheets (2.3 mm diameter contacts, 10 mm center–center spacing, Ad-Tech Medical Instrument, Racine, WI, USA). All decisions to implant, placement of electrodes, and the duration of invasive monitoring were determined solely on clinical grounds and without reference to this study. Electrodes were arranged as grid arrays (8 × 8 contacts, 10 or 5 mm spacing), linear strips (1 × 4 to 12 contacts), or depth electrodes (1 × 8 or 12 contacts). Depth electrodes were not analyzed in the present study, because surface grids/strips better covered our anatomical regions of interest. Subdural electrodes covered extensive portions of the lateral and medial frontal, parietal, occipital, and temporal cortex of the left and/or right hemisphere (see Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Within 24 h after surgical implantation of electrodes, patients underwent a post-operative brain MRI to confirm subdural electrode placement. Electrodes were localized and mapped onto the pre-implant and post-implant T1-weighted MRI using geometric models of the electrode strips/grids and the cortical surface49. Anatomical labels derived from individual mapping were used to distinguish electrodes based on anatomical ROI. Electrode-specific coordinates were transformed into common MNI space for visualization across participants.

iEEG recordings were made using one of two amplifier types. Data from participant #1 was recorded with a NicoletOne amplifier (Natus Neurologics, Middleton, WI), bandpass-filtered from 0.16–250 Hz and digitized at 512 Hz. Data from the remaining participants were recorded with the Neuroworks Quantum Amplifier (Natus Biomedical, Appleton, WI) at 2048 Hz, bandpass-filtered at 0.01–682.67 Hz and then downsampled to 512 Hz. ECoG signals were referenced to a two-contact subdural strip facing toward the skull near the craniotomy site during the recording (and re-referenced offline during analysis to a common-average reference). A similar two-contact strip screwed to the skull was used for the instrument ground.

We note that our analyses exclusively focus on data from ECoG electrodes, with no analysis of SEEG electrodes. As a result, we use the term ECoG throughout the manuscript.

iEEG data preprocessing

We used custom-written MATLAB (R2019b; The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) scripts and the Fieldtrip Toolbox (version: 20210212, http://www.fieldtriptoolbox.org/)50 for all preprocessing and analysis steps. First, we split the data based on triggers indicating task timing and button presses, which were sent via the laptop’s parallel port to the DC ports on the amplifier to sync task timing and the ECoG data stream. We first split the data into blocks and manually inspected the power spectrum and raw signal of each electrode. Electrodes with noisy recordings (e.g., due to saturation, pulse-, muscle-, or movement-related artifacts, poor contact, and epileptiform activity) or those that could not be localized on the post-implant T1-weighted MRI were excluded from further analysis.

Specifically, interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) were detected using an automated algorithm based on ref. 51. For each electrode, the broadband signal was z-scored after bandpass filtering (1–80 Hz), and putative IEDs were identified as sharp transients with a duration of 20–70 ms, and (when present) followed by a slow wave. Detected events were visually reviewed. Channels exhibiting a high IED rate (>6.5 events/min) were excluded from further analyses. In addition, all data segments, including detected IED events, were removed from analyses. Muscle artifacts, although rare in our data, were defined by (i) broadband high-frequency power elevations (>80 Hz) with a flattened spectral slope and (ii) short-duration, high-amplitude bursts in the time domain. These segments were detected using FieldTrip’s automatic artifact detection (z-thresholding of high-frequency power and line-length metrics), inspected visually, and subsequently removed using standard FieldTrip routines. Finally, heartbeat pulse-related artifacts were detected as narrow 1–2 Hz peaks in the power spectrum that were phase-locked to the electrocardiogram (ECG). Heartbeat-related artifacts were removed using a custom procedure described in detail in ref. 29. Examples of each artifact type can be found in Fig. S10.

For each block, we removed the mean and linear trends from the iEEG data. A high-pass filter of 0.05 Hz was applied to very slow electrode drifts. Next, we applied a zero-phase-shift third-order Butterworth band-stop filter centered on 60, 120, 180, and 240 Hz, with a 2 Hz bandwidth, to remove line noise. Separately for each block, we re-referenced data by computing a common-average reference based on all electrodes lying within 1.5–2 SD of the mean activity.

Next, we epoched data into trials, starting 1 s before the tone sequence started and lasting until 1 s after a response was provided. We further screened single trials for artifacts caused by muscle activity, cardiac activity, and eye movements. Artifacts were identified using a z-score-based algorithm implemented in FieldTrip and subsequently removed from the data. Trialwise data were screened for remaining interictal epileptiform discharges (IED) using a semi-automatic approach51 and contaminated epochs were removed. Trialwise data were baseline-corrected using a 500-ms period before sequence presentation. Next, we filtered data using a third-order Butterworth bandpass filter to extract specific signal components, including low-frequency activity (0.5–30 Hz), alpha-band activity (8–12 Hz), beta-band activity (15–30 Hz), gamma band activity (30–70 Hz), and high-gamma band activity (70–150 Hz). For frequency bands ranging from 8 to 150 Hz, we extracted the amplitude envelop using Hilbert transform, which was log-transformed and normalized by subtracting the mean across the trial, separately for each channel. For the gamma and high-gamma bands, we additionally smoothed data using a moving average window of 100 ms length.

Neural correlates of tone sequence tracking

To determine which components of neural activity are potentially central for the prediction of naturalistic auditory sequences, we first analyzed which signal components reliably tracked individual sequences. This was based on the rationale that effective prediction of p34* for an individual tone sequence requires the brain to differentially process the specific individual sequence in contrast to other, different sequences19. For each electrode and signal component (including 0.5–30 Hz filtered activity and amplitude envelops of alpha, beta, gamma, and high-gamma band, as described earlier), we computed Pearson correlations between neural data time series averaged with 50-ms time windows for (a) identical tone series presentations (i.e., repeated presentations of the same sequence) and (b) similar tone series presentations (i.e., presentations of different sequences that had the same p34*). Correlation coefficients for identical vs. similar sequences were Fisher-z-transformed and compared using a two-sample, one-sided t-test. Significant sequence tracking is defined as a higher correlation for repetitions of an identical sequence than between similar sequences. We used a relatively liberal statistical threshold (p < 0.05, uncorrected across electrodes) in this analysis to visualize all potential sequence-tracking neural activity as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. This analysis was used as a “frequency band localizer” for subsequent analyses, such that those signal components that showed the strongest correlations for identical sequences were selected as those that are reliably modulated by sensory inputs, and hence would be ideal candidates for prediction and prediction error analyses. Importantly, this analysis was not used to select specific electrodes for further analyses. Our premise is that a system generating sequence-based predictions must reliably encode (track) that sequence, i.e., show similar modulation over time across repetitions of the same sequence; this premise is also supported by prior work showing that history-dependent neural integration both tracks the ongoing sequence and predicts upcoming items19,20.

Neural correlates of prediction and prediction error

For these analyses, we focused on the two signal components that exhibited the most prominent effects of tone sequence tracking—low-frequency (0.5–30 Hz) activity and high-gamma (70–150 Hz) amplitude. We also conducted these analyses using amplitude fluctuations of the alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (15–30 Hz) bands that have been previously implicated in the predictive coding framework.

To determine the neural correlates of final tone pitch prediction, we assessed whether neural activity during the presentation of p33 contained information about the theoretically predicted upcoming final tone pitch (p34*). To do this, we computed a linear regression across trials for each tone-duration condition. To maximize temporal fidelity, we used sample-wise neural activity in each electrode during the presentation of p33. For each participant, we linearly regressed neural activity N at each electrode e, sample s, and trial n during the presentation of the 33rd tone onto p34*:

Any neural activity associated with the processing of p33 was assumed to remain constant across trials, as the pitch of the 33rd tone was always 440 Hz. Hence, this term was excluded from the regression analysis. β1,e,s describes how neural activity during p33 depends on p34*.

We then assessed whether neural activity during the presentation of p34 was linearly correlated to this prediction error. To control for instantaneous sensory processing, we also included the tone pitch value of p34 as a regression term. For both prediction and prediction error effects, significance at the single-electrode level was determined by a cluster-based permutation test that controlled for multiple comparisons across time. Specifically, for each participant, data were permuted 1000 times by randomly shuffling the trial label for the dependent variable (neural activity during p33 or p34). Temporal clusters were defined as contiguous time points where p < 0.05 for each time point and the t values were of the same sign. A temporal cluster was considered significant if its summary statistic (|∑t|, where t values had the same sign) was in the top 2.5th percentile of shuffled data, corresponding to a two-tailed test at p < 0.05. No upper or lower limits on the duration of a temporal cluster were used. Finally, p values resulting from linear regressions were corrected for multiple comparisons across electrodes for each subject by means of FDR52. This was done by entering the smallest p value for each electrode (if there were multiple significant time clusters) into the FDR correction, and the correction was carried out across all electrodes for each subject. To facilitate interpretation and presentation, results from both tone duration conditions were pooled and presented together by plotting the smallest p value (uncorrected) for each electrode across its significant time clusters from both tone-duration conditions.

We extracted the onset time of significant prediction or prediction error effects by extracting the onset time of the earliest significant temporal cluster within each electrode and averaging this time across significant electrodes within a tone duration condition. A control analysis whereby onset times were estimated by the earliest significant time point based on FDR correction across time for each electrode yielded similar results.

In both prediction and prediction error analyses, neural activity during a single tone (p33 or p34) was linearly correlated with an upcoming tone or pitch difference between tones across trials.

In a control analysis, we tested whether the signal components at the frequency of the tone sequences that were presented—5 Hz for the 200 ms tone sequence, and 2.5 Hz for the 400 ms tone sequence—carried any prediction or prediction error information. To do so, we filtered data using a third-order Butterworth bandpass filter to extract specific signal components for 2.5 Hz activity (2–3 Hz) and 5 Hz activity (4.5–5.5 Hz).

We also tested whether neural activity during the presentation of p34 was linearly correlated to the trialwise subject likelihood ratings. As with previous analyses, significance at single-electrode level was determined by cluster-based permutation test that controlled for multiple comparisons across time. FDR correction was carried out across all electrodes for each subject.

We note that for all the above analyses, although electrodes were pooled across subjects, statistical tests were performed at the level of single electrodes in individual subjects, and they thus capture within-subject variability.

To ensure that any residual sensory-evoked activity driven by the 32nd tone—if such activity lasted beyond the 32nd tone’s offset and lingered during the presentation of the 33rd tone—did not confound our prediction-related analysis, we performed the following control analysis.

For each electrode identified to contain significant prediction-related neural activity (large yellow spheres in Fig. 3B, C), we extracted its sensory-evoked activity driven by the 32nd tone from each trial, baseline-corrected using a 50-ms window prior to the onset of tone p32. We then tested whether this sensory-evoked activity correlated with the theoretically predicted upcoming final tone pitch (p34*). This analysis was carried out for each of the 13 significant prediction electrodes in Fig. 3B using low-frequency activity, and each of the four significant prediction electrodes in Fig. 3C using high-gamma amplitude.

Neural correlates of sensory history integration (SHI)

Any effective prediction of future sensory input requires the accumulation and integration of past sensory history information. This analysis is conceptually distinct from the computation of p34*, the mathematically optimal prediction of the final tone’s pitch. While p34* is derived deterministically from the entire tone sequence using the known 1/f autocovariance structure, the SHI analysis described here empirically estimates the integration window k′ from neural data, as well as the weights used in stimulus history integration, without assuming that the brain implements the precise AR model used for p34* or any specific AR model. To assess SHI, we analyzed how neural activity during the second half of a tone sequence depends on the pitch of preceding tones, following previously described methods20. We excluded p33 since it was always at 440 Hz and p34 since its pitch was determined independently of the preceding tone sequence. Analyses were performed separately for each TD condition, yielding 60 trials per analysis. Due to this analysis’s high computational demands, neural activity (low-frequency activity and high-gamma amplitude) was smoothed using non-overlapping 50-ms windows. In this framework, for each electrode and time window (w), the weights (βk+1) were independently estimated for different tone positions (i−k) relatively to the current tone (i). Thus, the model naturally captures whether more recent tones might be more strongly weighted. The resulting parameter k′, which is chosen empirically via cross-validation, indexes how many previous tones explained neural activity at the current electrode and time window. We tested 16 models with 1 to 16 regression terms, corresponding to the model explaining neural activity only as a function of the current tone (k′=0) or as a function of the current tone and k′ prior tones as well (1≤k′≤15).

To determine which model (i.e., which value of k′) best accounts for neural activity, a five-fold cross-validation approach was used. First, regression coefficients for each sensor, time window, and linear model were calculated based on the training set. The resulting regression coefficients were then used to calculate the current model’s prediction of neural activity in the test set. Model selection was based on the minimal sum of squared errors. The winning k′-value indicates how many previous tones in a sequence best explain the recorded neural activity. After computing k′ for all folds, we averaged the k′-values across folds for each electrode and time window. To increase the stability of k′ and minimize the influence of random trial-to-fold allocation on the results, we computed k′ across five iterations, each with separate random trial-to-fold allocation. Finally, we averaged k′ across all iterations to obtain the final k′ value.

Statistical significance of electrode-wise SHI effects was assessed via a nonparametric permutation test comparing k′ against a null distribution obtained by shuffling the tone order within each sequence to disrupt the temporal dependence between neural activity and tone sequence. To this end, we repeated the above-mentioned cross-validated regression analyses, but with randomly permuted tone order within each sequence in the training set, while leaving tone order in the test set intact. The order of the first 32 tones in the training set was shuffled, with the exception that the value of the current (i-th) tone pitch was kept the same as in the original sequence. Thus, information about current tone pitch was retained, whereas information about tone sequence history was disrupted. This procedure was repeated 1000 times for each dataset (i.e., for each of the five folds, separately for each TD condition and each participant). Tone order was shuffled anew for every test/training fold combination. Identical to the computation of k′-values, the same five-fold cross-validation technique was applied to extract the optimal k′-value for shuffled data (kshuff′) for each sensor and time window in each TD-specific dataset for each participant. This yielded a shuffled null distribution of 5000 k’-values for each electrode, time window, TD condition, and participant. kshuff′-values were subsequently averaged across folds to yield 1000 shuffled values per subject. To account for the averaging across iterations for the experimental k′-values, kshuff′-values were likewise averaged across five iterations, each randomly selected from the 1000 shuffled values.

For each participant, we assessed the statistical significance of k′-values for each electrode and time window by comparing the experimental k′-values against the shuffled null distribution of the corresponding electrode and time window. We computed the percentage of kshuff′-values that were equal or bigger than the respective experimental k′-value, resulting in a p value for each electrode and time window (ponding to a one-sided test). Resulting p values were corrected for multiple comparisons across electrodes for each participant by means of FDR. To allow a comparison of k′-values across tone duration conditions, k′ and kshuff′-values were additionally averaged across the first four time windows (0–200 ms). Comparison of k′-values across tone duration conditions was assessed by computing the ratio k200ms′/k400ms′ for each electrode that showed significant (FDR-corrected) effects in one of the two tone duration conditions.

As our stimuli contain only 1/f spectral statistics (a second-order structure), linear AR models are sufficient and optimal for capturing both p34* and SHI, as nonlinear mechanisms cannot extract additional predictive information12,19.

As with our previous analyses, although electrodes are pooled across subjects, we note that statistical tests were performed at the level of single electrodes in individual subjects, and they thus capture within-subject variability.

Directed connectivity analysis with Granger causality

To assess directed connectivity between brain regions over the course of tone sequence processing, we computed pairwise Granger causality in the time domain using the MVGC toolbox53. Specifically, we computed pairwise Granger causality between electrodes showing prediction and prediction error effects across different anatomical regions (see Fig. 6A for an analysis schematic).

First, we selected all electrodes that showed a significant (p < 0.05, uncorrected) prediction (P) or prediction error (PE) effect in either tone duration condition and in either the low-frequency (0.5–30 Hz) activity or high-gamma (70–150 Hz) amplitude. Next, we grouped these electrodes based on their anatomical location (frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes). We then defined electrode pairs for each combination of effects (i.e., P→P, PE→PE, P→PE, and PE→P) across all combinations of anatomical regions (i.e., frontal-parietal, frontal-temporal, and parietal-temporal) within each participant. The number of electrodes per configuration is listed in Supplementary Table 2.

To compute Granger Causality for each pathway (e.g., “frontal→temporal, P→P” indicates GC from prediction electrodes in the frontal lobe to prediction electrodes in the temporal lobe), we used baseline-corrected neural activity in the broadband (0.5–150 Hz) range. Since stationarity is a prerequisite for Granger causality analysis, we ensured stationarity of neural data by performing the following preprocessing steps for each trial and electrode (similar to ref. 6): (1) linear detrending of neural data, (2) temporal normalization (i.e., subtracting the across-sample mean from each sample, then dividing by the across-sample s.d.), and (3) ensemble normalization (i.e., subtracting the sample-wise across-trial average from each sample, then dividing each sample by the sample-wise across-trial s.d.).

We selected the model order for Granger Causality by first obtaining the optimal model order using the full model (Bayesian Information Criteria; maximal model order = 50) for each electrode pair and tone epoch. From these models, we chose the median model order pooled across all pairs of electrodes, resulting in a selected model order of 7–10 (mean: 8.75, SEM: 0.21). Next, we computed pairwise Granger causality estimates in both directions for each electrode pair and tone epoch (i.e., tone 1 to 34), separately for each tone duration condition. The resulting tone-specific pairwise Granger Causality estimates were then analyzed to determine changes in directed influence over the course of tone sequence processing.

First, we investigated whether Granger causality follows a linear trend (i.e., increasing or decreasing) over the course of the sequence. We selected two subsets of evenly spaced tones covering the entire length of the tone sequence (p33 and p34 were excluded since they followed unique properties). Due to computational constraints, we chose a gap of 5 tones, resulting in two subsets of tones, with the first subset including the 1st, 6th, 11th, 16th, 21st, 26th, and 31st tones. The second subset included the 2nd, 7th, 12th, 17th, 22nd, 27th, and 32nd tones. For each tone of these subsets, we computed Granger causality for each electrode pair. This analysis was done for both tone duration conditions and both subsets of tones, resulting in four sets of GC values. GC values were then averaged across these four sets. Next, we fitted a linear trend across the seven tones in the averaged sequence and evaluated the resulting slope estimates via a two-sided t-test against 0 across electrode pairs for each pathway investigated (e.g., “frontal→temporal, P→P”).

Second, we investigated Granger causality changes for the final two tones, p33 and p34, which had unique properties compared to the preceding sequence. To this end, we compared Granger causality estimates for p33 or p34 against the mean GC value across the three preceding tones (i.e., the mean across p30, p31, and p32) via a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test across electrode pairs for each pathway investigated. We reported as significant any effects that showed an effect in the same direction and were significant across both tone-duration conditions.

For both the analysis of the linear trend of GC values, as well as the comparison of GC values for the final tones with immediately preceding tones, we also grouped electrodes by anatomical regions of the auditory hierarchy, by selecting regions of interest based on Brodmann’s areas (BAs). Namely, we identified electrodes in the following regions: auditory cortex (BA 41 and 42), dorsal inferior frontal cortex (IFC ventral, BA 44), ventral inferior frontal cortex (IFC ventral, BA 45), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC, BA 8), inferior parietal lobule (BA 40), premotor cortex (PMC, BA 6), and superior temporal sulcus (STS, BA 22). For each auditory region and effect-combination (e.g., IFC ventral → PMC, P→P), GC results were pooled across all electrode pairs. All significant connections between auditory regions were plotted in Fig. S7 unless they showed opposite direction of changes for the two tone duration conditions or for the different effect-combinations.

To further test whether the changes in GC from parietal to frontal cortex at the end of the sequence reflected predictive processing rather than general attentional effects, we examined GC strength at p34 as a function of participants’ trial-by-trial FTPL ratings. To do so, for each connectivity type (P → P, P → PE, PE → PE, PE → P), we re-computed GC values for trials with FTPL ratings below or equal to the median FTPL rating (defined separately for each subject) and for trials with FTPL ratings above the median FTPL rating. We then subtracted GC values for low-FTPL trials from GC values for high-FTPL trials for all electrode pairs from parietal to frontal areas across subjects, and tested whether this difference score was significantly different from zero via a t-test.
