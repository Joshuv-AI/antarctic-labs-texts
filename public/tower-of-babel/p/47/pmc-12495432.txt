Immunotherapy-associated capillary leak syndrome in endometrial cancer: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Katherine Lambert, Quinn Kistenfeger, Julia Chalif, Jason M Prosek, Floor Backes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Capillary leak syndrome is a rare immune-therapy adverse related event.•It presents with hypervolemia, anasarca, vasculitis, pulmonary edema, fever of unknown origin, hypoalbuminemia, and hyponatremia.•Multidisciplinary evaluation allowed for thorough evaluation and exclusion of all other possible diagnoses.•Prompt recognition is vital to starting proper therapy, such as steroids with escalation to IVIG for an inadequate response.Keywords: Capillary leak syndrome, Pembrolizumab, Endometrial cancer, Immunotherapy, Immune-related adverse events

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has revolutionized the treatment of endometrial cancer. Pembrolizumab is a programmed cell death (PD-1) receptor inhibitor that is a highly effective treatment for endometrial cancers that are microsatellite instability high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) (Mechanism of Action of KEYTRUDA® (pembrolizumab) | Health Care Professionals. Accessed May 11, 2025). Studies have shown a robust objective response to immunotherapy as well as significant increases in progression free survival up to 13.1 months (Eskander et al., 2023, Marabelle et al., 2020, O’Malley et al., 2022). Although these therapies are effective, ICIs are associated with a range of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) (Martins et al., 2019, Yin et al., 2023). The overall incidence of irAEs is estimated to be approximately 66 % (Wang et al., 2019). In patients with endometrial cancer treated with pembrolizumab, the incidence rises to approximately 76 % (O’Malley et al., 2022). While providers are familiar with common adverse events such as pneumonitis and colitis, there are rare forms of irAEs for which clinical knowledge is limited. This review describes a case of capillary leak syndrome (CLS) that developed during maintenance pembrolizumab therapy for recurrent endometrial cancer—marking the first reported instance of CLS in a gynecologic cancer setting. Diagnostic approaches used to distinguish CLS from other conditions with similar presentations will be discussed. Given its rarity, however, there is limited data to guide the therapeutic management of CLS. Here, we summarize our diagnostic and therapeutic approach for treating CLS, including short term stabilization as well as long term maintenance. This article also provides a comprehensive overview of immune-checkpoint inhibitors, immune-related adverse events, the pathophysiology of CLS, and the relation of CLS to immune-checkpoint inhibitors.

Case presentation

Our patient is a 65-year-old female with recurrent mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) FIGO Grade 2 endometrioid adenocarcinoma on pembrolizumab maintenance therapy. She initially underwent a robotic-assisted hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and sentinel lymph node dissection demonstrating Stage IA disease. She re-presented one year later with a large vaginal mass with biopsy proven endometrioid carcinoma consistent with recurrent endometrial cancer with additional metastatic disease to her abdomen and pelvis, and axillary lymphadenopathy. Prior to starting treatment, baseline labs were obtained and were within normal limits, notably with a serum sodium (Na) of 135 mmol/L and an albumin of 4.4 g/dL. She subsequently completed 8 cycles of carboplatin/ paclitaxel/ pembrolizumab (AUC 5, 175 mg/m2, 200 mg every 3 weeks). She had a complete response to treatment and was transitioned to maintenance ICI. Following cycle 2 of maintenance pembrolizumab (after 10 total cycles of pembrolizumab), she developed new shortness of breath with cough and was diagnosed with IO-induced pneumonitis. She was treated with a high dose oral steroids and tapered over four weeks. Around the same time, she also developed a rash on her lower legs consisting of generalized erythema and swelling. Initially, this was also thought to vasculitis be due to ICI therapy. Over the course of her prednisone treatment and taper, the patient reported resolution of her rash, shortness of breath, and cough. She resumed pembrolizumab for 2 cycles. Shortly after, she was then admitted to an outside hospital with daily fevers of unknown origin and had an extensive inpatient workup that was all negative. A few days later, she represented with worsening generalized fatigue, lower extremity vasculitis changes, anasarca (weight gain of 20 lb), daily fevers, as well as hyponatremia. The initial differential was broad, and included several ICI-related toxicities that could cause her constellation of symptoms, including hypophysitis, thyroiditis, and adrenal insufficiency, rheumatologic diseases, as well as other non-IO causes of hyponatremia, fever, and fluid overload, such as systemic infection. The patient underwent an extensive workup conducted by the gynecologic oncology team with the support of our nephrology, rheumatology, infectious disease and endocrinology colleagues.

On admission to the hospital, she had multiple lab abnormalities including hyponatremia (125 mmol/L), hypoalbuminemia (2.0 g/dL), a leukocytosis (white blood cell count 16.8 K/uL), TSH 14 uIU/mL (suspected due to decreased absorption of levothyroxine), and a mild transaminitis. She additionally received bilateral lower extremity dopplers on admission that were negative for deep vein thrombosis. A transthoracic echocardiogram was performed no abnormalities with an ejection fraction of 60–65 %. She was tested for adrenal insufficiency with labs and an MRI of her pituitary gland, which were all negative. Despite maximal treatment by nephrology her hyponatremia, her sodium continued to drop to a nadir of 123 mmol/L. She was diagnosed with hypervolemic hyponatremia with poor renal perfusion as well as persistent hypotension, not responsive to fluid restriction, diuretics, salt tables and other measures. She developed intermittent mental status changes throughout this time that were responsive to reorientation measures.

The patient underwent an infectious disease workup, including blood cultures, urine cultures, a respiratory viral panel, and repeat chest imaging. Her urine culture grew 10–100 K colonies of E. Coli, and she was treated for a with ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID for 3 days. Her blood cultures, respiratory viral panel, and chest imaging was all negative. However, she continued to have fevers following treatment for her UTI. Her infectious workup was repeated with another set of blood and urine cultures, a respiratory viral panel, and chest imaging, which were all negative. She received a broad rheumatologic workup as well, which was significant for a positive anti-RNP antibody and an elevated C-reactive protein. Otherwise, she had a normal creatinine kinase, urine protein to creatinine ratio, and estimated sedimentation rate, and her workup was negative for all other antibodies.

After the above workup ruled out every diagnosis in the differential, immunotherapy mediated CLS was ultimately diagnosed by a process of exclusion. On hospital day 7, she was started on methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg per day (100 mg IV daily). After 3 days of steroids, the patient lacked a significant clinical response. Her sodium and albumin at this time were 126 and 2.3 respectively. On hospital day 10, IVIG was added (1 mg/kg × 5 days) after which she quickly started showing signs of improvement with resolution of fever and hypotension, good diuresis, and sodium and albumin ultimately improved to 134 mmol/L and 2.5 g/dL respectively by the day of discharge (hospital day 15). Discussion

Immune checkpoint inhibitors and endometrial cancer

Immune checkpoint inhibitors, particularly those targeting PD-1 and PD-L1, have shown efficacy in the treatment of endometrial cancer in both the recurrent and upfront setting (Eskander et al., 2023, Mirza et al., 2023, Westin et al., 2024, Gorp et al., 2024). Initially, candidacy for these therapies was based on tumor molecular profiling of mismatch repair (MMR) proteins and tumor mutational burden (TMB). Although tumor profiles remain a key factor in guiding treatment, recent studies in this rapidly evolving therapeutic landscape are expanding the number of patients eligible for ICI therapy (Eskander et al., 2023, Mirza et al., 2023).

Immune-related adverse events

Although immunotherapy has revolutionized the treatment landscape for metastatic and recurrent endometrial cancer it is not without risks. One major challenge associated with immunotherapy is the potential for irAEs which arise when the activated immune system begins to attack healthy tissues. These toxicities can impact multiple organ systems, such as the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, endocrine glands, and lungs, with severity ranging from mild to severe, and in some cases, life-threatening (Sullivan and Weber, 2022).

Immunotherapy is commonly used in combination with systemic chemotherapy for the treatment of gynecologic cancers. Studies have found that immunotherapy-based combination regimens have greater observed toxicities than immunotherapy monotherapy (Shoushtari et al., 2018, Wang et al., 2018). Combining PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors has been shown in studies to result in significantly higher rates of irAEs compared to PD-1 monotherapy, with high-grade irAEs occurring in approximately 60 % of patients on combination therapy versus around 20 % in those treated with PD-1 agents alone (Shoushtari et al., 2018). Preliminary data from a multi-institutional retrospective review assessing irAEs in women with gynecologic cancers receiving ICIs demonstrated 42 % of patients experienced an irAE. In this study the median time to first toxicity was 42 days. Notably of the patients who experienced toxicities, 50 % were classified as grade 1. The gastrointestinal tract was the most frequently affected, with colitis and hepatitis being the most common toxicities observed (Acker et al., 2021).

Both NCCN and ESMO provide clinical practice guidelines for the diagnosis and management of irAEs (Haanen et al., 2022, Thompson et al., 2024). irAEs are graded at the time of diagnosis using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), developed by the National Cancer Institute (Criteria, 2025). This standardized system assigns grades from 1 to 5 based on the severity of symptoms. When diagnosed promptly, most irAEs are fully reversible with the use of glucocorticoids providing temporary immunosuppression. For moderate irAEs or above (Grade 2 or above), NCCN recommends discontinuation of immunotherapy and initiation of prednisone or IV methylprednisolone at a dose of 1–2 mg/kg/day (Thompson et al., 2024). In many cases response to steroids is typically seen within 48–72 h. In more severe or steroid-refractory cases, second-line immunomodulators such as infliximab, a TNF-α inhibitor, can be used (Friedman et al., 2016, Ruf et al., 2024). Management of refractory irAEs is often individualized based on the affected organ system, and consultation with a specialist is typically recommended.

Interestingly, certain immunotherapy-mediated irAEs have been linked to improved treatment response and survival outcomes (Wang et al., 2019, Kostine et al., 2018, Cook et al., 2024). A retrospective study of patients receiving immunotherapy for advanced malignancies found that those who developed ICI-induced colitis had improved survival outcomes. Notably, diarrhea emerged as an independent predictor of better survival, regardless of the need for treatment intervention (Wang et al., 2018). This phenomenon has been observed in metastatic non-small cell lung cancer, where the development of irAEs has been linked to a 14-month improvement in median overall survival (Cook et al., 2024). While the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, it is thought that patients who develop irAEs are experiencing a heightened immune response. As such, the same immune activation that leads to inflammation in healthy tissues may also reflect effective immune recognition and targeting of tumor cells. The patient in this case developed a rapid complete response to treatment with chemotherapy/ICI combination and had evidence of pneumonitis and possibly mild vasculitis prior to the onset of capillary leak syndrome.

Capillary leak syndrome

Capillary leak syndrome (CLS) is a rare condition in which protein-rich fluid from the blood vessels leaks into the surrounding interstitial space. Endothelial cells of the vasculature are typically bound by adherens junctions (AJ) and tight junctions, rendering low permeability of vessels to protein-rich fluid in a healthy state (Siddall et al., 2017). When insulted by inflammatory stimuli, vascular endothelial cadherin in the AJs is disrupted, which compromises the integrity of the AJ leading to fluid extravasation (Siddall et al., 2017). This proposed mechanism of CLS leads to its characteristic presentation of diffuse pitting edema, fatigue, and flu-like symptoms. The relative lack of intravascular volume can manifest as hypotension, decreased organ profusion and shock with associated lab abnormalities, including hypoalbuminemia, hemoconcentration, AKI, and hyponatremia (Siddall et al., 2017, Druey and Parikh, 2017). Fluid extravasation may also present in other potential spaces leading to pleural and pericardial effusions and compartment syndrome (Druey and Parikh, 2017). CLS can be idiopathic or precipitated by medication use, autoimmune disease, cytokine release syndrome, and infection (Siddall et al., 2017). Drug induced CLS caused by several anti-neoplastic therapies has been described in the literature. CLS has been attributed to multiple classes of chemotherapy (most frequently pyrimidine analogues), immunostimulants, and monoclonal antibodies (Izzedine et al., 2022). In a review by Izzedine et al, rituximab has the highest number of reported cases of systemic CLS among monoclonal antibodies (nineteen cases), followed by bevacizumab (eleven cases) and trastuzumab (eleven cases) (Izzedine et al., 2022). At the time of this report, only three cases of pembrolizumab induced CLS have been reported in the literature, all in patients with squamous cell carcinoma (Izzedine et al., 2022, Qin et al., 2021, Itzam Marin et al., 2023, Ni et al., 2023). To our knowledge, this is the first case reported in gynecologic cancer.

Treatment of CLS is dichotomous, requiring acute resuscitation of intravascular hypovolemia and attempt at treatment of the underlying condition to inhibit further extravasation of fluid into the interstitial space. In cases of mild hypotension without shock, acute treatment with IV crystalloid administration, with or without diuretics to manage systemic volume overload, has been successful (Siddall et al., 2017, Druey and Parikh, 2017). While albumin infusion is an attractive choice for resuscitation in attempt to restore intravascular protein rich fluid and restore oncotic pressure, it has not been found to be superior to crystalloids due to the propensity to continue to leak albumin from the vessels. In severe cases with associated hypovolemic shock, pressor support and renal replacement therapy may be required (Siddall et al., 2017).

While vascular leak is often self-limited in idiopathic cases of CLS, treatment of provoked CLS focuses on decreasing inflammation likely through reducing cytokine expression, though reports of treatment are with varying success (Siddall et al., 2017). Attempted regimens include use of theophylline, IV immunoglobulins (IVIG), corticosteroids, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and anti-VEGF antibody (Anipindi et al., 2023). In case reports of immune check point inhibitor related CLS, patients were treated with supportive care, steroid taper with or without 0.4–2 g/kg IVIG infusions daily for 4–5 days with clinical improvement (Itzam Marin et al., 2023, Ni et al., 2023, Raja et al., 2024). Following treatment for the acute phase of CLS, prophylactic treatment with IVIG 0.4–2 g/kg monthly for 4–5 months has demonstrated success (Siddall et al., 2017, Druey and Parikh, 2017).

Conclusion/case resolution

Our patient was treated with seven days of IV methylprednisolone 1 mg/kg, five days of IVIG 1 mg/kg, and IV Lasix inpatient with an improvement in her symptoms and hyponatremia. She was transitioned to PO steroids tapered over the course of 4 weeks along with daily pantoprazole for peptic ulcer prevention and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, PJP, prophylaxis) throughout the tapering period. She has had no return of symptoms. She lost 40 lb, mostly in fluid weight since treatment of CLS. Her most recent CT scans demonstrated no evidence of disease progression in the abdomen or pelvis, which has been sustained since discontinuing maintenance therapy. There is currently no plan to restart this patient on pembrolizumab.

In retrospect, it is likely that this patient had CLS throughout the course of her treatment with pembrolizumab. When diagnosed with IO-pneumonitis and vasculitis after the initial two rounds of pembrolizumab, she had hyponatremia and hypoalbuminemia at that time as well. All her symptoms and lab abnormalities at that time improved with the first round of steroids. Unfortunately, this can be difficult to interpret in the setting of other comorbidities such as obesity and chronic venostasis. Additionally, signs and symptoms of pulmonary edema as a pulmonary manifestation of CLS and pneumonitis are difficult to distinguish. However, the addition of hyponatremia and hypoalbuminemia to the above symptoms, and their resolution with steroids, make CLS the more likely diagnosis.

The decision to delay starting steroids until hospital day 7 in this patient was multifactorial. Her constellation of symptoms was broad, and knowledge of the presentation of CLS at the beginning of this patient’s hospitalization was limited, Furthermore, the most common etiologies of the patient’s symptoms needed to be ruled out to come to the true diagnosis of CLS. Lastly, there was an ongoing concern for underlying infection. It is possible that this delay in treatment prolonged the duration of CLS in this patient.

This case highlights a rare instance of capillary leak syndrome (CLS) in a patient receiving pembrolizumab maintenance therapy for recurrent endometrial cancer. As immunotherapy becomes more widely used in gynecologic oncology, awareness of uncommon but potentially serious immune-related adverse events like CLS is essential. While it remains a diagnosis of exclusion, the combination of hypoalbuminemia and hypervolemic hyponatremia with symptoms such as vasculitis, pulmonary edema, anasarca, and fever of unknown origin should raise concern for immunotherapy-induced CLS. Prompt recognition, multidisciplinary evaluation, and tailored management are critical to improving outcomes and guiding future clinical decision-making in similar scenarios.
