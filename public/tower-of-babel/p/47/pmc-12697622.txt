Consensus Statement on Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization in Chronic Subdural Hematoma Treatment: A Guideline from the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards Committee
Authors: Farhan Siddiq, Muhammad Shakir, Thanh N. Nguyen, Ameer E. Hassan, Mohamad Abdalkader, Cynthia L. Kenmuir, David S. Liebeskind, Robin Novakovic, Shahram Majidi, Sunil A. Sheth, Mohammad El‐Ghanem, Santiago Ortega‐Gutierrez, Waldo R. Guerrero, Amer M. Malik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDEmerging evidence from observational studies, meta‐analyses, and clinical trials has reshaped the treatment landscape of chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH). This guideline provides updated recommendations on diagnosis, patient selection, and medical, surgical, and endovascular management.METHODSThe Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards committee formed a writing group of interdisciplinary experts to review current literature. Recommendations were based on clinical trial data, observational studies, and expert consensus, following the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Class of Recommendation and Level of Evidence framework.RESULTSEMBOLISE (Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery With ONYX Liquid Embolic System for Subacute and Chronic Subdural Hematoma), STEM (SQUID Trial for the Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery for Treatment of Chronic Subdural Hematoma), MAGIC‐MT (Managing Non‐acute Subdural Hematoma Using Liquid Materials: A Chinese Randomized Trial of MMA Treatment), and MEMBRANE (Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization for the Treatment of Subdural Hematomas With TRUFILL n‐butyl cyanoacrylate) trials highlight the efficacy and safety of middle meningeal artery embolization (MMAE) in CSDH. EMBOLISE (n = 400; MMAE with surgery, n = 197; surgery alone, n = 203) showed that MMAE plus surgery reduced 90‐day hematoma recurrence to 4.1% versus 11.3% with surgery alone (relative risk = 0.36; 95% CI = 0.11–0.80; P = 0.008). Functional deterioration was 11.9% versus 9.8%, and 90‐day mortality was 5.1% versus 3.0%. Serious embolization‐related events, including 2 disabling strokes, occurred in 2.0% by 30 days. The STEM trial (310 patients enrolled: MMAE plus standard treatment, n = 149; standard treatment alone, n = 161) found that MMAE with Squid significantly reduced 180‐day recurrent/residual hematoma or reoperation/rescue surgery to 16% versus 36% with standard treatment (odds ratio = 0.36; 95% CI = 0.20–0.66; P = 0.001). 30‐day mortality or major disabling stroke was 3% in both groups. However, 180‐day mortality was 8% with the MMAE group, and 5% with standard treatment, and no death was attributed to MMAE or embolic agent. MAGIC‐MT (722 patients; MMAE plus standard treatment, n = 360; standard treatment alone, n = 362) reported 90‐day symptomatic recurrence or progression in 6.7% of the MMAE group (using Onyx) versus 9.9% in the standard treatment group (difference: −3.3%; 95% CI: −7.4–0.8; P = 0.10). Serious adverse events were less common in the MMAE group (6.7% versus 11.6%; P = 0.02). The MEMBRANE trial (376 patients; MMAE plus standard treatment, n = 188; standard treatment alone, n = 188) found that MMAE using n‐butyl cyanoacrylate plus surgery improved outcomes at 6 months.CONCLUSIONSThese results suggest that MMAE plus standard treatment of CSDH shows promising results and may become a standard of care for managing CSDH in selected patients. These guidelines provide evidence‐based recommendations for the diagnosis, patient selection, and treatment of CSDH.

Chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is a collection of different ages of blood clots between the dura and arachnoid mater. It is associated with high recurrence and mortality rates. 1 , 2 The incidence of CSDH ranges from 1.72 to 20.6 per 100 000 individuals and mainly affects elderly (≥65 years). 3 Between 1990 and 2015, the incidence in adults over 80 years increased from 46.9 to 129.5 per 100 000. 4 A study in Switzerland reported a hospital admission rate of 8.2/100 000 due to CSDH. 5 In the Veterans Affairs population, the predicted incidence is 121.4 per 100 000. In the general US population, it is expected to reach 17.1 per 100 000 by 2030. 6 This increase is attributed to the use of antithrombotic medications, an aging population, the risk for multiple falls and head trauma. 7 The progressive increase in incidence and CSDH recurrence rates predict that CSDH may become the most common neurosurgical condition in the United States by 2030. 6 Although it is common in the elderly, it has also been reported in younger patients (ie, <50 years). In these cases, it is associated with conditions such as coagulation disorders, arachnoid cysts, ventriculoperitoneal shunts, and head trauma. 8

The standard treatment for symptomatic CSDH includes medical management or surgical evacuation, depending on the severity. However, a significant complication of surgery is hematoma recurrence, which occurs in 0% to 76% of cases, with reoperation of 10% to 20%. 9 Recurrent CSDH is characterized by reaccumulation or increased hematoma volume on the operated side, often with neurological symptoms, usually seen within 1 to 3 months after surgery. 10 , 11 There are no definitive criteria for recurrence and various criteria differ across studies. 12 Some studies suggest that the median time to recurrence after surgical evacuation is 12 to 15 days. 13 , 14 Factors such as bilateral hematomas, preoperative anticoagulant use, thick membranes, and larger hematoma volumes are associated with a higher likelihood of recurrence. 9 , 15 Recurrence and the need for reoperation can lead to significant complications, especially in older patients who may already have other health issues. Additionally, CSDH imposes a substantial financial burden on the healthcare system and society. Analysis of the National Inpatient Sample found median hospitalization costs of $35 366 for surgical patients and $20 341 for nonsurgical. 16 By 2030, annual CSDH hospitalizations are projected to reach 60 000. 6 As results, direct healthcare costs are expected to ∼$2 billion per year. 17

The rising incidence, high recurrence rate, and substantial financial burden of CSDH highlight the urgent need for standardized alternative management practices. Middle meningeal artery embolization (MMAE) was first identified in 2002 as a potential therapeutic approach, as the MMA was considered the feeding artery of the outer membrane of the hematoma cavity. 18 MMAE has since gained traction as a promising option as primary or adjunct treatment to surgical evacuation, with encouraging clinical outcomes. 19 Several recent multicenter studies have used randomized clinical trial designs. These studies now provide level one evidence that supports the development of comprehensive guidelines to better define its indications, safety profile, efficacy, and long‐term outcomes.

These guidelines aim to provide a structured diagnosis, treatment, and follow‐up framework to improve patient outcomes and reduce the burden on healthcare systems. By addressing existing gaps in management and incorporating emerging treatment modalities, these guidelines strive to inform the current level of evidence (LOE) and related recommendations for patient management.

METHODS

All data and supporting materials have been provided with the published article. The authors declare that all supporting data are available in the article (and its online Supplementary files).

These guidelines follow the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Guidelines and Practice Standards (GAPS) evidence‐based framework for guideline development. 20 The Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology GAPS committee formed a multidisciplinary writing group of neurologists, neurosurgeons, and interventional radiologists to review and evaluate the latest literature on CSDH systematically. The panel comprised content, methodology experts, and the GAPS committee chair. The authors’ disclosures are listed in Table S1. A population (P), intervention (I), comparators (C), outcomes (O), timing (T), and setting (S) question was formulated to direct the literature search, as outlined in Table 1. A comprehensive systematic literature review was performed across multiple databases including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Clinicaltrials.gov from inception until November 2024. The search strategy used terms (“chronic subdural hematoma” OR “CSDH” OR “cSDH” OR “chronic SDH”) AND (“CSDH management” OR “middle meningeal artery embolization” OR “MMA embolization” OR “MMAE” OR “endovascular treatment of CSDH” OR “burr hole surgery” OR “burr hole craniotomy” OR “twist drill craniotomy” OR “craniotomy” OR “craniostomy” OR “surgical evacuation of CSDH” OR “conservative management” OR “medical management of CSDH” OR “atorvastatin” OR “dexamethasone” OR “tranexamic acid” OR “bevacizumab” OR “antithrombotic treatment” OR “anticoagulant use in CSDH”). The literature review identified articles, which were then screened for relevance. Studies were categorized by study design, prioritizing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta‐analyses, followed by cohort studies, case–control studies, and expert opinions. Initial drafts of the guidelines were developed based on the synthesized evidence. Recommendations were developed by the writing group following the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology GAPS framework using the class of recommendation (COR) and LOE (Figure 1). 20 Consensus on all recommendations was achieved through a modified Delphi process, with each recommendation reviewed, revised, and refined by the entire writing group before submission to the GAPS committee and the Society of Vascular and Interventional Neurology Board of Directors for final approval.

Pathophysiology and Rationale for MMAE

The dural border cell layer is a thin layer of cells between the dura mater and arachnoid mater and is considered the site of origin of CSDH. This thin layer is more susceptible to separation due to the presence of less extracellular collagen and intercellular connections. 9

Classically, trauma is considered the pathophysiology of CSDH, but recent angiographic and histopathological studies have proposed inflammation and angiogenesis as the key mediators in the pathogenesis of CSDH. Due to trauma, prior subdural hemorrahge or other factors, disruption of the dural border cell layer triggers a foreign body response that leads to inflammation, granulation tissue formation, and macrophage deposition. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Angiogenesis creates a neomembrane with fragile, immature capillaries that easily rupture, sustaining and expanding the CSDH. 21 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

The membrane in CSDH is divided into internal and external membranes. Internal membranes contain collagen and fibroblast, which makes them comparatively nonfunctional, and the external membranes are derived from the dura mater and are considered the source of repeated bleeding in CSDH. 29 The external membrane of CSDH is categorized into 4 histological types based on the degree of inflammation and membrane maturity. Type 1, the noninflammatory subtype, is characterized by immature fibroblasts and collagen fibers with minimal cellular infiltration and neovascularization. Type 2 features significant inflammatory cell infiltration and new blood vessel formation. Type 3, the hemorrhagic‐inflammatory subtype, includes multiple layers with marked inflammation, numerous new vessels around the hematoma cavity, and associated hemorrhage. Type 4, the scar‐inflammatory subtype, is distinguished by inflammatory cell infiltration, neovascularization, and hemorrhage within a cicatricial outer membrane. 30 , 31 Recent histological studies confirmed that the new blood vessels that are the source of bleeding in CSDH are the distal branches of the MMA. These small branches of MMA penetrate through the dura mater and supply the new vessels in the outer subdural membranes. 29 , 32 Therefore, it is hypothesized that MMAE stops the blood supply to the neovasculature of subdural membranes and prevents recurrent bleeding and hematoma volume expansion. 15

Panel Consensus

To advance our understanding of CSDH pathophysiology, the panel supports establishing multicenter tissue banks and biorepositories for CSDH samples, including dura, membranes, hematoma fluid, and peripheral blood. Collecting these samples from a diverse patient cohort, including those with de novo and recurrent CSDH following failed surgery or MMAE, will enable the correlation of biological and clinical data. Systematic analysis of changes in dura and membrane tissues will improve our understanding of CSDH formation and recurrence, potentially leading to identifying new therapeutic targets and developing more effective pharmacological strategies. Clinical Presentation of CSDH

CSDH presents with a broad spectrum of signs and symptoms that can range from mild to severe. The clinical presentation of CSDH can range from asymptomatic to loss of consciousness. Patients can have headaches, cognitive decline, confusion, numbness, aphasia, seizures, weakness, and severely reduced Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). 3 , 6 , 33 Studies have shown that headaches and confusion are the most common symptoms, with 50%–70% of elderly patients presenting with altered mental status. 1 , 7 The majority of patients in the EMBOLISE (Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery With ONYX Liquid Embolic System for Subacute and Chronic Subdural Hematoma), 34 STEM (SQUID Trial for the Embolization of the Middle Meningeal Artery for Treatment of Chronic Subdural Hematoma), 35 and MAGIC‐MT (Managing Non‐acute Subdural Hematoma Using Liquid Materials: A Chinese Randomized Trial of MMA Treatment) 36 RCTs presented with symptoms such as headaches, gait instability, cognitive impairment, and contralateral limb weakness. Blaauw et al 37 reported that focal neurological deficits (46%), headache (41%), gait disturbance (31%), and cognitive impairment (31%) were the most common symptoms. They found that cognitive decline and decreased consciousness were associated with poor short‐term functional outcomes, whereas headache was associated with more favorable functional outcomes. Clinical presentation is also found to differ between age groups. A multicenter, population‐based study found that younger patients <50 years of age present with signs of raised intracranial pressure (eg, headache, nausea, and vomiting), whereas neurological deficits were predominant in patients ≥50 years old. 8 Factors that determine the severity of symptoms include hematoma volume and expansion, location, and mass effect due to hematoma. 33

The natural course of CSDH unfolds through a few stages. It starts with a subdural hygroma, where cerebrospinal fluid leaks into the subdural space, potentially triggering inflammation that builds up a membrane around it. It is difficult to predict which hygromas will progress to CSDH; ∼25% progress to CSDH. 38 The next phase is the homogeneous stage, where outer and inner membranes develop. There is ongoing recurrent bleeding events which could be from minor trauma or leaky vascualture. In a subtype of this stage, called the laminar stage, there is increased vascularity, leading to higher recurrence rates. This is followed by the separated stage, where the hematoma splits into layers, expanding in volume and exerting pressure on the brain, making it prone to further rebleeding. The gradation stage lies between the homogeneous and separated stages, where mild head movement can mix the hematoma layers. Finally, the trabecular stage marks the hematoma's transition toward resolution, with fibrous septa forming and a gradual reduction in volume, lowering the risk of bleeding. At this stage, symptoms usually stem more from the bulk of the CSDH compressing the brain rather than active bleeding. 39

For patients with profound neurological symptoms from CSDH, urgent surgery is the standard of care to prevent neurological deterioration. 40 Whereas ∼40% of hematomas resolve spontaneously, ∼20% of those managed conservatively eventually need surgery. 41 Evidence to guide treatment for nonemergent cases is limited, with no standardized criteria based on clinical presentation or imaging for deciding on surgery in stable patients. Outcomes can be poor for older patients, with high perioperative mortality (11%) and recurrence (5% and 30%). 42 Observation is often preferred in asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients, especially those at high surgical risk due to comorbidities.

Panel Consensus

The panel recommends adopting a tailored clinical diagnostic approach for CSDH, acknowledging its diverse symptom spectrum. Given the variation in presentation by age, headaches and confusion in younger patients versus neurological deficits in older patients, the panel supports the use of imaging and standardized assessment tools. Early surgical intervention should be considered for those with significant neurological impairment, as it can prevent further deterioration and improve outcomes. Ongoing research is needed to refine clinical diagnostic criteria and strategies to improve patient outcomes. Diagnosis of CSDH

The diagnosis of CSDH begins with a thorough clinical evaluation. A detailed medical history is important to identify potential risk factors, including previous head trauma, use of antithrombotic medications, alcohol abuse, and preexisting neurological conditions. A comprehensive neurological examination is also essential to assess the patient's cognitive status, mental status changes, level of consciousness, focal deficits, and other signs of intracranial pathology or raised intracranial pressure.

CSDH can appear in different locations, such as the cerebral convexities, interhemispheric fissure, tentorium posterior fossa and rarely in cranial base (sometimes with complex radiographic appearances). CSDHs can range in density from isodense to hypodense, in structure from homogeneous to mixed, and in compartmentalization from single‐compartment to loculated. 43 Due to this heterogeneity, there is no standard diagnostic criterion for CSDH. It is often defined by hypo‐ or isodense subdural fluid collections. 44 , 45 However, this definition has limitations, as radiodensity alone makes it difficult to differentiate it from subdural hygromas (cerebrospinal fluid accumulation in the subdural space). 38 Clinical diagnosis is also difficult due to a range of focal neurological symptoms and the time‐based criterion for chronicity is of limited practical value. Surgical exploration can show the composition of hematoma fluids, but not all patients are suitable for surgery. Thus, advanced noninvasive imaging techniques are important to develop more accurate diagnostic criteria. 43

Noncontrast Computed Tomography

A noncontrast computed tomography (CT) scan is the primary imaging modality used to diagnose CSDH, readily identifying the presence, size, and location of the hematoma. CSDH classically appears as a crescent‐shaped, hypodense collection along the convexity of the brain on a CT scan that crosses suture lines (Figure 2). In some cases, mixed densities can be seen, indicating different stages of blood degradation or rebleeding (Figure 3). Sagittal and coronal reformats provide a better fluid volume assessment and should be obtained when possible. 46 , 47

Nakaguchi et al 39 classified CSDHs based on internal architecture, density, and hematoma expansion on CT. They identified 4 types based on internal architecture: (1) homogeneous, (2) laminar, (3) separated (associated with a high recurrence), and (4) trabecular (associated with a low recurrence). They also categorized CSDHs by intracranial extension into convexity, cranial base (high recurrence) and interhemispheric types.

Measurements of CSDHs are not standardized, but common methods include maximal thickness and midline shift. Maximum thickness is measured on axial CT slices above the temporal bones and up to 2 slices superior to the lateral ventricles, taking into account high skull curvature and avoiding tentorial bleeding (Figure 4). Midline shift is assessed as the perpendicular distance between a midline structure, such as the septum pellucidum, and a reference line drawn along the midline at the level of the foramen of Monroe (Figure 5). A hematoma thickness >10 mm or a midline shift >5 mm often prompts consideration for surgical intervention. Although volumetric analysis provides a more accurate assessment of CSDH size, it is resource‐intensive and not routinely used. Siddiq et al 48 (n = 30 patients) assessed automated volumetric analysis of CSDH using Horos Open Source Medical Image Viewer and found the interobserver coefficient of variation 13.15% (0.07%–46.29%, with 97% within limits), and the intraobserver coefficient 13.76% (0.81%–48.34%, with 97% within limits). Advances in artificial intelligence can offer a solution for rapid and precise quantification of CSDH volumes. 49 , 50

Contrast Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a well‐established modality for visualizing and assessing the morphology of membranes in CSDH. 51 MRI imaging provides detailed information about the distribution and extent of membranes over cerebral lobes, which helps determine the appropriate craniotomy site. 52 Incomplete resection of membranes, particularly in difficult areas such as the inferior temporal or frontal lobes, can lead to recurrences. 39 Thus, assessing membrane distribution before craniotomy is helpful. Additionally, it differentiates between liquid and solid components of CSDH and improves the visualization of CT‐isodense hematomas. 53 Spreer et al 51 found that external membranes were visible in 16 out of 17 (94%) contrast MRI scans, regardless of hematoma stage, and internal membranes were seen only in late‐stage hematomas. Furthermore, contrast MRI shows triangular, spandrel‐like thickening in the transition zones between the external and internal membranes, 54 which histologically corresponds to areas with numerous newly formed capillaries. 55 Complete resection of these transition areas is important to reduce recurrences. 52 In cases of solidified hematomas, contrast MRI helps avoid ineffective twist‐drill or burr hole, guiding the preference for craniotomy with membranectomy. 53 Noncontrast CT, in these cases, often misrepresents the solid membranes as low‐density areas resembling liquid components. 56

Dual Energy CT

Dual‐energy CT (DECT) improves the localization and visualization of membranes in CSDH, similar to contrast MRI. It helps assess hematoma composition, including the liquid component and assesses membrane thickness, complexity, and maturity. 57 Iodine maps from 5‐minute delayed postcontrast DECT effectively segregate enhanced membranes from surrounding tissues by using iodine's contrast properties. These maps highlight contrast‐enhanced membranes, differentiating them from the brain parenchyma and hematoma. 58

DECT and contrast MRI show that external membranes progressively thicken and internal membranes develop in late‐stage CSDH. Membranes are graded on DECT as Grade I (enhanced external membranes), Grade II (early internal membrane formation with the “spandrel sign”), and Grade III (enhanced external and fully formed internal membranes). 57 These grades correlate with hematoma density and recurrence risk. 59 Studies have shown that higher DECT membrane grades increase recurrence rates after burr‐hole irrigation. 60 Using DynaCT during MMAE, Nakagawa et al 61 found similar patterns, indicating higher recurrence in patients with advanced membrane grades.

Follow‐Up Imaging

The optimal timing for follow‐up imaging after managing CSDH, whether through surgical intervention or nonoperative methods, remains undetermined. The timeline for CSDH resorption after MMAE is also unclear and there is no established consensus. However, most studies and ongoing trials perform imaging at 1 day post intervention (surgical or MMAE) and subsequently at 1, 3, and 6 months from the initial presentation. This protocol is commonly followed across many US institutions. For clinically stable patients who are initially asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic, less frequent neuroimaging might be sufficient, with additional CT scans performed as needed if symptoms develop or worsen. 62

Panel Consensus

The panel recommends a comprehensive clinical evaluation for diagnosing CSDH, including a detailed medical history and neurological examination to identify risk factors such as trauma, anticoagulant use, and neurological conditions. Noncontrast CT is the primary diagnostic imaging modality; however, it can be supplemented with contrast‐enhanced MRI or DECT and flat‐panel CT for a more detailed assessment of membrane morphology and hematoma composition in selected cases. Standardized measurement techniques, including automatic volumetric analysis and artificial intelligence tools, are promising tools in current research. Follow‐up imaging is advised at 24 hours, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post intervention, with MRI or DECT may be used as needed to evaluate recurrence and membrane characteristics. Further research is needed to refine diagnostic criteria and follow‐up protocols. Classification of CSDH: Predictors and Recurrence Risks

CSDHs can be classified using several methods. The classification scheme by Nomura et al, based on preoperative CT scan density, categorizes CSDH into 5 types: low density, isodensity, high density (can be acute SDH), mixed density, and layering. They found mixed CSDHs were more prone to recurrence, whereas hypodense CSDHs had a lower recurrence rate. 63 Nakaguchi et al classified CSDH into 4 types based on morphology: (1) homogenous, (2) laminar, (3) separated (hematoma with 2 components of different densities, demarked by a clear boundary, with the low density component above the high density), and (4) trabecular. 23 , 39 , 64 The homogeneous and laminar (the early subtypes) indicate a more immature hematoma. The separated subtype is often associated with chronicity and higher postoperative recurrence rates, as these types can obstruct drainage and contribute to inflammation and hematoma progression. 65 Takei et al recently proposed a modified classification system with 5 subtypes:homogenous, laminar, gradation, trabecular and separated, which showed better interrater agreement than the Nomura et al. and Nakaguchi et al., systems. 66 The study found that the gradation subtype had the strongest association with CSDH recurrence. The exploration of radiographic predictors of CSDH recurrence is ongoing and continues to evolve.

Imaging and assessing the morphology of CSDH has been challenging in clinical practice. Patients are often triaged and managed based on noncontrast head CT, which provides initial information on hematoma morphology. For hematomas that appear septated or compartmentalized or heterogeneous or recurred after intervention, contrast‐enhanced MRI or DECT can offer additional insights to guide surgical treatment and are often recommended in selected cases. 52 , 57

A meta‐analysis (n = 402 studies) by Zhu et al assessed 32 potential predictors of CSDH recurrence and found male sex, bilateral hematomas, and lack of surgical evacuation as significant risk factors. Other suggestive factors included larger hematoma volume, hematoma width ≥20 mm, midline shift ≥10 mm, and antithrombotic use. 15 Nair et al studied 88 patients who underwent burr hole hematoma evacuation and found that significant recurrence factors included hematoma width >2.3 cm, bilateral hematomas, gradation type on CT scans, and depth of brain (distance of the brain surface and the inner cranium) post evacuation. 67

Severity Grading of CSDH

Markwalder's grading scale combined with the GCS is often used to assess the severity of CSDH, predominantly in the elderly, where brain atrophy may obscure the correlation between imaging and clinical symptoms. Markwalder's grading scale‐GCS grading includes Grade 0: GCS of 15 with no symptoms; Grade I: GCS of 15 with symptoms such as headaches; Grade II: GCS (13–14) with drowsiness, disorientation, or mild neurological deficits such as hemiparesis; Grade III: GCS (9–13) where the patient is stuporous but responsive to painful stimuli, potentially with symptoms like hemiparesis; and Grade IV: GCS (3–8), a coma with no motor response to painful stimuli or presence of decerebrate. 68 , 69 The severity of CSDH based on Markwalder's grading scale‐GCS is outlined in Table 2.

Treatment Approaches for CSHD

CSDH is a common neurosurgical and neurological condition and is often managed through a multidisciplinary approach involving neurologists, neurosurgeons, neurointensivist, and neurointerventionalists. Management strategies may differ depending on patient‐specific factors and the expertise and preferences of the medical team.

Nonsurgical Treatment

Patients with small hematoma volume, no significant symptoms, or those unable to undergo surgical procedure, medical management can be an option. Atorvastatin and tranexamic acid are recognized for their potential efficacy in managing CSDH in selected cases. 71 However, dexamethasone was associated with a higher rate of unfavorable outcomes and serious adverse events at 6 months compared to placebo in the DEX‐CSDH (Dexamethasone for Adult Patients With A Symptomatic Chronic Subdural Haematoma) trial, and it was not considered cost effective. 72 Conservative management with medications also provides a relatively safe alternative for frail, elderly, or recurrent postoperative patients. 71 Nonsurgical treatment provides some insight into the natural course of CSDH, but the outcomes for patients and the rates of spontaneous stabilization or resolution remain unclear. Literature suggests that 20.8%–50% of asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients with CSDH resolve spontaneously and can be managed conservatively. 73 Careful monitoring is required in patients with conservative management, and repeated scan is advised in case of clinical deterioration, as 18%–20% of CSDH patients managed conservatively require surgical interventions. 73 , 74 However, the overall rate of treatment failure with conservative management is not well defined in contemporary practice. 75

Medical Management

Evidence‐based management guidelines are limited for patients with CSDH who do not require urgent surgery. Many such patients are on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy for various indications. The effects of these medications on CSDH formation, progression, and recurrence are not well established. Typically, discontinuation, with or without reversal, is considered for patients with symptomatic CSDH. However, in these patients when discontinuing the antithrombotic may involve significant risk to the patient (eg, patients with thrombophilia or mechanical valve, acute coronary event in patients with coronary artery disease). MMAE alone may be considered because the procedure can be performed without requiring antithrombotic reversal and allows for earlier resumption or continued use of the antithrombotic. In the MAGIC‐MT 36 RCT, 7.8% of patients in the MMAE plus standard treatment group and 6.9% in the standard treatment group were on antithrombotic therapy. Similarly, in the EMBOLISE 34 RCT, 38.1% in the MMAE with surgery and 38.9% in the surgery‐alone group were on antithrombotic therapy. In the STEM RCT, 35 38% in the MMAE plus standard treatment group and 42% in the standard treatment group were on antithrombotic therapy. Decisions about discontinuation or resumption of these medications are complex and tailored to individual patients, taking into account the medication's primary indication, hematoma size, and neurological status. 76 , 77 , 78 , 79

Different medical adjunctive treatments for CSDH have been explored, including statins, steroids, and tranexamic acid.

Atorvastatin

It is known for lowering cholesterol, but also offers anti‐inflammatory and proangiogenic benefits, helping CSDH management by promoting capillary repair and maturation. 80 , 81 Clinical studies found that low doses of atorvastatin are effective, and high doses can lead to excessive neovascularization. 82 Jiang et al 83 conducted a RCT with 196 patients with nonoperative CSDH, found that a low‐dose atorvastatin regimen (20 mg/day) led to greater reductions in hematoma volume, better neurological outcomes, and fewer surgical interventions compared to controls. Supporting these findings, de Monteiro et al's meta‐analysis showed that atorvastatin significantly reduced recurrence (risk ratio 0.46) without increasing adverse events. 84 Similarly, Wang et al's meta‐analysis of 7 trials found atorvastatin to be effective, with significant improvements in hematoma volume, daily living activities, and stroke scale scores. 85 The 2020 Chinese guidelines recommend a low dose of atorvastatin (20 mg/day) for a minimum of 8 weeks to improve symptoms and hematoma absorption, with continued use recommended for perioperative patients to reduce recurrence risk. Dosage adjustments are allowed if blood lipids remain high, up to 80 mg/day. 82 In the MAGIC‐MT RCT, 347 patients (96.4%) in the MMAE with standard treatment and 351 patients (97.0%) in the standard treatment group received statins. 36

Dexamethasone

The Dex‐CSDH trial, a large RCT of 748 patients, found that dexamethasone treatment led to fewer favorable outcomes and more adverse events compared to placebo at 6 months, although it resulted in fewer repeat surgeries. 45 Similarly, the DECSA (Dexamethasone Therapy Versus Surgery for Chronic Subdural Haematoma) trial, of 252 patients with symptomatic CSDH, showed that dexamethasone was not noninferior to burr‐hole drainage. 44 It was associated with more complications and a higher likelihood of additional surgery. High doses of dexamethasone (12–16 mg/day) can lead to complications such as osteoporosis, gastrointestinal ulcers, and obesity. As a result, high‐dose dexamethasone is not a standard management option for CSDH. 86 Current Chinese medical guidelines recommend low‐dose, short‐term dexamethasone (0.5–3 mg/day, not beyond 4 weeks or a total of 60 mg) as a safer option. 82 In the MAGIC‐MT RCT, 54 patients (15.0%) in the MMAE with standard treatment and 51 patients (14.1%) in the standard treatment group received glucocorticoids. 36

Tranexamic Acid

Tranexamic acid, an antifibrinolytic, has been shown to reduce CSDH volume. 87 Ebrahimi et al found that tranexamic acid reduces bleeding, but its impact on extradural and subdural bleeding was not statistically significant. 88 Yang et al reported that tranexamic acid as an adjunct can significantly improve residual CSDH absorption and accelerate its regression. 89 However, prolonged use of it can increase the likelihood of postoperative thrombosis and other adverse events. Thus, careful coagulation function monitoring and cautious treatment duration management are recommended. 47

Other Agents

Initial reports suggest that bevacizumab, an antiangiogenic drug, can be effective for treating recurrent CSDH after initial treatments have failed. This approach can potentially offer a new therapeutic option in the future. 90 , 91

Panel Consensus

The panel agrees that decisions regarding antithrombotic therapy for patients with CSDH should be individualized, considering the hematoma characteristics and patient‐specific factors. More research is needed to elucidate the effects of these medications on CSDH progression and recurrence. Final recommendations about the need for reversal of antithrombotic therapy also need more study. Nonsurgical treatment options are recommended for patients with minimal symptoms or those ineligible for surgery. Adjunctive use of atorvastatin (20 mg/day) is supported for its effectiveness in reducing hematoma volume and recurrence with minimal side effects. Dexamethasone can be considered in select cases, with careful monitoring for potential adverse events. Tranexamic acid can help reduce hematoma volume in selected cases but carries a risk of thrombosis. Bevacizumab shows promise for recurrent cases but needs further validation and data on its safety profile. Current practice for nonsurgical management is nonstandardized, with a limited understanding of clinical and radiological outcomes. Ongoing trials, including those on MMAE, are expected to provide insights into nonsurgical treatment efficacy and potential novel therapies. Surgical Treatment of CSDH

Surgery is the recommended treatment for patients with CSDH with hematoma thickness >10 mm, 33 pronounced neurological symptoms and midline shift >5mm. Surgery provides rapid relief from intracranial hypertension and helps prevent brain herniation. 92 Burr hole, twist drill craniotomy, and standard craniotomy are primarily used surgical techniques for CSDH. 93 Details on surgical treatment are provided in the Supplementary Appendix.

MMAE Management of CSDH

The MMA, a branch of the external carotid artery, enters the cranium through the foramen spinosum and runs along the squamous temporal bone. It then divides into anterior and posterior branches on the greater wing of the sphenoid bone (Figure 6). 94 , 95 MMAE is an endovascular technique where embolic agents are delivered via catheter to capillaries in the subdural neomembrane. This procedure aims to block blood flow to these vessels, which are believed to drive hematoma expansion and recurrence. 19 , 96 , 97 , 98 MMAE is an emerging approach as an adjunct or alternative to traditional treatments. 99

Observational Studies

Multiple single and multicenter retrospective studies show that MMAE of CSDH is an effective adjunct treatment in selected patients with low recurrence and procedural complications. Since Mandai et al first described the procedure in 2000, 26 subsequent observational studies and randomized trials have shown its benefit when added to standard CSDH treatment. 25 , 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 , 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 , 108 , 109 A multicenter observational study by Kan et al of 154 MMAE showed a 6.5% rescue surgery and 90.9% improvement in CSDH thickness, with general anesthesia (GA) used in 6.1% of cases. 110 Another study by Salih et al found a hematoma recurrence rate of 30.8% in the surgical evacuation alone group (n = 146) compared to 7.7% in the adjunctive MMAE group (n = 41). 111 A meta‐analysis by Jumah et al 112 (n = 177 patients) reported a 2.8% treatment failure, 1.2% complication, and 2.7% surgical rescue for MMAE. Ban et al found that 83.6% of nonsurgically managed patients without MMAE experienced treatment failure versus 0% with MMAE, and 18% of nonembolized surgical patients faced treatment failure compared to 2.2% with preoperative MMAE. 113 A meta‐analysis of 20 studies (MMAE group: n = 718 versus conventional management group: n = 698) by Ironside et al reported a surgical rescue of 4.4% and in‐hospital complication rate of 1.7% for MMAE compared to 16.4% and 4.9% for conventional treatments. 98 Onyinzo et al found a 5% rescue surgery for MMAE versus 15.1% for burr‐hole drainage. 21 Duerinck et al reported a 13.1% reoperation rate and a 6‐month mortality of 10.2% for mini‐craniotomy and MMAE showed mortality rates of 0%–7%, primarily due to underlying comorbidities. 114 Mortality rates for MMAE range from 0% to 7%, with most studies reporting <5%. 98 , 108 , 112 , 115 Mortality in these cases is generally due to underlying comorbidities rather than the CSDH or the procedure itself. 110 Similarly, Catapano et al reported significant reductions in CSDH thickness with MMAE compared to conventional treatment. 116 Thus, MMAE can offer an effective treatment option, providing a nonsurgical alternative for patients with mild to moderate symptoms and smaller hematomas. 117 , 118

Randomized Clinical Trials of MMAE

A prospectively randomized pilot study by Ng et al (n = 46) compared surgical treatment alone (n = 25) with adjunctive MMAE (surgery plus MMAE, n = 21). The study found that adjunctive MMAE resulted in significant hematoma volume reduction at a median of 90 days. One case of CSDH recurrence was reported in each group, with no treatment related events. 29

The recently published RCTs (EMBOLISE, 34 STEM, 35 and MAGIC‐MT 36 ) highlight the efficacy and safety of MMAE. EMBOLISE was a prospective, multicenter, open‐label, adaptive‐design RCT; that enrolled 400 patients with symptomatic subacute or CSDHs. Of these patients, 197 received Onyx MMAE plus surgery, and 203 underwent surgery alone. The mean age was 73.0±11.0 years in the MMAE group and 71.0±11.3 years in the surgery‐alone group. In both groups, 72.6% versus 73.4% were male, and the most common symptoms at initial presentation were headaches (68.5% versus 71.9%) and gait instability (71.1% versus 67.5%). Antithrombotic therapy use was 38.1% versus 38.9%, respectively. Hematoma characteristics included left‐sided (49.2% versus 54.7%), right‐sided (50.8% versus 45.3%), and bilateral (21.3% versus 18.2%) presentations, with mean volumes of 222.8±109.6 versus 235.7±118.2 ml. A modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of <3 was found in 63.5% versus 59.6%, with mean mRS of 2.2±1.12 versus 2.3±1.12. A total of 28.6% of the MMAE group and 39.6% of the surgery‐alone group underwent surgery prior to randomization. Burr‐hole surgery was performed in 53.6% versus 51.0%, and craniotomy in 46.4% versus 49.0%. MMAE was done under GA in 79.1% and after surgery in 42.2%. Embolization was successful in all 185 patients (100%), with reflux to nontarget vessels in 1.6%. Proximal and distal MMA occlusion occurred in 49.7% and 49.2%, respectively. The trial reported that 4.1% of patients in the MMAE group required reoperation for hematoma recurrence, compared to 11.3% in the surgery. Functional deterioration occurred in 11.9% of the MMAE group and 9.8% of the surgery‐alone group. At 90 days, mortality was 5.1% in the MMAE group and 3.0% of the surgery‐alone. Neurologic death occurred in 4.6% versus 2.0%, mostly due to subdural hematoma (4.1% versus 1.0%). None of the deaths were attributed to MMAE or the embolic agent. At 30 days, 4 patients (2.0%) in the MMAE experienced serious adverse events. Serious adverse events related to surgery were fewer in the MMAE group (16.2%) than in the surgery‐alone group (21.7%; P = 0.20). Stroke at 90 days was similar between groups (2.0% versus 1.5%; P = 0.72). No reflux‐related complications or ipsilateral vision loss occurred in the MMAE group. The trial concluded that patients who underwent MMAE plus surgery (treatment group) for subacute or CSDH had a significantly lower risk of rescue surgery compared to those who had surgery alone (control group). The risk of neurological deterioration was similar between the 2 groups. Although the 90‐day mortality rate was higher in the treatment group than in the control group (5.1% versus 3.0%), independent assessors did not attribute this difference to the embolic agent or the MMAE procedure. 34

The STEM trial was a multicenter, prospective RCT that enrolled 310 patients with symptomatic CSDH to compare MMAE plus standard treatment (n = 149) with standard treatment alone (n = 161). The mean age of patients was 72.8±10.4 years in the MMAE group and 73.4±11.3 years in the standard group, with 65% and 74% male, respectively. Antithrombotic therapy use was 38% (MMAE) versus 40% (standard). Baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (1.49±2.62 versus 1.68±2.34) and mRS (1.57±1.28 versus 1.58±1.30) scores were comparable. Hematomas were distributed as left sided (38% versus 32%), right sided (44% versus 45%), and bilateral (17% versus 23%), with similar thickness and midline shift. MMAE was performed in 144 of 149 patients (97%), with a 99% success rate and a mean procedure duration of 80 minutes. In the standard group, 187 of 189 patients (99%) underwent drainage via burr‐hole evacuation (89%) or subdural evacuating port system (7%). The trial reported that at 180 days, the MMAE group had a lower rate of recurrent/residual hematoma or reoperation (16% versus 36%). All‐cause mortality at 30 days was 3% in both groups, with no deaths attributed to MMAE or embolic agents. At 180 days, mortality was 8% in the MMAE group and 5% in the standard group, with neurologic deaths at 1% and 2%, respectively. Major disabling strokes occurred in 2% of standard patients. Functional outcomes (mRS scores) at 180 days were comparable (P = 0.65). The trial showed that adjunctive MMAE reduced treatment failure in symptomatic CSDH without increasing the risk of disabling stroke or short‐term mortality. 35

The MAGIC‐MT trial, a multicenter, investigator‐initiated study with a prospective randomized open blinded end point design, randomized 722 patients with symptomatic subacute or CSDH to MMAE plus standard treatment (n = 360) or standard treatment alone (n = 362). The standard treatment was burr‐hole drainage as needed, best medical management, or a combination of both. The median patient age was 68.5 years (interquartile range: 60–74) in the MMAE group and 70 years (interquartile range: 61–75) in the standard group, with males 81% and 84%, respectively. Common symptoms at the presentation included headaches (57% versus 54%) and hemiparesis (53% versus 48%). Prior antithrombotic use was low (7.8% versus 6.8%), and head trauma was confirmed in 49.4% versus 55.5%. Hematomas were similarly distributed between left‐sided (58.6% versus 55.0%), right‐sided (41.4% versus 45.0%), and bilateral cases (19.2% versus 22.7%), with median hematoma volumes and thickness comparable across groups. MMA embolization was successful in 98.3% of patients, and burr‐hole drainage was performed in 78.1% (MMAE group) and 78.5% (standard treatment alone), with 99.6% of drainage following embolization. Most patients (97.9%) received statins or glucocorticoids. The MMAE group demonstrated a lower symptomatic recurrence or progression rate (6.7% versus 9.9%), with symptomatic recurrence (4.7% versus 5.2%) and progression (1.9% versus 4.7%). Rescue surgery within 90 days was less frequent in the MMAE group (3.1% versus 6.4%). Serious adverse events were also lower in the MMAE group (6.7% versus 11.6%). Mortality at 90 days was 0.6% (MMAE group) versus 2.2% (standard treatment alone). Embolization‐related complications were 0.8%, including facial nerve paralysis (1 patient) and allergic reactions (2 patients). Burr‐hole drainage complications were observed in 2.5% (MMAE group) versus 1.1% (standard treatment alone). The trial concluded that in patients with symptomatic subacute or chronic SDH, MMAE plus standard treatment resulted in a similar incidence of hematoma recurrence or progression compared to standard treatment alone. However, MMAE was associated with a lower incidence of serious adverse events, including mortality. 36

The preliminary results of the MEMBRANE (Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization for the Treatment of Subdural Hematomas With TRUFILL n‐butyl cyanoacrylate [n‐BCA]) trial, a multicenter randomized open label controls trial, showed that of 376 patients with CSDH enrolled, 265 were assigned to surgery and 111 to nonsurgical standard care. Within the surgical arm, patients were randomized to surgery plus n‐BCA MMAE (n = 133) or surgery alone (n = 132), with mean ages of 69.9±10.44 and 69.7±12.54 years, and 76.7% and 74.2% male, respectively. Common presenting symptoms included headache (70.7% versus 69.7%), hemiparesis (56.8% for both), and gait instability (48.9% versus 49.2%). Baseline mRS scores (0–2) were similar (75.9% versus 75.0%), as were hematoma thickness (14.94±5.14 versus 14.55±4.83 mm) and midline shift (6.21±4.09 versus 6.26±4.10 mm). Antithrombotic use was comparable (23% versus 22.7%). In the nonsurgical plus MMAE group (n = 55) versus the nonsurgical‐alone group (n = 56), the mean ages were 73.3±10.34 and 71.9±10.75 years, respectively, with 74.5% and 73.2% male. Presenting symptoms included headache (50.9% versus 64.3%), hemiparesis (21.8% versus 28.6%), and gait instability (25.5% versus 37.5%). Baseline mRS score (0–2) was 72.7% versus 82.1%, whereas hematoma thickness (15.79±4.99 versus 16.29±5.58 mm) and midline shift (2.48±2.48 versus 3.05±2.68 mm) were comparable. Antithrombotic use was also consistent (27.3% versus 26.8%). Surgical techniques in the surgery plus MMAE group included burr holes (∼74%), craniotomy (∼30%), subdural evacuating port systems (∼12%), twist drill (∼5%), craniectomy (∼2%), and external ventricular drains (∼12%). Approximately 85% of procedures were performed under GA, with radial access used in ∼50% of cases. Target embolization was achieved in 99% of surgical and 100% of nonsurgical cases. 119

At 6 months, residual or reaccumulated CSDH (>10 mm) or the need for rescue surgery was 8.5% in the surgery plus MMAE group, 20.2% in the surgery‐alone group, 20.2% in the nonsurgical plus MMAE group, and 27.0% in the nonsurgical‐alone group. MMAE‐related severe adverse events were reported in 3.3% of patients in the MMAE groups and 2.2% in device‐related events. All strokes were comparable between the MMAE plus standard care group (2.8%) and the standard treatment‐alone group (2.2%). All‐cause mortality at 6 months was 4.0% in the MMAE groups versus 12.4% in the standard care‐alone groups. The trial demonstrated significant benefits of MMAE adjunct with surgery at 6 months, a favorable trend with MMAE in nonsurgical management and a significant treatment effect for MMAE in patients aged ≥75 years undergoing surgery. Additionally, MMAE was noninferior to standard care for achieving good functional outcomes at 3 months. 119

The results from these multicenter RCTs suggest that MMAE has the potential to become a standard of care for managing CSDH in selected patients. However, detailed evaluations of MMAE's effectiveness across patient subgroups, considering factors such as symptom severity, hematoma size and anticoagulation status, are underway. Final recommendations will depend on the outcomes of ongoing trials. CHESS trial (Chronic Subdural Hematoma Treatment with Embolization versus Surgery Study) is currently enrolling mildly symptomatic patients with CSDH and randomizing them to MMAE and surgical treatment. This is the only trial thus far that evaluates MMAE as a primary standalone treatment option for selected patients and is expected to complete enrollment by 2028. Embolic Agents

Embolic agents for treating CSDH each offer distinct mechanisms and benefits. Particle agents such as polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) work by lodging in vessels and inducing inflammation and necrosis. 120 They are well established in use despite the absence of a randomized trial 121 but can cause aggregation, leading to proximal occlusion and potential catheter blockage. 120 This can be avoided by appropriate sizing and dilution of the PVA particle. Embosphere (tris‐acyl gelatin) also triggers an inflammatory response 120 but is less likely to clog microcatheters and penetrates smaller vessels more effectively than PVA, 122 although it requires intermittent agitation. 120 Gelatin sponge, a hemostatic agent, forms a matrix for clotting and granulation but is less reliable in terms of degradation and not ideal for permanent occlusion. A meta‐analysis of 11 studies (n = 359) by Abdollahifard et al explored the use of particle embolic agents and found a recurrence and reoperation of 5% and a complication rate of 4%, hematoma reduction or resolution was achieved in 85% and 66% of cases, respectively. 123 Compared to conventional CSDH treatments, MMAE with particle agents demonstrated a significantly lower risk ratio of recurrence (0.10), reoperation (0.25), and complications (0.34). Scoville et al compared radiographic (50% hematoma reduction within 3 months) and clinical outcomes (need for rescue surgery within 3 months) after particle (PVA) versus liquid (Onyx or nBCA) MMAE in upfront (n = 133), recurrent (n = 59), and prophylactic (n = 16) treatments. Hematoma reduction was similar between particle and liquid in upfront (61.8% versus 61.3%; P = 0.45), recurrent (61% versus 55.6%; P = 0.81), and prophylactic (72.7% versus 20%; P = 0.75) groups. The need for rescue surgery was also comparable (upfront: 8.0% versus 0.0%; P = 0.19; recurrent: 2.4% versus 5.6%; P = 0.52; prophylactic: 0.0% versus 0.0%; P = 1.00). 124 Similarly, other studies found a comparable outcome (hematoma reduction and recurrence) between the 2 agents. 125 , 126 , 127 , 128

Liquid agents include Onyx Liquid Embolic System (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), which precipitates from external to internal, 129 offering a broad range of viscosities 130 but can result in reflux, temporary visibility loss, and imaging artifacts during lengthy injections. 131 The EMBOLISE trial used different Onyx formulations (Onyx 18 only, Onyx 34 only, Onyx 18 and 34) and found reflux to nontarget vessels (petrosal, collaterals) in 1.6% (3/185). Onyx distribution included proximal MMA occlusion (0.5%, 1/185), proximal MMA branches (49.2%, 91/185), and distal MMA branches (49.2%, 91/185), with no embolic agent‐related complications. 34 In the MAGIC‐MT trial, Onyx distribution in MMA included main trunk only (2.8%, 10/357), distal only (0.8%, 3/357), and both main trunk and distal (92.2%, 332/357), with no reported complications. 36 n‐BCA polymerizes on contact, 130 minimizing tissue infarction risks and allowing adjustable vessel penetration with Lipiodol, 99 and unlike Onyx embolization it does not elicit pain during injection; therefore, the procedure can be performed under conscience sedation. However, it can rapidly occlude the catheter with multiple injections and has a short working time. 122 Majidi et al reported no cases of cranial nerve palsy, vision loss, or thromboembolic events in 61 patients with SDH treated with MMAE using diluted n‐BCA with large volume of dextrose 5% in water. 132 Lipiodol alone provides radiopacity and is often used with other liquid agents. 99 Squid, like Onyx, offers better visibility during longer injections but causes similar imaging artifacts and requires more preparation time versus PHIL. 131 The STEM RCT used various squid embolic preparations (SQUID 12, SQUID 12 LD, SQUID 18, SQUID 18 LD, SQUID 34 LD) and reported no embolic agent‐related complications. 35 PHIL, another liquid option, provides high embolic capacity with fewer imaging artifacts compared to Onyx and Squid 131 but has fewer established safety data. 99

Coil‐based methods induce thrombus formation and collagen deposition. 133 Recent data suggest that there is no significant difference in the percentage of reduction in hematoma thickness between coil embolization and particle or liquid embolic agents. 110 There has been no published randomized clinical trial comparing safety and efficacy of different embolic agents. Coil‐only embolization cannot occlude distal MMA branches or penetrate into the SDH membrane. 134 Combining coils with particle or liquid embolization improve occlusion at the arteriole and capillary levels, potentially offering a more durable solution compared to using coils alone. 135

Safety Profile and Procedural Considerations for MMAE

The main risk of MMAE is unintended embolization of critical vessels, like the ophthalmic artery anastomoses (Figure 7), and reflux of embolic agent into proximal vessels, such as the petrosquamosal branch. However, MMAE procedures generally have a favorable safety profile, with overall complication of ∼3%, and individual risks for stroke, hemorrhage, and visual loss <1%. 136 To minimize embolization into dangerous anastomoses, such as dural‐pial or squamopetrosal branch or meningo‐ophthalmic arteries, it is recommended to use a microcatheter positioned above the anterior clinoid process on lateral image, preferably targeting the frontal or parietal branches of the MMA. Liquid embolics provide deep penetration into distal branches of MMA but can risk ischemic complications due to unseen anastomoses. Particles >150 µm reduce the risk of such complications but offer less penetration compared to liquid and smaller particles. Coils avoid reflux and compromise of key anastomoses but do not penetrate distal branches. 62

Comparing the safety profiles of different embolic agents is challenging and requires larger prospective studies. Both conscious sedation and GA, as well as radial and femoral access sites, are feasible options for MMAE. Salem et al (n = 778) compared outcomes of GA (n = 280) versus non‐GA (n = 667) in MMAE and found no significant differences in radiological improvement or clinical outcomes between the groups. 137 In the EMBOLISE RCT, 155 patients underwent MMAE under GA and 41 under conscious sedation, with groin access used in 118 cases and radial access in 78. 34 Similarly, in the STEM RCT, 135 patients underwent MMAE under GA and 9 under conscious sedation. 35 For elderly patients with coagulopathies and multiple comorbidities, conscious sedation and radial access can reduce risks. If liquid embolization with dimethyl sulfoxide/Onyx is performed under conscious sedation, intraarterial lidocaine can be used to improve patient comfort. 62

Panel Consensus

Based on patient characteristics, anatomy, and hematoma features, the panel recommends MMAE as an adjunct to standard treatment (surgical or nonsurgical) for symptomatic, nonemergent and nonrecurrent CSDH. This procedure has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing hematoma volume, recurrence and the need for further surgical interventions. For patients with recurrent CSDH, MMAE has shown promising results in preventing additional recurrences and offering a nonsurgical alternative to conventional methods.

As MMAE has a favorable safety profile, with low complication and mortality rates, the technique should be considered carefully based on individual patient factors, including hematoma size and symptom severity. GA or non‐GA anesthesia can be selected based on patient characteristics, whereas radial access is advisable for patients with coagulopathy. Liquid embolics (Onyx and PHIL) offer controlled, sustained embolization, whereas particle agents (PVA and Embosphere) are effective for vessel occlusion. However, differences in penetration and embolization territory between the two have not been compared in head‐to‐head studies. Ongoing trials are expected to provide further insights into the optimal use of MMAE and its role in the broader management of CSDH. Financial Burden of CSDH

The increase in the prevalence of SDH has doubled the national cost of treatment and a 60% increase in the national budget from 2001 ($1.0 billion) to 2007 ($1.6 billion). 138 In the National Inpatient Sample analysis, for patients without surgical drainage, the median hospital admission cost was $20 341.60 (±$38 327.30), and for patients who had surgical drainage, the cost was $35 366.0 (±$50 497.30). 62 This does not account for reoperations for recurrent CSDH after the first surgery. Surgeries for recurrent CSDH require more extended hospital stays and increased outpatient follow‐up. The total cost of treatment for recurrent CSDH was 132% higher than nonrecurrent CSDH treatment, according to a recent report. 92 Regan et al reported the mean cost per patient based on operating time $7588 for burr hole procedures and $10 416 for craniotomy. 139 Cost‐effectiveness may become an important consideration in evaluating MMAE as a treatment option for CSDH. Studies have shown that compared to surgical treatment, MMAE had lower 1‐year posttreatment hospital cost (mean difference $32 776; P<0.01). 140 The MAGIC‐MT RCT reported the mean (SD) hospitalization expenses in RMB (Renminbi) for MMAE plus standard treatment (surgery or medical management) as 44 440.01±24 809.10 (∼$6099±$3404 USD), compared to standard treatment alone at 21 591.99±16 964.79 (∼$2963±$2328 USD). 36 However, the trial was not designed to perform a comprehensive cost analysis or evaluate costs based on high‐risk CSDH features.

Future Research Directions

There are currently 16 active CSDH trials listed on ClinicalTrials.gov (Table S2). These ongoing trials will explore MMAE as a potential first‐line therapy for nonurgent CSDH cases and compare different embolic agents, techniques, and anesthesia methods. The panel suggests pooled analyses of individual patient‐level data to refine the evaluation of MMAE's efficacy and safety. This will support subgroup analyses and clarify the effects of comorbidities and antithrombotic use. The panel suggests that future RCTs should explore MMAE in neurologically unstable, emergent CSDH cases as an adjunct to standard care, as current trials (EMBOLIZE, 34 STEM 35 ) have primarily included symptomatic patients requiring evacuation but focused on stable, nonemergent cases. Future studies should also include investigating the potential benefits of bilateral MMAE compared to unilateral embolization and developing a universal grading system for MMAE that can be used to predict the efficacy of the treatment. The panel also suggests exploring the MMAE as a standalone treatment in stable, nonemergent cases, and as both adjunctive and standalone intervention for recurrent CSDH.

Conclusions

These recommendations for the management of CSDH are based on clinical trial results, observational studies, and expert consensus. MMAE as an adjunct to standard treatment (surgical or nonsurgical) is preferred for symptomatic, nonemergent and nonrecurrent CSDH. It can be performed under GA or conscious sedation, using liquid embolic agents (Onyx or Squid or n‐BCA) with radial or femoral access, tailored to the patient's condition and hematoma characteristics. As clinical trial results become available, these guidelines will be updated to incorporate the latest evidence to ensure that treatment strategies for CSDH continue to evolve in response to emerging data.
