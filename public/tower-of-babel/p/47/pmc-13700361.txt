Relationship between site procedural volumes and outcomes of left bundle branch area pacing using stylet-driven leads from the INSIGHT-LBBA Registry
Authors: Ilya Y Shadrin, Hillary Mulder, Brooke Alhanti, Nicholas Wold, Gaurav A Upadhyay, Christopher A Latanich, Seth Goldbarg, Kevin M Trulock, Apoor Patel, Zak Loring, Myat Shwe Yee Tun, Adi Lador, Jonathan Na, Paul W Jones, Stephen B Ruble, Daniel J Friedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft bundle branch (LBB) area pacing (LBBAP) is increasingly used for patients with a ventricular pacing indication. Multi-center studies analyzing predominantly lumenless leads have suggested a learning curve for LBBAP; however, analogous data on stylet-driven leads (SDLs) are lacking.ObjectiveTo assess the relationship between site volume and procedural outcomes and complications using the INGEVITY+ SDL for LBBAP.MethodsA secondary analysis of the INSIGHT-LBBA multi-site registry was performed, with retrospective data collected across 8 United States sites performing LBBAP with INGEVITY+ SDLs between 2020 and 2023. Outcomes included LBBAP and LBB capture success rates, pacing parameters, fluoroscopy time, number of leads used, and lead-related complications. Outcomes were assessed using a generalized estimated equation based on consecutive implants. A sensitivity analysis was performed restricted to only single- and dual-chamber LBBAP implants.ResultsA total of 1374 patients were enrolled in the primary INSIGHT-LBBA registry. Overall LBBAP success and LBB capture success rates were 94.5% and 78.1%, respectively. LBBAP success rate improved with experience (odds ratio, 1.09; 95% confidence interval, 1.03–1.14, per every 10 successive LBBAP implants; P = .001), with similar results in the sensitivity analysis. Fluoroscopy times and complication rates remained low (10.0 ± 9.2 minutes and 2.7%, respectively).ConclusionIn this secondary analysis of INSIGHT-LBBA using INGEVITY+ SDLs, LBBAP and LBB capture success rates were high and improved with consecutive implants, indicative of a learning curve for implantation. Fluoroscopy times and complication rates remained low without significant change during consecutive implants. These data add to the growing literature supporting safe and effective use of SDLs for LBBAP.Keywords: Left bundle branch area pacing, Conduction system pacing, Learning curve, Stylet-driven lead, Procedural success

Introduction

Since its inception in 2017,1 left bundle branch (LBB) area pacing (LBBAP) has rapidly become a preferred pacing strategy for patients with a ventricular pacing indication. Consistent with most novel technologies, a multicenter European registry of LBBAP implants (MELOS2) recently identified a learning curve for LBBAP implantation across 14 different sites. Because a majority (83.8%) of these procedures involved the use of a lumenless lead, analogous performance data for stylet-driven leads (SDLs) are relatively lacking. To this end, a multicenter registry (INSIGHT-LBBA3) recently assessed the safety and efficacy of an active fixation SDL (INGEVITY+) during consecutive LBBAP attempts across 8 United States high-volume centers. All pre-specified performance goals for pacing, sensing, and lead-related complications (LRC) were met, which subsequently contributed to the Food and Drug Administration approval for the use of the INGEVITY+ lead for LBBAP.4,5 Given the multi-site nature of the initial registry, we sought to address the learning curve knowledge gap surrounding SDLs. To this end, we performed a secondary analysis of the INSIGHT-LBBA registry assessing the relationship between site volume and procedural outcomes and complications using the INGEVITY+ lead for LBBAP.

Methods

Study design

The primary INSIGHT-LBBA study was a retrospective, multi-center, investigator-initiated non-randomized registry (NCT 06014866) funded by Boston Scientific (Marlborough, MA) that included patients with attempted LBBAP implantation for a bradycardia indication between Q3 of 2020 and Q3 of 2023. The Duke Clinical Research Institute served as the coordinating center for the study. All LBBAP implants collected for the primary registry were performed using the Boston Scientific INGEVITY+ lead, a 59 cm 6 Fr active fixation SDL delivered through 1 of 4 site-specific pacing catheters (SSPC 1–4).

Data from enrolled patients were collected retrospectively on consecutive implants at each participating center, and organized via a custom REDCap electronic database to maximize data uniformity and completeness. As all entered data was de-identified with no collection of source documents, each site was exempt from requiring patient consent by the local institutional review board. All patients were required to have at least 90 days of follow-up to be included in the study. Participating sites were requested to submit supplementary data collection voluntarily, which included additional procedural information not obtained in the initial data registry. The research reported in this paper adhered to guidelines for human subject research as outlined in the Helsinki Declaration.

Outcomes

Procedural success was measured in 2 ways—LBBAP success rate and LBB capture success rate. LBBAP success was defined as either capture of left interventricular septal tissue or capture of the left-sided conduction system (LBB capture), which was further defined based on MELOS criteria described previously.2,3 Briefly, LBB capture was confirmed when 1 of the following criteria were met: (1) QRS morphology change during either threshold testing or programmed electrical stimulation; (2) onset of pacing stimulus to R-wave peak time of lead V6 (V6-RWPT) < 80 ms (if narrow QRS or isolated right bundle branch block) or <90 ms (if more advanced ventricular conduction disease such as bifascicular block, LBB block, or complete heart block with wide escape rhythm); (3) LBB potential to V6-RWPT interval within 10 ms of pacing stimulus to V6-RWPT; or (4) V6–V1 interpeak interval (peak of R-wave in V6 to peak of terminal R-wave in V1) of >40 ms. Capture of left septal tissue was defined as the presence of a terminal R/r in lead V1 without meeting any LBB capture criteria.

Measured intraprocedural parameters included number of leads used per implant, and total procedure and fluoroscopy time. Pacing lead parameters at implant and 3 months (supplemented with 6-month data if not available at 3 months) included sensing (mV), impedance (Ω), and pacing threshold (V). Peri- and post-procedural complications included any LRC or lead dislodgement, as described in the initial INSIGHT-LBBA registry.3

Statistical analysis

Initial secondary analysis of INSIGHT-LBBA data was performed on all enrolled patients with non-missing device type, with a sensitivity analysis restricted to those patients who received either a single or dual-chamber pacemaker. For descriptive purposes, consecutive implants at each site were grouped into 4 categories—implants #1–25, #26–50, #51–100, and #101+. Missing data were excluded from denominators for descriptive analyses, and modeling was performed using a complete case approach. Baseline characteristics included demographics, medications at enrollment, comorbidities, pacing indication, heart failure status, and echocardiographic and electrocardiogram (ECG) parameters.

A generalized estimating equation model was used to evaluate the associations between implant order and either LBBAP success, LBB capture success, intraprocedural outcomes, or pacing parameters, taking note of clustering of patients within sites and using an independent working correlation matrix. The linearity assumption for implant order with respect to outcomes was evaluated using natural cubic splines. If major violations were found, 2 piecewise linear splines were created for inclusion in the model. For LBBAP success, LBB capture success, and leads per implant (1 vs 2 or 3 leads), a binomial distribution and logit link were used, whereas a normal distribution with identity link was used for the intraprocedural outcomes and pacing parameters. Results of the logistic regression models were presented as counts (%) within levels of the categorized implant order and odds ratios (OR) (95% confidence interval [CI]) per a 10-unit increase in continuous implant order. Results of the linear regression models were presented as mean (standard deviation) within levels of categorized implant order and mean difference (95% CI) in outcome per a 10-unit increase in continuous implant order. Modeling was performed both unadjusted and adjusted for age, sex, heart failure type, and device type (pacemaker vs other).

To evaluate the association between implant order and procedural complications, a Cox proportional hazards model was used. The proportional hazards assumption was checked using weighted Schoenfeld residuals, with no major violations noted. To account for clustering, a robust sandwich estimator was used to calculate standard errors. Results were presented using Kaplan–Meier event rates at 12 and 24 months, and Kaplan–Meier curves truncated at 24 months, as well as hazard ratios (HRs) (95% CI) per a 10-unit increase in continuous implant order. Outcomes were evaluated in both unadjusted and adjusted models.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1374 patients were enrolled in INSIGHT-LBBA with no missing data. Baseline characteristics demonstrated a mean age of 75.1 ± 10.6 years, 58.5% male sex, 81.1% Caucasian, mean ejection fraction (EF) of 54.7% ± 11.1% (19.2% with heart failure with reduced EF [HFrEF]), and a pacing indication of either high-grade atrioventricular block (47.4%) or sinus node dysfunction (36.0%) in a majority of patients (Table 1). Average baseline QRS duration pre-LBBAP implant was 117.7 ± 30.6 ms if intrinsic and 163.2 ± 29.5 ms if paced (Table 1). Relative to earlier LBBAP implants (1–25), later implants (101+) involved patients with a higher prevalence of idiopathic cardiomyopathy (26% vs 8.2%), HFrEF (27.3% vs 11.9%), and implantation of either a CRT-P or CRT-D (20% vs 5.1%) due to a higher number of patients meeting class I or II criteria for CRT (21.5% vs 2.6%, Table 1). A total of 1122 patients received either a single- or a dual-chamber pacemaker and were included in the sensitivity analysis, with similar baseline characteristics to the overall cohort (Table 2). In this cohort, no clinically significant baseline differences were observed when stratified by implant category.

Before the first recorded INGEVITY+ implant in the INSIGHT-LBBA registry, each of the 8 participating sites had varying degrees of familiarity with LBBAP implantation, with an overall higher level of experience using the lumen-less 3830 lead by Medtronic (5 of 8 sites with >50 implants) and less with other non-INGEVITY+ SDL leads by Biotronik or Abbott (1 site with >50 implants, Supplemental Table 1). Pacing outcomes

Overall LBBAP success rate was 95.3%, with moderate variability across different sites (Supplemental Figure 1), and a significant increase per higher implant order (adjusted OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04–1.17, per every 10 successive LBBAP implants; P < .001; Table 3). Similarly, LBB capture success rate averaged 78.8%, with significant variability by implanting site (Supplemental Figure 2), and increased for higher implant order (adjusted OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.01–1.07, per every 10 successive implants; P = .006; Table 3). The use of multiple leads per implant was highest among implants 101+ (13.4% of patients; adjusted OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.00–1.05, per 10 consecutive implants; P = .03), but this was driven largely by a single site, and subgroup analysis with exclusion of the outlier site eliminated all significant differences across implant categories (Table 3). Pacing parameters at implant and 3 months were consistent as implant order increased (Table 3).

When comparing groups by prior lumenless lead experience (high- vs low-experienced sites), periprocedural characteristics including LBBAP success, procedure time, and fluoroscopy times were similar across these groups (Supplemental Table 3). Complications

Overall, LRC were low at 2.7% at 1 year and 3.7% at 2 years (Table 5, Figure 1). A majority of these complications involved lead dislodgement, with 2.2% by 1 year and 2.4% by 2 years (Table 5). Notably, the Kaplan–Meier curves indicated no events of lead dislodgement past 6 months among implants >26 (Figure 1). No significant differences by implant volume were observed in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses (adjusted HR for LRC, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.99–1.03; P = .40; adjusted HR for dislodgement, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.97–1.04; P = .70; Table 5). Within the sensitivity analysis cohort of non-CRT devices, complication rates were similarly low at 2.8% (any LRC) and 2.2% (lead dislodgement, Table 6), with all complications occurring in the first ∼8 months (Figure 2) and no further lead dislodgement past 6 months for all implant categories (Figure 2). Similarly, no significant associations were observed between implant order and complications. Lastly, when comparing sites grouped based on prior lumenless lead experience, no statistically significant differences in lead-related complications or lead dislodgements were noted between the high- and low-experienced sites (Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we performed a secondary analysis of the INSIGHT-LBBA multi-site registry assessing the safety and efficacy of INGEVITY+ leads for LBBAP. To our knowledge, this is the largest LBBAP study assessing procedural outcomes based on the implant order of the INGEVITY+ SDL. Overall, we identified that patients receiving later LBBAP implants were more likely to have HFrEF and meet criteria for CRT pacing. Furthermore, our data indicated that LBBAP success and LBB capture success rates continued to improve with consecutive cases, indicative of a learning curve for implant success. Finally, total procedural and fluoroscopy times, as well as the low rate of overall complications, exhibited no learning curve during LBBAP implantation with non-CRT systems.

Analysis of the baseline characteristics identified an overall homogeneous population of patients across different implant categories, with several notable exceptions for the later implant group (101+ implants). Specifically, patients in this cohort had 3–11× higher prevalence of idiopathic cardiomyopathy and HFrEF, and received either a CRT-P or CRT-D due to having met class I/II criteria for CRT. This may reflect a higher tendency for operators with more experience to implant LBBAP leads in patients that would normally receive a traditional CRT system with a CS lead, as limited evidence has supported the use of LBBAP instead of traditional CRT for patients with HFrEF.6, 7, 8 Given this significant discrepancy in baseline characteristics, a sensitivity analysis restricted to only single- or dual-chamber pacemakers was performed, with expected elimination of these baseline differences. Notably, the original cohort demonstrated a significant increase in both LBBAP success and LBB capture success with successive implants, with a 4%–10% increase in the odds of success for every 10 implants. Although the sensitivity analysis did not identify significant differences in LBB capture success for consecutive implants continuously, there was a trend towards a higher LBBAP success rate with later implants that barely missed statistical significance (OR, 1.11; 95% CI, 0.99–1.24; P = .076), likely due to a smaller cohort relative to the overall population. Linear assessment of fluoroscopy times suggested a 0.4-minute increase in fluoroscopy time for every 10 implants among implants >100—while statistically significant, this is likely of minimal clinical significance and may represent a tendency for operators to take on more challenging cases with increasing experience that may necessitate more fluoroscopy use. In contrast to the current analysis, the landmark MELOS study adopting LBBAP demonstrated longer learning curves, with the probability of LBBAP success slowly increasing until 270 cases, whereas fluoroscopy time for LBB capture decreased over the initial 110 cases.2 This mixed prospective and retrospective study encompassed 2533 patients across 14 European centers with implants between June 2018 and November 2021, with an average LBBAP success rate of 89.6%, and as such, likely reflects earlier adoption of LBBAP requiring a longer time to develop proficiency. Furthermore, 83.8% of implants used the Medtronic 3830 lumenless lead, with the remaining SDLs comprising the Solia (Biotronik) and Tendril (Abbott) leads, and only 9 cases using INGEVITY, making the results difficult to compare directly to those of INSIGHT-LBBA. Interestingly, analysis presented in supplemental data indicated a higher LBBAP success rate with SDLs relative to lumenless leads (92.9% vs 89.2%, P = .02), albeit at a cost of higher lead dislodgement rates (3.8% vs 1.1%, P < .001).2 Overall, LRC (2.6%) was similar to our current analysis. Subsequent analyses have failed to demonstrate significant differences in LBBAP success between SDLs and lumenless leads.9,10

More recently, the Conduction-System Pacing Italian Network Group (C-SING) collected prospective data on LBBAP procedures performed between January 2022 and December 2023 and identified that high-experience operators (>50 implants) had shorter fluoroscopy times (5.9 vs 9 minutes, P = .005) and a higher LBBAP success rate of 98.6% vs ∼92.5% for operators with ≤50 implants (P < .001).11 Although this partially corroborates our observed learning curves for LBBAP success, the C-SING study categorized implant experience by operator rather than by center, as was established in this secondary analysis. Unlike MELOS, a large fraction of patients (63.9%) in C-SING received an SDL, with the majority (51.1% total) using the Solia (Biotronik), and only 9.3% of all patients using the INGEVITY+. Similarly, several smaller single- and multi-center registries have shown high performance rates with evidence of a learning curve when implanting SDLs, although these studies exhibited a modest number of patients (50–353) and were strictly limited to the use of the Biotronik Solia lead.12, 13, 14 As such, our data corroborate the results of these studies, and more importantly, extend the high-performance dynamics and learning curves to the Ingevity+ lead by Boston Scientific among a broader patient population across 14 centers. However, this learning curve did not extend to fewer leads wasted in higher implant categories. Interestingly, 1 participating center used >1 lead per implant attempt significantly more frequently (14%) for implants 101+ relative to earlier implants, suggesting that increased experience may prompt operators to switch leads earlier due to improved knowledge of lead behavior. Furthermore, when comparing sites based on prior lumenless lead experience, those with limited experience on average used more than 1 lead more frequently relative to the higher-experienced sites, likely reflecting less familiarity with LBBAP implantation technique. This, however, was not analyzed statistically due to low event rates, and overall procedural characteristics and implant success were not different based on prior lumenless lead experience.

With respect to complications, both our primary and secondary analyses indicated an overall low rate of LRC (2.7%), consistent with other reports (2.6% in MELOS,11 and 3.4% in C-SING,11 2%–5% across other studies,12, 13, 14, 15, 16 although with some smaller studies reporting ∼0% LRC complication rates17, 18, 19). Notably, the majority of the LRCs were due to lead dislodgement, which occurred early post-implant and was not observed after 5–8 months post-implant across all implant categories, with no evidence for a learning curve bias for increased early complications. The lead dislodgement rates trended towards a bimodal distribution, with the highest rates among the earliest and latest implanting categories, although not statistically significant. This may be a result of different factors, namely limited operator experience for the early groups, and increased tendency to take on tougher cases for the later groups, although the granularity of our data set does not provide details to support these speculations. Importantly, this low complication rate was independent of prior lumenless lead experience, indicating an excellent safety profile even for sites with less LBBAP experience.

Limitations

The current study has multiple limitations worth noting. Given the deidentified and retrospective design of the INSIGHT-LBBA registry, LBB capture classification relied on investigator-reported criteria without central ECG adjudication, as the raw ECGs were not collected in the original registry. Furthermore, as LBB capture was determined based on a binary assessment of capture criteria, absolute values for V6-RWPT or V6–V1 interpeak interval were not available for assessing the degree of improvement with later LBBAP implants, as was suggested through the MELOS study.2 Due to significant inter-site heterogeneity, several outliers were noted that required partial subgroup analysis; however, due to the limited number of sites, extensive subgroup analysis was not performed to maintain adequate statistical power. Lastly, as the participating sites involved relatively experienced operators, results may not be translatable to the overall implanting population.

Conclusion

This secondary analysis of the INSIGHT-LBBA registry identified that the success rate of both LBBAP and LBB capture was high and continued to improve with consecutive cases. Furthermore, overall complications remained low at 2.7% by 1 year, with no evidence of a learning curve bias for LRC, lead dislodgement, or procedural times. This study adds to the growing body of literature supporting the use of SDLs for LBBAP, and is the first to provide focused data on the relationship between site volume and procedural outcomes when using the INGEVITY+ lead for LBBAP.
