Prognostic value of nutritional risk assessment indices in patients with digestive system tumors
Authors: Tingting Zeng, Xiaolan Ling, Si Chen, Jianjin Yang, Yuemei Chen, Chunying Zhang, Shanying Deng, Juan Liao, Zhigang Mao, Jianke Yang, Yi He, Liang Du, Yali Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNutritional risk assessment indices impact disease prognosis, yet their prognostic roles in preoperative digestive system tumor (DST) patients remain unclear.MethodsIn this study, the Controlling Nutritional Status (CONUT) score, the Nutritional Risk Index (NRI) and the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) before surgery were applied to 17, 338 patients with 10 kinds of newly diagnosed DSTs. The distribution of nutritional risk and its correlation with mortality were examined in this study through the utilization of three nutritional risk assessment indices.ResultsThe study encompassed 17,338 cases of DSTs, comprising 7,644 cases of gastric cancer, 5,542 cases of esophageal cancer, 2,826 cases of pancreatic cancer, 570 cases of gastroesophageal junction cancer, 185 cases of liver cancer, and minimal instances of five other tumor types, each numbering approximately 100. According to the 3 nutritional risk assessment indices, CONUT, NRI and PNI, nutrition risk was common in patients with DSTs (79.24% with CONUT, 38.91% with NRI, 3.13% with PNI), and even in patients with normal or high BMI. In gastric cancer, in addition to age, stage, presence of metastasis, and tumor tissue type, three nutritional risk assessment indices could effectively stratify the prognosis based on nutritional status. CONUT was valuable for predicting the prognosis of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, whereas NRI was significant in Esophageal Cancer and Pancreatic Cancer.ConclusionsNutritional risk was common among DSTs patients and was strongly associated with increased mortality in GC according to CONUT, NRI and PNI Stratification. More attention should be paid to nutritional risk assessment indices to improve prognosis, and prospective clinical trials were needed to evaluate the efficacy of nutritional interventions in GC patients. However, the utility of these three nutritional risk assessment indices in other DSTs was limited.

Introduction

Tumors remain the primary cause of death and a significant barrier to increasing life expectancy worldwide. In many countries, the increase in tumor-related deaths is partially due to a decrease in mortality from stroke and cardiovascular diseases. Digestive system tumors (DSTs) include tumors from the digestive tract and associated glands. Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most prevalent malignant tumor and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. In 2020, there were 604,100 new cases of esophageal cancer globally, resulting in 544,100 deaths. This corresponds to age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of 6.3 and 5.6 per 100,000, respectively [2]. Pancreatic cancer is projected to become the second most common cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States by 2030[3].

Adequate surgery is still the basic strategy of DSTs treatment [4] and the extent of resection is determined by the disease stage [5]. Numerous factors influence the prognosis of patients with DSTs, such as TNM stage, positive surgical margins, lymphovascular and perineural invasion [6]Nutritional status is a potentially modifiable factor in patient treatment, with inadequate preoperative nutritional status in GC patients impacting complications and survival outcomes [7]. ASPEN and the American Gastroenterological Association recommend the malnutrition Screening 2002 (NRS 2002) for critically ill patients [8, 9]. NRS 2002 includes a nutrition status score, a disease severity score (including cancer), and an age score. Certain indicators, such as decreased food intake, pose challenges in estimation. Factors specific to tumors, such as staging and type, are not accounted for in NRS 2002.

This research selected three nutritional risk assessment indices to evaluate nutritional risk in DST patients. The aim is to identify independent nutritional risk assessment markers effective in predicting the prognosis of individuals with DSTs.

Methods

Subjects and study design

A retrospective study was conducted at West China Hospital, Sichuan University, on patients newly diagnosed with tumors of the digestive system. The inclusion criteria were DSTs confirmed by histopathology; underwent surgical resection. Exclusion criteria were age < 18 years; receiving private health care; previous cancer; oncological treatment started > 1 week before consent; and unable to provide informed consent; exploratory laparotomies or non-curative resections. Patients without measurement of height and weight (n = 69), and those without measurement of albumin level, lymphocyte count, and total cholesterol level (n = 361) before the surgery were excluded from this analysis. The study comprised 17,768 patients consecutively admitted between July 2008 and May 2021. This study finally included 17,338 newly diagnosed gastric cancer patients (Fig. 1). Tumors were classified according to the WHO International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) and staged based on the 8th edition of the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual. Gastrointestinal cancers included gastric cancer (C16), esophageal cancer (C15), pancreatic cancer (C25), gastroesophageal junction cancer (C16), small intestine cancer (C17), colorectal cancer (C18-20), ampulla of Vater cancer (C24.1), intrahepatic bile duct cancer (C22.1) and hepatocellular carcinoma (C22.0). The final cohort consisted of 7,644 gastric cancer cases, 5,542 esophageal cancer cases, 2,826 pancreatic cancer cases, 570 gastroesophageal junction cancer cases, 122 duodenal carcinoma cases, 161 colon cancer cases, 114 ampullary carcinoma cases, 89 intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma cases, 185 liver cancer cases, and 85 cholangiocarcinoma cases. 1363 patients lost follow-up. All work was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University.

The CONUT tool was developed by Ignacio de Ulíbarri and colleagues[10]in 2005, where patients scoring 0 to 1 were categorized as having a normal nutritional status. Scores of 2 to 4, 5 to 8, and 9 to 12 corresponded to mild, moderate, and severe nutritional risk, respectively. Nourishment Risk Index (NRI), a nutritional risk assessment tool that has gained prominence in recent years, was initially established by Buzby et al.[11, 12] in 1988 and has since been employed as a nutritional metric across various medical and surgical patient cohorts[13–15]. The original Nutritional Risk Index (NRI) calculates as follows: 1.519 × serum albumin (g/l) + 41.7 × (current body weight [kg] / ideal body weight [kg]). In this study, the ideal body weight replaced usual body weight, as obtaining usual weight is often unfeasible in elderly patients[16]. NRI formula was defined as 1.489×serum albumin (g/l) + 41.7× (current body weight [kg]/ideal body weight [kg]) in this study[17]. Ideal body weight was determined using the Lorenz formulas based on height. For men, the formula was height [cm] – 100 – ([height – 150] / 4) in kilograms (kg), while for women, it was height [cm] – 100 – ([height – 150] / 2.5) in kilograms (kg)[18]. If the current body weight exceeded the calculated ideal body weight, the body weight-to-ideal body weight ratio was capped at 1[17, 19]. The concept of Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) was initially introduced in 1980 as a nutritional indicator in the prognostic evaluation of gastrointestinal surgeries[20]. PNI is calculated by adding the serum albumin level (g/L) to 5 times the total lymphocyte count (×109/L). Body Mass Index (BMI) is computed as the body mass (kg) divided by the square of the body height (m) and was categorized according to the World Health Organization (WHO) standards.

Endpoints and follow-up

The study endpoint was defined as all-cause mortality. Patient follow-up commenced on the date of admission, with specific time points set at 1-month post-discharge, 2 months post-discharge, and every 6 months thereafter.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with 5% α risk or 95% confidence interval using SPSS version 25 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and R, version 4.0.5 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All P values were 2 sided.

Continuous variables were summarized as a median with interquartile range (25th to 75th percentiles), and categorical data were expressed as n (%). The Mann-Whitney U test is utilized for comparing continuous variables that deviate from a normal distribution. The proportions between groups were compared by the chi-square test. The Kaplan-Meier method was utilized to compare survival curves using the log-rank test by SPSS. While univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted by cox regression analysis by SPSS.

Factors for survival analysis undergo initial univariate screening, with variables showing statistical significance (P<0.05) proceeding to the Multivariate analysis stage. The factors included in the univariate analysis are age, gender, stage, metastasis, tissue group, population, drinking, smoking, CONUT score, NRI score, PNI score, Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), Cancer Antigen 125 (CA-125), cancer antigen 15-3 (CA 15-3), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9), cancer antigen 72-4 (CA 72-4), and Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA). Multivariate analysis initially uses nutritional index values in Cox regression. Significant factors (P<0.2) are included in subsequent regression. Variables with P>0.2 are eliminated, and those with P>0.05 are evaluated for individual impact. Factors with an impact >10% are retained. Finally, the nutritional index is stratified for Cox analysis.

Nutritional risk assessment indices and survival rates

The population was divided into three groups: CONUT#/ NRI# (Severe, Moderate, Mild+Absent). In gastric cancer, factors in the final multivariate analysis included age, tumor stage, metastasis, tumor tissue type, CA125, CA19-9, and CA72-4. HR results are shown in Fig. 3A and C. For esophageal cancer, the multi-factor analysis considered stage, metastasis, CA125, CA15-3, and CEA. For pancreatic cancer, the factors included stage, metastasis, tumor tissue type, population, and CA125. Discussion

Nutritional risk is prevalent in tumor patients, increasing mortality[22]. Nutritional related indices like BMI, blood albumin[23], total protein and total cholesterol[24] assess tumor prognosis. In gastric cancer, nutritional risk assessment indices[25] define nutritional risk severity, linked to poor outcomes. These parameters are easy to obtain and modifiable[26]. This study examines the impact of nutritional risk assessment indices on digestive system tumor prognosis and the potential for nutritional modifications to improve outcomes.

The study included 17,338 cases of digestive system tumors (DSTs): 7,644 gastric cancer, 5,542 esophageal cancer, 2,826 pancreatic cancer, 570 gastroesophageal junction cancer, 185 liver cancer, and around 100 cases of five other tumor types. The baseline comparison focused on gastric cancer. Significant differences in baseline characteristics were noted between different digestive system tumors and gastric cancer. Notably, all liver cancer patients were at stage four, likely due to the rapid progression of liver cancer, making early detection challenging.

The first thing that needed to be emphasized was the prevalence of nutritional risk in DSTs. The prevalence of nutritional risk in DSTs varies significantly due to different calculation methods and laboratory indicators. The CONUT index detected nutritional risk in 74.86% to 89.41% of cases across ten DST types. The NRI found nutritional risk in 34.3% to 57.02% of cases, while the PNI reported a prevalence of 1.01% to 9.65%. These indices did not consistently agree on nutritional risk levels. For example, cholangiocarcinoma had the highest nutritional risk rate with CONUT but ranked fifth with PNI. In our study, 3.31% (PNI) to 79.71% (CONUT) of gastric cancer patients were identified with nutritional risk before surgery. Furthermore, moderate to severe nutritional risk ranged from 3.31% to 12.34%. A few studies had analyzed the prevalence of nutritional risk in patients with GC before. Kuroda et al.[26] reported that 10.7% of patients with GC who underwent gastrectomy were with moderate or severe nutritional risk using CONUT. Ma et al[27]. reported that 33% of patients with metastatic gastric and esophageal adenocarcinoma were moderate or severe nutritional risk using NRI. Kuroda et al. and Ma et al.reported comparable data about prevalence of nutritional risk using CONUT and NRI as in this study. In 2020, Park et al.[28] reported that 22.5% patients with stage II/III GC treated with gastrectomy having low PNI (<49.7). In our study, this ratio is much higher (50.29%), which might relate to the inclusion of patients at stages I and IV. Evidence-based medicine has demonstrated a lack of optimal nutritional support before gastric cancer resection in GC patients[29]. Further high-quality trials are needed to develop specific clinical practice guidelines. Until then, current surgical oncology guidelines should be used.

The second thing that should be highlighted is that the nutritional indices indicated different prognostic capabilities in different digestive system tumors. In gastric cancer, age, stage, metastasis, tumor tissue type, and three nutritional indices effectively stratify prognosis based on nutritional status. However, tumor markers like CA125, CA19-9, and CA72-4 have a minimal influence on prognosis, with HR values close to one. Only CA72-4 shows a modest impact with an HR value of 0.806 in the CONUT grouping analysis. CONUT is valuable for predicting intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma prognosis, while NRI is significant for esophageal and pancreatic cancers. However, predictive efficacy is limited due to overlapping survival curves in esophageal and pancreatic cancers. In other DSTs, nutritional risk assessment indices lack prognostic value, even in gastroesophageal junction cancer, which is closely related to gastric cancer.

Third, as univariable predictors, these three nutritional risk assessment indices had a predictive ability for mortality in gastric cancer that even exceeded age and tumor markers. Recent studies have reported the predictive roles of the three nutritional risk assessment indices separately, but their predictive abilities might be underestimated. Improving the nutritional status of gastric cancer (GC) patients during treatment is crucial. This study used three nutritional risk assessment indices to assess the preoperative nutritional status of ten different digestive system tumors (DSTs) and conducted a comparative analysis. For esophageal cancer patients, NRI-based grouping shows significant prognostic differences between severely nutritional risk patients and others. However, for pancreatic cancer patients, the predictive efficacy of NRI was suboptimal, with considerable overlap between survival curves for severe and mild nutritional risk, indicating a need to refine nutritional risk assessment indices for accurate assessment.

Fourth, CONUT uses a scoring method based on serum albumin concentration, cholesterol concentration, and lymphocyte count for calculation, while PNI formula includes serum albumin concentration and lymphocyte count, and NRI adds serum albumin concentration to weight changes. These confounders mean 3 indices often act as composite markers of disease severity or/and inflammatory state rather than purely nutritional status. All these indicators heavily rely on serum albumin levels. Albumin itself is highly sensitive to inflammation (e.g., from cancer, infection, autoimmune disease), liver disease, and fluid overload, not just nutritional intake. ASPEN and ESPEN now consider it an inflammatory marker. Therefore, in this article, we collectively refer to CONUT, PNI, and NRI as nutritional risk assessment indices.

Fifth, there was a noticeable overlap in the survival curves of COUNT and NRI, potentially due to various factors. The prominent role of albumin in both scoring systems significantly contributes to their correlation. Additionally, patients with severe underlying diseases may score poorly on both assessments due to non-nutritional risk factors, resulting in overlapping survival curves driven by the shared burden of illness. Moreover, methodological constraints, including categorization, threshold selection, population characteristics, and sample size, could hinder the ability to accurately detect subtle differences.

Finally, in this study, apart from gastric cancer, esophageal cancer, pancreatic cancer, and gastroesophageal junction cancer, the number of cases collected was relatively small. It might be necessary to increase the number of cases in the future to supplement the research.

Conclusion

Different levels of nutritional risk were common in DST patients, even with normal or high BMI, and were well identified by CONUT, NRI, and PNI indices. These indices accurately distinguished gastric cancer prognosis based on nutritional status. However, except for NRI in esophageal and pancreatic cancer, these markers do not reliably forecast prognosis in other gastrointestinal malignancies. Further research on alternative nutritional evaluation strategies is needed. These findings highlight the importance of addressing nutritional risk in gastric cancer patients to improve survival rates.
