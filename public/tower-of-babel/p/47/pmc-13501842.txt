CME-KGDTI: integrating clustered mutations into knowledge graph embedding for drug-target interaction prediction
Authors: Jiaming Jin, Xinmiao Zhao, Jiarui Liu, Chengjun Gong, Wanjie Zheng, Guijie Jiang, Changxian Li, Li Guo, Tingming Liang, Xiangcheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputational prediction of drug-target interaction (DTI) is critical for drug discovery and precision medicine. Herein, we constructed a biologically enriched heterogeneous knowledge graph (KG) integrating clustered mutations, synthetic lethal interactions, drug structures, protein sequences, and functional annotations. This multi-dimensional framework was designed to enable the identification of actionable diagnostic signatures and precision therapeutic strategies by leveraging multi-layered biological network factors.ResultsEntities within the KG were embedded into low-dimensional vectors using various graph embedding techniques (TransE, RotatE, DistMult, Node2vec and R-GCN). These multimodal embeddings served as input for deep learning models (DNN, NFM, AutoInt), with standardization and PCA-based dimensionality reduction applied. Under a challenging protein cold-start scenario, the CME-KGDTI model demonstrated a better performance. These results highlight the multi-source biological information in enhancing positive sample identification and overall model generalization. Additionally, the CME-KGDTI platform (https://www.tmliang.cn/cmekgdti/#/home) was developed, integrating resources for clustered mutation identification, cancer-specific SL-based genetic networks, DTI prediction, and multi-omics analysis, enabling users to comprehensively explore mutation detection, target prioritization, and mechanistic insights.ConclusionsBy incorporating biological features, the CME-KGDTI model exhibits high accuracy and robust generalization, highlighting its essential complementary role in drug target discovery. The developed CME-KGDTI platform will serve as a flexible, interactive, and implementable technical support platform, contributing to the advancement of precision oncology research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13040-026-00576-8.

Introduction

Research on drug-target interaction (DTI) is critical to modern drug discovery and the process of precision medicine. Traditional experimental validation methods are hindered by high costs and prolonged timelines [1, 2], making them inefficient for screening the large number of candidate drugs against potential targets. Thus, computational prediction techniques, particularly those utilizing deep learning and knowledge graphs (KGs), have emerged as innovative solutions for drug development. Many deep learning approaches have been proposed for DTI prediction, employing strategies such as molecular graph modeling, sequence feature extraction, and cross-modal attention mechanisms. For example, a deep learning framework based on 3D molecular spatial visual information and multi-perspective representations provides mechanistic insights into drug discovery [3], graph-based prediction of miRNA-drug associations offers a novel method to advance miRNA-drug prediction [4], and GraphDTA employs Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) to extract structural features from drug molecular graphs [5]. DeepDTA, which uses Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to encode SMILES strings and protein sequences separately, is noted for its structural simplicity and computational efficiency [6]. Models such as DeepAffinity [7] and DeepCDA [8] further incorporate attention mechanisms, showing advantages in modeling semantic interactions between drugs and proteins. DTI prediction has garnered widespread attention, and numerous approaches and tools have been developed, including CDI-DTI [9], a cross-domain interpretable framework, MML-DTI [10], CAMA-DTI [11], DCGAT-DTI [12], and DeepMCL-DTI [13]. However, some existing methods are limited to single-modal information inputs, struggling to fully capture the complex biological regulatory mechanisms underlying drug action. Drug efficacy is also influenced by multi-layered biological network factors, such as gene mutations, genetic networks based on interactions of synthetic lethality (SL) and pathway regulation factors, and these are critical to model accurately. Driven by the biological nature of tumor targets, this study employs a task-specific integrated design. Clustered mutations, also referred to as mutation showers, offer a precise fingerprint of mutagenic processes [14] and have gained attention due to their contributions to tumorigenesis [15–18]. Understanding the distinct characteristics and biological processes driving clustered mutagenesis can enhance our systems-level understanding of carcinogenesis and cancer progression, facilitating the identification of actionable diagnostic signatures and precision therapeutic strategies. Synthetic lethality offers a transformative approach for identifying therapeutic targets, and this principle has been extensively studied for anticancer treatment as a promising therapeutic strategy [19–22].

By embedding these specific biological rules into the knowledge graph construction, our model bridges the gap between molecular similarity and mechanism rationality. The core contribution lies in translating tumor-specific biological priors (clustered mutations and synthetic lethality) into computable triplet features, thereby enhancing the biological interpretability and clinical translational potential of target prediction. The Yamanishi_08 dataset [23] was used to construct a DTI prediction model that integrated multi-source heterogeneous data, addressing the limitations of traditional structural and sequence-based information. A heterogeneous knowledge graph, along with graph neural network-based embedding methods, was constructed to combine molecular features with biological prior knowledge. Subsequently, this study designed combinations of multiple deep learning models and systematically compared the performance of 15 model variants for the DTI prediction task. Ablation studies were performed to assess the impact of different feature types and knowledge augmentation strategies (Fig. 1). To ensure the proposed method’s adaptability and robustness in real-world scenarios, all experiments were conducted under a strict protein cold-start setting, simulating predictions for targets unseen during training. Finally, a CME-KGDTI platform (https://www.tmliang.cn/cmekgdti/#/home) was developed, offering an integrated resource that supports clustered mutation identification, cancer type-specific SL network visualization, DTI prediction, and multi-omics differential expression/enrichment analysis, allowing users to conduct comprehensive exploration from mutation detection to target prioritization and mechanistic insights.

Results and discussion

Construction of CME-KGDTI framework

This study proposed a knowledge graph framework enhanced with clustered mutations and SL triples, integrating multi-source heterogeneous data for DTI prediction. The performance of this framework was systematically evaluated across multiple model configurations (Table S1). Analysis of 15 model variants revealed that the node2vec + DNN combination were emerged as the top performer (Fig. 2 and Table S2), achieving the highest average scores across AUROC, AUPR, and F1-score while maintaining a low standard deviation (std), indicating strong performance and robust cross-validation stability. Following closely behind were node2vec + NFM and node2vec+AutoInt, demonstrating that the node2vec embedding method exhibited strong stability and generalization capability. In contrast, some combinations based on R-GCN and TransE ranked relatively low, possibly due to the introduction of excessive relational noise or overfitting risks in the current distribution of graph data with structure-aware embeddings. Notably, although the AutoInt model had advantages in modeling high-order feature interactions, its performance was heavily influenced by embedding quality, yielding suboptimal results under DistMult and RGCN embeddings. In the ablation study, models using only knowledge graph embeddings (without the fusion of traditional structural/sequence features) outperformed those incorporating fusion (Fig. 3A, Tables S3-S4), suggesting potential information redundancy or interference between structural fingerprints and sequence descriptors in this task. This aligns with observations in DeepAffinity and DeepCDA, which noted the diminishing marginal benefits of attention mechanisms for feature interaction [7, 8]. Under the KG-only condition, the inclusion of clustered mutation information provided the most significant performance boost, particularly in AUPR and F1-score (Fig. 3A; Table 1). This suggests that associations between clustered mutation genes and functional annotations offer critical biological insights for predicting novel targets in cold-start scenarios. In protein cold-start experiments (Fig. 3B and Tables S5-S6), the proposed CME-KGDTI model outperformed competing methods (AUPR = 0.765, AUROC = 0.923). The AUROC improvement of CME-KGDTI relative to KGE_NFM was marginal and not statistically significant, and should be interpreted with caution; while the advantage on the AUPR metric provided more sufficient and statistically validated supporting evidence, which better reflected the performance superiority of CME-KGDTI in the protein cold start scenario. The input features of CME-KGDTI were entirely derived from the Node2vec embeddings trained on the cluster mutation-enhanced heterogeneous knowledge graph (34,568 nodes, 217,675 edges, including 368 cluster mutation triplets). The fused embedding vectors of drugs and proteins were reduced to 256 dimensions via PCA and then concatenated, which were input into the DNN classifier. Additionally, the optimal classification threshold was dynamically determined by maximizing the F1 score on each fold of the validation set. This outcome highlights that traditional regression-threshold dynamic selection strategies, which convert affinity regression into classification predictions, struggle with cold-start challenges when underlying feature representations are insufficient. In contrast, KG embeddings enhanced by clustered mutations not only improve positive sample ranking (AUPR) but also enhance model robustness. Detailed information on the hyperparameters for the models in the study was also provided (Tables S7-S9). The final model, CME-KGDTI, was selected based on a comprehensive ranking across three metrics: AUROC, AUPR, and F1-score, derived from ablation studies. Consequently, Base_B_Cluster (Node2vec embedding + DNN predictor + KG-only features + cluster-mutation augmented triplets) was designated as the final model.

Results of the ablation study on enhanced triplets

Compared to earlier single-modality methods, our findings demonstrate that incorporating biologically grounded triplets, including clustered mutations and genetic interactions based on synthetic lethality, significantly enhances the predictive performance of KG-enhanced models. Specifically, the integration of clustered mutation information showed marked advantages across multiple metrics, further validating its potential in complex task scenarios. This is consistent with the preliminary findings on knowledge graph embedding [23], which emphasize that integrating disease mechanism-related information improves DTI prediction. Clustered mutations, a common phenomenon in cancer genomes, play a pivotal role in cancer development and progression [14, 18], thereby contributing to the identification of potential drug targets. The concept of synthetic lethality has been widely explored for developing targeted therapies [24, 25], holding significant promise in driving the discovery and identification of genetic cancer targets and effective drugs. Introducing biological information such as clustered mutations and synthetic lethality into drug target prediction demonstrates promising application prospects, providing a referenceable technical approach for subsequent target discovery and individualized therapy research.

The DTI prediction field continues to evolve, with recent models proposing diverse technical approaches. For example, DTIAM [26] employs self-supervised learning to jointly model DTI, binding affinity, and mechanism of action, showing robust generalization in cold-start scenarios. DrugAgent [27] combines large language model (LLM) reasoning with multi-agent systems, emphasizing interpretability and drug repositioning. GraphBAN [28] leverages graph-based inductive learning to enhance rapid prediction for new compounds/proteins, particularly useful with limited structural data. The CME-KGDTI framework focuses on integrating tumor-specific features, specifically clustered mutations and SL triples. This targeted multi-modal feature integration may enhance performance in the high-complexity task of protein cold-start target prediction by incorporating biological interpretability. Our framework holds potential for identifying drug targets based on specific biological features, such as the crucial roles of clustered mutations in tumorigenesis [15, 18], and the important clinical application of genetic interactions based on the concept of synthetic lethality [29, 30]. Herein, on the basis of existing knowledge graph representation learning and downstream prediction frameworks, a task driven integrated design centered on biological mechanisms is carried out around the specific scientific problem of tumor drug target prediction. However, this study still has some limitations that need improvement. Despite significant performance improvements achieved through multi-source information integration, redundancy and collinearity among multi-source features remain an issue. Future work will incorporate feature selection mechanisms or adaptive weighting strategies, utilizing attention mechanisms to better control the impact of noisy features, thereby enhancing both the expressive power and generalization performance of the model. Regarding the modeling of the synthetic lethality mechanism, the study highlighted its limited role in the drug-target prediction task, though its potential value should not be underestimated. Synthetic lethality is more suitable for modeling binary gene interactions and screening combinatorial targets. Future research could explore tasks such as combinatorial drug response prediction and multi-gene interaction modeling to optimize the optimal application strategy of SL information within frameworks like graph neural networks and graph contrastive learning. Additionally, the SL gene screening criteria should be tailored for different cancer types to improve their tissue/disease specificity matching, thereby reducing signal redundancy and uncertainty.

Case validation and construction of the integrated CME-KGDTI platform

To validate the applicability of the CME-KGDTI model in real biological contexts, an in-depth analysis was performed on key cancer-related genes, EGFR (hsa:1956) and HER2 (ERBB2, hsa:2064). To confirm the validity of this case study under the cold-start protocol, EGFR and HER2 were verified to have no sample-level overlap across all 10 cross-validation folds, with zero drug ID overlap under the same target protein in train/test splits. EGFR and HER2 are two well-known cancer targets, and their abnormal expression patterns and mutation landscapes suggest potential contributions to cancer diagnosis, therapy, and prognosis (Fig. 4A and B, S1 and 6). For example, targeting the SYVN1-EGFR axis has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy for TKI-resistant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [31], while targeting the MKK3/c-Myc interaction can overcome osimertinib acquired resistance in EGFR mutant lung cancer [32], highlighting its clinical potential in anticancer therapeutics. The CME-KGDTI model successfully predicted the related drugs for these two cancer-related genes (Fig. 4C). For EGFR, the top-ranked drugs included clinically established tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as Gefitinib, Erlotinib, and Lapatinib [33–35], along with investigational candidates like Canertinib and Mubritinib. The successful prediction of Gefitinib and Erlotinib, both standard treatments for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), was consistent with their known EGFR inhibitory activity [33]. Lapatinib, a dual EGFR/HER2 inhibitor, also ranked highly. However, the high scores for Imatinib, Bosutinib, and Axitinib—drugs primarily targeting kinases like BCR-ABL, c-KIT, Src, or VEGFR, but without strong evidence for direct EGFR inhibition—suggested potential false positives possibly arising from structural similarities within the kinase family. For HER2, Lapatinib [36] was accurately predicted as well. Overall, this case validation not only supports the significance of clustered mutations as effective signals for target identification but also confirms the scientific rationale and practical value of the model, both in terms of prediction performance and biological interpretation.

To facilitate efficient translation and reuse of our findings, a comprehensive CME-KGDTI platform (https://www.tmliang.cn/cmekgdti/#/home) was developed. The platform established a relatively complete closed-loop design in three aspects: resource integration, interface experience, and model deployment. It offers an integrated, user-friendly solution that spans the entire workflow, from somatic clustered mutation detection and SL network construction, to DTI prediction and multi-omics functional annotation analysis (Fig. 5), providing a one-stop online analysis resource. Users can access related DTIs, explore the SL Network model to understand the genetic network associated with the gene of interest based on SL, visit the CMCS platform [37] to survey clustered mutations, and perform multi-omics analysis using the Omics Tool module (Figure S3). CME-KGDTI is designed to offer an intuitive and integrated platform for drug-target discovery, and also provide tools to explore clustered mutations, SL-related genetic networks, and molecular features across pan-cancers. This will help address the clinical potential of specific genes as drug targets, particularly those involving clustered mutations and SL-based genetic networks. CME-KGDTI bridges the current gap in the field by integrating clustered mutations and synthetic lethality with KG-enhanced target prediction, coupled with a visual multi-omics analysis platform. It provides a flexible, interactive, and implementable technical support platform for precision oncology research.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily demonstrated the potential feasibility of a multi-modal knowledge graph enhancement strategy in improving accuracy and generalization capability of drug-target interaction prediction. The CME-KGDTI platform aims to provide an integrated resource for exploring the clinical potential of specific genes as drug targets based on molecular features in cancer.

Materials and methods

Data resource and data preprocessing

A knowledge graph was constructed based on the drug–target interaction dataset (Yamanishi_08) [23], which includes four sub-datasets (enzyme, ion channel, GPCR, nuclear receptor) and is widely used for benchmarking DTI models. The heterogeneous KG version integrates multi-source relationships, such as drug–disease, protein–functional domain, and protein–pathway, sourced from databases including KEGG, DrugBank, UniProt, and InterPro. This graph contains 25,487 nodes and 95,579 edges [23, 38]. To enhance the biological context of the graph and improve the model’s generalization capability in drug target prediction tasks, we further incorporated two types of biologically relevant relationships associated with cancer, synthetic lethal interactions [39] and pathway/structural functional associations of clustered mutation genes [37]. These additional triplets enrich the knowledge graph, providing deeper semantic support for subsequent representation learning and drug target prediction.

The synthetic lethality subgraph contained 121,728 triplets involving 8,951 unique gene nodes. The clustered mutation subgraph included 130 standard human genes and 76 functional/pathway nodes, totaling 206 entities [37]. These entities were fully connected by 368 HAS_CLUSTER_MUTATION_IN_DOMAIN triplets, where a single gene node could map to multiple functional domains, reflecting the pleiotropic biological characteristics of mutation clusters (Figures S4-S6, Table S1). To better understand the molecular characteristics of potential targets, particularly multiomics features, multiomics data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) across pan-cancers. Additionally, the three-dimensional structures of some specific targets were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database [40], then visualized and rendered by PyMOL [41].

To fully capitalize on the advantages and complementarity of different feature types, this study designed a comprehensive multi-source information integration framework (Fig. 1) that converted discrete biological data into vector representations, combining KG features with drug fingerprints and protein sequence features. In addition to feature extraction for drugs and proteins, a KG was introduced to capture the complexity of real-world drug–protein interaction networks. KGs stored data as triplets, providing an intuitive way to represent intricate relationships among drugs, proteins, and their interactions in a network structure. However, raw graph data were inherently discrete and could not be directly input into machine learning models. To effectively transform the structural information and biological relationships from heterogeneous KGs into low-dimensional vector representations suitable for modeling, this study employed multiple mainstream embedding methods to train entities and relationships within the graph. These methods included triplet-based embeddings (e.g., TransE, DistMult, RotatE), structure-agnostic random walk strategies (Node2vec), and R-GCN. The resulting embedding vectors for protein entities were concatenated with their structural features to serve as input for downstream predictive models. To systematically evaluate the impact of graph enhancement strategies, all embedding methods were trained on both the base graph and the enhanced graph (incorporating clustered mutation and synthetic lethality triplets), with performance comparisons to assess the effects of graph enrichment. The multi-source feature vectors for drugs and proteins were fused via concatenation, combining graph embeddings with structural features to form a unified input representation. To ensure uniform numerical scales across features from different sources and mitigate bias in data distribution, the fused feature vectors were standardized. Each data source was standardized so that all features followed a normal distribution with zero mean and unit variance, preventing excessive influence from features with large or small value ranges. This normalization also improved model convergence speed, facilitating the effective fusion of features from diverse data sources on a uniform scale. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to reduce the fused high-dimensional feature vectors to 256 dimensions, following standardization. This dimension choice was based on experimental validation results, significantly reducing computational overhead while preserving information and minimizing the risk of overfitting from excessively high feature dimensionality. PCA also addressed multicollinearity issues between features, enabling the model to capture independent and critical feature patterns. Standardization and PCA were fitted exclusively on the training data within each fold, with the learned transformation applied to the validation/test data. Through the design and implementation of this feature engineering approach, this study generated low-dimensional, highly expressive, and biologically meaningful feature vectors, providing a solid data foundation for the efficient training and prediction in subsequent deep learning models.

Interaction data construction and model training

After feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, and fusion for drugs and proteins, a complete interaction dataset was constructed to enable accurate prediction of DTIs, meeting the input requirements for deep learning models. The drug–target interaction dataset, sourced from a published study [38], contained experimentally validated positive interaction pairs and negative samples generated through a standard construction strategy. For each drug, negative pairs were generated by randomly sampling proteins with no documented interactions, with negative samples set to ~ 10 times the positives. We verified zero overlap between positive and negative sets across folds to ensure integrity. Reflecting real-world sparsity, the dataset maintained a ~ 1:9 positive-to-negative ratio. Unrecorded pairs were used only as a sampling pool, not definitive negatives, to mitigate false negatives.

The interaction feature matrix was built based on this data framework, integrating the multi-source information and KG enhancement methods proposed in this study, for downstream predictive model training and evaluation. This approach ensured experimental reproducibility and facilitated a fair comparison with existing DTI methods, particularly KGE_NFM [38], under the same data context. Given the complex, nonlinear relationships between various features in DTI prediction, it is crucial to select a model capable of efficiently capturing high-order feature interactions. In this study, the DNN model was employed, known for its performance in feature interaction modeling, along with Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Neurofactorization Machine (NFM), and Self-Attention Interaction Network (AutoInt), to predict drug-target affinity. To thoroughly evaluate the model’s generalization capability and robustness, 10-fold cross-validation was used. For each fold, separate training and test datasets were constructed, and model training and evaluation were conducted independently. Within each fold of cross-validation, a temporary knowledge graph that excluded test-side information was dynamically constructed. Specifically, based on the partition of that fold, all DTI triples belonging to the test set were first removed from the complete graph, retaining only the DTI edges from the training set as well as other auxiliary relationship edges, thereby forming a “training knowledge graph” that contained no test labels whatsoever. Additionally, to optimize classification performance, a data-driven automatic threshold determination strategy was employed in each cross-validation fold. This strategy dynamically selects the optimal threshold by maximizing the F1 score on the validation set, converting continuous output values into final binary classification results. This rigorous validation approach ensures the stability and reliability of the model predictions. Finally, widely recognized evaluation metrics were used to comprehensively assess the model’s performance, including Area Under the ROC Curve (ROC-AUC), Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and the overall F1 Score.

Optimal thresholds determination

In the drug-target affinity prediction task of this study, the core objective of the model is to quantitatively evaluate the strength of the interaction between the drug and the target protein, specifically the binding affinity [42]. Binding affinity is typically represented as continuous numerical values, such as dissociation constant, half-maximal inhibitory concentration, or inhibition constant. However, practical application scenarios often require a binary classification to determine whether a biologically meaningful interaction exists between the drug and protein. To address this, this study extended the SimBoost model [43], which was originally designed for regression prediction of binding affinity using the similarity of drug and protein features. A data-driven dynamic threshold selection strategy was adopted to convert the continuous prediction output into binary classification results. This threshold selection method aimed to dynamically determine the optimal classification threshold that maximized model performance. Given that different datasets had varying distributions of affinity values, a flexible threshold determination strategy was employed instead of a fixed artificial threshold. By using the continuous affinity scores predicted by the model and the true affinity values, multiple candidate thresholds were generated on the validation dataset. These thresholds were uniformly distributed within the range of predicted continuous values. Each threshold was then used sequentially to binarize the continuous prediction values:

By iterating through each candidate threshold and recording F1 Score, the threshold that maximized the F1 Score was selected as the optimal classification threshold. To ensure the generalizability and stability of this threshold across different data splits, a ten-fold cross-validation method was employed. Finally, the definitive binary classification decision threshold was determined by aggregating the thresholds from all folds, either by calculating the mean threshold or selecting the threshold with the most robust performance. This combination of multi-fold cross-validation and data-driven threshold selection can ensure the model’s generalization capability and robustness in practical predictions. Regarding the classification threshold determination and final model selection criteria, we employed a data-driven dynamic threshold selection strategy in each cross-validation fold. Specifically, we traversed a candidate threshold interval for the model’s predicted scores on the validation set and selected the threshold that maximized the F1-score. This optimal threshold was then applied to convert continuous predictions into binary classification results. This adaptive approach accommodates varying data distributions and mitigates performance degradation caused by fixed thresholds.

Construction of CME-KGDTI

CME-KGDTI was developed as an integrative database for related studies, combining deep-learning–based predictions, cluster-mutation analysis (CMCS), a synthetic-lethality network, and multi-omics modules with interactive visualization. The frontend of CME-KGDTI was built using Vue2 and Element UI, bundled via npm and served by Nginx on Linux. The backend utilized SpringBoot with Maven, packaged as a standalone JAR and deployed on Linux to handle data processing, user/role and permission management, and RESTful APIs.
