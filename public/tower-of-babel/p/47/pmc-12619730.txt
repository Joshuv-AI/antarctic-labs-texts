Current scenario of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in adult patients with relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia: a narrative review
Authors: Fen Huang, Kaipo Yang, Jing Du, Li Xuan, Qifa Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is the most common type of acute leukemia, of which relapsed/refractory AML (R/R AML) is the major cause of death in these patients. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains to be the mainstay curative treatment for patients with R/R AML. However, the prognosis of R/R AML is still unsatisfactory. Systematic analysis of the clinical-biological management and characteristics throughout the HSCT process in R/R AML patients may break through the current predicament of poor prognosis. This review provides a holistic analysis of the complete clinical management process for R/R AML, from disease mechanisms to post-transplantation therapies. Novel conditioning and post-transplantation regimens incorporating target/immune-based therapies have improved outcomes of HSCT in R/R AML patients. The widespread adoption of haploidentical transplantation has ended the era of donor scarcity, while donor switching remain a critical clinical outcome for patients with second-transplantation. This study provides guidance for clinical decision-making, though further optimization of prognostic models and integration of novel biomarkers are needed to advance personalized treatment strategies for R/R AML patients in the future.Keywords: Relapsed/refractory AML, Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Clinical management, Conditioning regimen, Therapy, Prognosis

Introduction

AML is one of the most common acute leukemia with high heterogenicity in adults. Despite improvements in the AML therapeutic landscape, about 30% of AML patients failed to achieve complete response (CR) and up to 50% will go to relapse [1]. R/R AML remains limited in terms of improvement in prognosis, with mortality rate of 81.9% [2]. Primary refractory AML is defined as failure to achieve CR after 2 cycles of intensive chemotherapy [3]. While 10–40% of younger AML patients are primarily refractory to AML induction therapy, and relapsed AML as bone marrow leukemic blasts ≥ 5%; or reappearance of blasts in the peripheral blood; or development of new extramedullary disease [4]. Allo-HSCT remains to be main curative treatment for patients with R/R AML. Compared to conventional consolidation chemotherapy, allo-HSCT is associated with a significantly lower 2-year cumulative incidence of relapse (20% vs. 58%, p < 0.001) and a higher 2-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate (69% vs. 40%, p = 0.001) [5]. Another study demonstrated that allo-HSCT was associated with a time-dependent survival benefit, resulting in a superior 5-year OS of 29% compared to 13.8% with chemotherapy. However the allo-HSCT cohort having a higher prevalence of treatment-related mortality (TRM) in the first 9 months post-CR1 [6]. This increased risk can compromise the survival benefit afforded by allo-HSCT, underscoring the imperative for optimal clinical management in patients with R/R AML undergoing this procedure. Thus, it is imperative to understand and constantly improve the current treatment landscape of allo-HSCT in patients with R/R AML. In the current review, we aim to discuss recent advances in allo-HSCT for patients with R/R AML, starting from mechanism of disease to complete process of clinical management in HSCT, especially focusing on recent updates on prognostic factors, conditioning regimens, maintenance therapy, pre-emptive therapy, and post relapse therapy, thus providing support for clinicians in their decision-making process.

Potential mechanisms for R/R AML

Understanding the mechanisms of relapse and refractory would be of fundamental importance for the development of novel therapeutic and/or prophylactic strategies [7]. From tumor intrinsic mechanisms aspect, clonal evolution which includes new oncogenes proliferation and tumor suppressor genes loss could lead to relapse [8]. HLA loss or downregulation of HLA-II, upregulation of immune checkpoint ligands, promotion of NK cell inhibitory receptors and down-regulation of NK cell activating receptors are recognized as main reasons for immune escape [9–14]. From tumor extrinsic mechanisms, production of immunosuppressive proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, IL-4 etc., and inhibition of proinflammatory cytokines such as G-CSF, IL-15 etc. could accelerate disease progression [9, 15, 16]. Also, production of immunosuppressive enzymes including IDO1, CD39 and CD73 were also reported [17–19]. Prognostic factors for R/R AML patients receiving HSCT

High-risk karyotype, prior grade III-IV graft versus host disease (GVHD), high percentage bone marrow blast before HSCT, shorter first transplantation to recurrence period, high hematopoietic cell transplantation-specific comorbidity index (HCT-CI) score, worse performance status and older age were the most common inferior prognostic factors reported in R/R AML patients receiving first HSCT [2, 20–25]. A study involving 200 R/R AML patients identified age >55 years, a white blood cell (WBC) count at diagnosis < 4.0 × 10⁹/L, and time from AML diagnosis to HSCT >250 days as independent predictors of poor overall survival (OS). Low WBC counts were also linked to worse leukemia-free survival (LFS). A prognostic score model based on these factors stratified patients into low-risk (0 points), medium-risk (1–2 points), and high-risk (3 points) groups, with strong predictive accuracy (AUROC: 0.783). Additionally, low WBC counts were associated with higher infection-related mortality, highlighting the need for improved immune management [26].

Systemic prognostic model which integrates various prognostic factors to comprehensively predict patient outcomes is imperative but still under exploration. Breem et al. incorporated Elderly Prognostic Index (EPI) score and determined that shorter CR1 duration, increasing age at the time of relapse, non-favorable karyotype at initial diagnosis, or history of prior allo-HSCT were associated with poor survival outcomes in first relapse AML patients (Table 1). Favorable prognostic group reported 5-year OS rate of 46%, comparing to intermediate risk group with 18% and adverse risk group with 4% [14].The scoring system by Breem et al. was recognized by ESMO clinical practice guidelines for diagnosis, treatment and follow-up of AML is adult patients [2, 15]. A recent study by Lu et al. demonstrated minimal residual disease (MRD)-negative CR, HCT-CI score of 0, and new mismatched haploidentical as predictive factors of improved OS and LFS in second allo-HSCT. Based on a predictive scoring system combining these three factors, patients with a prognostic score of 3 showed a superior 2-year OS of 63.3%, LFS of 63.3% and a lower cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) of 5.5% than patients with a prognostic score of 0 [16]. Park et al. developed a prognostic model including factors such as intermediate and poor cytogenetic risk at diagnosis (0.5 and 1 point), peripheral blast ≥ 20% at relapse (1 point), duration from the first transplantation to relapse < 9 months (1 point), and failure to achieve morphologic CR after second allo-HSCT (1 point). Patients achieving a score ≥ 2 were classified as high-risk group. The model identified 2 subgroups according to the 4-year OS rate: 51.3% in the low-risk group (score < 2) and 2.8% in the high-risk group (score ≥ 2) (P < 0.001) [27]. Prognostic factors in second HSCT of R/R AML have also been reported. Commonly recognized inferior prognostic factors include non-complete remission (CR) status before second HSCT, shorter time from first allo-HSCT to relapse, non-myeloablative conditioning (MAC) regimen and worse performance status [12, 13]. Additionally, in AML patients, replacement of a suitable donor for a second HSCT is a critical therapeutic strategy, particularly for those with primary transplant failure or relapse. A retrospective study showed that compared to using the same donor for second transplantation, switching to a different donor was associated with significantly improved neutrophil engraftment and platelet recovery, as well as lower 1-year transplant-related mortality and superior OS. Therefore, effective donor replacement is critical in prognostic model construction for the second HSCT [28].

However, previous prediction models have several limitations: (1) the construction of prediction models is often based on a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the finding, (2) the included population is not specific for AML, and disease-specific factors may influence outcomes but were not fully explored, (3) most studies did not incorporate molecular or cytogenetic markers beyond the Disease Risk Index (DRI), which might provide additional prognostic insights, especially for diseases like AML, (4) some studies assessed prognosis mainly based on short-term survival, long-term outcomes such as relapse and chronic GVHD require further evaluation, (5) demographics, disease characteristics, treatment heterogeneity and availability of healthcare resources in patient could influence the applicability of the study findings to other settings. Artificial intelligence-based model construction represents a new direction for the future [29]. Machine and deep learning may offer significant advantages in AML prognostic evaluation, including the ability to integrate multimodal data, automate feature selection, identify new prognostic markers, and provide personalized risk assessments. For example, a machine learning-based AML prognostic model found that mutations in U2AF1, SF3B1, and IKZF1 were significantly associated with lower odds of achieving CR and 2-year OS [30]. These findings will provide new insights into the prognostic assessment of R/R AML patients receiving HSCT.

Complete process of clinical management in R/R AML

Pre-HSCT management in R/R AML

Induction therapy and timing of first allo-HSCT

The induction chemotherapy regimens for AML patients prior to transplantation are essentially consistent with those for AML patients not intended for transplantation, with the goal of rapidly achieving complete remission (CR) to reduce tumor burden before transplantation and improve success of transplantation. For decades, the daunorubicin and cytarabine regimen (also known as the “7 + 3” regimen) has remained the standard induction therapy for adult AML, particularly in younger patients.

For elderly patients or those with multiple comorbidities who cannot tolerate intensive chemotherapy, the goal of the induction regimen is also to strive for CR and create conditions for transplantation. In the VIALE-A trial involving 431 untreated AML patients ineligible for intensive chemotherapy, the combination of azacitidine and venetoclax significantly improved efficacy outcomes compared to azacitidine alone, demonstrating a superior median overall survival (14.7 vs. 9.6 months; HR 0.66, P < 0.001) and higher rates of CR (36.7% vs. 17.9%) and composite complete remission (66.4% vs. 28.3%) [31]. Another phase 3 study evaluated venetoclax plus low-dose cytarabine (LDAC) versus LDAC alone in AML patients ineligible for intensive chemotherapy. While the planned primary analysis showed a non-significant trend toward improved OS with the combination (median OS 7.2 vs. 4.1 months; HR 0.75; P = 0.11), an unplanned analysis after an additional 6-month follow-up demonstrated a statistically significant survival benefit. The combination also resulted in a markedly higher composite complete remission rate of 48% compared to 13% with LDAC alone, with a manageable safety profile characterized by higher but manageable rates of grade ≥ 3 neutropenia (47% vs. 16%) and febrile neutropenia (32% vs. 29%), confirming venetoclax plus LDAC as a clinically meaningful frontline treatment option for this patient population [32].

For patients with definite gene mutations, target therapy are used before transplantation. In a phase 3 trial involving 717 adults with newly diagnosed FLT3-mutated AML, the addition of the multitargeted kinase inhibitor midostaurin to standard chemotherapy significantly improved survival outcomes compared to chemotherapy plus placebo, demonstrating a statistically significant prolongation of both overall survival and event-free survival, with the benefit being consistent across all FLT3 mutation subtypes and a similar rate of severe adverse events between the two groups [33]. In a phase 3 trial involving patients with newly diagnosed IDH1-mutated AML who were ineligible for intensive chemotherapy, the addition of the mutant IDH1 inhibitor ivosidenib to azacitidine resulted in statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in outcomes compared to placebo plus azacitidine, demonstrating a substantial prolongation of event-free survival with 12-month event-free rates of 37% versus 12%, and a markedly improved median overall survival, alongside a distinct safety profile that featured lower rates of febrile neutropenia and infections but higher rates of neutropenia and bleeding events with the combination therapy [34]. The outcome of induction therapy, whether utilizing intensive chemotherapy or novel targeted combinations, is to achieve a deep remission, thereby establishing the critical foundation for a successful transplant. Future management requires personalized selection based on the patient’s clinical characteristics and physiological status, which inherently includes determining the optimal timing for transplantation.

The timing of allo-HSCT for R/R AML has been discussed for years. A study from the ALWP of the EBMT analyzed outcomes of HSCT in 1041 patients with refractory AML. In multivariate analysis, time from diagnosis of refractory disease to transplant was positively associated with LFS and relapse incidence [35]. A randomized phase III study with 281 patients with R/R AML firstly reported that CR induction intensive chemotherapy before allo-HSCT did not improve OS and LFS, while direct sequential conditioning and allo-HSCT resulted in comparable overall CR rates and survival [36]. Furthermore, studies comparing patients with early pre-emptive allo-HCT (within 4 weeks of reinduction chemotherapy, prior to count recovery) and late pre-emptive allo-HCT (4 weeks after reinduction chemotherapy, with count recovery) showed similar OS and PFS at 2 years [37]. Therefore, primary refractory patients with AML are recommended to conduct early allo-HSCT, while relapsed patients with AML could go to one cycle induction therapy following allo-HSCT. More studies are anticipated to discuss the timing of first allo-HSCT.

Donor selection

HLA typing plays a critical role in donor selection for allo-HSCT. Current donor selection algorithms prioritize donors as follows: the first choice is HLA-matched sibling donor (MSD), followed by matched unrelated donor (MUD) who is fully matched at HLA-A, -B, -C, and -DRB1. Other options include mismatched unrelated donor (MMUD), haploidentical donors (Haplo), and umbilical cord blood (UCB) [38, 39].

Whether R/R AML could benefit from Haplo or MUD HSCT is still controversial. A study comparing HSCT between MSD and MUD in primary R/R AML showed no differences in LFS and OS rates at 2 years, while severe grade III–IV acute GVHD (aGVHD) and 2 years cumulative incidence of chronic GVHD (cGVHD) also showed no difference between the two approaches of HSCT [35]. In a multicenter study, the LFS rates at 2 years of recipients of grafts from the haploidentical donor, unrelated 10/10-matched donor and unrelated 9/10-mismatched donor were no significant differences and showed comparable rates of LFS, OS, relapse, non-relapse mortality, or graft-versus-host disease-free relapse. Another comparation between Haplo-HSCT and MSD from two prospective studies, showed that Haplo-HSCT has lower relapse, higher infection-related mortality and similar survival rates in refractory acute leukemia by the strategy of sequential intensified conditioning followed by DLI compared with MSD [40]. 2021 consensus from The Chinese Society of Hematology recognized Haplo-HSCT with a lower incidence of relapse than MSD-HSCT in high-risk hematological malignancy patients [41]. Most of these studies were retrospective and may have introduced selection bias, particularly as donor/recipient age can affect transplant outcomes. Additionally, heterogeneity in pre- and post-transplantation treatment regimens among patients could influence the clinical outcomes. Future studies should aim to standardize inclusion/exclusion criteria, data collection, and treatment regimen to minimize selection bias and confounding factors.

In addition, age and gender are also key factors for donor selection. Studies have shown that an increase of 10 years in donor age is associated with poorer OS and PFS, as well as a higher risk of GVHD [42]. When multiple Haplo are available, the youngest adult donor with a compatible body size should be prioritized. The combination of donor and recipient gender has a significant impact on patient survival outcomes, particularly when the donor is female and the recipient is male, which is associated with a poorer prognosis [43].

In allo-HSCT for R/R AML, donor selection is crucial. Priority is given to HLA fully matched sibling or unrelated donors, while selecting younger and gender-matched donors helps improve transplantation success rates and reduce complications.

Conditioning regimen

Developing an optimal conditioning regimen that offers superior antileukemic activity with tolerable toxicity for allo-HSCT patients is challenging [44]. Several agents including chemotherapy agents such as clofarabine, cladribine, decitabine etc., monoclonal antibody such as daratumumab, and newer agents such as anti-CD45 Iodine (131I) Apamistamab (Iomab-B) were tested for conditioning regimens. In a prospective, single-arm, multicenter clinical trial, a novel conditioning regimen featuring melphalan/cladribine/busulfan/cyclophosphamide (MCBC) regimen based on the busulfan/cyclophosphamide regimen enhanced the anti-leukemia activity versus conditioning regimen(busulfan and melphalan, combinded with fludarabine), with all patients achieving hematology remission and full donor chimerism on the day of reconstitution after HSCT [51]. CASSIOPEIA trial demonstrated that daratumumab increased the rate of complete response in patients with multiple myeloma when combinded to Bortezomib, thalidomide, and dexamethasone (VTd) before and after allo-HSCT [54]. As a radioimmunoconjugate to CD45, Iomab-B-based conditioning resulted in a statistically significant improvement in durable complete remission (dCR) at 6 months compared with R/R AML patients with conventional care, and Iomab-B therapy safely delivered high doses of targeted radiation to leukemia cells [55].

Bridging therapy can also improve the prognosis of patients after HSCT. A retrospective analysis showed that patients who received CD19 CAR-T therapy bridging with allo-HSCT achieved a 1-year OS of 70% and a 1-year LFS of 95%, significantly higher than those treated with CAR-T alone [56]. In a phase I study, all patients treated with bridging haploidentical HSCT after CAR-T therapy achieved 100% MRD-CR/CRi and significantly improved median PFS (300 days) and OS (348 days) [57]. A Phase I study explored AVC-101 (UniCAR-T-CD123) as a bridging therapy for R/R AML patients with CD123 expression, including those eligible for allo-HSCT. The ORR was 53% in R/R AML and 75% in MRD-positive AML [58]. CPX-351, a liposomal formulation of cytarabine and daunorubicin, delivers these agents at a fixed synergistic ratio, enhancing efficacy and reducing toxicity. Recent trials combining CPX-351 with agents such as venetoclax, gemtuzumab ozogamicin, and gilteritinib have been designed to improve response rates, achieve MRD-negative status, and make allo-HSCT available to more patients [59].

BCL-2 inhibitors have become a cornerstone in the treatment of R/R AML, especially when combined with hypomethylating agents or chemotherapeutics. By targeting anti-apoptotic pathways, these agents have shown significant efficacy in overcoming resistance and improving remission rates. A study evaluated lisaftoclax, a novel BCL-2 inhibitor, alone or combined with azacitidine or homoharringtonine in R/R AML and related myeloid neoplasms. In total, 115 patients, including 64 with R/R AML, were enrolled. In the R/R AML cohort, the ORR was 75%, and the CRc was 44.4%, with a median PFS of 10.22 months [60]. A multicenter Phase 2 trial investigated the efficacy and safety of the VAH regimen (venetoclax, azacitidine, and homoharringtonine) in R/R AML. A total of 96 patients were enrolled, including 37 primary refractory AML and 59 relapsed AML. The composite complete remission rate (CRc) was 70.8%, and MRD-negative status achieved in 58.8% of CRc patients [61]. A Phase I study evaluated the safety and efficacy of adding navitoclax to venetoclax and decitabine in heavily pretreated R/R AML patients previously treated with venetoclax regimens. Among 15 evaluable patients, the composite remission rate (CRh/CRi) was 20%, with 60% achieving a reduction in marrow blasts, and three proceeding to allo-HCT. At a median follow-up of 3.5 months, the 3-month survival rate was 92%, and 6-month survival was 54% [62].

KIT mutations are known to adversely impact the prognosis of core-binding factor AML (CBF-AML), despite its generally favorable outcomes. Targeting KIT mutations with tyrosine kinase inhibitors has emerged as a potential strategy to overcome this challenge, particularly in R/R AML. A study evaluated the efficacy and safety of avapritinib, a KIT-targeting tyrosine kinase inhibitor, in CBF-AML patients with KIT mutations, with notable outcomes in R/R AML. Among 40 patients, 13 had R/R AML, achieving an ORR of 46.2%, with 5 responders successfully undergoing allo-HSCT and achieving MRD negativity [63].

In allo-HSCT for R/R AML, optimizing conditioning regimens is crucial to enhancing antileukemic efficacy while reducing toxicity. Sequential and FLAMSA-based conditioning protocols have demonstrated lower relapse rates and improved OS, while the incorporation of novel agents such as biological/target agents and liposomal formulation further improve engraftment and remission. Additionally, CAR-T cell therapy as a bridging strategy shows potential in reducing MRD and improving post-transplant outcomes. Clinical decisions should be tailored to individual patient characteristics, such as disease status and genetic mutations, using intensified or targeted conditioning regimens and leveraging innovative therapies to optimize transplant outcomes and improve survival.

Post-HSCT management in R/R AML

Post HSCT maintenance and pre-emptive treatment which are used for prophylaxis have received significant attention lately. Usually, maintenance therapy refers to early post-HSCT therapy initiated while patient is still in MRD negativity, and pre-emptive therapy is initiated upon detection of MRD [64]. Maintenance therapy and pre-emptive therapy

Here we reviewed clinical studies focusing on patients with R/R AML. Patient characteristics among high-risk patients with AML often overlap with patients having R/R AML, and hence we also reviewed studies related to high-risk AML [65]. Several studies focusing on newer agents which are used in non-R/R AML patients were also included, since these agents are also preferred in patients with R/R AML.

Hypomethylating agents (HMAs)

A meta-analysis including 12 studies which include high risk AML patients showed that HMA maintenance therapy with azacitidine or decitabine after allo-HSCT had better outcomes in OS, RFS, NRM, CIR was significantly lower compared with control group [66]. The combination of minimal-dose decitabine with recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (rhG-CSF) after allo-HSCT can reduce the 2-year CIR [67]. In addition, azacitidine is now being studied in combination with agents including venetoclax (NCT04161885, NCT04128501, NCT04809181), pevonedistat (NCT04712942), valproic acid (NCT02124174), eprenetapopt (NCT03931291) and sabatolimab (NCT04623216).

Histone deacetylases (HDAC) inhibitors

HDAC inhibitors are currently under investigation after the promising findings with panobinostat as maintenance therapy. In a phase I/II study for patients with high-risk AML in hematologic CR following reduced intensity conditioning (RIC) allo-HSCT, panobinostat showed 2-year OS and RFS rates of 81% and 75%, respectively. The CIR and NRM across all dose levels was 20% and 5% respectively. These findings suggest panobinostat does not impair development of peripheral tolerance and may actually mitigate GVHD [68]. Vorinostat in combination with idarubicin, cytarabine or decitabine have been studied as front line or salvage therapy in AML, but still lack data on maintenance or pre-emptive therapy in R/R AML [69–71]. Novel drugs such as tefinostat and nanatinostat are still in early phase clinical trial in advanced haematological malignancies or refractory solid tumors, and further results are anticipated regarding its clinical use [72, 73].

Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT-3) inhibitors

The introduction of first- and second-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) with activity against FLT-3 has changed prognosis for FLT-3 mutated patients. Sorafenib has been found to be effective after HSCT for maintenance therapy in FLT3-ITD AML in several studies. Phase III randomized clinical trial (RCT) from our center showed that sorafenib maintenance group revealed a significant advantage over non-maintenance group in both LFS and OS, with a relapse rate and OS rate of 70.0% and 72.0% in the sorafenib group compared with 49.0% and 55.9% in the non-maintenance group, within median follow up of 60.4 months [74]. The German-Austrian AMLSG 16 − 10 trial (NCT01477606) investigating the efficacy of midostaurin 50 mg twice daily as maintenance therapy after allo-HSCT in patients with FLT3-ITD AML reported an EFS and OS of 39% and 34% in younger and 53% and 46% in older patients at 2 years [75]. Additionally, crenolanib (NCT02400255, NCT01657682 and NCT01522469 [76], Randhawa et al. [77]), clifutinib (NCT04827069) [78] and gilteritinib (NCT02997202 [79], NCT02421939 [80, 81], NCT03625505 [82]) are being studied for treatment in R/R AML patients with FLT3 mutations, which might be considered as maintenance therapy in future.

Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1/2 (IDH1/2) inhibitors

Isocitrate dehydrogenase inhibitors such as ivosidenib and enasidenib have been considered for maintenance therapy in patients with R/R AML. Enasidenib has reported a 2-year OS rate ranging between 70% and 100%, while Ivosidenib reported 2-year OS of 88% as maintenance therapy in AML patients [83, 84]. Long-term data are still awaited to utilize this class of drug for R/R AML treatment.

BCL-2 inhibitor

Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of BCL-2 inhibitors as maintenance therapy for R/R AML patients following transplantation. For instance, a prospective study investigated low-dose decitabine combined with venetoclax (Dec/Ven) as maintenance therapy, initiated approximately 3 months after allo-HSCT. The regimen exhibited tolerable toxicity, and with a median follow-up of 598 days, the 2-year EFS and OS rates were encouraging at 84.7% and 85.2%, respectively [85]. However, since this was a single-arm trial, the superiority of the maintenance therapy could not be directly established. Another prospective study investigated off-label post-SCT maintenance therapy in high-risk patients, reporting 1-year OS and RFS of 70% and 67%, respectively. The most common adverse reactions were cytopenia (40.8%) and gastrointestinal events (34.7%). This study represents real-world experience rather than a clinical trial, and the follow-up duration was relatively short. Therefore, these outcomes require further evaluation in larger-scale prospective trials with longer follow-up periods [86]. A phase I trial explored venetoclax combined with azacitidine (Ven/Aza) as prophylactic maintenance in high-risk AML patients after allo-HSCT. The Ven/Aza regimen demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with no dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) observed and no significant impact on acute/chronic GVHD or T-cell activity. The study reported a 2-year OS of 67% and a PFS of 59% [87]. Nevertheless, as a nonrandomized, open-label phase 1 study, these findings require validation through randomized controlled trials to confirm any potential clinical benefit. Future studies should validate the efficacy of BCL-2 inhibitors as post-HSCT maintenance therapy through larger-scale cohorts and more rigorous clinical trials, thereby facilitating the optimization of maintenance treatment strategies.

Donor lymphocyte infusions (DLI)

DLI has been widely used in maintenance and pre-emptive treatment after R/R AML transplantation, and DLI combined with targeted therapy has also been commonly used in recent years. DLI can reduce the recurrence rate, and the number of DLI infusions shows a decreasing trend. DLI has the potential to enhance immunological graft-versus-leukemia (GVL) effect, while its efficacy varied in different types of leukemia. Unfortunately, relapsed AML demonstrated a weaker response to DLI than chronic-phase chronic myelogenous leukemia with response rates of 30–40% [88]. Meanwhile, studies reported about 10% grade II-IV aGVHD and 30% cGVHD cumulative incidence rates [89, 90]. At the same time, the incidence of aGVHD with DLI on maintenance therapy was 19%−33% and with cGVHD was 33%−43% [90]. Another study observed an OS of 67% at 7 years after transplant compared to 31% in the control group (P < 0.001) following preventive or pre-emptive DLI after RIC allo-HSCT for high-risk AML [91]. A multicenter, real-world analysis of 932 R/R acute leukemia patients reported that prophylactic/pre-emptive DLI was associated with significantly better LFS than non-DLI for both AML and ALL patients without increasing NRM [92]. Yang L et al. reported that in high-risk acute leukemia patients, prophylactic DLI could reduce post-transplant relapse and improve long-term survival compared to pre-emptive DLI, with 3-year PFS 63.4%vs.53.0% (P = 0.026) [93]. A multicenter prospective study reported that prophylactic DLI followed by MRD and GVHD–guided multiple DLIs reduced relapse and increased survival after HSCT in patients with R/R acute leukemia [94]. Therefore, the EBMT Handbook recommended DLI for AML to be initiated only in the absence of GVHD and uncontrolled infections with a recommended starting cell dose [95].

In recent years, more exploration were focused on DLI. Considering risk of GVHD by repetitive low dose DLIs, studies are exploring low dose DLI as maintenance therapy to decrease GVHD [96]. A study discussed duration for maintenance DLI therapy in R/R AML, showing that delaying DLI to day + 90 post HSCT for patients could lower extensive cGVHD incidence and improve GRFS without increasing incidence of leukemia relapse compared with DLI on day + 60 post HSCT [97].

Studies have demonstrated that pre-emptive DLI based on MRD and chimerism monitoring are beneficial in preventing relapse. In 2021, Rettig et al. reported the 5-year survival rate was much better with pre-emptive DLI (43%) versus therapeutic DLI (10%) with minimal incidence of high-grade GVHD [98]. Although results from published studies have revealed that outcomes from Haplo-identical and HLA-matched DLI are equivalent in terms of outcomes with a similar incidence of DLI-induced aGVHD, it has been proposed that Haplo-DLI may be more advantageous in increasing GVL effect [99].

Manipulated DLI could contribute to improving GVL while reducing GVHD risk. Selective transfer of lymphocyte subsets such as NK and TCRγδ T cells targeting tumor increases the GVL effect without causing GVHD [100]. In another approach, leukemia-specific T cells (LSTs) were selectively reactive to multiple antigens expressed by AML/MDS cells with no activity against recipient’s normal cells. Of the 17 patients with high-risk AML/MDS receiving LST infusion, 11 patients never relapsed with a median LFS at a median follow-up of 1.9 years; 2-year OS rate was 77% [101].

Relapse after allo-HSCT

Treament of relapse after hematopotietic stem cell transplantation is very difficult with poor prognosis. In recent years, combination therapy has shown promising efficacy. A meta-analysis also revealed 32% CR and 50% ORR of venetoclax in combination with HMAs in patients who experienced post-transplantation relapse of AML [105]. Several newly discovered agents including anti body drug conjugate (ADCs), HMAs, HDAC inhibitors, FLT-3 inhibitors, IDH1/2 inhibitors, BCL-2 inhibitors, CAR-T and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors are currently underway.

If such patients receive salvage therapy and achieve persistent MRD negativity, long term follow-up will be needed. For patients who cannot achieve persistent MRD negativity after salvage therapy, second HSCT should be considered. In a retrospective analysis on behalf of the ALWP of EBMT, 1540 adults with relapsed AML receiving second HSCT were included from year 2000 to 2019. Four-year OS rate increased from 23% to 35%, showing survival outcomes significantly developed by years [106]. In a meta- analysis involving 20 studies with 2772 patients, second allo-HSCT resulted in pooled CR, OS, PFS/DFS, NRM and relapse rates of 67%, 34%, 30%, 27%, and 51% respectively [12]. Multivariate analyses revealed risk of relapse was lower in patients who relapsed after 6 months from first transplantation and in CR before second transplantation. Future directions and conclusion

HSCT is the most effective treatment for R/R AML, and the whole management process pre- and post- HSCT significantly impacts patient outcomes. Current research, particularly in China, has demonstrated that the outcomes of haplo-HSCT are comparable to those of MUD-HSCT, thereby supporting the increased utilization of haploidentical donors in transplantation [120]. For patients requiring a second transplant, switching to a new donor may be considered a critical prognostic factor that should not be ignored. Although the prognostic models for allo-HSCT in AML patients are not satisfactory, AI-based applications will provide new support for optimization.

To date, post-transplant relapse remains a major challenge, and strategies to enhance GVL effects are a key focus of research. Current evidence suggests that DLI, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and AML-specific antigen-based CAR-T or CAR-NK therapies show potential in reducing relapse rates. Future research should focus on developing regimens with lower toxicity but enhanced immunomodulatory potential, such as incorporating hypomethylating agents or immune checkpoint inhibitors, to reduce the rate of TRM and enhance anti-leukemia effects. Furthermore, gene editing technologies (e.g., CRISPR-Cas9) offer new avenues for improving the efficacy of allo-HSCT. These techniques can modify donor T cells or NK cells to enhance GVL effects while reducing the risk of GVHD.

In conclusion, R/R AML continues to be a common scenario associated with poor clinical outcomes. The whole management process pre- and post- HSCT has an important impact on prognosis. Comprehensive analysis of prognostic factors, conditioning regimens and post-HSCT strategies, along with integration of emerging therapeutic advances and AI-based prognostic model construction, will facilitate evidence-based clinical decision-making and advance personalized treatment approaches.
