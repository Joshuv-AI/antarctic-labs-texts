Reproductive outcomes after antenatal corticosteroids: Secondary analysis of 50‐year follow‐up of the Auckland steroid randomized trial
Authors: Sophie L St Clair, Anthony G B Walters, Caroline A Crowther, Stuart R Dalziel, Carl Eagleton, Gregory D Gamble, Christopher J D McKinlay, Barry J Milne, Jane E Harding, for the ANCHOR Study Group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAntenatal corticosteroids are widely used to prevent morbidity and mortality after preterm birth, but there are ongoing concerns about the possible risk of long‐term adverse effects, including perturbation of endocrine systems, with potential implications for reproduction. A small number of animal studies have suggested possible adverse effects on reproduction after antenatal exposure to corticosteroids, but there is a paucity of human data.Material and MethodsThis is a secondary cohort analysis of the 50‐year follow‐up of the Auckland Steroid Trial (1969–1974) comparing antenatal exposure to corticosteroids or placebo. Participants whose mothers took part in the placebo‐controlled randomized trial of antenatal corticosteroids completed a questionnaire reporting reproductive outcomes at 50 years of age. The main outcome was at least one pregnancy ≥20 weeks or fathered at least one pregnancy ≥20 weeks. Additional outcomes included a number of pregnancies or fathered pregnancies ≥20 weeks, outcomes relating to female reproductive lifespan (including age at menarche and menopause), and outcomes relating to their offspring (including birthweight and gestation).ResultsOf 917 eligible participants, 415 (45% of eligible) completed the questionnaire at a mean (SD) age of 49.3 (1.0) years. The proportion of participants who had experienced at least one pregnancy ≥20 weeks or fathered at least one pregnancy ≥20 weeks was similar in betamethasone and placebo‐exposed groups (163/217 [75%] vs. 136/190 [72%]; RR 1.08, (95% CI 0.95 to 1.22); p = 0.23). Participants exposed to betamethasone had a slightly higher number of pregnancies or fathered pregnancies ≥20 weeks compared to those exposed to placebo (mean 1.89 vs. 1.60; marginal mean difference 0.20, (95% CI 0.03–0.37); p = 0.03). Other outcomes, including female reproductive lifespan and offspring‐related outcomes, were similar in both randomized groups. There were also no differences in any outcomes between those born preterm and those born at term.ConclusionsAntenatal exposure to corticosteroids appears to have no clinically important effect on reproductive outcomes to 50 years.Keywords: follow‐up studies, pregnancy, premature birth, reproductive behavior, steroids

Key message

We found no clinically significant differences in reproductive outcomes at 50 years between participants exposed to antenatal betamethasone compared to those exposed to placebo. There were also no clinically significant differences between those born preterm and at term.

INTRODUCTION

Antenatal corticosteroids have become the cornerstone of preventing respiratory and other morbidities in preterm infants since the initial findings of the first randomized trial, the Auckland Steroid Trial, were published in 1972, 1 and are a recommended treatment internationally. 2 , 3

However, there remain concerns about the potential long‐term effects of antenatal exposure to corticosteroids, including on reproductive outcomes. Animal studies in several species suggest that antenatal corticosteroid exposure can affect the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenocortical (HPA) axis of offspring. 4 , 5 , 6 One study reported that female offspring of corticosteroid‐exposed guinea pigs required more ovulatory cycles before successful reproduction. 7 However, other studies have reported that offspring HPA axis function is not significantly affected by glucocorticoid exposure, and found normal reproductive capability in female and male offspring of mice exposed to repeat doses of antenatal betamethasone. 8

There is a paucity of evidence regarding the effect of antenatal glucocorticoid exposure on human reproduction. Women with congenital adrenal hyperplasia, many of whom may have been exposed to corticosteroids before birth to prevent virilization, are reported to have reduced pregnancy and birth rates, 9 , 10 but the contribution of antenatal corticosteroid exposure to these outcomes has not been determined.

In addition, it is known that reproductive outcomes can be influenced by preterm birth. A recent systematic review found no difference in age of menarche between those born term and preterm, 11 but other evidence suggests earlier menarche in those with lower birthweight. 12 , 13 , 14 Furthermore, there is an established intergenerational risk of preterm birth, with those born preterm more likely to have offspring born preterm, 15 with this effect more pronounced in females. 16

Antenatal corticosteroid therapy is recommended before preterm birth for the prevention of neonatal mortality and morbidity, and increasing numbers of individuals exposed to this treatment, particularly extremely early gestations, are surviving into adulthood. Thus, it is important to determine the effects, if any, on reproductive outcomes, as this knowledge would allow for appropriate counseling and reproductive assistance if needed.

The aim of this study was to determine whether antenatal corticosteroid exposure influenced reproductive outcomes at 50 years of age in adults whose mothers participated in the Auckland Steroid Trial. Secondary aims were to report previously collected data on reproductive outcomes at 30 years in this cohort, and to examine the effect of preterm birth on reproductive outcomes at both ages.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Auckland Steroid Trial

The Auckland Steroid Trial (AST) was a randomized double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial undertaken at the National Women's Hospital in Auckland, New Zealand between 1969 and 1974. The trial has been well described elsewhere. 1 , 17 In brief, women at risk of preterm birth at 24 to <37 weeks' gestation were randomized to receive an intramuscular injection of either standard dose betamethasone (6 mg of short‐acting betamethasone phosphate +6 mg of long‐acting betamethasone acetate) or placebo (6 mg of cortisone acetate), with a second injection after 24 h if the birth had not occurred. After 717 women had been enrolled, the doses for both betamethasone and the control were doubled to assess whether a higher dose had greater efficacy at preventing respiratory distress syndrome. The trial randomized a total of 1142 pregnancies (1115 women) (F0) with 1218 babies (F1).

Follow‐up

All surviving offspring of women who took part in the AST were eligible for follow‐up at 30 and 50 years of age.

30‐year follow‐up

Participants completed a questionnaire, either in‐person or via post, which asked questions about physical health, reproductive outcomes, mental health, general well‐being, education, employment, and lifestyle. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

50‐year follow‐up

Participants completed an online questionnaire based on the New Zealand Health Survey, 22 which asked questions about physical health, reproductive outcomes, mental health, general well‐being, education, employment, and lifestyle.

The follow‐up study received ethics approval from the Northern A Health and Disability Ethics Committee (20/NTA/37). Participants gave written informed consent.

Outcomes

The outcomes in this report were prespecified as tertiary outcomes in the statistical analysis plan for the 50‐year follow‐up. 23 They are therefore referred to as main and additional outcomes to reflect the exploratory nature of this analysis.

The main outcome of this study was the number of participants who had at least one pregnancy ≥20 weeks in females or fathered at least one pregnancy ≥20 weeks in males. Additional outcomes included the number of pregnancies or fathered pregnancies ≥20 weeks; outcomes relevant to female participants: age at menarche, the proportion who had reached menopause, age at menopause, need for assisted reproductive technology, diagnosis of the polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), number of miscarriages (<20 weeks gestation), gestational hypertension, and gestational diabetes; and outcomes related to their offspring: age at birth of the first child, the gestation of the child, birthweight of the child, multiple pregnancy (twins), and number of biological grandchildren.

To undertake a similar analysis for the 30‐year follow‐up, the main outcome was at least one pregnancy or fathered at least one pregnancy. Additional outcomes included the number of pregnancies or fathered pregnancies; outcomes relevant to female participants: age at menarche, number of miscarriages, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, pregnant at the time of follow‐up assessment, and contraceptive use (contraceptive pill, injection, or emergency contraceptive); and outcomes related to their offspring: age at birth of the first child, the gestation of the child, birthweight of the child, and multiple pregnancies.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) according to the prespecified statistical analysis plan. 23 All statistical tests were two‐sided with a significance level of 5%. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

Baseline characteristics of those who completed the questionnaire and those who did not, and of betamethasone and placebo randomized groups, were compared using Chi‐square tests for categorical variables and t‐tests for continuous variables.

The primary analyses for both 50‐year and 30‐year data used self‐reported gender and compared the main and additional outcomes between participants whose mothers were randomized to betamethasone or placebo using generalized linear models (GLM) (log‐binomial for categorical and identity‐normal for continuous) with adjustment for the prespecified confounder of prioritized ethnicity and clustering within families (twins and siblings). The results are presented as adjusted risk ratio (RR) or mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals. Exceptions to this are the count variables (number of pregnancies or fathered pregnancies ≥20 weeks, number of biological grandchildren, and number of miscarriages). These outcomes were compared using GLM with a Poisson distribution for the former and negative binomial distributions for the latter two variables, with the “ilink” function employed to put the between‐group differences back onto the counting scale. The results for these outcomes are presented as model‐derived adjusted marginal mean differences (MMD). Sensitivity analyses carried out on all outcomes in the primary analyses were exclusion of participants with the same mother (multiple births or >1 pregnancy during the trial), exclusion of participants whose mothers were randomized more than once in the same pregnancy, and exclusion of those identifying as a different gender to their assigned sex at birth.

Subgroup analyses were undertaken on all outcomes for study drug dose (standard vs. double dose) and gestation (preterm vs. term), and all outcomes except female‐specific outcomes for assigned sex at birth. Analyses were carried out in the same way as that of the primary analyses for each subgroup, and the p‐value for the interaction term between each variable and subgroup is presented.

The exploratory cohort analysis comparing participants born preterm and at term utilized the same statistical approach as above, except these GLM adjusted for randomized group and clustering of participants with the same mother.

A post‐hoc analysis for size at birth (small for gestational age (<10th centile, SGA) vs. not SGA) requested during manuscript review was carried out by additionally adjusting for SGA status in the main analyses, and testing for interactions between SGA and corticosteroid exposure in each model.

The sample size for this study is limited by the size of the inception cohort and the number of participants able to be located for follow‐up. To demonstrate whether this cohort analysis would detect clinically important differences between randomized groups, a retrospective sample size calculation was performed (PASS 16 Power Analysis and Sample Size Software, 2018, NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA). Given that 407 participants provided data for the main outcome (n = 217 betamethasone, n = 190 placebo), we estimated that the study had 80% power for the two‐tailed 5% significance level to detect differences of 12%–15% in the proportion of those with at least one pregnancy or fathered pregnancy ≥20 weeks between randomized groups, assuming the observed rate of parenthood in the cohort falls between 70% and 80%.

RESULTS

Of the 1218 babies born to 1115 women in the Auckland Steroid Trial, 231 died before 28 days and a further 70 died before 50 years, leaving 917 participants eligible for follow‐up. Of the 424 who participated in the 50‐year follow‐up (46% of eligible), the reproductive outcomes from completed questionnaires were available for 415 participants (Figure 1).

Participants who completed the questionnaire were more likely to have a planned preterm delivery, be female, and be born at a lower gestational age and birthweight compared to those who did not complete the questionnaire (Table 1). Of those who completed the questionnaire, randomized groups had similar characteristics. There were the expected differences in baseline characteristics for preterm and term participants, including gestational age, birthweight, delivery mode, low Apgar score, and respiratory distress syndrome. Participants who were born at term were more likely to smoke compared to those born preterm.

There were no significant differences between groups in the other reproductive outcomes: age at birth of the first child, female‐specific outcomes (age at menarche, the proportion reached menopause, age at menopause, need for assisted reproduction, PCOS, number of miscarriages, gestational hypertension, or diabetes), and those relating to their offspring (gestation, birthweight, multiple pregnancy, and number of biological grandchildren) (Table 2).

On sensitivity analysis, results were not altered when they were analyzed without 33 participants with the same mother (multiple births or siblings), without 9 participants whose mothers were randomized more than once in the same pregnancy, or without 3 participants who identified as a different gender to their assigned sex at birth (Supporting Information Table S1).

On subgroup analysis, there was a significant interaction between betamethasone exposure and dose for having had or fathered at least one pregnancy, with those exposed to the double dose being more likely to have had or fathered at least one pregnancy (double dose betamethasone 65/80 [81%] vs. placebo 44/69 [64%], RR 1.33 [95% CI (0.98 to 1.82)]; standard dose betamethasone 98/137 [72%] vs. placebo 92/121 [76%], RR 0.97 [95% CI (0.81 to 1.15)]; p = 0.02 for interaction) (Table 3). There was also a significant interaction between betamethasone exposure and dose for age at menarche, with a later age of menarche in the betamethasone‐exposed group compared to the placebo‐exposed group only in the double dose subgroup (double dose betamethasone 12.7 vs. placebo 11.6 years, MD 1.10 [95% CI (−0.51 to 2.72)]; standard dose betamethasone 12.6 vs. placebo 12.6 years, MD −0.03 [95% CI (−0.86 to 0.81)]; p = 0.02 for interaction).

Furthermore, we found a significant interaction between betamethasone exposure and sex for age at birth of first child, with males but not females exposed to betamethasone being more likely to be younger when their first child was born compared to those exposed to placebo (males exposed to betamethasone 24.2 vs. placebo 26.8 years, MD −2.43 [95% CI (−4.34 to −0.52)]; females exposed to betamethasone 24.1 vs. placebo 23.8 years, MD −0.10 [95% CI (−9.69 to 9.50)]; p = 0.03 for interaction). There was no evidence that sex influenced the effect of antenatal exposure to betamethasone on the main outcome (p = 0.09 for interaction).

Betamethasone exposure and prematurity showed a significant interaction for gestational age at birth of offspring, with participants born at term after betamethasone exposure having children born at an earlier gestational age compared to those exposed to placebo, whereas those born preterm after exposure to betamethasone had children born at a later gestation than those exposed to placebo (term exposed to betamethasone 38.1 vs. placebo 39.4 weeks, MD −1.16 [95% CI (−3.24 to 0.91)]; preterm exposed to betamethasone 39.7 vs. placebo 38.9 weeks, MD 0.57 [95% CI (−0.18 to 1.32)]; p = 0.006 for interaction). There was no evidence that gestation at birth influenced the effect of antenatal exposure to betamethasone on the main outcome, p = 0.64 for interaction. There was also no evidence that additional adjustment for SGA birth altered any of the findings (p = 0.78 for interaction for the main outcome, Supporting Information Table S2).

In the exploratory cohort analysis comparing participants born preterm to those at term, there were no differences in any of the measured reproductive outcomes, including the gestational age of the child (39.3 vs. 38.7 weeks; MD 0.60, 95% CI (−0.26 to 1.46); p = 0.17) (Table 4).

At the 30‐year follow‐up, 533 participants answered at least part of the reproductive questionnaire, of whom 179/252 [71%] in the betamethasone and 164/281 [58%] in the placebo‐exposed group participated in the 50‐year follow‐up. There were no significant differences in reproductive outcomes between randomized groups at 30 years, including similar rates of at least one pregnancy or fathered pregnancy in betamethasone compared to placebo‐exposed groups (115/249 [46%] vs. 142/279 [51%]; RR 0.97, 95% CI (0.82 to 1.16); p = 0.76) (Supporting Information Tables S3 and S4). However, those born preterm were likely to be older at the birth of their first child (24.9 vs. 23.1 years; MD 1.77, 95% CI (0.52 to 3.01); p = 0.01). Those born preterm also had fewer miscarriages than those born at term (0.56 vs. 1.09; MD −0.70, 95% CI (−1.25 to −0.15); p = 0.02). There were no other differences in reproductive outcomes at 30 years between those born preterm and at term.

DISCUSSION

Antenatal corticosteroids are recommended internationally for the prevention of neonatal mortality and morbidity after preterm birth. 2 , 3 We have previously reported that corticosteroids, compared with placebo, were safe for offspring to 30 years of age, with no clinically significant differences between randomized groups in most health outcomes including body size, blood pressure, lipids, bone density, lung function, psychiatric morbidity, and health‐related quality of life, although in those exposed to betamethasone, there was a small increase in insulin response to the oral glucose tolerance test of unclear significance. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 At 50 years, there were no significant differences between groups for cardio‐respiratory outcomes. 23 Here we report that there were also no clinically important differences in reproduction between the randomized groups to 50 years, which is likely to be near the end of reproduction for most participants.

While there was no difference in the categorical outcome of at least one pregnancy or fathered pregnancy ≥20 weeks, those in the betamethasone group had a slightly greater number of pregnancies or fathered pregnancies ≥20 weeks compared to placebo, but this was not apparent at the 30‐year follow‐up. Although the cohort of participants assessed at 30 years was not exactly the same as that assessed at 50 years, the proportion of participants with at least one pregnancy increased 29% for the betamethasone group and 21% for the placebo group between the 30‐ and 50‐year assessments. Birth and fathering rates in New Zealand in the year 2000, close to the time of the 30‐year follow‐up, were highest for women and men in the 25–29‐ and 30–34‐year age groups, with a median age at birth of their child of 29.7 years for women and 32.3 years for men. 24 Thus most participants at the 30‐year follow‐up were likely only part way into their parenting journey, and small differences between randomized groups were unlikely to be detected. By the 50‐year follow‐up, more participants in the betamethasone group had larger families which resulted in a slightly greater mean number of pregnancies in this group.

Although there was no effect on the main outcome overall, subgroup analysis suggested that those exposed antenatally to the higher dose of betamethasone may have been more likely to have at least one pregnancy or fathered a pregnancy ≥20 weeks, despite the fact that females exposed antenatally to the higher dose of betamethasone appeared to have slightly later reproductive maturation. In the original trial, the double dose did not further reduce the incidence of respiratory distress syndrome, which was the primary focus of that trial, 1 and subsequent animal studies have found that even the standard steroid dose is well in excess of that required to saturate glucocorticoid receptors and induce lung maturation. 25 , 26 Although the double dose may be more analogous to that used in many animal studies reporting adverse effects after antenatal corticosteroid exposure, 27 the effects seen in this study could arguably be interpreted as beneficial rather than adverse for reproductive outcomes. However, the effect sizes are small and the double dose is not recommended practice, so these findings are of limited clinical significance.

There was also an interaction between betamethasone and sex for the outcome of age at birth of the first child, with males but not females exposed to betamethasone tending to be younger in fatherhood. There is weak observational evidence that antenatal corticosteroid exposure may be linked to behavioral disturbances, with reduced psychosocial behavior in children, including greater shyness, emotionality and avoidance, and higher internalizing behaviors, although sex‐specific differences were not reported. 28 , 29 Animal studies have also reported sex‐specific differences in behavior after antenatal corticosteroid exposure, including differential gene expression in the prefrontal cortex, 30 and demasculinization of sexual behavior in the male steroid‐exposed offspring. 31 However, none of these effects offer an explanation for our findings, and there remains the possibility of a type 1 error.

In the exploratory cohort analysis of babies born preterm compared to at term, there were no differences in reproductive outcomes at 50 years, including no difference in the gestation at birth of their children. Large studies elsewhere have reported an intergenerational effect of preterm birth that appears to be sex‐specific, with women born preterm more likely to have preterm offspring. 15 , 32 However, in our cohort, all participants had received corticosteroids or placebo because they were at risk of preterm birth, and in common with all similar cohort studies, comparison between those born preterm and at term is inevitably confounded by the known and unknown reasons for being at risk of preterm birth. This may provide a potential explanation for why no differences were seen between the term and preterm groups in any reproductive outcomes by 50 years.

Interestingly, participants born at term after exposure to betamethasone had babies born at earlier gestations, whereas those born preterm after exposure to betamethasone tended to have babies born at later gestations when compared to placebo. Although the reasons for these effects are not clear, the differences in gestation length were small (0.8–1.3 weeks) and unlikely to be of clinical significance. There was further evidence of differential effects in the subgroup analysis, with term offspring exposed to betamethasone having a greater number of pregnancies and a later age of menarche when compared to term offspring exposed to placebo.

At 30‐year follow‐up, participants born preterm were older at the birth of their first child than those born at term. This difference was no longer significant by 50 years. In a large cohort study in Norway, adults born preterm were less likely to be married or have children than those born at term, 33 possibly reflecting the greater predominance of internalizing rather than externalizing behaviors in those born preterm. 34 , 35 This may have contributed to their later age at birth of their first child in our cohort, but there was no difference in overall reproductive success by age 50.

Our study had multiple strengths, including a broad assessment of reproductive outcomes, a prospective randomized design, and adjustment for potential confounders. This is also the oldest cohort in the world exposed to antenatal corticosteroids as part of a randomized trial, studied at an age when most would have completed their reproduction. Limitations of our study were the relatively low follow‐up rate, the limited power to detect small differences between randomized groups, and missing data for some subgroups, although the proportion missing was low (<10%). We also examined multiple outcomes, increasing the risk of type 1 errors, with a total of 242 comparisons made, of which 18 (7.4%) were statistically significant with a p < 0.05.

CONCLUSION

We found no clinically significant differences in reproductive outcomes at 50 years between participants exposed to antenatal betamethasone compared to those exposed to placebo. There were also no clinically significant differences between those born preterm and at term. These findings support the long‐term safety of antenatal corticosteroid administration and are reassuring that the short‐term benefits of corticosteroids in reducing neonatal mortality and morbidity are not outweighed by long‐term adverse effects on reproduction, including in those born at term.
