Volume over location: prioritizing case volume over regional distribution in ovarian cancer treatment
Authors: Byeong-Chan Oh, Sokbom Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to examine the impact of hospital surgical volume and hospital region on overall survival (OS) in patients with ovarian cancer.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized nationwide claims data from Korea (2012–2020) to analyze ovarian cancer patients who underwent surgery. Hospitals were classified as high-volume (≥20) or low-volume (<20) based on the annual volume of upfront ovarian cancer surgeries. Propensity score matching (PSM) (1:1) addressed confounder imbalances between the groups. OS was assessed via Kaplan-Meier analysis, log-rank tests, and Cox regression, with subgroup analyses by cancer stage.ResultsA total of 11,510 patients were included in the cohort (high-volume: 8,241; low-volume: 3,269), with 3,236 matched pairs identified through PSM. Compared with low-volume hospitals, treatment at high-volume hospitals was associated with a 21% reduction in mortality risk (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]=0.79; 95% confidence interval [CI]=0.70–0.89). This survival advantage persisted across localized/regional (aHR=0.77; 95% CI=0.63–0.95) and distant-stage disease (aHR=0.81; 95% CI=0.71–0.92). In contrast, hospital location (capital vs. noncapital) was not significantly associated with OS in the entire cohort (aHR=1.09; 95% CI=0.97–1.21) or in stage-specific analyses.ConclusionThese findings highlight that instead of simply distributing hospitals geographically, establishing high-volume surgical centers is crucial to improving survival outcomes for patients with ovarian cancer.Keywords: Ovarian Neoplasms; Hospitals, High-Volume; Hospitals, Low-Volume; Survival

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death among women, with 5-year relative survival rates of approximately 50% in the United States and 65% in South Korea [1,2]. Standard treatment for ovarian cancer typically involves surgical procedures followed by platinum-based chemotherapy [3]. In early ovarian cancer, the primary goal of surgery is complete tumor resection and staging, whereas in advanced stages, achieving complete or optimal cytoreduction is critical for improving the prognosis [3,4,5].

Previous studies have demonstrated that hospitals with higher surgical volumes achieve better outcomes across various cancers requiring complex surgical procedures [6,7,8,9]. Similar findings have been reported for ovarian cancer [10,11,12,13], where higher hospital surgical volumes are associated with improved short- and long-term mortality. However, few studies have examined the impact of hospital surgical volume on early-stage ovarian cancer [14,15]. Given the differences in surgical complexity and prognosis between early and advanced stages, assessing the impact of hospital surgical volume on prognosis requires a stage-specific approach. Additionally, as the surgical management of ovarian cancer increasingly emphasizes complete resection, there is a need to assess the impact of hospital surgical volume on prognosis.

In addition, a previous study reported that 35.1% of patients with cancer residing in provinces received treatment at hospitals in the capital region [16], reflecting the strong preference for capital region hospitals in Korea. However, whether hospital region is associated with survival outcomes in patients with ovarian cancer remains underexplored. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of hospital surgical volume on overall survival (OS) while also examining whether hospital region contributes to survival differences after ovarian cancer surgery. Furthermore, we conducted stage-stratified analyses to separately examine the associations in each stage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data source and study population

This study utilized national claims data from the Cancer Public Library Database (CPLD) in South Korea [17]. The CPLD combines data from four major population-based public sources: the Korea National Cancer Incidence Database in the Korea Central Cancer Registry, cause-of-death data in Statistics Korea, the National Health Information Database in the National Health Insurance Service, and the National Health Insurance Research Database in the Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service. Data from these sources were linked using encrypted join keys, allowing longitudinal tracking, with survival information available regardless of hospital type or patient transfer. The database also includes comprehensive data on patient demographics, clinical cancer characteristics, healthcare service utilization, and cost information. With approximately 98% completeness [18], the CPLD is a representative resource for generating real-world evidence under universal health coverage and fee-for-service delivery systems in South Korea. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center, Republic of Korea (NCC2025-0101). The requirements for informed consent were waived by the institutional review board, as the patient data were anonymized and deidentified in the database.

This retrospective cohort study utilized claims data from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2020 (hereafter referred to as the study period). The study population comprised patients with newly diagnosed ovarian cancer obtained from the CPLD. Because our study aimed to evaluate survival outcomes after ovarian cancer surgery according to hospital surgical volume, the analysis was restricted to patients who underwent ovarian cancer surgery. To select eligible patients, the claims data of adult patients with ovarian cancer (the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision code ‘C56’), newly diagnosed between 2012 and 2019, were obtained from the CPLD. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients with an initial diagnosis of ovarian cancer between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2019 (hereafter referred to as the index period), to allow at least one year of follow-up and ensure eligibility criteria could be applied; 2) patients without a history of gynecological surgery before the initial diagnosis of ovarian cancer; 3) patients who underwent initial ovarian cancer surgery at a tertiary or general hospital within 6 months following the initial diagnosis, with the surgery date defined as the index date; and 4) patients with available cancer stage information based on Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) summary staging. The resulting cohort of all eligible patients before propensity score matching (PSM) is hereafter referred to as the overall cohort. The study design is illustrated in Fig. S1. Ovarian cancer surgery was defined based on gynecological surgery claims during hospitalization, with ovarian cancer documented as either the primary or secondary diagnosis.

2. Definitions of key variables and study outcomes

Hospital surgical volume was defined based on the average annual number of ovarian cancer surgeries performed at each institution. Hospitals were categorized into two groups: those with an average of 20 or more surgeries per year (high-volume hospitals) and those with fewer than 20 surgeries per year (low-volume hospitals). This annual surgical volume was calculated by dividing the total number of ovarian cancer surgeries performed at each hospital, from the first surgery date within the index period to December 31, 2019, by the number of follow-up years in the study period. The cutoff value of 20 surgeries per year was determined based on the minimum annual institutional volume target presented in the quality indicators proposed by the European Society of Gynaecological Oncology (ESGO) [19]. This threshold has also been used in previous studies examining the association between hospital surgical volume and clinical outcomes in patients with ovarian cancer [13,20,21]. Patients were classified into high- or low-volume cohorts based on the surgical volume category of the hospital where they underwent the first surgery.

The study outcome was OS based on date of death information linked to Statistics Korea. Patient follow-up occurred from the index date until either death or the dataset cutoff date, and the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities were evaluated in both cohorts. The median follow-up time was estimated using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method [22]. Demographic characteristics, including age, histology, insurance type, and income level, were assessed as of the index date. The SEER cancer stage was determined based on the initial diagnosis of ovarian cancer. We also assessed whether patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, defined as administration of platinum-based chemotherapy within 90 days prior to ovarian cancer surgery. Histories of other cancers were assessed from the data start to the initial diagnosis of ovarian cancer. We examined the hospital region based on the hospital location at the index date, where the patient underwent initial ovarian cancer surgery. The capital area included hospitals in Seoul, Incheon, and Gyeonggi-do, whereas hospitals outside these regions were classified as noncapital areas. This classification was used to explore potential regional differences in survival outcomes.

3. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics. Continuous variables are presented as the means and standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables are presented as numbers and frequencies. In the overall cohort, differences between the high- and low-volume cohorts were assessed using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. To address the imbalance of confounders, patients treated at high- and low-volume hospitals were matched at a 1:1 ratio using propensity scores. Propensity scores were calculated using a logistic regression based on the age, SEER cancer stage, histology, use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, history of other cancers, insurance type, and income level. Nearest-neighbor matching was applied with a caliper width of 0.2. The covariate balance between the two cohorts was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values less than 0.1 indicative of adequate balance [23]. The distributions of propensity scores in both cohorts were visually inspected via kernel density estimation.

OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and patients who did not die during the study period were censored at the dataset cutoff date. The difference in OS between the high- and low-volume cohorts was evaluated using the log-rank test. We used the Cox proportional hazards model to assess the association of hospital surgical volume with OS, with adjustments for hospital region, age group, SEER cancer stage, use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, histology, history of other cancers, insurance type, and income level. This model was applied to both the overall cohort and the propensity score-matched cohort to account for potential residual imbalances after matching. Additionally, Cox proportional hazards models were separately applied to SEER cancer stage subgroups, stratifying patients into localized/regional stage and distant stage subgroups, to evaluate the association of hospital surgical volume with OS within each subgroup. To account for the potential correlation of outcomes among patients admitted to the same hospital, we employed marginal survival models that incorporated clustering by hospital to adjust the standard errors [24]. A 2-tailed value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed via SAS Viya version 03.05 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.1.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

4. Additional analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings, sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative hospital volume thresholds of 27 and 50 surgeries per year. The threshold of 27 surgeries per year was selected based on a previous study conducted in Japan [25], while the threshold of 50 surgeries per year represents the intermediate target specified in the ESGO quality indicators for the annual number of cytoreductive surgeries performed per center [19]. For the exploratory analysis, we assessed OS differences according to the geographic region of the hospital within the high- and low-volume cohorts separately via the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test.

RESULTS

Among the 19,745 patients with ovarian cancer, a total of 11,510 patients met the inclusion criteria, including 8,241 patients treated at high-volume hospitals and 3,269 patients treated at low-volume hospitals (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the overall cohort are presented in Table 1. The proportion of patients aged ≥65 years was lower in the high-volume cohort than in the low-volume cohort (19.2% vs. 23.1%, p<0.001). Regarding the SEER cancer stage at initial diagnosis, distant disease was more common in the high-volume cohort (54.1% vs. 40.9%, p<0.001). A greater proportion of patients had serous ovarian cancer in the high-volume cohort than in the low-volume cohort (55.2% vs. 47.0%, p<0.001). After PSM at a one-to-one ratio, 3,236 patient pairs were identified. After matching, SMDs were below 0.1 for several covariates (Fig. 2A, Table S1). However, residual imbalances remained in age group, histology, and income level, with SMDs of 0.10, 0.17, and 0.26, respectively. Therefore, we also applied the Cox proportional hazards model to the matched cohort, adjusting for the same covariates as in the overall cohort. The propensity score distributions before and after matching are presented in Fig. S2. The median follow-up duration was 48.4 months (interquartile range [IQR], 29.6–71.1 months) in the overall cohort and 48.9 months (IQR, 29.4–71.7 months) in the propensity score-matched cohort.

Table 2 presents the associations between hospital surgical volume and OS in the overall cohort. After adjustments for baseline characteristics, patients treated in high-volume hospitals had a 21% lower hazard of death than those treated in low-volume hospitals did (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]=0.79; 95% confidence interval [CI]=0.70–0.89). Subgroup analyses by cancer stage revealed that the survival benefit associated with high-volume hospitals persisted in both the localized/regional stage subgroup (aHR=0.77; 95% CI=0.63–0.95) and the distant stage subgroup (aHR=0.81; 95% CI=0.71–0.92). The hospital region (capital vs. noncapital area) was not significantly associated with OS in the overall cohort (aHR=1.09; 95% CI=0.97–1.21). Similarly, no significant associations were detected in the localized/regional stage subgroup (aHR=1.04; 95% CI=0.85–1.26) or the distant stage subgroup (aHR=1.08; 95% CI=0.96–1.21). Fig. 2B and C show the survival curves for OS for the high- and low-volume cohorts before and after PSM. Before PSM, OS did not differ significantly between the 2 cohorts (p=0.109). However, after PSM, OS was significantly higher in the high-volume cohort than in the low-volume cohort (p=0.045) (Fig. 2C). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities were 93.7%, 78.9%, and 68.0%, respectively, in the high-volume cohort and 92.1%, 77.3%, and 67.4%, respectively, in the low-volume cohort (Table S2).

DISCUSSION

Our findings indicate that hospital surgical volume, rather than the geographic region of the hospital, is a key prognostic factor for OS in patients with ovarian cancer. Patients who underwent surgery at high-volume hospitals had significantly better OS than those treated at low-volume hospitals did, even after controlling for hospital region, age group, SEER cancer stage, histology, use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, history of other cancers, insurance type, and income level. Notably, while hospital surgical volume was significantly associated with improved survival in patients with localized/regional and distant disease, the geographic region of the hospital did not significantly affect OS.

As expected, our findings align with those of previous studies showing a survival benefit associated with increased surgical volume in patients with ovarian cancer [10,11,12,13]. Wright et al. [13] reported superior 2-year survival rates in high-volume centers (≥20 cases/year) than in low-volume centers (≤2 cases/year) based on data from the National Cancer Database in the United States (77.4% vs. 64.4%). Similarly, Savoye et al. [12] found that patients treated in the lowest-volume centers (<10 patients over 5 years) had a 47% higher hazard of death than those treated in the highest-volume centers (≥42 patients over 5 years) in Belgium. The potential reason for this survival advantage in high-volume hospitals is likely multifactorial. First, high-volume hospitals are more likely to have experienced gynecologic oncologists who specialize in ovarian cancer surgery. Second, well-established multidisciplinary teams may facilitate comprehensive perioperative management. Third, better infrastructure and adherence to standardized treatment protocols may enhance the quality of care. These factors collectively enhance the quality of care and may explain the observed survival benefit in high-volume hospitals [26,27].

While many studies support a positive association between surgical volume and survival, some studies have reported inconsistent findings. Schrag et al. [15] analyzed SEER-Medicare data (1992–1999) in the United States and reported no statistically significant association between hospital surgical volume and 60-day mortality; however, a modest association with OS was reported (p=0.031). Kim et al. [21] also reported that a lower hospital surgical volume was associated with higher in-hospital and 1-year mortality rates but found no significant difference in 3- or 5-year mortality rates in Korea. However, the study of Schrag et al. [15] focused on women aged 65 years and older, whereas over 80% of our cohort was under 65 years, making direct comparisons difficult. Additionally, Kim et al. did not account for cancer stage, which could bias survival outcomes against high-volume hospitals, as high-volume cohorts are more likely to have a greater proportion of patients with distant-stage disease. Notably, in our study, the log-rank test indicated no statistically significant difference in survival between the 2 cohorts before PSM (p=0.109). However, after key characteristics, including the cancer stage, were balanced, the difference became statistically significant (p=0.045).

Together with previous studies and the observed volume-outcome relationship, our findings support the centralization of care for patients with ovarian cancer. Numerous studies have reported the benefits of centralization in some complex cancer surgeries [28,29,30]. In the United States, increased centralization of pancreatectomy, esophagectomy, and lung resection was reported to be associated with lower rates of postoperative complications and mortality [29]. Similar results were reported for ovarian cancer in studies from Sweden and the Netherlands, where centralization of primary treatment significantly improved patient prognosis, including disease-free survival and relative survival [31,32]. Furthermore, the centralization of care for advanced ovarian cancer aligns with the ESGO guidelines, which recommend a minimum required caseload as a quality indicator to achieve optimal surgical outcomes in patients with ovarian cancer [19].

Interestingly, our results indicate no significant differences in OS between patients treated at hospitals in capital and noncapital regions, regardless of surgical volume. In South Korea, the concentration of patients at large hospitals in capital areas has been frequently criticized as a social issue, prompting government efforts to decentralize healthcare facilities. However, this study highlights that simply redistributing hospitals to regional areas without ensuring sufficient surgical volume can lead to inferior outcomes. Even if regional hospitals are established, failing to secure adequate surgical volume through measures such as regulating patient referral areas can compromise treatment outcomes. This, in turn, may damage the trust of patients in regional hospitals, accelerating the preference for large hospitals in the capital.

This study utilized a large, nationally representative dataset and robust statistical methods, including PSM and adjustments for hospital-level clustering. However, limitations remain. First, key confounders such as residual disease extent and surgeon-specific volume were not accounted for due to data limitations. Hospital-level characteristics that may explain differences in surgical volume, such as hospital capacity or referral patterns, were also unavailable. Future studies incorporating detailed hospital-level data are warranted to clarify these factors. Second, SEER summary staging was used instead of International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics staging, which was unavailable. Third, the hospital volume cutoff requires further validation, although sensitivity analyses with thresholds of 27 and 50 surgeries per year both produced consistent results. Finally, the findings may not be generalizable to countries with different healthcare systems.

In conclusion, this nationwide population-based study suggests that higher hospital surgical volume is associated with improved OS in patients with ovarian cancer, whereas the geographic region of the hospital does not significantly impact survival outcomes. The survival benefit associated with high surgical volume was consistent across both localized/regional and distant disease stages. These findings underscore the need for centralizing care for advanced ovarian cancer patients and establishing high-volume hospitals in noncapital regions to improve patient outcomes.
