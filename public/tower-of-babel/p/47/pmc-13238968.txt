The effect of different non-drug therapies on Achilles tendinopathy: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Shutong Yan, Weipeng Li, Meng Ding, Ningxin Jia, Shuwen Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAchilles tendinopathy is a chronic disease that seriously affects the daily life of patients. Non-drug therapy can enhance Achilles tendon function and reduce patient pain. The purpose of this article was to analyze the therapeutic effect of non-drug therapy on Achilles tendinopathy.MethodsNetwork meta-analysis was performed using a Bayesian Markov chain Monte Carlo approach, with a random-effects model for data synthesis and analysis. This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: registration number CRD42022304459).ResultA total of 30 studies (available up to January 30, 2023) were included. For VISA-A, acupuncture (SUCRA:89.6%, standardized mean difference (95% CI): 23 (−3.9, 49)) and shock wave + Kinesio (SUCRA: 84.4%, MD (95% CI): 18 (−9.1, 45)) showed better effects than placebo. For VAS, the top three therapies were eccentric training + laser +cold airflow (SUCRA: 92.5%, MD (95% CI): −4.5 (−8.0, −1.0)), eccentric training + electroacupuncture (SUCRA:88.0%, MD (95% CI): −3.7 (−6.5, −1.1)) and shock wave + massage (SUCRA: 87.7%, MD (95% CI): −3.8 (−6.6, −1.1)). PRP injection (SUCRA: 22.4%, OR (95% CI): 1.7 (0.71, 3.9)) was the most acceptable non-drug therapy.ConclusionAcupuncture and shock wave + Kinesio were the most effective non-drug therapies for improving function in Achilles tendinopathy. Eccentric training + laser + cold airflow was the most effective non-drug therapy for reducing pain in Achilles tendinopathy. PRP injection was the most applicable non-drug therapy.Highlights• Acupuncture and shock wave + Kinesio were the most effective non-drug therapies for improving function in Achilles tendon disease. • Eccentric training + laser + cold airflow was the most effective non-drug therapy for reducing pain in Achilles tendon disease. • PRP injection was the most applicable non-drug therapy.

Introduction

Achilles tendon disease is one of the injuries caused by excessive use of the ankle joint (1) and causes repeated pain in the Achilles tendon area. It occurs in people worldwide, and this disease can cause inconvenience to the patients. The group most susceptible to Achilles tendon disease is athletes engaged in running or jumping events, and the pain caused by Achilles tendon disease may affect 9% of leisure runners and can lead to 5% of athletes ending their professional careers prematurely (2). Non-insertional Achilles tendon disease is the most common, accounting for 55–65% of all incidence rates (3).

Non-pharmacological therapy refers to the way of treatment that does not rely on drugs, and generous evidence proved its effect on Achilles tendon disease: eccentric training can enhance the strength of calf muscles, strengthen and extend tendon fibers, reduce the generation of new blood vessels, and reduce pain caused by Achilles tendon disease (4); it has good effects in the early application (5). The study found that using shock wave therapy can improve local symptoms and have high patient satisfaction (6). Wang & Ao (7) found that treatment of Achilles tendinitis according to the Kidney Meridian was more effective. Han (8) conceived that orthopedic insoles may change the mobilization mode of the triceps crus, keeping the load inside the Achilles tendon normal and thus alleviating Achilles tendinitis. J M Bjordal et al. (9) found that low-level laser therapy (LLLT) at a dose of 5.4 J/point may play a role in diseases with inflammatory components. Zhang (10) found that manual release therapy had a significant therapeutic effect on Achilles tendon periarthritis. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injection can promote tendon repair and regeneration by directly introducing high concentrations of growth factors into the degraded site (11, 12). However, previous analysis was limited due to the therapeutic effects of non-pharmacological therapy on Achilles tendon disease, and there was no clear answer on the most effective one.

This study further enriched the types of non-pharmacological therapies based on previous studies, ranked the effects of various non-pharmacological therapies according to different outcome indicators, providing evidence for future research.

Methods

This meta-analysis followed the criteria of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement for systematic reviews and meta-analyses and was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO: registration number CRD42022304459).

Criteria for inclusion and exclusion

Study types

RCTs and animal studies were excluded.

Participant types

Patients with Achilles tendinopathy aged 18 and above were included in the study, regardless of nationality, gender, race, and history of exercise. The diagnostic criteria mentioned in the included studies of this article were sorted out, and the diagnostic criteria for Achilles tendinopathy obtained were as follows: ultrasound examination reveals tendon thickening; swelling occurs in the Achilles tendon area of the patient, accompanied by pain, and the pain is more significant when a pressure is applied; and the ankle joint is in the dorsiflexion and plantar flexion positions. Sometimes, a sense of friction is felt. Studies targeting patients with Achilles tendon rupture, studies with less than 10 participants in the control group or experimental group, studies without a control group, studies without specifying the type of Achilles tendon disease, and studies with concurrent diseases such as gout and ankylosing spondylitis in patients were excluded. Studies on single and combination non-drug therapies were included, and studies on surgery were excluded.

Intervention types and controls

Single non-drug therapy (placebo, eccentric training, shock wave, acupuncture, PRP injection, low-frequency pulse, electroacupuncture, centripetal training, slow resistance training, vibration training, ultrasound, heel booster pad, and orthopedic insole) and a combination of two or more non-drug therapies were considered for the review. Detailed definitions are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article).

Outcomes measures

The primary outcome is the Victoria Institute of Sport Assessment-Achilles (VISA-A) tendinopathy questionnaire score. The secondary outcomes are the recovery of physical activity and the measurement score for evaluating the clinical effectiveness of pain relief in Achilles tendon disease (visual analog scale, VAS).

Literature searches

We searched PubMed, Cochrane Central, Web of Science, Embase, CNKI, VIP, and Wanfang till January 30, 2023. Included articles were limited to those published in English or in Chinese or other languages that can be translated into English or Chinese. The search strategy used ‘a combination of Achilles tendon disease and randomized controlled trials’ to identify all possible non-drug therapies (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Two reviewers conducted the screening independently, imported the retrieved articles into the software for preliminary screening, eliminated duplicate articles, screened the abstracts and titles of articles according to the inclusion criteria, and eliminated articles according to the exclusion criteria. Finally, full text was obtained if the study met the inclusion criteria. The two reviewers discussed and negotiated with each other to resolve any differences. If there were any differences, a third reviewer made the decision.

Data extraction

The data extraction work was carried out by two reviewers independently, and the extracted data was fed into the Excel table. If the data entered by the two reviewers were inconsistent, the third reviewer was involved and a consensus was reached through negotiation. The reviewers extracted the following information from the literature: research information (first author, year of publication, country/region), sample characteristics (source of subjects, the number of subjects, age, gender), intervention characteristics (experimental group intervention measures, control group intervention measures, intervention measures description), and outcome measures (VISA-A index and study shedding index were the primary outcome measures, and the secondary outcome was the measurement score to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of pain relief in Achilles tendon disease, such as VAS index). In addition, there were two classification indicators, such as cure efficiency.

For outcome measures, the mean and standard deviations (SD) of the difference between the result value and the baseline value were extracted for statistical analysis. The significance of outcome indicators used in this study is different. Some outcome indicators are such that the higher the score, the more severe the symptoms, such as the VAS index. Therefore, the mean difference of improvement of such outcome indicators is negative, indicating that the treatment is effective. For some other outcome indicators, the higher the score, the milder the symptoms, such as VISA-A index, where the mean difference of improvement of such outcome indicators is positive, indicating that the treatment is effective. If the baseline change difference was not reported in the original study, the difference between the endpoint and the baseline was extracted for formula conversion. The formula used is as follows:Schange = sbaseline2 + sfinal2 −(2 × r × Schange × Sfinal), r = 0.5.

As the included documents came from different countries and regions, the units may not be unified; hence, we needed to unify the units. For VAS indicators, we used 10 equal parts of the ruler as the standard. The unit in cm and was converted to mm, 1 cm = 10 mm.

Quality/risk-of-bias assessment of included studies

Two reviewers used the Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment tool (ROB) of RevMan 5.4 software to evaluate the methodological quality of the RCTs. Sources of bias were explored from the following seven dimensions: i) random sequence generation, ii) allocation concealment, iii) blinding of research participants and personnel, iv) blinding of result outcome assessments, v) incomplete outcome data, vi) selective reporting of results, and vii) other biases. Risk of bias of evaluated articles was stratified into three levels: low risk, unclear, and high risk. Any reviewer differences in risk-of-bias evaluations were resolved by a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

General data description

We designed descriptive statistical tables to describe study characteristics and variables such as year of study publication, number of participants, age, and intervention description. We used network evidence maps to present direct comparisons between different non-pharmacological interventions and control groups. The size of the node is equal to the sample size corresponding to the non-pharmacological intervention, and the thickness of the edge is equal to the number of studies directly compared.

Standard meta-analysis and network meta-analysis

For each outcome measure, we performed a traditional head-to-head meta-analysis using STATA16 software. Traditional meta-analyses were used to compare all interventions directly with at least two study interventions, with effect size odds ratio (OR) for binary outcome measures and mean difference (MD) or SMD for continuous outcome measures. Heterogeneity of studies was evaluated using I2 and P values (13). If P > 0.1 I2 > 50%, it indicates heterogeneity among studies. The source of heterogeneity should be found first, and then the heterogeneity should be reduced through subgroup analysis. If the source of heterogeneity could not be found, a random-effects model was used for statistical purposes (14, 15). Network meta-analysis is an extension of standard meta-analysis, integrating direct comparison evidence and indirect or mixed comparison evidence. The Markov chain Monte Carlo method under the Bayesian framework was used for calculation and statistics, and random-effects model was used for data consolidation and processing. Measures of effect were presented using the effect size OR or MD and 95% confidence intervals. R language software was used to establish Bayesian network evidence model, and R Studio software and ‘gemtc’ package were used to calculate, count, and draw the network Meta forest map. OR was used to evaluate the binary classification data, while MD and 95% CI were used to evaluate the continuous data (16). R Studio software was used to calculate and organize the league table, and the results of direct comparison and mixed comparison among interventions were presented in the league table. STATA software and its ‘network’ package were used to draw network evidence plots. Each result corresponded to a network evidence plot. The size of nodes is equal to the sample size corresponding to non-drug intervention, and the thickness of edges was equal to the number of directly compared studies. We also used STATA software to draw a SUCRA chart of surface area values under the cumulative probability chart to rank each non-drug therapy. The larger the SUCRA value, the more effective the intervention.

Assessment and handling of inconsistencies

Inconsistencies are defined as inconsistencies between direct and indirect evidence, including ring-based inconsistencies (direct versus indirect comparisons within the ring) and design inconsistencies (indirect comparisons between two arms and multiple arms), or ring-based inconsistencies if there are only two arms in the study. We used node resolution to evaluate local inconsistencies in the network meta-analysis model. If P > 0.05, there is consistency; if P < 0.05, there is inconsistency.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

The types of Achilles tendinopathy included in different studies were inconsistent. Some patients with non-stationary Achilles tendinopathy and others with stationary Achilles tendinopathy may have some influence on the results when we include these two different types of Achilles tendinopathy in the same study. In this regard, we conducted a subgroup analysis according to the type of Achilles tendon disease to explore whether the same non-drug therapy has the same therapeutic effect for patients with different types of Achilles tendon. In addition, there are differences in the patients in the included studies, such as gender and the number of patients included. Therefore, we used sensitivity analysis to exclude the studies in which the gender of patients and the number of patients in the experimental group and the control group are significantly different, so as to observe the impact of these factors that may lead to bias on the results. In addition to sensitivity analysis for patient gender and number of participants, we also conducted sensitivity analysis for study design type, mainly by excluding several studies whose study type is retrospective.

Publication bias

We assessed publication bias using the corrected comparison funnel plot, where the horizontal axis represents the difference between the effect value of an effect comparison and the combined effect value of all similar comparisons, and the vertical axis represents the standard error s x¯ (SE) of the effect value. If there was no small sample effect, it was symmetric around the zero line.

Results

Study characteristics

After searching the databases, a total of 95,494 articles were obtained (Fig. 1). After using the software to remove duplicate articles and to read the topic abstracts, the number of remaining articles was 183. After reading the entire article, 62 RCTs were obtained. After removing data that could not be extracted and merged, 44 RCTs were included in the network meta-analysis.

Studies selection flow diagram.

Quality/risk of bias of included studies

Of the 44 RCT articles included, 39 studies described specific randomization methods, 19 studies reported on allocation blinding methods. Twelve articles described how blinding was implemented in the trials, of which six were single-blind trials and six were double-blind trials. Fifteen studies reported the blinding of outcome assessment. Twenty-five studies reported the number of study dropouts, of which 15 studies reported the specific reasons for dropouts. Eleven articles reported published and registered research plans. Most of the original studies were of medium or low quality, and one article was of high quality. The overall risk of bias was controlled at around 10% (Fig. 2). More details are shown in Fig. 3.

Subgroup analysis

Considering the different locations of Achilles tendinopathy, subgroup analysis was conducted based on the different locations of Achilles tendinopathy. The results showed that VISA-A (SMD (95% CI): −0.88 (−1.86, 0.10)) and VAS (SMD (95% CI): −0.59 (−1.80, 0.63)) were not the factors affecting treatment effectiveness to the site of Achilles tendinopathy (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Results of network meta-analysis

Network meta-analysis results of VISA-A

A total of 30 RCTs were included in the network meta-analysis of VISA-A, of which 26 were two-arm studies and 4 were three-arm studies, involving 1,946 participants, and 17 intervention measures, forming a total of 3 closed loops (Fig. 4). Seventeen non-drug interventions were compared with placebo, including acupuncture (SUCRA: 89.6%, MD (95% CI): 23 (−3.9, 49)), which was the most effective intervention, followed by shock wave + Kinesio (SUCRA: 84.4%, MD (95% CI): 18 (−9.1, 45)) and shock wave (SUCRA: 71.2%, MD (95% CI): 9.4 (−4.6, 23)) (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis

We excluded the studies with a large proportion of men or women (i.e. the difference between the proportion of men and women was more than 20%), studies with fewer participants, and retrospective studies. The SUCRA ranking results of sensitivity analysis showed that, among VISA-A indicators, eccentric training ranking changed significantly in the gender sensitivity analysis;however, the results were relatively stable. Shock wave and PRP injection ranking changed significantly in the study design sensitivity analysis and the results were not stable. In VAS and study dropouts, the ranking order of individual interventions changed slightly but the results were generally stable. The SUCRA diagram is detailed in Supplementary Figs 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13.

Inconsistency test results

We used a ring-based inconsistency test to assess the difference between direct and indirect comparisons, and the closer the inconsistency factor IF is to 0, the better the agreement. There are inconsistencies, and the results have to be analyzed for sensitivity and further interpretation (Supplementary Tables 4, 5, 6).

Discussion

Summary of evidence

Our study showed that non-drug therapies had positive effects on pain and functional improvement in patients with Achilles tendinopathy. The study found that acupuncture was the most effective single non-drug therapy for improving patients’ function, and shock wave + Kinesio is the most effective combination therapy. This study demonstrates that shock wave is the most effective single non-drug therapy for reducing pain, while eccentric training + laser + cold airflow is the most effective combination therapy. The results of the prior network meta-analysis on dropout rates suggest that PRP is more readily accepted by patients. The quality of the evidence in this study is high, and various non-drug therapies are widely applicable to a wide range of people, which can provide very good reference significance for patients with Achilles tendinopathy.

Agreements and disagreements with other reviews

Effect of different non-drug therapies on pain and function indicators in patients with Achilles tendinopathy

Our research confirmed that acupuncture, shock wave + Kinesio and shock wave were quite effective in enhancing function of patients with Achilles tendinopathy;eccentric training + laser + cold airflow, eccentric training + electroacupuncture, and shock wave + massage have better effects on reducing pain, the reason why they were all combination therapy may be due to the better synergistic effects compared to single, while there is only one combination therapy among functional indicators. The results obtained are different from those of other published meta-analyses (17). A previous study had shown the same effect in combination and single therapy (17); however, this study did not specify the therapies. Our study provided a more detailed division of intervention measures and more accurate ranking results, which may complement previous studies. In the study, acupuncture ranked first in functional indicators. Acupuncture is an analgesic tool for different musculoskeletal conditions by the release of endorphins (18) and serotonin (19). A systematic review concluded that acupuncture could alleviate the pain and improve the function of patients with Achilles tendinopathy whether in short- or long-term follow-up (20). A previous study showed that shock wave is more effective in reducing pain compared to eccentric training (21), which is consistent with our research results. Kinesio is a patch developed for the therapy of joint and muscle pain; it is widely used and has better effects when combined with shock waves. Generally, non-drug therapies have therapeutic effects on Achilles tendinopathy, and patients can choose the appropriate therapy based on their actual situation.

Effect of different non-drug therapies on the applicability of patients with Achilles tendinopathy

Our research confirmed that the acceptability of PRP injection was the best, which may be due to its convenience and more safety compared to surgery. As a therapy that has relatively long-lasting effects after treatment without any allergic reactions, PRP injection was more accepted and popular for patients. Eccentric training + nighttime splint and eccentric training + orthotic stent also demonstrated good acceptability. Eccentric training is a training method that lengthens muscles during contraction, which helps reduce pain. Nighttime splints provide mechanical support and partial pain relief. When combined with eccentric training, they enhance therapeutic outcomes, contributing to high patient satisfaction. An orthopedic insole is a type of foot orthosis that can choose the most suitable wearing angle for patients and serve as a fixation. For the elders, the higher pain level in the Achilles tendon area and psychological pressure may be relieved by wearing orthopedic insoles.

Clinical and research implications

This study conducted a network meta-analysis to directly and indirectly compare the therapeutic effects of various non-drug therapies for Achilles tendinopathy. The analysis aimed to identify the most effective treatment methods, rank their efficacy, and provide evidence-based recommendations for clinicians and patients to optimize non-drug therapy selection. Ultimately, this approach seeks to reduce pain severity, slow disease progression, and improve quality of life in individuals with Achilles tendinopathy.

Strengths and limitations

Traditional meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity in the outcomes of three intervention pairs based on the Victoria Institute of Sport Assessment-Achilles (VISA-A) questionnaire, as well as in one pair measured by a visual analog scale (VAS). This heterogeneity primarily stemmed from direct comparisons of different interventions within the included studies. Inconsistency tests indicated inconsistency in the VISA-A outcomes for specific intervention pairs, including placebo versus eccentric training, placebo versus PRP injection, and shock wave therapy versus PRP injection.

The observed heterogeneity can be attributed to three main factors. First, eccentric training – a researcher-vprescribed intervention – was subject to variability in participants’ adherence and performance quality, as well as considerable differences in training dosage and duration across studies. Second, methodological quality disparities among the included trials may have contributed to the heterogeneity. Specifically, variations in the technical proficiency of shock wave operators, differences in shock wave parameters (e.g. energy intensity), and the use of different device types could all influence treatment outcomes. Third, for the same outcome measure, differences in diagnostic criteria or assessment methods across countries or regions may have introduced variability. Beyond these factors, patient-specific characteristics might also account for part of the heterogeneity observed across the studies.

Our study has several strengths. First, our study included a significant number of non-drug therapies and ranked their efficacy. Second, we obtained final rankings for both indicators of evaluating Achilles tendon disease, thereby providing evidence for the treatment of Achilles tendinopathy. On the other hand, there are also some issues with our research. First, the study included three retrospective studies; we conducted sensitivity analysis to decrease the bias. Second, the treatment time of patients included was inconsistent; different times may cause changes in the ranking of certain therapies. We provided a detailed explanation of the treatment time and the specific situation in the characteristics of the selected studies (Supplementary Tables 7.1 and 7.2), and the difference in treatment time is not significant. Third, the sample size of some RCTs was small, which may cause some bias.

Conclusion

Acupuncture and shock wave + Kinesio were the most effective non-drug therapies for improving function in Achilles tendinopathy. Eccentric training + laser + cold airflow was the most effective non-drug therapy for reducing pain in Achilles tendinopathy. PRP injection was the most applicable non-drug therapy.
