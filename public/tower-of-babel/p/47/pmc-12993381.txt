A Novel Preoperative Calculator for 90-Day Prosthetic Joint Infection Following Total Joint Arthroplasty Based off the Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Collaborative Quality Initiative Database
Authors: Daniel Gerow, Huiyong Zheng, Richard E Hughes, Brian Hallstrom, Kory Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal joint arthroplasty has become one of the most successful surgeries in regard to quality of life and cost-effectiveness. Periprosthetic joint infections can have catastrophic effects on a patient’s surgical outcome, quality of life, and function. Shared decision-making can help patients reduce their risk by changing modifiable risk factors.MethodsLogistic regression was used to construct an infection risk prediction model for primary total joint arthroplasty of hips and knees using data from the Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Collaborative Quality Initiative dataset, which included 140,884 total knee arthroplasties and 82,110 total hip arthroplasties. The resulting model was deployed using a web-based interface that allows surgeons to counsel their patients in the clinic by showing them in real time how adjusting modifiable risk factors will affect their overall risk of infection.ResultsTo our knowledge, this study is novel in finding preoperative opioid use and use of assistive devices to be correlated with infection after primary total knee arthroplasties and total hip arthroplasties. Modifiable risk factors that were found to significantly affect risk included body mass index, smoking, and preop narcotic use. Nonmodifiable risk factors included sex assigned at birth, marital status, race, type of payer, preoperative use of assistive devices, and annual surgeon volume <100 cases per year.ConclusionsWhile some of these risk factors are nonmodifiable, others can be addressed and optimized prior to surgery. Surgeons must be equipped with infection risk information to accurately counsel patients preoperatively, while also using data to drive patient selection.Keywords: Prosthetic joint infection, Total knee, Total hip, Risk factor, Infection prediction modeling

Introduction

Relative to quality of life and cost-effectiveness, total joint arthroplasty has become one of the most successful surgeries in modern medicine [1]. In total hip arthroplasty (THA), for example, Lavernia and Alcerro examined the cost of THA utilizing the concept of quality well year (QWY), which is defined as a year of relatively symptom-free living that represents extreme satisfaction with one’s quality of life. The cost of a QWY is then obtained by dividing the cost of the operation by the number of QWYs obtained. Due to the cost of revision hip surgery, the QWY of a revision THA is $10,775 per QWY compared to $5572 per QWY for a primary THA [1].

However, periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) can be catastrophic for the patient and incredibly costly for the healthcare system. The incidence of PJIs in the United States has been shown to be 0.72-2% of total knee arthroplasties (TKAs) and 0.57%-0.88% of THAs [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. In 2017, the estimated hospitalization cost of treating a total hip PJI was $32,100 and for total knee PJI it was $28,161. By 2030, the annual cost to treat infected hip and knee PJIs in the United States has been projected to be $1.85 billion [4]. In addition to the financial burden on the healthcare system, PJI has an incredibly high patient mortality rate. Lum et al showed that the 1-year mortality rate of a prosthetic joint infection for an average 66-year-old patient was 3 times that of the baseline mortality rate of this age group at 4.33%. Furthermore, the 5-year mortality rate after TKA and THA PJI was found to be 21% [7,8].

While some risk factors for infection are nonmodifiable, many can be optimized prior to surgery. It is essential for surgeons to be equipped with tools that allow them to display a patient’s risk factors leading to infection and to show patients how altering a few factors may decrease or increase their risk. In light of the catastrophic nature of infections in arthroplasty, surgeons must empower patients to change these modifiable risk factors while also being data driven in their patient selection.

The objective of this project was to develop and deploy infection risk prediction models for elective primary total hip and knee arthroplasty cases. To our knowledge, this is the first model to include data on preoperative opioid use, preoperative use of assistive devices, and surgeon volume. From our data, we have created an easy to access web-based tool (Fig. 1) and reference guide that surgeons can use in real-time in the clinic when counseling patients regarding surgery. We hypothesize that perioperative opioid use, preoperative use of assistive devices, and surgeon volume will have a significant effect on infection risk.

Material and methods

The Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Collaborative Quality Initiative (MARCQI) was used as a source of data as well as a platform for risk calculator deployment. Background information on MARCQI, its data sources, and the variables collected have been previously described [9]. This is a multihospital consortium, through which all elective total hip and knee arthroplasties at participating sites are entered into a registry with clinical laboratory values and preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative information. The registry tracks elective primary hip and knee arthroplasty cases including conversion hip arthroplasty and arthroplasty for post-traumatic arthritis. THA cases to treat hip fractures are excluded. Trained clinical medical record abstractors follow each case for 90 days after surgery using available data sources, such as hospital medical records and surgeon office notes. Infections were diagnosed using a modification of the Musculoskeletal Infection Society clinical guidelines [10]. The Michigan Inpatient Database of the Michigan Health and Hospital Association Service Corporation was used to obtain Supplemental Information for diagnoses and events occurring at the index hospital, as well as at external hospitals, over time. Patients who left the state of Michigan would be lost to follow-up. However, cases were manually abstracted out to 90 days, and this is estimated to be a rare scenario [10,11]. All variables were abstracted by MARCQI except for chronic lung disease, which was obtained from Michigan Inpatient Database and the Michigan Outpatient Database. MARCQI receives support from Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan/Blue Care Network.

A cohort of 222,994 primary cases, 82,110 THAs (36.8%) and 140,884 TKAs (63.2%), performed between February 2012 and December 2018 were extracted from the database of MARCQI used to develop the model. The data were contributed by 68 sites including 436 surgeons performing TKA and 360 surgeons performing THA. The risk factors were determined through literature review, domain expert knowledge (MARCQI clinical champions), and the availability in the database. Each site has a surgeon acting as a clinical champion who can facilitate the program at that site. The initial screening list included joint, age, body mass index (BMI), sex assigned at birth, smoking, marriage status, race/ethnicity, primary payer, preoperative narcotics, diabetes, use of insulin, cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, alcohol abuse, assistive devices, American Society of Anesthesiologists classification, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus screening, and yearly volumes of surgeon and hospital (Table 1, Table 2). The final model, aimed to be a web-based, easy-to-use tool to assist the preop clinical decision-making included sex, BMI, preop narcotics, primary payer, smoking, marriage, assistive device, diabetes mellitus, race/ethnicity, chronic lung disease, age, and annual case volume of surgeon. The distributions of variables were examined prior to the analysis. Age and BMI were categorized into subgroups consistent with previous studies and the World Health Organization BMI classification of obesity [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

Data analyses

The logistic regression models were developed to predict 90-day deep infections. Causal modeling using directed acyclic graph (DAG) methods was performed using DAGitty (DAG, DAGitty Software R, http://dagitty.net/dags.html) to assure no collider variables in the DAG were included in the model [17]. DAG methods have been shown to reduce bias and confounding relationships between variables during analysis involving multiple variables. [18,19] Collinearity was evaluated by examining correlations among predictors determined by the expert panel, variance inflation factor, and eigenvalues. The data were randomly split into training, validating, and testing sub-datasets (60%, 20%, 20%, respectively). The model was trained, validated, and calibrated using multiple statistical measures, including C-statistics, Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit testing (which describes the association between actual events and predicted probability, P value >.05 indicating a good fit), and Brier score (which describes the mean-squared difference between the predicted probability and the actual outcome, lower Brier score indicates better model calibrations). The model performance was further evaluated by the decile plot of probability of infection (a graphical analog of the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test). The analyses were further stratified by diabetes and opioid use (Appendix). The institutional review board at our institution determined the work to have a status of not being regulated.

Results

Within our dataset, the 90-day infection risk for TKA was 0.47 % (CI: 0.42%-0.52%) and for THA 0.31% (0.28-0.34%). We found that age and BMI had a nonlinear, U-shaped curve for infection risk. Therefore, BMI and age groups were used in the modeling as described above (Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5).

For TKA, modifiable risk factors were found to be BMI, smoking status (never, current, past), and preoperative narcotic use. Regarding BMI, the BMI ranges at increased risk of infection were found to be the cohort with BMI 30-35 and >45. Nonmodifiable risk factors included sex, marital status, preoperative use of assistive devices, and annual surgeon volume <100 cases per year.

For THA, modifiable risk factors were found to be BMI, smoking status, and preoperative narcotic use. Interestingly, patients with BMI <25 and >40 were found to have increased infection risk. Nonmodifiable risk factors were found to be sex, race, type of payer, preoperative use of assistive devices, and annual surgeon volume <100 cases per year.

Logistic regression analysis and the resulting odds ratios allowed unique comparisons of risk factors and examined the degree to which each risk factor individually impacts infection risk when other variables are controlled (Figure 6, Figure 7). For example, in TKA, a BMI of greater than or equal to 45 had a 105% increase in infection risk compared to a BMI of 25-30. Smoking increased the risk of infection by 46%. In examining sex, males had a 173% higher risk of getting an infection in a TKA than females. Preoperative opioid use increases one’s infection risk in TKA by 52%, while being unmarried was associated with a 45% increase in infection. Surgeon volume of less than 100 cases per year was found to increase a patient’s infection risk by 32%, similar to the increased infection risk of 36% associated with assistive device use preoperatively.

Risk factors for infection in total knee arthroplasty. Odds ratios comparing risk factors for infection in total knee arthroplasty.

In the analysis, the impact of race was evaluated. The P values for race were 0.6172 and 0.0199 for TKA and THA analyses, respectively. We analyzed removing race from the modeling. This did not alter the performance dramatically. The maximum percent changes in absolute values across multiple statistical measures <0.6% for knees and <0.8% for hips, including Akaike information criterion, −2log(maximum likelihood), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Race has subsequently been removed from the online calculator.

Similar comparisons were made using the THA infection data. In examining BMI, a BMI ≥ 45 compared to a BMI of 25-30 increased a patient’s infection risk by 600%. A BMI of 40-45 compared to 25-30 increases the infection risk by 450%. While BMI was found to have the strongest association with infection risk in THA, use of assistive devices (26%) and male sex (28%) we associated with increased infection risk similarly while preoperative narcotic use (57%) had roughly twice as much effect on infection. The analysis of nonmodifiable risk factors found that in THA, patients with noncommercial (Medicare and Medicaid) payers had double (114%) the infection risk compared to patients with commercial payers. Surgeon volume <100 cases/year (43%) had the same increase in infection risk as smoking (43%).

Diabetes is one historically considered risk factor for infection that did not show significance in our data. When isolated in the analysis, diabetes was not found to significantly increase one’s infection risk for either TKA or THA.

The C-statistics for the model was 0.72 and the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test had a P value of 0.8091, which is greater than 0.05 indicating a good fit to describe the association between actual events and predicted probability. The Brier score was 0.0036 (misclassification/error rate is 0.36%), which describes the mean squared difference between the predicted probability and the actual outcome.

Discussion

The model is novel because it shows preoperative opioid use, use of assistive devices, and surgeon volume to be risk factors for infection after TKA and THA. In 2013, the universal American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program Surgical Risk Calculator was created as a tool for patients and surgeons to use in preoperative counseling. However, it is not specific to total joint arthroplasty as it incorporates data from all surgical subspecialties and it only captures infections occurring within 30 days postoperatively [20].

For usability as a joint decision-making tool in the clinical settings, a model must be effectively validated, calibrated, and reported [21]. Kunutsor reviewed 18 different total joint infection prediction models and found that the C-index ranged from 0.56 to 0.74 and only 3 risk scores were reported to have a discriminative ability of >0.70 [22]. Comparatively, the MARCQI risk prediction model has a c-index of 0.72, indicating a good discriminative ability. Similar to a previous study looking at recalibration of the NSQIP surgical risk calculator, our subsets of data were split into training, validation sets, and testing (60%, 20%, and 20%, respectively) allowing us to effectively calibrate our model [23].

Our model displays the individual amount that each factor independently influences infection risk, allowing us to build a novel online infection risk calculator accessible to surgeons (Figure 1, Figure 4, Figure 5). Recent infection models by Parvizi and Everhart were able to similarly weigh each infection factor utilizing a points-based system to reach a final infection risk number. These studies did not examine surgeon volume, preoperative opioid use, or preoperative use of assistive devices. These studies capture the majority of infections occurring as they follow patients at least 1 year following surgery, but their application may be limited by the fact that they both include data from only 1 academic center [20,24]. A strength of this study is that it captures data from 68 different sites including 436 surgeons performing TKA and 360 surgeons performing THA, thereby making the results more generalizable to other centers. Our online infection risk calculator allows surgeons to calculate a patient’s preop risk easily and efficiently during their office visit. It also permits them to show the patient in real-time how changing their modifiable risk factors will influence their infection risk. This calculator can be used to empower patients to decrease their preoperative risk of infection, while also guiding surgeons in patient selection.

In considering the significant morbidity and mortality involved in total joint infections, a surgeon must be equipped with the tools to counsel patients on how to reduce their infection risk while also being intentional with patient selection. Risk can be effectively reduced by targeting the factors with the greatest impact. For example, we found BMI, smoking, and preoperative narcotic use to be modifiable risk factors with the strongest influence on risk of infection after TKA and THA. With our infection calculator, a surgeon can show a patient in clinic how changing these risk factors would change their risk of infection. It is ultimately up to the surgeon and patient to decide whether to delay surgery until risks are further optimized or to proceed to the operating room. Our calculator can serve as a helpful tool in shared decision-making.

It is important to note that the infection rate found in this study (TKA was 0.47 % and for THA 0.31%) is below what is commonly seen in the literature for the infection rate in the United States (0.72-2% TKA and 0.57%-0.88% THA) [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Although this study does not determine the cause of this, the 10-step infection-prevention protocol that is applied at every MARCQI center could be a contributing factor [25]. Our study does not capture later PJI but only early PJI within 90 days of surgery. This likely decreased our overall infection rate, as we likely did not capture a hematogenous infections and indolent infections. Although, when looking at infection rates after primary and revision TKAs and THAs at one academic center in the midwestern United States, Everhart found 52.9% of surgical site infections (SSIs) occur greater than 30 days postoperatively with an average time for infection appearance to be 63 days postoperatively [20]. We believe our model captures a large portion of SSIs because our data are abstracted to 90 days postoperatively. While our calculator does not capture late infections, we believe it is an accurate model of risk of early infection rate.

A study by Katz examined the relationship between surgeon and hospital volume on complications after TKA. They found surgeons performing >200 TKAs/year had a statistically significant lower complication rate compared to surgeons performing ≤ 25 TKAs/year. Furthermore, they found that surgeons who performed >50 TKAs/year had a 40% lower infection rate compared to surgeons performing ≤ 25 TKAs/year [26]. In a similar study, Katz showed lower surgeon THA volume to be correlated with higher infection risk [27]. Similar to the Katz studies, this study found surgeon volume <100 TKAs/year having a 32% increased rate of infection and surgeon volume <100 THAs/year having a 43% increased rate of infection. The annual volume of 100 per year was determined early in the establishment of the MARCQI database and infection calculator based on consensus among the clinical champions at the various surgical sites [25]. One key limitation of the Katz studies is that their samples in each study were limited to the Medicare population and may not be true when extrapolated to all patients [26,27]. Alternatively, our data include private, Medicaid, and Medicare insured patients, again increasing generalizability to all patients and centers. Although these data do not reveal the factors causing surgeon volume to affect infection risk, they support previous literature that higher surgical volumes appear to have a protective effect against infection [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. While the 100-case threshold was determined by experienced surgeons, who serve as the clinical champions. This is based on the average number of yearly cases that an individual surgeon has in the registry over the entire data time period. The 100-case threshold has been previously used in a large meta-analysis of 3 national joint registries [32]. For future models, a sensitivity analysis could be helpful to determine the most accurate volume threshold.

Similar to our data, multiple studies looking at infection rates of hip and knee arthroplasties at the Mayo Clinic have found a bimodal distribution of infection rates relative to BMI [33,34]. Malnutrition and challenges with soft tissue envelopes can occur at high and low BMIs. We know patients with low BMI can often have less nutritional reserve, which can affect their ability to heal surgical wounds [35]. A surgeon must critically assess the soft tissue envelope surrounding a joint prior to any surgery while also considering the patient’s BMI as well as general metabolic and nutritional status.

In a large-scale analysis of SSIs in the National Healthcare Safety Network database, male sex was found to increase the risk for infection after TKA by 142% compared to our data revealing a 173% increase [36]. Other recent registry studies have also found men to have an increased risk of infection [37]. Further investigation is needed to identify the underlying causes of males having increased infection risk. Identifying these causes may allow presurgical risk interventions to minimize this increased infection risk.

Diabetes is one historically considered risk factor for infection that did not show significance in our data. Despite previous studies showing diabetes as a significant risk factor for infection, our data did not find this to be significant. This may be secondary to the fact that our database did not distinguish severity of disease (number of diabetic complications) which has been shown to have a strong association with PJI risk, does not have A1c values on patients, and treats all diabetes as a binary variable [36]. The diabetic patients in our study were all optimized with a well-controlled A1C. Clinicians using this risk calculator should also consider this in their patient education.

Other limitations of our model include data from one geographic region (Michigan) within the United States along with the likelihood of a small number of infections being missed by our chart abstractors. The out of state migration rate in the United States has been reported to be just under 1% [11]. As a registry study, the model is based only on associations and cannot determine causation. The factors identified such as use of assistive devices and preop opioids may reflect other factors that influence the actual infection rate. Previous research has shown that acute and chronic opioid use has immunosuppressive effects [38]. We suspect patients who used assistive devices preoperatively had decreased mobility before surgery which likely translated to decreased mobility after surgery and increased fall risk. Postoperative falls can often lead to wound dehiscence or hematoma formation which can be precursors to infection [39]. Also, the overall infection rates found in this study were significantly lower than those outlined in previous studies, which may result in lower predicted infection risk values [[5], [6], [7]]. These decreased infection rates may be due to follow-up only to 90 days. We believe our model accurately predicts acute infection risk. Unlike the Parvizi model, our study also does not currently have an external validation completed yet, but this is currently in process [10,24,25]. Before widespread clinical use, external validation in other states and national registries is needed. We have included variables in our model based on previous literature, expert opinion of surgeons, and what was included in the database. We acknowledge a limitation is that we were only able to include variables included in the database, whereas including other variables such as preoperative nutritional status could improve the model. Furthermore, including more granular data in our model, such as narcotic dosage and duration of use as well as type of assistive device used could be helpful to increase the accuracy of the model. We hope to build upon our current model in creating new models to predict long-term infection risk while also including more granular data points.

Conclusions

With the significant morbidity and increased mortality seen with prosthetic joint infections, the patient needs to be involved in shared decision-making before undergoing arthroplasty. Surgeons must be equipped with accurate infection models that can educate the patient on their current estimated risk of infection along with empowering them to change their modifiable risk factors. We present a novel infection prediction model with a simple, easy to use online infection risk calculator accessible via internet webpage to help guide patient selection while also empowering patients to act preoperatively to decrease their risk of infection. Our infection model predicts acute 90-day infection risk with current goals for future models to predict long-term risk.

Appendix B: Subgroup analysis of preoperative opioid use and diabetes

Appendix B: Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analyses were performed by preop opioid use and diabetes. The crude analysis showed that in hips, the chi-square P value for diabetes and infection is P < .0001 and chi-square P value for opioid use and infection is P < .0001. In knees, the chi-square P value for diabetes and infection is P = .0472 and chi-square P value for opioid use and infection is P < .0001.

Multiple regression models were further stratified by preop opioid use and diabetes.

a.For patients not using opioid, the significant factors associated with hip infections include BMI, sex, race, primary payer, assistive devices use, and surgeon volume. For knees, significant factors include BMI, sex, marital status, assistive devices use, and surgeon volume. All these P values <0.05.

For patients not using opioid, the significant factors associated with hip infections include BMI, sex, race, primary payer, assistive devices use, and surgeon volume. For knees, significant factors include BMI, sex, marital status, assistive devices use, and surgeon volume. All these P values <0.05.

For patients using opioid, the significant factors associated with hip infections include BMI, primary payer, and diabetes, with knee infections include sex and chronic lung disease. All these P values <0.05.

b.For patients with diabetes, the significant factors associated with hip infections include BMI, opioid use, and surgeon volume, with knee infections include age, sex, and opioid use. All P values <0.05.

For patients with diabetes, the significant factors associated with hip infections include BMI, opioid use, and surgeon volume, with knee infections include age, sex, and opioid use. All P values <0.05.

For patients without diabetes, the significant factors associated with hip infections include BMI, sex, smoking, primary payer, opioid use, assistive devices use, and surgeon volume, with knee infections include BMI, sex, smoking, marital status, opioid use, assistive device use, and surgeon volume. All P values <0.05. The goodness-of-fit statistics and C-statistics showed good model performances for stratified analysis. This has been included in an appendix for the paper.
