Fun Exercise for Older Adults (FEXO): study protocol for a randomised controlled trial on intrinsic capacity, adherence and motivation
Authors: Pedro Azanon-Nogueira, Celia García-Lucas, Carlos Salvador-Huerta, Juan José Amer-Cuenca, Miguel Picher Martínez, Mar García-Zamora, Juan Francisco Lisón, Vicent Benavent-Caballer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAgeing is associated with declines in physical and cognitive function that increase the risk of disability and dependence. Intrinsic capacity (IC), proposed by the WHO, provides a multidimensional framework to assess health in older adults. This study aims to evaluate whether a multicomponent recreational exercise programme (Fun Exercise for Older Adults (FEXO)) improves IC, motivation and adherence compared with a conventional programme (OTAGO).Methods and analysisA randomised controlled trial with two groups (2:1 ratio) will be conducted among 120 community-dwelling older adults (≥60 years). Participants will be randomly assigned to FEXO (intervention) or OTAGO (control). Both programmes will consist of 3 weekly sessions for 14 weeks. Primary outcomes: IC, assessed through validated measures (Short Physical Performance Battery, Mini Nutritional Assessment, Mini-Mental State Examination, Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia and sensory evaluation), motivation (BREQ-3) and adherence rate. Secondary outcomes include body composition, cardiovascular parameters, frailty, cognition and resilience (PRIFOR). Data will be analysed using two-way ANOVA (group×time) under an intention-to-treat approach. Effect sizes (ηp²) and 95% CIs will be reported. Additional analyses (correlation, regression, mediation and moderation) will explore associations and intervention effects.Ethics and disseminationApproved by the Ethics Committee of Universidad CEU Cardenal Herrera (Ref. CEEI23/487 and CEEI25/639). Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and scientific conferences. The findings of this study will contribute to improving evidence-based strategies for promoting healthy ageing and will support the development of more engaging and effective exercise interventions for older adults.Trial registration numberThis study has been prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT07133568).

Introduction

Population ageing

Population ageing represents a major global challenge.13 According to recent estimates from the WHO, by 2050 more than 20% of the global population will be aged ≥60 years.4 In Western Europe, this demographic shift has shown a continuous rise over the past decades and is expected to increase further.5 Ageing is associated with physical and physiological changes and a higher prevalence of age-related conditions that increase the risk of multimorbidity, geriatric syndromes, disability, hospitalisation, institutionalisation, dependency and mortality.4 6 This situation highlights the need for comprehensive approaches to assess the health of older adults, including domains such as physical function, cognitive status, emotional well-being, independence in daily activities and health-related quality of life.7 Importantly, such assessment must remain applicable across different living situations (community-dwelling, institutionalised or hospitalised) and health states.

Intrinsic capacity

To address these challenges, the WHO introduced in 2015 the concept of intrinsic capacity (IC), defined as ‘the composite of all the physical and mental capacities that an individual can draw on at any point in time’.8 IC comprises five key domains: locomotion (mobility, muscle strength and balance), vitality (nutritional status, metabolism and energy), cognition (memory, attention and executive function), psychological (emotional well-being and mental health) and sensory (vision and hearing).9

Since its introduction, IC has been widely adopted as a comprehensive metric for healthy ageing, in contrast to frailty models that focus on individual vulnerability to stressors such as illness, hospitalisation or bereavement.9 IC instead reflects physiological reserves and functional potential.81012 Its multidimensional structure has been validated, and strong predictive value has been shown for decline in activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL).10 11

Moreover, IC is recognised as a key predictor of future functional capacity in older adults, highlighting recovery potential and physiological resilience. Evidence indicates that a 10% decline in IC is associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes, including frailty, sarcopenia, falls, functional decline, lower quality of life, prolonged hospital stays and increased short-term and long-term mortality.1318

Multicomponent exercise

Regular physical exercise is one of the most effective strategies to prevent or attenuate cognitive and physical decline associated with ageing, as well as the social and psychological challenges that may arise later in life.1922 Current evidence supports the implementation of exercise programmes that integrate multiple modalities to promote healthy ageing and preserve functional capacity.42325

Recent international consensus guidelines recommend strength training two to three times per week with systematic progression, including power-based exercises; moderate-intensity aerobic training 3–7 days per week; and balance training 1–7 days per week to prevent falls.24 The consensus highlights that structured and progressive multicomponent programmes combining these modalities are the most effective approach to maintain functional independence in older adults.24

Several multicomponent exercise programmes have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing disability in older populations. One of the most widely studied is the OTAGO programme,26 which has shown efficacy in reducing frailty and improving strength, mobility and balance.27

Adherence

Despite the well-established benefits of physical activity, inactivity and sedentary behaviour remain highly prevalent in older adults.23 28 Enhancing motivation and adherence is therefore essential when designing exercise programmes for this population. An umbrella review including eight studies identified enjoyment of exercise sessions as one of the most influential factors in improving adherence among older adults.29

To date, no studies have implemented multicomponent exercise programmes incorporating a playful or recreational component specifically aimed at enhancing motivation and adherence in this population.

Accordingly, the aim of this study is to determine whether a playful and recreational multicomponent exercise programme—designed to increase enjoyment and engagement—can improve adherence, motivation and, consequently, IC in older adults.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study will be a randomised clinical trial with a two-group design in a 2:1 ratio (experimental group: control group). This study protocol has been developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items for Randomised Trials (SPIRIT) recommendations to enhance protocol quality (see online supplemental file 4). Recruitment and intervention are planned to start in September 2025, and the study is expected to be completed by December 2025.

Ethics committee and registration

The study design complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the CEU Cardenal Herrera University (reference numbers CEEI23/487 and CEEI25/639). Participation in the study will be voluntary and will require written informed consent from each participant (see online supplemental file 2). Eligible participants will be informed about all relevant aspects of the study prior to beginning their exercise programme (online supplemental file 3). This study has been prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT07133568).

Participants: recruitment and eligibility criteria

A total of 120 individuals will participate in this study. Recruitment is expected to take place between September 2025 and November 2025. Eligible participants will be community-dwelling adults aged ≥60 years who are able to ambulate independently or with an assistive device and are willing and able to attend the scheduled exercise sessions and assessments throughout the study period. Exclusion criteria will be (1) severe psychiatric or psychological disorders, (2) physical inability to exercise, (3) participation in any other multicomponent exercise programme during the intervention period, (4) cognitive impairment preventing understanding and/or performance of the programme, (5) fractures or uncontrolled cardiovascular diseases that contraindicate exercise and (6) any other condition for which exercise is contraindicated. Participants may discontinue the intervention due to adverse events, medical contraindications or personal requests.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants will be recruited from two sources. First, participants will be recruited in Alfara del Patriarca, a small municipality in L’Horta Nord (Valencia, Spain), with a population of approximately 3700 residents. Recruitment in this setting will be conducted through a community-based database of older adults who have previously participated in cognitive screening and related community health activities (DeCo Chair) and who agreed to be contacted for research purposes.

Second, participants will be recruited through AGMT (Asociación Grupo Mayores de Telefónica), a non-profit association composed mainly of retired and pre-retired Telefónica employees and open to older adults in the city of Valencia. This second source represents an urban-based population. In both settings, individuals will be included if they express interest in participating and meet the eligibility criteria.

The randomisation process will follow a controlled design with partial masking. Participants who meet inclusion criteria will be randomly assigned to one of the two intervention groups: OTAGO or Fun Exercise for Older Adults (FEXO). To ensure balanced group distribution, stratified randomisation will be performed by frailty level and sex. Randomisation will be carried out using a computer-generated allocation sequence prepared by an independent researcher not involved in recruitment, assessment or intervention delivery.

Participants will be informed that they will be assigned to one of the study groups, but they will not know which specific programme corresponds to their allocation. A single physiotherapist will deliver the exercise sessions for both groups and will be aware of group allocation, while the evaluation personnel will remain blinded to the group assignments.

Measurements will be conducted at two time points: baseline (t0) and post-intervention (t1). One physiotherapist will deliver the exercise intervention, while the remaining physiotherapists will conduct the participant assessments. Two of these physiotherapists will also be involved in the design and planning of the exercise programme.

The physicians will have different roles within the study. One physician, a geriatrician, will participate in the clinical assessment of participants. The second physician, a specialist in sports medicine, will contribute to the design and planning of the exercise programme together with the two physiotherapists. The pharmacist will participate in the assessment process, specifically in the evaluation of nutritional status within the vitality domain of intrinsic capacity.

Intervention

Participants who agree to take part will be randomly assigned to the intervention group (FEXO programme) or the control group (OTAGO programme), stratified by frailty and sex. Both groups will complete three non-consecutive weekly sessions over a 14-week period. The same physiotherapist will conduct both programmes, and the instructor-to-participant ratio will not exceed 1:15 to ensure appropriate supervision and safety.30

Exercise sessions will take place in a large indoor room equipped with non-slip flooring and air conditioning. Facilities will be provided by AGMT and the municipality of Alfara del Patriarca.

The control group will receive the previously published Otago Exercise Programme (OEP).26 Therefore, this study will use an active control group rather than a no-treatment or usual-care control. The OEP was selected as a standardised and widely used exercise-based comparator, allowing the effects of the experimental intervention to be evaluated against an established exercise programme. The programme will be delivered in group sessions three times per week on non-consecutive days for 14 weeks, with the same frequency and duration as the intervention group. Sessions will be supervised by the same physiotherapist to ensure consistency in programme delivery. The OEP will include strength, balance and functional exercises according to the standard protocol, with exercise progression tailored to each participant’s functional capacity. As an adaptation to the original protocol, elastic bands will be used instead of ankle weights for resistance exercises.

The intervention group will complete our novel playful, recreational, multicomponent exercise programme designed for older adults: Fun Exercise for Older Adults (FEXO).

To ensure treatment fidelity, the physiotherapist delivering both programmes will follow a standardised session checklist. Regular supervision meetings with the research team will be conducted to maintain consistency across sessions.

Participants in the present trial were not formally involved in the co-design of the intervention. However, FEXO was developed based on current evidence on multicomponent exercise for older adults and refined through the lead investigator’s prior clinical experience delivering group-based exercise programmes to other older adult populations in community and institutional settings. During this earlier development phase, programme components were informally tested and adjusted according to observed preferences, tolerability, engagement and barriers to participation. This process informed key features of the programme, including group-based delivery, individual adaptation, close supervision, music and ludic-recreational elements intended to enhance acceptability and adherence.

Programme structure

The programme will last 14 weeks and will include a 2-week familiarisation phase followed by three progressive levels based on participants’ functional capacity. Allocation to one of the three levels will be based on baseline performance on the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), a validated and widely used measure in older adults that enables pragmatic stratification of physical performance and supports individual tailoring of exercise prescription.31 32 In this study, the SPPB will be used to guide exercise adaptation according to functional status, rather than to classify participants using a frailty index intended to identify frailty as a clinical syndrome. This approach is consistent with the Vivifrail framework, in which the SPPB is used as a core tool to guide exercise prescription according to functional capacity.31 32

Each level will last 4 weeks, increasing intensity (Borg scale), number of repetitions and elastic band resistance. A detailed description of the intervention is provided in online supplemental file 1.

Materials

Accessible, portable, low-cost materials will be used,33 including chairs with and without armrests, elastic bands, balls, scarves, pool noodles, handheld shakers (eg, egg shakers or maracas), a bubble machine and a portable speaker to play music during the exercise sessions. In addition, a visual panel displaying the modified Borg scale will be used during the sessions to indicate the target exercise intensity and help participants monitor their perceived exertion while performing the exercises.

Session structure

Each session will include (1) welcome and explanation of session intensity (modified Borg scale); (2) warm-up using dance-like movements; (3) main section including strength, balance, coordination and cognitive stimulation exercises; (4) cool-down with guided relaxation focused on breathing and body scanning; and (5) closing remarks with positive reinforcement and advice to maintain an active lifestyle.

Outcomes and measurements

Assessments will be performed at baseline (t0) and post-intervention (t1). Evaluators will be blinded to group allocation and will administer the same test throughout the study to avoid inter-observer bias. Participants who discontinue the intervention will be invited to complete outcome assessments.

Sociodemographic data (sex, age, weight, height, BMI, smoking status, marital status and educational level) will be collected at baseline. Moreover, the number of medications taken chronically (polypharmacy), comorbidities and number of falls in the past year will be collected at baseline.

Primary outcome

The primary outcomes will be intrinsic capacity (IC), participants’ motivation to exercise and adherence to the intervention. Motivation to exercise will be assessed using the Behavioural Regulation in Exercise Questionnaire (BREQ-3),34 based on Self-Determination Theory. Adherence will be calculated as the percentage of exercise sessions attended.

IC will be assessed using specific measures for each domain, in accordance with the recommendations of López-Ortiz et al.9 The functional domain will be evaluated using the SPPB, a brief test battery that includes standing balance, gait speed and the 5-times Sit-to-Stand test (5-STS). The SPPB has been shown to predict falls, morbidity, and mortality in older adults.35 The sensory domain will be assessed through two questions addressing visual and hearing difficulties, considering habitual visual correction or hearing aids when applicable. The cognitive domain will be evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), which assesses five areas: spatiotemporal orientation, memory, attention/calculation, language, and visuospatial function.36 An unauthorised version of the Spanish MMSE was used by the study team without permission; however, this has now been rectified with PAR. The MMSE is a copyrighted instrument and may not be used or reproduced in whole or in part, in any form or language, or by any means without written permission of PAR. The psychological domain will be assessed using the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia (CSDD), which has also been validated for use in older adults without dementia.37 The vitality domain will be evaluated using the Mini Nutritional Assessment, which identifies malnutrition or risk of malnutrition in older adults.38 Each domain will be scored from 0 to 2, giving a total IC score ranging from 0 to 10.

Secondary outcomes

Anthropometric measurements will include height, weight, waist circumference and calf circumference. Bioelectrical impedance will be used to determine body mass index and percentages of body fat, water and muscle mass.

Blood pressure (systolic and diastolic) and resting heart rate will be measured using a validated sphygmomanometer following European Society of Hypertension guidelines. Heart rate will also be monitored during sessions using Polar Verity Sense devices to determine mean and maximum heart rate.

Frailty will be assessed using Fried’s criteria10: unintentional weight loss, fatigue, low physical activity, grip strength and gait speed.

Grip strength: measured with a hand dynamometer in the dominant hand, following standardised positions and protocols. Three trials will be performed, and the mean value will be recorded.

10-metre walk test (10MWT)39: habitual and maximal gait speed assessed across a 6-metre timed section within a 10-metre walkway. Three trials at habitual speed and three at maximal speed will be conducted; mean values will be analysed.

Additional functional assessments will include Sit-to-Stand-5 (STS-5) for lower-limb strength and endurance; the Berg Balance Scale (BBS)40 for static/dynamic balance and fall risk; the Timed Up and Go (TUG)41 for functional mobility; and the Modified TUG dual-task (manual and cognitive conditions).42

Depression will be assessed using the 30-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS).43 Cognitive decline will be assessed using Trail Making Test-A and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).44

Resilience will be evaluated using the Physical Resilience Instrument for Older Adults (PRIFOR).45

Sample size justification

The required sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.2 (F tests, ANOVA: repeated measures, within-between interaction), assuming a significance level of α=0.05, statistical power (1–β) = 0.80 and an effect size of f=0.149 for the primary functional outcome, based on a previous study conducted by our group.46 This calculation indicated that 96 participants are required. To account for potential attrition of up to 25%, in line with the literature on dropout rates in intervention studies, the final target sample size will be 120 participants.

Statistical method

The Shapiro-Wilk test will be used to assess the normality of the dependent variables, as it is more suitable for moderate sample sizes. A two-factor ANOVA (group×time) will be used to compare the effects of the recreational multicomponent intervention on the dependent variables, with time (t0, t1) as the within-group factor and group (experimental vs control) as the between-group factor.

Effect sizes (ηp²) and their 95% CIs will be calculated. Interpretation of ηp² will follow commonly accepted thresholds in the literature: small (≈0.01), moderate (≈0.06) and large (≈0.14). Pearson correlations, multiple regression and mediation and moderation analyses will also be conducted to explore relationships and intervention effects.

Statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS V. 27.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

Data management

All data will be entered into a database using unique study codes and stored securely on password-protected computers. Only the data manager, who is independent from the research team and free of competing interests, will have access to the data. A data monitoring committee will not be required because the study involves minimal risk. Any relevant protocol amendments occurring during the study will be reported to the trial registry and to the journal in which this protocol is published. No interim analyses or stopping rules are planned.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this protocol. However, results will be shared with participating community organisations.

Participants and community partners will be involved in the dissemination process by reviewing plain-language summaries of the results.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee of CEU Cardenal Herrera University (CEEI23/487 and CEEI25/639). All participants will provide written informed consent prior to enrolment. The intervention involves minimal risk, and all exercise sessions will be supervised by a physiotherapist to ensure participant safety. Any adverse events will be recorded and reported to the ethics committee. Data will be handled in accordance with GDPR, anonymised before analysis and stored securely.

The results of the study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conference presentations, and reports provided to AGMT and local community partners. Participants will receive a plain-language summary of the findings. De-identified data may be made available on reasonable request. Patients were not involved in the design of this protocol but will be involved in the dissemination of results.

No identifiable personal data will be published. Any protocol amendments will be reported to the ethics committee, updated in the trial registry, and reflected in subsequent publications.

The full protocol, anonymised dataset and statistical code will be made available on reasonable request.
