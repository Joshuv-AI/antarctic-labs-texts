Addition of thiotepa to total body irradiation-based conditioning in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for relapsed/refractory acute leukemia and lymphoma: a propensity score-matched retrospective study
Authors: Minglu Li, Shuqin Zhang, Xiaoyong Man, Hongxia Wen, Wei Wei, Yixin Yang, Jingbo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Relapsed/refractory (R/R) acute leukemia (AL) carries a poor prognosis, with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) the primary curative option. Total body irradiation (TBI)-based conditioning is a cornerstone, but optimizing intensity remains challenging. Thiotepa, a bifunctional alkylating agent, may enhance disease control when added to TBI, but data in R/R AL are lacking. Methods: This single-center retrospective study (2020–2025) included R/R AL and lymphoma patients receiving TBI-based conditioning with (TBI + TT) or without (TBI) thiotepa. Propensity score matching (1:2, caliper = 0.2) used 9 covariates. Primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS). Secondary endpoints included relapse, non-relapse mortality (NRM), engraftment, GVHD, CMV infection, and mucositis. Kaplan-Meier, Fine-Gray competing risk, and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analyses were employed. Results: After matching, 48 TBI + TT and 86 TBI patients were analyzed. OS (HR = 0.901, 95% CI 0.569–1.425, P = 0.655) and DFS (HR = 0.809, 95% CI 0.504–1.299, P = 0.380) were comparable. The 1-year relapse cumulative incidence was lower with TBI + TT (6.7% vs. 17.8%, Gray’s P = 0.035; multivariable sHR = 0.280, 95% CI 0.083–0.942, P = 0.040), though hypothesis-generating given only 3 relapse events. NRM was comparable (Gray’s P = 0.374). Mucositis was significantly higher with TBI + TT (81.2% vs. 64.0%, P = 0.049). IPTW confirmed comparable OS (P = 0.690) and DFS (P = 0.975). Conclusion: Adding thiotepa to TBI-based conditioning for R/R AL is associated with lower relapse incidence without compromising OS, DFS, engraftment, or GVHD, at the cost of increased mucosal toxicity. Introduction

Relapsed or refractory (R/R) acute leukemia and lymphoma remain a major therapeutic challenge in hematologic oncology. For acute myeloid leukemia (AML), relapse after initial therapy is common, and mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL) carries an even poorer prognosis with limited consensus on optimal treatment [1, 2]. Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is the only potentially curative option for R/R disease across these subtypes [2, 3], leveraging the graft-versus-leukemia effect to eradicate residual malignant cells. However, disease status at transplantation is a dominant predictor of outcomes, and patients with active disease or measurable residual disease (MRD) face sharply diminished chances of sustained remission [4, 5], underscoring the need for more effective conditioning approaches that maximize disease control without prohibitive toxicity.

Total body irradiation (TBI)-based myeloablative conditioning has long been a cornerstone of preparative regimens, particularly in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). The FORUM trial demonstrated that fractionated TBI at 12 Gy with etoposide was superior to chemotherapy-only conditioning in pediatric high-risk ALL [6], and meta-analyses have confirmed a survival advantage for TBI-based regimens [7]. In haploidentical transplantation with post-transplant cyclophosphamide, TBI-based conditioning has been associated with lower non-relapse mortality and better leukemia-free survival [8]. However, TBI efficacy is dose-dependent and must be balanced against pulmonary toxicity [9], long-term pediatric sequelae including growth retardation and secondary malignancies [10–12], and the need for conformal techniques such as total marrow and lymphoid irradiation to spare organs at risk [13]. Thiotepa, a bifunctional alkylating agent with excellent tissue penetration and blood-brain barrier crossing, has emerged as a key component of TBI-free myeloablative conditioning. The thiotepa-busulfan-fludarabine (TBF) regimen is widely used in haploidentical transplantation [14, 15], with favorable outcomes reported in older adults with ALL [16] and R/R AML [17]. In autologous transplantation for lymphoma, thiotepa-containing regimens have demonstrated durable disease control across multiple histologic subtypes, including primary central nervous system lymphoma [18–21]. A promising strategy involves adding thiotepa directly to TBI-based conditioning rather than substituting for irradiation, as demonstrated in a pediatric cohort where this combination was associated with lower relapse rates without increased transplant-related mortality [22]. Intensified conditioning with agents such as decitabine has also shown encouraging results [14, 23]. Despite these advances, relapse remains the primary cause of treatment failure in R/R patients [8, 24, 25], and most existing comparisons have evaluated TBI and chemotherapy as mutually exclusive alternatives, primarily in patients transplanted in first complete remission [24, 25, 26, 27].

A critical unanswered question is whether adding thiotepa to a TBI-based platform yields superior outcomes compared with TBI-based conditioning alone in the R/R setting. The relative efficacy of TBI-based and thiotepa-based conditioning in the post-CAR T-cell era for relapsed lymphomas has not been systematically studied [28], and the ability of intensified conditioning to overcome MRD remains contested [23, 29, 30]. To address this evidence gap, the present study performed a single-center retrospective analysis with propensity score matching, comparing TBI-based conditioning with and without thiotepa in R/R acute leukemia and lymphoma patients, with overall survival and disease-free survival as primary endpoints.

Methods

Patients

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at the Department of Hematology, Aerospace Center Hospital, Beijing, China. Patients with R/R AL who underwent allo-HSCT between January 2020 and June 2025 were consecutively screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) confirmed diagnosis of AML, ALL, mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL), or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) with relapsed or refractory disease status; (2) age less than or equal to 60 years; and (3) receipt of TBI-based myeloablative conditioning with or without thiotepa. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; and (2) active, uncontrolled infection at the time of conditioning. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Aerospace Center Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Conditioning regimens

Patients were categorized into two groups based on the conditioning regimen received. The TBI group received a TBI-based regimen consisting of TBI combined with either CLAG (clofarabine, cytarabine, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) plus cyclophosphamide (CY) and anti-thymocyte globulin-Fresenius (ATG-F), or FLAG (fludarabine, cytarabine, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) plus CY and ATG-F. The TBI + TT group received an identical backbone with the addition of thiotepa at a dose of 5 mg/kg/day administered intravenously for 2 consecutive days (total dose 10 mg/kg) on days − 7 and − 6, preceding the administration of TBI. TBI was delivered using a linear accelerator with a total dose of 8–12 Gy, fractionated over 3–4 days, with dose adjustments based on patient age, disease type, and organ function. GVHD prophylaxis was uniform across both groups and consisted of calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine A or tacrolimus) combined with short-course methotrexate. ATG-F was administered for ex vivo T-cell depletion in recipients of haploidentical or unrelated donor grafts.

Endpoint

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS), defined as the time from allo-HSCT to death from any cause, and disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the time from allo-HSCT to relapse or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Secondary endpoints included the cumulative incidence of relapse, defined as morphologic or molecular evidence of disease recurrence; non-relapse mortality (NRM), defined as death without preceding relapse; neutrophil and platelet engraftment; acute GVHD (aGVHD) graded by consensus criteria; chronic GVHD (cGVHD); CMV infection detected by polymerase chain reaction; and mucositis. Safety assessments additionally included mucositis-associated infections and mucositis-associated mortality.

Statistical analysis

Propensity score matching was performed using logistic regression to estimate the probability of receiving TBI + TT conditioning. Covariates included in the propensity score model were age, diagnosis, gender, disease status prior to transplant, central nervous system leukemia involvement, donor type, donor gender, minimal residual disease (MRD) status, and bone marrow blast percentage. A 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm was employed with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD of less than 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance.

Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. OS and DFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Cumulative incidence functions for relapse and NRM were estimated using the Fine-Gray competing risk model and compared using Gray’s test. Relapse and NRM were reported as 1-year and 3-year cumulative incidences, while CMV infection and aGVHD were reported as 100-day cumulative incidences.

Sensitivity analyses included subgroup analyses stratified by disease type (AML versus ALL) and age group (pediatric [age < 18 years] versus adult), as well as inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using the full unmatched cohort. Interaction tests were performed to evaluate effect modification by disease type and age group. A post hoc statistical power analysis was conducted to determine the range of hazard ratios detectable given the observed number of events. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 415 patients were initially identified during the study period, comprising 337 patients in the TBI group and 78 patients in the TBI + TT group. After data cleaning, 400 patients were retained for analysis (337 TBI, 63 TBI + TT), with 15 patients in the TBI + TT group excluded due to missing overall survival time data. Following 1:2 propensity score matching, 134 patients were included in the matched cohort (48 TBI + TT, 86 TBI). All covariates achieved adequate balance with SMD values below the 0.1 threshold, with the exception of donor type (SMD = 0.135) and bone marrow blast percentage (SMD = 0.122), which slightly exceeded the threshold but remained within an acceptable range for comparative analysis. Engraftment, GVHD and other complications

Neutrophil engraftment was achieved at a median of 13 (11.75-15) days in the TBI + TT group versus 14 (11–17) days in the TBI group (P = 0.208). Platelet engraftment occurred at a median of 15.5 (12.75-42) days in the TBI + TT group versus 16 (12-27.5) days in the TBI group (P = 0.450). The success rates of neutrophil engraftment were 48/48 (100) in the TBI + TT group and 83/86 (96.5) in the TBI group (P = 0.552), while platelet engraftment success rates were 41/48 (85.4) and 71/86 (82.6), respectively (P = 0.809). Neither engraftment parameter differed significantly between groups, indicating that the addition of thiotepa did not impair hematologic recovery. The cumulative incidence curves for engraftment are shown in Fig. S2.

With respect to transplant-related complications, the incidence of grades II-IV aGVHD was 16/48 (33.3%) in the TBI + TT group compared with 34/86 (39.5%) in the TBI group (P = 0.577). The incidence of cGVHD was 19/48 (39.6%) versus 36/86 (41.9%) (P = 0.856), and CMV infection occurred in 20/48 (41.7%) versus 42/86 (48.8%) (P = 0.473). Notably, mucositis was significantly more frequent in the TBI + TT group (39/48 (81.2%) vs. 55/86 (64%), P = 0.049). Mucositis-associated infections showed a trend toward higher incidence in the TBI + TT group (31/48 (64.6%) vs. 42/86 (48.8%), P = 0.103), though this did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, mucositis-associated mortality did not differ between groups (9/48 (18.8%) vs. 17/86 (19.8%), P = 1.000) (Figs. 1 and 2). These results are detailed in Table 2 and visualized in Fig. 3.

Competing risk analysis

In the competing risk analysis, the 1-year cumulative incidence of relapse was 6.7% in the TBI + TT group compared with 17.8% in the TBI group, and the 3-year cumulative incidence was 6.7% versus 21.9%, respectively (Gray’s P = 0.035). The univariable subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR) for relapse was 0.279 (95% CI 0.083–0.932, P = 0.038), and the multivariable sHR was 0.28 (95% CI 0.083–0.942, P = 0.040). A total of 3 relapse events occurred in the TBI + TT group and 20 in the TBI group.

For NRM, the 1-year cumulative incidence was 14% in the TBI + TT group versus 21.1% in the TBI group, and the 3-year cumulative incidence was 22.8% versus 28%, respectively (Gray’s P = 0.374). The univariable sHR for NRM was 0.676 (95% CI 0.321–1.425, P = 0.300). These findings are presented in Table 3; Fig. 2.

Survival analysis

The 1-year OS rate was 51.6% in the TBI + TT group versus 50.8% in the TBI group, and the 3-year OS rate was 35.9% versus 34.3%, respectively (log-rank P = 0.660). The hazard ratio for OS was 0.901 (95% CI 0.569–1.425, P = 0.655), and the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (Schoenfeld P = 0.589).

For DFS, the 1-year rate was 51.7% in the TBI + TT group versus 42.9% in the TBI group, and the 3-year DFS rate was 34.9% versus 30.7%, respectively (log-rank P = 0.378). The hazard ratio for DFS was 0.809 (95% CI 0.504–1.299, P = 0.380), with a satisfied proportional hazards assumption (Schoenfeld P = 0.590). It should be noted that at 5 years, the number of patients at risk was small (TBI + TT: 1, TBI: 11), and these estimates should be interpreted with caution. Sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings across disease types and age groups. In the AML subgroup (n = 90), the OS HR was 0.913 (95% CI 0.516–1.616, P = 0.755) and the DFS HR was 0.906 (95% CI 0.5-1.641, P = 0.744). In the ALL subgroup (n = 27), the OS HR was 0.757 (95% CI 0.3-1.913, P = 0.556) and the DFS HR was 0.618 (95% CI 0.252–1.514, P = 0.292).

In the pediatric subgroup (n = 31), the OS HR was 1.001 (95% CI 0.394–2.542, P = 0.998) and the DFS HR was 0.962 (95% CI 0.369–2.506, P = 0.936). In the adult subgroup (n = 103), the OS HR was 0.869 (95% CI 0.521–1.448, P = 0.590) and the DFS HR was 0.764 (95% CI 0.454–1.285, P = 0.309).

IPTW analysis using the full unmatched cohort (n = 400) yielded an OS HR of 1.083 (95% CI 0.731–1.605, P = 0.690) and a DFS HR of 1.006 (95% CI 0.679–1.492, P = 0.975), consistent with the primary PSM analysis. Interaction tests for disease type (OS P = 0.938, DFS P = 0.499) and age group (OS P = 0.744, DFS P = 0.590) were not statistically significant, indicating no evidence of effect modification by these factors.

A post hoc power analysis indicated that with 81 OS events, the study was powered to detect hazard ratios of 0.733 or less, or 1.364 or greater, at 80% power. For DFS, with 86 events, the detectable hazard ratio range was 0.739 to 1.353. These results are summarized in Table 5, and the subgroup forest plot is provided in Fig. S3.

Discussion

This propensity score-matched analysis using a 1:2 matching design with cluster-robust standard errors provides real-world evidence that augmenting TBI-based conditioning with thiotepa for patients with R/R acute leukemia and lymphoma is associated with comparable OS (HR = 0.901, 95% CI 0.569–1.425, P = 0.655) and DFS (HR = 0.809, 95% CI 0.504–1.299, P = 0.380). Notably, the cumulative incidence of relapse was significantly lower in the TBI + TT group (1-year: 6.7% vs. 17.8%, Gray’s P = 0.035), and this association persisted in both univariate (sHR = 0.279, 95% CI 0.083–0.932, P = 0.038) and multivariable Fine-Gray models (sHR = 0.280, 95% CI 0.083–0.942, P = 0.040). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for both OS (cox.zph P = 0.589) and DFS (cox.zph P = 0.590). Cluster-robust standard errors were employed to account for within-matched-set correlation [31].

The significant reduction in relapse represents the first evidence that adding thiotepa to TBI-based conditioning may improve disease control in R/R acute leukemia and lymphoma. The persistence of significance after adjustment for age and disease type, with virtually unchanged point estimates (0.279 vs. 0.280), indicates robustness to confounding by these factors. Thiotepa exerts its antileukemic effect through DNA cross-linking and is cell-cycle independent, potentially complementing TBI’s mechanism of action. However, this finding must be interpreted with considerable caution given the extremely limited number of relapse events in the TBI + TT group (n = 3). The estimate is inherently fragile, and the confidence interval is wide. Accordingly, this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. The dissociation between GVHD and relapse suggests that the GVL effect may be preserved without increased alloreactivity [32].

The significantly increased mucositis in the TBI + TT group (81.2% vs. 64.0%, P = 0.049) represents the primary safety concern, reflecting thiotepa’s non-selective cytotoxicity on rapidly dividing mucosal epithelial cells. However, mucositis-related infections (64.6% vs. 48.8%, P = 0.104) and mucositis-related deaths (18.8% vs. 19.8%, P = 1.000) did not differ significantly, suggesting that with adequate supportive care, this toxicity is manageable and does not translate into excess mortality [33]. The comparable incidence of aGVHD (33.3% vs. 39.5%, P = 0.577), cGVHD (39.6% vs. 41.9%, P = 0.856), and CMV reactivation (41.7% vs. 48.8%, P = 0.473) indicates that thiotepa intensification does not exacerbate alloreactivity. NRM was not significantly different between groups (1-year: 14.0% vs. 21.1%, 3-year: 22.8% vs. 28.0%, Gray’s P = 0.374; multivariable sHR = 0.694, 95% CI 0.332–1.451, P = 0.330). The fact that increased mucositis did not translate into higher NRM further supports the feasibility of this regimen.

A notable finding is the direction reversal between PSM and IPTW analyses. In the PSM cohort, both OS (HR = 0.901) and DFS (HR = 0.809) point estimates favored TBI + TT, whereas IPTW on the full cohort of 400 patients yielded opposite directions (OS HR = 1.083, DFS HR = 1.006), both non-significant. PSM analyzes only the 134 matched patients with overlapping propensity score distributions, while IPTW includes all 400 patients, including those with extreme propensity scores [34]. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables may differentially affect the two methods [35], and neither analysis had adequate power to detect a clinically meaningful difference [36]. Subgroup analyses by disease type and age group showed no significant interactions (OS P = 0.938 for disease type, P = 0.744 for age; DFS P = 0.499 and P = 0.590, respectively), supporting generalizability across the studied population. The divergent point estimates between pediatric (OS HR = 1.001) and adult (OS HR = 0.869) subgroups may reflect biological differences in treatment response [37], warranting validation in larger cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective, single-center design inherently carries selection bias, and despite 1:2 PSM, two covariates remained slightly above the 0.1 balance threshold [38]. Second, 15 TBI + TT patients could not be matched; these had higher propensity scores and more adverse features, meaning the matched cohort may not fully represent the spectrum of TBI + TT recipients. Third, the combination of AML, ALL, lymphoma, and MPAL across adult and pediatric populations introduces substantial heterogeneity. Fourth, the sample size is limited: post-hoc power calculations indicated the study could only detect hazard ratios of < = 0.733 or > = 1.37 for OS at 80% power, meaning it was underpowered for the observed effect sizes. Fifth, the relapse finding is based on only 3 events in the TBI + TT group, making it inherently fragile. Sixth, the events-per-variable ratio in the multivariable Fine-Gray model was 5.75, below the recommended minimum of 10. Finally, detailed mucositis severity grading and patient-reported quality-of-life data were not available.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the addition of thiotepa to TBI-based myeloablative conditioning for allo-HSCT in patients with relapsed or refractory acute leukemia yielded comparable overall survival and disease-free survival relative to TBI alone, accompanied by an observational trend toward reduced relapse incidence and increased mucosal toxicity. Engraftment, graft-versus-host disease, and cytomegalovirus infection were not adversely affected. These findings support the further exploration of the TBI+thiotepa regimen in carefully selected patients, but prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these observations and establish definitive efficacy and safety.
