Childhood adversity and depressive symptoms in undergraduates: a partial proportional odds model reveals severity-dependent life event associations and stress sensitization
Authors: Jun Luo, Fen Cheng, Yulan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood adversity is an established risk factor for depression, yet critical gaps remain in understanding its nuanced impact on emerging adults in academic settings, especially in conjunction with stressful life events and how these associations vary across the severity spectrum of depression.MethodsWe employed probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling to recruit 1,470 undergraduates from a Chinese university. Partial proportional odds models (PPOM) were fitted to analyze data, as Brant tests indicated a violation of the proportional odds assumption specifically for life events (χ²=7.35, p = 0.03). A likelihood ratio test confirmed that the PPOM provided a significantly better fit than the standard proportional odds model (χ²(7) = 28.97, p < 0.001).ResultsCompared to students without childhood adversities, those with moderate and high adversities had 1.34-fold (aOR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.02–1.75) and 1.84-fold (aOR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.22–2.77) higher odds of depressive symptoms, respectively. Positive childhood experiences showed protective association, with 32% (aOR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.51–0.91) and 52% (aOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.34–0.68) lower odds of depressive symptoms for moderate and high levels. Life events exhibited severity-dependent associations: the aORs increased progressively from mild (2.61, 95% CI: 2.02–3.37) to moderate (4.60, 95% CI: 2.91–7.27) to severe depressive symptoms (7.67, 95% CI: 2.85–20.62). Post-hoc pairwise Wald tests confirmed that these category-specific associations were statistically distinct across adjacent severity thresholds (e.g., mild vs. moderate: χ²(1) = 8.77, p = 0.003).ConclusionsOur findings reveal a severity-dependent stress-reactivity pattern consistent with stress sensitization principles, where the association between recent life events and depression becomes disproporionately stronger among students with more severe symptoms. The results highlight the importance of considering both childhood adversity and current stressors in campus mental health interventions, with particular attention to students exhibiting moderate-to-severe symptoms. The PPOM approach proved valuable for detecting these severity-specific associations that operationalize a key manifestation of sensitization, which conventional analyses would obscure.Keywords: Childhood adversity, Depressive symptoms, Life events, Stress sensitization, University students

Introduction

The stress sensitization model posits that exposure to early-life adversity, such as childhood adversity (CA), heightens an individual’s vulnerability to the deleterious impact of subsequent stressors encountered in adulthood [1]. This model suggests that early adverse experiences may alter neurobiological and psychological stress response systems, rendering individuals more reactive to later stressors, even those of relatively minor severity [2, 3]. Empirical evidence supports this hypothesis, demonstrating that individuals with a history of CA exhibit an increased risk for mood and anxiety disorders following exposure to adult stressful life events (SLEs), compared to those without such a history [1, 4].

Research has consistently shown that CA, including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction, is associated with dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and other stress-related physiological systems [5, 6]. These alterations may lead to a persistent state of heightened stress reactivity, which in turn amplifies the impact of subsequent stressors on mental health outcomes [1, 7]. For instance, McLaughlin et al. found that individuals with three or more CAs exhibited significantly greater increases in the risk of major depressive episodes following adult SLEs than those without CA exposure [1]. Similarly, Lin et al. reported that patients with major depressive disorder who experienced both CA and adulthood adversity had a 68% higher risk of suicidal ideation compared to those exposed to neither, underscoring the synergistic interaction between early and later stressors [4].

The stress sensitization framework also highlights the role of cumulative risk, where the number and severity of adversities contribute to a threshold effect. Studies indicate that exposure to multiple CAs, particularly three or more, significantly elevates the risk for psychopathology, suggesting a dose-response relationship [1, 8]. This cumulative burden may overwhelm coping mechanisms and exacerbate maladaptive stress responses, further increasing susceptibility to depression and related disorders [1, 4].

The theoretical divergence between stress sensitization and inoculation effects reveals several critical gaps in our current understanding of stress-response mechanisms. While the stress sensitization model posits that early adversity amplifies reactivity to later stressors, the inoculation theory suggests that manageable early challenges can foster resilience [9]. The current study situates itself within this theoretical debate by proposing and testing a specific, operationalizable corollary of the sensitization model in a non-clinical undergraduate population: a severity-dependent acceleration pattern. If early adversity heightens reactivity to subsequent stress (sensitization), this heightened reactivity at the population level may manifest as a pattern where the association strength between recent life events and depressive symptoms increases nonlinearly across ascending severity categories. Testing this pattern provides a novel lens through which to examine the heterogeneous impact of sensitization across the symptom continuum.

First, while both models acknowledge the dose-dependent nature of adversity [1, 10], the precise symptomatic manifestation of sensitization across the depression severity spectrum remains unclear. Previous research has largely focused on clinical populations [4] or extreme adversity [8], potentially overlooking the nuanced stress-response patterns in normative populations facing developmental challenges. Although extensive studies have established dose-dependent associations between childhood adversity and depression in undergraduates, as well as interactions between childhood adversity and recent stressor [11–14], these studies have predominantly treated depression as a binary or continuous outcome, implicitly assuming homogeneity in the effect of recent stressors across all severity levels (i.e., proportional odds). This assumption may obscure the very heterogeneity in stress reactivity that sensitization theory predicts.

Second, existing studies have typically examined childhood adversity and adult stressors as independent predictors or tested their multiplicative interaction [1], neglecting to test whether the impact of recent stressors itself varies systematically as a function of current symptom severity—a pattern that could serve as an indirect, cross-sectional signature of sensitization. The accelerating pattern of life events - where stress impact increases nonlinearly with exposure - represent crucial but understudied aspects of stress sensitization. Within the stress sensitization framework, early adversity is posited to alter stress-response systems, leading to heightened reactivity to subsequent stressors. This heightened reactivity may manifest as a severity-dependent pattern where the impact of recent stressors becomes disproportionately stronger at higher levels of depression severity.

Our study directly examines this manifestation by proposing “severity-dependent acceleration” as a testable phenotypic pattern consistent with sensitization. We test whether the association between recent life events and depression varies across severity thresholds, which represents an operationalization of key sensitization hypothesis in a cross-sectional context. Conventional analytic approaches (e.g., standard ordinal regression assuming proportional odds) may obscure such nonlinear, severity-specific patterns of stress reactivity across the depression spectrum. Moreover, while positive childhood experiences are recognized as protective factors [14, 15], their potential to mitigate stress sensitization effects remains poorly quantified.

Building upon the theoretical framework of stress sensitization and addressing the identified knowledge gaps, we propose the following hypotheses to systematically examine how early adversity shapes vulnerability to depressive symptoms in undergraduate students:

H1: Drawing on the principle of heightened stress reactivity in sensitization, we hypothesize that the association between recent stressful life events and depressive symptoms will demonstrate a severity-dependent accelerating pattern. Specifically, the strength of association (odds ratio) will increase significantly across ascending severity thresholds (minimal→mild→moderate→severe), leading to a violation of the proportional odds assumption for life events in an ordinal regression model.

Testing H1 requires an analytic approach that can detect whether the impact of life events differs across specific thresholds of depression severity. Standard ordinal regression models assume proportional odds (i.e., that the model coefficients for predictors are constant across all outcome thresholds), an assumption that may be untenable if stress sensitization produces severity-dependent reactivity. To empirically test this possibility, we employed the Partial Proportional Odds Model (PPOM). The PPOM allows us to formally evaluate whether the proportional odds assumption holds for life events and, if violated, to estimate category-specific coefficients that directly quantify how the association varies across severity levels. This methodological approach enables a nuanced test of the severity-dependent acceleration pattern—a key corollary of stress sensitization—moving beyond confirming an overall dose-response to modeling its potentially nonlinear contour across the severity spectrum.

Methods

Study design and participants

We implemented a cross-sectional design, collecting data via self-administered questionnaires at a major Chinese university with a diverse student population exceeding 20,000 across disciplines, such as medicine, pharmacy, science, engineering, humanities.

To ensure representativeness, we employed systematic sampling with probability proportional to size (PPS) of enrollment in each discipline. Participants were eligible if they (1) were aged ≥ 18 years and (2) were currently pursuing an undergraduate degree at the university.

For sample size estimation, we hypothesized that depressive symptoms would affect 60% of students with childhood adversities (CAs) versus 40% of those without CAs, based on prior evidence [16]. Using the epicalc package in R [17], we computed a minimum requirement of 214 participants (107 per group) to detect this difference (two-proportion comparison, α = 0.05, power = 80%). Accounting for a 15% nonresponse rate, we increased this to 250 (125 per group). To enable the number of individuals with severe depressive symptoms [18], we multiplied the target by five (n = 1,250). Finally, assuming an 85% attrition rate for online invitations, we expanded recruitment to 1,470 students to ensure adequate power.

Measures

Demographic characteristics

Sociodemographic and socioeconomic information, as well as family relationship and clinical history, were collected using the same self-administered questionnaire package that contained the standardized scales described below. The assessed variables included: gender (male/female, collected as binary, self-identified sex), age (in years, self-reported integer), academic discipline (medicine, pharmacy, engineering, science, or humanities, selected from a provided list corresponding to official enrollment), family economic status (categorized as low, average, or high based on a single-item, self-reported assessment of the family’s financial situation), relationship with father and mother (each rated as poor, neutral, or positive on a single-item Likert-scale question), history of mental illness (yes/no, based on self-report of a prior diagnosis by a healthcare professional), and history of suicidal behavior (yes/no, based on self-report of any prior suicide attempt).

Childhood adversities (CAs)

We evaluated childhood adversities using the Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) questionnaire [19, 20] with a validated 10-item scale to assess exposure (occurring before 18 years of age) to 10 distinct CAs categories: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, emotional neglect, and living in a dysfunctional household (witnessing maternal abuse; living with a substance abuser; living with a mentally ill household member; parental loss, such as through divorce; and incarceration of a household member). Each endorsed CA item was scored as 1 point (unreported items = 0), with total scores ranging from 0 to 10, reflecting cumulative childhood adversity exposure. In our analytical sample, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency for a binary-response checklist, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.66. This level of reliability is consistent with the psychometric properties of the widely used ACE scale, whose binary (yes/no) format and cumulative scoring model are established for identifying individuals at higher risk of trauma [21].

Positive Childhood Experiences (PCEs) Scale

We assessed positive childhood experiences with the 7-item Positive Childhood Experiences (PCEs) scale [15], which queries about experiences during the first 18 years of life. Each item was rated on a 5-point frequency scale (ranging from “never” to “always”). Choosing “often” and “always” were scored as 1 point, while “never”, “rarely”, and “sometimes” were scored as 0. The total PCEs score was calculated by summing all item scores and the range of scores were 0–7, with higher scores indicating more frequent positive experiences. Total scores can be categorized into three levels: low (0–2), moderate (3–5), and high (6–7) [15]. In our analytical sample, the PCEs scale showed good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81.

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)

We assessed depressive symptom using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) [22]. This 21-item scale evaluates various depression-related symptoms (e.g., depressed mood, feelings of guilt, sleep disturbances) using a 4-point rating scale (0–3), with total scores ranging from 0 to 63. The BDI-II demonstrates good reliability and validity [22] and has been widely used in college student populations [23]. In our analytical sample, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88.

Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC)

Negative life events and associated stress levels during the preceding 6 months were assessed with the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC), which was developed by Liu et al. [24]. The scale includes 27 items and measures stressful experiences across six domains: interpersonal relationships, academic pressure, punishment, loss of loved ones/property, health/adaptation problems, and other issues. Items were scored on a 5-point scale (1 = no impact to 5 = extreme impact), with unscored items (0) indicating non-occurrence of the event. In the present sample, the ASLEC showed excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93.

Data collection procedure

We implemented a structured recruitment and data collection protocol. In November 2024, we systematically sampled 1,470 potential participants from the university’s official registry of all enrolled undergraduate students. Electronic invitations containing study information were systematically distributed to students by the university’s e-learning platform.

Participants attended one of five scheduled big classrooms to fill questionnaires. At each classroom, trained research assistants provided detailed study information with standardized scripts, emphasized voluntary participation and the right to withdraw without penalty. Consenting participants came to adjacent classrooms configured with enforced distancing protocols (one participant per three seats) to ensure privacy and prevent response bias. Questionnaires were administered under supervised conditions, with completed instruments deposited confidentially into locked collection boxes by participants themselves. This procedure maintained anonymity while allowing researchers to verify completion rates.

Data management and statistical analysis

Following data collection, research assistants systematically assessed questionnaire completeness and validity. Among 1470 potential participants, 1256 of them came to classroom take the survey. Questionnaires with fewer than two-thirds of items completed were excluded as incomplete, resulting in a final analytic sample of 1,212 valid responses. To ensure data accuracy, trained personnel performed double-entry verification using Microsoft Excel 2016. Among the included participants, the missing data rate was extremely low (0.41%), and missing values were handled using single imputation methods—median imputation for continuous variables and mode imputation for categorical variables.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). Given that the primary variables (depressive symptoms, life events, childhood adversity, and positive childhood experiences) were affected by gender, family economic status, parent-child relationship, these variables were categorized for subsequent ordinal analyses as follows. The rationale for cut-off selection was based on established clinical guidelines, prior literature, or sample distribution to ensure meaningful group distinction and sufficient sample size in each category:

Depressive symptoms (ordinal outcome): The Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) total score was categorized using widely adopted clinical cut-offs: ≤4 (minimal), 5–13 (mild), 14–20 (moderate), ≥ 21 (severe) [23]. This classification aligns with the interpretation guidelines provided in the BDI-II manual and facilitates the identification of clinically relevant symptom severity levels.

Childhood adversity (ordinal predictor): Cumulative adversity scores (range 0–10) were categorized as: 0 (low), 1–2 (moderate), ≥ 3 (high). This grouping is consistent with research on adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) [20], which indicates that exposure to three or more adversities represents a threshold associated with significantly increased risk for later health problems.

Life events (ordinal predictor): The Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist (ASLEC) total stress score was categorized into tertiles based on sample distribution to identify groups with relatively low, moderate, and high recent stress exposure. Cut points were determined using the 27th and 73rd percentiles [25, 26], resulting in low (≤ P27), moderate (P27-P73), and high (≥ P73) stress groups. This data-driven approach ensures balanced group sizes and highlights the highest-stress quartile.

Positive childhood experiences (ordinal predictor): Total scores on the PCEs scale (range 0–7) were categorized according to the levels proposed in the original validation study: 0–2 (low), 3–5 (moderate), 6–7 (high).This classification has been used in prior research to reflect meaningful differences in the cumulative level of supportive childhood experiences [15].

We first applied descriptive statistics to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, childhood adversity, depressive symptoms, life events, and positive childhood experiences. To examine associations between childhood adversity, positive childhood experiences, recent life events, and depressive symptom severity (ordinal categories), we used ordinal logistic regression (via the “MASS” package in R).

To specifically test Hypothesis H1 (severity-dependent acceleration of life event association), we employed a two-step approach. First, we used the Brant test to assess whether the proportional odds assumption (POA) held globally and specifically for the life events variable. A significant violation of POA for life events would provide initial evidence supporting H1, indicating that the effect of life events is not homogeneous across severity categories. Second, given the anticipated violation, we fitted a partial proportional odds model (using the “ordinal” package in R). This approach allowed the coefficients for life events to vary across depression severity levels while maintaining POA for all other predictors, thereby directly quantifying the category-specific odds ratios and testing for the hypothesized accelerating pattern.

All regression models adjusted for the following covariates:

Demographic factors: gender, academic discipline, family socio-economic status

Family relationships: quality of relationships with father and mother

Clinical history: history of mental illness, history of suicidal behavior

For analyses of childhood adversity, the reference group consisted of participants reporting no CAs history. Model diagnostics included checks for multi-collinearity (all variance inflation factors < 2.5) and influential observations.

Model comparison and evidence for PPOM

A likelihood ratio test comparing the standard proportional odds model (POM) with the PPOM showed a significantly better fit for the PPOM (χ²(7) = 28.97, p < 0.001). McFadden’s pseudo-R² increased by 0.008, indicating a modest but statistically meaningful improvement in model fit after allowing the association of life events with depression to vary by severity.

Associations of predictors with depressive symptoms

Compared to those undergraduates without childhood adversities, the students with moderate adversities (aOR = 1.34, 95% CI [1.02, 1.75])) and high adversities (aOR = 1.84 [1.22, 2.77]) had higher risk of depressive symptoms.Participants with moderate and high positive childhood experiences had 32% (aOR = 0.68 [0.51, 0.91]) and 52% (aOR = 0.48 [0.34, 0.68]) lower risk of depressive symptoms compared to those with low positive childhood experiences.

To formally test whether these escalating odds ratios represented statistically distinct associations across severity thresholds as predicted by H1, we conducted post-hoc pairwise Wald tests on the category-specific coefficients. The results confirmed that the strength of the association increased significantly with severity: the coefficient was greater for the transition between moderate and mild depression than for that between mild and minimal depression (χ²(1) = 8.77, p = 0.003). Furthermore, the coefficient for the transition between severe and moderate depression was greater than that for the moderate-to-mild transition (χ²(1) = 4.95, p = 0.026). These tests provide direct statistical evidence supporting H1, confirming a severity-dependent acceleration in the association of life events with depressive symptoms.

Interaction analysis

We attempted to investigate the interaction between life events and childhood adversity on depressive symptoms, but it was not statistically significant. This suggests that in this cross-sectional sample, the influence of early adversity may be more directly reflected in the established baseline vulnerability (indexed by symptom severity category), which in turn moderates the impact of recent life events—a process captured by the severity-dependent pattern tested in H1.

Discussion

Our findings provide novel evidence for distinct associations of childhood adversity and recent life events with depressive symptoms in undergraduates. The severity-dependent pattern of life event associations, revealed through partial proportional odds modeling, is consistent with a stress sensitization mechanism where the association is disproportionately stronger among students with more severe depressive symptoms. These results extend current understanding of depression etiology in young adults while offering clinically relevant insights for targeted intervention.

Childhood adversity and the context for stress sensitivity

The dose-response relationship between childhood adversity and depressive symptoms aligns with established models of stress sensitization [27]. Students with high childhood adversity exposure exhibited 1.84 times depression risk (aOR = 1.84) comparing with those with no childhood adversities, supporting neurobiological evidence that early-life stress amplifies reactivity to later stressors [28]. The gradient effect (moderate adversity: aOR = 1.34; high adversity: aOR = 1.84) suggests that cumulative, rather than threshold-based, adversity exposure drives risk—consistent with the allostatic load hypothesis [29]. Notably, positive childhood experiences were associated with a compensatory pattern, linked to up to 52% lower risk of depressive symptoms. This aligns with resilience frameworks positing that nurturing environments buffer against stress-induced psychopathology [15]. Together, these findings establish a context of varying baseline vulnerability, against which the impact of recent stressors can be examined.

Severity-dependent acceleration: a pattern consistent with stress sensitization

The core methodological contribution of this study is the proposal and confirmation of a severity-dependent accelerating pattern in the life event-depression association. The violation of proportional odds for life events and the escalating ORs across depression severity (mild: OR = 2.61; severe: OR = 7.67) provide clear support for H1. This pattern is interpretable as a cross-sectional signature consistent with stress sensitization. If early adversity heightens stress reactivity, individuals who have developed more severe symptoms (potentially reflecting greater underlying vulnerability) would be expected to show a disproportionately stronger association with recent stressors—exactly the pattern we observed.

This finding advances beyond simply confirming an interaction between early and recent adversity. It demonstrates that the sensitization process, when viewed through the lens of symptom severity, manifests as a non-uniform effect of stressors across the depression continuum. This pattern mirrors clinical observations that major life events are more potent triggers of severe episodes in vulnerable individuals [30] and aligns with the “kindling” hypothesis [31], wherein stress sensitivity may increase with illness progression. Our use of PPOM, which captures threshold-specific associations, provides a novel quantitative menthod for detecting such patterns that conventional analyses would obscure [32, 33].

The non-significant interaction between childhood adversity and life events, while seemingly at odds with classic sensitization tests, can be integrated into this interpretation. In a cross-sectional sample, the long-term effect of early adversity may already be embedded in an individual’s current symptom profile (i.e., their placement along the severity continuum). The severity-dependent pattern we identified may thus be capturing how this embedded vulnerability modulates the impact of recent stressors. Future longitudinal studies should combine traditional interaction analysis with severity-dependent modeling (PPOM) to trace how early adversity predicts differential stress reactivity trajectories across developing symptom severity.

Clinical implications and methodological contribution

From a clinical perspective, our findings underscore that the link between life events and depression is not static but intensifies with symptom severity. This highlights the need for targeted interventions for students presenting with moderate-to-severe symptoms, for whom addressing concurrent stressors might be particularly crucial. The transformation of depressive symptom scores into ordinal levels, necessitated by our modeling approach, also suggests that clinical cut-offs may meaningfully correspond to shifts in how individuals engage with environmental stress.

Methodologically, this study showcases the utility of PPOM for mental health research. By testing and relaxing the proportional odds assumption, researchers can uncover complex, severity-specific relationships that remain hidden in conventional models. Operationalizing and testing the “severity-dependent acceleration pattern” offers a replicable analytical framework for investigating sensitization and other heterogeneous psychological processes.

Limitations and future directions

Limitations of this study should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationships between childhood adversity, recent life events, and depressive symptoms. Second, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce recall bias and common-method variance. Third, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the use of a single-university sample from China. Although the sample was drawn systematically across disciplines, the cultural, educational, and socioeconomic context may differ from other undergraduate populations. Fourth, as with most observational studies, our results may be susceptible to unmeasured confounding. Factors such as genetic vulnerability, personality traits (e.g., neuroticism), social support beyond childhood, and detailed family psychiatric history were not assessed and could influence both exposure to adversity and depressive outcomes.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs to test whether early adversity predicts the future development of severity-dependent stress reactivity. Integrating biomarkers (e.g., cortisol) could elucidate the neurobiological underpinnings of the observed pattern. Extending the PPOM framework to other populations and stressors will validate its utility.

Conclusion

This study delineates the associations of childhood adversity and recent life events with the severity of depressive symptoms in undergraduates. More importantly, it identifies and validates a severity-dependent accelerating pattern linking life events to depression, a finding consistent with a stress sensitization mechanism. This pattern, alongside gradients of risk related to childhood adversity, highlights distinct risk profiles that may benefit from stratified prevention strategies. By introducing and applying a novel analytical approach (PPOM) to test this pattern, our findings inform both the theoretical understanding of stress-depression dynamics and the development of targeted interventions in higher education settings.
