The association between nurses’ personality types and attitudes towards older patients and their clinical decision-making styles
Authors: Shahin Salarvand, Milad Ajamian, Elham Goodarzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was conducted to determine the association between nurses’ personality types and their attitudes towards older patients and clinical decision-making styles.MethodsThis is a descriptive cross-sectional study. The calculated sample size was 200 people, with a response rate of 98%; the final sample size was 196. The participants were randomly selected from the list of nurses working in educational-therapeutic hospitals in Khorramabad city who met the eligibility criteria. The questionnaire used in the present study comprised four sections: (a) Demographic characteristics collection form, (b) The short version of the NEO Five-Factor Inventory-Revised (NEO-FFI-R), (c) Kogan attitude questionnaire towards older people, and (d) The nursing clinical decision-making questionnaire.ResultsThe personality traits of openness to experience and agreeableness had the highest and lowest percentages, respectively. The mean and standard deviation of the overall clinical decision-making score of nurses was 61.81 ± 13.52, which generally indicates analytical decision-making of nurses. This study showed a relationship between all personality traits and attitudes towards older adult patients among nurses. So there is a significant relationship between the attitude of appreciation and the high level. This study showed a significant relationship between openness to experience and analytical decision-making. The more positive the nurses’ attitude towards older adult patients, the greater the tendency towards intuitive interpretive decision-making.ConclusionThis study found that nurses’ attitudes toward older patients and clinical decision-making can be associated with their personality traits. Given the importance of clinical decision-making in caring for older adult patients, employers should consider nurses’ personality traits and their attitudes toward older adults. Nurses with appropriate personality traits, such as conscientiousness, agreeableness, openness to experiences, and extroversion, respectively, should be recruited. Background

The older adult population is increasing around the world, including Iran, due to improvements in the health standards of communities [1]. Along with the aging of the population, older adults, due to their need for special care [2], are a significant part of the recipients of medical and health care services in the health system [3].

Nurses are one of the pillars of accounting for the health needs of the community, and they have a pivotal role in influencing the quality of care; therefore, attention to their knowledge, attitudes, and personality traits about the ageing phenomenon is a growing need. So that it deserves special attention in the direction of needs assessment, research, and meeting existing needs, it requires planning for the future [4].

The review of the literature indicated some direct relationships between nurses’ feelings and attitudes and the quality and type of care provided [5–8]; personality differences; and nurses’ professional values [9, 10]; personality traits and professionalism in nurses [7, 11], nurses’ personality traits and attitudes towards older people [12, 13], professional values and clinical decision-making [14, 15], and the community’s values and culture, with the concept of age discrimination [16].

Among the factors influencing the professional values of nursing, we can mention culture [17], professional education [18], personal values [19], and personality traits [9]. These perceptions and attitudes towards older adults are connected to nurses’ behavior and the quality of care provided to elders [20]. So, attitude is the main factor in making decisions in the care of older adult patients [4]. It is necessary to address the attitudes towards older adults [21].

Attitude is the expression of beliefs, feelings, and experiences about an object or concept. It manifests in people’s cognition, affection, and behavior [22]. Moreover, various factors can explain the differences in people’s attitudes towards older adults, such as their culture [23], psychological background, and personality traits. because the role of personality traits of people in predicting their specific behaviour and attitude towards various phenomena is significant [24, 25]. According to the definition, personality is a pattern of feelings, ideas, and actions that arises from personal traits and is covered by both visible and hidden strategies [13]. The “NEO’s Five Factors Theory of Personality” has introduced five broad dimensions of personality, including extraversion, openness to experience, conscientiousness, agreeableness, and neuroticism [13]. As mentioned above, people’s personality traits play a key role in their job performance, shaping their motivation and attitude towards their job, how they respond to job demands, and their specific professional values in various job situations [26]. As previously stated, individuals’ behavior is intrinsically linked to their attitudes, beliefs, and personality traits [4]. Furthermore, providing qualified care depends on having adequate knowledge of the concepts, dimensions, and characteristics of care and caregivers [27]. Choosing the desirable personality trait for each job can be associated with employees’ job performance and their attitudes towards their work [13].

Clinical decision-making is a cognitive, dynamic, and multifactorial process in which nurses identify problems and prioritise actions that directly or indirectly affect patients and act to solve the problem by benefiting from clinical knowledge, experience, and information collected from the patient [28, 29]. Clinical decision-making is a crucial aspect of nurses’ clinical practice and directly impacts each patient’s health and well-being [30]. It is a skill that enables the nurse to make effective decisions in dynamic and challenging care environments [28]. Scientific resources have provided various definitions of clinical decision-making, all of which emphasize the multitude of factors relating to it [31, 32].

Clinical decision-making is associated with a series of peripheral factors that can be summarized as cognitive, emotional, environmental, and cultural factors; risks; reasoning style (inductive-deductive); and medical errors [29]. Appropriate decision-making, which is considered a qualification in nursing, leads to a kind of correct judgment and is the basis of clinical practice [28]. The two ends of the decision-making spectrum are analytical and intuitive decision-making. Intuitive decision-making involves connecting prior learning with current perceptions of a clinical situation and relies on the senses and perceived information from multiple previous situations and the present. The analytical process is a linear method for deciding an issue. In the analytical process, there is a possibility that a person does not pay attention to their own beliefs and values in making a decision [33].

Given that nurses’ personality traits and attitudes are associated with their clinical decision-making, the relationships among these three issues have not been investigated in the literature. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the association between nurses’ personality types and their attitudes towards older adult patients and clinical decision-making style.

Methods

This is a descriptive cross-sectional study. The research population in the present study was all nurses working in educational-therapeutic hospitals of Khorramabad city who met the inclusion criteria: at least a bachelor’s degree in nursing, having at least 1 year of work experience, having a history of nursing work experience with older adult patients, and not working in the newborns, pediatric, or gynecology departments. Exclusion criteria: Failure to answer more than 5% of the questions.

Sample size

Using the above correlation coefficient (ρ = 0.36) reported by Yazdanian et al. [13]. The required sample size was calculated. Given the study’s multiple correlational analyses across five distinct personality domains and their subscales, a conservative alpha level of 1% (α = 0.01) was selected to strictly control Type I error (false-positive findings) and to enhance the robustness of the significance tests. Furthermore, a high statistical power of 99% (β = 0.01) was favored to ensure that even moderate yet practically and clinically meaningful correlations among personality traits, attitudes, and clinical decision-making could be reliably detected, especially given the potential for subgroup analyses (e.g., by hospital or gender). The preliminary calculation yielded a sample size of 164 participants. To account for an anticipated non-response rate of 20%, consistent with previous similar studies, the final sample size was inflated to 200 participants. Ultimately, with a 98% response rate, 196 questionnaires were analyzed.

Sampling

The sampling method was a stratified random sampling approach, with each hospital considered as a separate stratum to ensure proportional representation. First, a comprehensive master list of all nurses meeting the eligibility criteria was prepared separately for Shohadaye-Ashayer, Shahid-Madani, and Shahid-Rahimi hospitals, using the official nursing staff records. Each nurse on these lists was assigned a unique sequential numeric code. Subsequently, within each hospital stratum, simple random sampling was performed by using a random number generator function to select the predetermined quota of nurses, proportionate to the total number of eligible nurses in that hospital. This process ensured that every nurse had an equal chance of being selected within each stratum. The calculated sample size was 200 participants; with a response rate of 98%, the final analyzed sample comprised 196 questionnaires.

Instrumentation

The data collection tool consisted of four parts: (a) a demographic characteristics form (age, gender, employment status, marital status, education, and work experience), (b) the short version of the NEO Five-Factor Inventory-Revised (NEO-FFI-R), (c) Kogan’s Attitudes Toward Older People Scale (KAOPS), and (d) the nursing clinical decision-making questionnaire.

NEO-FFI-R: This 60-item instrument, developed by Costa & McCrae [34] and validated in Iranian samples [35–39], measures five personality domains: neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness [40]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree), with each domain comprising 12 items, including several reverse-scored items [41, 42]. For each domain, total scores range from 12 to 60 and are interpreted as low (12–24), moderate (25–48), or high (49–60), where higher scores indicate the dominance of that particular trait (e.g., higher neuroticism scores reflect greater emotional instability, while higher extraversion scores indicate greater sociability and energy) [43].

Kogan’s Attitudes Toward Older People Scale (KAOPS); Developed by Kogan [44] and psychometrically evaluated in Iran by Rejeh et al. [45]. This scale assesses nurses’ affective attitudes toward older adults [44]. It contains 34 items (17 appreciation and 17 prejudice items) on a 7-point Likert scale (with reverse scoring for the prejudice subscale). Total scores range from 34 to 238 [45], with higher scores reflecting a more positive attitude [44].

Clinical Decision-Making Questionnaire: This instrument, developed by Lauri [46] and previously validated in Iranian studies [33, 47–50]. It consists of 24 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (with 12 reverse-scored items). Total scores range from 24 to 120, categorized as analytical (scores < 67), flexible/intuitive-analytical (68–77), and intuitive clinical decision-making (scores ≥ 78) [46, 51]. Intuitive clinical decision-making is typically employed for implementation and evaluation, whereas analytical processing is used for data processing and problem identification [46].

Although all instruments had been previously validated in Iranian populations, we further evaluated their internal consistency specifically within our study sample to ensure reliability in the present context. Accordingly, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated for each scale based on the collected data (n = 196). For the NEO-FFI-R, the alpha coefficients for the five domains were as follows: Neuroticism (α = 0.86), Extraversion (α = 0.73), Openness (α = 0.56), Agreeableness (α = 0.68), and Conscientiousness (α = 0.87). The KAOPS total scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83, and the Clinical Decision-Making Questionnaire yielded an alpha of 0.91. All coefficients exceeded the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70 (except Openness and Agreeableness, which were marginally acceptable), indicating acceptable to excellent internal consistency across most instruments.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0) software. The Smirnov Cosmograph test was used to check the normality of the data. Due to the normality of the data, one-way analysis of variance and t-test were used for the analytical analysis at a significance level (P < 0.05).

Normality of all continuous outcome variables, including the scores for the five NEO-FFI-R domains (neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, and conscientiousness), the total KAOPS score (attitude toward older adults), and the total clinical decision-making score, was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (with Lilliefors significance correction) and by examining the skewness and kurtosis values. Parametric or non-parametric tests were chosen based on the normality results.

To address the primary aim of the study, which was to examine the relationships between personality traits, attitudes toward older adults, and clinical decision-making, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (for normally distributed variables) or Spearman’s rho (for non-normally distributed variables) was used. Moreover, multiple linear regression analyses (enter method) were conducted to identify the independent predictors of attitudes toward older adults and clinical decision-making scores, controlling for possible confounding demographic variables such as age, gender, work experience, and educational level.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample, and the chi-square test was used to compare categorical characteristics (e.g., gender and marital status) between groups. The independent samples t-test was also used to compare mean scores of continuous outcome variables (e.g., gender) between two independent groups. Comparisons between more than two groups (e.g., educational level) were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

The present study was conducted according to relevant guidelines and regulations. At first, the ethics code was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences (No. IR.LUMS.REC.1401.265). Informed and freely written consent was obtained from the participants.

Results

The mean age of the nurses participating in the study was 31.87 ± 5.74 years. Other demographic characteristics were mentioned in Supplementary 1.

The majority of nurses working in educational hospitals of Khorram-Abad had moderate scores in all personality trait domains. The type and percentage of their personality traits are presented in detail in Supplementary 2.

The personality traits with the highest and lowest percentages were openness to new experiences and agreeableness, respectively. This means that among the five personality dimensions, the nurses in the study had the highest percentage of openness to new experiences; they were at a medium level on this dimension, meaning that while they were not conservative, they were also not very interested in new topics. In other words, their desire for new experiences is in equilibrium. Nurses also had the lowest percentage in the agreeableness personality dimension. In this area, the majority were also at an average level, meaning that although nurses do not always disagree with others’ opinions, they do not agree with them much either. A moderate level of agreeableness was observed among the nurses in this study (Supplementary 2).

In the present study, the overall score of clinical decision-making was 61.81 ± 13.52, indicating analytical decision-making. Also, the mean and standard deviation of appreciation and prejudice were 70.12 ± 31.0 and 472.12 ± 29.78, respectively. The total score of attitude towards older adult patients was 142.25 ± 59.62. This means that, with a slight difference, nurses’ attitudes towards older adult patients are moderate and slightly in favour of a positive attitude towards the older adults.

As shown in Table 1, there was a significant relationship between personality traits and gender: females reported significantly higher levels of openness to experience, and males reported significantly higher levels of agreeableness. A statistically significant relationship was found between age and the overall score of personality traits. Nurses aged 30 years and above scored significantly higher on openness to experience and significantly lower on extraversion, agreeableness, and responsibility than did their younger counterparts (less than 30 years of age). Married nurses reported significantly higher scores in the items of openness, agreeableness, and responsibility than single nurses. The level of extraversion was significantly higher in single nurses than in married nurses. No significant relationship was found between the nurses’ level of education and their personality traits. The statistical tests used were the Variance analysis and the Independent T-test(Table 1).

Conscientiousness was significantly positively correlated with higher clinical decision-making scores (r = 0.38, p < 0.01). This means that nurses who were more organized, accountable, and self-disciplined were more successful in making intuitive clinical decisions. Moreover, clinical decision-making was positively and significantly correlated with KAOPS scores (r = 0.41, p < 0.01), which showed that nurses with more positive attitudes towards older adults were more likely to make intuitive clinical judgments in geriatric care. The two personality traits that showed the strongest connections to attitudes and decision-making were conscientiousness and agreeableness. This may be related to their specific importance in geriatric nursing care. Furthermore, the high intercorrelations among the personality traits were consistent with the theoretical model of NEO-FFI-R and supported the construct validity of this applied instrument (Table 5).

Predictors of attitudes toward older adults (KAOPS)

The final regression model for attitudes toward older adults was statistically significant (F = 9.45, p < 0.01) and explained approximately 28% of the total variance in KAOPS scores (Adjusted R2 = 0.28). Among the personality traits, Agreeableness was the strongest positive predictor (β = 0.38, p < 0.01), followed by Conscientiousness (β = 0.22, p < 0.01). This means that nurses with higher empathy, altruism, cooperation, and responsibility had significantly more positive attitudes toward older adult patients. Neuroticism was a significant negative predictor of attitudes (β = -0.25, p < 0.01), suggesting that nurses with higher levels of emotional instability and anxiety had less favorable attitudes. Among the demographic covariates, both age (β = 0.15, p < 0.05) and work experience (β = 0.20, p < 0.05) showed modest positive associations with attitudes, whereas gender and education level were not significant predictors.

Predictors of clinical decision-making

The regression model for clinical decision-making was also statistically significant (F = 10.12, p < 0.01) and explained about 31% of the variance (Adjusted R2 = 0.31). Conscientiousness was the strongest positive predictor in this model (β = 0.33, p < 0.001), followed by Agreeableness (β = 0.28, p < 0.01). Also, Extraversion (β = 0.15, p < 0.05) and Openness to experience (β = 0.14, p < 0.05) were positively related to intuitive clinical decision-making, showing that nurses who were sociable, energetic, and imaginative had more intuitive decision-making styles. On the other hand, Neuroticism again showed a significant negative effect (β = -0.18, p < 0.05), indicating that emotional distress harms clinical decision-making. Of the demographic covariates, work experience was the only significant covariate (β = 0.25, p < 0.01), meaning that nurses with more experience are more intuitive in clinical decision-making, irrespective of their personality profile (Table 6).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the relationship between nurses’ personality traits, attitudes towards older adults, and their clinical decision-making. In this section, we discuss the findings for each of the three variables and then the relationships among them.

This study showed that the personality traits of openness to experience and agreeableness had the highest and lowest percentages, respectively. However, Nurses were at a medium level on these two dimensions of openness to experience and agreeableness. Other studies confirmed that the personality trait of openness to experience obtained the highest [34, 52, 53]. Conversely, the findings of several studies reported that nurses’ agreeableness personality trait scores were the highest [51, 54, 55]. It can be concluded that several studies reported different findings, including that the maximum level of personality traits among nurses or nursing students was openness to experience [52], higher extraversion and/or conscientiousness [24, 54, 56, 57], agreeableness and conscientiousness were the highest, with slight differences [54, 58], and the minimum level of neuroticism [52, 56, 57]. As mentioned above, most studies reported that neuroticism had the lowest score among nurses. Therefore, neuroticism is an unfavorable trait among nurses, which decreases the quality of care.

In the present study, there was a significant relationship between personality traits and gender. So that females reported significantly higher levels of openness to experience, and males reported significantly higher levels of agreeableness. Kwon’s study confirmed that females with a high level of openness to experience and males with a high level of extroversion [59]. Shalchi’s study reported a higher level of openness to experience in both genders [34]. Conversely, the Baldacchino study reported no significant differences between females and males in the five personality traits [56].

In the present study, there was a statistically significant relationship between age and overall scores of each personality trait. So, the nurses’ openness to experience score for those aged 30 and over was significantly higher than that of their counterparts under 30. The nurses’ extraversion, agreeableness, and conscientiousness scores for those aged under 30 were reported to be significantly higher than those aged over 30. According to the Baldacchino study, there were no appreciable variations between the two age groups’ five personality traits (< 20 years and > 20 years) [56]. Conversely, another study reported there was an association between age and personality traits. So older nurses tended to have a higher level of conscientiousness [51].

The present study reported that married nurses obtained significantly higher scores in openness, agreeableness, and Conscientiousness than single nurses. The level of extraversion in single nurses was found to be significantly higher than in married nurses. We didn’t find any related papers about this subject.

The present study showed no significant relationship between the education level and nurses’ personality traits. Conversely, other studies showed that nurses with a master’s degree and specialist nurses were more open to experience than those with a bachelor’s degree. Also, professional competencies correlated with extraversion, so specialist nurses were more likely to display extraversion than nurses with a bachelor’s degree [60, 61]. It is more reasonable that there were no relationships between personality traits and education level.

The present study showed that the total score for the attitude towards older adult patients was 142.25 ± 59.62. This means that, with a slight difference, nurses’ attitudes towards older adult patients are moderate and slightly in favour of a positive attitude towards the older adults. The different studies reported varied attitude levels toward older adult patients among nursing students and professionals, with mean KAOPS scores above the median level/positive attitude [62–64], 125.33 ± 13.14, and 126.33 ± 7.82 among the Turkish nursing students and the African nursing students, respectively [65]; 119.11 ± 14.80 [66]; a negative attitude towards older people among students and qualified nurses [25]; a neutral attitude [67], and marginally negative attitudes toward the older adults [21, 25, 64], which are associated with quality of care.

This variety in study findings may be related to a lack of knowledge of the ageing process and gerontology, or differences in social and cultural structures. The present study reported that the mean and standard deviation of appreciation were 70.12 ± 31.04, and the prejudice score was 72.12 ± 29.78. There are no related studies to this finding.

In the present study, a significant relationship was found between gender, age, marital status, and education level and attitude towards the older adults, so males, those under 30 years of age, married persons, and those with higher education reported significantly higher percentages in both the overall score of attitudes towards older adults and its sub-items, including appreciation and prejudice. Asadi et al. and Hamedanizadeh et al. reported that the attitude towards older adults score was higher in males than in females, and there was a significant relationship between gender and nurses’ attitude score towards older adults [66, 68, 69]. Sanagoo et al. confirmed a significant relationship between education level and attitudes toward older adults [70]. Conversely, Hamedanizadeh’s study reported no significant relationship between education level and attitudes towards older people [68]. Given that nurses with higher education levels have a better understanding of older adults and their specific needs, they have greater knowledge of ageing and gerontology and therefore more positive attitudes than their counterparts in this area [71]. Moreover, Asadi reported that single persons have more positive attitudes toward older adults than married persons [66]. There were no significant differences in attitudes toward older adults’ scores across age groups, marital status, age, and education level [66]. A possible explanation for these differences may be the homogeneity of our sample, because the majority of nurses who participated in this study had bachelor’s degrees. In addition, differences in attitude towards older adults’ scores in the extant studies may be due to differences in culture, workplace environment, personality type, expectations, and professional development of the individuals participating in the study. Therefore, due to cultural differences in the varied settings, both positive and negative attitudes are expected to be displayed towards older adults.

In the present study, the nurses’ overall clinical decision-making score was 61.81 ± 13.52, which indicates analytical decision-making. Although the overall clinical decision-making score was in the analytical clinical decision-making domain, a higher score in this domain indicates a closer alignment with the intuitive analytical clinical decision-making style. Other studies have indicated a low level of intuitive decision-making and a high level of analytical decision-making approaches [15, 72, 73]. Several studies have reported a tendency towards an intuitive decision-making style among nurses [50, 51]. However, it has been confirmed that nursing decision-making requires both analytical and intuitive processes, depending on the type of nursing situation [73]. In addition, research has shown that a high workload environment decreases the likelihood of using an intuitive decision-making style [72, 74, 75]. The nursing staff shortage and/or elevated workload may lead nurses to adopt a less intuitive decision-making approach, favoring an analytical decision-making method instead.

The present study reported that socio-demographic variables such as being male, under 30 years old, married, and having higher education were associated with higher clinical decision-making score and intuitive clinical decision-making. Abate’s study reported that being female and/or having higher education were more likely to associate with an intuitive decision-making approach than their counterparts [72]. Conversely, the studies by Salehi et al. and Arkan et al. reported that gender and level of education were not predictors of clinical decision-making style [76, 77]. There is a discrepancy about this relationship between gender and clinical decision-making style. On one hand, given that decision-making is affected by how individuals perceive the choices they face and the outcomes they wish to achieve [78]. It seems that there was no relationship between gender and clinical decision-making style [77, 79]; on the other hand, gender and profession are two intersecting factors influencing healthcare professionals’ decision-making style [80].

This study showed a relationship between all personality traits and attitudes towards older adults among nurses. So, there is a significant relationship between the attitude of appreciation and the high level of neuroticism. This means that nurses who had higher neuroticism had a more appreciative attitude toward older adults. Conversely, another study showed that the relationship between neuroticism and a negative attitude toward older people [63]. The more extroverted and conscientious the nurses were, the higher the prejudice level in their attitudes towards older adults. Given that the prejudice items were scored in reverse, it indicates nurses have more positive attitudes toward older adult patients. Baykal et al. confirmed the significant relationship between extroversion and a positive attitude toward older people [63]. This study identified conscientiousness and agreeableness as the two personality traits most strongly linked to positive attitudes and intuitive decision-making. Yazdanian et al. reported that two personality traits of neuroticism and agreeableness could significantly predict nurses’ attitudes toward older people. There is a positive and significant association between agreeableness in nurses and their attitudes toward aged people [13]. It seems that nurses with the personality traits of agreeableness and conscientiousness should be recruited to work with the older adult clients.

This study showed a significant relationship between openness to experience and analytical decision-making; the higher the openness to experience score, the greater the nurses’ tendency to make analytical decisions. Xu et al. reported that personality traits are good predictors of clinical decision-making [81]. Takase et al. found that nurses’ openness to experiences personality trait was inextricably related to good nursing qualifications [82]. Paiva et al. cited that there were significant correlations between the domains of clinical decision-making and their personality traits [51].

The study showed that the higher the nurses’ attitude score towards the older adults in the two domains of prejudice and appreciation, in other words, the more positive the nurses’ attitude towards the older adult patients, the greater the tendency towards intuitive interpretive decision-making. We didn’t find related papers with this finding.

Limitations

Data gathering was done in a self-reported manner by nurses. Moreover, other limitations of this study were as follows: a cross-sectional design means there were no causal relationships among the variables, possible social desirability bias, possible selection bias, a discrepancy between 200 recruited participants and 196 analyzed questionnaires, a lack of multivariable analysis, and limited generalizability. Moreover, the large number of questionnaires and the time-consuming nature of completing them were reasons for the lack of cooperation in completing them properly. We gave a small present to the nurses participating and enough time to complete the questionnaires to motivate them.

Conclusion

This study reported that nurses’ personality traits, which are connected to cultural values, can be associated with their attitudes toward older patients and clinical decision-making style. In the present study, Nurses with more working experience and older nurses have more positive attitudes toward older people and more intuitive clinical decision-making. Although the intuitive decision-making approach was lower than in previous studies. Nurses’ higher education or knowledge of older adults’ needs, and their more positive attitudes toward older adults, are significantly related to intuitive clinical decision-making. Two personality traits, conscientiousness and agreeableness, strongly predict a positive attitude toward older people and intuitive clinical decision-making. More positive attitudes toward older people, extraversion, and openness to experience were positively related to intuitive clinical decision-making. Neuroticism is an unfavorable trait among nurses, which decreases the quality of care. Given that improving nurses’ attitudes toward older adults is vital to providing high-quality care, the association of clinical decision-making with the quality of care is also important.

Implication for nurses’ management

Employers should consider nurses’ personality traits and their attitudes toward older adult patients. Nurses with appropriate personality traits, such as conscientiousness, agreeableness, openness to experiences, and extroversion, respectively, should be recruited. Moreover, policymakers should enforce a standardized nurse-to-patient ratio and develop training for nurses to manage patients in different situations to achieve a favorable decision-making approach.
