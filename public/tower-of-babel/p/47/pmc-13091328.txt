Targeting Nipah virus replication: A fluorescence polarization assay for high-throughput modulators screening against the NTAIL–XD interaction
Authors: Hugo Bost, Carine Derviaux, Sonia Longhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nipah virus (NiV) is a biosafety level 4 pathogen for which no licensed antiviral or vaccine is currently available. Given its pandemic potential and the serious threat it poses to human health, there is an urgent need for robust assays enabling the screening of chemical libraries in search of antiviral candidates. Here, we report the development of a high-throughput competitive fluorescence polarization assay designed to screen compound libraries and identify modulators of the NiV NTAIL–PXD interaction. Using an initial minimal setup, we successfully titrated and displaced this interaction with a nonlabeled peptide serving as a reference competitor. To enhance both assay window and dynamic range, we engineered a thioredoxin–XD fusion construct. We then assessed the assay’s tolerance to dimethyl sulfoxide and found that the interaction remained stable at concentrations as high as 10%. Benchmarking of the final assay conditions using a screening-scale Z′-factor calculation yielded an excellent mean value of 0.8. Finally, we applied this optimized assay to screen a protein–protein interaction–focused chemical library of more than 10,000 compounds, resulting in a primary hit rate of 0.81%, encompassing both stabilizers and inhibitors. This assay expands the drug-discovery toolkit available against NiV and holds promise for adaptation to other Henipaviruses and, more broadly, to paramyxoviruses.

The Hendra virus (HeV) and Nipah virus (NiV) are zoonotic agents that have emerged in the last 30 years and whose natural reservoir is fruit-eating bats of the Pteropus genus. NiV and HeV are prototypic members of the Henipavirus genus (Paramyxoviridae family, Mononegavirales order) (1, 2). During initial outbreaks (Australia in 1994 [HeV], Malaysia in 1998–1999, and Bangladesh in 2001 [NiV]), horses and pigs were identified as intermediate hosts driving HeV and NiV spillovers, respectively (3, 4). Clinical manifestations linked with NiV and HeV infection in humans mainly include brain and lung systemic damage, often leading to fatal encephalitis or respiratory disease, respectively (5). Nowadays, almost every winter sees another NiV outbreak (6), with a current count reaching hundreds of deaths (7). Furthermore, the NiV strain from Bangladesh has an improved ability for human-to-human transmission and increased respiratory tropism, with related clinical manifestations, along with a case fatality often exceeding 70% (7, 8, 9). As no vaccine or therapeutic has yet been approved for humans against Henipaviruses, only local and practical measures have been applied in the affected areas to limit direct transmission from fruit-eating bats or avoid spread among livestock (10, 11, 12). The very high case fatality rate of HeV and NiV, along with the current absence of treatment or vaccine for humans, underlies their classification as biosafety level 4 pathogens. With a high risk of pandemic spread, NiV is on the World Health Organization Blueprint list of viruses against which treatment and vaccine research is a top priority. Awareness of the evolution of future outbreaks of NiV and surveillance of viral circulation are critical for preventing dramatic pandemic events. In parallel, significant efforts need to be put into the discovery and development of antivirals and vaccines toward humans and exposed animals.

From a molecular point of view, Henipaviruses are nonsegmented RNA viruses, which transcribe and replicate their negative-sense ssRNA genome using three main proteins: the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, or L protein, the nucleoprotein (N), and the phosphoprotein (P) (13, 14). The nucleoprotein encapsidates viral genomic RNA, and the resulting nucleocapsid (i.e., the N–RNA complex) serves as a substrate for transcription and replication. The viral polymerase is composed of L and P, the latter enabling tethering of the L protein onto the nucleocapsid template. According to the so-called cartwheeling mechanism (15), the L–P complex is dynamically anchored on the nucleocapsid substrate through a transient and multivalent N–P interaction (Fig. 1A). Interaction of P with the nucleocapsid template relies on binding of the C-terminal X domain (XD) of P (residues 660–709) with the C-terminal, intrinsically disordered NTAIL region of the N protein (residues 400–532) (19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24) (Fig. 1). By analogy with the related measles virus, the NTAIL–XD interaction presumably dictates the efficiency of transcription reinitiation at intergenic regions (25).

As in the measles virus, in NiV and HeV, this key interaction involves a molecular recognition element (MoRE) located within NTAIL (Fig. 1). This MoRE, also referred to as box3, and encompassing residues 473 to 493 (20), folds into a stable α-helix upon interaction with XD (19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24). Of a relatively weak affinity (KD in the 1–10 μM range), the formation of the NiV NTAIL–PXD complex is enthalpy driven, with an unfavorable entropic contribution, mostly linked to the folding event of the involved MoRE (19, 20, 23). Additional studies showed that formation of this 1:1 complex mostly relies on hydrophobic contacts and that flanking regions within NTAIL remain highly flexible in the bound state, typical of fuzzy complexes (19, 22, 23, 26). The crystal structure of a chimeric construct of NiV XD, covalently linked to residues 473 to 495 of N, revealed that the latter region adopts an antiparallel orientation with respect to XD (Fig. 1A). The MoRE is embedded within a hydrophobic groove delimited by helices α2 and α3 of XD and surrounded by acidic residues. This complex is characterized by a relatively low buried surface area (<1000 Å2). While box3 hydrophobic residues are buried within the α2/α3 core, flanking basic residues are exposed on the surface of XD (27).

The affinity of the NTAIL–PXD interaction and its structural and biophysical features, along with its biological importance, make it a prime antiviral target. The availability of a minigenome system for NiV (18) should allow antiviral screening campaigns to be carried out without the need for a biosafety level 4 confinement, with great promise for cellular hit identification. However, the elucidation of the mode of action of these hits, essential for preclinical and clinical progression, remains a laborious path. Therefore, there is a persistent need for safe, robust, cost-effective, and high-throughput (HTP) target–based methods supporting antihenipaviral drug discovery.

In order to meet this need, we selected a competitive fluorescence polarization (CFP) approach to set up and optimize an in vitro platform enabling the screening of thousands of molecules within a day. Fluorescence polarization (FP) is a spectroscopic technique based on the rotational freedom of a fluorescent molecule of interest (referred to as “tracer”) under polarized light excitation upon interaction with another nonlabeled larger molecule. The initial signal (free tracer) mostly depends on the nature of the fluorophore (i.e., its fluorescence lifetime) and the molecular mass of the labeled molecule. A free tracer is characterized by a high rotational freedom and thus emits a depolarized signal upon excitation. When bound to a significantly larger molecule, the more constrained environment of the fluorescent molecule will increase the rotational correlation time of the tracer, inducing a polarization of the emitted fluorescence (29). These features primarily enable the confirmation of an interaction, along with the measurement of its affinity through direct titration assays. In addition, using this method in a competition mode enables the high-throughput screening (HTS) of chemical libraries for the identification of modulators of the targeted interaction (30, 31, 32, 33, 34).

In the present work, we report the establishment of a robust CFP-based HTS assay for modulator screening against the NiV NTAIL–PXD interaction. With our initial minimal setup, we could titrate and displace this interaction using a nonlabeled peptide as a reference competitor. After improving the assay window and dynamic range through the design of a thioredoxin (TRX)–XD (TXD) fusion, we benchmarked our final assay conditions by performing a higher-order Z′-factor calculation, yielding a very good value of 0.8. Finally, we used this setup to screen the Fr-PPIChem, a protein–protein interaction (PPI)–focused chemical library of 10,314 compounds (35), with a final primary hit rate of 0.81%, including both stabilizers and inhibitors.

Results

Minimal designed FP system enables accurate titration and displacement of the targeted NiV NTAIL–PXD interaction

Since the binding of P to the assembled form of N relies on the interaction established between the XD of P and the box3 region of NTAIL, we started by setting up an FP assay using this minimal functional system. To this end, we used a synthetic N-terminally 5-carboxyfluorescein (5-FAM)-labeled box3 peptide (2.9 kDa) encompassing residues 470 to 490 of N and a recombinant 6xHis-tagged XD domain (6.7 kDa) (Fig. 2A). We then performed a titration, where increasing concentrations of XD (from 0 to 125 μM) were mixed with 50 nM of fluorescent tracer peptide (36, 37) (Fig. 2B). This tracer concentration was selected to ensure accurate titration while minimizing any nonspecific binding event or aggregation of this rather hydrophobic molecule. First, the baseline signal for tracer peptide alone showed a low and stable FP value (≈40 mP) (Fig. S1A). The dose-dependent increase of FP signal upon addition of increasing protein concentrations translates to interaction titration until a plateau is reached at saturation (Fig. 2B). Fitting this initial titration assay enabled deriving an apparent KD in the low micromolar range (16.1 ± 0.9 μM) (Fig. 2B). These data recapitulate an affinity consistent with measured KD values obtained in previous studies using other in vitro orthogonal techniques, such as isothermal titration calorimetry (i.e., KD of 2 μM) and/or NMR spectroscopy (i.e., KD of 16 μM) (19, 23). Around the apparent KD, signal amplitude (ΔmP) reaches a value of 40, which defines the approximate assay window in this initial setup. Furthermore, readings at various times of incubation showed assay stability even after 1 day, along with a high internal homogeneity, as well as reproducibility, and no significant apparent signal interference, even at high protein concentrations (Fig. 2B).

Having proved the relevance of this minimal system for interaction recapitulation, and in light of our final screening objective, we then aimed at testing the potential of the present setup for CFP. To this end, increasing concentrations of nonlabeled box3 peptide (0–400 μM) were added to a fixed XD concentration of 15 μM (corresponding to the apparent KD) (Fig. 2C). After 3 h or 6 h of incubation, a stable and consistent dose-dependent decrease of FP signal, arising from interaction displacement, was observed. After 24 h of incubation, the signal was found to deviate in the high concentration range (40–400 μM), consistent with a reduced inhibition and possibly linked to a limited stability of the competitor at room temperature. Disappointingly, even at high competitor concentrations, a residual signal comprised between 30% and 55% was observed. This suggests that nonlabeled box3 is not able to fully displace the interaction, even at 400 μM, that is, at a concentration close to 25 times the apparent KD of the system. Nonetheless, data fitting enabled the calculation of an IC50 of 29.7 ± 0.5 μM, giving a Ki of 15.4 μM, a value in line with expectations for a competitive inhibitor.

Altogether, these data show that we could set up a minimal and simple in vitro assay recapitulating the NiV NTAIL–PXD interaction, returning an affinity consistent with previous data, and that this system could serve as a basis for discovery campaigns of modulators interfering with the NiV NTAIL–PXD complex. However, the inability of the nonlabeled box3 to fully displace the interaction points to the need to further optimize the assay window and dynamic range so as to enable screening compounds in optimal conditions.

The use of a TXD fusion greatly improved assay HTS potential

Even though the initial minimal system seemed to efficiently recapitulate the original target interaction, this setup suffered from several technical and practical limits. Indeed, our primary objective was to screen a PPI-focused chemical library, where highly hydrophobic compounds are over-represented (35, 38). Keeping a high target protein concentration in an assay in order to guarantee a satisfying assay window would increase compound concentration requirements for detectable modulation. As a consequence, the identification of modulators (mainly inhibitors) would be restricted to highly potent ones, and compound solubility issues would be faced.

At a given partner concentration, the affinity of the interaction and the molecular mass difference between the two interactants significantly affect the FP signal. We therefore decided to increase the molecular mass of the partner protein (32). To this end, we cloned, produced, and purified a TXD fusion (21 kDa) (Fig. 3A), which resulted in a three-fold increase in the initial molecular mass. After confirming that the signal of tracer alone was low and stable over time in TXD titration assay conditions (Fig. S1B), TXD was titrated as described above for XD (Fig. 3B). As observed in the previous assay, the FP signal increases in a dose-dependent manner. Interestingly, with a TXD concentration as low as 5 μM, the FP signal already reached an amplitude (ΔmP) above 50 units. Thus, a TXD concentration of 5 μM yielded a greater assay amplitude than with 15 μM XD (cf. Figure 2, Figure 3B and 3B). Furthermore, data fitting enabled the calculation of an apparent KD of 4.2 μM, which is even closer to previously reported data (19, 23). Surprisingly, at TXD concentrations higher than 50 μM, a quasi-linear increase of FP was observed (Fig. 3B), which could reflect an interference of the TRX moiety in the assay, as judged by its absence in XD titration (Fig. 2B). In order to explore this possibility, we first assessed whether the TRX moiety alone could give rise to any signal when mixed with the tracer. TRX alone was therefore titrated into a fixed concentration of 5-carboxyfluorescein (5-FAM) box3 peptide in the same conditions described above (Fig. S2A). Results showed a weak but significant apparent interference of TRX in the assay at concentrations above 125 μM. To discriminate between interference related to TRX–tracer interaction and the intrinsic effect of the TRX moiety on the FP signal, the purified TRX protein was serially diluted in the absence of tracer and was read as in interaction assays (Fig. S2B). This assay showed a linear increase of both parallel and perpendicular components as a function of TRX concentration. The parallel component showed a significantly greater slope than that of the perpendicular one, mathematically leading to a linear increase of the resulting FP signal (Fig. S2B and equations) (39). Finally, in order to assess a possible effect of TRX on box3–TXD apparent affinity, the FP signals obtained with TRX and tracer (Fig. S2A) were subtracted from TXD titration values (Fig. 3B). This approach did not affect the apparent KD, even though it appeared to correct the plateau region of the titration curve, attenuating the observed linear behavior (Fig. S2C).

Overall, these results suggest that the linear behavior of the FP signal in TXD titration mostly arises from the intrinsic behavior of the TRX moiety at very high concentrations, rather than from an aspecific TRX–box3 interaction. Indeed, TRX concentrations exceeding 125 μM presumably induce a weak light-scattering effect through protein self-association or even slight precipitation, which may cause FP signal perturbation (36, 40, 41).

Having ruled out any possible TRX interference on the interaction of interest at TXD concentrations below 125 μM, we proceeded to displacement assays using nonlabeled box3 peptide. To this end, increasing concentrations of nonlabeled box3 were added to a fixed TXD concentration of 5 μM (close to the measured apparent KD of the box3–TXD interaction, Fig. 3B), resulting in a dose-dependent decrease of FP signal (Fig. 3C). At the highest concentration of competitor (400 μM), a residual signal of 7% or 17% was observed after 3 h or 6 h and after 24 h of incubation, respectively (Fig. 3C). This residual signal, reflecting the presence of tracer still bound to (T)XD, is however much lower compared with that observed with nonfused XD (cf. Figure 2, Figure 3C and 3C).

Data fitting yielded an IC50 of 13.6 ± 0.3 μM, for a corresponding Ki of approximately 6 μM (Fig. 3C). Derived Ki recapitulates the KD obtained in the initial titration, indicating that the observed signal arises from a box3–TXD specific interaction and that this setup is suited for displacement assays using a protein concentration as low as 5 μM. Overall, these results translate to a drastic improvement in competition assay dynamic range, highly beneficial for modulator screening and selection.

System optimization yielded a robust assay, suited for HTP modulator screening

In addition to optimized assay window and dynamic range, a prerequisite for further efficient HTS is the statistical robustness of the developed setup under future screening conditions.

As our objective was to use this assay to screen highly hydrophobic compound libraries, in which compounds are dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), we then sought to test the effect of increasing concentrations of DMSO in the assay. In order to limit the over-representation of critically insoluble compounds within primary hits and avoid a poor amenability of compounds to further assays, our final DMSO% was set to 2%. Indeed, by screening at a higher DMSO percentage, one may select compounds likely to be poorly translatable to cells because of DMSO toxicity.

In the objective of benchmarking our final assay conditions, we finally evaluated the screening-scale Z′-factor of our assay by calculating SD and mean signal of both negative (5-FAM–box3 peptide alone) and positive (TXD + 5-FAM–box3) controls. To this end, a quarter of a 384-well plate was filled with negative and positive controls (48 wells each). Measurement of FP values of these wells enabled calculating the Z′-factor (Fig. 4B). A mean Z′-factor exceeding 0.8 was obtained in final assay conditions after 3 h of incubation (Fig. 4B), with this value remaining stable over time (i.e., after 6 h or 24 h of incubation) (Fig. 4B, inset, and Fig. S3). Overall, these results demonstrate the high robustness and readiness of the assay for large-scale HTS.

Improved FP assay enabled the primary screening of a +10,000 compound PPI-focused chemical library

We next made use of our optimized CFP assay to perform the primary screening of the Fr-PPIChem chemical library. The latter, containing 10,314 compounds, was built in 2020 using two models (42, 43) trained with two updated chemical databases: 2P2Idb and iPPI-DB (44, 45). The thorough structural information on experimentally validated protein–ligand complexes gathered in these databases enabled machine learning–based extraction and filtering of a wide number of commercially available molecules. Subsequent drug-likeness filtering and structural clustering enabled the final selection of +10,000 ready-to-screen compounds, with a proven enhanced hit rate toward a PPI of interest as compared with a nonfocused chemical library (46).

Primary screening was performed in optimized conditions, in the presence of 5 μM TXD, 50 nM 5-FAM-box3, and 2% DMSO brought by compounds, each at 20 μM (from 1 mM stocks in 100% DMSO). The plates were incubated for 3 h at room temperature. All 33, 384-well plates were first statistically characterized. The mean values of negative and positive controls were 39.5 mP and 100.7 mP (32 wells per plate each), with SDs of 2.5 mP and 1.8 mP, respectively. Calculation of all Z′-factors within the screening campaign showed that all plates had a Z′-factor above 0.5 (Fig. 5A). More precisely, except for plates 1 to 4, all plates showed a value between 0.7 and 0.9, overall reflecting a very robust campaign. The comparatively lower Z′ value of the first four plates apparently correlates with a higher SD value from the negative control (not shown), likely originating from an insufficient mix homogenization before dispensing the negative mix. Discussion

Along with this work, we could develop an initial minimal in vitro system recapitulating the key NiV NTAIL–PXD interaction. Further adaptations comprised the increase of partner molecular mass by cloning and expressing a recombinant TRX fusion of the XD domain, thus multiplying its molecular mass by three. This approach improved both the assay window and dynamic range while having no deleterious effect on the interaction. Indeed, a concentration of 5 μM TXD already gave rise to a ΔmP of 50, whereas the same concentration of XD only gave a ΔmP of 20, a finding in line with the expected theoretical effect of increasing partner molecular mass on FP signal amplitude (32).

Noteworthy, the final setup proved to be adapted for competitive assays, showing a more efficient displacement and a better sensitivity compared with assays that made use of XD and entailing lower competitor concentration requirements.

Obstacles met with the initial setup included poor assay amplitude and dynamic range, linked to the low molecular mass difference between box3 and XD (the ratio of 2.31), in the context of a medium affinity interaction. Increasing the molecular mass of the partner (TXD) was expected to improve the discrimination of bound and unbound tracer (i.e., improvement of dynamic range) via a significantly increased difference in the correlation time of those two states (49). Since the addition of the TRX moiety was found not to affect the apparent affinity of the system (Fig. S2C), the difference in dynamic range between XD and TXD is likely the main source of the discrepancy in apparent KD values. Furthermore, even though XD is quite stable and robust in many buffer conditions, the TRX tag may improve its solubility, overall conformational homogeneity, and competency for binding to box3, thus lowering the apparent KD of the system and improving assay sensitivity. In addition, the poor 280 nm absorption of XD (ε = 0.221, one tyrosine residue) intrinsically entails more uncertainty in protein concentration estimation and may lead to misestimation of the apparent KD. Finally, the use of a lower TXD concentration in displacement assays reduces the fraction of free target protein available for “silent” binding of the nonlabeled competitor and further improves the sensitivity in the competition mode at a given competitor concentration.

Interestingly, we showed that DMSO concentrations as high as 10% had no deleterious impact on the final system. Although we set to 2% the DMSO concentration in our screening, the high tolerance of the system toward DMSO is of interest, as it could be exploited in further orthogonal assays that may require higher hydrophobic compound concentrations and hence higher DMSO concentrations.

The simple mix-and-read format of the assay enabled screening of +10,000 compounds within a day. The great robustness, HTP potential, cost-effectiveness, and accessibility of this technique make its application attractive in the academic environment as well as in the industry for in vitro chemical library screening. In addition, in the context of essentially dynamic target interactions, the ability of CFP to reveal potential inhibitors (decrease of polarization signal) as well as stabilizers (increase of polarization signal) in the same assay is an invaluable asset (31, 34, 50, 51). Nevertheless, as any screening method, CFP can be affected by artifacts and false positives. In that respect, bona fide modulators must be discriminated through rigorous secondary tests and orthogonal validation (32). Besides, even after rigorous orthogonal validation, one should keep in mind that some compounds might not be translatable in cells because of high toxicity and/or poor solubility or cell penetration.

By enabling fast, affordable, and safe chemical library screening and/or hit validation, we anticipate that this system will enrich the drug discovery arsenal against Henipaviruses and, more generally, Paramyxoviridae.

Experimental procedures

Tracer selection, design, and reconstitution

The tracer peptide was selected on the basis of the native interaction ((27); Protein Data Bank code: 7PNO) and of previous NMR data that identified residues 471 to 491 as involved in the interaction with PXD (23). Given our objective of using a peptide of roughly 20 residues—short enough to maintain a low baseline signal while limiting challenges in chemical synthesis—and to minimize hydrophobicity to prevent solubility issues, the designed tracer peptide comprises residues 470 to 490 of the NiV N protein (UniProt ID: Q9IK92) (TRLTNSLLNLRSRLAAKAAKE) with an additional C-terminal tyrosine to enable estimating its concentration using UV absorbance. The 22-residue peptide was purchased from GenicBio in both N-terminally 5-FAM-labeled and unmodified forms. Peptides were reconstituted either in water or in final 10% DMSO at a final concentration of 2 mM. This initial stock was aliquoted, and the necessary volume was diluted to 1 μM in water for further assays. All peptides were kept at −20 °C.

Generation of expression constructs, protein expression, and purification

NiV XD (UniProt ID: Q9IK91; residues 660–709) with a C-terminal 6xHis tag was expressed from a previously described pDEST14 expression construct (19). The TXD fusion construct was generated by PCR using primers designed to amplify the XD-coding region (without the 6xHis tag) from the pDEST14 construct. The forward primer was designed to also contain an attB1 site followed by a DNA fragment encoding a tobacco etch virus protease cleavage site (ENLYFQG) at its 5′ end, whereas the reverse primer was conceived to add two TAA stop codons at the end of the XD ORF followed by an attB2 site. The amplification product was digested with DpnI (New England Biolabs) to remove the methylated DNA template and then transferred into the pDONR vector (Invitrogen) through a Gateway BP clonase–mediated recombination reaction (Invitrogen). From the resulting entry vector, the sequence of interest was then transferred into the pETG20A vector (52) using Gateway LR clonase (Invitrogen). The resulting construct drives the inducible expression under the T7 promoter of a hexahistidine-tagged fusion protein in which XD is preceded by a TRX tag, a 6xHis tag, an attB1-encoded amino acid stretch (GTMTSLYKKAGS), and a tobacco etch virus protease cleavage site.

The TRX construct bearing a C-terminal 6xHis tag was generated by PCR from a TRX–6his–box3 pETG20A-derived plasmid, using primers designed to insert two stop codons between the 6xHis tag and box3. The forward primer contained the attB1 site, two TAA stop codons, and a fragment of box3. The attB2 sequence was used as a reverse primer. The amplification product (attB1–TAATAA–box3–attB2) was digested with DpnI (New England Biolabs) to remove the methylated DNA template and then transferred into the pDONR vector (Invitrogen) through a Gateway BP clonase–mediated recombination reaction (Invitrogen). The sequence of interest was then transferred into the pETG20A vector (52) using Gateway LR clonase (Invitrogen). The resulting construct drives the inducible expression under the T7 promoter, of a C-terminally hexahistidine-tagged TRX. All constructs were checked by sequencing (Genewiz).

TRX, XD, and the TXD fusion were produced in Escherichia coli T7 pRos cells. After transformation and overnight preculture at 37 °C, cells were seeded in Terrific broth medium at a starting absorbance of 0.1 at 600 nm and induced with 0.3 mM IPTG after cultures reached an absorabance of 0.6 to 0.8 at 600 nm. Expression was carried overnight at 17 °C. Bacterial pellets were resuspended (5 ml/g of cells) in resuspension buffer, supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 0.25 mg ml−1 lysozyme, 10 μg ml−1 DNAse I, 20 mM MgSO4, and cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche) (one tablet per 50 ml of lysate). Resuspension buffer consisted in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7), 200 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole supplemented (TXD) or not (XD) with 1 mM Tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP). In the case of TRX, resuspension buffer was 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8), 300 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Triton X-100.

The cells were disrupted by sonication (using a 750 W sonicator and three cycles of 30 s each at 45% power output). The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. The clarified samples were purified through immobilized metal affinity chromatography. To this end, the samples were loaded onto a HiTrap (Cytiva) affinity column pre-equilibrated in the corresponding resuspension buffer. The column was then washed with 10 column volumes of resuspension buffer, and proteins were eluted with a gradient of resuspension buffer supplemented with 500 mM imidazole. The fractions of interest were pooled, concentrated using Centricon concentrators (Merck–Millipore) with a molecular mass cutoff of either 3 kDa (XD and TRX) or 10 kDa (TXD), then loaded onto a size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) column (HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75pg) pre-equilibrated in SEC buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7], 200 mM NaCl) supplemented (TXD) or not (XD) with 1 mM TCEP. For TRX, SEC equilibration and elution buffer contained 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8) and 300 mM NaCl. After SEC elution and analysis by SDS-PAGE (15% or 18%), the fractions of interest were pooled and concentrated as described above. The final purified protein stocks were supplemented with glycerol at a final concentration of 10% (v/v), aliquoted, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80 °C. All the immobilized metal affinity chromatography purification steps were performed at 4 °C, whereas SEC purification steps were performed at room temperature.

Protein concentrations were calculated using the theoretical absorption coefficients at 280 nm as obtained using the program ProtParam at the EXPASY server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Direct titration by FP

Each assay was performed using freshly prepared assay buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% Tween-20, supplemented with 1 mM TCEP in the case of TXD and TRX). The assay was performed in a final volume of 10 μl, with 50 nM of tracer and an increasing concentration of the partner protein up to 250 μM (TXD and TRX) or 125 μM (XD). Each data point was performed in triplicate using 384-well Greiner microplates (ref. 784900). The plates were briefly centrifuged to pellet any droplet and remove any air bubble before sealing with adhesive aluminum sealing films. Plates were incubated at room temperature for 3 h to 24 h. All titration experiments were performed at least three times.

Following the incubation, plates were read using a PHERAstar FSX plate reader equipped with a polarizing filter (excitation λ = 480 nm; emission λ = 520 nm). The FP signal of the negative control (tracer only) was subtracted from the signal of the mixtures containing tracer and partner protein, and the resulting FP data were expressed as delta millipolarization units (ΔmP) and plotted as a function of protein concentration. GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, Inc), and its one site—total saturation binding equation were used for data fitting, which enabled deriving the KD values—assuming a 1:1 binding mode—according to the following equation:(Eq. 1)Y=Bmax·[P]KD+[P]+NS·[P]+Backgroundwhere, Y is the calculated FP signal, [P] is the concentration of partner protein, Bmax is the maximal FP signal, KD is the equilibrium dissociation constant of the target interaction, NS is the nonspecific binding slope, and Background is the FP signal of the tracer with no partner protein.

CFP assays

Competitive assays were performed in a final volume of 10 μl, with 50 nM of tracer, with either 15 μM of XD or 5 μM of TXD, and an increasing concentration of nonlabeled box3 peptide up to 400 μM. Each data point was performed in triplicate using 384-well Greiner microplates (ref. 784900). The plates were briefly centrifuged as described above, then sealed with adhesive aluminum sealing films, and incubated at room temperature for 3 h to 24 h. All competition experiments were performed three times.

After incubation, plates were read as described above. Data were expressed as %signal by normalizing each point by negative and positive controls according to Equation 2:(Eq. 2)%signal(x)=x−Ctrl(−)Ctrl(+)−Ctrl(−)·100where, %signal(x) is the normalized percentage of FP signal for a given well, x is the raw signal of a given well, Ctrl(−) corresponds to the signal measured for free tracer, and Ctrl(+) corresponds to the signal measured for tracer in the presence of protein partner only.

The negative control is therefore expressed as 0% interaction, and the positive one is expressed as 100% interaction. After normalization, data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9, and a classical four-parameter logistic model allowed the determination of the IC50 according to Equation 3:(Eq. 3)Y=Min+Max−Min1+(IC50[I])Hwhere, Y is the normalized percentage of FP signal, Min is the signal for the control with free tracer, Max is the signal for the control with bound tracer, IC50 is the concentration of competitor that induces 50% loss of normalized signal, [I] is the concentration of competitor (i.e., inhibitor), and H is the Hill coefficient, describing the curve and binding mode.

The Ki, which corresponds to the KD of the inhibitor for the target in the case of a 1:1 competitive inhibition, was determined using the Cheng–Prusoff equation:(Eq. 4)Ki=IC501+[P]KDwhere, Ki is the inhibition constant of the competitor, IC50 is the concentration of competitor that induces 50% loss of signal, [P] is the concentration of partner protein in the assay, KD is the dissociation constant of the target interaction, as previously determined by direct titration.

DMSO tolerance assays

To assess the DMSO tolerance of our assay, positive (5 μM TXD, 50 nM tracer) and negative (50 nM tracer only) mixes containing 0%, 2%, 5%, and 10% DMSO were prepared. Mixes were then distributed as triplicates in a 384-well Greiner microplate, which was briefly centrifuged, sealed, and read after 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h of incubation at room temperature. The experiment was repeated three times, and data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 and a two-way ANOVA test to assess the impact of DMSO% and incubation times on measured signal.

Z’-factor determination

For Z′-factor determination, screening buffer supplemented with 1 mM TCEP and 2% DMSO was used. Positive (5 μM TXD, 50 nM tracer) and negative (50 nM tracer only) mixes were prepared and dispensed in 48 wells of each condition in a 384-well Greiner microplate (ref. 784900). The plate was briefly centrifuged and incubated for 3 h, 6 h, or 24 h at room temperature and read. Means and SDs for each control and replicate were determined and used to calculate Z′-factors at all timepoints using the following equation: (53)(Eq. 5)Z′=1−3∗(σ(+)+σ(−))(μ(+)−μ(−))where, σ is the SD of either positive (+) or negative (−) control values, and μ is the mean of either positive (+) or negative (−) control values.

Primary and secondary chemical library screening

For primary screening, a preliminary 1:10 dilution of the Fr-PPIChem library (46) (3 μl, 10 mM in 100% DMSO) was performed by addition of 27 μl of anhydrous DMSO to each well of all 33 mother plates using a Freedom EVO platform 150 liquid handler (Tecan Life Sciences). Then, 200 nl of compounds at 1 mM in 100% DMSO (or DMSO only) were transferred to 384-well Greiner assay microplates using a mosquito liquid handling robot. Each plate then contained four columns of DMSO only (1-2 and 23-24), whereas columns 3 to 22 contained compounds to screen.

Final conditions for screening were 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% Tween-20, 1 mM TCEP, 2% DMSO with 50 nM tracer, and 5 μM TXD (or no protein in the negative mix).

In primary screening, negative and positive mixes were prepared to enable the distribution of positive mix in columns 1 to 22 and negative mix in columns 23 and 24, yielding a plate with 32 positive controls, 32 negative ones, and 320 tested molecules. This distribution was performed using a Certus flex liquid dispenser (nNano Gyger AG).

All plates were sealed, centrifuged, and incubated at room temperature for 3 h before reading as described above.

The primary screening campaign was statistically analyzed through calculation of positive and negative control means, SDs ,and Z′-factors. Following these verifications, all compound wells were plotted as a function of their FP signal, visually highlighting compounds of interest.

For secondary screening, hit compounds were selected based on Z-score. The latter indicates the statistical significance of the difference between a given datapoint and the global dataset. Z-scores were calculated according to the following equation:(Eq. 6)Z−score=(x−μscreen)σscreenwhere, x is the FP value for a test well (i.e., for a given compound), μscreen is the mean signal of all screened compounds, and σscreen is their SD.

After removing frequent hitters, that is, compounds listed in an internal database compiling various types of primary screening data from the Fr-PPIChem chemical library, as well as most compounds originating from plates exhibiting a global plate–related signal drift over the entire screening campaign (i.e., plate 1 [first] and plate 33 [last] for stabilizers and inhibitors, respectively [cf. Fig. 5B]), Z-score thresholds specific to each hit category were set to an absolute value of 1.96. This Z-score value corresponds to a p value of 0.05 and yielded 46 stabilizer hits and 38 inhibitor ones.

Secondary screening was performed by assessing the dose dependence of the selected compound effect on the interaction. The six-point dose–response range—from 0.63 μM to 20 μM—was prepared using an Echo 650 Acoustic Liquid Handling system (Beckman Coulter), whereas negative and positive mixes were distributed using a Certus flex liquid dispenser (nNano Gyger AG). All plates were sealed, centrifuged, and read after 3 h of incubation at room temperature. For inhibitor hits, dose–response data were treated as done for initial competition assays. For stabilizer hits, data were normalized using the positive control only and expressed as a percentage of interaction according to the following equation:(Eq. 7)%signal(x)=x−Ctrl(+)Ctrl(+)·100where %signal(x) is the normalized percentage of FP signal for a given well, x is the raw signal of a given well, and Ctrl(+) corresponds to the signal measured for tracer in the presence of a protein partner.

These data were then fitted using nonlinear regression with a normalized Hill model equation:(Eq. 8)Y=100·XHEC50H+XHwhere, Y is the normalized response, X is the compound concentration, H is the Hill coefficient, and EC50 is the concentration of competitor that induces 50% increase of normalized signal.

Secondary hits were selected based on their dose–response effect, IC50, EC50, and Hill slope.
