Picosecond switching of magnetic tunnel junctions by spin-orbit torque
Authors: Chen Xiao, Wenlong Cai, Hongchao Zhang, Dinghao Ma, Kaihua Cao, Ao Du, Shiyang Lu, Xiangyu Zheng, Boyu Zhang, Hongxi Liu, Kewen Shi, Weisheng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultrafast switching of magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs) is essential for future high-bandwidth memory and in-memory computing. However, state-of-the-art electrical switching of MTJs remains constrained to hundreds of picoseconds, leaving a substantial gap between memory and processors. Here, we experimentally demonstrate field-free switching of three-terminal MTJs using picosecond electrical pulses driven by spin-orbit torque (SOT), surpassing the speed limit by an order of magnitude. We further use voltage-gated (VG) effect to probe SOT switching mechanisms over wide pulse-width ranges and identify a nonmonotonic VG-SOT efficiency. While the efficiency is pronounced under long-pulse excitation, it diminishes in the subnanosecond regime but reemerges as the pulse width scales down to 13.2 ps, yielding an energy consumption about 30 fJ per bit. Micromagnetic analysis reveals that, from dc to picosecond timescale, the dominant magnetization switching dynamics evolve from thermally activated reversal to incoherent precession to coherent precession. These results establish picosecond VG-SOT as a pathway toward ultrafast and energy-efficient memory and logic.

INTRODUCTION

High-bandwidth memory plays a critical role in accelerating data processing speeds and enabling the development of emerging paradigms such as in-memory computing, which are essential for the advancement of big data and artificial intelligence (1–4). Spintronics, leveraging electron spin manipulation, is crucial for developing ultrafast memory technologies (5, 6). In particular, magnetic random-access memory (MRAM) has emerged as a pivotal nonvolatile memory technology, going beyond traditional charge-based memories due to its fast operation and superior energy efficiency (7–9). Recently, spin-transfer torque (STT) MRAM has been widely adopted for embedded last-level cache, embedded Flash, and artificial intelligence processor applications (10–16), while its speed is limited in nanosecond range due to long incubation time and tunneling barrier constraints (17, 18).

Alternatively, spin-orbit torque (SOT) has been proposed for the next generation of MRAM, offering faster writing speeds by mitigating the current limitation imposed by the tunneling barrier (19–24). However, specific device configurations, such as type-x and type-z SOT magnetic tunnel junctions (SOT-MTJs), typically require external magnetic fields to break symmetry, posing challenges for field-free operation (25–31). As comparison, type-y SOT-MTJs provide a promising route to achieve deterministic, field-free switching with low critical current densities at ultrafast timescales (19, 25, 32–34). The voltage-controlled magnetic anisotropy (VCMA) effect, as an extremely low-power voltage-gated (VG) mechanism (35–38), can be combined with SOT in type-y MTJs to achieve deterministic magnetization switching (39). By tuning the effective out-of-plane magnetic anisotropy (Kouteff), VG effect can further reduce the switching energy (37–39). However, the achieved switching time is still insufficient to match the operating speeds of advanced complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) transistors (40, 41), limiting its broader application in high-bandwidth memory and in-memory computing systems.

Recently, on-chip photoconductive switches have been used to apply sub–10-ps electrical pulses to switch the magnetization of GdFeCo and ferromagnetic thin films, showcasing an approach for exploring the picosecond realm of electrical magnetic manipulation (42, 43). Subsequently, the coherent switching mechanism has been unveiled, and its high-energy efficiency has been further confirmed by varying picosecond pulse width through laser fluence modulation (44, 45). However, the realization of picosecond switching in MRAM-compatible device structures—particularly in energy-efficient VG-SOT-MTJs—remains elusive. In addition, further investigation is needed to verify whether the energy-efficiency at picosecond timescales (45) exists without magnetic field assistance.

In this work, we report the picosecond, energy-efficient, all-electrical switching of MTJs. By developing standalone picosecond electrical pulsers, we successfully liberate the devices from substrate-compatibility dilemma and further explore the SOT switching of MTJs into picosecond regime. Moreover, the gate voltage is found to exert a strong influence on SOT switching dynamics, leading to a VG-SOT efficiency that evolves nonmonotonically with pulse durations. Specifically, while the gating effect sharply diminishes in the subnanosecond regime, it becomes efficient again when the pulse width approaches the picosecond scale, accompanied by a substantial reduction in power consumption. Micromagnetic simulations of the in-plane magnetic anisotropy (IMA) system further suggest that the regime-specific inefficiency around nanosecond originates from the slower domain-wall motion under reduced Kouteff, whereas coherent precessional switching dominates for shorter pulses.

RESULTS

Three-terminal MTJs and their picosecond measurement platform

We use low-temperature-grown gallium arsenide (LT-GaAs) photoconductive switches to generate picosecond pulses. Because of the difficulty of integrating LT-GaAs with the standard MRAM fabrication processes, the previously proposed on-chip photoconductive design (42) is not adopted. Instead, independent photoconductive switches are designed and packaged, with their output port connected to a 110-GHz high-bandwidth connector. Electrical connection to the MTJ is then established via high-bandwidth wires and probes as shown in Fig. 1A. The instantaneous voltage across the MTJ pillar is carefully balanced by precisely adjusting the amplitudes of the 2-ps pulsers and tuning their relative time delay via the delay line. A high-bandwidth oscilloscope (80 GHz) connected through the top electrode is used to monitor the real-time resistance state of the device.

The core stack of the MTJ consists of W (3.5)/CoFeB (1.4)/MgO (1.6)/CoFeB (1.9)/CoFe (0.5)/Ru (0.8)/CoFe (2)/PtMn (7.5), where the numbers denote layer thicknesses in nanometers. The multilayer film was patterned into an elliptical shape with lateral dimensions of 180 nm by 480 nm using micro/nanofabrication processes. The electrodes are configured in a three-terminal coplanar waveguide geometry, as shown in Fig. 1B. The size of bottom electrode is designed as 1000 nm by 700 nm. Under a magnetic field applied along the y direction, the device exhibits two well-defined stable states, with a tunneling magnetoresistance (TMR) ratio of ∼85.7%. A full hysteresis loop covering the reversal of reference layer is included in note S1. By statistically analyzing the field-dependent switching probability (Fig. 1C and note S1), the thermal stability factor Δ is of ∼64.2. This value is further validated by field pulse width–dependent switching measurements across multiple devices, which yield an average Δ of 65.3 (note S1). This thermal stability is sufficient to meet the short-term data retention requirements for high-bandwidth memory and in-memory computing applications. After determination of IMA field (note S1), we estimate the switching volume being equal to a 149-nm-diameter dot, which is a reasonable nucleus size according to our device dimensions.

Picosecond SOT switching and its average dynamics

We then investigated picosecond-scale SOT switching in our MTJ devices. At a laser fluence of 0.3 mJ cm−2, the LT-GaAs photoconductive switch generates electrical pulses with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 13.9 ps, as directly calibrated using an 80-GHz oscilloscope shown in inset of Fig. 2A (notes S2 and S3). By tuning the dc bias applied to the photoconductive switch, the amplitude of the picosecond pulse can be precisely controlled, enabling the acquisition of the SOT switching loop shown in Fig. 2A (see Materials and Methods). Under 13.9-ps pulses, the SOT switching threshold voltage (Vth) is estimated to be ∼4.36 V corresponding to a current density of about 1.5 TA m−2. Subsequently, we varied the laser fluence to modulate the pulse width of the photoconductive switch (45) and statistically extracted the switching threshold voltages of the device under different pulse widths, as summarized in Fig. 2B. As the pulse width (τFWHM) decreases, the threshold voltage gradually increases, although the dependence is slower than the expected 1/τFWHM trend. This deviation may originate from the imperfect temporal shape of the picosecond pulse and the Joule heating effects induced during the pulse excitation.

To investigate the magnetization dynamics during picosecond-pulse excitation, we performed time-resolved electrical measurements using a high-bandwidth oscilloscope to capture the resistance evolution of the MTJ before and after pulse application. To improve readout sensitivity, a 2 ns, −0.2-V flat-top voltage pulse was simultaneously applied for resistance monitoring. A laser fluence of 20 mJ cm−2 was used to generate electrical pulses with 1-kHz repetition rate from the pulser. The pulse timing was synchronized through a trigger circuit, such that the picosecond pulse can be centered within the 2-ns readout window, and the combined waveform was recorded in real time using an 80-GHz oscilloscope (see Materials and Methods).

To extract the magnetoresistive signal, reference traces were obtained by applying ±50-mT magnetic fields along the y direction to stabilize the device in the antiparallel (AP) and parallel (P) states, respectively. Each reference trace was averaged more than 3000 acquisitions. Subsequently, SOT-induced switching measurements were performed under zero external field, also averaged more than 3000 events, as shown in Fig. 2C. Before the picosecond pulse, the switching trace (VSW) overlaps perfectly with the AP reference (VAP), confirming that the initial state is consistently AP. At the pulse peak, the two traces remain nearly identical, indicating that the picosecond pulse itself does not cause large-scale magnetization reversal. A pronounced deviation appears shortly after the pulse peak, where VSW gradually approaches Vp, indicating that the device progressively completes the magnetization reversal. To visualize the switching dynamics more directly, we calculated a normalized voltage signal, (VSW − VAP)/(Vp − VAP), and repeated the measurements under picosecond pulses of varying amplitudes (30). The resulting normalized traces are shown in Fig. 2D. As the amplitude of the picosecond electrical pulse increases, the time required for complete magnetization reversal becomes progressively shorter, reaching as low as ∼280 ps at the amplitude of 3.1 TA m−2. These results indicate that the picosecond pulse primarily triggers magnetization precession and possible nucleation events, whereas the subsequent reversal arises from the trailing edge of the pulse and the ensuing dynamic evolution.

VG-SOT induced energy-efficient picosecond switching

To explore the VG modulation of SOT switching dynamics, a gate voltage (Vg) was applied to the top electrode of the MTJ concurrently with the SOT pulse, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. The gate voltage tunes the interfacial perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA) of the free layer through the VCMA effect, thereby influencing the SOT-induced magnetization switching. During picosecond-pulse excitation, a 300-ps gate pulse was simultaneously applied to the MTJ, temporally aligned such that the picosecond pulse was centered within the Vg window. When relatively long picosecond pulses were used (22.0 ps, corresponding to a laser fluence of 10 mJ cm−2 ), the switching threshold voltage exhibited only a weak dependence on Vg, as shown in Fig. 3B. In contrast, when the pulse width was further reduced to 13.2 ps (laser fluence of 0.2 mJ cm−2), a pronounced gate-voltage dependence emerged, as seen in Fig. 3C.

The statistical analysis of the switching threshold under different Vg values (Fig. 3D) reveals a nearly linear relationship between the critical switching voltage (or current density) and Vg, indicating that the modulation is predominantly governed by the VCMA effect, whereas contributions from spin-transfer torque or thermal effects are negligible (30). The corresponding SOT switching energy can be reduced by more than half, reaching as low as 232 fJ per bit. Considering that the bottom electrode area is much larger than the junction itself, the equivalent optimized SOT energy consumption could be further reduced to about 30 fJ for a rectangular bottom electrode of 180 nm by 480 nm, showing its potential for ultraefficient operations.

To investigate the possible other effect (such as SOT, STT, or thermal) and the endurance issue induced by the gate voltage, we analyzed the current density flowing through the junction and the reliability of the device at Vg = 2 V (note S8). The STT current density is estimated as ∼0.12 MA·cm−2 at 300 ps, much smaller than the typical switching threshold (∼3 MA·cm−2 at dc) (27), indicating that the contribution from STT is negligible. In our experiments, the relatively low resistance area product (46), large MTJ size, and 300-ps-long voltage pulse result in a gating energy contribution of ∼60 fJ per bit. This can be further easily reduced to sub ∼ few femtojoule per bit(46), scaling dimensions and NAND-like structure (38, 47), etc. Moreover, in endurance assessments at Vg = 2 V, the picosecond VG-SOT configuration was verified up to 108 cycles, with the upper limit determined by the 1-kHz laser repetition rate; using synchronized 0.4-ns SOT and gate voltage pulses at a high repetition rate (∼28 MHz), the device maintained stable P and AP resistance states beyond 1012 cycles (note S4), demonstrating excellent stability and reliability under high gate voltage with subnanosecond narrow pulsing conditions.

Micromagnetic simulations of picosecond magnetization switching by VG-SOT effects

To better understand the distinct effects of VG-SOT under different picosecond pulse durations, we performed micromagnetic simulations. Considering that the VCMA effect mainly affects the interfacial PMA energy density (Ki), we set different PMA density (Ku) values, defined as Ki/tCoFeB, to simulate the VCMA effect in OOMMF (48). The simulations were conducted with Ku values of 0.96 and 0.90 MJ·m−3, corresponding to Vg = −2 V and +2 V, respectively (see Materials and Methods).

Figure 4 (A and B) shows the simulated magnetization dynamics under 22.0- and 13.2-ps SOT pulses, respectively, for Ku = 0.96 and 0.90 MJ·m−3, corresponding to the VCMA effects induced by Vg = −2 V and +2 V. In Fig. 4A, with a current density of 2.4 TA m−2, the magnetization exhibits coherent precession during the first 100 ps. However, because the current density is relatively low, my does not cross zero within this time window. After 100 ps, because of the nonuniform distribution of demagnetizing and shape anisotropy fields, incoherent precession develops, and nucleation appears near the two ends of the elliptical MTJ. These nucleation sites then expand into magnetic domains, leading to complete reversal, consistent with the experimental switching dynamics observed in Fig. 2 (C and D).

A clear contrast between Vg = −2 V and + 2 V can be identified. At Vg = −2 V, the reduction of Kouteff facilitates and accelerates nucleation, leading to an earlier onset of domain formation. However, the smaller Kouteff also slows down the subsequent domain-wall propagation, resulting in a prolonged coexistence of multidomain states and a slower overall reversal. Thus, the gate voltage exerts opposite influences on precessional nucleation and domain propagation, leading to a nearly neutral net modulation of the total switching speed. The corresponding spatially averaged my trace and the 22.0-ps pulse profile are shown in Fig. 4C.

When a shorter 13.2-ps pulse is applied, the reduced pulse width and weaker trailing tail require a higher current density of 5 TA m−2 to achieve switching. The simulated magnetization evolution in Fig. 4B reveals that, at these high excitation amplitudes, the magnetization precession is spatially more uniform within the first 70 ps for both Vg values. For Vg = −2 V, my successfully crosses zero and completes a coherent full reversal. In contrast, for Vg = +2 V, the increased Kouteff suppresses the precession amplitude, preventing my from coherently crossing zero. Around 90 ps, only partial magnetization near the long-axis edges reverses, leading to transient domain formation that eventually relaxes back to the initial state due to insufficient driving torque. The spatially averaged my evolution and the corresponding 13.2-ps pulse profile are shown in Fig. 4D.

Pulse width dependence of VG-SOT across distinct reversal modes

To further clarify the role of VG effect as a knob to distinguish and understand the different SOT switching dynamics, we extended our study to a wider range of pulse widths, from picoseconds to subnanoseconds and up to hundreds of nanoseconds (note S4). SOT switching loops measurements were repeated 10 times under various Vg for each SOT pulse width. To quantify the modulation effect of Vg on SOT switching more intuitively, we introduce a coefficient η defined as the ratio of the threshold current density Jth at Vg = +2 V to that at Vg = −2 V, that is, η = (Jth |Vg = +2 V)/(Jth |Vg = −2 V). The extracted results are summarized in Fig. 5.

VG-SOT thresholds ratio dependence on writing pulse durations.

(A) Experimental validation of VG-SOT thresholds induced by picosecond and nanosecond SOT pulses with three switching regimes I to III, respectively. Here, we calculated the VG-SOT efficiency as a figure-of-merit η (top red dashed line) according to the theoretical analysis (note S6). The process of nucleation and propagation can occur in both regime I and regime III.

As shown in Fig. 5, in the long-pulse regime (region I, 10 ns and above), η is ∼1.48. This could be that SOT-induced magnetization reversal follows a thermally activated regime (including random nucleation and propagation), where Vg can effectively increase or decrease the energy barrier, which dominates the nucleation possibility.

From the nanosecond range down to 300 ps and further to the picosecond pulses (22 ps with trailing edge) generated by the photoconductive switch (region II), η remains nearly constant around 1.05. In this region, the magnetization reversal behavior deviates markedly from the macrospin-type switching, which results in a noticeable discrepancy between the experimentally determined η and its theoretical value ηtheoretical as shown in Fig. 5. This is rather different from previously reported VG-SOT studies on PMA system in the nanosecond regime (30, 49). In our IMA system, a negative Vg assists the current-induced precessional nucleation by lowering the energy barrier but tends to hinder the in-plane domain wall propagation by reducing the Hk,⊥eff, and thus its overall promotion effect becomes less notable, as reflected in our micromagnetic simulations shown in Fig. 4 (A and C).

When the pulse width is reduced below 14 ps (region III), η rises sharply again, indicating that coherent precession switching becomes dominant, as explained by the micromagnetic simulations in Fig. 4 (B and D). Since the magnetization reversal is dominated by coherent precession, domain formation becomes negligible, and the modulation effect of Vg gradually recovers. As a result, the value of η progressively converges toward the theoretical value predicted by the macrospin model.

From the magnetic-field measurement method (note S5), the VCMA coefficient of the device is determined to be ∼31 fJ V−1 m−1, which is comparable to that measured in previous in-plane VG-SOT-MTJs (39) and larger than the VCMA coefficient observed in perpendicular type-z VG-SOT-MTJs (38, 49). According to the macrospin model of current density, the theoretical value of η (ηtheoretical) is calculated to be 1.52 (note S6), which agrees well with the experimental result. The minor deviation should be attributed to the fitting uncertainty of the VCMA coefficient. Although Fig. 5 serves as a visual guide, the boundaries between the three switching modes are not sharply defined. Similar behavior has been consistently reproduced across multiple devices (note S9).

DISCUSSION

This work demonstrates picosecond, energy-efficient, all-electrical switching of three-terminal SOT-MTJs driven by VG-SOT, advancing the operational speed of spintronic devices by an order of magnitude. By using standalone LT-GaAs photoconductive switches, 13.9-ps electrical pulses are precisely delivered to three-terminal MTJs, enabling reliable magnetization reversal at current densities down to 2.5 TA m−2. A pronounced voltage-gating effect emerges under 13.2-ps pulses, reducing the SOT switching energy by more than half to 30 fJ per bit and highlighting the strong coupling between voltage control and SOT at ultrafast timescales.

Systematic studies over a wide range of pulse widths reveal the landscape of three distinct switching regimes. In the few nanoseconds and longer-pulse region, magnetization reversal is thermally activated, including random nucleation and propagation, and the gate voltage modulates the switching threshold mainly through changes in the interfacial PMA, yielding a VCMA coefficient of about 31 fJ V−1 m−1.

When entering the ∼20 ps to few nanoseconds region, switching becomes increasingly SOT-driven, but full reversal still requires multiple precessional cycles. During the extended evolution, spatial inhomogeneities such as edge demagnetizing fields, local anisotropy variations, and defects progressively accumulate, leading to phase dispersion across the device. Thus, the SOT dynamics are dominated by incoherent precession (nucleation and propagation), where a negative Vg lowers the nucleation barrier but simultaneously reduces Hk,⊥eff, slowing domain propagation speed and weakening the overall modulation effect.

When the pulse width falls toward below 14 ps, the quasi-coherent precessional switching dominates, as verified by micromagnetic simulations. In this coherent regime, the spin-orbit torque is sufficiently strong to drive most local magnetization vectors across the switching trajectory within approximately one precessional cycle. Although spatial inhomogeneities remain, the ultrafast timescale suppresses their accumulation into substantial phase decoherence. Therefore, a negative Vg efficiently reduces the energy barrier without hindering magnetization uniformity, leading to the reemergence of a strong VG effect.

These results not only clarify the pulse width–dependent crossover between domain-mediated and coherent magnetization reversal but also establish voltage gating as an effective control knob to manipulate ultrafast spin dynamics. The achieved picosecond-scale operation with low energy consumption demonstrates the feasibility of integrating SOT-MTJs into future high-speed, energy-efficient nonvolatile memory and in-memory computing architectures. Further optimization of picosecond pulsers may push spintronic devices into the faster regime, providing deeper validation of coherent switching and a more comprehensive understanding of ultrafast magnetization dynamics. Moreover, enhanced VG-SOT efficiency promises faster and lower-power device operation, positioning in-plane SOT-MTJs as highly suitable candidates for future ultrafast spintronic memory technologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample fabrication

Multilayer thin films are sputter-deposited at room temperature on a 200-mm wafer substrate using the dc/radio frequency magnetron sputtering. The full SOT-MTJ stack is composed of W (3.5)/CoFeB (1.4)/MgO (1.6)/CoFeB (1.9)/CoFe (0.5)/Ru (0.8)/CoFe (2)/PtMn (7.5) and covered by Ta/Ru/Ta capping layers, whose numbers in parentheses signify every layer thickness in nanometers. The stacks are annealed at 300°C in a vacuum for 1 hour in a magnetic field of 1 T along the −y direction and are then cooled to room temperature under the same field. The SOT-MTJs are patterned into an ellipse of 180-nm short axis and 480-nm long axis through ion beam etch. The heavy metal (HM) layer, free layer, barrier layer, and reference layer (RL) consist of W/CoFeB/ MgO/CoFeB, respectively. Here, we use W as the HM layer due to its large spin Hall angle of ∼−0.3, as estimated in our previous work (50, 51). The CoFe (0.5)/Ru (0.8)/CoFe (2.0)/PtMn (7.5) multilayers serve as a synthetic antiferromagnetic layer to pin the RL layer, which can also effectively regulate and control the stray field. MTJ stacks are capped with by Ta/Ru/Ta multilayers to prevent oxidation.

Picosecond electrical setup

Standalone photoconductive switches: a GaAl0.8As buffer around 300 nm is grown on GaAs (100) commercial substrate (∼350 μm) by molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) at 550°C and then deposit the LT-GaAs layer (∼1.2 μm) by MBE at 260°C. Subsequently, a SiO2 dielectric layer, ∼70-nm thick, is deposited on LT-GaAs at 100°C using inductively coupled plasma chemical vapor deposition to provide electrical isolation between the coplanar waveguide ground and signal electrodes. The dielectric layer in the switch region is subsequently removed using a lift-off process. Following this, Ti (50 nm)/AlCu (200 nm)/TiN (30 nm) electrodes are deposited and patterned, forming interdigitated electrodes with a 12-μm spacing and a coplanar waveguide for pulse transmission. The switches are then individually diced and separated, followed by packaging steps including fixation, wire bonding for power supply, and compression with 110-GHz bandwidth connectors, resulting in standalone photoconductive switches.

Optical setup: an amplified Ti:sapphire laser system is used with pulse width of 35 fs at a central wavelength of 800 nm. Its repetition rate is adjusted from 1 kHz to 1 Hz using a pulse picker. The laser beam is split into the pump beam 1 and the pump beam 2 and then focused on the two photoconductive switches using objective lens with a focal length of 75 mm. A delay line with a displacement accuracy of 0.25 μm is placed along the path of pump beam 2 to precisely control the relative time interval between the two femtosecond laser pulses reaching the switches, ensuring that both picosecond pulses arrive simultaneously at the bottom electrode of MTJs. Moreover, the pulse energy can be precisely tuned through two set of motorized half-wave plates and polarizers.

Picosecond electrical test: In the sole SOT picosecond switching experiment, we first use an oscilloscope with 80-GHz bandwidth to characterize the relationship between the bias voltage and output voltage (V1 and V2) of the two switches. In the measurement setup, all components through which the picosecond pulses propagate, including the bias tee, resistive combiner and high-frequency probes, support a bandwidth from dc up to 110 GHz, and their insertion losses have been taken into account in the analysis to ensure accurate delivery and calibration of the picosecond pulses to the MTJ. By setting the bias voltage of the two switches such that V2 = −V1, the net voltage across the MTJ is effectively kept at zero when the picosecond pulses arrive at the bottom electrode. Next, the repetition rate of the femtosecond laser is set to 2 Hz, meaning the two switches generate picosecond pulses that simultaneously reach the MTJ bottom electrode every 0.5 s. During this interval, the resistance state of the MTJ device is measured using a semiconductor analyzer. The bias voltage supplied to the switches is adjusted to change the amplitude of the picosecond pulses for the next test point, ultimately completing the SOT switching loop characterization. The SOT pulse VSOT can be estimated as 2(V1 − V2) Rb/(Rb + 50). In the picosecond VG-SOT experiment, we connect one channel of the commercial pulser to the top of the MTJ to apply a 300-ps-width Vg and use the trigger function of the pulse picker to synchronize the Vg pulse with the picosecond SOT pulses.

Nanosecond electrical test: a commercial pulse generator and a semiconductor analyzer are used to generate the short pulse ranging from 0.3 to 100 ns, defined by their FWHM, for SOT switching measurements. Two pulse channels are connected with two bottom electrodes of the MTJ and generate two synchronized electrical pulses V1 and V2, respectively. The SOT pulse VSOT can be estimated as 2(V1 − V2) Rb /(Rb + 50) and the gate pulse Vg can be written as (V1 + V2) Rb /(Rb + 50), where Rb is the resistance of the bottom electrode. Considering that the resistance of MTJs (60 to 120 kΩ) is much larger than that of bottom electrode (∼600 Ω), the current flowing through the bottom electrode induced by Vg is much smaller than that of VSOT, making its contribution to the SOT effect negligible. For the magnetic test, we perform the R-H measurement by using a magnetic field autotest probe station. These results are characterized by Wafer-level Magnetic Test System for MRAM and Magnetic Sensors (Truth Instruments, SPIN Tester M).

Critical current and energy cost definitions

The critical current is given by I = 2(V1 − V2)/(Rb + 50), where V1 and V2 are the calibrated voltages of two switches or two channels of the commercial pulser, measured using the 80-GHz bandwidth oscilloscope. The energy cost of SOT pulses is calculated as the integral over time of the delivered power, given by P(t) = {2[V1(t) − V2(t)] Rb/(Rb + 50)}2/Rb, where V1(t) and V2(t) are the time-resolved traces of the two incident pulses.

Time-resolved dynamics measurements

To characterize the real-time magnetization dynamics of the device under picosecond excitation, picosecond current pulses are injected into one side of the bottom electrode of the three-terminal MTJ. To improve readout sensitivity, a 2 ns, −0.2-V flat-top voltage pulse is simultaneously applied from the other side of the bottom electrode for resistance monitoring. The timing of the picosecond pulse is synchronized through a trigger circuit such that the pulse is centered within the 2-ns readout window. A high-bandwidth oscilloscope (80 GHz) is connected to the top electrode of the MTJ, in series with its input impedance. As the nanosecond and picosecond pulses propagate through the bottom electrode, a small portion of the signal is coupled through the MTJ into the oscilloscope, enabling real-time detection of the resistance variation during switching.

Because the MTJ used in this study has a 1.6-nm-thick MgO barrier, its resistance ranges from around 60 to 120 kohm, resulting in a small signal amplitude that is difficult to distinguish from noise. To ensure sufficient signal-to-noise ratio, identical devices with the same layer stack and electrode geometry but with a thinner MgO barrier (∼1.2 nm) are fabricated, giving a resistance of around 4.5 ∼ 8 kohm. It is worth noting that the applied −0.2 V readout pulse is far below both the SOT switching threshold and the picosecond pulse amplitude; therefore, the SOT effect induced by this nanosecond read pulse can be neglected.

To extract the magnetoresistive signal, reference traces are obtained by applying ±50 mT magnetic fields along the y direction to stabilize the device in the AP and P states, respectively. The nanosecond read pulse and picosecond write pulse are applied simultaneously to the MTJ under these two fixed states and repeated 3000 times, with the oscilloscope averaging the acquired signals to obtain VAP and VP as reference curves. Subsequently, the external magnetic field is removed, and the device is reset to the AP state using a large-amplitude reverse pulse. The read and write pulses are then applied again to capture the switching event. This reset-and-switching sequence is repeated 3000 times, and the averaged trace is recorded as VSW. To visualize the switching dynamics more directly, a normalized voltage signal (VSW − VAP)/(VP − VAP) is calculated.

Micromagnetic simulation

In the simulation section, we use the OOMMF software to simulate the switching dynamics of various canted devices under SOT and VCMA effects, with pulse widths of 2 ns, and simultaneously obtain the magnetization switching trajectories. The dimensions of the free layer are set to 180 nm by 480 nm by 1.4 nm. The mesh size is set to 1 nm by 1 nm by 0.7 nm. The field-like torque effective field in our W/CoFeB structure is one order smaller than that of damping-like torque, and therefore its influence on magnetization switching can be negligible (50). The perpendicular anisotropy energy density is set from 0.90 to 0.96 MJ m−3 to simulate the impact of VCMA effect induced by Vg. The saturation magnetization is set to 1.3 MA m−1, and the exchange stiffness is set as 16 pJ m−1. The Gilbert damping coefficient is set to 0.02.
