Transition of obesity in India, 2006–2021: a state-wise investigation from an equity perspective
Authors: Jagyenswar Rath, Dayeeta Bera, Lucky Singh, Jyoti Ghosal, Sanghamitra Pati, Ambarish Dutta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObesity and its downstream non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are the greatest health challenges of the 21st century, even in low-income and middle-income countries such as India. As traditional diseases of poverty wane, obesity and NCD burden rise. A crucial lens for understanding this transition is nation/state-wise ‘economic gradient’ of obesity. Therefore, we aimed to study the socioeconomic gradient of obesity and its changes (2006–2021) across India’s major states vis-à-vis their varying prosperity from an equitable policy perspective.MethodsWe analysed data from non-pregnant women aged 15–49 years from National Family Health Surveys (NFHS-3 in 2006 and NFHS-5 in 2021), excluding the North-Eastern states and Union Territories. Obesity was defined as body mass index ≥25 kg/m². State-wise economic gradient was quantified using Slope Index of Inequality (SII) based on state-level wealth quintiles, assessed using gross state domestic product (GSDP). We examined associations between GSDP and both obesity prevalence and SII in both time periods.ResultsObesity prevalence increased in all states as well as nationally between 2006 and 2021 (12.3% in NFHS 3 to 23.6% in NFHS 5), but the economic gradient neither flattened nor reversed. The association between GSDP and obesity prevalence remained positive. However, the relationship between GSDP and SII (r=0.69, p=0.001 in 2006 to r=0.05, p=0.8 in 2021) weakened over time in wealthier states and steepened in poorer ones.ConclusionsDespite its ubiquitous growth, obesity was (2006) and remains (2021) concentrated among the rich in every state, though states are at varying stages of obesity transition. While none have experienced reversal of the economic gradient, green shoots of reversal are evident in affluent states through weakening of their economic gradients, while gradients intensified in poorer ones. These findings underscore the need for nuanced, state-specific strategies to ensure equitable distribution of healthcare resources to address rising obesity and NCD epidemic, without neglecting the unfinished agenda of communicable diseases.

Introduction

The global obesity crisis has escalated to critical levels, emerging as one of the most pressing public health challenges of the 21st century, with profound implications for both population health and economic development.1 2 Between 1975 and 2016, the prevalence of obesity globally surged by a staggering 138%, with a notable 21% rise occurring in just the decade from 2006 to 2016.3 Projections suggest that, if current trajectories persist, by 2030, 38% of the global adult population will be overweight, 20% will be obese.4

The rising obesity trends were ushered in by a phenomenon known as ‘nutrition transition’, which is the shift in dietary patterns and lifestyles that occurs as societies become wealthier, more urbanised and more integrated into global trade.5 6 It is characterised by a move away from traditional, fibre-rich diets towards ‘Western-style’ diets, accompanied by a decline in physical activity. This transition, which has been the primary driver of increasing obesity worldwide, is largely shaped by macro-level socioeconomic shifts in society rather than being defined by individual choices.7

Obesity is one of the critical determinants of many non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular diseases and their proximal risk factors that include hypertension and diabetes, and many types of cancer.1 8 Consequently, the increasing trends of obesity that tracked the economic growth globally were associated with a rise in NCDs—the so-called ‘diseases of affluence’ as they are largely associated with food excess and sedentariness.9 These started eclipsing ‘diseases of poverty’ that include communicable diseases and diseases associated with undernutrition and childbirth, in many regions, a phenomenon referred to as ‘epidemiological transition’.51012 The obesity epidemic started first in the developed nations, and the ‘contagion’ started following the prosperity trail to every corner of the world, currently ravaging through the emerging low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). This pervasive phenomenon has been aptly termed the ‘globesity’ epidemic, reflecting its widespread nature.7 LMICs like India are experiencing a steady rise in rates of overweight and obesity at the back of considerable decline in undernourished and underweight population9 in the last two decades.

Meanwhile, researchers, in addition to estimating population averages of obesity, started exploring its distribution in different economic strata of societies—known as the ‘economic gradient’ of obesity—to check the socioeconomic inequality in the burden of excess bodyweight and its downstream NCDs. The gradient was found to be positive in developing nations, signifying greater concentration of obesity in richer echelons in poorer countries, whereas it was negative in economically developed nations, marking greater obesity among the poorer in high-income nations.1316 This phenomenon led to the framing of the ‘obesity transition’, which posits that as a nation or region grows economically, obesity not only rises but it also experiences a shift in its concentration from richer to the poorer sections of society,17 leading to ‘reversal’ of the economic gradient of obesity. And this transition is more clearly noticeable among women.10 This phenomenon closely tracks a similar reversal in the socio-economic distribution of NCDs.18 Zhou further suggested that, along with economic development, an increasing level of globalisation of a country also gradually propels the gradient from positive to negative.15 Studies of this gradient and its reversal became central to the equity debate in the developing nations around the focus of resource allocation between the emerging burden of NCDs and traditional diseases of poverty. Once the constellation of non-communicable conditions starts reversing from being diseases of affluence to diseases of poverty, greater investment of public resources is needed to equitably address them. But if policymakers start investing heavily in NCDs prematurely before the reversal happens, it could mean inequitable diversion of investment from the traditional diseases of poverty, many of which still remain as ‘unfinished business’ among the less affluent majority in the developing world.19 20 Therefore, the gradient of obesity and NCDs can be a guiding beacon for appropriate policy making and resource allocation in transitioning nations.

India’s notable economic growth during 2006–2021,21 despite its lower-middle-income status,22 raises important questions about how this development has modified the economic gradient of excess body weight in the country.23 There is conflicting evidence regarding the change in the economic gradient of obesity and its associated NCDs in India. While Corsi and Subramanian suggest that in Indian populations, the economic gradient of obesity continues to be positive,24 Patel et al argue on the contrary, suggesting that the gradient is fast flattening as people across all SES are getting affected25 by obesity and NCDs. This paradox in evidence is likely to play in the policy circles as well, serving as a key impediment to decisive policy implementation.

Therefore, there is an urgent need to comprehensively disentangle this discordance in the body of evidence. Meanwhile, India, as a country, has a vast geographic and sociodemographic diversity, and its economic growth has been markedly uneven, resulting in wide socioeconomic differences between states.2628 Therefore, judging India’s economic gradient of obesity and its transition through a national lens might be misleading. The gradient, when viewed from a state-wise perspective, can provide valuable insights into the various stages of transition of obesity and NCDs the different Indian states are in, and hence can help nuanced local policy making13 14 and unbiased resource apportionment. However, a notable knowledge gap in this field is the insufficient analysis of the heterogeneity of the gradient within states and their different trajectories over time.

Therefore, in this study, we systematically aimed to quantify the socioeconomic gradient of obesity among women across India’s major states, and also, the change in this gradient over time. Since the economic gradient of obesity is contingent on the collective wealth of a nation or its states—the crux of the reversal hypothesis—we also aimed to assess how the state-wise economic gradient of obesity and its temporal changes were influenced by the prosperity of the Indian states.

Methods

Data and participants

The study used data from two rounds of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), round 3 (2005–2006) and round 5 (2019–2021). We omitted the data of the fourth round (2015–2016) because it was too close to the fifth round, and major population-level changes in the prevalence of chronic conditions cannot be anticipated within such a short period. NFHS is the Indian version of the Demographic and Health Survey, which is carried out periodically using harmonised tools in almost every developing country. This survey provides state and national-level data for India, on its child and infant mortality, fertility rates, maternal and child-care, family planning practices, nutritional status, reproductive health, nutritional status, anaemia and other qualities of family and health planning facilities. In rounds 3 and 5 of NFHS, data were collected from 109 041 and 636 699 households, respectively, across all 29 states and 7 union territories of India. The individual-level response rates were 95% in NFHS-3 and 97% in NFHS-5.29 30

In this analysis, we used data of 15–49-year-old women who were not pregnant at the time of the interview. Smaller northeastern states, Union Territories (both current and erstwhile) and National Capital Region of Delhi were excluded from the analysis as these geopolitical units had been receiving additional central financial assistance which often distorts their per capita gross state domestic product (GSDP), and since GSDP was an important state-level indicator to identify the prosperity of the states in our analysis, we excluded these units (figure 1). Therefore, our study included 19 states for the final analysis.

Variables

Obesity (or combined overweight/obesity) is the binary outcome variable in this study. We adopt the classic WHO international classification, in which individuals with BMI ≥25.0 kg/m² are classified as overweight/obese.31 This threshold remains the standard benchmark in many epidemiological studies globally, as well as in India’s national surveys and health monitoring systems. Although alternative lower thresholds (BMI ≥23.0 kg/m²) have been proposed in the literature, particularly for Asian and Indian populations to account for earlier onset of cardiometabolic risk, such cut-offs are not yet universally codified in global or national classification frameworks.3234 Consequently, our primary analyses are anchored on BMI ≥25.0 kg/m².

Principal explanatory variables

We consider wealth index and state-level prosperity (GSDP) as principal explanatory variables, reflecting economic status both at the household and state level, respectively:

Wealth Index: A composite measure derived via principal component analysis on 26 household assets. The first principal component score for each household is used to assign a continuous wealth score, which is then categorised into state-specific quintiles.

Per capita GSDP: a state-level variable —for 2006 and 2021, was used as a measure of state-level prosperity. GSDP for these two timepoints was not re-estimated to a common base year (was not inflation-adjusted), as we did not intend to track its growth over time but we used them to rank the Indian states based on their relative prosperity at two separate timepoints.

Other variables

Educational attainment was categorised into four categories: illiterate, primary, secondary and higher. Caste or social group status was classified into scheduled caste, scheduled tribe, other backward classes, general caste and don’t know. Place of residence was dichotomised as urban or rural, based on the respondent’s location of dwelling during data collection.

Missing data

In the main sample (NFHS-3: N=89 484, NFHS-5: N=530 244) we encountered missing data in caste (NFHS-3: N=2081 (2.3%), NFHS-5: N=18 000 (3.4%)) and education (NFHS-3: 84 (0.1%)).

Out of non-pregnant women of 15–49 years from 19 states (NFHS-3: N=96 159, NFHS-5: N=619 296) BMI data was missing in NFHS-3: N=6630 (6.9%) and NFHS-5: N=88 296 (14.2%). These observations with missing BMI data were excluded from the analysis (figure 1).

Statistical analyses

Analysis with individual-level data

Sociodemographic characteristics of the two samples were described using unweighted numbers and weighted percentages (national-level sample weights) as others have done.35 We estimated the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) to capture the absolute difference in the outcome across the full socioeconomic gradient. The SII was obtained by regressing the binary measure of obesity on the relative rank (from 0 to 1) of socioeconomic groups, yielding the predicted difference of obesity between the lowest and highest position of wealth. This method integrated all groups (quintiles), accounted for group sizes and expressed inequality in the same units as the outcome itself.36 The SII ranges between −1 to +1; the further it is away from zero on the positive side, the greater is the inequality due to concentration of the outcome among the rich, and the further it is away on the negative side, the greater is the inequality due to concentration of the outcome among the poor. Zero denotes no inequality. CIs were derived to assess statistical uncertainty. State sample weights were used in the computation of the SII.

Analysis with state-level data

Further, we created a state-level dataset, comprising state-wise overall proportion of obesity, wealth quintile-wise proportion of obesity and their SII for 2006 and 2021. The GSDP data for these two time points were also mapped to the respective states (online supplemental table 1). Based on this dataset, we visualised the relationship between GSDP and state-wise prevalence of obesity, as well as GSDP and state-wise SII at two time points (2006 and 2021) and also estimated the correlation coefficients of these relationships.

Sensitivity analysis

We assessed the robustness of our findings by repeating all principal analyses (individual-level SII, state-level gradients and correlation models) using an alternative cut-off of BMI ≥23.0 kg/m²—a threshold supported by Indian consensus guidelines for overweight/obesity among Asians.37

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research due to the retrospective nature of this secondary data analysis.

Discussion

We set out to estimate the economic gradient of obesity and its changes over time among Indian women of 15–49 years across India’s major states, in the backdrop of increasing obesity in the country during the past many years. We also aimed to search for signs of reversal of this economic gradient in Indian states characterised by their state-level prosperity.

Between 2006 and 2021, India and all its states witnessed a significant increase in both prosperity38 and obesity.39 40 The positive association between state-level prosperity and their respective burdens of obesity28 41 remained steadfast—‘richer the state, the greater the prevalence of obesity’ as this was true in 2006 and remained equally true in 2021. The level of obesity varied immensely between Indian states; for instance, in 2006, the less affluent Bihar and Jharkhand had 4.7% and 5.1% obesity, respectively, while richer Punjab and Kerala already had 29.6% and 27.9%, respectively. In 2021, these estimates changed to 15.9% and 11.8% in Bihar and Jharkhand, respectively, and 40.5% and 39.0% in Punjab and Kerala, respectively. This exemplifies that poorer states continue to lag considerably behind the richer states in terms of their obesity burden, despite an across-the-board increase in obesity in all Indian states during this 15-year period.

The globalisation and westernisation of the food system continue to play a decisive role in this obesity transition in developing countries,6 42 including India. These changes in the food system have rendered ‘ready-to-eat foods’, ‘fast foods’, ‘sugary beverages’ and ‘food high in fat, salt and sugar’, in a nutshell ‘westernised’ foods, as status symbols and often as the more affordable option.612 14 23 4345 Financial strains further exacerbate an inclination towards these unhealthy dietary options, creating a situation of calorie overload.6 46 Moreover, shrinking physical spaces due to poor urban planning of rapidly growing Indian cities and increasing mechanisation of manual work as well as household chores has restricted calorie expenditure.6 47 These have fuelled the obesity epidemic in India in an unrelenting manner across all states.24 48

Zhou’s hypothesis postulated that a nation’s economic advancement—measured by Gross Domestic Product per capita—attenuates the obesity-inducing influence of income.15 Furthermore, economic development may eventually invert this relationship, transforming income’s role from promoting obesity to suppressing it.15 49 Therefore, we expected that richer Indian states by 2021 may witness some weakening, if not reversal, of the economic gradient of obesity.14 However, we observed that the economic gradient did not flatten in any Indian state, let alone reverse, which implies obesity still continues to ‘burn’ the richer section of the Indian society more than their poorer counterparts. But, at the same time, it was found in 2006, ‘the richer the state, the greater was the economic gradient of obesity’. This association between GSDP and SII was lost by 2021, while poorer states witnessed further accentuation of the economic gradient of obesity in these 15 years, the richer states experienced at least a modest weakening of their gradients during that period. The latter can be considered as the ‘green shoot’ of reversal of the economic gradient of obesity in India. This is because the poorer women in the richer states acquired obesity at a higher rate than richer women between 2006 and 2021.39 This pattern may reflect greater affordability of the poor within the rich states to procure calories, mainly the ‘fashionable’ ones, and increasing mechanisation of manual labour linked to rising sedentariness. Meanwhile, growing health literacy and aspiration among richer women to attain a slim figure—another western symbol of prosperity—may be associated with the slower growth of obesity among the affluent section in these richer states. These factors could have contributed to the weakening of the economic gradient of obesity in these prosperous provinces. On the contrary, during these 15 years, obesity among the rich in the poorer states grew at a faster rate than in the poor. This may be due to women of higher echelons in the poorer states mainly being able to afford an increase in consumption of calories as their economic resources grew. Also, perhaps the aspiration for a slimmer figure has not yet ‘kicked in’ among them as these regions lag behind the richer states in trends of westernisation and globalisation.44 Whereas the poorer section in the poorer states is still riddled with some food insecurity coupled with harder labour in these less mechanised economies, this insecurity continues to largely ‘protect’ the poor from obesity in the less affluent states. This is likely to have led to accentuation of the economic gradient of obesity in poorer states between 2006 and 2021, in contrast to its modest weakening in the richer states.

Jaacks et al narrated four stages of obesity transition and reversal as a phenomenon occurring in one of these stages: stage 1: characterised by low obesity prevalence and steep positive economic gradient, stage 2: increased obesity prevalence with modest flattening of the economic gradient, stage 3: stationary high obesity prevalence and reversal of the economic gradient and stage 4: decline in obesity and everything else remaining constant.10 Even in 2021, the poorer Indian states remain entrenched in stage 1 of this framework, where the burden in absolute terms is not too high, but the economic gradient is steep and has further steepened between 2006 and 2021.10 The richer states are in stage 2, where the burden has risen to high and slight weakening of the economic gradient could be noticed in these 15 years. None of the states has, however, shifted to stage 3. Perhaps some of the rich states with already flat economic gradient, like Kerala, may reach stage 3 soon.10

A unique revelation thus emerges from our study—India as a nation is undergoing a multitude of different subnational obesity transitions, and therefore perhaps similar NCD transitions also, forcing us to rethink the existing nationalised assumptions. Each state is a sovereign territory in this transition—some still trapped in undernutrition’s grip, others already caught in obesity’s undertow. Thus, the path to its mitigation lies in accommodating these varied transition dynamics and abandoning one-size-fits-all solution for this vast, diverse country. The emergence of this clear message with important policy implications from our study was only possible because we examined the state-wise economic gradient of obesity. To the best of our knowledge, until now, only one study has estimated state-wise gradients and their changes over time. But the two time points they chose were 2016 (NFHS-4) and 2021 (NFHS-5), only 5 years apart, and therefore they could observe only ‘a mixed pattern’ as economic gradients of obesity are likely to show shorter-term random fluctuations.39 On the other hand, our study has been able to elicit longer-term stable trends which can have larger policy implications.

Equity and policy implications

Lastly, coming to the equity debate which has inspired us to write this article, Corsi and Subramanian and others recommended the Indian government should not focus too much on obesity and its downstream non-communicable health problems yet.24 This is because, despite the early signs of transition and reversal, obesity and the risk factors of NCD are still more common in the rich Indians, and the authors emphasise that the rich can afford more healthcare than their poorer counterparts through private insurance or out-of-pocket expenditure, which is evident from their larger longevity gains over the past few decades.24 Mobilising public resources for obesity and its allied outcomes may be inequitable, they argue, especially when the poverty-related diseases still remain an unfinished business of the country. Whereas the opposition to this thesis argues that the growing burden of obesity and NCDs needs immediate attention from the government before higher rates of these chronic degenerative conditions become entrenched in higher numbers in the poorer sections, which they claim will be highly inequitable.25

Our results partially align with the views of Corsi and Subramanian, because we have not noticed any reversal of the economic gradient of obesity in any Indian state, but rather noted further exacerbation of these gradients in the poorer states.24 But moving away from Corsi and Subramanian, we foresee Kerala may witness a ‘reversal’ soon. Moreover, all states, especially the richer ones like Tamil Nadu and Punjab, harbour a sizeable poor yet obese population in absolute terms, who would need help from the welfare state for mitigation of their NCDs and allied problems. Therefore, more public investment is definitely needed in neutralising the threats of the rapidly growing burden of obesity and NCDs in India. Otherwise, their increase will continue unabated, and the worst consequences of all, we may also witness a reversal of the gradient, tilting their burden towards the poor, as the high-income countries observed a few decades ago. Since time immemorial, the poor in our country have borne the disproportionate brunt of communicable diseases. So, it would be unfair to let them carry the burden of NCDs too, simply because we failed to act on time, even though the warning signs and experiences from other regions were clearly in front of our eyes. But, given the current scenario of obesity and NCDs in India, they should be addressed mainly through good quality, cost-effective, universal primary care comprising preventive, promotive and early-stage therapeutic services; and it will also be equitable because public health system-based primary care is more likely to be utilised by the less affluent.50 51 Therefore, the ongoing National Programme for NCDs in India, which stresses delivering screening and primary care for NCDs and their risk factors through peripheral Sub Health-Centres (rebranded as Ayushman Arogya Mandir), is currently well suited for the Indian situation. But perhaps it needs further strengthening in states with weaker public health systems.52 Though, if we jump the ‘proverbial’ gun and act too hastily, such as providing public insurance/assurance-based universal coverage for expensive tertiary care for advanced stages of NCDs—especially when purchased from private-sector hospitals—that can lead to disproportionate allocation of public resources for the wealthy, because obesity and NCDs are still concentrated among the affluent in India and its states.19 And also, this could mean diversion of scarce resources and focus from diseases of poverty, especially in the poorer states, where the poor majority are still grappling with a considerable burden of these ailments.19 20 However, even in such low-resource settings, the policy focus should not ignore the emerging NCDs and their risk factors like obesity. Thus, the resultant divergent state-level obesity gradients imply that policy decisions on NCD investment should prioritise preventative primary care for all to promote equitable delegation of the public resources. Therefore, we suggest that the transition of the obesity situation in India and its reversal—if it unfortunately occurs at all in future—should guide changes in policy responses to the obesity and NCD burden of the country.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its use of nationally representative data from multiple rounds of the NFHS, allowing for robust comparisons over time and across states. Second, the use of the SII as a metric of the economic gradient of obesity is a major strength of this analysis. SII is a stable and weighted marker of inequality and is independent of whether one uses national or state-specific wealth quintiles to estimate it. Also, SII measures inequality in absolute scale (difference in prevalence of obesity between two extreme groups), which is easy to understand, track, act on and evaluate. It is also more appropriate for exposure variables whose distribution does not change over time, such as the exposure in our analysis, which is state-level wealth quintile, the distribution of which stays constant over time.53 On the contrary, relative measures like the Relative Index of Inequality or Concentration Index can overstate inequality in poorer low-obesity states because of the low base effect.53

However, this study is not without its limitations. To start with, first, the reliance on NFHS data, while helpful for its scale and representativeness, may be subject to measurement error. Nonetheless, given the standardised data collection methods employed across survey rounds, any such error is likely to be random and non-differential rather than systematic; therefore, it is unlikely to bias our inferences seriously. Second, we did not examine the educational gradient of obesity. In the Indian context, education does not closely track wealth, as high rates of unemployment and underemployment still persist among the educated population.54 Third, this study excludes the North-Eastern states, Union Territories and the National Capital Region of Delhi, which limits the national generalisability of our findings to some extent. However, this exclusion was deliberate and grounded in methodological necessity. These excluded regions follow distinct fiscal frameworks, receiving additional central assistance and special grants that substantially inflate their per-capita GSDP. Because GSDP served as the principal state-level indicator of prosperity in our study, therefore, retaining these units would have introduced potential misclassification. Lastly, as commonly encountered in large population surveys, there may be some measurement errors in NFHS data as well, but mostly they are non-differentially and randomly distributed, thus unlikely to introduce any significant bias in the study results.

Conclusions

To conclude, India has grown economically over the past few decades, but the growth has been heterogeneous across different states. Consequently, obesity and its ensuing downstream NCDs have also grown in every state between 2006 and 2021, but at different paces, commensurate with their economic position and growth: ‘richer the state, greater is the burden of obesity’. Although the national and all the state-specific economic gradients of obesity remained positive during the same period, marking a continuing concentration of greater obesity among the affluent, the economic gradient of obesity has also evolved differentially across states. The poorer states have witnessed intensification of the economic gradient, implying further widening of the rich-poor gap, whereas it has somewhat weakened in the richer states, bringing the affluent and non-affluent closer in terms of their obesity burden. The latter phenomenon is an early sign of reversal of the gradient, which we refer to as the ‘green shoots of reversal’, because, as per the hypothesis of obesity transition, the gradient is expected to flatten or reverse as a state climbs the prosperity ladder. Therefore, the rising obesity and its downstream NCD burden should be addressed primarily through sustained investment in cost-effective, universal primary healthcare initiatives that focus on prevention, health promotion and early-stage management—approaches most likely to benefit the poor and yield equitable population-level impact. At the same time, as India’s epidemiological transition advances, building progressive pathways for affordable and equitable access to tertiary care, particularly for the most vulnerable, should remain a long-term policy goal. Such a phased approach—anchoring immediate action in primary care while planning for inclusive tertiary services—can prevent premature diversion of resources from the unfinished business of diseases of poverty and ensure that both preventive and curative responses for NCDs evolve in a balanced and equitable manner.
