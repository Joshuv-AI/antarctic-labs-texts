Buccal mucosal graft interposition for recurrent ileal conduit stomal stenosis: A case report
Authors: Abraham Jaafar, Patrick Etta, Simon Wilder, Nabeel Shakir, Marcus L Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecurrent ileal conduit stomal stenosis is challenging, particularly in patients with prior abdominal surgery and poor local tissue quality. We present a 43-year-old female with recurrent stenosis after ileal conduit urinary diversion and prior stomal revision. A 4 × 5 cm buccal mucosal graft was applied as a segmented onlay at the mucocutaneous junction following circumferential stomal mobilization and adipocutaneous flap advancement. Despite partial wound dehiscence, the graft remained viable postoperatively. At 6-month follow-up, the stoma was healthy, well-everted, and accommodated a 14 Fr catheter. This technique may offer an alternative for select patients with recurrent ileal conduit stomal stenosis.Keywords: Ileal conduit, Stomal stenosis, Buccal mucosal graft, Stomal revision

Introduction

Ileal conduit urinary diversion is a well-established reconstructive option following cystectomy and in select patients with complex lower urinary tract pathology. Although durable, ileal conduits are associated with stomal complications including parastomal hernia, retraction, and stenosis. In a series of patients undergoing cystectomy with ileal conduit urinary diversion, Kouba et al. reported stomal complications in nearly 15% of patients.1 Wood et al. similarly demonstrated that stomal complications can be particularly problematic in women undergoing ileal conduit diversion for intractable urinary incontinence, reporting an overall stomal complication rate of 34.4%.2 These complications may lead to impaired drainage, leakage, frequent appliance changes, peristomal skin breakdown, and decreased quality of life. Stomal stenosis in particular typically results from cicatricial contracture at the mucocutaneous junction, compounded by ischemia, tension, and peristomal skin irritation.

Management of recurrent stomal stenosis is particularly challenging in patients with prior abdominal surgery, elevated body mass index, large ventral hernias, prior bowel injury, or multiple prior abdominal operations. Traditional revision techniques often require conduit mobilization and stoma recreation. However, repeat intra-abdominal surgery may be undesirable in patients with dense adhesions or prior difficult conduit mobilization.

Buccal mucosal grafts are widely used in reconstructive urology due to their thick epithelium, favorable graft characteristics, resistance to infection, and ability to function in moist environments.3 Their use has been described in urethral reconstruction, ureteral reconstruction, genital resurfacing, and repair of stenotic catheterizable continent stomas.3, 4, 5, 6 However, their application in ileal conduit stomal revision remains poorly described.

We present a case of recurrent ileal conduit stomal stenosis managed with buccal mucosal graft interposition and local flap reconstruction to avoid repeat intraperitoneal surgery.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old female with a complex surgical and urologic history initially presented with a large vesicovaginal fistula and bladder stone secondary to eroded mesh placed at an outside hospital for pelvic organ prolapse. She initially underwent robot-assisted vesicovaginal fistula repair with peritoneal advancement flap but developed recurrence. After extensive discussion, she elected to proceed with ileal conduit urinary diversion because of compromised bladder function and prior failed repairs.

Her surgical history was notable for at least six prior abdominal and pelvic operations, including prior anterior abdominal wall reconstruction with mesh, recurrent ventral hernia repair, abdominal hysterectomy, cholecystectomy, appendectomy, and mesh-related pelvic surgery. Given the location of the hernia and the need for synchronous hernia repair, a left-sided ileal conduit was selected. Intraoperatively, a short mesentery and increased skin-to-fascial distance made stoma maturation technically challenging.

Although she initially recovered well, she developed stomal stenosis approximately five months after ileal conduit creation. She subsequently underwent revision with conduit mobilization and stomal rematuration approximately seven months after the initial diversion. Despite early improvement, she developed recurrent stenosis approximately 10 weeks later.

Given her extensive prior abdominal surgery, large ventral hernia, elevated body mass index, and prior difficult conduit mobilization, repeat intra-abdominal surgery was considered high risk. A decision was therefore made to proceed with local tissue rearrangement and buccal mucosal graft interposition.

Preoperative and clinical photographs demonstrated a large abdominal pannus and ventral hernia, which contributed to the technical complexity of stoma maturation and revision. Intraoperative photographs demonstrated the retracted stenotic ileal conduit stoma, local adipocutaneous flap mobilization, and buccal mucosal graft interposition at the mucocutaneous junction (Fig. 1).

Flexible endoscopy of the ileal conduit confirmed a healthy conduit with a narrowed mucocutaneous junction.

To aid in stomal rematuration, the surrounding cicatrix was incised circumferentially around the stenotic stoma. Deep abdominal wall fat was then excised for approximately 5 cm circumferentially down to the level of the rectus fascia. Four broad-based adipocutaneous flaps, each measuring 7 × 5 cm (140 cm2 total), were raised in the four quadrants surrounding the stoma. The overlying skin was incised in a 7 × 5 cm elliptical pattern for each flap to allow rotation around the rematured stoma while preserving adequate subcutaneous tissue and fat with each skin paddle (Fig. 2).

Technical schematic of four-quadrant adipocutaneous rotational flap advancement and buccal mucosal graft interposition. Following circumferential cicatrix release and peristomal fat excision, four 7 × 5 cm adipocutaneous flaps are rotated around the rematured stoma. Segmented buccal mucosa is quilted to bowel serosa and exposed subcutaneous tissue of the rotational flaps, with graft edges sutured to the rosebudded conduit mucosa and skin edges of the flaps.

A buccal mucosal graft measuring approximately 4 × 5 cm was harvested from the left cheek, prepared, and divided into multiple segments. The stoma was then re-everted using interrupted absorbable sutures.

The buccal mucosal graft was onlaid to augment the rematured stoma, with the mobilized adipocutaneous flaps serving as the vascularized graft bed. The graft was quilted to the bowel serosa and exposed subcutaneous tissue of the rotational flaps to secure it to its bed and promote graft take. Its edges were sutured to the rosebudded bowel mucosa on one side and to the skin edges of the rotational flaps on the other, creating an epithelialized interface between conduit mucosa and peristomal skin. Tie-over bolsters were applied, and a 16 Fr Foley catheter was placed within the stoma at the conclusion of the procedure. The patient tolerated the procedure well without intraoperative complications.

Approximately two weeks postoperatively, the patient presented to an outside hospital with concern for wound infection and was subsequently transferred to our institution. Her postoperative course was notable for partial wound dehiscence requiring bedside debridement and prolonged local wound care. Despite this complication, the buccal mucosal graft remained viable with excellent overall graft take.

Serial postoperative photographs demonstrated partial wound dehiscence with preservation of graft viability, followed by progressive wound healing and near-circumferential buccal mucosal graft take at follow-up (Fig. 1).

At approximately two months postoperatively, the patient was doing well clinically. The peristomal wound was nearly completely healed, with viable buccal mucosa in a near-circumferential distribution. The stoma remained patent on examination, with improved function and decreased appliance-related issues.

At approximately six months postoperatively, the stoma appeared healthy and well-everted on examination and accommodated passage of a 14 Fr catheter without difficulty. The patient did not require catheterization to maintain patency; the conduit drained continuously with low residual into the urostomy appliance, and catheter passage was performed solely as an office-based demonstration of patency. During an unrelated readmission for suspected pyelonephritis, flexible endoscopy of the ileal conduit using a 16.2 Fr flexible cystoscope revealed a patent stoma and viable ileal conduit.

Discussion

Recurrent stomal stenosis following ileal conduit urinary diversion represents a difficult clinical problem, particularly in patients with prior revisions and complex abdominal surgical histories. Stomal complications after ileal conduit urinary diversion are well described in the urologic literature. Kouba et al. reported stomal complications in nearly 15% of patients undergoing cystectomy with ileal conduit urinary diversion, while Wood et al. reported even higher rates in women undergoing ileal conduit diversion for intractable urinary incontinence.1,2

Stomal stenosis can lead to impaired conduit drainage, appliance leakage, recurrent skin irritation, and the need for catheterization or repeat surgical revision. In many cases, definitive revision may require conduit mobilization and stomal rematuration. However, this approach can be challenging in patients with dense adhesions, prior bowel injury, recurrent hernia, obesity, or multiple previous abdominal operations. In the present case, the patient's prior abdominal wall reconstructions, ventral hernia, elevated body mass index, and previous difficult conduit revision made repeat intraperitoneal surgery undesirable.

Buccal mucosal grafts are well established in reconstructive urology. Their thick epithelium, thin lamina propria, favorable handling characteristics, and ability to survive in moist environments make them useful graft material.3 Although most commonly associated with urethral reconstruction, buccal mucosa has also been applied in ureteral reconstruction and genital resurfacing procedures.3, 4, 5.

The closest prior analogue to the present case is the use of buccal mucosal grafts for repair of stenotic catheterizable continent stomas. Radojicic et al. described buccal mucosal graft repair for stenotic catheterizable continent stomas and reported no graft necrosis or repeat stenosis during follow-up.6 In that series, buccal mucosa was used to salvage stenotic continent catheterizable channels and avoid creation of a new channel.6 In contrast, our case involves an incontinent ileal conduit rather than a continent catheterizable channel.

In this case, the rotational adipocutaneous flaps provided a vascularized bed for buccal graft inset and structural support for stomal eversion. The graft bridged the circumferential defect between the rosebudded conduit mucosa and peristomal skin, thereby creating a durable epithelialized interface intended to interrupt cicatricial contracture at the mucocutaneous junction (Fig. 2).

This concept is further supported by reconstructive principles described in genital resurfacing. Beamer et al. described the “buccal belt” technique for recurrent penile adhesions, in which buccal mucosa is placed as a subcoronal resurfacing graft to function as a barrier between epithelial surfaces.5 Similarly, in the present case, buccal mucosa was used to create a durable epithelialized junction between the ileal conduit and peristomal skin. This approach was intended to interrupt the cycle of inflammation, cicatricial contracture, and recurrent narrowing at the mucocutaneous interface.

The success of this approach may relate to several favorable reconstructive properties of buccal mucosa. Buccal mucosa provides a durable, non-keratinized epithelial surface that is well suited to chronic moisture exposure and relatively resistant to inflammation and contracture. In the present case, graft interposition at the mucocutaneous junction may have limited opposing scar formation between the conduit and peristomal skin while promoting a more stable epithelialized surface.

At six-month follow-up, the stoma remained clinically patent and drained continuously into the urostomy appliance without need for catheterization. Passage of a 14 Fr catheter was performed only to demonstrate patency. Flexible endoscopy of the ileal conduit during an unrelated readmission for suspected pyelonephritis also demonstrated a patent stoma and viable ileal conduit.

This case has several limitations. First, it represents a single-case experience, limiting the ability to draw broad conclusions regarding safety, reproducibility, or comparative effectiveness. Second, although 6-month follow-up demonstrated excellent clinical appearance and objective patency, longer follow-up is still needed to assess durability and risk of recurrent stenosis. Finally, generalizability is unknown, as this technique may be most applicable to select patients with recurrent stenosis, prior difficult abdominal surgery, and adequate local tissue for flap advancement.

This case suggests that buccal mucosal graft interposition may represent a useful option for select patients with recurrent ileal conduit stomal stenosis, particularly when prior abdominal surgery makes repeat intraperitoneal revision high risk.

Conclusion

Buccal mucosal graft interposition combined with local flap reconstruction may provide an effective alternative for the management of recurrent ileal conduit stomal stenosis. This approach avoids repeat intraperitoneal surgery and directly targets cicatricial narrowing at the mucocutaneous junction. At 6-month follow-up, the stoma remained clinically patent. Longer follow-up is needed to confirm durability.
