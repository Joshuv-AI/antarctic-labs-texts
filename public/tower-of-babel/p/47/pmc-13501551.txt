Longitudinal association between sarcopenia and comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment and depression among older adults in China: the moderating effect of non-obesity
Authors: Yilin Zhou, Yichen Cao, Hao Chan, Xianguo Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and depression could cause great distress in the lives of older adults and frequently co-occur, yet their interrelationships remain elusive. This study seeks to assess the link between sarcopenia and the comorbid MCI and depression in older Chinese, and their longitudinal impact.MethodsWe used data from the 2015 (wave 3) and 2018 (wave 4) surveys of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Cross-sectional analysis of the 2015 data estimated the baseline prevalence of sarcopenia and its comorbidity with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and depression. Longitudinal analysis of the 2015–2018 data employed multivariate logistic regression models, adjusted for demographic and health covariates, to examine the prospective association between sarcopenia and incident comorbidities; stratified analyses were additionally conducted among obese individuals. To illustrate the impact of sarcopenia on comorbidity onset, Cox proportional-hazards models were fitted to the 2011–2018 (waves 1–4) longitudinal data to estimate the association between sarcopenia and incident comorbidities.ResultsDuring the follow-up period from 2015 to 2018, among 2773 individuals, 190 developed comorbidities of mild cognitive impairment and depression. After adjusting for covariates, compared to non-sarcopenic individuals, those with possible sarcopenia (OR = 2.952, 95% CI: 2.104–4.141, P < 0.01) and sarcopenia (OR = 3.105, 95% CI: 2.064–4.670, P < 0.01) were associated with a higher incidence of comorbidity (p < 0.001). Furthermore, we observed that the incidence of comorbidity was 15.38% points lower in non-obese individuals with sarcopenia compared to those who were obese and had sarcopenia. Compared to the normal group, individuals with obesity who had possible sarcopenia exhibited a higher risk of developing comorbidities(OR = 2.411, 95% CI: 1.472–3.946, P < 0.001), and the sarcopenia group had an even higher risk (OR = 7.580, 95% CI: 2.578–22.289, P < 0.001) in the fully adjusted model accounting for age, gender, residential area, education level, marital status, former/current smoking, former/current alcohol use, other comorbidities. However, in the non-obese subgroup, neither possible sarcopenia group (OR = 1.875, 95% CI: 0.858–4.097, P > 0.05) nor sarcopenia group (OR = 1.222, 95% CI: 0.590–2.529, P > 0.05) showed a statistically significant association with the development of comorbidity.ConclusionThese findings suggest that sarcopenia increases the risk of comorbid MCI and depression in older adults; however, this association was only observed in obese individuals, with non-obesity status significantly attenuating the risk. These findings suggest that sarcopenia may represent a potential target for preventive strategies, particularly among obese older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07806-y.

Background

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) refers to a clinical condition characterized by cognitive decline that is greater than expected for an individual’s age and education level, but not severe enough to interfere significantly with daily functioning or meet the criteria for dementia [1]. Every year, 6–15% of people with MCI progress to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [2], posing a significant expensive burden on public health. Depression, the leading cause of disability worldwide [3, 4], is tightly inter-woven with this trajectory: meta-analytic evidence shows that older adults with major depressive disorder (MDD) are approximately twice as likely to develop MCI as non-depressed controls [5], and the severity of depressive symptoms predicts incident MCI independently of traditional risk factors [6]. Longitudinal studies further indicate that the combination of MCI and clinically significant depressive symptoms accelerates progression to dementia [7, 8], whereas effective treatment of depression in MCI slows cognitive decline and improves quality of life [9]. Recent analyses of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) have extended these findings by demonstrating that a multidimensional psychological frailty index–integrating depressive symptoms, subjective cognitive complaints, coping ability, and emotional instability–is significantly and linearly associated with incident stroke in a nationally representative sample of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years [10]. Parallel evidence from NHANES further links metabolic dysfunction to depressive symptoms, reinforcing the cross-cultural importance of depression surveillance in ageing cohorts [11]. This also suggests that obesity may exert an impact on both depression and cognitive impairment.

Sarcopenia was estimated to influence 10%-16% of older adults worldwide [12]. Sarcopenia has been recognized as an independent disease entity under the International Classification of Diseases-10 code (ICD-10) [13], for less than a decade. As an age-related condition, sarcopenia is primarily characterized by a progressive and accelerated decline in skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical performance. Accumulating evidence has established a strong correlation between sarcopenia and an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including falls [14],mild cognitive impairment [15], frailty [16], and reduced quality of life [17]. Furthermore, sarcopenia has been implicated in poor prognostic outcomes across a spectrum of diseases, such as malignancies [18], liver cirrhosis [19], Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease [20], Diabetes mellitus [21] and cardiovascular disorders [22].

Extensive research has elucidated the independent associations of sarcopenia with mild cognitive impairment and depression. Employing diverse methodologies, including meta-analyses [23], Mendelian randomization [24], and cohort studies [15], these studies have consistently established that sarcopenia significantly increases the risk of mild cognitive impairment [25]. Furthermore, in the context of comorbid conditions such as advanced cancer [26], diabetes [27], and other disease states, sarcopenia has been demonstrated to substantially elevate the prevalence of depression among older adults. For instance, depression has been shown to exacerbate sarcopenia risk through mechanisms such as elevated dietary inflammatory index [28] and disability in activities of daily living (ADL) [29]. However, the impact of sarcopenia on the comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment and depression remains largely unexplored. Although existing studies suggest that sarcopenia may be associated with mild cognitive impairment or depression, the interrelationship between these factors remains underexplored in elderly populations.

This study examines the link between sarcopenia and the co-occurrence of mild cognitive impairment and depression in older adults, aiming to inform health assessments and interventions. Identifying these connections allows for early prevention and mitigation strategies against cognitive and mood disorders in older adults. The research provides empirical evidence to support interdisciplinary efforts in understanding the complex relationships among sarcopenia, cognitive health, and mental well-being.

Methods

Data

This investigation employed data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a comprehensive national longitudinal survey targeting Chinese individuals aged 45 and above. The CHARLS dataset is accessible for download on the official CHARLS website. Initiated in 2011, the CHARLS national baseline survey encompassed 28 provinces, covering approximately 10,000 households and over 17,000 participants. Subsequent waves of data collection occur biennially or triennially, with the fifth wave completed in 2020. These datasets are instrumental in analyzing China’s aging population and fostering interdisciplinary research [30].

For this study, two sets of data were employed: the 2015 (Wave 3) and 2018 (Wave 4) surveys, as well as the longitudinal cohort spanning 2011–2018 (Waves 1–4). In the first set, individuals aged 60 years or older at the time of the 2015 survey were included. Participants with incomplete data regarding sarcopenia, mild cognitive impairment, or depression were excluded, resulting in a sample of 5,544 for the cross-sectional analysis. Furthermore, individuals who did not participate in the 2018 follow-up, those diagnosed with both depression and mild cognitive impairment in 2015, and those with missing data on mild cognitive impairment and depression in 2018 were excluded from the longitudinal analysis, yielding a final sample of 2773 participants. In the second dataset, we restricted the sample to respondents who (i) entered the study in 2011 and (ii) were aged ≥ 60 years at baseline. After excluding any participants with missing values on sarcopenia, depression, or mild cognitive impairment, 1 631 individuals remained for analysis(Fig. 1).

Measurement of cognitive function

We assessed cognitive function using the validated Health and Retirement Study (HRS) [31] cognitive battery, which evaluates four key domains: orientation to time, episodic memory (immediate and delayed word recall), numerical calculation, and visuospatial function (assessed via a figure-drawing task). A composite cognitive score was calculated as the sum of scores across these domains, ranging from 0 to 31, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance.

There is no consensus on the diagnostic criteria for Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). In our study, we defined MCI using Age-Associated Cognitive Decline (AACD), which is characterized by performance at least one standard deviation (SD) below the age-specific mean. Participants over 60 years old were grouped into every five-year age bracket. Those who met the AACD criteria within each age group were classified as having MCI [32].

Assessment of depression

Depression was assessed using the 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), a widely used and validated screening instrument for depressive symptoms in epidemiological and geriatric research. The CESD-10 was selected due to its brevity, ease of administration, and strong psychometric properties—particularly its demonstrated validity and reliability in older Chinese populations. A cut-off score of ≥ 10 is commonly used in community-based studies of older adults to identify individuals with clinically significant levels of depressive symptoms, as this threshold balances sensitivity and specificity for detecting probable depression. Participants with a CESD-10 score of ≥ 10 were categorized as exhibiting depressive symptoms, while those with a score < 10 were classified as non-depressed.

Assessment of sarcopenia

The assessment of sarcopenia is based on the criteria recommended by AWGS 2019 [33], which includes muscle strength, appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), and physical performance. Grip strength (unit: kg) was measured in both the dominant and non-dominant hands, with participants squeezing a YuejianTM WL-1000 dynamometer as hard as possible. Each participant was tested twice for both hands, and the average of the available maximum strength data was used. The cut-off point for low grip strength was defined as less than 28 kg for men and less than 18 kg for women. Weight was measured by an Omron TM HN-286 scale, and height was measured by a SecaTM213 height meter. If male, gender was set to 1, otherwise to 0. Based on previous studies, the cut-off for low muscle mass was based on the sex-specific lowest 20% of the height-adjusted muscle mass (ASM/height²) among the study population, which in this study was < 6.87 kg/m² for women and < 8.77 kg/m² for men. Physical performance included gait speed, the five-time chair stand test, and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB). For gait speed, each participant was asked to walk a 2.5-m distance at a normal pace two times (there and back). The time to complete was recorded to calculate the speed. The five-time chair stand test measures the time needed for participants to rise continuously five times from a 42-cm highchair while keeping their arms folded across their chest. In addition to the previous gait speed and five-time chair stand test, the SPPB assessment also included two-position tests, each lasting 10 s: (1) semi-tandem position (the heel of one foot beside the big toe of the other foot); (2) full tandem position (the heel of one foot in front of and touching the toes of the other foot). According to AWGS 2019 recommendations, low physical performance was defined as a gait speed of < 1.0 m/s, a five-time chair stand test of ≥ 12 s or failing one of the SPPB tests. Based on the screening methods provided by AWGS2019, participants with low grip strength or a five-time chair stand test of ≥ 12 s were classified into the possible sarcopenia group. Participants with low muscle mass, low muscle strength, or low physical performance were classified into the sarcopenia group. Therefore, all participants were divided into three groups: non-sarcopenia, possible sarcopenia, and sarcopenia. At the baseline assessment (wave3), 853 participants were diagnosed with sarcopenia, while 1466 were classified as having possible sarcopenia. In subsequent Kaplan-Meier survival analyses, participants with sarcopenia and those with possible sarcopenia were combined into a single group.

Assessment of obesity

General obesity is typically defined using Body Mass Index (BMI), while abdominal obesity is assessed via Waist Circumference (WC). In this study, we selected BMI and WC as our primary adiposity measures because they are widely accepted, non-invasive, cost-effective, and strongly associated with cardiometabolic risk in epidemiological research—particularly in large-scale population-based studies like ours. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m²). Waist circumference was measured at the level of the navel using a flexible tape measure. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m² or a waist circumference ≥ 85 cm in men or ≥ 80 cm in women [35, 36].

Covariates

According to prior knowledge, we also considered sociodemographic characteristics and health-related factors in our study (Fig. 2). Sociodemographic characteristics included age, gender, residential area (urban and rural), education (illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school/vocational high school, and junior college or above), and marital status (married, separated, and unmarried/divorced/ widowed). Health-related factors included ever/current smoke, ever/current alcohol, and 12 common comorbidities (cancer, chronic lung diseases, heart disease, stroke, arthritis, hypercholesterolemia, hepatic disease, kidney disease, digestive system disease, asthma, hypertension, and hyperglycemia).

Statistical analyses

For quantitative data that does not follow a normal distribution, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) is utilized for display. The Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test was used to examine differences between the three groups (non-sarcopenia, possible sarcopenia, and sarcopenia). Qualitative data is presented as percentages, and group differences are analyzed using the χ2 test.

In 2015, the χ2 test was employed to examine the cross-sectional relationship between various comorbidity patterns of comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment and depression and sarcopenia. Logistic regression models were used to investigate the connection between comorbidity and sarcopenia. Results were reported as Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We also used multiple logistic regression models to analyze the relationship between the occurrence of sarcopenia and comorbidity based on longitudinal data from 2015 to 2018, expressed as Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), as well as χ2 test to analyze differences in comorbidity incidence among different sarcopenic populations. We utilized logistic regression models with Odds Ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to investigate the influence of sarcopenia comorbidity in normal and obese populations. We used various covariate combinations in the two models. Model 1 only contains sarcopenia status, whereas Model 2 adds age, gender, residential location, education level, married status, obesity status, ever/current smoking, ever/current drinking, and a variety of comorbidities. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 25.0 software. All p values were two-sided, and the statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves provide a more intuitive representation of sarcopenia’s impact on comorbidities. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate sarcopenia’s interaction with comorbidities. Model1 assessed the hazard ratios for comorbidities across different sarcopenia statuses without adjusting for other confounders; Model2 evaluated whether distinct sarcopenia statuses independently associated with comorbidities after incorporating covariates.

Results

To visually illustrate the risk of comorbidities in the sarcopenia group, we performed Kaplan-Meier survival analyses using data from Waves 1–4 (2011–2018), and the results are presented in Supplementary Figure A. Kaplan - Meier survival curves provide a more intuitive representation of the risk of sarcopenia. As shown in the figure, the survival probability for individuals with sarcopenia declines more rapidly over time compared to those without sarcopenia, indicating a higher cumulative risk of developing comorbidities. The significant difference between the two curves is evident throughout the follow-up period, with a log-rank test yielding p < 0.0001, reinforcing the statistical significance of this association.

In the Cox proportional hazards model, sarcopenia was associated with a higher risk of comorbidities compared to the non-sarcopenia group (HR = 2.093, 95% CI 1.581–2.772, P < 0.001) in the unadjusted Model 1. After adjusting for potential confounders including age, sex, residence, education level, smoking, alcohol consumption, and chronic diseases in Model 2, the association remained statistically significant (HR = 1.519, 95% CI 1.126–2.049, P < 0.001), suggesting that sarcopenia independently contributes to an increased risk of comorbidities. These findings highlight the clinical importance of identifying and addressing sarcopenia as a modifiable risk factor in the prevention and management of multimorbidity. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study using data from Chinese individuals aged 60 and above to investigate the relationship between sarcopenia and the comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and depression, controlling for multiple confounders. We identified a significant positive correlation between sarcopenia and the comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment and depression. Furthermore, we discovered that non-obesity significantly modifies the sarcopenia-comorbidity association.

The mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of sarcopenia with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and depression remain unclear. However, existing studies have demonstrated an interaction between muscle and brain, with muscle secreting myokines, including Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [37], which play a role not only in communication between muscle and brain but may also affect systemic homeostasis [38, 39]. BDNF is one of the most studied neurotrophic factors, associated with neuronal maintenance, survival, plasticity, and neurotransmitter regulation. Patients with depression and neurodegenerative diseases [40] often have reduced BDNF concentrations in their blood and brain. Additionally, the muscle-secreted myokine cathepsin B can cross the blood-brain barrier, enhancing the production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [40], thereby promoting neurogenesis, memory, and learning. Exercise also increases the gene expression of FNDC5 in neurons (encoding PGC1α-dependent myokine FNDC5), which can similarly contribute to increased BDNF levels [41]. Therefore, we speculate that sarcopenia patients may experience muscle atrophy and functional decline that could affect the secretion of BDNF. Moreover, individuals with sarcopenia, due to the decline in physical function, may develop aversion to physical activities, thereby reducing physical activity and subsequently affecting BDNF secretion, increasing the risk of developing depression, MCI, and other diseases. Existing studies also mention that interventions targeting the muscle-brain axis, such as increasing physical activity, healthy diet, and vitamin supplementation, can prevent and slow the progression of sarcopenia, depression, and mild cognitive impairment [42].This study also conducted a subgroup analysis based on obesity. Adipose tissue is a well-established source of pro-inflammatory cytokines. We therefore hypothesize that sarcopenic obesity exerts a “double hit” on the muscle-brain axis: obesity-driven inflammation suppresses the skeletal-muscle secretion of neurotrophic myokines such as BDNF, while circulating inflammatory mediators directly impair neuronal function and plasticity. This dual insult may explain our subgroup finding that the absolute incidence of MCI-plus-depression comorbidity was 15.38% points higher in obese sarcopenic adults (9.62%) than in their non-obese counterparts (25.00%).

Consistent with our longitudinal findings, the fully adjusted model (Table 3) showed that sarcopenia remained significantly associated with the comorbid MCI and depression, whereas its links to “depression-only” or “cognitive-impairment-only” were attenuated. To the best of our knowledge, this selective association has rarely been reported. Most previous CHARLS-based studies modelled depression and MCI as separate binary outcomes (depressed vs. non-depressed; impaired vs. normal cognition). By creating three mutually exclusive endpoints, we demonstrate that the previously described sarcopenia-depression and sarcopenia-MCI relations are largely driven by participants who already manifest both conditions. Moreover, depressive symptoms can directly impair cognitive performance and lower quality of life [8]. We therefore speculate that the effect of sarcopenia on MCI may be partly mediated or moderated by depressive symptoms, and vice versa, which could explain why sarcopenia emerged as a significant predictor only for the comorbid, but not for the single-domain, outcomes in our data.

In the fully adjusted longitudinal analysis of the overall sample, sarcopenia was not significantly associated with incident mild cognitive impairment in the absence of depression. However, subgroup analyses by obesity status revealed a significant positive association among non-obese participants (OR = 1.907, 95% CI: 1.065–3.415, p < 0.05), while no such association was observed in obese individuals. This suggests that the relationship between sarcopenia and isolated cognitive decline may be modified by body weight status, with a stronger effect evident in non-obese older adults. We speculate that this may be due to the significant mediating effect of depression on the correlation between sarcopenia and mild cognitive impairment [43].

It is well known that obesity is often associated with metabolic-related diseases [44], including abnormal blood sugar, abnormal blood lipids, and high blood pressure, thereby increasing the risk of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [45]. The coexistence of sarcopenia and obesity is called sarcopenic obesity (SOB) [46], which means that the loss of muscle and the accumulation of fat may have a synergistic effect, thereby maximizing their health threat. Studies have shown that SOB can increase the risk of metabolic disorders and physical disability more than sarcopenia or obesity alone. In addition, obesity can lead to muscle function impairment and the development of sarcopenia through multiple mechanisms, such as inducing adipose tissue inflammation, muscle lipid metabolism disorder, insulin resistance, and chronic low-grade inflammation [47, 48]. In addition, individuals with sarcopenia should pay more attention to weight management, healthy eating, and physical exercise. Therefore, clinicians should prioritize weight management alongside muscle-preserving strategies in older patients with sarcopenia. Tailored lifestyle interventions that combine strength training, caloric moderation (to reduce ectopic fat without exacerbating muscle loss), and mental health support may be especially beneficial for individuals with sarcopenic obesity.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is among the first longitudinal studies to examine the association between sarcopenia and the comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and depression specifically in elderly Chinese populations—a demographic that remains underrepresented in global research on the muscle-brain axis and geriatric syndromes. By adjusting for multiple confounding factors and leveraging longitudinal data, this study strengthens the evidence for a temporally plausible association between sarcopenia and the comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment and depression in this large, community-based cohort. Secondly, this study performed subgroup analyses based on obesity, offering valuable insights into sarcopenic obesity, and laying the groundwork for targeted interventions in high-risk subgroups. By adjusting for multiple confounding factors, this study aimed to examine the association between sarcopenia and the comorbidity of mild cognitive impairment and depression among elderly individuals in Chinese communities.

However, we must acknowledge that this study also has some limitations. First, the study population was limited to individuals within China, which restricts its generalizability. Second, the gait speed measurement in CHARLS was 2.5 m rather than the standard 6 m; but studies utilizing the CHARLS database for sarcopenia research have predominantly employed a 2.5-meter gait speed measurement [49]. The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) measurement includes three-position tests, but most samples lacked data for the third position (side-by-side standing with feet together), so this study did not consider it. Third, sarcopenia also occurs in some young people [50], but this study only focused on older adults and did not analyze the young. Forth, we defined MCI using the Age-Associated Cognitive Decline (AACD) criterion (≥ 1 SD below the age-specific mean). This approach may encompass individuals with more severe cognitive impairment, including dementia, and lacks clinical validation or repeated measurements, potentially inflating case heterogeneity and biasing the observed associations toward the null. Fifth, our analytic sample excluded participants with missing data on key variables (e.g., grip strength, gait speed, cognitive tests) or those with pre-existing comorbidities (such as dementia, stroke, or major depression) at baseline. While this exclusion enhances internal validity by reducing reverse causality, it may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness of the cohort—particularly if missingness or exclusions were systematically associated with health status, socioeconomic factors, or frailty. As a result, our findings may not fully reflect the risk profile of more vulnerable or complex older adults. Sixth, the follow-up period of three years may be insufficient to capture the full trajectory of cognitive decline, especially for individuals with slowly progressive forms of mild cognitive impairment. Longer-term longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the dynamic interplay between sarcopenia, obesity, and the development of neuropsychiatric comorbidities in aging populations. Additionally, this study did not consider whether treatments for sarcopenia would affect comorbidities [51]. Finally, we cannot explore its potential biological mechanisms and further experimental studies are needed to prove this association.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that sarcopenia significantly increases the risk of incident comorbid MCI and depression in older adults. Notably, our findings reveal that non-obesity status modifies this association: the significant association between sarcopenia and comorbidity observed in the overall population appeared to be primarily driven by the obese subgroup, whereas no significant association was found in non-obese individuals. This suggests that the excess risk conferred by sarcopenia is substantially attenuated in the absence of obesity. These results highlight the importance of obesity status in stratifying sarcopenia-related neuropsychiatric risk and suggest that combined management of sarcopenia and obesity may be warranted for optimal prevention of comorbid MCI and depression.
