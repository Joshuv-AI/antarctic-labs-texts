A functional Schneiderian membrane-preserving maxillectomy: leveraging mucosal bone-forming potential and clinical effectiveness verification
Authors: Wenzhao Guan, Weiran Liu, Shuling Rong, Jianxing Han, Yufeng Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to propose an improved maxillary resection technique preserving Schneider’s membrane and evaluate its clinical value in postoperative bone regeneration, complication control, and functional recovery.Materials and methodsThis study included 17 patients who were randomly divided into a modified group (with Schneiderian membrane preserved, n = 8) and a traditional group (n = 9). We compared the two groups on bone regeneration in the maxillary sinus floor after surgery, complication rates (oronasal fistula and empty nose syndrome), and functional recovery.ResultsAfter surgery, clear bone regeneration was seen in all patients in the modified group (bone height 1.89 ± 0.16 mm, volume 614.30 ± 58.73 mm³). The traditional group showed no effective bone regeneration (p < 0.001). None of the patients in the modified group developed oronasal fistula or empty nose syndrome. The modified group also had significantly better outcomes than the traditional group in postoperative bite force (180.63 ± 16.23 N vs. 92.22 ± 29.75 N, p = 1.8 × 10⁻⁶), swallowing function grade(2.75 ± 0.46 vs. 2.11 ± 0.33,p = 0.003) and total UW-QOL score (77.75 ± 5.56 vs. 66.11 ± 6.48,p = 0.001).ConclusionsThis method reduces complication rates and improves functional recovery and quality of life.Clinical relevanceThe modified technique leverages the bone-forming potential of the mucosa, enabling immediate closure of the oral-nasal communication and promoting gradual bone regeneration. This approach represents a conceptual shift from simple lesion removal toward functional and structural reconstruction, offering new possibilities for function preservation and structural restoration after maxillary tumor resection. Furthermore, given the small sample size of this study, subsequent validation in larger prospective studies could further facilitate its broader clinical application.Trial registrationTrial was retrospectively registered at clinicaltrials.gov [NCT07320417/2025-12-22].Keywords: Schneiderian membrane, Maxillectomy, Osteogenesis, Functional preservation

Introduction

The maxilla is a key part of the facial skeleton. It has a complex three-dimensional structure with multiple processes and cavities. Maxillectomy is a common procedure used to treat maxillary diseases, including benign and malignant tumors, trauma, and congenital deformities [1]. Because the maxilla has a complex structure and is involved in key functions like facial support, breathing, chewing, and clinical procedures (such as dental implants and sinus treatments), its removal can affect a patient’s appearance and function and may lead to serious postoperative problems [2, 3]. However, Traditional approaches often leave a large defect in the maxilla after tumor removal. This can lead to severe postoperative complications, such as oronasal fistulas caused by the breakdown of the barrier between the mouth and nose, empty nose syndrome from nasal collapse, and facial sinking. These issues can severely affect patients’ quality of life and physical and mental health [4].

After maxillary tumor removal, besides the possible complications, repairing the bone defect is also a major challenge for patients. Maxillofacial repair, such as dental implants or bone reconstruction, is difficult to perform [5]. Using various vascularized bone-muscle composite flaps can better close off the mouth and nose and restore facial appearance. But, this approach involves more surgical trauma and higher risks. It also demands greater skill from the surgeon and better physical condition from the patient [6, 7]. Therefore, it is very meaningful to create a good seal between the mouth and nose and to establish favorable surgical conditions for the patient during the initial maxillectomy.

The Schneiderian membrane, which is the lining of the maxillary sinus, closely covers the inner wall of the sinus. Traditional maxillectomy procedures often do not pay enough attention to preserving this membrane. Removing the maxilla also destroys the integrity of the Schneiderian membrane. This leads to loss of sinus function, affects nasal airflow and mucus clearance, and increases the risk of postoperative problems [8]. Multiple studies have shown that the Schneiderian membrane has the potential to form bone on its own. It can obtain mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which can turn into osteoprogenitor cells, from sources like the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of the mucoperiosteal epithelium, the rich blood vessels in the lamina propria and the mucoperiosteum itself. With certain outside stimulation, the osteoprogenitor cells inside the membrane are activated. They mature and differentiate within the fibrin structure attached to the blood clot under the periosteum, forming the center of bone formation.

This study introduces and systematically evaluates a new type of maxillectomy—a functional surgery that keeps the Schneiderian membrane in place. Unlike traditional methods, this new approach makes a window in the outer wall of the maxillary sinus before removal. The membrane is carefully separated and kept completely intact. This allows for immediate closure between the mouth and nasal cavity after surgery and helps rebuild the bone defect biologically. To test the clinical value of this method, we carried out a single-center study with 17 patients. We looked at how it compared to traditional surgery in terms of bone regrowth in the maxillary sinus floor, control of complications, recovery of oral function, and quality of life. This work not only reports a new surgical technique but also offers a new way of thinking and a practical clinical approach for preserving function and rebuilding structure after maxillectomy.

Study subjects and methods

Ethics and consent to participate declarations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (Approval [2025]YX No.175). It was also registered on the ClinicalTrials.gov platform (No. NCT07320417). All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Inclusion criteria were as follows:

Diagnosed with maxillary benign tumors (ameloblastoma, ossifying fibroma, localized sequestrum formation due to maxillary osteomyelitis) or malignant tumors (gingival squamous cell carcinoma staged as T1 or T2, staging criteria according to AJCC 8th edition), confirmed by imaging (CT) and pathology, requiring maxillectomy.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: Lesions involving the maxillary sinus mucosa; presence of septa or severe inflammation in the maxillary sinus; previous radiotherapy to the head and neck; immune system diseases or metabolic bone diseases; pregnant or breastfeeding women; mental illness or inability to comply with follow-up; T3 or T4 stage gingival squamous cell carcinoma (AJCC 8th edition). Cases with cortical bone involvement or suspected invasion of the sinus wall (which would upstage the tumor to cT4a according to AJCC 8th edition) were excluded.

Samples and grouping

This was a single-center cohort study aimed at comparing the clinical outcomes of modified maxillectomy with preservation of the Schneiderian membrane and traditional maxillectomy. The study was designed and carried out by the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University from June 2024 to December 2025. Patients were randomly divided into a modified surgery group and a traditional surgery group. The design and implementation process is shown in Fig. 1.

Surgical procedures

The surgical steps for the modified surgery group were as follows:

Preoperative imaging evaluation was performed to observe the condition of the maxillary sinus bone wall. CT scans were used to record the condition of the maxillary sinus floor bone in the coronal and sagittal planes, based on which the surgical plan was developed (Fig. 2).

Surgical diagram for the modified surgery group

For the traditional surgery group, the first and second steps were the same as those in the modified group. After that, the affected bone and the overlying maxillary sinus mucosa were directly removed. Oroantral fistulas were repaired using local tissue flaps or free flaps, and the cavity was packed with iodoform gauze.

Preoperative and postoperative management

A stepwise antibiotic strategy was used for patients in both groups. This involved a routine preventive intravenous dose of a second-generation cephalosporin 30 min before surgery, with an additional single dose given every 3 h during the procedure. Antibiotics were continued for 5 to 7 days after surgery and were adjusted based on the results of drainage fluid culture and sensitivity tests. After surgery, patients were instructed not to blow their nose forcefully.

Postoperative follow-up and evaluation indicators

Patients were followed up at 1, 6, and 12 months after surgery. Assessments included wound healing, signs of infection, and imaging evaluations to check for tumor recurrence in the surgical area and bone formation at the maxillary sinus floor. CT scans were used for these imaging evaluations, with the first molar on the opposite side of the surgical area used as an anatomical reference point for comparing preoperative and postoperative conditions. Bone regeneration (bone density, volume, and continuity) was assessed using the Planmeca ProMax® 3D tool. This included measurements of the coronal and sagittal length and height of the newly formed maxillary sinus floor bone.

In addition, patients were followed up at 1 year after surgery to assess for complications such as oronasal fistula and empty nose syndrome. The type of repair chosen by the patient was recorded, and the preservation of oral function was evaluated. Maximum bite force of the prosthesis was measured using an electronic bite force meter and pressure-sensitive film. For masticatory efficiency, because patients with prostheses often have difficulty with the peanut sieve test due to base adhesion, a two-color gum (Hue-Check Gum) color release test was used. Patients chewed the gum 50 times, and the degree of color mixing was assessed using a color scale (1 to 10, with 10 indicating complete mixing). The proportion of chewing on the healthy side was also recorded to evaluate compensatory function. All values were measured three times under the same conditions, and the average was calculated.

A clinical swallowing evaluation (CSE) was performed on patients, including the water swallow test and tests with foods of different textures. Patients were seated and asked to drink 3 ml, 5 ml, and 10 ml of warm water in separate trials to check for coughing or delayed swallowing, which may indicate a risk of aspiration. Food samples with different textures (water, thick liquid, pudding, soft food, and solid food) were then given in order to observe chewing, bolus formation, and laryngeal elevation. Swallowing sounds beside the thyroid cartilage were auscultated to assess clarity and completeness. A comprehensive grading was made based on coughing frequency, swallowing delay time, and decreased blood oxygen saturation. The Frenchay Dysarthria Assessment was used to test the clarity of dental sounds (such as /t/ and /d/) and palatal sounds (such as /s/ and /z/) to evaluate speech function and dysarthria.

To assess quality of life in patients after partial maxillectomy, the University of Washington Head and Neck Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (UW-QOL) was used. This scale is concise, specific to head and neck cancer, and covers key areas including psychological state and social function.

Quality control

All surgeries were performed by the same surgical team from our department. All surgical data were collected and analyzed by two other oral and maxillofacial surgeons.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis for this study was performed using the SPSSAU platform. Measurement data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were made using the independent samples t-test. Data not following a normal distribution or ranked data were expressed as median (interquartile range) or frequency, and comparisons between groups were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. Count data were expressed as number of cases (percentage), and comparisons between groups were made using Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bone formation at the maxillary sinus floor

Imaging evaluations at 6 months after surgery showed significant differences in bone regeneration at the maxillary sinus floor between the two patient groups (Table 2). In the modified group, clear bone regeneration was observed in all patients. The mean bone regeneration height was 1.89 ± 0.16 mm, the mean bone regeneration volume was 614.30 ± 58.73 mm³, and the mean bone continuity score was 3.38 ± 0.52. In contrast, no effective bone regeneration was observed in any patient in the traditional group, and all measurements were significantly lower than those in the modified group (P < 0.001).

Bone density analysis (Fig. 6) showed no statistically significant difference in preoperative bone density at the maxillary sinus floor between the two groups (P > 0.05). After surgery, the mean Hounsfield unit (HU) value at the maxillary sinus floor in the modified group (472.25 ± 27.10) was significantly higher than that in the traditional group (72.56 ± 13.85, P < 0.001). Furthermore, the decrease in bone density (ΔHU: -170.25 ± 18.15) in the modified group was much smaller than that in the traditional group (ΔHU: -560.00 ± 34.68, P < 0.001). These results suggest that effective bone regeneration occurred after the modified surgical procedure.

To further compare the overall differences between the groups, a radar chart displayed the standardized percentages for 11 key indicators (Fig. 7C). The modified group performed well on multiple functional measures, including peak bite force, normal rate on the multi-texture food test, low aspiration risk rate, good prosthesis fit rate, and good dysarthria assessment rate. The mean peak bite force in the modified group (180.63 ± 16.23 N) was significantly higher than that in the traditional group (92.22 ± 29.75 N, P = 1.8 × 10⁻⁶). Furthermore, the modified group had a significantly better bone continuity score (75.1%), which reflects structural repair potential, compared to the traditional group (19.8%). These findings suggest that the modified surgical technique offers comprehensive advantages in terms of functional preservation and structural support.

Postoperative quality of life

Assessment using the University of Washington Head and Neck Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire (UW-QOL) showed that the total postoperative quality of life score for patients in the modified group (77.75 ± 5.56) was significantly higher than that for patients in the traditional group (66.11 ± 6.48, p = 0.001). A raincloud plot clearly illustrated the distribution of scores in the two groups. It showed that the scores in the modified group were more concentrated and fell within a higher range, visually reflecting the overall advantage in quality of life (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

In this study, the main purpose of the new membrane-preserving technique proposed is not to provide bone support for dental implants. Instead, it aims to promote natural bone formation in the maxillary defect area by preserving the self-repair ability of the sinus membrane [10],thereby offering a function-oriented biological reconstruction strategy. Its key advantages include the immediate closure of oral-nasal communication after surgery, restoring the anatomical barrier between the oral and nasal cavities. At the same time, it preserves the intact Schneiderian membrane, maintains normal nasal airflow dynamics as well as warming and humidifying functions, and reduces the risk of empty nose syndrome. More importantly, this modified technique forms a continuous bony plate at the maxillary sinus floor, providing a rigid supporting base for the obturator prosthesis, reducing soft tissue settling, and improving the transmission of masticatory forces.

Compared to the traditional way of simply clearing out the sinus lining during maxillectomy, this method fits better with the modern surgical ideas of “organ preservation” and “function protection.” It shows respect for the body’s own healing ability and puts it to use. Keeping the sinus membrane during surgery helps maintain normal sinus function. It also uses the membrane’s bone-forming potential to support local tissue repair and add new bone naturally [11]. This leads to better closure after surgery, more stable structure, and good bone support. It can effectively prevent common problems seen after traditional maxillectomy, such as oronasal fistula and empty nose syndrome [12, 13].Also, compared to patients who had traditional surgery, those with this new method have a good seal between the mouth and nasal cavity. This reduces the need for a second operation and improves how well a prosthetic device stays in place. It greatly improves patients’ quality of life and dignity. At the same time, it allows for dental restoration later on, which helps ease their financial burden down the road [14].

The results of this study showed that patients in the modified surgery group had clear bone regrowth in the maxillary sinus floor after the operation. The height of new bone was 1.89 ± 0.16 mm, and the volume was 614.30 ± 58.73 mm³. Their bone continuity scores were also much higher than those in the traditional surgery group (3.38 ± 0.52 vs. 0.89 ± 0.78, P < 0.001). These findings not only confirm that the Schneiderian membrane plays a key role in local bone regrowth [15] but also suggest that this surgical method successfully creates a good environment for bone healing [16]. The Schneiderian membrane is rich in mesenchymal stem cells and blood vessels. Keeping it intact provides a natural support structure for the blood clot. Through processes like epithelial-mesenchymal transition, it helps bone-forming cells develop and lay down new bone material [17, 18]. In contrast, the traditional method removes the membrane, so the local source for bone formation is lost. Healing then mainly happens through scar tissue, making it hard to truly rebuild bone structure. In this study, the continuity of new bone in the modified group was significantly better than that in the traditional group. Although the height of this continuous bone plate is limited, its anatomical location at the floor of the maxillary sinus and its smooth and continuous shape are important for the stability of the obturator prosthesis. The continuous bone plate can serve as a rigid base for the obturator prosthesis, distribute chewing loads, and reduce the sinking of the prosthesis into the soft tissue, thereby improving the fit of the denture and the patient’s chewing efficiency. This point has clinical value that cannot be ignored in obturator prosthesis repair [19].

In the postoperative period, none of the patients in the modified surgery group developed an oronasal fistula or empty nose syndrome. In contrast, the rates in the traditional group were 44.4% and 55.6%, respectively. This big difference highlights how important the Schneiderian membrane is as a physical barrier between the nasal and oral cavities. Keeping the membrane intact not only closes off the surgical area but also helps maintain a moist environment in the nose and supports the cleaning function of the cilia. This effectively prevents problems caused by an open connection between the mouth and nose, such as repeat infections, speech issues, and discomfort when breathing. By keeping the nasal structure and membrane function intact, the modified method avoids abnormal airflow and drying of the nasal lining [20]. This gets to the root cause and lowers the risk of these two common and hard-to-manage complications.

The value of keeping the Schneiderian membrane is especially clear when looking at how it improves patients’ quality of life after surgery. It helps restore function and appearance, allowing patients to return more quickly to their normal daily life and work. This provides a stronger foundation for their continued recovery and long-term health, which matters a lot, especially for those expected to live for many years after treatment. The modified group scored higher on the University of Washington Quality of Life Questionnaire (UW-QOL) for head and neck cancer, with a total score of 77.75 ± 5.56 compared to 66.11 ± 6.48 in the traditional group (P < 0.05) [21, 22]. Improvements were seen in areas like pain, chewing, swallowing, and appearance. This reflects a shift in modern surgery from just treating the disease to also focusing on function and quality of life [23].It is worth noting that there is still room for improvement in the modified group for some social and psychological aspects, such as “confidence eating in public” and “satisfaction with speech.” This suggests that even when function is well restored, patients may still have psychological challenges related to their surgical experience. It shows that functional recovery should go hand in hand with psychological support. In the future, clinical work should include more guidance on postoperative recovery and psychological help to support patients’ complete well-being.

The surgical method presented in this study, which preserves the Schneiderian membrane, has two main benefits. On a technical level, it allows for careful separation and protection of the membrane. On a conceptual level, it marks a shift in thinking from just removing the disease to also rebuilding function. This method does three things in a single surgery: it removes the diseased tissue, closes off the structure, and helps guide bone regrowth. This reduces the need for additional surgeries, lowers medical costs, and shortens the overall recovery time [24, 25]. Making this surgery work well depends on careful planning before the operation, skilled surgical technique, and systematic care after the surgery.

Although the Schneiderian membrane was successfully preserved in all cases in this series, iatrogenic perforation may occasionally occur during the separation process, particularly in cases with anatomical variations or previous inflammation. Based on our clinical experience, we propose a graded management strategy. For small perforations (< 5 mm), direct closure with 6 − 0 absorbable sutures or coverage with a collagen membrane is recommended to achieve a watertight seal and prevent postoperative oronasal communication. For moderate perforations (5–10 mm), a pedicled buccal fat pad flap or a rotational flap from the adjacent local mucosa can be used to cover the defect. Large perforations (> 10 mm) or irreparable tears are rare but, if encountered, warrant abandonment of the membrane-preserving technique and conversion to conventional maxillectomy followed by soft tissue flap reconstruction.

Given the limited sample size of the current cohort, our next step will be to conduct larger-scale, multicenter prospective studies with extended follow-up (at least 3–5 years) to further validate the oncological safety and long-term efficacy of this modified technique. These studies will include more patients and longer observation periods, which will help confirm the long-term results, oncological safety, and stability of this membrane-preserving approach. Also, using digital surgery tools like 3D printing and surgical navigation could allow for more precise planning before surgery and more accurate membrane separation. This would make the procedure more predictable and safer [26]. Combining custom-made 3D-printed prosthetics or biological scaffolds with the preserved membrane could lead to a repair model where biological healing and structural support work together. This could open up new paths for rebuilding function in a way that fits each patient’s specific jaw and face defect [27].

Conclusion

This study introduced and tested a modified maxillectomy technique that preserves the Schneiderian membrane. By retaining the sinus lining and utilizing its osteogenic potential, this method facilitates natural bone healing of the maxillary defect. Compared with traditional methods, the modified technique demonstrated superior outcomes in bone regeneration, complication prevention, functional recovery, and quality of life. This work provides theoretical support for functional reconstruction after maxillectomy. Nevertheless, given the small sample size and limited follow-up duration of this study, further validation in larger prospective studies is needed to confirm its oncological safety and long-term efficacy. Overall, this study offers a feasible and practical option for clinical application, which holds promise for broader adoption in clinical practice.
