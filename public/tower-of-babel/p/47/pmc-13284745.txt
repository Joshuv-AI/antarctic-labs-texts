Mexican Teachers’ Cohort Cognitive Study: A pilot of home‐based assessments
Authors: Jocelyn Jaen, Adrian Cortés‐Valencia, Aaron Salinas Rodríguez, Betty Manrique‐Espinoza, Omar Yaxmehen Bello‐Chavolla, Brenda Martinez Tapia, Teresa Shamah Levy, Veronica Mundo‐Rosas, Ana Luisa Sosa Ortiz, Isaac Acosta Castillo, Liliana Gomez‐Flores‐Ramos, Melissa Lamar, David A Bennett, Francine Grodstein, Martin Lajous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONExpanding research on aging and cognition in low‐ and middle‐income countries is critical, yet logistically complex. Large, established cohorts, such as the Mexican Teachers’ Cohort (MTC; >100,000 women), pose unique opportunities for neurocognitive research in Latin America.METHODSWe evaluated the feasibility of home‐based cognitive assessments in 150 Mexico City MTC participants, equally distributed across ages 55 to 59, 60 to 64, and ≥65. Tests included immediate/delayed word list recall, forward/backward digit span, and semantic verbal fluency.RESULTSOver 3 weeks, 274 homes were approached; 186 participants (56%) were at the known addresses, and 153 (82%) participated. Participants (median age = 61; 53% college‐educated) demographically resembled non‐participants. Cognitive scores spanned a broad range, demonstrating no floor/ceiling effects (immediate recall: 3 to 10; delayed recall: 1 to 10; digits forward: 3 to 8; digits backwards: 0 to 6; semantic fluency: 6 to 38 animals).DISCUSSIONHome cognitive assessments may be feasible for large established cohorts in Latin America. These studies are promising for aging research.

INTRODUCTION

Most countries’ populations are aging rapidly. 1 The health impact of these demographic shifts can be particularly challenging for low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). 2 , 3 Estimates indicate that in less than a decade, four out of five older adults in the world will be in LMICs. 4 Since negative neurocognitive outcomes are most prevalent among older age groups, 5 better understanding of the epidemiology of cognitive health in LMICs is especially important.

An efficient approach to expanding research on aging is to leverage existing cohorts. Launched in 2006, the Mexican Teachers’ Cohort (MTC) is an ongoing, prospective cohort study of over 100,000 female teachers living in one of 12 of Mexico's 32 Ediciones Normalismo Extraordinario states. 6 The study was designed to investigate risk factors for chronic diseases. Information on risk factors has primarily been collected via self‐administered questionnaires sent to participants through the public school system. For a subset of 7000 participants, information was additionally collected via interviews conducted in healthcare facilities. More recently, healthcare utilization databases were used to obtain information on health outcomes. 7 Questionnaires are sent to the participants approximately every 4 to 6 years to collect updated information on reproductive factors, lifestyle, medical conditions, and social determinants of health. Although the educational attainment of the MTC participants is higher than average for women in Mexico, 8 the historical processes related to becoming a Mexican public‐school teacher promoted the hiring of individuals from rural areas, from indigenous backgrounds, or of lower socioeconomic status. 9 In consequence, there is reasonable diversity in sociodemographic factors in this otherwise specialized cohort.

Nearly two decades since its inception, the MTC now offers an important opportunity to study aging and neurocognitive outcomes in this Latin American region. Nonetheless, adding face‐to‐face cognitive assessments to a cohort that has been conducted largely by self‐administered questionnaires could be challenging. Thus, we conducted a pilot study to evaluate the feasibility of implementing a home‐based cognitive evaluation in women 55 years or older from the MTC and to explore the variability of cognitive performance in this population.

At MTC baseline (2006 to 2010), 115,275 women completed a self‐administered questionnaire to collect information on health and lifestyle. 6 The median age at baseline was 44 years. Follow‐up questionnaires were sent every 4 to 6 years, for a total of three follow‐up waves to date.

The pilot study of home‐based cognitive assessments was conducted on a short timeline with a limited budget, in a subgroup of women 55 years or older between January 1 and 27, 2023. For feasibility, we used a non‐probabilistic sample, focused on women (1) from Mexico City, (2) with home address in the district of Tlalpan or Xochimilco, (3) with a registered telephone number at baseline, and (4) who lived in neighborhoods with a higher density of MTC participants (at least four teachers within each neighborhood) based on the last updated addresses.

Given that this pilot aimed to assess the feasibility of home‐based interviews in the MTC and explore cognitive test score distributions in a well‐educated population, we selected our sample size based on operational feasibility, budget, and scientific “face value,” targeting 150 home interviews. We also aimed to have those interviews equally distributed within the following older age groups: 55 to 59 years, 60 to 64 years, and 65 years or older.

Cognitive assessment

We used a cognitive protocol previously implemented in the Mexican population 10 by members of our team within the multi‐site World Health Organization Study on global AGEing and adult health (WHO‐SAGE). 11 Designed for ease of implementation in lower‐income settings, 11 included the following tests: Verbal recall. Teachers were read a 10‐item word list in Spanish (brazo, cama, avión, perro, reloj, bicicleta, oreja, martillo, silla, gato) and asked to recall as many items as possible within 1 min. This was executed across three consecutive learning trials using the same word list in the same word order. Scoring for this immediate verbal recall corresponded to the number of words correctly recalled in the third trial only. To evaluate delayed verbal recall, teachers were asked to recall as many words as possible in 1 min from this same list at the end of the cognitive assessment (average time between immediate and delayed recall was approximately 15 min). The possible score range for both immediate and delayed recall was 0 to 10. Semantic verbal fluency. Teachers were asked to name as many animals as possible within 1 min; the total score reflected the number of unique animals named within the time limit. Digit span. Teachers were asked to repeat unique series of numbers either in the order read (Digit Span Forward, administered first to completion) or in reverse order (Digit Span Backward, administered after Digit Span Forward). For both Digit Span Forward and Backward, participants who failed to repeat a number series of a given length received a second trial in a different sequence. Successful recall on either attempt advanced them to the next, longer series. Two incorrect responses at the same span length led to the termination of the task. The score reflected the number of digits correctly recalled in the longest span that the teacher was able to repeat without error: Forward (range: 0 to 9) and Backward (range: 0 to 8, as the number of digits in each series was lower).

Other variables

Information about the general sociodemographic characteristics were already available in the cohort, such as speaking an indigenous language (themselves or parents), marital status (single/married/ divorced or widowed), and education level (elementary or middle school/ high school/completed university/any graduate studies).

Fieldwork

Fieldwork was conducted by two teams, each composed of two interviewer pairs, a supervisor, and a driver. Additionally, two general supervisors and a coordinator accompanied select visits to monitor test administration and evaluate the need for logistical adjustments. Interviewers and supervisors received training from experts in cognitive assessment. Each household was entered by a pair of interviewers, at least one of them a female, and a supervisor joined occasionally.

To contact participants, we first attempted notification via WhatsApp, widely used in Mexico, and by phone. Messages were sent to the last registered mobile number and calls placed to the most recently updated home phone; however, due to time constraints and outdated contact information, most teachers were not reached before the home visit. Field teams then visited the last registered address. If the teacher was not home, a flyer about the pilot (without identifying information) was left. Teams revisited households when in the vicinity. If a teacher was present, interviewers explained the study objectives and protocol, obtained written consent, and, upon receiving consent, conducted the cognitive assessment. At the end of the visit, teachers received a booklet on healthy cognitive aging, offered as educational material. For those declining participation, interviewers recorded any stated reasons for non‐participation. This study protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Public Health of Mexico (INSP).

From the field visits we were interested in several primary metrics: (1) percentage of correct addresses, defined as homes where we found MTC participants continued to live divided by the total number of addresses visited (our list of home addresses had not been updated for over a decade); (2) the proportion of participants at home at the time of the visit out of the correct addresses; (3) the reasons for non‐response of participants such as changes of address, death, or other; and (4) the participation rate, which is the proportion of participants agreeing to participate from those found at home at the time of the visit.

Statistical analysis

We used median, interquartile ranges (IQRs), and percentages, where appropriate, to describe characteristics of participants and to evaluate participation. For cognitive tests, we described the means, standard deviations (SD), and ranges of scores, including by age (55 to 59 years, 60 to 64 years, ≥65 years) and education (elementary or middle school, high school, university, or graduate). While we did not test the associations between age, education, and cognitive performance, we present distributions by demographic characteristics for qualitative evaluation, aiming to examine these factors with future larger samples. All analyses were conducted in the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 7.8.

RESULTS

Initially, 929 teachers had addresses meeting the pilot study criteria. Visits were planned to be equally distributed between Tlalpan and Xochimilco; however, during fieldwork, restricted access to apartment buildings, often for safety‐related reasons, made homes in Tlalpan less accessible. Thus, our efforts in the last week of the 3‐week pilot focused exclusively on Xochimilco. Ultimately, to achieve our goal of 150 interviews, we attempted to visit 274 addresses (final n = 153, or 56%). However, much of the 44% “non‐participation” was due to our inability to find home addresses or participants at home. Specifically, of the 274 homes we attempted to visit, we were not able to find 34 due to street or address issues (12%) and 18 due to the participant having moved (7%); further, an additional 33 women (12%) were not home when we arrived, and three were deceased (1%) (Figure 1). All told, approximately one‐third of participants could not be reached secondary to many issues that we anticipate will be resolved in a more comprehensive cognitive study with more time and resources.

Flow diagram of MTC participation in pilot of home‐based assessments.

Subsequently, of the 186 teachers reached at home at the time of the visit, 82% agreed to participate (n = 153), an objectively high participation rate. For the 33 teachers (18%) that opted to not participate in this study, the most common concerns raised were (1) personal safety, as the study required that at least two study staff enter homes; (2) retirement, as some participants no longer wanted to continue being part of the MTC after leaving their employment; and (3) perceived value, as some did not see any practical benefit of participating.

Among participants, the median age was 61 years (IQR: 59.0 to 65.0), 58% lived in Xochimilco, and 58% were married. Educational attainment ranged from elementary–middle school (17%) to high school (∼25%), with over half reporting university education and 3% postgraduate studies. Two percent reported that they or their parents spoke an indigenous language. By age group, the oldest participants were less often single (55 to 59 years: 21%; 60 to 64 years: 25%; ≥65 years: 12%). Both the youngest and oldest groups had a higher proportion with elementary–middle school education (55 to 59 years: 23%; 60 to 64 years: 6%; ≥65 years: 22%). Compared to participants, teachers who could not be located or declined participation qualitatively had a very slightly higher proportion of university‐level education (participants: 53%; unfound: 48%; declined: 45%) (Table 1).

We also examined the mean and overall distribution of scores on the cognitive tests (Figure 2). The mean score for immediate verbal recall was 7.6 (SD 1.4); scores ranged from 3 to 10 correctly recalled items. Thus, participants generally scored across much of the possible range, although results were somewhat skewed toward higher performance. For delayed recall, the mean score was 6.1 (S.D. ± 1.8); scores ranged from 1 to 10 and the distribution appeared fairly symmetrical. For the test of semantic verbal fluency, scores ranged from 6 to 38, and the mean was 23.1 unique animals named (SD ± 5.7); again, scores were fairly symmetrical. For Digits Forward, the mean score was 4.8 (SD ± 0.9), and scoring ranged from 3 to 8. Lastly, for Digits Backward, the mean was 3.4 (SD ± 1.8), with scores ranging from 0 to 6, and no participants scoring at the upper range.

DISCUSSION

In this pilot study to add home‐based cognitive assessments to women in the MTC, we met our goal of 150 teachers with complete cognitive assessments within the 3‐week period allotted for fieldwork. Importantly, although our sample was non‐probabilistic, we did find that women who had moved from their known home addresses, and women who refused had characteristics similar to those who participated, suggesting limited opportunity for bias within our sample here. Not only were women from the MTC amenable to cognitive testing, but their performance across the protocol represented a wide range of scores. Additionally, we learned several important lessons regarding study recruitment that will facilitate more efficient implementation of a future, larger study.

Among contacted women, participation reached 83%, showing the success of our pilot fieldwork despite the limited time and funds, outdated addresses, and initial home‐only contact. With expanded resources, we can anticipate higher participation. Importantly, participants and non‐participants had similar general characteristics, suggesting no major bias in forming a cognitive subcohort. We additionally learned that strategies to improve address information, and the dissemination of study information through different media (e.g., start date, staff identifiers such as descriptions of their standard uniforms, scientific utility of collected data), could help to mitigate the identified barriers to participation (e.g., perceived safety, scientific value of research). Thus, overall, our pilot work here demonstrates that our proposed addition of home‐based cognitive assessment is feasible in terms of conducting an ancillary study of cognitive health in aging among participants of this existing cohort.

In addition, we successfully administered the cognitive protocol from WHO‐SAGE with 153 teachers of the MTC. The SAGE protocol is utilized in many countries, although to our knowledge, this protocol has not been used by cohorts in Mexico other than in the large WHO‐SAGE study. We adopted it for our pilot due to its ease of administration and because our institution had experience administering the SAGE cognitive protocol, 10 there were training materials available for test administration, staff available for leading training, and existing software available for data entry. Although the test protocol was relatively brief, in our sample, we found a wide distribution of scores across the cognitive tests, with no clear indication of floor or ceiling effects, although immediate verbal recall scores were somewhat skewed toward higher performance. Overall, given the success of the administration of this short cognitive protocol, we plan to expand the number of tests in future research, enabling more detailed evaluation of included cognitive functions as well as other cognitive domains to ensure we capture early stages of cognitive decline.

Our work highlights the feasibility of adding in‐home cognitive assessments to existing epidemiological studies in Mexico and Latin America. While focused on cognition, this pilot also demonstrated the viability of major shifts in data collection within such studies. The MTC, originally based on self‐administered surveys, is likely to successfully incorporate home visits for older participants. These results suggest broad opportunities – in our cohort and potentially other cohorts in the region – to expand research requiring more intensive/in‐person data collection. Building on these pilot results, we plan to extend home visits to include specimen collection and physical function test, which self‐administered surveys cannot capture. Given challenges of aging – mobility, vision, cognition – and other barriers to participation, home visits offer a practical alternative that enhances participation and data quality. Overall, implementing home‐based methods in large cohorts can significantly advance health research in Mexico, Latin America, and beyond.
