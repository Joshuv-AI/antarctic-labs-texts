Metamaterial-enhanced MIMO antenna array system for non-invasive cerebral hemorrhage assessment in elderly and disabled patients
Authors: Nathirulla Sheriff, Arshad Karimbu Vallappil, Bilal A. Khawaja, Taufiqqurachman, Adel BenAbdennour, Hassan Tariq Chattha, Sharul Kamal A. Rahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microwave imaging (MWI) presents a promising, safer, more affordable, and portable alternative for detecting strokes compared to traditional methods, such as CT and MRI, which have limitations related to radiation exposure, cost, and accessibility. This paper focuses on the design and performance evaluation of a compact, T-shaped, two-port Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (MIMO) antenna, developed explicitly for microwave-based brain imaging. MWI operates by exploiting differences in tissue dielectric properties to identify and visualize abnormalities, with ultra-wideband (UWB) antennas configured in a two-port setup to improve signal penetration. The antenna, fabricated on an FR4 substrate with a dielectric constant (εr) of 4.3, effectively functions within the 3.1–6 GHz frequency range. It demonstrates a broad impedance bandwidth and maintains excellent isolation. Both simulated and measurement results confirm its reliable performance, showing consistent return-loss characteristics and stable omnidirectional radiation patterns. Furthermore, the antenna exhibits a very low envelope correlation coefficient (ECC < 0.005), a high diversity gain (DG > 9.9 dB), and favorable mean effective gain and total active reflection coefficient (TARC) values. When integrated into a human head model, this MIMO antenna configuration has shown minimal mutual-coupling (below − 15 dB) and significant potential for non-invasively detecting hemorrhages by carefully monitoring changes in scattering parameters. This proposed system underscores the capability of compact MIMO antennas to enable portable, non-invasive, and rapid stroke diagnosis, offering particular advantages for patients who lack access to conventional imaging facilities.

Introduction

The medical condition related to Stroke, also sometimes referred to as a Brain Hemorrhage, is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, resulting from an interrupted blood supply to the brain due to a ruptured blood vessel1. The stroke can cause a range of neurological deficits, including motor impairments, sensory loss, cognitive dysfunction, and speech difficulties2. For disabled individuals, the impact of a brain hemorrhage can be particularly devastating, worsening pre-existing conditions and further limiting their independence and quality of life1,2. Therefore, the preemptive diagnosis of stroke or stroke-like symptoms is necessary to reduce critical brain damage in patients. The primary diagnostic tool for stroke is currently computed tomography (CT) scanning, which can quickly identify hemorrhagic stroke. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a more sensitive tool for detecting ischemic stroke, especially in its early stages3. However, both CT and MRI techniques have their own limitations. CT scans expose patients to ionizing radiation3,4, and MRI is often contraindicated for patients with metallic implants or devices4, like pacemakers. Both imaging techniques can be time-consuming and expensive, and they require the patient to be transported to a specialized imaging center4,5. As disabled individuals have difficulty with transportation, positioning, or tolerating the scans, these limitations can be even more pronounced5.

In contrast, microwave imaging (MWI)1,2 has emerged as a promising technique for brain imaging, offering a safe, affordable, non-ionizing, portable, and rapid method for identifying strokes or brain hemorrhage-like symptoms. MWI is considered a viable option for brain imaging, particularly in resource-limited settings, due to its cost-effectiveness and acceptable image quality1,2. The limited availability of timely and specialized stroke diagnosis in rural areas, combined with the limitations of current imaging technologies, motivates the development of portable MWI systems. The integration of wireless communication and increased computing power further propels the research and development of MWI technology for various applications in human head imaging.

Principles of MWI for stroke detection

The MWI technique leverages differences in the dielectric properties of tissues to detect and visualize abnormalities, such as blood clots, that occur due to brain hemorrhage. The MWI technique involves transmitting microwave signals into the brain and capturing the scattered signals to form a microwave image6. To address the limitations of traditional methods, the use of ultra-wideband (UWB) antennas7,8 in a two-port configuration, offers several advantages. UWB antennas provide improved signal penetration and capture a broader range of frequency data, enhancing image resolution and diagnostic accuracy. The two-port configuration enables more sophisticated signal processing techniques, such as diversity and beamforming, which can minimize signal scattering and improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). This approach enhances the accuracy and reliability of MWI for brain imaging. Recent advancements2,9,10 in wireless communication and computing power have paved the way for further research and development of MWI technology for various applications in human head imaging. However, the accuracy and resolution of microwave-based diagnostic systems are heavily dependent on the antenna performance. The design of suitable antennas that can operate efficiently across the desired frequency bands, provide high gain, and maintain compactness is crucial for the success of portable brain imaging systems.

Antenna design considerations

The antenna arrays6 are considered a critical component in electromagnetic imaging systems, significantly influencing overall system performance. An appropriate antenna design for brain imaging systems should meet requirements that include ease of integration, compactness, simple geometric structure, enhanced bandwidth, gain, and directionality. Portable, non-invasive, low-powered electromagnetic head imaging systems can be designed using metamaterial-loaded7 compact directional 3D antennas. These systems typically operate in the 0.5–6 GHz range to strike a balance between image resolution and signal penetration. Multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) antenna configurations11–13, particularly two-port designs, offer additional benefits through sophisticated signal processing techniques, such as diversity and beamforming, which can mitigate signal scattering and enhance the SNR ratio.

Researchers have proposed various unique antenna design techniques for electromagnetic (EM) brain imaging systems. Alqahtani et al.14 proposed a modified slotted monopole patch antenna that can be used for microwave-based recognition of hemorrhagic or ischemic strokes. The proposed antenna was fabricated on a cost-effective FR4 substrate, which can achieve a wide bandwidth and suitable gain, with minimal signal distortion and acceptable SAR values, making it suitable for MWI applications. Samsuzzaman et al.15 proposed that circular slotted patch antennas with a defective grounded monopole patch can be used for MWI, achieving a broad impedance bandwidth and sufficient gain. These designs can acquire multiple data points for monitoring, which is crucial for high-resolution images. On the other hand, Islam et al.16 proposed a compact directional 3D antenna loaded with metamaterials for a portable EM brain imaging system. The incorporation of a slotted dipole antenna with finite metamaterial array elements enhanced bandwidth, gain, efficiency, and directionality. Similarly, Inum et al.17 explored the use of an electromagnetic band-gap (EBG) structure on the antenna ground plane to enhance the performance of conventional microstrip patch antennas. Mahmud et al.18 reviewed the use of UWB antenna sensors in microwave breast imaging systems. While the authors focused their discussion on breast imaging systems, the UWB antenna principles discussed are also relevant to head imaging. The authors summarize that UWB radar imaging is preferred due to its fast image processing capabilities. Naem Abbas et al.19. presents a compact, dual-polarized MIMO antenna designed for implantable biotelemetric applications. Using a water-based substrate, it achieves enhanced biocompatibility, improved isolation, and stable radiation characteristics within the human body. Unlike the proposed MWI antenna, this design targets reliable intra-body communication for IoMT devices rather than non-invasive stroke detection. In20, Lalitha et al. proposed the design of a UWB Vivaldi antenna for stroke detection and monitoring based on a qualitative MWI technique. The antenna was designed using a FR4 substrate for the frequency bands of 2.2 GHz and 3.5 GHz.

The proposed work presented in this paper examines the current state of research on MWI for brain blood-clot detection, which typically leads to strokes or brain hemorrhages. Moreover, this paper further focuses on MWI applications and the challenges they pose for individuals with disabilities. Figure 1 shows the proposed application framework for the fabricated two-port T-shaped MIMO antenna for microwave-based assessment of cerebral hemorrhage. The compact FR4-based antenna is used as the sensing element in a multi-antenna arrangement around the Hugo head model, where transmitted and received microwave signals are analyzed to detect dielectric disturbances caused by hemorrhagic tissue. The figure also summarizes the relationship between antenna performance, scattering-parameter variation, brain imaging output, and SAR evaluation, thereby providing a visual overview of the proposed non-invasive diagnostic methodology. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 discusses the step-by-step design process of the proposed Two-Port T-shaped MIMO antenna. Section 3 presents the comparison between the simulated and measurement results. Section 4 discusses the performance of the proposed antenna with the Hugo Model for the detection of hemorrhages using MWI techniques. Finally, Sect. 5 concludes the paper by summarizing the key findings and contributions of this work.

Two-port semi-circle-shaped MIMO antenna design

A novel, compact, two-port MIMO antenna with T-shaped antenna elements is implemented on an FR4 substrate. The thickness of the substrate is measured to be hsub =1.6 mm, while its dielectric constant (εr) is determined to be 4.3. Figure 2 illustrates the symmetrical configuration of the antenna under consideration, while Table 1 provides a comprehensive overview of the optimal parameters of the proposed antenna design. Effective isolation is achieved by implementing a direct decoupling structure within the ground plane. CST Microwave Studio (CST-MWS) simulation software was used to model and assess antenna characteristics during the design phase. The MIMO antenna exhibits a total volume of 50 mm × 20 mm, roughly equivalent to 0.40λ × 0.32λ at the central frequency of 4.85 GHz. The following sections delineate the design process and its associated variables. Within the context of the proposed MIMO system, the basic setup of a single-port antenna is illustrated in Fig. 3. The antenna possesses dimensions of 20 mm in length, 25 mm in width, and 1.6 mm in thickness. It illustrates the progressive phases of the proposed antenna’s design development.

Step-by-step design configuration of single-element antenna

Figure 4 illustrates the results of the return-loss (S11) measurements that were taken at each stage of the evolutionary method used in designing the suggested MIMO antenna. The preliminary design involves a configuration of a rectangular patch antenna with a full ground plane and an antenna element shaped like the letter T, as depicted in Fig. 3a. This antenna configuration could achieve better impedance matching (S11 < 10 dB) only in the frequency range of 12.3 GHz, as illustrated in Fig. 4. To achieve the required frequency below 6 GHz, slots are etched in the top layer, and their lengths are adjusted in the antenna element, which can increase the electrical length and provide the capacitive effect. This configuration could provide a frequency shift to a lower value but still be unable to achieve the required bandwidth. To further achieve the required frequency bandwidth, the ground plane is modified to a partial ground21 as shown in Fig. 3c. The plot presented in Fig. 3 reveals that the value of S11 is below − 10 dB, indicating that the desired frequency range of 3.6–6 GHz is effectively encompassed, with a 50% impedance bandwidth.

Step-by-step design configuration of two-port MIMO antenna

The two-port MIMO antenna design is based on the single-port antenna design as previously discussed in the preceding section. In the preliminary stage, two antennas are strategically placed close to each other on the topmost layer of the FR4 substrate, as illustrated in Fig. 5. The antenna’s dimensions measure 25 mm in length, 50 mm in width, and 1.6 mm in thickness. The positioning of each antenna element is such that it is located 7.8 mm away from the end of the substrates, and it possesses a feeding port with a width (w_port) of 3 mm. The separation between the two antenna components measures 30 mm. The dimensions have been suitably modified to maximize bandwidth coverage across the required frequency spectrum and ensure sufficient isolation.

The sequential arrangement of the antenna proposed in this design is depicted in Fig. 5a–c. The S-parameters simulated, as shown in Fig. 6, are based on the design outlined in Fig. 5a, referred to as Step-1. The findings suggest that a return loss of -10 dB commences at a frequency of 3.6 GHz. Implementing a modified upper layer and stubs22 in the ground plane of Step-2, as illustrated in Fig. 5b, led to enhanced return-loss performance, as evidenced by S11 values surpassing − 10 dB from a frequency of 3.3 GHz onwards. The final configuration, as illustrated in Fig. 5c, integrates the inclusion of a complementary split ring resonator (CSRR)7 in the ground plane during Step-3. The presented design exhibits a return-loss of -15 dB, commencing at a frequency of 3.1 GHz, as shown in Fig. 6a. Additionally, it showcases favorable resonance characteristics at frequencies of 3.7 GHz and 5.3 GHz. The stubs were arranged in a U-shaped configuration22, with an inverted orientation, and were positioned at a distance of 1 mm from the center of the ground plane on both sides. This setup accomplished isolation among the ports, with an acceptable isolation of less than − 15 dB over the frequency range of 3.1 GHz to 6 GHz. Similarly, the implementation of the CSRR in the horizontal plane demonstrated exceptional isolation, with measurements consistently below − 15 dB across the entire frequency range, as illustrated in Fig. 6b.

Notably, the measured return-loss results of the proposed antennas demonstrate a significant level of resemblance, accompanied by a satisfactory bandwidth of 58%. The provided bandwidth adequately encompasses the frequency range from 3.3 GHz to 6 GHz. The measured return-loss results demonstrate a slight discrepancy within the designated frequency spectrum. Figure 9a-d depicts the radiation patterns in the two-dimensional E-plane and H-plane, showcasing both simulated and measured results. These patterns were observed when port-1 was excited initially and port-2 connected to a load impedance of 50 ohms at frequencies of 3.5 GHz and 4.5 GHz. The antenna exhibits omnidirectional radiation patterns across the simulated frequencies of 3.5 GHz, 4.5 GHz, 5.5 GHz, and 6 GHz, implying consistent power distribution in all horizontal directions. This power distribution shows interference-free reception and maintains an omnidirectional radiation pattern in both the E-plane and H-plane. This will provide the necessary angle and spatial diversity for a strong diversity gain. While the measured radiation patterns validate this omnidirectional behavior at corresponding frequencies, there is a divergence in magnitude between the measured and simulated values. This disparity in magnitude can be attributed to practical considerations such as ambient environmental conditions, fabrication tolerances, and inherent losses associated with the measurement apparatus.

Evaluation of antenna performance with Hugo model

Detailed head model

The detailed human head model, specifically the Hugo model, is constructed from various biological tissues, each possessing unique electromagnetic properties. These tissues encompass the skin, fat, cortical bone (such as the skull), cerebrospinal fluid, gray matter, and white matter. Each of these layers exhibits specific dielectric characteristics, such as εr and electrical conductivity (σ), which are crucial in determining how electromagnetic waves are absorbed and scattered.

For instance, at a frequency of 1.6 GHz, the skull bone has an approximate conductivity of 0.24 S/m and an εr of 12.45. In contrast, brain tissues, such as gray and white matter, exhibit higher εr values around 56.8 and a conductivity of approximately 0.34 S/m. These differences in dielectric properties significantly impact how electromagnetic waves propagate within the head, with the gray and white matter layers causing substantial reflection and absorption due to their elevated permittivity. Implementation and antenna array configuration

In the proposed design, a two-port, four-element MIMO antenna system is arranged around a human head model, as shown in Fig. 12a-b. These antennas are strategically oriented to maximize coverage and ensure effective penetration of electromagnetic waves into the brain. In this configuration, Port-4 functions as the transmitting antenna, while the other ports operate as receivers, collecting backscattered signals. To ensure reliable MIMO performance, the antennas are carefully spaced and positioned around the human head model to minimize mutual-coupling effects while still allowing for sufficient interaction with the head tissues. A spacing of 25 mm is maintained between the head and the antennas, aiming for a mutual-coupling below − 15dB. This multi-port configuration is crucial for enhancing spatial diversity and enabling more accurate monitoring of localized abnormalities, such as hemorrhages.

Comparing scattering parameters in the presence and absence of hemorrhage

To assess diagnostic performance, the S-parameters were examined under two conditions: a healthy head and a head containing a hemorrhage positioned near Port-4. The dielectric properties of blood clots or hemorrhages with a relative permittivity (εr) of approximately 69 and an electrical conductivity of around 2.4 S/m are significantly different from those of the surrounding brain tissue. This disparity leads to noticeable disturbances in the scattered signals. Figure 13a,b illustrates the scattering parameters, with Port-4 acting as the transmitter and the remaining ports working as receivers. In the absence of a hemorrhage (healthy model), the receiver antennas’ reflection coefficients consistently stay below − 20 dB across the 3–6 GHz frequency band, indicating stable operation. When a hemorrhage is introduced into the model, the scattering parameters demonstrate distinct changes. Specifically, the S24 parameter exhibits a notable increase, indicating stronger coupling between Ports-2 and − 4.

This enhanced coupling is attributed to abnormal scattering caused by the presence of the hemorrhage (blood clot). In the healthy brain, the peak resonance for S24 reached − 80 dB, but with the hemorrhage, it shifted to -70 dB. Furthermore, the S84 parameter exhibits more significant fluctuations compared to the healthy head. This lack of deviation confirms that localized alterations in dielectric properties, such as those caused by a hemorrhage, can be dependably identified by monitoring changes in the S-parameters.

Clinical significance of antenna-based MWI

The analysis reveals that variations in specific S-parameters, particularly S24 and S84, reliably indicate the presence of an intracranial hemorrhage near Port-4. The consistency of these deviations across different frequencies confirms the system’s sensitivity to tissue abnormalities. Additionally, the low mutual-coupling (below − 15 dB) confirms that the observed changes are due to the biological material rather than interference between the antennas. Consequently, the proposed two-port MIMO antenna configuration, when integrated with the Hugo head model, demonstrates significant promise for non-invasively detecting hemorrhages/blood clots by carefully monitoring the behavior of scattering parameters.

Specific absorption rate (SAR) analysis

The electromagnetic safety of the proposed MWI system was validated by evaluating the Specific Absorption Rate (SAR) within a high-fidelity ‘Hugo’ human head model. To assess the thermal impact of non-ionizing radiation, the global average SAR was calculated using the power conservation principle for an N-port network. The total absorbed power (Pabs) was determined by subtracting the reflected and coupled power from the incident power (Pin)32,33. Comparative analysis was performed for both healthy brain and hemorrhagic clot scenarios across three input power levels: 10 mW, 100 mW, and 500 mW. The results demonstrate that the presence of a high-conductivity blood clot (σ ≈ 2.4) slightly increases energy absorption compared to the healthy baseline. For the recommended operating power of 100 mW, the maximum average SAR was 0.0199 W/kg for the clot case and 0.0198 W/kg for the healthy case. Both values remain significantly below the localized safety limits of 1.6 W/kg (FCC/IEEE) and 2.0 W/kg (ICNIRP)33 providing a substantial safety margin for continuous medical monitoring.

Comparative analysis and novelty of the proposed MIMO Antenna for MWI

Table 3 highlights the novelty of the proposed antenna relative to representative state-of-the-art microwave head imaging antennas. The comparison emphasizes antenna structure, metamaterial / decoupling features, Ports/Configuration, Operating bandwidth, Substrate, SAR characteristics, and application-level validation for hemorrhage or stroke detection. It is important to note that most prior designs are based on single-element monopoles, patches, Vivaldi antennas, or directional 3D antennas, with an emphasis on bandwidth, gain, or directional performance, whereas MIMO-specific diversity metrics are generally not reported. In contrast, the proposed work presents a compact two-port T-shaped MIMO antenna operating over 3.1–6.0 GHz on a low-cost FR4 substrate, with excellent diversity performance characterized by ECC < 0.005, DG > 9.9 dB, acceptable MEG, and TARC below 0 dB. In addition, the proposed configuration demonstrates low average SAR values of 0.0199 W/kg for the clot case and 0.0198 W/kg for the healthy case at 100 mW input power, while also enabling hemorrhage-sensitive monitoring through changes in scattering parameters in the Hugo head model.

Conclusions

This study focused on the development and evaluation of a compact, two-port T-shaped MIMO antenna, specifically optimized for microwave brain imaging and the detection of hemorrhages. The antenna demonstrated a wide impedance bandwidth, operating effectively from 3.1 GHz to 6 GHz, and maintained minimal mutual-coupling, a critical factor for precise tissue differentiation. Simulations conducted with the Hugo head model further confirmed the antenna’s diagnostic capabilities. These simulations showed that changes in scattering parameters could accurately distinguish between normal brain tissue and hemorrhagic regions. The results indicate that this antenna offers a dependable, economical, and portable solution for the early diagnosis of stroke, proving particularly advantageous in remote or resource-limited settings. While the present study establishes the feasibility of sensing using the Hugo head model, future work will focus on extending the proposed system toward real-time microwave imaging and validating its performance through physical phantom experiments, clinical data, and advanced reconstruction algorithms such as DMAS.
