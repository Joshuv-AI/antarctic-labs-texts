A dual-labeled dataset and fusion model for automatic teeth segmentation, numbering, and state assessment on panoramic radiographs
Authors: Wenbo Zhou, Xin Lu, Dan Zhao, Meng Jiang, Linlin Fan, Weihang Zhang, Fenglin Li, Dezhou Wang, Weihuang Yin, Xin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecently, deep learning has been increasingly applied in the field of dentistry. The aim of this study is to develop a model for the automatic segmentation, numbering, and state assessment of teeth on panoramic radiographs.MethodsWe created a dual-labeled dataset on panoramic radiographs for training, incorporating both numbering and state labels. We then developed a fusion model that combines a YOLOv9-e instance segmentation model with an EfficientNetv2-l classification model. The instance segmentation model is used for tooth segmentation and numbering, whereas the classification model is used for state evaluation. The final prediction results integrate tooth position, numbering, and state information. The model’s output includes result visualization and automatic report generation.ResultsPrecision, Recall, mAP50 (mean Average Precision), and mAP50-95 for the tooth instance segmentation task are 0.989, 0.955, 0.975, and 0.840, respectively. Precision, Recall, Specificity, and F1 Score for the tooth classification task are 0.943, 0.933, 0.985, and 0.936, respectively.ConclusionsThis fusion model is the first to integrate automatic dental segmentation, numbering, and state assessment. It provides highly accurate results, including detailed visualizations and automated report generation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04984-2.

Background

In 2012, the release of the AlexNet model marked a significant milestone, showing superior performance over traditional machine learning methods in image classification tasks [1]. The integration of deep neural networks within the AlexNet structure has garnered substantial attention and has spurred rapid advancements in artificial intelligence (AI). Since then, deep neural networks, commonly referred to as deep learning, have been applied across various fields [2]. Currently, the primary applications of deep learning include computer vision, natural language processing, speech recognition, and related areas. In particular, computer vision, which has been widely used in the field of oral health [3], primarily involves processing two-dimensional and three-dimensional images, videos, and other image data. The key subtasks in this domain include image classification, object detection, semantic segmentation, instance segmentation, and image generation. These subtasks have been explored in dental research. For example, Bofan Song’s team employed a modified VGG19 network for the automatic classification of oral cheek mucosa images, successfully distinguishing between benign, malignant, and premalignant cases [4]. Similarly, Shintaro Sukegawa’s team applied customized deep convolutional neural networks to accurately detect and classify third molars in panoramic radiographs, effectively evaluating the complexity of third molar extraction [5]. Numerous studies have focused on the automatic segmentation of normal tissue structures, such as teeth, implants, maxillae, mandibles, mandibular canals, and mandibular condyles [6–9], as well as the auxiliary diagnosis of oral diseases, including dental caries, apical periodontitis, periodontitis, root fractures, bone fractures, and tumors [10–15].

Among these various tasks, one of the most fundamental is the automated segmentation of teeth. To date, numerous studies have been conducted on this subject. For example, Jader’s team [16] pioneered research on tooth instance segmentation using dental panoramic radiographs, although their work did not include automatic tooth numbering. Tuzoff’s team [17] was the first to integrate automatic numbering into detection and segmentation research. Karen Panetta’s team [18] released the world’s first multimodal dental panoramic radiograph database. This dataset includes not only segmentation information of the teeth but also a range of comprehensive data, such as eye tracker-generated maps, text information describing each radiograph, maxillomandibular region-of-interest masks, and more. The team from Peking University [6] expanded research on automatic tooth segmentation to encompass all age groups, including deciduous, mixed, and permanent dentition. Zhiming Cui’s team [19] conducted automatic segmentation and numbering of teeth using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) and released portions of their dataset.

However, there are still significant shortcomings in the current research. First, the datasets used for training and testing in these studies have not been fully disclosed, making it difficult for other researchers to reproduce the results. Second, most of the studies involve data collection and labeling by the researchers themselves, followed by model testing on their own datasets, which prevents direct comparisons of model performance across different studies. Finally, existing studies on automatic tooth segmentation conclude with the construction of a segmentation model without subsequent practical application, leaving research at the experimental stage.

To address the shortcomings of previous studies, we established a publicly available dual-labeled dataset on dental panoramic radiographs. This dataset features teeth outlined with both numbering and state labels, making it highly comprehensive. It includes images from multiple hospitals and dental clinics, ensuring diversity. Additionally, we developed a fusion model based on the YOLOv9-e instance segmentation model [20] and the EfficientNetv2-l classification model [21]. It is the first model used for dental status assessment following automatic tooth identification, with an output that includes result visualization and automatic report generation. The model is used to identify the following states of teeth: ‘Tooth without anomalies’, ‘Tooth with fillings’, ‘Tooth with root canal treatment (RCT)’, ‘Tooth with crown’, ‘Tooth with caries’, ‘Residual root’, and ‘Tooth with RCT and crown’.

The significance of our study can be summarized as follows:

Public availability of the dataset

Our dataset is publicly accessible, significantly reducing the workload for other researchers. They can use it to develop their own instance segmentation models and make predictions on new data, thereby minimizing the need for manual labeling.

Evaluation of models

The dataset can serve as a test set for model evaluation, enabling comparison of model performance across different research groups.

Practical application of the fusion model

In clinical practice, the visual results of the fusion model can help doctors reduce the frequency of missed diagnoses. The reports also assist with medical documentation. In education, the model helps students identify teeth, restorations, and certain diseases. Additionally, our team believes that the model is particularly well suited for large-scale data analysis.

Methods

Dataset and image annotation

Our dataset comprises a total of 5,000 panoramic radiographs, divided into two sections. The first section was collected by our research team, whereas the second section was derived from public datasets. The self-collected data include 2,000 panoramic radiographs from three hospitals (China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University, Wuxi Stomatology Hospital, and People’s Hospital of Zhengzhou) and three dental clinics collected between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2023. The equipment used was sourced from various suppliers, including Orthophos XG (Dentsply Sirona, Germany), Planmeca ProMax (Planmeca, Finland), and Bondream 1020 (Bondream, China). The study population consisted of individuals aged 16 years and older, with a mean age of 36.6 years. There were 989 males and 1011 females. The remaining data were obtained from publicly available datasets created by Danning Wang’s team [22] and Silva, Bernardo Peters Menezes’s team [23]. Further details on the data can be found in previous articles [22, 23]. All images were anonymized and converted to portable network graphics (PNG) format.

The data inclusion and exclusion criteria were set loosely to maximize data diversity. The inclusion criterion was patients older than 16 years with permanent dentition. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with deciduous dentition, mixed dentition, permanent dentition with deciduous teeth, and images that were too blurred to interfere with labeling.

Data were annotated by five medical workers who underwent annotation training before starting the process. Two postgraduate students and two doctors with over five years of experience were responsible for the annotation, and a senior doctor with over 15 years of experience reviewed the annotations. The software used for data annotation was Labelme (v5.4.1), and the annotation process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Four doctors performed the annotation simultaneously, using polygons to delineate the teeth. Zooming was allowed for accurate outlining. The implants were not labeled. After delineation, the teeth were assigned numbering and status labels. The teeth were numbered according to the Federation Dentaire International (FDI) notation, ranging from 11 to 48 and 91 for supernumerary teeth. A total of 33 different numbering labels were used. There are seven status labels: ‘Tooth without anomalies’, ‘Tooth with fillings’, ‘Tooth with RCT’, ‘Tooth with crown’, ‘Tooth with caries’, ‘Residual root’, and ‘Tooth with RCT and crown’, which are represented by Arabic numerals 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively. Examples of each status are shown in Fig. 2. If the labeling process encountered unclear tooth boundaries, difficulty in determining tooth numbering, or difficulty in determining tooth status, it was resolved through joint discussion among the doctors. The numbering labels are used for training the tooth instance segmentation model, and the state labels are used for training the classification model. Statistical information about the labels for this dataset is presented in Table 1. The entire dataset comprises 124,568 teeth, and the annotation process took our team five months to complete.

Trial design

Our model is a fusion of the YOLOv9-e instance segmentation model and the EfficientNetv2-l classification model after optimization and fine-tuning. These two models were selected through preliminary experiments, with the results provided in the supplementary materials. The training process is divided into two parts: training the instance segmentation model and training the classification model. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 3.

For the instance segmentation model, 5000 panoramic radiographs are split into training, validation, and test sets at a 3:1:1 ratio. The validation set is used for hyperparameter tuning, whereas the test set is used to evaluate the model’s performance. Data augmentation refers to the process of generating new training samples by applying a series of transformations to the original training data. For a specific task and model, appropriate data augmentation can improve model accuracy, whereas unsuitable augmentation may reduce accuracy. After extensive experimentation, the data augmentation techniques used in preprocessing include random rotation, scaling, translation, and mosaic augmentation. Similarly, model hyperparameters significantly impact model performance. For example, a larger learning rate can accelerate model learning but may also prevent convergence. After repeated experiments, the final key hyperparameters for training include initial learning rate of 0.01, final learning rate of 0.0001, momentum of 0.937, and optimizer weight decay of 0.0005. The batch size is set to 2, and the input image size is resized to 1280*1280. The stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizer is used, with the training process spanning 80 epochs. If there is no significant improvement in performance within 30 epochs, training is automatically stopped.

Fig. 3Flow chart of the fusion model

Flow chart of the fusion model

For classification model training, we first create a dataset by cropping the outlined area of each tooth from the panoramic radiographs after expanding 1.5 times and classifying them through state labels. A total of 2000 samples are randomly selected from each of the seven categories, resulting in a classification dataset of 14,000 samples. This dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets at a 3:1:1 ratio. Data augmentation methods include random horizontal flipping, random vertical flipping, and random rotation. The key hyperparameters for training include an initial learning rate of 0.01, a final learning rate of 0.0001, a batch size of 16, an input image size of 224*224, and the SGD optimizer. The training epoch is set to 50.

The inference process is also divided into two parts. First, the original panoramic radiographs are given as input to the fusion model. The instance segmentation model predicts the position and numbering of teeth. Then, the predicted tooth regions are expanded by 1.5 times, cropped, and sent to the classification model for tooth state evaluation. The final prediction results integrate the tooth position, numbering, and state information. The output includes result visualization and automatic report generation.

Experimental platform

An NVIDIA RTX4070Ti graphics card, an Intel i14700 CPU, and 32 GB of memory were procured for the experiment. The experiment was conducted on a Windows 10 system, utilizing PyCharm as the development platform and Python 3.8 with the PyTorch 2.3.0 deep learning framework.

Evaluation metrics

During the training of the instance segmentation model, we evaluated the performance in terms of precision, recall, false negative rate (FNR), false positive rate (FPR), intersection over union (IoU) and mean average precision (mAP) metrics. During the training of the classification model, we assessed precision, recall, specificity and F1 score. Precision is calculated as TP/(TP + FP), recall as TP/(TP + FN), specificity as TN/(TN + FP), FNR as FN/(TP + FN), FPR as FP/(TN + FP), IoU as area of intersection​/area of union, and F1 score as 2*Precision*Recall/(Precision + Recall), where TP denotes true positive, FP denotes false positive, FN denotes false negative, and TN denotes true negative. Discussion

The data in Tables 2 and 3 indicate that the model performs well overall in both the instance segmentation and classification tasks. However, a closer look reveals that the accuracy for some categories is not satisfactory.

In Table 2, the precision for the category ‘supernumerary tooth’ is 1.0, indicating that all the predicted supernumerary teeth are correct. However, the recall is only 0.565, meaning that many supernumerary teeth are not detected. Consequently, the mAP50 value is only 0.726, whereas the mAP50 for other tooth categories exceeds 0.95. We identified three reasons for this discrepancy. First, supernumerary teeth can develop at any location. They can be located between the mesial incisors, between the premolars, distal to the third molar, or anywhere in the maxilla and mandible. They can also be in an eruption or obstruction state. Second, supernumerary teeth have diverse forms, appearing as either normal or malformed teeth. Third, the sample of supernumerary teeth is insufficient. Owing to their variable positions and diverse morphology, a larger sample is required for training. However, our dataset contains a much smaller sample of supernumerary teeth. These factors contribute to the low accuracy for supernumerary teeth, reflecting a problem with our dataset: the insufficient sample size and homogenization of supernumerary tooth types.

As shown in Table 3, ‘Tooth with fillings’, ‘Tooth with RCT’, ‘Tooth with crown’, ‘Residual root’ and ‘Tooth with RCT and crown’ had F1 values greater than 0.9, whereas ‘Tooth with caries’ had an F1 value of only 0.876. This difference aligns with our visual judgment. Categories such as ‘Tooth with fillings’, ‘Tooth with RCT’, ‘Tooth with crown’ and ‘Tooth with RCT and crown’ show high-density shadows in oral panoramic radiographs, making them easier to identify visually. The ‘Residual root’ category, which differs significantly from normal tooth morphology, is also easily judged by the naked eye. These five categories are straightforward for visual identification, making it easier for the model to extract features and resulting in high classification accuracy. However, the ‘Tooth with caries’ category presented difficulties during data labeling. Caries can be classified into superficial, intermediate, and deep caries. While intermediate and deep caries are easily assessed and labeled on panoramic radiographs, superficial caries are much more challenging to evaluate. Panoramic radiographs are two-dimensional images where many structures overlap, and poor clarity or artifacts in some images complicate the diagnosis of superficial caries. Consequently, many superficial caries labels are determined through clinician consultation, leading to potential inaccuracies. For the model, extracting features for caries is more challenging than for other categories, resulting in poorer prediction outcomes.

In the classification task, in addition to the seven tooth conditions previously mentioned, other categories, such as microdontia, macrodontia, geminated teeth, root resorption, periapical periodontitis, root fracture, cracked teeth, and tetracycline-stained teeth, could also be considered. However, not all dental conditions are suitable for this task. For example, cracked teeth and tetracycline-stained teeth cannot be reliably diagnosed using panoramic radiographs. Likewise, some cases of periapical periodontitis cover a large area, making it difficult to identify the affected tooth. Conditions such as microdontia, macrodontia, geminated teeth, root resorption, and root fracture are well suited to this task, but owing to the rarity and difficulty of collecting these cases, they were not included in the current study. Expanding the collection of panoramic images of rare cases represents a potential future direction for research, broadening the model’s applicability.

Numerous studies have investigated the automatic identification of teeth and assisted in the diagnosis of oral diseases. Initially, these studies predominantly used periapical and panoramic radiographs. However, with the increasing application of CBCT, the development of 3D models, and improved computing power, CBCT data are more frequently used. CBCT data are three-dimensional and provide more detailed information about teeth and the maxillofacial region, making them optimal for automatic tooth identification and auxiliary diagnosis of oral diseases [24]. However, in clinical practice, CBCT is used sparingly because it involves higher radiation doses and costs than panoramic radiographs and is reserved for difficult or special cases [25, 26]. Panoramic radiographs remain the most commonly used imaging examination in dentistry. Therefore, we selected the panoramic radiograph dataset for this study. Panoramic radiographs are also suitable for analyzing oral health status at the population level in hospitals or regions.

Numerous classification, object detection and segmentation models have emerged in deep learning studies that utilize panoramic radiographs as data. The final prediction results of our model integrate the tooth position, numbering, and state information. A similar outcome can be achieved by combining an object detection model with a classification model, which would require less computational power. For example, Md Anas Ali’s team [27] used an object detection model to predict tooth position and numbering. However, in contrast to object detection, which only predicts a bounding box, instance segmentation provides more detailed information by outputting the exact contours of the teeth. The bounding box includes more redundant information, and using it as input for the classification model may even reduce its performance. For this reason, we chose to combine an instance segmentation model with a classification model.

To date, our fusion model is the only instance that integrates an instance segmentation model with a classification model. Moreover, it is the first model to perform dental status assessment following automatic tooth identification, with outputs that include result visualization and automatic report generation. In clinical practice, visual results can help doctors reduce the frequency of missed diagnoses. The reports also assist with medical documentation, especially in complex cases involving patients with residual roots, multiple restorations, or numerous fillings. However, owing to the model’s limited accuracy and ability to detect only a small range of diseases, it currently serves as a supplementary tool in clinical settings. In education, the model helps students identify teeth, restorations, and certain diseases while also teaching them how to use AI tools. Additionally, our team believes that the model is particularly well suited for large-scale data analysis, such as assessing the oral health status of a population within a hospital or region. For example, if we want to evaluate the condition of residual roots in patients aged 50 to 60 at a specific hospital or determine the percentage of teeth that have undergone root canal treatment, we can collect panoramic radiographs, run them through the model to generate results, and then analyze the data. This method would be far more efficient than manual image review. The dataset used in our research is publicly available and can be utilized by other researchers to streamline the process of data preparation for subsequent studies, thereby reducing the time and workload involved. Furthermore, the dataset can serve as a benchmark for automatic tooth identification tasks, allowing researchers to assess the strengths and weaknesses of different models.

Our study has several highlights but also several shortcomings. The classification model cannot evaluate teeth containing two or more states simultaneously. For example, a root canal treated tooth with secondary caries should belong to both ‘Tooth with RCT’ and ‘Tooth with caries,’ but our classification model will only classify it as ‘Tooth with RCT’. In our model, ‘Residual root’ has the highest priority, followed by ‘Tooth with RCT’, ‘Tooth with crown’, ‘Tooth with RCT and crown’, ‘Tooth with fillings’, finally, ‘Tooth with caries’ and ‘Tooth without anomalies’. Figure 5 shows examples of inaccurate predictions from the fusion model. Issues such as omission, misdiagnosis, poor tooth localization accuracy, and incorrect numbering predictions stemmed from errors in the instance segmentation model, whereas inaccurate state assessments were due to the classification model’s misjudgment. These inaccuracies are common and challenging problems in deep learning models, often hindering their practical application. Therefore, improving the accuracy of model predictions is essential. To address these issues, first, our team plans to further expand the dataset. Modern deep learning models are highly data-driven, and the larger the dataset, the better the model’s performance. Second, we aim to increase the diversity of the data by collecting panoramic images from more hospitals, regions, and devices, including more special cases to improve the model’s generalizability. Finally, we refine the structures of both the instance segmentation and classification models. In this study, the models used were the original architectures, optimized mainly through hyperparameter tuning, without significant structural modifications. In the future, we plan to improve these models’ architectures to achieve better results.

Conclusions

We constructed a dual-labeled dataset that is substantial in quantity, rich in variety, and accurate in annotation. The labels include both the numbering and state information of teeth, providing a valuable reference for other researchers. The fusion model we propose is the first to combine automatic dental segmentation and status assessment, with outputs that include result visualization and automatic report generation. It demonstrates high accuracy and can be used for teaching and big data analysis. This research significantly contributes to the advancement of deep learning applications in dentistry, offering valuable insights for future investigations in this field.
