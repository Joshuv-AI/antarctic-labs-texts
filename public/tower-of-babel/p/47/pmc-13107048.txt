A homecage operant paradigm reveals behavioral dynamics of social motivation in mice
Authors: Yiru Chen, Susan E. Maloney, Alexxai V. Kravitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present an open-source, homecage-based social operant paradigm that enables continuous, multi-day measurement of social motivation in mice without experimenter intervention. In this task, a nose-poke response triggers an automated door that provides controlled access to a social partner in an adjacent cage. Using this system, we demonstrate that operant responding is specifically driven by access to a social partner and reveals robust daily rhythms of social motivation across the circadian cycle. We further show that social seeking in this task is sensitive to pharmacological manipulation of dopamine signaling. By combining operant rigor with continuous homecage monitoring, this method increases ecological validity, temporal resolution, and scalability, providing a flexible platform for studying social motivation and its neural regulation in both basic and disease-relevant contexts.

Introduction

Social motivation, the drive to initiate and maintain social contact, is a fundamental component of behavior across species and is essential for survival and mental well-being.1,2,3 In humans, disruptions in social motivation are suggested to underlie the social challenges that hallmark several neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD),3 schizophrenia,4 and depression.5,6 Indeed, the Social Motivation Theory of Autism posits that social interaction may be less motivating to autistic individuals, and this reduced motivation may lead to poor acquisition of social skills, leading to further social disengagement.3,7

Social motivation is defined as the internal processes that drive social interactions, and it has been theorized that deficits in these motivational processes underlie the abnormalities in social interaction and communication that define neurodevelopmental disorders such as ASD. Behavioral tests in rodents have uncovered many neural and molecular underpinnings of social motivation. Three types of assays often used include the social interaction, social approach, and social operant tasks. Social interaction tests include the dyadic interaction test, where two mice are allowed to explore one another, and social behaviors such as sniffing or allogrooming are quantified.8 Social approach tests include the three-chambered test, in which mice are given the option to investigate a novel mouse or a novel object.8,9 This test can measure the relative time investigating and the number of approaches to the mouse vs. the object, which is indicative of sociability and social preference. Despite the power of social interaction and approach tests, neither class is suited to quantifying how motivated a mouse is for social interaction, cost-benefit decisions about social drive vs. other drives, or learning across days. Social operant tasks were developed to address these questions about social motivation. In a social operant test, an animal is trained to perform an action (typically a lever-press or a nose-poke response) to gain access to a social interaction with a conspecific.7,10,11,12 The number of times a mouse performs the action, or “works,” to obtain social access is interpreted as a proxy for their level of social motivation. This type of assessment allows researchers to probe motivational behaviors and neural circuitry using classic operant paradigms, definitions, and computational modeling. This is especially powerful in studies that use animal models of genetic and environmental liability for neurodevelopmental and other psychiatric disorders that present with social interaction challenges.

Social operant paradigms have provided key insights into the neural and molecular basis of social motivation. For example, Hu et al. identified an amygdala-to-hypothalamus circuit involving projections from the basomedial amygdala to the medial preoptic area that promotes social motivational behavior.11 This pathway was shown to influence the activity of ventral tegmental area (VTA) dopamine neurons, providing a link between limbic social processing and dopaminergic motivation circuits. Ramsey et al. showed that female CD-1 mice exhibit significantly stronger social operant self-administration and a preference for social interaction over palatable food compared to female C57BL/6J mice, highlighting strain-dependent variation in social motivation.12 Our recent work revealed sex-specific deficits in social motivation in Shank3b mutant mice (a mouse model of genetic ASD risk) and that blocking oxytocin receptors reduced social seeking in C57BL/6J mice,7 further demonstrating the power of the social operant paradigm to dissect both circuit-level and genetic contributions to social behavior.

However, social operant tasks typically utilize specialized chambers, into which mice are transported for daily testing. Moving mice into new environments can introduce both stress and novelty, which can interfere with behavior13,14 and confound interpretations of the task. For instance, it is not always clear whether operant responses truly reflect a desire for social interaction or reflect test chamber discomfort or exploration of the new environment. In addition, these paradigms often require several days of training before data collection can begin, and testing sessions are typically limited to short durations (e.g., one hour per day), which restricts data yield and may miss critical temporal dynamics, such as daily patterns of social motivation.

To address these limitations, we developed an open-source homecage-based social operant paradigm to assess social motivation without removing mice from their homecages. Our system is automated, which allows us to assess social motivation for several consecutive days without experimenter intervention, reducing stress and increasing the scale of data collection. The system consists of a battery-powered servo-driven social door that provides access to mice in the neighboring cage, which is controlled by a Feeding Experimentation Device v.3 (FED3).15 The door also integrates proximity sensors that report whether Test and Stimulus mice approach the door after each social operant response, allowing us to distinguish social seeking from general exploration of the FED3 device. As our paradigm runs for multiple days, we can assess social motivation across the circadian cycle to determine when social motivation peaks and how it relates to other daily rhythms of behavior. We validated this paradigm by demonstrating that trained mice increase nose-poking behavior when a social partner is present and that this behavior is sensitive to pharmacological manipulation of dopamine signaling. Dopaminergic systems, especially the D1 and D2 receptor pathways, have been widely implicated in motivated behavior and social reward processing.16,17,18 Using SCH23390 and haloperidol to selectively block systemic D1 or D2 receptors, respectively, we found that social motivation can be modulated without affecting general locomotion or feeding, especially under D1 blockade. Finally, we have released all design files online, along with instructions on how to build the system. Taken together, our paradigm offers a powerful and flexible platform for investigating social motivation in rodent home cages.

Results

The homecage social operant test

We developed a battery-powered social door system that mounts between two standard homecages to measure social motivation. The door incorporates a 3D printed sliding door mechanism, proximity sensors, and electronics that enable continuous measurement of operant social behavior over multiple days (Figure 1; Figure S1). To validate the device, we housed 10 Test mice (four males and six females) in homecages with same-sex “Stimulus” mice housed in the neighboring cage. In our first experiment, mice completed three experimental phases: (1) an empty-cage period (5 days) during which valid active pokes opened the door to an adjacent empty cage, (2) a social stimulus period (9 days) during which a same-sex stranger mouse was introduced into the adjacent cage and replaced every 3 days with a new same-sex mouse to maintain novelty, and (3) an extinction period (4 days) during which the social partner was removed so the door opened to an empty adjacent cage. When comparing the last three empty-cage days and first three social days, two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of period [F(1, 9) = 59.4, p < 0.0001, η2p = 0.47], a significant main effect of poke type [F(1, 9) = 79.0, p < 0.0001, η2p = 0.75], and a significant interaction between period and poke type [F(1, 9) = 142.5, p < 0.0001, η2p = 0.55]. Test mice significantly increased their active nose-poking behavior during the first 3 days of the social stimulus period, compared to the prior 3 days, when the adjacent cage was empty [t(9) = 11.53, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 3.65, paired t test] (Figures 1E and 1F). This demonstrates both task acquisition and the ability to quantify social motivation.

Identification of social seeking vs. exploration trials

Two time-of-flight (ToF) proximity sensors were used to measure the latency from the Test mouse nose-poke to the Test and Stimulus mice approaching the door (Figures 2A and 2B, and S1). Test mice approached the door within 12 s on most trials (91.3%; mean latency = 2.84 s), which were classified as “Seeking” trials (Figures 2A, 2B, and 3A). Trials in which the Test mouse did not approach the door within 12 s were classified as “Exploring” trials (Figures 2A and 2B). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of period [F(2, 18) = 47.4, p < 0.0001, η2p = 0.56], a significant main effect of trial type [F(1, 9) = 256.8, p < 0.0001, η2p = 0.86], and a significant interaction between period and trial type [F(2, 18) = 68.6, p < 0.0001, η2p = 0.58]. Test mice exhibited a significant increase in Seeking trials when Stimulus mice were present [t(9) = 11.0, p < 0.0001, Hedges’ g = 3.81, pairwise post hoc test with Holm-Bonferroni correction] in the originally empty cage condition, with a gradual decline across the extinction phase [t(9) = −7.67, p < 0.0001, Hedges' g = 1.98] (Figures 2C and 2D). In contrast, the number of Exploring trials was not impacted by the presence of the Stimulus mouse (Figures 2C and 2D; Figure S2A and S2B). A social interaction was defined as the presence of both the Test and Stimulus mice at the door as detected by the proximity sensor. For “Seeking” trials, social interactions lasting ≥1 s within the 12-s reward window were categorized as “Reciprocal,” whereas trials in which the Test mouse approached but the Stimulus mouse did not engage were classified as “Nonreciprocal” (Figure 3A; Videos S1 and S2). The daily rhythm of social seeking is delayed relative to the feeding rhythm

We quantified the daily rhythms of feeding and social seeking behaviors in the task simultaneously. The acrophase (timing of the peak) of the social seeking rhythm was delayed by 1.96 ± 0.26 h relative to the feeding rhythm (N = 14, T = 0.0, p < 0.0005, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) (Figures 4A and 4B), suggesting that mice prioritize caloric needs before initiating social engagement. Consistent with this idea, individual mice also shifted their feeding rhythms 1.16 ± 0.32 h earlier when the target cage contained a Stimulus mouse, compared to when the same mouse was tested in the empty cage condition (N = 11, T = 5.0, p < 0.01) (Figures 4C and 4D). Dopaminergic impacts on social seeking

We tested the sensitivity of our behavioral assay to pharmacological manipulation of dopamine signaling, which has been implicated in motivated behavior and social reward processing.16,17,18 We first tested the ability of SCH23390, a D1 receptor antagonist, and haloperidol, a D2 receptor antagonist, on locomotor suppression. To minimize confounding reductions in movement, we selected a dose of 0.3 mg/kg for both compounds, as these doses had minimal effects on locomotion (Figures S4A–S4D). One-way Friedman ANOVAs did not reveal overall effects of drug treatment on social seeking [χ2(2) = 3.37, p = 0.185; Figure 5B]. Although the overall Friedman test was not significant, exploratory Wilcoxon tests (Holm-Bonferroni corrected) showed significant reductions in social seeking following both SCH23390 (T = 12.5, p < 0.05) and haloperidol (T = 20.0, p < 0.05). Because the overall ANOVA for social seeking was not significant, these post-hoc results should be interpreted with caution, but they suggest that systemic antagonists of either D1 or D2 receptors reduce social seeking in this task. We also did not observe a significant effect of drug treatment on exploring behavior [χ2(2) = 1.76, p = 0.42] and no significant effects of either drug vs. saline with exploratory Wilcoxon post-hoc tests (SCH23390: T = 21.5, p = 0.303; Haloperidol: T = 20.0, p = 0.267; Figure 5C). We detected a significant effect of drug treatment on median approach latency [N = 14, χ2(2) = 16.74, p < 0.0005], with both SCH23390 (T = 15.0, p = 0.033) and haloperidol (T = 0.0, p < 0.0005) increasing the time required for Test mice to approach the door (Figures S4E and S4F). This increase in latency could reflect drug-induced slowing of movement rather than a failure to initiate approach. However, while we observed a significant overall effect of drug treatment on activity levels [χ2(2) = 9.85, p < 0.01; Figure 5D], this effect was driven selectively by haloperidol (T = 0.0, p < 0.0005), with no significant reduction in activity following SCH23390 treatment (T = 39.0, p = 0.68). This argues that for SCH23390, the increased latency did not reflect a change in movement but likely reflects a reduction in social seeking. Finally, we observed a significant effect of drug treatment on feeding [χ2(2) = 10.86, p < 0.005; Figure 5E], which was again selective to haloperidol (T = 3.0, p < 0.005) and not SCH23390 (T = 38.0, p = 0.39). Overall, the pattern is consistent with a selective reduction in social seeking from the D1 antagonist SCH23390 and a non-selective reduction in all measured behaviors from the D2-antagonist haloperidol.

Discussion

This study introduces a homecage social operant task that complements existing tests of social motivation. Our system consists of a battery-powered, sliding door positioned between two cages that can be opened by the Test mouse with a nose-poke. When the door opens, it allows limited social interaction (visual sensing and sniffing through a steel grate) between the Test mouse and the Stimulus mouse in the neighboring cage. By testing mice in their home environments, our system minimizes stress, reduces experimenter intervention, and enables continuous monitoring of social behavior over weeks. Mice learned to nose-poke to open the door within one day, as evidenced by a significant increase in operant responding on the first day that a mouse was introduced to the neighboring cage vs. when the neighboring cage was empty. This finding is consistent with prior work demonstrating that rodents are motivated to work for social access.7,10,11

We believe our homecage approach complements more traditional social operant paradigms but also improves on them. In typical social approach and operant paradigms, stimulus animals are often confined within small chambers that may artificially constrain or force social proximity. In such contexts, Test animals may initiate interaction not due to genuine social motivation but in response to distress cues or escape attempts from the stimulus animal, raising the possibility that social contact initiated by the test animals reflects empathic or rescue-driven responses rather than voluntary social engagement. Our paradigm differs by allowing the Stimulus animals free movement within an adjacent cage, enabling voluntary interaction and minimizing confounding stress responses. Video tracking provides a valuable approach for quantifying social behaviors in rodent operant tasks.7,19 However, due to the length of our experiments (1–2 weeks), this analysis of social interactions would have been challenging with video-based analyses. Our paradigm also included ToF proximity sensors that allowed us to classify the behavior of the Test and Stimulus mice on each trial over multiple days without video. The proximity sensors also allowed us to measure the latency from nose-poke to approaching the social door for interaction. This revealed that the majority of Seeking trials (84.5%) occurred within 5 s of the nose-poke. This sharp concentration of early approaches suggests that rapid approach behavior reflects a strong social motivation signal in most events, whereas the long right tail likely reflects variability in internal state or competing behaviors. The ToF sensors also revealed that trials where the Test mouse never approached the door (“No approach,” 8.7% of total events) indicate that some nose-pokes were unrelated to social motivation, potentially arising from exploratory poking. Another benefit of our homecage paradigm is to measure the daily rhythms of social motivation and compare them with other behaviors because our system can operate continuously for 24 h for over a week. This revealed that social seeking follows a daily rhythm that peaks approximately 2 hours after the peak of the feeding rhythm. Interestingly, adding a social partner also shifted the timing of feeding behavior earlier by ∼1 h relative to the rhythms observed in the same mice without a social partner, without altering total pellet consumption (Figure S5).

Several researchers have linked social motivation to the dopamine system. For instance, multiple researchers have found that the activity of dopamine neurons increases while animals are performing social behaviors.16,17,18,20,21 In a critical finding, Solié et al. used a social operant task to determine that ventral tegmental area dopamine (VTADA) neurons encode a “social prediction error,” which can guide learning about social outcomes,21 complementing a large literature showing “reward prediction errors” for many distinct outcomes in VTADA neurons.22 This work has also led to understanding the molecular underpinnings of dopamine function and how these mechanisms can be altered in ASD. For instance, ASD mouse models with a deficiency in SHANK3 protein have reduced VTADA neuron activity, which was linked to social deficits.7,18 We were motivated by this prior literature to test if pharmacological manipulation of the dopamine system could alter operant behavior in the homecage social operant test. Consistent with the prior work on this topic, we found that D1 receptor blockade via systemic SCH23390 administration selectively impaired social seeking without affecting feeding or general activity levels. In contrast, D2 receptor blockade via systemic haloperidol administration suppressed social behavior, but also feeding and activity levels, consistent with its broader motor-suppressive effects. Both drugs also increased the Test mice’s latency to approach the door relative to saline, reflecting a general drug-induced slowing of behavior. Importantly, this locomotor slowing does not affect our classification of Seeking versus Exploring trials, because mice had the full 12-s reward window in which to reach the door. Thus, even with increased approach latencies, socially motivated animals were still able to reach the interaction zone in time to be classified as Seeking. The observed reductions in social seeking under drug treatment, therefore, cannot be attributed to slowed movement alone but instead reflect genuine decreases in social motivation. These findings are consistent with reports that SCH23390 selectively impairs social learning and social interaction without disrupting feeding.23,24 Haloperidol, on the other hand, exerted more widespread effects on social behavior,25 locomotion, and feeding.26 A limitation of these experiments is that systemic drug administration lacks regional and receptor-type selectivity. While dopaminergic receptors in the nucleus accumbens are known to play key roles in social approach and motivational drive,20 systemic delivery likely influences multiple brain regions and receptor populations beyond this target. Investigating circuit and cell-type-specific effects using this paradigm will be an important future direction.

Together, these findings demonstrate that the homecage social operant task is a powerful and versatile tool for investigating social motivation under naturalistic and low-stress conditions. Its compatibility with long-term, continuous monitoring makes it especially well suited for studies involving neuromodulation, circadian dynamics, and individual variability in social behavior. In addition to replicating and extending key findings from traditional assays, this paradigm offers new insights into the temporal structure and neurochemical basis of social behavior. Moving forward, this system can be adapted to test developmental trajectories, sex differences, disease models, and circuit-specific manipulations, opening new avenues for research into the biological foundations of social interaction and their disruption in neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions.

Limitations of the study

There are limitations to our study. First, we chose C57BL/6J mice to validate this new behavioral paradigm because they are the most widely used in neuroscience research27 and serve as the background strain for many genetically modified models of human disease. Although strains such as CD-1 show greater social preference12, we aimed to avoid complications from increased aggressive tendencies that could confound the interpretation of social motivation. However, it is possible that our results with C57BL/6J will not generalize to other strains of mice. Second, we did not test the impact of higher-effort behavior on social seeking. The single nose-poke we use is a relatively low-effort response. Most relevant to our current results, dopamine signaling seems to be most important for high-effort ratios on operant tasks.28 As such, future studies that increase the nose-poke requirement (e.g., FR5 or FR10) may reveal greater effects of dopamine manipulations on social seeking. Third, while both sexes were used in all experiments, we were underpowered to assess sex differences in social motivation. As our primary goal was to validate our device and model, this will require future experiments to assess. Similarly, in our current study, we used same-sex partners to remove any potential influence of reproduction- and mating-related behaviors. Future studies will examine whether opposite-sex interactions evoke distinct patterns of social seeking. Fourth, the social-door module requires drilling holes in the cage to allow mice to interact and a “DIY” building of the device. While this approach allows for flexibility, it may be challenging for researchers who are not accustomed to fabricating their own devices. It is worth noting that all electronic components can be fully removed for cleaning, and cages can be sanitized using standard procedures. Related, we also used an FED3 device in this experiment to record operant effort and trigger door opening. However, the system is not dependent on FED3 and can be triggered with alternative input formats, such as capacitive touch sensors.29 Finally, our experiments were performed in single-housed mice, as there are technical and conceptual challenges with assessing operant social seeking in group-housed animals. However, it would be interesting to test in the future if group-housed mice in our paradigm would be similarly motivated to seek social interactions with a Stimulus mouse, or a group of Stimulus mice, in the neighboring cage.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal models

C57BL/6J (JAX: 000664) male and female mice were bred in-house. Upon weaning at postnatal day (P)21, mice were group-housed according to sex. All mice were housed on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:00 a.m.) with room temperature (∼20°C–22°C) and relative humidity (∼50%). During testing, both test and stimulus animals were single-housed in ventilated (28.5 × 17.5 × 12 cm) translucent plastic cages with corncob bedding, nestlet squares, food pellets (Dustless Precision Pellets, Rodent, Grain-Based, 20 mg; Bio-Serv, Product #F0163), and water provided ad libitum. The bedding was changed when necessary, with a maximum time of 14 days between cage changes. Weights were recorded daily between 2 and 3 p.m. All behavioral tasks were conducted by a female experimenter. All procedures were approved by and performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Washington University in St. Louis and conformed to NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

In the validation experiment, we used two independent cohorts of mice, which were run serially to accommodate equipment availability. The first cohort contained 9 test mice (4 males and 5 females, postnatal day 50–55) and 9 same-sex stimulus mice (P53–56 when introduced) in the first cohort. The second cohort contained 6 test mice (3 males and 3 females, P50–55) and 6 same-sex stimulus mice (P48–60 when introduced) in the second cohort.

In the dopamine antagonist experiment, we also used two independent cohorts of mice. The first cohort contained 7 test mice (4 males and 3 females, 11–14 weeks old) and 7 same-sex stimulus mice (P85 when introduced) in the first cohort. The second cohort contained 10 test mice (5 males and 5 females, mostly 15–16 weeks old, one female was 20 weeks old) and 10 same-sex stimulus mice (mostly 18–20 weeks old, one female was 22 weeks old) in the second cohort. Before Experiment 2, 8 mice (4 males and 4 females, 14 weeks old) were used in the pilot test to determine the appropriate dosage of dopamine receptor antagonists.

Method details

Homecage social operant assay

The social homecage operant paradigm consisted of three components: 1) a pair of home cages (28.5 × 17.5 × 12 cm), 2) a custom social door, and 3) a FED3 device. A 5.4cm hole was drilled into each homecage, and the social door was installed between the cages to control social interactions between the cages (Figures 1A–1D; Figure S1). FED3 is a smart pellet dispensing device that includes two nosepoke ports for mice to interact with and a pellet dispenser to quantify patterns of food intake.15 FED3 was programmed to deliver a 500-ms 3V pulse to the social door each time the Test mouse maintained a nose poke on the active port for at least 200 ms. This pulse triggered the social door to open for 12s (Figure 1D). The other port was inactive, and a poke within did not result in an outcome. The mouse that controlled the door opening via FED3 was termed the “Test” mouse, and the mouse in the target cage was termed the “Stimulus” mouse. To minimize the risk of injury, the door also made several small movements to warn the mice before it closed. Once open, the Test and Stimulus mice could interact through a stainless-steel grate that was perforated with five 1-cm holes, which enabled visual contact and limited physical interactions (e.g., nose touching and sniffing), but did not allow the mice to cross between the cages. Two Time-of-Flight (ToF) proximity sensors were used to track the behavior of the Test and Stimulus mice while the door was open (Figure 1D). Nosepokes, feeding events, door openings, and proximity data were stored on SD cards and retrieved after the experiment.

Assembly of the social door

The social door system consisted of a 3D-printed frame and sliding door, a servo motor (Feetech FS90R), a PCF8523 real-time clock (RTC, Adafruit 5189), a limit switch (HiLetgo 3-01-1546), two VL6180× ToF proximity sensors (Adafruit 3316), and a 4400 mAh rechargeable battery (Adafruit 356). Arrangement of components is shown in Figure 1A and Figure S1A. A custom printed circuit board (PCB) connected these components to an Adafruit Feather M0 Adalogger (Adafruit 2796), which controlled door movement and interfaced with the sensors and an OLED screen module (Adafruit 4650) to display real-time information such as battery level, proximity values, and system status (Figure S1B and S1C). A portion of the sliding panel was covered with a laser-cut perforated stainless steel plate through which mice could interact (five centered holes with a diameter of 1 cm, Figures 1A and 1B). A 5.4 cm-diameter hole was drilled into the side of the test and stimulus cages to provide an interaction port for the door. Magnets were used to connect and align the door to the two cages.

Experimental timeline

Test mice were single-housed for 5 days with an empty cage as a stimulus (Empty cage baseline), and then a Stimulus mouse was introduced in the neighboring cage (Social stimulus period) for 9 days (with a novel mouse introduced every 3 days), followed by four days without the stimulus partner (Extinction period). Five days of empty-cage exposure were included to provide a within-subject baseline and ensure enough time for mice to learn to eat from the FED3 device.

Quantification of daily rhythms of feeding

Feeding was assessed concurrently with social behavior using the FED3 device.15 Pellets were dispensed on a “free feeding” schedule, such that the pellet (20mg grain-based pellet, BioServ) was replaced each time it was removed, and FED3 logged times of each pellet retrieval on its internal SD card for later analysis.

Quantification of daily rhythms of locomotion

Locomotor activity was continuously monitored using battery-powered in-cage motion sensors (Pallidus MR1). These sensors use a passive infrared (PIR) sensor to detect locomotion in the cage and upload it to a cloud database using LoRaWAN. Data was viewed on the cloud, and raw data was downloaded for analysis.

Administration of dopamine receptor antagonists

Mice were single-housed and linked to an empty cage for four days (Figure 5A). During this time, they received saline injections on Days 2 and 4 to acclimate them to the injection procedure. A social partner was introduced on Day 5, followed by randomized administration of saline or dopaminergic antagonists every 2 days. D1 receptor antagonist SCH23390 (0.3mg/kg SC; Abcam Cat# ab120597) and the D2 receptor antagonist haloperidol (0.3mg/kg SC; Abcam Cat# ab120080) were injected 1.5 h before dark onset, and behavior was quantified during the first four hours of the dark cycle. Dosages were determined in pilot tests using single-housed mice monitored with motion sensors, optimizing doses that caused minimal reductions in locomotion (Figures S4A and S4D). On Days 6, 8, and 10, mice received randomized injections of saline, SCH23390, or haloperidol. Treatment order was counterbalanced: 5 mice received saline → SCH23390 → haloperidol; 5 mice received SCH23390 → haloperidol → saline; and 4 mice received haloperidol → saline → SCH23390.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Sample sizes were determined based on pilot studies and subsequent power analysis. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups with randomized drug injection orders. Behavioral analysis and figure plotting were conducted with Python 3.13 using Visual Studio Code. Data are presented as histograms, polar plots, boxplots, or mean ± SEM plots with “N” representing the number of animals and “n” representing the number of trials. In boxplots, the center line indicates the median, and the box edges represent the 25th and 75th percentiles. Daily rhythms of behavior (seeking, exploring, feeding, activity) were analyzed using cosine fitting to extract acrophase.30 All analyses were conducted under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 6:00 a.m.). Jupyter notebooks containing analysis code are provided at Zenodo at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18748341.

Data distributions were examined for the fit of parametric assumptions. Statistical tests included repeated measures ANOVAs, two-sided paired t-tests, post hoc pairwise t-tests, Friedman tests, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons, where appropriate. Asterisks on the figures indicate statistical significance: p < 0.05 (∗), p < 0.01 (∗∗), p < 0.001 (∗∗∗), and p < 0.0001 (∗∗∗∗).

Data exclusions

Overall, 32 mice were tested for social motivation in these studies, and data from 8 (25%) were excluded from analysis. These included data from 5 mice (3 males and 2 females) in our validation experiments and from 3 mice (2 males and 1 female) in the dopamine antagonist experiments. The reasons for exclusion included device failures of the social door or failure of the mouse to use the FED3 device to obtain food. In addition, data from specific days of a few animals were excluded due to temporary device failures. Notably, the five excluded mice from the validation experiment were part of the first testing cohort, during which the newly assembled device system experienced initial hardware issues that were identified and resolved by the second partner-swap phase. No device issues occurred in the subsequent cohort.
