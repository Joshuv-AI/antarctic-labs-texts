Adipocytes in cancer a metabolic paradox and the dawn of novel therapeutics
Authors: Zhuoran Fang, Danni Zhu, Teng Yang, Ruolan Zhang, Qianyu Zhao, Qing Wei, Xiangdong Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, the dual regulatory role of adipocytes in the tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a critical focus in cancer research. Advances in the fields of metabolic biology and tumor immunology have shown that adipocytes play a crucial role in tumor occurrence, progression, and treatment outcome by secreting adipokines, regulating metabolic reprogramming, and mediating immunosuppressive networks. This article systematically reviews the physiological functions of adipose tissue and its intricate crosstalk with cancer cells, with particular emphasis on dual metabolic and immune mechanisms by which adipocytes dynamically modulate tumor progression within the TME. Furthermore, by integrating preclinical research and translational medicine evidence, a precise treatment framework targeting the interaction between fat and tumor is proposed, providing theoretical basis for the development of metabolic immune combination therapy.

Introduction

The epidemiological characteristics of obesity related cancers reveal the importance of adipocytes in tumor biology. According to data from the World Health Organization, about 40% of cancer cases worldwide are associated with metabolic abnormalities, among which abnormal proliferation of adipose tissue is significantly positively correlated with the risk of 13 types of malignant tumors (Fig. 1) [1]. This phenomenon suggests that adipocytes are not only energy stores, but also key regulators of the tumors. In recent years, research has confirmed that adipocytes mainly affect tumor progression through the following dual mechanisms: (1) oncogenic mechanism: secreting pro-inflammatory factors such as leptin and IL-6 to drive epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT), and promoting tumor metabolic reprogramming through lipid transfer; reshaping the TME to promote tumor occurrence, development, and metastasis; weakening treatment-related metabolic effects, forming metabolic barriers, assisting tumors in resisting chemotherapy and immunotherapy [2–5] ; (2)Anti-cancer mechanism: healthy adipose tissue secretes protective factors such as IGFBP2 to inhibit tumor invasion; forming physiological barriers to resist tumor invasion; reshaping the systemic immune metabolism microenvironment to suppress tumor progression [6, 7]. Analyzing the molecular basis of this double-edged sword effect may help inform the development of innovative strategies for metabolic intervention combined with immunotherapy.While prior studies have independently addressed the metabolic and immunoregulatory roles of tumor-associated adipocytes, this review unifies the underlying dual regulatory axis of metabolism and immunity. It incorporates recent findings from single-cell spatial multi-omics analyses, outlines a targeted therapeutic framework centered on adipocyte-tumor crosstalk, and offers fresh theoretical references to guide subsequent exploration of metabolic-immune combined intervention strategies.

Adipocyte subtype diversity and dynamic regulatory networks in metabolic physiology and pathology

The functional diversity of adipocytes in the physiological environment is closely related to their biological heterogeneity. In recent years, breakthrough advances in single-cell transcriptomics and spatial metabolomics techniques have revealed the specific functional differentiation of adipocyte subsets in tumor development and progression. Several important adipocytes, including white adipocytes (WA), brown adipocytes (BA), beige adipocytes (BeA), and bone marrow adipocytes (BMAds)(Fig. 2A), exhibit significant functional polarization in different physiological environment [8].Adipose tissue can be mainly divided into four types based on its characteristics and functions.

White adipocytes (WA)

White adipose tissue is the predominant adipose tissue type in humans, accounting for 88%-90% of total adipocytes in adults, predominantly distributed in subcutaneous and visceral regions, where it functions to store excess energy in the form of triglycerides. In obesity, the volume or number of adipocytes will increase, which is closely related to diabetes, fatty liver and other diseases.Beyond its physiological metabolic roles, white adipocytes also act as the primary cellular source of cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) within the tumor microenvironment. Robust lipolysis, sustained lipid secretion and abundant pro-inflammatory factor release from WA collectively drive tumor metabolic reprogramming, local immune suppression and the emergence of chemoresistance, marking WA as a core pro-tumor adipocyte subtype [4].

Brown adipocytes (BA)

It accounts for a very low proportion of total adipocytes, about 0.2% -3.0%, Their main function is to produce heat and maintain energy balance in the body. Brown adipose tissue is rich in mitochondria, which consume energy through non shivering heat production (NST) and help maintain body temperature, especially in cold environment. Unlike white adipose tissue, brown adipose tissue converts chemical energy into thermal energy through the mediation mechanism of UCP1 (uncoupling protein 1), thereby increasing energy consumption [9]. Distinct from the pro-tumor property of white adipocytes, BA exhibits prominent tumor-suppressive potency. It competes with malignant cells for limited glucose and nutrient resources, reshapes mitochondrial metabolic patterns, and strengthens local anti-tumor immune competence. Therapeutic activation of brown adipocytes via cold exposure or pharmacological intervention has thus emerged as a viable metabolic strategy for tumor intervention [13].

Beige adipocyte (BeA)

Also called as “Brite fat”, is a special type of fat discovered in recent years. It is sporadically present in white fat, accounting for about 1%. Their characteristics are dynamic plasticity: they resemble white fat in a resting state, storing energy; they can activate “browning” under cold, exercise, and other stimuli, consuming energy through heat production. (Fig. 2B) Although some studies suggest that it belongs to a subtype of white fat, beige fat is gradually being recognized as an independent functional fat category due to its distinct activation mechanism and therapeutic potential, such as improving metabolic diseases such as obesity and insulin resistance [10, 11]. Emerging evidence further reveals the vital involvement of beige adipocytes in breast cancer progression, with regulatory effects covering tumor growth, migration and metastatic dissemination. These cells actually perform dual and context-dependent regulatory roles within cancer lesions. Paracrine signaling derived from BeA facilitates breast cancer cell proliferation and invasive behavior, while thermogenically activated beige adipocytes restrain tumor growth via metabolic competition. Such unique phenotypic plasticity endows BeA with a distinctive regulatory status in modulating tumor progression [13–16].

Bone marrow adipocytes (BMAds)

BMAds account for over 10% of body fat.It is traditionally believed to only fill the bone marrow cavity. But with the deepening of research, the functions involved in bone metabolism regulation and hematopoietic support have been discovered, and in recent years, it has been regarded as a metabolic organ that can respond to nutritional challenges and secrete cytokines. At the same time, researchers found that there is a bidirectional interaction between bone marrow adipocytes (BMAds) and myeloma cells: bone marrow adipocytes promote the survival and chemotherapy resistance of myeloma cells by releasing cytokines and fatty acids, while myeloma cells induce metabolic abnormalities and senescence associated secretory phenotypes (SASP) of bone marrow adipocytes, jointly forming a microenvironment that supports tumor progression [12, 17, 18]. These different types of adipose tissue play important roles in the human body, from energy storage and temperature regulation to metabolic regulation and control of inflammatory responses. A deeper understanding of the characteristics of these adipose tissues can help us gain a more comprehensive understanding of the dual regulatory role of adipocytes in the TME [19].

Endocrine and metabolic functions of adipocytes

Adipocytes, as highly active metabolic and endocrine units, achieve systematic regulation by secreting adipokines. Leptin, adiponectin, and resistin are hormone like proteins specifically secreted by adipocytes, regulating energy metabolism and inflammatory response. The leptin (16 kDa peptide) secreted by subcutaneous adipocytes reflects energy reserve status and plays a key role in regulating energy balance and metabolic homeostasis. Its expression is positively regulated by insulin, glucocorticoids, and C/EBP α. The impaired leptin function is closely related to the pathogenesis of obesity, diabetes and other metabolic diseases [20, 21]. Adiponectin, as a multifunctional protein, plays an important role in enhancing insulin sensitivity, promoting fatty acid oxidation, and reducing blood glucose and ceramide levels. Its receptor exhibits potential for anti lipotoxicity and anti-fibrosis by activating the PPAR α/γ and AMPK signaling pathways [22].

Adipocytes are not only victims of metabolic disorders, but also core regulators actively involved in disease progression through multiple metabolic pathways [23–26]. An increasing number of studies indicate that lipid metabolism in adipocytes exhibits dynamic plasticity. Zhou’s team revealed that the CIDE-ATGL-PPAR α pathway mediates the coordinated regulation of energy metabolism by lipid droplets, peroxisomes, and mitochondria, and its dysfunction leads to ectopic lipid deposition; The Seipin gene maintains mitochondrial metabolism through calcium homeostasis, providing intervention targets for lipid malnutrition [27]. At the level of glucose metabolism, Chen Y.et al. found that IRE1 α inhibits the expression of thermogenic genes by degrading PGC1 α mRNA, revealing the molecular association between endoplasmic reticulum stress and adipocyte metabolic remodeling. It is worth noting that adipocytes recruit macrophages to infiltrate through the secretion of chemokines such as MCP-1, and form a positive inflammatory feedback loop dominated by TNF α/IL-6. This process is closely related to the abnormal activation of NF - κB pathway and the imbalance of autophagy lipolysis coupling, which ultimately aggravates insulin resistance and atherosclerosis [28].

Adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs): origin and contribution from adipocytes

Adipose tissue consists of mature adipocytes alongside a diverse and heterogeneous stromal cell compartment. Within this microenvironment, ASCs stand out as essential progenitor cells endowed with potent multidirectional differentiation capacity. Mature adipocytes are capable of dedifferentiating into ASCs, a biological process that serves as a major cellular source of ASCs in both normal physiological tissues and tumor-resident adipose depots. Such adipocyte-to-ASC conversion is markedly intensified within the tumor microenvironment. Cytokines released by malignant cells erode the inherent phenotypic features of adipocytes and drive the acquisition of stem-like traits, expanding the local ASC reservoir to fuel tumor development. Originating from adipocyte lineage with dynamic cellular plasticity, ASCs mediate extensive metabolic exchange and signal communication between adipose tissue and cancer cells, establishing a solid cellular basis for adipose-driven tumor modulation [29–31].

Adipocytes participate in immune regulation

Adipocytes participate in immune regulation. Serving as multifunctional metabolic-immune hubs, adipocytes orchestrate tissue homeostasis by acting on three pivotal immune cell populations including regulatory T (Treg) cells, group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), and macrophages through unique secretory and metabolic pathways.

Accumulating evidence has validated that adipocytes are capable of actively sculpting the local immune microenvironment via secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α as well as chemokines like CCL2 [32], which further guide immune cell recruitment and polarization within adipose tissue. Distinct phenotypic and functional Treg cell subsets with prominent sexual dimorphism reside in the visceral adipose tissue (VAT) of mice. The adipose microenvironment in male individuals facilitates androgen-dependent IL-33 secretion, which in turn boosts the differentiation and immunosuppressive capacity of ST2⁺ Treg cells, while IFN-γ signaling prevails in females to drive CXCR3⁺ Treg cell formation and maintain the balance between metabolic homeostasis and inflammatory status [30]. Such sexual dimorphism mirrors the adaptive characteristics of Treg cells responding to visceral adipose tissue and systemic metabolic alterations. Under metabolic stress induced by high-fat conditions, free fatty acids and oxidative stress byproducts released from adipocytes directly reshape the transcriptional profile of Treg cells via PPARγ inhibition and T-bet activation, thereby facilitating CXCR3⁺ Treg expansion to accommodate chronic inflammatory conditions [33].

Adipose tissue constitutes a major reservoir of ILC2s, a specialized innate immune subset whose activation and homeostasis are sustained by adipocyte-derived signaling cues [34]. IL-33, a pivotal cytokine abundantly secreted by adipose tissue, is recognized as the canonical trigger for ILC2 activation [35]. Highly enriched within adipose compartments, ILC2s are irreplaceable for maintaining local immune stability, metabolic adaption and regional inflammatory responses [36].

A core signaling axis is constructed by adipocyte-derived chemokines represented by CCL2 and pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, which potently drive macrophage infiltration into adipose tissue. Reciprocal crosstalk between these cell types builds a self-sustaining inflammatory loop, linking adipose metabolic dysfunction to persistent systemic low-grade inflammation. Beyond classical immune modulation, adipocytes secrete epidermal growth factor analogs such as amphiregulin (AREG); signaling transduction through the EGFR axis facilitates tissue repair and restrains aberrant immune activation, protecting tissues from unrestrained inflammatory injury. This dynamic metabolic-immune crosstalk collectively positions adipocytes as central regulatory hubs that bridge energy imbalance, immune dysregulation and end-organ damage in obesity-associated disorders [33].

Distinctions between normal adipose tissue and tumor-associated adipose tissue

Given that the morphological and functional characteristics of normal adipose tissue have been elaborated in the preceding context, distinct phenotypic and functional reprogramming can be distinctly observed within tumor-associated adipose tissue (TAAT), which evolves into a specialized pro-tumorigenic niche. Cancer-associated adipocytes (CAAs) in this microenvironment present shrunken cell morphology and depleted lipid droplets, coupled with fibroblast-like transformation. Excessive lipolysis is triggered, and abundant free fatty acids are released to sustain tumor progression [37, 38]. A unique secretory signature is also shaped in TAAT, with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), chemokines (CCL2, CCL5) and pro-angiogenic factors (VEGF, PDGF-AA), along with diminished adiponectin secretion. Such molecular alterations induce extensive infiltration of immunosuppressive CD163 + M2 macrophages, and crown-like structures are formed surrounding necrotic adipocytes, jointly constructing an immune-suppressive microenvironment [39, 40]. Obvious extracellular matrix fibrosis and vigorous lymphatic vessel remodeling are likewise manifested in TAAT. Deviating from the homeostatic properties of normal adipose tissue, TAAT acts as a pivotal pro-tumor niche to facilitate cancer cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis and therapeutic resistance [37, 40].

Role in tumor development: a double-edged sword of “promoting cancer” and “inhibiting cancer” of adipocytes

Cancer driver potential: metabolic imbalance and immune reprogramming

Obesity related adipocytes often exhibit a significant pro-cancer tendency in the initial stage of tumors, and their core mechanism is generally manifested as the synergistic effect of lipid overload and chronic inflammation. At the beginning, it is generally a genome-wide instability mediated by lipid toxicity, which is usually manifested as the release of excessive free fatty acids (FFA) through abnormal lipolysis in the state of metabolic imbalance of adipocytes, directly disrupting cellular homeostasis and driving malignant transformation [41]. CD36, as a fatty acid transmembrane transporter, plays a central role in the lipid uptake pathway mediated by certain tumors: it can promote the uptake of exogenous fatty acids by tumor cells to meet metabolic needs. For example, in gastric cancer, CD36 mediates fatty acid induced metastasis through the AKT/GSK-3 β/β - catenin signaling pathway. Inhibition of CD36 significantly reduces tumor growth and metastasis. At the same time, it enhances endogenous fatty acid uptake by interacting with adipocytes in the TME, further accelerating tumor progression. Related studies on cervical cancer have shown that CD36 can upregulate SRC kinase and downstream ERK1/2 pathway, promoting tumor cell proliferation [42–45].Further research has found that the mitochondrial fatty acid β - oxidation rate limiting enzyme CPT1A plays a critical role in the fatty acid oxidation pathway. Its sustained upregulation can lead to an imbalance in the NAD+/NADH ratio in precancerous colorectal cancer cells, activate members of the histone deacetylase (HDAC) family (such as HDAC1/3), induce high methylation of CpG islands in the APC promoter region of tumor suppressor genes, and thus form a malignant positive feedback loop of “metabolic reprogramming epigenetic silencing“ [46–48].

ASCs exhibit tight intrinsic biological affinity and cellular connectivity with adipocytes in adipose tissue. Far beyond immune regulatory functions, ASCs exert extensive pro-tumor effects through a spectrum of non-immunomodulatory mechanisms, which aggravate metabolic dysfunction and stromal remodeling within the tumor microenvironment. Metabolically, ASCs leverage the CD36 axis to actively transfer fatty acids, lactate and core metabolic substrates to cancer cells, markedly enhancing tumor lipid uptake and fatty acid oxidation to sustain malignant proliferation and cell survival. Driving extracellular matrix remodeling, ASCs differentiate into cancer-associated fibroblasts and secrete collagens, MMPs and fibronectin, triggering stromal desmoplasia and endowing tumor cells with stronger invasive and metastatic capacity. Tumor angiogenesis gains robust support from ASCs as well; these cells release VEGF and multiple angiogenic mediators to accelerate neovascularization, ensuring adequate nutrient and oxygen provision for sustained tumor growth. ASCs also upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins and induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition in cancer cells, collectively blunting tumor sensitivity to chemotherapy and targeted therapy [49–52].

In the TME, the mechanism by which adipocytes reshape the immunosuppressive pattern by secreting specific cytokines has gradually become clear. Cancer-associated adipocytes (CAA) and ASCs directly inhibit effector T cell function and induce regulatory T cell (Treg) expansion by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as IL-10 and TGF-β ASCs possess bidirectional immunomodulatory properties that remodel the immunosuppressive landscape of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Established insights into stromal immune regulatory patterns illustrate that ASCs secrete IL-10, TGF-β, and CXCL12 to constrain CD8 + cytotoxic T cell activity, expand regulatory T cells (Tregs), and drive macrophage polarization toward the pro-tumor M2 phenotype [53]. Hidden within ASC biological characteristics lies a therapeutically meaningful immune-potentiating function: targeted clearance of ASC-originated cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) greatly elevates intratumoral infiltration of CD8 + T cells and B cells. This intervention reverses the immune-desert phenotype of tumor tissues, and works in synergy with anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade to inhibit pancreatic tumor growth and limit liver metastatic progression [54]. Bearing this dual regulatory capability, ASCs act as pivotal intermediate modulators that bridge adipose stromal components with tumor immune escape and treatment responsiveness.At the same time, cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-4 produced by it activate the STAT3/STAT6 signaling pathway, driving macrophages to polarize towards M2 subtypes (including M2a, M2b, and M2d), forming a tumor promoting immunosuppressive microenvironment. Of note, tumor-associated adipose tissue (TAAT) constitutes a specialized, immune-permissive niche that supports the colonization and persistence of tumor-associated macrophages [55]; moreover, adipocyte necrosis within the tumor stroma acts as a potent pro-inflammatory stimulus, further driving macrophage recruitment and accumulation into the peritumoral microenvironment [56]. These M2 macrophages further secrete chemokines such as CCL18 and CCL22 to recruit Th2 cells and Tregs, and promote angiogenesis and matrix remodeling through molecules such as VEGF and MMP9. ILC2s serve as essential innate regulators for type 2 immunity, abundantly distributed in adipose tissue and triggered to activate by IL-33 released from adipocytes and the tumor microenvironment [35]. Such cells work closely with Th2 cells to mold the local type 2 immune microenvironment [57], and engage in direct interactive communication with tumor-associated macrophages to regulate immune monitoring [57–59]. Metabolic remodeling taking place in tumor and adipose tissues alters the functional characteristics of ILC2s [60], thereby establishing a vital bridge connecting metabolic activity and immune regulation, and linking adipose tissue to type 2 immune responses during cancer progression.In addition, the free fatty acids produced by adipocyte lipolysis inhibit CD8 + T cell mitochondrial function through metabolic reprogramming, synergize with chemokines CCL2/CCL5 mediated monocyte infiltration, and ultimately construct a multidimensional immune suppression network to promote tumor immune escape and treatment resistance [61–65].

Adiponectin, as a metabolic protective hormone secreted specifically by adipose tissue, plays a central role in maintaining insulin sensitivity and anti-inflammatory homeostasis. Recent studies have further revealed that this high molecular weight polymer (HMW) participates in multi-stage inhibition of tumor occurrence and development through multidimensional regulatory mechanisms. There is substantial evidence that can confirm the negative correlation between plasma adiponectin levels and the occurrence and development of colorectal adenomas, which is relatively independent of BMI and insulin resistance, although it shows relatively little specificity in the prognosis of related tumors. But we have every reason to suspect that adiponectin may be a biomarker for cancer that develops through specific pathways that may be associated with worsening prognosis, although further research is needed to verify [66–68].

Numerous studies have shown that many tumor cells can reprogram their metabolic phenotype through the Warburg Effect to enhance glycolytic activity [69–71]. In this metabolic pathway, adipocytes provide key metabolic substrates for tumors by secreting metabolic intermediates such as lactate, free fatty acids (FFA), and ketone bodies [72–74]. Mechanistic studies have revealed that adipocytes form metabolic symbiosis with tumor cells through MCT4 mediated lactate shuttle: in a low oxygen microenvironment, lactate produced by glycolysis of adipocytes is taken up by tumor cells and catalyzed by LDHA to enter the TCA cycle to replace glucose for energy supply. At the same time, NAD+regeneration promotes the pentose phosphate pathway, enhancing tumor metabolic adaptability. This process works in synergy with the release of free fatty acids from adipocytes, promoting lipid accumulation through the PPAR γ pathway and forming a positive feedback loop with VEGF driven angiogenesis, continuously maintaining tumor promoting metabolic homeostasis [75, 76].In addition, studies have shown that adipose derived ketone bodies (β - HB) can drive liver cancer metabolic reprogramming through the SUCLA2/OXCT1 axis, mainly manifested in the following two aspects: ① Succinyl CoA dual function activation - serving as a TCA cycle metabolic substrate and increasing enzyme activity by 2.3 times through succinylation modification at the OXCT1-K168 site; ② The proportion of ketone body energy supply in liver cancer cells increased from 12% to 37%,which further activates the AMPK/mTORC1 oncogenic signaling axis, corresponding to a 58% elevation in Ki67-positive proliferative cells [65]. These mechanistic findings reveal an unprecedented therapeutic strategy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by targeting ketolysis [74, 77].

Antitumor potential: physiological barriers and metabolic homeostasis

Under physiological conditions, adipose tissue exerts its role as a tumor mechanical barrier through distinct structures. Its defense mechanism is mainly reflected in: (1) the physical barrier effect mediated by matrix remodeling: the subcutaneous adipose layer, through the biomechanical gradient formed by the polar arrangement of adipocytes, cooperates with the dense extracellular matrix (ECM) rich in laminin and collagen type I/III to form a mechanical barrier that limits the invasion of in situ cancer cells [78, 79]. Zheng Y.et al. found that the physical hardness of adipocytes directly affects the invasive ability of breast cancer cells through computer simulation and in vitro experiments. When the hardness of adipocytes is high, the arrangement of their extracellular matrix (ECM) becomes denser, forming a physical barrier that significantly hinders the migration speed and penetration ability of cancer cells. For example, in the microenvironment enriched with collagen I, adipocytes increase the rigidity of ECM, forcing cancer cells to require stronger mechanical force to break through the barrier [29, 80]. (2) Regulation of paracrine signaling network: Recent research has found that healthy adipocytes can secrete insulin-like growth factor binding protein 2 (IGFBP2), which blocks cancer cell autocrine signals by chelating the pro-invasion factor IGF-II, forming an anti-invasion barrier. Studies have shown that breast adipocytes can reduce the risk of invasive ductal carcinoma progression by 40% through the IGFBP2/IGF-II axis [81–83]. In addition, the synergistic interplay between adipocytes and the basement membrane architecture represents a critical factor in tissue organization. Structural biology studies employing high-speed atomic force microscopy (HS-AFM) have revealed that Laminin-332 undergoes dynamic conformational rearrangements upon engaging integrin α6β4, thereby contributing to basement membrane assembly and hemidesmosome stabilization. Therapeutic targeting of this specific molecular interface holds promise for enhancing treatment outcomes and suppressing tumorigenesis and progression [84]. It is worth noting that adipocytes can also secrete anti-inflammatory factors (such as adiponectin) to inhibit the polarization of tumor related macrophages, and maintain matrix stability by regulating ECM metalloproteinase inhibitor (TIMP-1). The related metabolic regulation mechanism has been verified in the obesity related breast cancer model in Cell Metabolism [85, 86]. In terms of clinical translation, Xu L et al. reported that a six-gene prognostic signature related to the basement membrane (LAMC2, GPC2, AGRN, ITGA3, LAMA3, and LOXL4) effectively predicts overall survival in esophageal carcinoma and is associated with distinct immune microenvironment profiles and chemotherapeutic drug sensitivity, providing a potential tool for risk stratification and personalized therapy [87]. These findings suggest that the paracrine network of adipocytes constitutes an anti-cancer defense line through a triple mechanism of molecular chelation, mechanical barrier construction, and immune regulation, providing a theoretical basis for the development of innovative cancer prevention strategies based on lipid microenvironment regulation.

Cold stimulation can trigger a dual metabolic reprogramming effect by activating brown adipose tissue (BAT), significantly inhibiting tumor progression: ① At the systemic metabolic competition level, BAT activation significantly increases glucose uptake efficiency (PET-CT tracer SUVmax value increases by 3.1 -fold increase), leading to a glucose deficient state in the TME; ② At the level of local immune metabolism regulation, the level of adiponectin secreted by BAT was upregulated by 42%. Through the AdipoR1/AMPK signaling axis, tumor cell metabolism was regulated bidirectionally - inhibiting the expression of the key glycolytic enzyme HK2 (decreasing by 58%) while promoting the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation ability of CD8 + T cells (increasing ATP production by 1.8 -fold increase), resulting in a 37% increase in tumor infiltration density. This systemic metabolic intervention resulted in a 62% [13] reduction in the volume of mice xenograft tumor, revealing the therapeutic potential of temperature stress in regulating fat tumor metabolic interactions [13, 88, 89].

The adipose microenvironment also has a bidirectional regulatory effect on tumor angiogenesis: ① c-Kit+mesenchymal stem cells in adipose tissue activate the c-Kit signaling pathway by secreting factors such as IL-3, SDF-1, and VEGF-A, and synergistically promote tumor angiogenesis with EPCs. At the same time, tumors in the adipose microenvironment trigger lipolysis through hypoxia induced by anti angiogenic drugs (AAD), releasing free fatty acids (FFA). Tumor cells then rely on FAO metabolism, using FFA as an energy source to bypass their dependence on angiogenesis [90–92]; ② In terms of anti angiogenesis, adiponectin activates caspase-mediated apoptotic pathways in endothelial cells by triggering sequential activation of caspases-8, -9, and − 3, and potently inhibits tumor angiogenesis. Consequently, adiponectin treatment significantly suppresses primary tumor growth by reducing neovascularization and increasing tumor cell apoptosis [93, 94].Adipose-resident lymphatic vessels exert tangible impacts on malignant progression. These specialized vascular structures govern immune cell migration and metabolic waste clearance within local adipose niches, and impaired lymphatic function in this compartment drives tumor metastasis while triggering pervasive immune suppression in cancer lesions [95, 96] (Fig. 3).

Clinical translation of adipocytes: predictive and therapeutic value in cancers

Predictive value in tumor diagnosis and treatment

With the continuous deepening of research on adipocytes, an increasing number of adipocyte biomarkers have been proven to serve as tumor markers, providing predictive basis for early screening, progression assessment, and prognosis judgment of different tumors.At the 2024 San Antonio Breast Cancer Symposium (SABCS), preliminary proteomic profiling data focusing on breast tumor-surrounding adipocytes were disclosed and remain unpublished to date. These unpeer-reviewed preliminary observations reveal obvious differences in proteolytic peptide signatures between adipocytes adjacent to malignant breast tumors and those surrounding benign breast lesions. Preclinical animal evidence further indicates that aberrant expression of the MASP6 peptide facilitates breast cancer invasion and metastatic progression, while its antagonist peptide MASP6-IN-1 can reduce breast cancer lung metastasis by 73% in preclinical experimental models. Despite the lack of formal publication and peer-reviewed validation for these preliminary results, MASP6 has emerged as a compelling candidate biomarker and prospective therapeutic target, with rigorous independent confirmation and systematic clinical verification still indispensable for further translational development [97–99].

The imaging evaluation of adipose tissue demonstrates distinct and significant clinical value in the diagnosis and treatment of tumors. Advanced PET-CT and MRI techniques can accurately determine the distribution and metabolic characteristics of adipose tissue, providing a solid objective basis for tumor prognosis evaluation and treatment monitoring. Research has shown that the CT attenuation value and FDG uptake (SUV) of peritumoral visceral adipose tissue (VAT) are closely related to CD163 + macrophage infiltration and IL-6 expression, and can therefore serve as independent indicators for predicting recurrence free survival in colorectal cancer patients. The PET/CT radiomics model based on VAT can also accurately predict the expression status of Her-2 and Ki-67 in gastric cancer, providing key imaging biomarkers for targeted therapy. A series of related discoveries fully demonstrate the potential of adipose tissue imaging features in the precise diagnosis and treatment of tumors, opening up a new direction for the development of personalized treatment strategies [100–102].

At the same time, a quantitative MRI analysis based on a large population revealed that the spatial characteristics of fat distribution also had important predictive value for specific cancer risks: every increase in visceral fat (VAT) by one standard deviation (SD) increased the risk of colorectal cancer in men by 57% (HR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.29–1.92), and the risk of postmenopausal breast cancer in women by 49% (HR = 1.49, 95% CI 1.22–1.81); Elevated abdominal subcutaneous fat (ASAT) is significantly associated with the risk of endometrial cancer in women (highest quartile HR = 4.25) and renal cancer in men (HR = 3.07); Muscle fat infiltration (MFI), as a promising indicator, is independently associated with breast cancer risk (HR = 1.62) [103].This association is partially attributed to VAT driven systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and ASAT mediated sex hormone metabolism disorders, and remains significant even after adjusting for BMI, indicating that precise quantification of fat distribution can surpass traditional obesity indicators and provide key imaging biomarkers for screening high-risk cancer populations.

And the related secretions of adipocytes can also serve as early warning indicators for chemotherapy resistance. According to relevant research, adipocytes can secrete exosome microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP) as a key early warning marker for tumor resistance. Specifically, the expression of MTTP in plasma exosomes of obese colorectal cancer (CRC) patients is significantly elevated, and its mechanism is the formation of a complex between MTTP and PRAP1, which inhibits the expression of transcription factor ZEB1 and upregulates GPX4 and xCT proteins. This process leads to a decrease in the proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids, a decrease in lipid peroxidation levels, ultimately inhibiting ferroptosis and enhancing tumor cell resistance to chemotherapy drugs such as oxaliplatin. Clinical evidence shows that plasma exosome MTTP levels in patients with high body fat percentage are significantly correlated with advanced TNM staging, lymph node metastasis, and poor survival rate. Chemotherapy drugs (such as oxaliplatin) further stimulate adipocytes to secrete MTTP, forming a positive feedback loop of drug resistance. Therefore, monitoring plasma exosomal MTTP can provide non-invasive resistance warning indicators for obese CRC patients and lay the foundation for targeted MTTP reversal resistance strategies, such as exosomal MTTP inhibitors [104].

Treatment in the new era: pioneering approaches

Adipocytes have always been considered by most people to play a key role in drug resistance mechanisms and poor prognosis, so the exploration of their potential in tumor treatment has not stopped. With the continuous integration of biotechnology and medical fields, Nguyen H.P. et al. have proposed a new strategy for tumor treatment based on metabolic game theory - fat regulated transplantation technology (AMT), which upregulates heat producing genes such as UCP1, PPARGC1A, or PRDM16 in WA through CRISPRa gene editing, transforming them into “brown like” adipocytes with high metabolic activity. These engineered adipocytes compete with tumor cells for limited nutritional resources by significantly enhancing glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation capacity, which showed tumor-suppressive activity in preclinical models of breast cancer and pancreatic cancer, with clinical translation yet to be evaluated. Research has confirmed that this metabolic intervention strategy can not only directly reduce glucose and fatty acid concentrations in the TME, but also indirectly inhibit tumor progression by systematically improving the body’s insulin sensitivity. This work innovatively utilizes the transplantable nature of adipocytes, providing new ideas for developing tumor therapies based on metabolic reprogramming, but it remains confined exclusively to preclinical investigation and has not yet advanced into formal clinical trials [105–107].

In recent years, there have been increasing achievements in the field of tumor targeted therapy using adipocytes. Svensson R.U. et al. have discovered a method of using ND-646 based ACC inhibitors to block the key pathway of tumor lipid synthesis by targeting acetyl CoA carboxylase (ACC). This method can inhibit the growth of tumors such as non-small cell lung cancer by blocking critical processes in the body, just like blocking the transportation of building materials to construction sites. This method can inhibit the transportation of components required for cancer growth to cancer cells. Preclinical studies have shown that the combination of this inhibitor and chemotherapy drug carboplatin can exert a synergistic effect, increasing the tumor inhibition rate to 87% (compared to only 50% with carboplatin alone), without damaging normal cells [108]. Simultaneously, using fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitors to target FASN and block energy supply to adipocytes, such as orlistat and TVB-2640, induces apoptosis in breast cancer cells and inhibits metastasis. It is also a relatively mature targeted therapy plan.FASN inhibitors have shown significant potential in the treatment of breast cancer by targeting the core enzyme FASN in tumor cell lipid metabolism, effectively blocking its energy supply and membrane structure synthesis. Research shows that FASN is highly expressed in breast cancer cells (almost not expressed in normal tissues), and its catalytic production of fatty acids accounts for more than 93% of the total fatty acids of tumor cells, which is the key to maintain the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells. Inhibitors such as orlistat and TVB-2640 directly induce cancer cell apoptosis and reverse drug resistance by inhibiting FASN activity.TVB-2640 has been clinically tested to reduce liver fat content and improve metabolic indicators in patients [109],and new inhibitors such as 6p have reduced the tumor volume of non-small cell lung cancer and melanoma by over 50% in animal models, and significantly improved efficacy when combined with cisplatinum (inhibition rate of 87%) [110].These advances highlight the broad prospects of FASN inhibitors as metabolic targeting therapy, especially for patients with breast cancer with high FASN expression.

The ketogenic diet (KD; 70% fat, 20% protein, 10% carbohydrates) functions as an adipocyte-targeting adjuvant therapy, exerting antitumor effects through multimodal mechanisms by exploiting the 20–30-fold accelerated glucose-fermentative dependency of malignancies—which lack essential ketolytic enzymes—to force systemic lipid oxidation and ketogenesis that metabolically starves tumors, while simultaneously suppressing obesity-driven adipocyte hypertrophy through hypoglycemia and insulin reduction to attenuate proinflammatory cytokine secretion (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), abrogate insulin/IGF-1 axis activation, and disrupt protumorigenic signaling; restrict carbohydrates to inhibit Warburg-effect-driven energy production while elevating ketone bodies (e.g., β-hydroxybutyrate) to impose defective mitochondrial metabolism-induced energetic collapse and stearoyl-CoA desaturase (SCD) activity downregulation-mediated lipid dyshomeostasis; and synergistically enhance chemo/radiotherapy sensitivity by suppressing compensatory hyperinsulinemia from PI3K inhibition while improving CD8⁺ T-cell cytotoxicity via β-hydroxybutyrate-driven histone acetylation and PD-L1 suppression, coupled with NLRP3 inflammasome inhibition for complication mitigation [111, 112].

Also, the previously mentioned antagonistic peptide designed for MASP6 (such as MASP6-IN-1) reduced breast cancer lung metastasis by 73% in preclinical mouse models, suggesting its value as an individualized therapeutic target. Although it is only a research introduction and there is no relevant literature published, this finding not only indicates that MASP6 is expected to become a biomarker for personalized diagnosis of breast cancer, but also indicates its application prospect as a potential therapeutic target, highlighting the substantial potential of adipocyte biomarkers in tumor diagnosis and treatment.Collectively, these advances substantiate tumor-associated adipocytes as critical regulators of malignancy, establishing adipose-derived molecular signatures as wellsprings for rational therapeutic paradigms [97].

Limitations

This review carries inherent research limitations to acknowledge. The bulk of existing supporting evidence originates from preclinical experimental systems, creating tangible bottlenecks for effective and scalable clinical translation. Tumor-associated adipose tissue presents striking heterogeneity across distinct cancer backgrounds, and such vital intertumoral differences have not been fully elucidated in the current analytical scope. Beyond cellular-level observations, the intricate biological crosstalk between adipocytes and diverse stromal or immune components—including ILC2s and lymphatic vessels—still lacks solid clinical corroboration. Rigorous, large-sample clinical research is therefore required to validate these interactive mechanisms in real disease contexts.

Summary and prospects

Adipocytes serve as central regulatory components within the tumor microenvironment, mediating the balance between oncogenic and tumor-suppressive processes through intertwined metabolic–immune axes encompassing metabolic reprogramming such as lipid transfer and glycolytic potentiation, as well as immune network remodeling including Treg expansion, M2 macrophage polarization and modulated ILC2 activity. Future research efforts ought to center on three clinically valuable translational avenues. In-depth exploration is required to dissect the intrinsic heterogeneity of tumor-associated adipose tissue, alongside clarification of the interactive crosstalk among tumor-associated adipose tissue, ILC2s, macrophages and lymphatic vessels, while single-cell and spatial multi-omics technologies can be harnessed to pinpoint key molecular switches driving the pro-tumor polarization of adipocytes. Translational therapeutic strategies targeting the adipose–tumor metabolic–immune axis also deserve sustained development, with clinical-stage regimens such as FASN inhibitors prioritized for practical application and preclinical approaches including brown adipose tissue activation and ILC2 checkpoint modulation systematically validated to optimize treatment efficacy and alleviate therapeutic resistance. It is likewise essential to build multimodal precision research systems by establishing organoid models and metabolic imaging platforms, enabling real-time dynamic monitoring of fat–tumor interactions, and facilitating the development of blood-derived or imaging-based biomarkers to support disease risk stratification and individualized treatment decision-making. Progress in these research dimensions will drive the evolution of cancer therapy toward microenvironment-oriented precise intervention, delivering clinical benefits to patients with fat-rich tumors and holding extended application prospects across a wider spectrum of malignant malignancies.
