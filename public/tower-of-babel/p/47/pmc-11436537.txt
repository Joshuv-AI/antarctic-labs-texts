Thoracic epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma: a rare and aggressive disease with case report and literature review
Authors: Linke Yang, Pei Li, Runze Liu, Baomin Feng, Huiqing Mao, Xiaoyong Tang, Guangjian Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma (EIMS) is a rare subtype of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor, characterized to be an aggressive disease with high frequency of ALK rearrangement, rapid recurrence, and poor prognosis. Primary EIMS of thoracic origin is rarely observed. Herein, we described a case of 28-year-old female developed primary EIMS in the anterior mediastinum with hepatic metastasis. The EIMS displayed sheet-like growth of epithelioid and spindle cells with enlarged nuclei, abundant and eosinophilic cytoplasm, and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Immunohistochemical staining revealed positive expression of ALK in the nuclear membrane, and ALK rearrangement was identified by polymerase chain reaction assay. Alectinib showed partial response, and achieved a meaningful survival benefit for four months. Based on this case report and literature review, ALK inhibitor reveals promising activity on the rare but aggressive EIMS. Awareness of EIMS in thoracic disease and its clinicopathological features is essential to avoid erroneous diagnosis.

Introduction

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a mesenchymal neoplasm with intermediate biological potential, composed of neoplastic myofibroblastic tumor cells, inflammatory infiltrate of plasma cells and lymphocytes, which frequently occurs in the abdominal cavity of young adults [1]. As a rare variant of IMT, epithelioid inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma (EIMS) was first described in 2011, which is a malignacy with aggressive behavior, rapid recurrence, and poor prognosis, characterized of sheet-like growth of epithelioid and spindle cells with enlarged nuclei, abundant and eosinophilic cytoplasm, and composed of infammatory neutrophils, lymphocytes, or plasma cells [2, 3]. All described EIMS cases have perinuclear or cytoplasmic positivity for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and sometimes associated with ALK fusions. It is observed that rearrangement of ALK gene occurs in nearly 60% of IMTs, and in almost all EIMS cases [3, 4]. To date, only five cases of primary EIMS of pulmonary origin have been reported [5–9]. Herein, we present an unusual case of thoracic EIMS harboring ALK rearrangement in a young female patient. In addition, the clinical and histological features of EIMS, and its targeted response to ALK inhibitor in this case are comprehensively discussed.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old female with husky cough and facial swelling was diagnosed with anterior mediastinal tumor in May 2022. The neck, chest and abdomen enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan revealed a large mass measured 9.1 cm in the anterior mediastinum, associated with cervical, mediastinal and axillary metastatic lymph nodes, hepatic and bone metastases (Fig. 1A). The needle biopsy of anterior mediastinal mass discovered sheet-like growth of epithelioid and spindle cells with enlarged nuclei, abundant and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Loose mesenchyme and infiltration of inflammatory cells was visible (Fig. 2A-B). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed positive expression of ALK in the nuclear membrane (diffuse+++, Fig. 2C), CD30 (+, Fig. 2D), CD10 (+, Fig. 2E), CKpan (+, Fig. 2F), EMA (focal +, Fig. 2G), and Ki-67 (+, Fig. 2H). In addition, other stainings showed negative expression for Desmin (Fig. 3A), SMA (Fig. 3B), SALL4 (Fig. 3C), MyoD1 (Fig. 3D), CD20 (Fig. 3E), and CD34 (Fig. 3F). According to the histological characteristics and IHC feature, her anterior mediastinal tumor was finally confirmed to be EIMS. Amplification refractory mutation system-polymerase chain reaction (ARMS-PCR) assay via tumor tissue identified ALK rearrangement with the 10-gene targeted panel (EGFR, KRAS, HER2, ALK, ROS-1, RET, BRAF, MET, NRAS, PIK3CA).

Since May 19, 2022, she received alectinib 600 mg twice daily as targeted therapy. Though the husky cough and facial swelling relieved in a short period of time, her weakness of lower extremities progressed and occured incomplete paraplegia. Examination of magnetic resonance imaging on May 26, 2022 showed multiple bone metastases, together with worse bone destruction and pathologic fracture at the seventh and eleventh thoracic vertebra. Afterwards, decompression and excision of spinal canal lesions was performed, and the CT scan in June (Fig. 1B) and August (Fig. 1C) showed significant decrease both in anterior mediastinal tumor and hepatic metastases. The patient achieved partial response (PR) during alectinib treatment. Unfortunately, she suffered sudden death due to acute pulmonary embolism in September 2022.

Discussion

According to 45 cases of EIMS that have been reported, the age of the patients ranged from 4 months to 72 years (average, 34.3 years), and the majority were male (60%) [10]. The diagnosis of EIMS is difficult due to its rarity and resemblance to other tumors of mesenchymal or lymphoid origins. The EIMS is characterized by loosely arranged round or epithelioid neoplastic cells with vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli and myxoid stroma surround by amphophilic to eosinophilic cytoplasm, and neutrophil-rich inflammatory infiltrates are the striking characteristic [11]. In most cases of EIMS, the ALK protein is usually positive, and additionally, Desmin, SMA, and CD30 may be expressed [12]. In our case, the diagnosis of EIMS was established on the predominance of epithelioid subtype of IMT, with atypical nuclei in the presence of inflammatory cells and spindle cells.

Together with our case presented above, a total of six cases of pulmonary EIMS between 2014 and 2024 have been detailed reported. We summarized these cases, and revealed their clinicopathological features and targeted outcomes with ALK inhibitors (Table 1). Among these six cases, primary EIMS from pulmonary origin could been found in pleural cavity, lung and anterior mediastinum, and most of them presented a large mass sized between 6.9 and 12.5 cm. Only two cases occured distal hepatic metastasis at baseline, and five of them positively expressed ALK, Desmin and CD30 in IHC staining, which is consistent with literature reported [12]. One case was detected to be RANBP2-ALK fusion by next-generation sequencing (NGS), and the others were confimed ALK rearrangement by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or PCR assay. Five of them received ALK inhibitor crizotinib as targeted therapy, but only one achieved PR, with a progression-free survival (PFS) of 19 months. The other three cases kept stable disease during crizotinib treatment, but with a short period of PFS of 3 months. Our case displayed PR to ALK inhibitor alectinib after one month of therapy, but she only achieved a PFS of four months because of sudden death of acute pulmonary embolism.

The largest study on adult IMT enrolled 30 cases, including seven cases of EIMS [13]. Among these seven patients of EIMS, they all originated from the abdominal cavity, with a median age of 26 years, which is younger than other IMT patients (median, 38 years). Compared to typical IMT, EIMS is more aggressive, associated with high degree of invasion and metastasis [2]. ALK rearrangement in EIMS gives rise to ALK inhibitors to be an optional treatment. Heterogeneous ALK fusion partners have been discovered in IMT including NPM, TMP3/4, CARS, CLTC, EML4, DCTN1, SEC31L1, ATIC and FN1, while, RANBP2-ALK and RRBP1-ALK fusions were prevalent in EIMS [3, 10, 12]. In addition, PRRC2B-ALK, VCL-ALK, and EML4-ALK were also recently considered as the oncogenic driver in EIMS [14–16]. Furthermore, G1269A has been reported as a secondary mutation after resistance to crizotinib in EIMS, and the second generation ALK inhibitor has been observed a significant response [17]. According to the IHC assay, the nuclear membrane, cytoplasmic or perinuclear staining of ALK was observed in different cases of EIMS, which is a widely used detection for positivity of ALK in EIMS [11]. In addition, FISH assay, PCR assay or NGS testing can contribute to the molecular diagnosis of EIMS. Of note, NGS testing could detect a certain or uncommon ALK fusion, which is a complement for the detection of ALK rearrangement in EIMS.

As ALK rearrangement occured in most of EIMS cases, ALK inhibitor crizotinib has been reported of therapeutic activity for EIMS [3, 18–21].The results of EORTC CREATE trial showed objective response rates (ORRs) were 66.7% and 14.3%, and the median PFS were 18.0 and 14.3 months in ALK-positive and ALK-negative metastatic IMT patients who received crizotinib therapy, respectively [22]. Another study showed that adult IMT patients were also sensitive to crizotinib (ORR 81.3%, median PFS 20.8 months) [13]. Currently, the mechanism of crizotinib resistance and subsequent treatment in IMT patients remain unknown. For crizotinib-resistant IMT, ceritinib showed promising antitumor activity (ORR 70%) in a phase I trial and some studies [13, 23, 24]. To some degree, the next generation of ALK inhibitors could be an option for crizotinib-resistant ALK-positive IMT. However, for the EIMS, the overall efficacy of ALK inhibitors is difficult to be confirmed, owing to its low frequency and lack of large sample-sized study. Notably, a 15-year single-center study observed crizotinib revealed an ORR of 72% for seven ALK-positive patients with EIMS. However, the prognosis of patients with EIMS was similar to that of non-EIMS after treatment with ALK inhibitor [13]. Alectinib, as a highly selective ALK inhibitor, has been showed superior efficacy in ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer [25]. However, only a few cases on alectinib in EIMS have been reported [14, 15]. In our case, the patient was detected to carry ALK rearrangement, but the fusion partner was uncertain owing to the PCR assay. Alectinib as first-line targeted therapy displayed therapeutic response of PR and meaningful PFS benefit.

Currently, it lacks standard recommendation for EIMS, and surgical resection is still considered the primary option. Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy reveals no direct effect on the prognosis of EIMS [2, 18, 20]. Most EIMS express positive staining of ALK and CD30, and CD30 expression is a characteristic feature of EIMS, representing a potential therapeutic target for EIMS [2, 26–28]. Some in vivo studies suggest that reduced CD30 expression might have contributed to resistance to CD30-targeted agents [29]. Furthermore, the combination therapy targeting both CD30 and ALK at diagnosis would be more efficacious than single agent therapy or using combination therapy at the point of relapse, suggesting a potential option in preventing recurrence or targeting ALK-positive EIMS relapsed to ALK inhibitors [26, 29]. Comprehensive pathologic and genetic profiling of EIMS at diagnosis to evaluate CD30 expression and define the ALK fusion is essential, and may identify patients who would potentially benefit from treatment with CD30-targeted-therapy and ALK inhibitors. Currently, though IHC for CD30 is not routinely performed in EIMS diagnosis, we suggest the evaluation of EIMS should include CD30 expression, ALK IHC staining, molecular detection for ALK fusions or secondary drug-resistant mutations, and identification of other targetable fusions in ALK-negative EIMS cases.

In conclusion, we present a case of pulmonary EIMS, a highly aggressive variant of IMT with ALK rearrangement, with detailed literature review. ALK inhibitors reveal promising activity on this rare but aggressive entity. Awareness of EIMS in thoracic disease and its clinicopathological features is essential to avoid erroneous diagnosis.
