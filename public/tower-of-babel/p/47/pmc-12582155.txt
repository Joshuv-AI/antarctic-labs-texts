A Route to Design Novel Functional Peptides by Applying a Denoising Diffusional Model to mRNA Display Libraries
Authors: Pearl Qi, Yash Pragnesh Gandhi, Kexin Zheng, Farzad Jalali‐Yazdi, Justin N. Ong, Terry T. Takahashi, Rajiv K. Kalia, Richard W. Roberts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In vitro directed evolution techniques, such as mRNA display, enable peptide ligand discovery and optimization. However, physical libraries that rely on a genetic code can only search a small fraction of sequence space due to inherent biases in the genetic code and experimental limitations. To address this challenge, denoising diffusion implicit models (DDIMs) are applied to generate novel peptide ligands against B‐cell lymphoma extra‐large (Bcl‐xL), a key cancer target. Starting with high‐throughput sequencing data from previous selections, a DDIM is trained to produce novel sequences with high affinity binding. Experimental validation confirms that most generated sequences are functionally equivalent to the original library members for Bcl‐xL binding and demonstrated comparable binding kinetics and affinity relative to the wildtype and nearest original neighbors. Importantly, this approach generated rare sequences not easily accessible via mutation and directed evolution. These results indicate that DDIMs can complement and expand directed evolution data, efficiently exploring underrepresented regions of sequence space. This approach provides a broadly applicable framework for accelerating ligand discovery and optimizing molecular properties across diverse targets.

Introduction

Phage display,[ 1 , 2 ] ribosome display,[ 3 ] yeast display,[ 4 ] and mRNA display[ 5 , 6 ] are in vitro selection techniques that enable the design of protein or peptide ligands for specific targets. mRNA display libraries of up to 1014 unique sequences can be constructed. High throughput sequencing of mRNA display selection libraries allows identification of thousands of putative ligands.[ 7 ] However, even the largest mRNA display selections can only search a vanishingly small fraction of sequence space for proteins longer than ≈10 amino acids, since sequence space rapidly expands as protein chain length increases.[ 8 ] For instance, a 20 amino acid peptide has 2020 (≈1026) possible combinations, underscoring the need for innovative search methods beyond experimental approaches.

Deep learning[ 9 , 10 ] (DL) approaches have enhanced protein engineering by enabling the exploration of vast sequence spaces beyond those that are experimentally accessible. DL‐guided approaches are faster, lower cost, and can explore larger sections of sequence space as compared to experimental approaches.[ 10 , 11 – 12 ] Predictive DL models can estimate protein function while generative models can sample unexplored sequence space. These models often rely on large but low‐resolution datasets,[ 13 ] limiting their precision for specific design tasks.

Here, we use a high‐quality dataset resulting from high‐throughput sequencing of an mRNA display selection to train a DL generative model with the goal of producing novel sequences for a specific target. The sequencing dataset originates from our previous selection against B‐cell lymphoma extra‐large (Bcl‐xL), which contains 21 amino acid‐long peptide ligands, along with their respective binding affinities (K D).[ 14 ] We use this dataset to train a denoising diffusion implicit model (DDIM),[ 15 ] which provide an efficient way to generate high‐quality sequences through short generative chains, reducing computational cost (Figure 1a). After training and validating the DDIM, we demonstrate that the model is capable of rapidly generating novel target‐specific functional sequences, which do not appear in the original dataset but still retain high affinity binding.

These encoded images are then used to train the DDIM. Once trained, the model generates new images sampled from the same probability distribution. Finally, the generated images are converted back into ligand sequences. b) The DDIM generates new sequences using non‐Markovian processes. The training process learns to predict and remove noise, the forward process gradually adds noise to a sample, and the reverse process removes noise step by step to generate new data.

Previously, we performed several in vitro selections against Bcl‐xL using mRNA display.[ 14 , 16 , 17 ] Using an extension selection, we first engineered the E1 peptide, which is a 21‐residue linear peptide with picomolar binding affinity (K D = 46.3 ± 4.3 pM).[ 14 , 16 , 17 ] We subsequently doped the E1 sequence and performed a second selection (doped selection), which gave rise to a pool of sequences with even higher affinity. The affinities of these sequences were determined en masse using high‐throughput sequencing kinetics (HTSK).[ 14 ] This dataset thus includes both sequence and binding affinity data and is a high‐quality dataset for training DL models.

First, we needed to convert the dataset into a format compatible with the DDIM. Each sequence was encoded as a 20 × 20 binary matrix using one‐hot encoding (Figure S1, Supporting Information).[ 18 ] In this format, each row represents the amino acid position within a peptide sequence while each column corresponds to one of the 20 natural amino acids. This encoding transforms each peptide sequence into an image, where each matrix element reflects the presence or absence of a specific amino acid at a given position. We also confirmed that the sequences in the dataset belong to the same probability distribution[ 19 ] (Figure S2a, Supporting Information), which is necessary for compatibility with the DDIM.

The data was randomly split into training and testing subsets at a ratio of 70:30, respectively, to ensure robust model evaluation. We used mean square error as a loss function to measure model performance. After 5,000 steps, both training and testing losses reached their minimum, indicating optimal training (Figure S2b,c, Supporting Information). The best‐performing epoch, as determined by the lowest testing loss, was then used for sequence generation. More than 99% of the DDIM‐generated sequences are novel and are not found in the training data, and each matches the original peptide length (20 amino acids) (Figure 1b) and retains key functional residues.

We generated 10 000 sequences using the model and randomly chose 12 sequences, termed “fully generated,” for experimental characterization (Figure 2a and S3, Supporting Information). These sequences were chosen to span the range of Hamming distances to their nearest original neighbors (NON) (Table S4, Supporting Information). Each sequence was synthesized as a radiolabeled mRNA‐peptide fusion and the kinetic on‐ and off‐rates were measured for binding to immobilized Bcl‐xL. 11 of the 12 generated sequences were functional. FG1 exhibits the highest affinity, and 75% (9/12) of the tested sequences have higher affinities than the wildtype E1 sequence. FG5 was the only nonfunctional sequence; however, with a Hamming distance of 10 relative to E1, it may have drifted outside of functional sequence space. Three Hamming distances of 10 relative to E1, it may have drifted outside generated sequences exhibited slightly slower off‐rates and retained on‐rates comparable with E1 (Figure 2a). Overall, the binding data demonstrate that our well‐trained DDIM can rapidly and effectively generate novel Bcl‐xL binding sequences with similar or better binding characteristics than the starting E1 sequence.

We also compared the binding of several generated sequences to their NON from the starting dataset. The NON of a generated sequence is the sequence in the original pool with the smallest Hamming distance, i.e., the sequence that is closest in sequence space. The generated sequences exhibited off‐rates very similar to their NON counterparts (Figure 2c,d), confirming comparable performance to the original sequences.

Interestingly, the 12 generated sequences exhibited Hamming distances[ 20 ] ranging from 1 to 10 to their NON, with the exception of nonfunctional FG5. No correlation was observed between Hamming distance and K D (data not shown) and sequences with Hamming distances of 1–9 demonstrated similar or better binding to E1. While FG4 (Hamming distance = 9) showed a ≈10‐fold decrease in K D (138 ± 13 pM), it still retains picomolar affinity. We note that in traditional screening and postselection analyses, sequences with large Hamming distances would not normally be considered for study because even a single point mutation at a binding interface can easily result in 10‐fold changes in affinity.[ 21 , 22 ] Thus, one advantage of our DL analysis is that more dissimilar sequences could be generated with a high likelihood that these sequences would still be functional.

Somewhat surprisingly, two of the highest affinity sequences (FG1 and FG12) contained proline at position 3. Alphafold structure prediction of E1 in isolation indicates the peptide is fully alpha helical (Figure S6, Supporting Information). Proline is a helix‐breaking residue and incompatible with forming a fully helical peptide.[ 23 , 24 ] Interestingly, AlphaFold3[ 25 ] predictions of FG‐Bcl‐xL complexes indicate that the majority of FG peptides are not helical at position 3, but rather give φ and ψ backbone angles compatible with a polyproline type II (PPII) structure (Figure S7a,b, and Table S5, Supporting Information). Further, overlay of E1 and FG12 predicted Bcl‐xL complexes show PPII structure excellent overlap in the backbone (Figure S7a, Supporting Information). These results highlight the ability of DDIM to generate functional sequences that are counterintuitive from a rational peptide design perspective.

DL models generally benefit from training datasets that are larger in size. The original training HTSK dataset contained tens of thousands of sequences; however, it is unique in that it includes both sequence and binding affinity data that can be ordered according to affinity. We thus tested if it were possible to generate sequences with similar functionality by training the model with a smaller dataset, which would make it more challenging to train the model, yet only include the highest affinity sequences (those with K D values between 1 pM and 5 pM as determined by HTSK). We hypothesized that such a model, if well trained, might favor the generation of new sequences with only the highest binding affinities.

We truncated the training dataset to approximately one‐tenth the size of the original and used this reduced‐sized dataset for DDIM training. After training the model, we then generated new sequences, termed “small generated”. Six sequences (SG1, SG2, SG3, SG4, SG5, and SG6) were randomly chosen for testing and have Hamming distances ranging from 1 to 7 compared to E1.

We synthesized these sequences as radiolabeled mRNA‐peptide fusions and determined their binding parameters as above. Out of the six sequences, five were functional, except for SG2. The on‐rates for the five functional sequences were similar to E1 (Figure S4, Supporting Information). However, four of the five functional sequences exhibited an increase in off‐rate, with SG3 showing a particularly significant increase (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Only one sequence (SG1) displayed binding properties comparable to the wildtype and the fully generated sequences that were generated from the larger dataset. Taken together, these findings suggest that a more diverse and larger dataset is critical for model training and generating high‐quality ligands. A larger dataset likely provides the model with a more comprehensive understanding of functional sequence space, leading to better‐performing outcomes.

While generative models such as DDIMs can generate novel sequences, the performance of these sequences will be constrained by the properties of the training dataset; the properties will correspond to the same probability distribution. Thus, the K D values of the generated sequences will fall within the range of the training dataset. Binding affinity is only one of many important characteristics of a peptide ligand and other properties such as solubility, protease resistance, binding specificity, and other pharmacokinetic properties often need to be considered when designing a ligand, especially for therapeutic applications. Often, compromises must be made, for example, a high affinity ligand may not be soluble and vise versa; optimizing multiple properties simultaneously can be challenging. Our generative DDIM can provide a solution by generating novel sequences that can bind to a target of interest, but have a range of values for a second property, thereby providing more potential peptide sequences for screening to optimize both properties simultaneously.

To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the peptides generated by our well‐trained DDIM with respect to hydrophobicity, which is related to the solubility of a molecule. We calculated the grand average of hydropathy (fGRAVY) score[ 26 ] for 5,000 generated sequences to estimate the hydrophobicity of each peptide (Figure S5, Supporting Information). The generated peptides display a nearly linear distribution of scores ranging from −0.7 (hydrophilic) to +0.5 (hydrophobic). Our functional analysis indicates that the vast majority of these sequences are functional for binding. The DDIM‐generated sequences thus increase the chemical variability of peptide candidates, allowing the optimization of physicochemical properties, binding efficacy, and therapeutic potential.

Lastly, the probability distribution in DL models is not biased by the genetic code. Individual codon changes requiring one, two, or three nucleotides have dramatically different likelihood and combinations of rare substitutions are effectively inaccessible (Figure S8, Supporting Information). This constraint significantly restricts the sequence space accessible during directed evolution, often leaving rare, potentially more functional variants undiscovered. The DL models thus generate sequences that are difficult to access by directed evolution.

We compared the likelihood of sequences occurring in the original doped pool with those that were DDIM‐generated. Figure 3a shows the probability of ≈100 000 DDIM‐generated sequences (red) compared with the most abundant ≈100 000 sequences from the final pool of the Bcl‐xL doped library (red). The DDIM probability distribution is shifted by ≈100‐fold downward compared to the directed evolution library (Figure 3a), resulting in 37% more sequences in the low probability region. The Hamming distance is similarly shifted to higher values in the DDIM dataset compared to the directed evolution library, giving 20% more sequences with more than six point mutations out of 20 variable positions. Further, more than 99% of the generated sequences are not in the training dataset, indicating that the DDIM approach expands the scope of functional peptides by more than 100‐fold. DDIMs are readily trained using high‐throughput sequencing data as a route to generate novel peptide ligands. The DDIM‐generated sequences show equivalent binding affinities and binding kinetics to the training dataset. While we have applied this approach to mRNA display data, our approach should be general for datasets generated by other display methods. Taken together, this analysis highlights the model's capacity to expand the searchable sequence space into regions inaccessible by directed evolution, resulting in functional peptides with increased sequence diversity, functionality, and physicochemical properties.
