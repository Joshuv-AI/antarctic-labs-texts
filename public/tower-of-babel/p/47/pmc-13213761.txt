Localised malignant mesothelioma presenting as an esophageal mass: An unusual manifestation
Authors: Kaspar L Yaxley, Tristan Rutland, Thisuri Jayawardena, David Law, Yuranga Weerakkody
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant mesothelioma is an aggressive neoplasm arising from mesothelial cells, typically presenting with pleural thickening, pleural effusions, or respiratory symptoms. Dysphagia as a presenting symptom is extremely uncommon, and esophageal involvement is rare. We report a 71-year-old male with an 18-month history of progressive dysphagia and significant weight loss. Initial gastroscopy was unremarkable, but repeat endoscopy revealed a smooth lower esophageal stricture. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated a well-circumscribed esophageal mass with minimal pleural thickening, and PET-CT confirmed high FDG uptake in the mass and subtle anterior and posterior mediastinal pleural foci. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration of the esophageal lesion revealed epithelioid cells positive for mesothelial markers (CK5, D2-40, WT1, Calretinin, CK7) with loss of BAP1 and MTAP, consistent with epithelioid mesothelioma. The patient was commenced on immunotherapy but lost to follow-up. This case represents an unusual manifestation of localized malignant mesothelioma presenting primarily as an esophageal mass, with minimal pleural disease. The diagnosis was established via endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration, highlighting the utility of minimally invasive techniques in atypical presentations. It underscores the importance of considering mesothelioma in the differential diagnosis of unexplained esophageal or mediastinal masses, even in the absence of classic pleural findings. Mesothelioma can rarely present with dominant esophageal involvement. Awareness of this atypical presentation facilitates timely diagnosis, appropriate management, and may prevent diagnostic delays in patients presenting with dysphagia.Keywords: Dysphagia, Mesothelioma, Esophageal stricture, Computed tomography, F18-FDG PET, Endoscopic ultrasound

Case report

A 71-year-old male presented with an 18-month history of dysphagia. The dysphagia had worsened over the preceding week to the point where he was unable to tolerate any solid food. He also reported 10 kg of weight loss over the last year. The dysphagia was worse in the morning and was occasionally followed by vomiting. He reported a globus sensation but denied odynophagia or respiratory symptoms. The patient’s medical history was significant only for hypertension. He had a 10-pack-year history of smoking (although had ceased smoking 30 years ago). He previously had worked as an engineer and gave an occupational history of possible asbestos exposure. Apart from a mild anemia (serum hemoglobin of 123 g/L), routine laboratory investigations were unremarkable.

The patient had a previous gastroscopy in August 2024 to investigate the dysphagia, but this had demonstrated no cause. Repeat endoscopic evaluation during this presentation demonstrated esophagitis in the proximal esophagus with a large amount of mucous pooling and food residue, requiring food bolus removal. A smooth, esophageal stricture was visualized extending over a total length of 2 cm, 30-32 cm from the incisors, with associated local hyperkeratosis. The squamocolumnar junction appeared normal with no evidence of Barrett’s esophagus. The stomach also appeared normal. Erosive duodenitis was demonstrated at the first and second parts of the duodenum. Biopsy of the esophageal stricture demonstrated only benign superficial squamous nonkeratinising epithelium.

Imaging findings

A postcontrast CT chest was performed 60 seconds post-intravenous contrast injection (no oral contrast was administered) with 0.5 mm slice thickness and sagittal, coronal, and axial reformats available in lung and soft tissue windows. This demonstrated a focal mass that appeared to diffusely involve the esophageal wall and was located at the lower third of the esophagus at the level of the left atrium (spanning T6-T8), measuring up to 59 mm in diameter and approximately 61 mm in height (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The mass was smoothly marginated with homogeneous attenuation and no focal calcification. There was evidence of associated esophageal obstruction with upstream esophageal dilatation and an air-fluid level just proximal to the mass. There was minimal nodular pleural thickening seen predominantly at the anterior mediastinal pleura on the right and adjacent to the esophageal mass in the posterior mediastinum, which was only recognized on retrospective review (after FDG-PET imaging was performed). However, there was no pleural effusion or calcified pleural plaques. There was also no intrapulmonary nodule or mass and no intrathoracic lymphadenopathy. A diagnostic CT abdomen and pelvis (portal venous phase) demonstrated no transdiaphragmatic disease spread or evidence of primary peritoneal disease.

Axial postcontrast CT chest demonstrating the esophageal mass.

FDG-PET was performed with low-dose whole body non-contrast CT for attenuation correction purposes (285 mBq injected activity, patient blood sugar level 5.3 mmol/L, imaging performed 57 minutes after radiopharmaceutical injection). This demonstrated high-grade uptake corresponding to the circumferential mid-esophageal mass referenced on CT. There were a few small areas of multifocal uptake in the right lung pleura along the anterior mediastinal pleura, which correlated with enhancing soft tissue foci on the diagnostic CT. There were also a couple of small foci of FDG avidity at the right posterior mediastinal pleural recess adjacent to the esophageal mass (Fig. 3, Fig. 4), which were not well seen on CT. No FDG-avid mediastinal or hilar lymph node or evidence of extra-thoracic FDG-avid metastatic disease was demonstrated.

Axial fused FDG-PET CT chest showing small foci of FDG-avid pleural thickening at the right anterior and right posterior mediastinum.

Axial fused FDG-PET CT showing the FDG-avid esophageal mass and additional small FDG-avid pleural thickening at the right anterior and right posterior mediastinum.

Endoscopic ultrasound findings

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) was performed 1 week after the PET-CT. Correlating with the initial imaging findings, this identified a hypoechoic lesion measuring 4-5 cm in total length, which appeared to involve the deep mucosa (layer 2—which includes the lamina propria and muscularis mucosae) of the esophagus. The lesion appeared to be compressing the esophagus from outside it (compressing layers 3-5, which were hard to visualize separately). The mucosa appeared intact, and lesion was therefore felt to be extrinsic rather than intrinsic to the esophagus. This was sampled via fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) (Fig. 5).

Pathological findings

The cytological smears showed a highly cellular population of epithelioid cells with mild atypia, many of which were in large tissue fragments. These cells had round nuclei with small nucleoli, moderate amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm, and vague cell borders. There was some entrapped smooth muscle with tumor cells within some of the fibers (Fig. 6).

Cytology specimen demonstrating a highly cellular population of epithelioid cells with mild atypia, H&E (x200).

Immunohistochemistry demonstrated that the epithelioid tumor cells expressed mesothelial lineage markers, including CK5, D2-40, WT1, Calretinin, and CK7. To exclude nonmesothelial carcinoma, the tumor cells were specifically negative for p40 and CK20, with additional negative staining for Claudin 4, Ber EP4, S100, SOX10, synaptophysin, chromogranin, CD34, and CD45.

Loss of MTAP (cytoplasmic) and BAP1 (nuclear) expression was identified in the tumor cells, with retained staining in surrounding non-neoplastic cells serving as appropriate internal positive controls (Fig. 7). The combined immunophenotype-mesothelial marker expression, absence of carcinoma-associated markers, and demonstrable loss of MTAP and BAP1 were supportive of a diagnosis of epithelioid mesothelioma.

Immunohistochemistry: top left—loss of BAP1, top right—D2-40 expression, bottom left—loss of MTAP, bottom right—WT1 expression.

The patient was subsequently referred for oncologic management and commenced on first-line immunotherapy for mesothelioma (nivolumab and ipilimumab), but was lost to follow-up after this point.

Discussion

Malignant mesothelioma is a highly aggressive malignant neoplasm arising from mesothelial cells of the pleura, and less commonly, the peritoneum. It is strongly associated with occupational asbestos exposure. Clinically, patients typically present with gradually progressive dyspnea, dull chest pain, or both. Despite treatment advances, the overall prognosis remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 10% [1].

Radiologically, mesothelioma most often manifests on chest radiograph or contrast-enhanced CT as nodular or lobular pleural thickening exceeding 1 cm, which is typically diffuse, concentric, and may involve the mediastinal pleura. The disease frequently extends along pulmonary fissures, and multiple pleural masses with pleural effusions are common. In advanced stages, the pleura forms a circumferential “rind-like” encasement of the lung with associated ipsilateral hemithoracic volume loss [2]. Intense FDG uptake is typically demonstrated on PET-CT within the tumor and metastases, and PET imaging is more sensitive than CT for the evaluation of chest wall, mediastinum, and diaphragm invasion, as well as lymph node involvement [3]. Although calcified pleural plaques are highly associated with prior asbestos exposure, they are observed in only 20% of mesothelioma cases on CT [4]. The imaging differential diagnosis includes pleural metastatic disease, invasive thymoma, localized fibrous tumor of pleura, pleural fibrosis, and asbestos-related diffuse pleural thickening.

Mediastinal organ invasion, including esophageal involvement, is classified as T4 disease only when accompanied by diffuse pleural involvement of the ipsilateral hemithorax [5]. This case is highly unusual in that esophageal involvement was the dominant clinical, endoscopic, and radiological feature, while pleural disease was minimal. In fact, the pleural disease was relatively subtle and was not identified on initial contrast-enhanced CT chest imaging, and only became evident on FDG PET-CT, highlighting the utility of PET imaging in this case. The majority of this pleural disease was confined to the right anterior mediastinal pleura, remote from the esophageal disease.

Dysphagia is an uncommon presenting symptom of malignant pleural mesothelioma. When it occurs, it is usually secondary to advanced disease with extensive pleural involvement, pulmonary parenchymal spread, chest wall invasion, or mediastinal lymphadenopathy [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. The pathophysiological mechanisms described include pseudo-achalasia due to infiltration of the myenteric plexus, intrinsic esophageal obstruction, or extrinsic compression by mediastinal lymphadenopathy [14]. In diffuse malignant mesothelioma (DMM), dysphagia generally reflects advanced mediastinal invasion and has therefore been reported as a poor prognostic sign [14].

This presentation likely represents a form of localized malignant mesothelioma (LMM)—a rare, poorly understood entity that shares identical histopathological and immunohistochemical characteristics with DMM. Most cases reported in the literature have been described as a localized thoracic tumor presenting as a pleural, chest wall, mediastinal, and/or pulmonary mass on initial imaging, suspicious for a carcinoma or a mesenchymal tumor. The dominant thoracic tumor may or may not be accompanied by additional small, adjacent, but separate pleural nodules. It remains unclear whether LMM is a different tumor than DMM or whether it is part of a continuum of malignant neoplasms of mesothelial origin. Unlike DMM, the association between LMM and asbestos exposure is less well established. Importantly, LMM has been reported to carry a more favorable prognosis, highlighting the potential for clinical management differences between these two entities [15].

In this case, the initial appearances of the lower esophageal mass on CT and PET-CT were favored to represent an esophageal leiomyoma, based on CT appearances of a smooth, ovoid, and discrete esophageal mass. Alternatively, a gastrointestinal stromal tumor, based on FDG avidity, was considered. Less likely consideration was given to esophageal carcinoma, lymphoma, and metastatic disease, due to absence of pathological appearing lymph nodes or metastatic disease elsewhere on FDG-PET. On cross-sectional imaging, it was difficult to determine if the mass was intrinsic to the esophagus itself or was extrinsic and compressing the esophageal lumen. The pathological findings consistent with mesothelioma were surprising, although in retrospect (after EUS-FNA findings), may have been suggested by the presence of small foci of adjacent pleural disease demonstrable on both CT and PET, as well as possible patient historical exposure to asbestos.

Histopathological confirmation of mesothelioma may, in fact, be challenging, particularly on cytology or where biopsy material is limited. A standard immunohistochemical panel demonstrating at least two mesothelial markers (such as calretinin, WT1, and cytokeratin 5/6) and absence of carcinoma markers (such as CEA, MOC-31, or Ber-EP4) is recommended to confirm the diagnosis. Loss of expression of BAP1, MTAP, or homozygous deletion of CDKN2A provide highly specific markers of malignancy once mesothelial lineage has been established (differentiating it from reactive mesothelial proliferation) [16]. In this case, the EUS-FNA specimen yielded sufficient cellular material for immunohistochemical profiling, which demonstrated a characteristic mesothelial immunophenotype.

To our knowledge, there is only one other case report of LMM presenting as an esophageal mass, but which was diagnosed via surgical pathology rather than EUS-FNA [17]. While EUS-FNA has previously been utilized for diagnosing mesothelioma, the sampled lesions have typically been located entirely separate to the esophagus with no evidence of an esophageal mass—such as mediastinal lymph nodes, extrinsic mediastinal masses, or distant lesions involving the upper abdomen [11,12,18,19]. Given that the patient’s endoscopy 1 year earlier was normal, we postulate that the esophageal mass in this case originated external to the esophagus (as suggested on EUS) as localized mediastinal pleural disease with esophageal involvement resulting from gradual trans-esophageal invasion, ultimately presenting with progressive dysphagia. Patient loss to follow-up meant that the pleural disease was not biopsied, and no further invasive investigations (such as thoracoscopic evaluation) were performed. Thus, pleural origin of the mass is surmised based on the imaging findings only.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare presentation of malignant pleural mesothelioma manifesting primarily as an esophageal mass, diagnosed by EUS-FNA. It emphasizes that mesothelioma can rarely present with minimal pleural disease and that esophageal invasion may be the dominant imaging finding. It also highlights the importance of investigating dysphagia with imaging (eg, via fluoroscopic barium swallow or CT) even if endoscopy is normal, as was the case here 1 year previously. Awareness of this atypical manifestation and consideration of mesothelioma in the differential diagnosis of unexplained esophageal or mediastinal lesions can facilitate timely, minimally invasive diagnosis and appropriate multidisciplinary management.
