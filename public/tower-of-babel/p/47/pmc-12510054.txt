Identifying Ethical Issues Encountered in a Pediatric Postacute Care Setting
Authors: Erica K Salter, Allison Bajada, Maggie Mullane, Nicholas A Holekamp, Patrick G Hogan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify the nature and frequency of ethical challenges experienced by employees at a pediatric postacute care (PPAC) hospital, and to inform institutional efforts to better meet the needs of employees facing ethical challenges and improve patient care.Study designWe surveyed health care providers at a PPAC hospital in the United States to investigate the ethical challenges they face. The anonymous, mixed-methods survey instrument included 8 multiple choice and free response questions covering the following: demographic information, frequency of encountering 33 potentially ethically-challenging issues, description of 2-3 challenging ethical scenarios, and an assessment of available institutional ethics resources.ResultsOne hundred four individuals completed the survey (response rate: 30%). The most commonly encountered ethical issues in the PPAC setting included discharge disposition (41%), inter-team (33%) and team-parent communication issues (27%), behavior problems (28%), and goals of care (24%). Further, many (54%) employees did not feel they have appropriate institutional resources to navigate ethical dilemmas, with the development of an institutional ethics committee as the most identified needed resource.ConclusionsThe most commonly encountered ethical issues in PPAC settings relate to discharge, communication, behavior, and goals of care and these challenges may be due to the unique nature of PPAC institutions. Several ethically challenging issues arising in the PPAC setting, like admissions and discharge decisions, are underexplored in the pediatric ethics literature and deserve additional research and attention. PPAC employees would likely benefit from additional resources, like the development of an institutional ethics committee to provide education and consultation.Keywords: pediatric postacute care, ethical issues

Pediatric clinical ethics is a sub-field of bioethics which investigates and provides normative guidance on ethical conflicts and dilemmas arising in the provision of clinical care to children. Pediatric clinicians and others involved in the healthcare of children sometimes face ethical dilemmas that require expert consultation or other supportive resources for resolution (often via clinical ethics committees or trained clinical ethics consultants). Since 2000, demand for ethics consultation services at hospitals belonging to the American Hospital Association (which includes both adult and pediatric institutions) has increased drastically in the United States (US), rising by 94% to an estimated 68,000 annual consultations in 2018.1 Common issues requiring ethics consultation described in the literature include decision-making authority, goals of care or treatment, and end of life, with questions arising surrounding capacity, patient wishes and autonomy, and surrogate decision-making.2, 3, 4 These typologies, which inform educational initiatives, policy development, and further research, have largely been developed in adult healthcare settings; much of the work done specifically on pediatric clinical ethics issues attend only to acute-care contexts.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12

Emerging pediatric ethics consultation data from large, acute children's hospitals shows physicians are the most likely consultation requesters, typically on patients one year old or younger.8,12 The most common issues prompting consultation in pediatric contexts include questions of decision-making authority (eg, decision-making capacity, child assent, and parental informed consent), end of life issues (eg, withholding or withdrawing life sustaining therapy, code status, and futility), and questions of patient best interest (eg, quality of life, pain and suffering, and goals of care).5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 While historically, ethics consultation has been an infrequently utilized resource at pediatric facilities,7,12, 13, 14 more recent data suggest that consult volume is increasing, with one-third of pediatric ethics consultation services participating in a 2023 study receiving 25 or more ethics consults in the past 12 months.15 The pediatric cases that receive ethics consultation are highly complex7 and typically involve patients located in an intensive care unit.9,11 Children with chronic critical illness rarely receive ethics consults.16 Thus, there is little empirical evidence describing the types of ethical issues that arise in nonacute care pediatric settings, including pediatric postacute care (PPAC).

Also referred to as pediatric specialty or pediatric rehabilitation hospitals, PPAC facilities admit children who are not ready for home, require continued hospital-level care following acute illness, and have medical or therapy goals and potential for functional improvement.17,18 PPAC facilities include a diverse range of institutions, including long-term acute care, inpatient rehabilitation, and sub-acute care, whose boundaries are frequently blurred because of a lack of consistent regulatory designation and definition.18 PPAC facilities provide heterogenous services to previously healthy children and those with preexisting comorbidities,18 including inpatient care (most commonly: occupational, physical, or speech therapy, school/education, respite), outpatient care, palliative care, medical rehabilitation, post-traumatic care, and neonatal care.19 The need for PPAC continues to grow due to technological advances which have increased survival from critical illness and help support the ongoing medical needs of children with medical complexity (CMC).20, 21, 22 In 2018, 69% of 26 surveyed PPAC facilities reported an increase in the medical complexity of patients over the past decade.19 Patients in PPAC facilities are commonly technology-dependent (eg, tracheostomy, mechanical ventilation, and parenteral nutrition) and are unable to go home due to continuous monitoring needs.18,19

There is evidence in the adult context to suggest that ethical issues arising in non–acute-care settings are different from those in acute-care settings, yet little attention has been given to pediatric nonacute care settings.23, 24, 25 While there are some limited data describing the types of ethical issues arising in the care of children with medical complexity and chronic critical illness,16,26,27 there is no direct investigation of ethical issues encountered in the PPAC setting specifically. This study investigates the nature and frequency of common ethical challenges experienced by employees at a PPAC hospital in the US and is the first empirical study to do so. A clearer sense of situations and decisions that arise as ethical dilemmas or conflicts will better direct institutional efforts to meet the needs of employees, including aiding in resolving ethical dilemmas, developing policies that address common moral questions, and creating targeted educational initiatives, all of which are aimed at improving the quality of care for children and families.

Methods

Study Population

To better understand which issues and scenarios constitute ethical dilemmas for health care providers in a PPAC hospital setting, we contacted employees at Ranken Jordan Pediatric Bridge Hospital, a 60-bed pediatric postacute care hospital in the U.S. offering both inpatient and outpatient services. Physicians, nurse practitioners, nurses, respiratory therapists, psychologists, therapists (inpatient and outpatient), child life specialists, social workers, and administrators involved in patient decision-making who had worked at the hospital for more than 3 months were included.

Survey Instrument

The mixed-methods survey instrument (Supplement) consisted of 8 multiple choice and free response questions. The first section gathered demographic information regarding professional designation and number of years at the PPAC hospital. The following section provided a list of 33 potentially ethically-challenging issues and topics and asked participants to address the frequency with which they encounter each issue as challenging – participants responded “never,” “occasionally,” “frequently,” or “does not apply to me.” Based on feedback from pilot testing, respondents were instructed to distinguish between topics they do encounter in their role, but don't experience as ethically challenging by selecting “never,” and those issues they don't encounter at all by selecting “does not apply to me”. Given that there are no validated coding schemes for pediatric ethics consultation topics, the list of potentially challenging issues and topics was generated using the issues identified in existing pediatric ethics consultation literature.8 Participants were asked to identify any additional issues they encounter that were not listed. The next section invited participants to describe two or three challenging ethical scenarios which they would have appreciated help in resolving. The last section asked participants whether they felt they had access to appropriate institutional resources when facing ethical dilemmas. Finally, responders elaborated on which ethics-related resources were currently available and which they wished were available.

Survey Process

In April 2024, an initial recruitment email and one reminder email were sent to potential participants; reminders were also announced in hospital huddles. The recruitment email stated that “The purpose of this study is to better understand what types of decisions and scenarios health care providers in a pediatric postacute care setting experience as ethical dilemmas (there is uncertainty about the morally right course of action) and ethical conflicts (there is a difference of opinion about the morally right course of action).” Participants were given 3 weeks to complete the 15-25-minute anonymous online survey through REDCap.28 Participants reviewed and consented to a recruitment statement prior to beginning the survey. No incentives were offered for participation. The study was approved by the Saint Louis University Institutional Review Board.

Coding Process

To determine which themes were most prevalent in the 117 ethically-challenging scenarios which respondents would have appreciated help in resolving, 3 coders independently assigned a primary and secondary (only if necessary) code to each scenario, intended to best capture the issue or topic addressed in each response. The coding process utilized a mixture of framework and thematic methodologies, whereby we began with a predefined, structured set of 33 topics, but allowed for the emergence of previously undefined themes. Responses were cross-referenced, with 2 of 3 coder agreement decided as a minimum threshold for final code assignment. Cases in which no initial agreement was present among coders were discussed before a final code was unanimously assigned.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

One hundred fifty of 351 employees agreed to participate and began the survey; 104 participants (response rate: 30%) reported the frequency with which they encountered ethically-challenging issues and were included in analysis. Survey respondents were most commonly nurses (N = 29, 28%) and therapists (physical, occupational, speech, recreational, and music; N = 25, 24%). Also represented were physicians and nurse practitioners (N = 16, 15%), administrators involved in patient care decision-making (N = 13, 13%), respiratory therapists (N = 10, 10%), and employees in the fields of psychology, social work, and child life (N = 9, 9%). Most participants had been employed at the PPAC hospital for between one and five years (N = 46, 44%). Other respondents worked at the PPAC hospital for 10-20 years (N = 20, 19%), 5-10 years (N = 19, 18%), 3 months-1 year (N = 14, 13%), and more than 20 years (N = 5, 5%).

Issues most commonly encountered “frequently” by respondents included discharge disposition (41%), interteam communication issues (33%), behavior problems (28%), team-parent communication issues (27%), goals of care (24%), professional boundaries (21%), admissions decisions (21%), team-patient communication issues (20%), and allocation of resources (20%). Issues most commonly encountered overall (“frequently” or “occasionally”) were discharge disposition (87%), behavior problems (83%), team-parent communication issues (82%), intrafamily conflict (82%), patient best interest (81%), patient/family demand for treatment (80%), and patient/family and provider conflict (80%). Ethically-Challenging Scenarios

Respondents provided 117 ethically-challenging scenarios which were coded to identify the most prevalent themes (Table 2). Codes utilized included the original 33 topics offered to respondents as potentially ethically-challenging and six additional codes not present in the initial survey which were added due to their frequency of usage in the free-text responses for ethically-challenging issues and scenarios. All scenarios were coded with a primary theme (N = 117) and, if applicable, a secondary theme (N = 74). Discharge disposition (eg, no discharge plan, unsafe discharge plan) was the most prevalent theme identified (15%) and is exemplified in the response: “When families have extended stays because of their own lack of resources or ability to find housing.” Lack of caregiver support was the second most coded theme (13%), illustrated by the response: “2-year-old who is no longer trach and vent dependent. Mom is able to do G-tube feeds but comes to the hospital apparently under the influence on several occasions. Mom is hospitalized for psych issues. The state will not take custody, and no family member steps up to help. What is our obligation to that child? Send him home knowing the danger? Or keep him safe in the hospital?”

Institutional Resources, Available and Necessary

While a plurality of respondents (46%) agreed they have appropriate institutional resources to face ethical dilemmas and conflicts, 33% neither agree nor disagree and 22% disagree. The currently available resources respondents (n = 59) most readily identified in a free-text format included supervisors, managers, and department leads (34%), peers and team members (24%), legal counsel (15%), the quality and safety team (15%), social work and care coordination (12%), and the medical team (10%). In a free text format, employees (n = 43) most commonly pointed to the need for an ethics committee (47%), followed by increased education and training (9%), and the ability to seek outside counsel (9%).

Discussion

The survey results indicate that the most commonly encountered issues in our PPAC hospital include admission and discharge decisions, interteam and team-parent communication/conflict, behavior problems, goals of care, and professional boundaries. While coding schemes are inconsistent, ethical issues arising most commonly in ethics consultation at children's hospitals in the acute care setting include questions of decision-making authority (eg, decision-making capacity, child assent and parental informed consent), end of life issues (eg, withholding or withdrawing life sustaining therapy, code status, futility), and questions of patient best interest (eg, quality of life, pain and suffering and goals of care).5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 While ethical challenges related to goals of care emerge in both settings, there appear to be notable differences between the most frequently identified issues in our PPAC hospital and those leading to ethics consultation in acute care settings. Notably, most existing literature on ethical challenges in pediatrics reports ethics consultation data, rather than directly surveying employees about their experiences with ethical challenges. It is reasonable to expect that pediatric caregivers experience ethical challenges in scenarios that don't ultimately lead to an ethics consultation. Further, the pediatric ethics literature focuses predominantly on issues leading to ethics consultation in the acute care setting. For example, the most commonly cited pediatric ethics textbook29 has 41 chapters covering pediatric ethics topics including “Pediatric decision-making: Informed consent, parental permission and child assent,” “The role of quality of life assessments in neonatal care,” and a section of seven chapters on end of life issues in pediatrics, but contains no discussion of admission or discharge decisions, and only indirectly references communication issues within teams and between teams and parents.

We hypothesize that certain distinctive features of the PPAC setting give rise to this set of ethical issues. For example, because demand for PPAC services is increasing while beds remain extremely limited, not all children who would benefit from admission are able to be admitted, making admission decisions a matter of scarce resource allocation.18,19 Scenarios offered in the survey acknowledged capacity limitations and pointed to ethically considering which patients would benefit the most from our services when making admission decisions. Further, because PPAC patients often have long lengths of stay compared to the acute care setting,30 quality clinical care requires the ongoing communication and coordination of many clinical team members across multiple disciplines for long periods of time alongside parent decision-makers, which may naturally give rise to more opportunities for conflict and communication difficulties. Survey respondents described conflict between teams on a patient's plan of care or the appropriate frequency of therapy services given a patient's deficits. Finally, safe discharge home from PPAC facilities requires extensive caregiver training regarding a child's complex medical needs. Thus, timing of discharge is often dependent on family readiness and availability for training, leading to discharge delays which can be a cause of staff distress. In our survey, employees recounted patients medically ready for discharge but parents hesitant to learn their care.

The ethical issues encountered in PPAC facilities may be due to PPAC's place in the care continuum for hospitalized children. A 2022-23 survey of PPAC facilities found that pediatric-specific PAC units are sparse and unevenly distributed across the US.18 According to surveyed PPAC facilities, challenges emerge frequently surrounding admissions (eg, bed unavailability, insurance approval, and transfers from referring institutions) and discharge (eg, family housing, obtaining medical equipment for the home, and financial or employment issues).18 Similarly, admission decision and discharge disposition issues were frequently reported as ethically challenging in our study. These issues are functions of external factors, including outside hospital referrals, insurance authorization, and availability of downstream options for discharge. To mitigate the impact of these external factors, it is important for PPAC facilities to maintain robust referral and care coordination teams, utilizing defined admission criteria and detailed discharge plans, the creation of which would likely benefit from an ethics perspective. The development of additional PPAC options, including increased availability of home health nursing, will also alleviate many of the ethical dilemmas encountered in PPAC facilities.

The results of this PPAC facility survey overlap with surveys of clinicians caring for NICU patients with extremely long hospitalizations and parents of CMC. Providers report debating whether it is best for patients to stay in the NICU, transfer to the PICU or another pediatric unit, or search for limited home care services.31 Continuous monitoring recommendations lead to indefinite discharge delays due to lack of family or professional in-home caregivers.32 These concerns intersect with the admissions decisions and discharge disposition themes conveyed in our study. Parents of CMC report frequent and destressing disagreements with their child's medical team about treatment27 while NICU clinicians note frequent conflict between themselves and parents or other providers about who is in charge.31,33 In our survey, respondents expressed concerns regarding interteam and team-parent communication and conflict. These limited data suggest that the ethical issues encountered in extremely long NICU stays, or in the care of CMC more generally, may be similar to those encountered in PPAC.

The survey results highlight employees’ views regarding the resources available to aid in navigating ethical conflicts and dilemmas. Less than half of respondents (46%) agreed that they have appropriate resources to navigate ethical dilemmas, indicating room for improvement from the hospital regarding the provision of available ethical resources. The most cited ethical resources utilized in our survey included supervisors, managers, team leaders, as well as peers and team members. Less prominent resources included legal counsel, quality and safety teams, social work and care coordination, and the medical team. These results indicate that both interpersonal and hierarchical relationships play a role within the institution in helping guide staff through ethical challenges. One limitation could be whether the perceived interpersonal resources (eg, supervisors, managers, team leaders, and peers) feel that they have the appropriate education and knowledge with which to guide staff. Recent literature has identified that families of CMC similarly lack ethics resources with adequate training to navigate their complex challenges.26,27 Finally, interteam communication and intrateam conflict, frequently coded ethical topics in this study, indicate the need for outside counsel or an ethics committee to serve as a neutral third party to aid resolution.

The need most expressed by respondents was the formation of a formal ethics committee. Nine percent of respondents also emphasized the need for better education and training in ethics as well as the ability to seek outside counsel. A recent survey of US hospitals found 97% have health care ethics programs whose most common functions include ethics education, policy review, and, less frequently, ethics consultation.1,34 The survey identified a deficit in ethics program services and utilization in smaller, non-academically affiliated hospitals.34 These hospitals, including PPAC facilities, may benefit from the formation of an ethics committee in coordination with internal or external ethics experts. The committee could develop and deliver ethics education to empower hospital staff to navigate the complexities of ethical situations and provide ethics consultations when serious ethical issues arise. Two factors that appear to improve clinical ethics consultation services are identifying cases in which a clinical consultation was not requested but would have improved care and earlier detection of cases in which consultation was later sought.35 Thus, the proactive identification of situations perceived as ethically challenging by employees may aid in the development of a more effective ethics consultation service.

Regardless of the type of PPAC setting, whether a unit embedded in a large acute-care hospital or a free-standing facility operating independently, these survey data suggest there is an opportunity to better address the distinct ethics support needs of PPAC providers. Additionally, there may be opportunities for collaboration with acute care-based ethics committees to share education and consultation resources, but an awareness of the distinct issues encountered in PPAC will be required. Further research is needed to determine the most appropriate and effective ethics resources to support providers in this unique context which may not mirror the tools traditionally utilized by acute care ethics committees and consultation services.

Strengths and Limitations

The survey results must be viewed within the lens of the hospital's identity as a specialty hospital. PPAC takes place in heterogenous environments with heterogenous patient populations,17 and the results from one pediatric specialty hospital might not be applicable to all PPAC settings, eg, pediatric long-term care facilities or rehabilitation hospitals. The 30% response rate may further limit generalizability of our findings. The hospital also offers both inpatient and outpatient services, and the anonymous survey did not differentiate responses from or about the 2 services. Further, this study investigated the frequency certain ethical issues were encountered, which may not indicate the relative importance of that ethical issue. The differences identified in ethical issues encountered in our PPAC hospital and those encountered in acute care settings are based on ethics consultation literature; given that ethics consultations are just one avenue used to address ethical issues,13 there may be more overlap between settings than the literature suggests. Future research, specifically designed to elucidate differences between settings, could address the under-explored ethical challenges in PPAC.

This study represents the first empirical exploration of ethical issues commonly encountered in a PPAC hospital context and demonstrates that the issues most commonly encountered in a postacute care setting include admission and discharge decisions, interteam and team-parent communication/conflict, behavior problems, goals of care, and professional boundaries. While studies are limited, data suggest that the issues most commonly leading to ethics consultation in the acute care setting are patient best interest, decision-making authority, and end of life. Several topics arising in the PPAC setting, like admissions decisions and discharge decisions, are under-explored in the pediatric ethics literature and require additional scholarly attention. Further, this study suggests that the creation of an institutional ethics committee with some expertise in these and other pediatric ethics issues would be perceived by PPAC employees as a beneficial resource.
