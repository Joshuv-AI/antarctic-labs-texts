Engineering PVC/TPU hybrid membranes for high-efficiency pharmaceutical wastewater treatment using MBR technology
Authors: S. Masoumi, Y. Jafarzadeh, H. Hazrati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pharmaceutical industries discharge complex wastewaters rich in organic pollutants and microorganisms to the environment, which necessitate the engineering of high-performance and antibiofouling ultrafiltration (UF) membranes for long-term membrane bioreactor (MBR) operations. Nevertheless, the intrinsic hydrophobicity of conventional polyvinylchloride (PVC) membranes restricts their practical usage. In this study, a series of novel blended PVC/thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) ultrafiltration membranes were fabricated via the non-solvent induced phase separation (NIPS) technique, aiming at treating actual pharmaceutical wastewater by an MBR system. Spectroscopic analyses (FTIR, XRD, DSC, and Raman) were conducted to confirm the molecular interactions and compatibility between PVC and TPU, while EDX and elemental mapping verified the existence of TPU-related elements and their homogeneous distribution. The inherent hydrophobicity of PVC was effectively overcome through TPU incorporation, resulting in a reduced contact angle from 84.9° to 69.4°. The incorporation of TPU significantly altered membrane morphology, increasing the porosity to 83.6% and exhibiting a nearly threefold enhancement in pure water flux (PWF), rising from 43.3 to 173.1 LMH, accompanied by enhanced equilibrium water content of 80.4%. During MBR operation, 96.7% COD removal, 41% reduction in irreversible fouling ratio (IFR), and a 60.7% flux recovery ratio (FRR) were achieved. The addition of TPU increased elongation up to 96.2%, abrasion resistance by 10%, and antibacterial properties by > 97%. Overall, the developed PVC/TPU blended UF membrane exhibits great potential for stable and efficient application in MBR systems for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42781-9.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of pharmaceutical production and consumption, driven by medical advancements and population growth, has led to the continuous release of pharmaceutical pollutants (PPs) into aquatic environments1. The continual presence of PPs in aquatic environments poses serious health risks, including growth suppression, cancer risk, and bioaccumulation within the food chain2. The removal of such materials from water resources is not only critical for public health protection but also essential for addressing global water scarcity. This objective can be achieved through a range of purification and elimination methods, such as enzyme decolorization, adsorption, solvent extraction, biological treatments, electrochemical treatment, photocatalysis, and membrane-based separations3–5. Among the existing treatment technologies, Membrane Bioreactors (MBRs) have emerged as a highly effective technology by integrating biological treatment with membrane filtration, thereby achieving superior separation efficiency and enhanced effluent quality6. Indeed, the incorporation of membranes into biological treatment systems markedly improves biomass retention and contaminant removal efficiency, while simultaneously providing greater operational flexibility, reducing spatial requirements, and minimizing excess sludge production. 7–9. Polymeric membranes are widely utilized in MBRs because of their superior separation performance, mechanical strength, and resistance to chemical degradation10. Among various polymers, polyvinyl chloride (PVC) stands out as a favorable choice owing to its low cost, excellent processability, thermal and chemical stability, and long operational lifespan11. Nevertheless, the inherently hydrophobic nature of PVC membranes leads to severe fouling and biofouling, resulting in permeate flux decline, increased energy consumption, and reduced operational sustainability in MBR applications12. So, to overcome fouling, surface and bulk modification has been developed13. Bulk modification is particularly attractive as it improves both surface characteristics and internal pore structure and is typically achieved through two main approaches: blending with hydrophilic nanoparticles or with hydrophilic polymers. Incorporation of hydrophilic nanoparticles often suffers from nanoparticle aggregation, leaching during operation, and poor interfacial compatibility with the polymer matrix14,15. In contrast, blending with hydrophilic polymers offers more homogeneous distribution, improved interfacial stability, and enhanced mechanical integrity16.

Amphiphilic polymers represent an effective class of modifiers that possess a dual-segment structure comprising both hydrophobic and hydrophilic moieties17. During membrane formation, the hydrophobic segment anchors firmly within the membrane matrix while the hydrophilic segment naturally migrates toward the membrane surface, forming a hydration layer that enhances water permeability and fouling resistance18. Previous studies have demonstrated that amphiphilic polymer blending can significantly improve membrane hydrophilicity, permeability, and antifouling performance. For example, Wu et al.19 prepared an amphiphilic copolymer of PVC-g-PEGMA and constructed a blended ultrafiltration membrane of PVC/PVC-g-PEGMA, which showed improved hydrophilicity, PWF, and antifouling property compared with pure PVC membrane.

Thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) is a versatile block copolymer with alternating sequences of hard and soft segments offering superior elasticity, inherent hydrophilicity, and resistance to chemical degradation20. As a blending polymer material in membrane technology, there is a growing effort to exploit the hydrophilic property and superior flexibility offered by TPU21. Thus, adding this polymer can be a reliable method to produce high-quality composite membranes with high permeability. Yuan and Dan-Li22 showed that incorporating TPU into PVDF dramatically altered membrane morphology and transport properties. The PVDF/TPU (80:20) blend membrane showed much higher water flux and porosity than pure PVDF. Thus, TPU acts both as a mechanical modifier and as a morphology controller in phase-inversion systems, often increasing porosity and permeation.

In MBR systems treating real wastewater, biofouling caused by microbial attachment and biofilm formation remains one of the most critical challenges23. Microorganisms’ accumulation leads to biofilm formation, raises transmembrane pressure, lowers permeate flux, damages the membrane’s structure, and shortens its lifespan24. Therefore, integrating antibacterial functionality with enhanced surface hydrophilicity is essential for maintaining long-term MBR performance.

This study is motivated by the need for developing and fabricating a low-cost and efficient membrane for applications in MBR systems to treat increasingly complex pharmaceutical wastewater by reducing fouling, enhancing flux, and improving membrane flexibility through PVC/TPU blending. Unlike most previous studies conducted using synthetic or model wastewaters, this work focuses on real pharmaceutical wastewater, which is significantly more complex and challenging, thereby enhancing the practical relevance of the findings.

Therefore, in this study, PVC/TPU blend ultrafiltration membranes were fabricated via the phase inversion method to address this knowledge gap. The effects of TPU content on membrane morphology, porosity, hydrophilicity, mechanical properties, and antibacterial/antifouling performance were systematically investigated. Furthermore, the membranes were evaluated within an MBR system treating real pharmaceutical wastewater to assess their permeability, rejection efficiency, and operational stability under realistic conditions.

Experimental

Materials

PVC ((C2H3Cl)n, grade E-6834, Density = 1.41 g/cm3, molecular weight (MW) = 90,000 g/mol) was selected as the primary membrane-forming polymer and obtained from Arvand Petrochemical Co., Iran. Silicon carbide (400 mesh) and Polyethylene glycol (PEG, MW = 400 Da) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. TPU (Density = 1.19 g/cm3) was supplied by Wanhua Chemical Group Co., Ltd, China. Based on the manufacturer’s specifications, a polyester-based TPU material was used for this study. This polymer has hard segments made from methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI) and soft segments made from polyester polyols, and is typically found as a commercially available polymer in industrial use where flexibility and mechanical durability are needed; Fig. 1 illustrates the molecular structure of the TPU with hard and soft segments utilized in this work. DMF and Isobutanol were purchased from Merck. The pharmaceutical effluent and activated sludge applied to the experiments were obtained from the wastewater treatment facility of Dana Pharmaceutical Company, located in Tabriz, Iran.

Preparation of pure PVC and PVC/TPU blend membrane

The non-solvent induced phase separation (NIPS) method was used to make all of the membranes. To fabricate the neat PVC membrane, PEG 400 was first dissolved in DMF at 60 °C. Then, 15 wt% PVC was slowly added. The solution was stirred for 24 h to make sure it was completely mixed. The same process was used for the blended membranes, but after adding PEG, known amounts of PVC and TPU were added to the solvent and stirred for 24 h to make a uniform mixture. The homogeneous casting solutions were then left at 60 °C for 2 h to be degassed. Then, flat-sheet membranes were cast by pouring the dope solution onto glass plates at 60 °C using a film applicator at 150 μm. The phase inversion process started when the coated glass plate was quickly put into a distilled water coagulation bath that was kept at 30 °C. Once the phase separation was complete, the membranes were carefully taken off the glass substrate and kept in distilled water for 24 h to remove any leftover solvent. Lastly, the membranes were dried in the air and kept in sealed zip-lock bags. Table 1 shows the exact compositions of the materials used in membrane fabrication.

Characterization of membranes

Morphology

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, MIRA3 Tescan-Xmu Co., Czech Republic), applied at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, was used to analyze the morphology and pore structure of the fabricated membranes. The membranes were first submerged in liquid nitrogen for a minimum of 5 min before being broken to create clean break surfaces for cross-sectional imaging. To improve electrical conductivity, a thin layer of gold was applied to the surface and cross-sectional specimens before imaging. Using ImageJ software, the FESEM surface images were analyzed to determine the average surface pore diameter, pore density, and pore distribution. Additionally, Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was used to verify the presence of PVC and TPU, while elemental mapping was applied to ensure their even distribution throughout both the surface and cross-sectional areas of the membrane.

Chemical, thermal, and mechanical properties

To determine if the TPU structure affected the amorphous nature of the PVC membranes, XRD analysis was conducted over a 2θ range from 5° to 50° with a step size of 0.02° using a Tongda TD-3700 instrument (China). This analysis employed a Cu-Kα radiation source with a wavelength of λ = 1.5406 Å at room temperature.

A Tensor 27 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) was used to perform attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy to examine the chemical structure in the M0 and M3 membranes. For this aim, at room temperature, KBr pellets were used for measurements within a 400–4000 cm⁻¹ scanning range.

Raman spectroscopy was performed on M0 and M3 membranes using a Raman microscope (Andor Technology-UnimDron-A, Korea) equipped with a 633 nm laser and a 1 μm spot size, covering the spectral range of 500–4000 cm⁻¹.

The thermal properties of M0 and M3 membranes were evaluated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC 200 F3, Netzsch, Germany), including the glass transition temperature (Tg), melting point (Tm), and crystallization temperature (Tc), where 10 mg of each membrane was placed in aluminum pans and sealed, equilibrated at 20 °C, heated to 200 °C at 10 °C/min, cooled from 200 °C to − 70 °C, and reheated from − 70 to 200 °C, while an inert nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate of 50 mL/min was maintained to avoid oxidation.

The mechanical properties of all membranes fabricated were evaluated by means of a tensile testing device (Tensile Strength Test Zwicky & Roell, Germany). Three identical pieces from different positions on the membrane were carefully cut into 1 × 5 cm sections, and each sample was stretched at 10 mm per minute. The mean value has been calculated and reported for each of the membranes25.

The abrasion resistance of the fabricated membranes was evaluated to assess their mechanical durability under MBR operation. Detailed experimental procedure is provided in the Supplementary Information (Section S1-1).

Surface properties

AFM analysis using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Nanosurf Mobile S microscope, Nanosurf Co., Switzerland) was used to investigate how TPU blending affected the top surface roughness of the membranes. Membranes were measured 1 cm by 1 cm, and imaging was carried out over an 8 μm by 8 μm surface area.

Using a contact angle goniometer (ZAM104-B, Zolalan Co., Iran), the contact angle between the water droplet and the membrane’s surface was measured to assess the hydrophilic nature of the membranes26. In this instance, each sample was meticulously cut to 1 × 2 cm2 measurements, and the mean value of the water contact angles was reported after being measured three times from various locations on the membrane surface. Lastly, Cooling Tech software was used to analyze the collected data.

Furthermore, the surface charge properties of the membranes were characterized by determining the point of zero charge (PZC). The detailed experimental procedure is presented in the Supplementary Information (Section S1-2).

Antibacterial properties of the membranes

Plate colony enumeration of bacterial growth on the membrane surfaces (plate colony counting), as described by Ahsani et al., Ranjbaran et al., and Yang et al., was used to assess antibacterial efficacy, along with a few adjustments25,27,28. Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria were represented by S. aureus and E. coli, respectively. Autoclaving of all tools used was done before conducting this experiment to ensure no contamination. Bacteria were produced from mixing 20 µL of inoculum with 50 µL of growth medium and incubating for 20 h. Each of the membranes (M0 and M3) was then submerged in 20 mL of a growth medium, with growth media without membranes used as the blank control. The mixture was autoclaved for 3 h at 120 ℃ with 20 mL of fluid growth devoid of additives. A specific amount of the pre-cultured bacterial mixture was then added to all liquid media (both those with and without membrane samples) and shaken for 3 h in order to cultivate the bacteria. All incubations were conducted at 35–37 °C as commonly applied for antibacterial plate counts to allow bacterial colony growth, and the contact time between bacteria and membrane surfaces was set at 24 h to align with standard antibacterial exposure periods used in literature. Following a 100-time dilution of each bacterial suspension, 20 µL of each was evenly distributed on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar dishes. After each prepared plate was incubated for a full day, the number of colonies on each plate was counted and recorded. The ratio of colony numbers on agar plates (N) to colony numbers on blank agar plates (NBlank) determines the membranes’ antibacterial capacity. The mean value was recorded after each test was run three times.

PWF and MBR operation

Before PWF, porosity and equilibrium water content (EWC) of all fabricated membranes were determined using gravimetric methods. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information (Section S1-3). All fabricated membranes undergo filtration and permeability/PWF tests in a lab-scale submerged MBR system with a 3 L total effective volume. A Teflon flat-sheet module was used for membrane assembly. It offered an effective filtration area of 7.065 cm² and had an average thickness of 60 ± 10 μm, measured with a micrometer. An air disperser was positioned beneath the membrane modules to continuously aerate the sludge in order to reduce the buildup of activated sludge flocs on the membrane’s surface, avoid cake layer formation by shear-induced cleaning of the membrane surface, and supply dissolved oxygen for microorganisms to maintain. A vacuum pump was utilized to maintain the required critical transmembrane pressure (TMP), and the module was connected to a graduated vessel for the collection and measurement of permeate. In the current study, the TMP-step method reported by Wang et al. was used to determine the TMP of all membranes29. Filtration was carried out under sub-critical conditions at a stable pressure of 0.1 bar over 6 h. Results and discussion

Morphology

Figures 3 and 4 illustrate the surface and cross-sectional FE-SEM micrographs along with the corresponding EDX spectra and the pore size distribution (PSD) of all fabricated membranes. The M0 membrane exhibits a relatively smooth and dense surface morphology with limited visible porosity, which is typical of pure PVC due to its rigid polymeric structure and low free volume39,40. Increasing the TPU content from 2 to 8 wt% leads to a decrease in surface pore size while simultaneously increasing pore density. This behavior can be attributed to the flexible and amorphous molecular structure of TPU, which enhances chain mobility and disturbs the compact arrangement of PVC molecules and facilitates faster penetration of the nonsolvent (water) into the casting solution41. Moreover, due to the presence of polar urethane groups (–NH, –C=O), TPU exhibits greater hydrophilicity than PVC, which accelerates the solvent–nonsolvent exchange rate at the membrane interface. This enhanced exchange promotes a higher nucleation rate during phase separation, resulting in the simultaneous formation of numerous smaller pores42.

Based on the cross-sectional images of membranes, a typical asymmetric structure analogous to conventional PVC NIPS membranes, featuring a dense selective top layer, a porous finger-like sublayer, and a macro-void-rich bottom region were observed12,43. The cross-sectional images reveal that with increasing TPU content from 2 to 8 wt%, the thickness of the bottom dense layer gradually decreases. Moreover, the finger-like pores become more continuous and interconnected with the macro-voids at the lower region. In membranes M2, M3, and M4, these finger-like voids are elongated throughout the membrane structure. Such morphological evolution enhances pore connectivity and facilitates more efficient mass transport, thereby improving water permeability while maintaining acceptable selectivity induced by decreased surface pore size. In addition, the cross-sectional micrographs show that the finger-like pores become narrower with increasing TPU content. These morphological changes, including the thinning of the bottom dense layer, elongation of finger-like pores, and their stronger interconnection with the macro-voids, can be attributed to the enhanced hydrophilicity of the casting solution at higher TPU concentrations44.

EDX spectroscopy was used to characterize the elemental composition of all fabricated membranes, with the spectra shown in Fig. 3. In the case of the M0 membrane, the EDX spectrum showed that the main components were carbon (C) and chlorine (Cl), consistent with pure PVC’s intrinsic chemical structure, and a minor oxygen (O) peak was also present due either to superficial oxidation during the manufacturing process or an external source such as contamination from the atmosphere. Upon TPU incorporation in M1 to M4 membranes, the spectra exhibited a pronounced O peak accompanied by nitrogen (N) signals, which highlight the contribution of urethane functional groups within the TPU chains. This finding is consistent with the characteristic presence of urethane linkages within the molecular backbone of TPU and confirms successful TPU integration into the PVC matrix. Increased oxygen content has been associated with the introduction of hydrophilic functional groups. These groups can improve membrane wettability and overall hydrophilicity. This leads to better water flow and less fouling in composite membranes. For instance, adding hydrophilic nanofillers like TiO₂ and ZnO has been shown to boost surface oxygen content and enhance hydrophilicity and performance in PVC-based membranes. This improvement happens because of increased water affinity and better pore structure, as earlier studies have shown45. Additionally, higher nitrogen signals in TPU-containing membranes have been tied to nitrogen-rich polymer segments or functional groups that change surface chemistry. This change can affect interactions with solutes and foulants in water.

One important factor influencing the final membrane’s overall performance is the uniform dispersion of TPU in the PVC matrix. Optimal interaction, stable mechanical properties, improved hydrophilicity, and thermal stability are all guaranteed by proper TPU dispersion. On the other hand, pronounced phase separation and the creation of heterogeneous domains may result from inadequate or poor TPU dispersion within the PVC matrix46. In blended polymeric membranes that are fabricated via the NIPS method, the dispersion state of the secondary polymer is strongly governed by thermodynamic compatibility and phase inversion kinetics, which directly affect the final membrane morphology and performance47. In light of these circumstances, elemental distribution across the surface and cross-sectional area of M0 and M3 membranes was assessed using EDX mapping; the findings are shown in Supplementary Information (Section S2-1, Figure-S1) and Figs. 5, respectively. The M0 membrane’s EDS elemental mapping verified the uniform distribution of C and Cl elements. The presence of the anticipated elements (C, Cl, O, and N) is evident on the M3 membrane in both surface and cross-section areas. The emergence of N and O signals in the M3 membrane, which are absent in the M0 membrane, can be directly attributed to the incorporation of TPU segments containing the urethane functional group48. A successful integration of TPU segments (urethane linkages) throughout the membrane structure is indicated by the homogeneous dispersion of N and O across the surface and the sublayer, while the uniform distribution of C and Cl validates the preservation of the PVC backbone. Such elemental homogeneity is widely regarded as a strong indication of favorable interfacial compatibility between blended polymers and the absence of macroscopic phase separation during membrane formation46. This elemental uniformity is a clear sign that the PVC and TPU interfacial compatibility was maintained during the fabrication process, preventing agglomeration. The even distribution of N and O in the porous substructure, as seen from the cross-sectional view, indicates that the TPU chains were adequately embedded in the bulk membrane in addition to being confined to the surface. This finding suggests that TPU migration was not limited to surface enrichment but occurred throughout the membrane matrix, which is beneficial for achieving consistent hydrophilicity, mechanical reinforcement, and fouling resistance across the entire membrane thickness46.

Structural, chemical, thermal, and mechanical properties

The XRD characterization of M0 and M3 membranes was performed to elucidate the structural features of the membranes, as the degree of crystallinity plays a pivotal role in determining their mechanical stability, thermal resistance, permeability, and fouling behavior. Figure 6a presents the diffraction patterns of the M0 and M3 membranes. For the M0 membrane, the diffractogram indicates PVC’s amorphous and/or semi-crystalline structure, with three broad reflections located at 2θ = 17.69° (d = 5.01 Å), 19.08° (d = 4.64 Å), and 24.65° (d = 3.60 Å) 49,50. The peaks at 17.69° and 24.65° may be attributed to the ordered packing of PVC chains in crystalline regions, whereas the smaller peak at 19.08° may be associated with an alternative chain packing arrangement. Upon incorporating TPU into the PVC matrix, two new diffraction signals appear at 2θ = 20.16° (d = 4.40 Å) and 23.78° (d = 3.73 Å), where the broader reflection corresponds to an amorphous phase, while the relatively sharper peak suggests either the presence of minor crystalline domains induced by TPU or a rearrangement of PVC chains triggered by TPU blending51,52. Interestingly, the disappearance of the 19.08° reflection, typical of neat PVC, suggests that the crystallinity of PVC is reduced due to the disruption of PVC chain packing. Furthermore, the characteristic PVC peaks at 2θ = 17.69° and 24.65° shift slightly toward higher diffraction angles, which could be attributed to a decrease in the interplanar spacing (d-spacing) that may be related to an increase in chain interactions and increased molecular packing in the composite membrane.

The ATR-FTIR spectrum of M0, as illustrated in Fig. 6b, exhibits asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching vibrations at 2954.38 cm⁻¹ and 2859.08 cm⁻¹, respectively, as well as C–H vibrations at 745.11 cm⁻¹, C–Cl stretching at 1221.77 cm⁻¹, C–Cl bending at 698.65 cm⁻¹, C–H bending at 1459.11 cm⁻¹, and CH₂ bending modes at 1382.22 cm⁻¹, with the appearance of extra peaks due to chemical additives added during PVC synthesis53. Meanwhile, the FTIR spectrum of the M₃ membrane reveals additional absorption peaks, verifying the effective incorporation of TPU into the PVC matrix. A noticeable band appearing at around 3505.28 cm⁻¹ corresponds to N–H stretching vibrations of urethane groups, providing direct evidence of TPU presence. Furthermore, the strong signal near 1729.91 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C=O stretching, which is characteristic of urethane and ester moieties in TPU chains. Additional peaks at 1599.93, 1278.00, and 1072.69 cm⁻¹ are linked to aromatic C=C stretching, C–N stretching, and C–O–C stretching vibrations in ether linkages, respectively. At the same time, the preservation of the fundamental absorption bands of PVC confirms that both polymers are successfully retained within the hybrid membrane structure.

Raman spectroscopy was employed to elucidate the molecular interactions between PVC and TPU. Raman spectra of the M0 and the M3 membranes are presented in Fig. 6c. In the M0 membrane, bands observed at 623.46 cm⁻¹ and 679.77 cm⁻¹ are assigned to C–Cl stretching modes, the band at 1090.28 cm⁻¹ is assigned to C–C stretching in the polymer backbone, and signals at 1424.47 cm⁻¹, 1333.82 cm⁻¹ and 2911.26 cm⁻¹ are due to CH₂ bending and C–H stretching vibrations, respectively. These spectral features are consistent with reported Raman fingerprints of PVC, thereby confirming the structural stability of the unmodified polymer54. Blending TPU with PVC gave rise to new Raman signals, highlighting the contribution of TPU-specific functional groups. The bands observed at 1248.00 cm⁻¹ are assigned to C–N stretching of the urethane groups, while the bands observed at 1620.83 cm⁻¹ and 1687.61 cm⁻¹ are assigned to aromatic ring vibrations of diisocyanate groups and C=O stretching of the carbonyl group of the urethane functional groups, respectively55. However, the broad band observed at 3334.91 cm⁻¹ is assigned to hydrogen-bonded N–H stretching. The presence of TPU-related peaks in addition to the PVC bands indicates the successful inclusion of TPU in the PVC matrix, while the lack of any deviation in the PVC fingerprint bands suggests that the TPU inclusion does not alter the PVC structure. However, N–H band and slight band broadening reveal weak intermolecular interactions, probably hydrogen bonding between TPU urethane groups and PVC chains, which are likely to enhance interfacial compatibility and improve the physicochemical properties of the composite membrane48.

As indicated in the data highlighted in Table 3, the delta solubility parameter difference between PVC and TPU is approximately 0.13. Based on solubility parameter theory, polymer pairs with a Δδ = (δSPi – δSPj) value below 1.02 MPa⁰·⁵ are regarded as miscible. Therefore, PVC and TPU can be considered thermodynamically compatible. In this research, thermal analysis (DSC) was employed to assess the miscibility of PVC with TPU and to examine the influence of TPU on the glass transition temperature (Tg) of PVC-based membranes. It is generally recognized that the presence of a single Tg in DSC thermograms provides strong evidence of good miscibility between two polymers57. Accordingly, DSC analyses were performed for the M0 and the M3 membranes, and the resulting thermograms are presented in Fig. 6d. As illustrated, both M0 and M3 exhibited a single Tg, confirming the formation of a homogeneous single-phase system and demonstrating successful miscibility between PVC and TPU. Moreover, the incorporation of TPU reduced the Tg from 80.9 °C in M0 to 77.0 °C in M3 membrane. This downward shift can be ascribed to the flexible soft segments of TPU, which enhance chain mobility and consequently lower the energy barrier for the glass transition. In addition, TPU behaves as an internal plasticizer within the blend, imparting flexibility and slightly decreasing the rigidity of the membrane matrix, thereby accounting for the observed reduction in Tg.

The mechanical performance of all fabricated membranes is presented in Table 4. In the M0 membrane, a tensile strength of 3.50 MPa in combination with an elongation of 56.59% indicated the rigid nature of this membrane. With increasing TPU in the PVC membrane, a marked transition in the mechanical properties of the membranes occurred. The tensile strength of the membrane slightly reduced to 3.16 MPa, whereas the elongation increased to a marked extent of 96.21%. This opposing response of the membrane characteristics undoubtedly reveals the TPU moiety’s influence on the mechanics of the material. TPU is a type of segmented elastomer that contains rigid hard segments in combination with flexible soft segments58. These flexible segments help in increasing the chain mobility of the material, thereby increasing the deformability of the co-polymers before failure59. Moreover, the rigid segments impart a certain extent of structural integrity to these co-polymers.

The slight reduction in tensile strength mainly stems from the decreased chain entanglement and intermolecular interactions within the PVC network once TPU chains are introduced. Moreover, the introduction of TPU causes a change in the internal morphology of the membrane as evidenced by FESEM analysis, producing a more homogeneous morphology characterized by smaller, more densely populated pores. This porosity, combined with high free volume, generates a greater degree of space to facilitate chain mobility, thus improving ductility at the expense of load transfer efficiency60,61.

Surface properties

AFM characterization provides quantitative and topographical insights into the membrane surface, enabling the evaluation of morphological changes induced by polymer blending. The three-dimensional AFM images and the corresponding roughness parameters for each membrane are presented in Fig. 7 and Table 5, respectively. According to the AFM images, the average surface roughness (Sa) of the M0 membrane was 4.43 nm, which gradually increased to 4.49, 5.07, 5.81, and 7.94 nm with the incorporation of 2, 4, 6, and 8 wt% TPU, respectively. This indicates a slight yet consistent enhancement in nanoscale roughness upon TPU addition. The observed trend can be attributed to the intrinsic molecular and chemical structural differences between PVC and TPU. PVC possesses linear chains containing polar chlorine atoms, which promote relatively rigid chain packing and a smoother surface morphology62. In contrast, the TPU used in this study is a polyester-based segmented copolymer that can establish intermolecular hydrogen bonds and form microphase-separated domains63. In addition, the carbonyl groups in the polyester backbone facilitate the formation of strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds with urethane linkages, leading to microphase-separated domains and localized surface heterogeneities64,65. The introduction of TPU into the PVC matrix, therefore, induces subtle phase heterogeneities and localized density variations at the nanoscale, resulting in the formation of fine surface asperities. However, the overall roughness variation remains within the nanometer scale, suggesting good interfacial compatibility and homogeneous dispersion of TPU within the PVC network, which further influences the membrane’s wettability. A comparable roughness enhancement mechanism has been observed by Monsefi et al. 66, wherein the incorporation of amphiphilic PEG-b-PDCE copolymer into PVC blend resulted in micro-phase separated domains on its surface, thereby resulting in increased nano-roughness on its surface as verified by AFM.

Hydrophilicity refers to the material’s affinity for water, and an increase in this property allows water to pass through the membrane more easily67. To assess the level of hydrophilicity in the fabricated membranes, their static water contact angles were measured, and the results are shown in Table 6. As shown in Table 6, the M0 membrane displayed a high contact angle of 84.99°, reflecting the hydrophobic nature of the PVC membrane. With the incorporation of TPU, the contact angle progressively decreased to 81.45°, 76.55°, 70.65°, and 69.45° for M1 to M4 membranes, revealing a clear improvement in surface hydrophilicity. The improved hydrophilicity can be attributed to the presence of carbonyl (–C = O), ester (–COO–), and urethane (–NH–CO–O–) linkages in the polyester-based TPU, which can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules68. During the phase inversion process, TPU behaves as an amphiphilic polymer; its polar ester segments tend to migrate toward the membrane-water interface to minimize surface free energy, while nonpolar moieties remain within the bulk as molecular anchors69. This orientation enhances surface wettability and ensures TPU stability within the PVC matrix. Furthermore, based on the Wenzel model, the slight increase in surface roughness caused by TPU addition reinforces the intrinsic hydrophilicity (contact angle < 90°) through capillary effects70. Therefore, the observed decline in contact angle can be ascribed to the combined effects of chemical modification and morphological changes induced by TPU blending. To illustrate this concept, a schematic representation depicting the possible hydrogen-bonding interactions between water molecules and the active functional groups present in the PVC/TPU composite membrane was designed and is presented in Supplementary Information (Section S2-3 and Figure-S3). Antibacterial properties

The antibacterial activity of the M0 and M3 membranes was evaluated against Escherichia coli (Gram-negative) and Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive) by the plate colony counting method on agar plates, and the results are shown in Fig. 9. As illustrated in Fig. 9a, the control plate without any membrane displayed the highest colony counts, as a result of unimpeded bacterial growth. In contrast, both investigated membranes suppressed colony formation, with the M3 membrane exerting a stronger antibacterial effect than the M0 membrane. To shed light on this specific property of the membranes, quantitative analysis in Fig. 9b is presented as further support. These data emphasize that adding TPU to PVC improves the efficiency of the membrane’s inhibitory property against bacteria. The E. coli colonies reduced to 4400 CFU/mL and 3800 CFU/mL for the M0 and M3 membranes, respectively, and the S. aureus colonies decreased to 4400 CFU/mL and 3000 CFU/mL for the M0 and M3 membranes, respectively. These results were obtained from 154,000 CFU/mL E. coli and 13,400 CFU/mL S. aureus colonies in the control samples. The data for N/NBlank ratios also indicated similar results, with a decrease to 2.85 for E. coli and 2.46 for S. aureus. This clearly explains the high antibacterial effectiveness of the altered membranes. Consequently, both fabricated membranes demonstrated antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus, and this effect was further enhanced with the incorporation of TPU71. Such behavior is likely associated with the synergistic effects of PVC’s structural stability and TPU’s hydrophilic, flexible segments, which together create a surface environment unfavorable for bacterial proliferation72. This dual mechanism not only reduces bacterial adhesion but also disrupts biofilm formation, making the composite membrane a promising material for applications requiring enhanced antimicrobial properties.

Filtration

The porosity and equilibrium water content (EWC) results are reported in the Supplementary Information (Section S2-5, Table-S3), demonstrating that TPU incorporation promotes higher porosity and water uptake in the membranes. As well as improved hydrophilicity, water content, and porosity, the PWF data indicate a significant increase in PWF in PVC membranes when blended with TPU. ​This enhancement is critical for high-performance membrane applications, especially in water treatment41,76. As indicated in Table 6 M0 membrane exhibits a PWF of 43.29 ± 6.89 LMH, which dramatically increases with the addition of TPU and reaches the maximum amount (173.10 ± 4.98) in the M4 membrane. This result can be attributed to improved hydrophilicity and increased porosity, which are introduced by the addition of TPU. In a study, Qu et al. demonstrated that the reduced contact angle induced by TPU incorporation confirmed the improved hydrophilicity of the PVDF membrane, which in turn contributed to a higher PWF77.

Table 6 reports the COD removal efficiencies obtained, serving as an indicator of the effluent quality following membrane treatment. In MBR systems, the removal of COD is largely governed by the metabolic activity of the activated sludge. The membrane’s role is primarily that of a physical barrier, with minimal contribution to the actual biodegradation of organic pollutants78. The observed COD removal efficiencies for the membranes showed that the initial COD removal efficiency for the M0 was 94.26% ± 0.12. This efficiency progressively increased with the incorporation of TPU and reached 96.72 ± 0.13 in the M3 membrane. It is evident that all membranes illustrated more COD removal efficiency than that of M0. This continuous enhancement can be primarily ascribed to the improved hydrophilicity of the membrane surface induced by TPU incorporation, which facilitated stronger interactions between the membrane and water molecules79. Consequently, the enhanced surface wettability promoted more effective permeation pathways to water molecules and increased the ability of the membrane to reject organic pollutants, thereby elevating the overall COD removal efficiency80. Additionally, TPU contributed to the development of smaller surface pores, as illustrated in the PSD figure, offering more COD removal efficiency in all blended membranes. Along with this, electrostatic interaction between the negatively charged membrane surface and negatively charged organic foulants further suppresses the approach of pollutants to the membrane surface and contributes to the higher COD removal efficiencies81. However, when the TPU content was further increased to 8 wt%, a slight decline in COD removal efficiency (96.47%) was recorded. This marginal decrease may be attributed to the excessive TPU loading, which can lead to agglomeration within the polymer matrix, resulting to ununiform surface pore structure.

To evaluate the practical applicability of the fabricated membranes, filtration experiments were carried out using real pharmaceutical wastewater in a submerged MBR. The flux–time profiles were monitored over a period of 360 min under a subcritical TMP pressure of 0.1 bar. The use of real wastewater rather than synthetic feed provides a more realistic assessment of membrane performance, as it contains a complex mixture of recalcitrant organic compounds, suspended solids, and microbial populations typically encountered in pharmaceutical effluents82,83. Operating at a subcritical pressure was deliberately chosen to minimize the influence of excessive compaction or pressure-driven fouling, thereby allowing a clearer evaluation of the intrinsic antifouling properties of the membranes84. As is evident from Fig. 11, the flux profiles during pharmaceutical wastewater treatment in the MBR were descending for all fabricated membranes. The M0 membrane exhibited the lowest and most unstable performance, with a sharp decline in flux at the initial stages of filtration, which declined from 41.60 LMH at the start of the filtration process to 6.79 LMH after 360 min. This rapid deterioration can be attributed to the inherent hydrophobicity of the base polymer and the strong tendency of organic matter and microbial species to adhere to its surface, leading to severe fouling85. In contrast, the incorporation of TPU (M₁–M₄ membranes) significantly enhanced both the initial and steady-state fluxes. On the other hand, incorporation of TPU (M₁–M₄ membranes) resulted in a marked enhancement of both initial and steady-state flux. The flux percentages retained are 49.29% for M₁ (from 56.83 to 28.82 LMH), 65.89% for M₂ (from 72.16 to 47.54 LMH), 68.18% for M₃ (from 112.06 to 76.41 LMH), and 69.32% for M₄ (from 82.35 to 56.83 LMH). The incorporation of increasing amounts of 2–8 wt% of TPU resulted in improved hydrophilicity, pore formation, and surface characteristics. This resulted in increased water flux and resistance to fouling. Also, membrane M₃ possessed the largest and most consistent flux over the whole operational period. This means that there is a proper balance between hydrophilicity and flexibility. The improved performance of TPU-modified membranes can be ascribed to several synergistic effects, such as enhanced surface wettability and reduced pore size distribution. In addition, the reduced flux decline can also be attributed to electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged membrane surface and the charged foulants at the operational pH, which effectively suppresses foulant deposition and pore blockage86. Interestingly, although the incorporation of 8 wt% TPU (M₄) further improved the flux compared with M₀–M3, no substantial enhancement was observed relative to M₃. This suggests that beyond a certain concentration, the additional TPU may partially obstruct pore channels or induce phase aggregation, offsetting further hydrophilic benefits87.

Fouling is one of the biggest challenges in membranes that are utilized in treating water because it is known to greatly influence the permeate, selectivity, and stability of operation88. Fouling is known to be caused by the accumulation of organic and inorganic matter, colloids, and biological materials onto the membranes or in their pores, thus restricting mass transfer or increasing the transmembrane pressure. Fouling causes a reduction in process efficiency, increased operation costs, and decreased lifespan of the membranes89. For membranes to be quantitatively analyzed in their foulability performance, there is a wide usage of parameters such as total fouling ratio (TFR), reversible fouling ratio (RFR), irreversible fouling ratio (IFR), and flux recovery ratio (FRR)12.

TFR indicates the overall fouling that consists of both reversible and irreversible fouling. RFR represents the fraction of fouling that can be easily removed through physical cleaning methods such as backwashing or rinsing, typically associated with weak interactions between foulants and the membrane surface90. Conversely, IFR reflects the portion of fouling that remains even after physical cleaning, implying stronger adhesion or adsorption of foulants within the membrane pores, which generally requires chemical cleaning91. A membrane exhibiting a high RFR and FRR alongside a low IFR value is considered to possess superior anti-fouling properties, as most of the accumulated foulants can be efficiently removed without damaging the membrane structure92. Therefore, analyzing these fouling parameters provides valuable insights into the cleaning efficiency, surface interaction behavior, and long-term stability of membrane systems93. The fouling parameters calculated for all fabricated membranes are illustrated in Fig. 12. As shown, the M₀ membrane exhibited TFR (78.45%) and IFR (60.78%), along with the lowest FRR (39.21%). These results indicate a strong interaction between foulants and the M0 membrane surface, leading to severe irreversible fouling and limited flux recovery after cleaning, which arises from the inert hydrophobic nature of PVC and the ununiform and large pore size of it94. Incorporation of TPU into the PVC matrix considerably improved the antifouling characteristics. With increasing TPU content from 2 wt% (M₁) to 8 wt% (M₄), both TFR and IRR decreased markedly (to the minimum value of 46.20%), while FRR showed a continuous increase (to 60.68%). The decreased values of TFR and IFR reflect that the incorporation of TPU increased the hydrophilicity of the membrane surface and decreased foulant adhesion. Reversible Fouling Ratio (RFR) percentages of the composite membranes remained relatively low and stable (5–7%). Thus, it can be concluded that the primary enhancement in performance occurred because of the mitigation of reversible fouling rather than the enhancement of it. Thus, the improved performance of PVC/TPU composite membranes can be ascribed to the combined effects of increased hydrophilicity, decreased pore size distribution, and decreased foulant-membrane interactions. Similar antifouling enhancements have also been observed by Wen et al. 95, who reported that blending hydrophilic sulfonated polysulfone (SPSF) by PES significantly improved fouling resistance and flux recovery because of the formation of a more hydrated layer on its surface.

Conclusion

This study illustrated that the addition of TPU to PVC membranes by the NIPS method significantly increased their structural strength and operational performance in MBR-based actual pharmaceutical wastewater treatment. Polymer miscibility increased and crystallinity reduced by TPU incorporation, leading to a more flexible and amorphous structure. The optimized blend membrane (M₃) exhibited a higher porosity (83.6%), water uptake (80.4%), and PWF (173.1 LMH, about 300% increase), along with an enhanced surface hydrophilicity (84.9° to 69.4°) due to moderate nanoscale roughness (Sa = 7.9 nm) and a negatively charged surface (pHpzc = 6.3) which reduced foulant adhesion. TPU addition boosted elongation up to 96.2%, abrasion resistance by about 10%, and toughness while maintaining adequate tensile strength in 3.2 MPa. Antibacterial performance was higher than 97% against E. coli and S. aureus, which was consistent with the lower EPS/SMP accumulation and the weaker biofilm formation. As a proof of concept, COD removal efficiency was 96.7% in real-wastewater MBR tests, and total and irreversible fouling decreased to 46.20% and 39.31%, respectively, with the FRR increasing to 60.68%. These findings suggest that TPU-modified PVC membranes are promising candidates for long-term MBR operation where mechanical durability and fouling resistance are critical.
