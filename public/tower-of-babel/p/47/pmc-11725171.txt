Spinal astrocyte-derived interleukin-17A promotes pain hypersensitivity in bone cancer mice
Authors: Huizhu Liu, Xuejing Lv, Xin Zhao, Lanxing Yi, Ning Lv, Wendong Xu, Yuqiu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Spinal microglia and astrocytes are both involved in neuropathic and inflammatory pain, which may display sexual dimorphism. Here, we demonstrate that the sustained activation of spinal astrocytes and astrocyte-derived interleukin (IL)-17A promotes the progression of mouse bone cancer pain without sex differences. Chemogenetic or pharmacological inhibition of spinal astrocytes effectively ameliorates bone cancer-induced pain-like behaviors. In contrast, chemogenetic or optogenetic activation of spinal astrocytes triggers pain hypersensitivity, implying that bone cancer-induced astrocytic activation is involved in the development of bone cancer pain. IL-17A expression predominantly in spinal astrocytes, whereas its receptor IL-17 receptor A (IL-17RA) was mainly detected in neurons expressing VGLUT2 and PAX2, and a few in astrocytes expressing GFAP. Specific knockdown of IL-17A in spinal astrocytes blocked and delayed the development of bone cancer pain. IL-17A overexpression in spinal astrocytes directly induced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia, which could be rescued by CaMKIIα inhibitor. Moreover, selective knockdown IL-17RA in spinal Vglut2+ or Vgat+neurons, but not in astrocytes, significantly blocked the bone cancer-induced hyperalgesia. Together, our findings provide evidence for the crucial role of sex-independent astrocytic signaling in bone cancer pain. Targeting spinal astrocytes and IL-17A/IL-17RA-CaMKIIα signaling may offer new gender-inclusive therapeutic strategies for managing bone cancer pain.

Introduction

Pain is the most common symptom of bone cancer, and it severely reduces the quality of life of patients. Bone cancer results from primary bone tumors or skeletal metastases of common cancer types such as breast, prostate, and lung cancer1. Bone cancer pain occurs through a complex process involving a combination of inflammatory, neuropathic, ischemic, and compressive mechanisms2. Activation of spinal cord glia including astrocytes and microglia is thought to contribute to the development of pathological pain3, 4, 5, 6, 7. However, the role of spinal glia in cancer pain is highly controversial, particularly in rodent models of bone cancer pain. Variability in spinal glial responses may be attributed to differences in species, strain, or cancer cell origins. Our previous study showed that in a female rat model of bone cancer, both spinal astrocytes and microglia are involved in the maintenance of advanced bone cancer pain4. In contrast, C3H/HeN mice with osteosarcoma NCTC-2472 cell-induced bone cancer exhibited robust astrogliosis and less prominent microgliosis8, 9, 10, 11. Increasing evidence has demonstrated sex dimorphism in the neuroimmune modulation of chronic pain12, 13, 14, 15, 16. Spinal microglia signaling has been shown to specifically regulate neuropathic pain in male mice15, but it remains unclear whether spinal microglia and astrocytes exhibit sex-specific differences in bone cancer pain.

Interleukin-17A (IL-17A), the most widely-studied member of the IL-17 cytokine family, is a proinflammatory cytokine primarily produced by Th17 cells17, 18, 19. IL-17A is involved in the pathogenesis of multiple inflammatory and autoimmune diseases such as psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, multiple sclerosis, and autoimmune encephalomyelitis20,21. IL-17 signaling has also been shown to promote the development of tumors in multiple organs22. Substantial evidence has shown that IL-17A from peripheral immune cells has a role in chronic neuropathic, inflammatory, and cancer pain. For example, high numbers of IL-17+ T cells or/and IL-17 levels have been detected in patients with arthritic pain and in mouse models of neuropathic pain23. Moreover, IL-17A from Th17 and Treg cells infiltrating into the spinal cord exacerbate NCTC-2472-induced bone cancer pain in C3H/HeN mice24. In addition, IL-23 signaling-mediated IL-17A release from macrophages has been reported to promote mechanical pain exclusively in female mice25,26. Despite the existing research on peripheral IL-17A, the role of spinal-derived IL-17A in the central sensitization of chronic pain has not been extensively studied. In a study from our laboratory, we showed that IL-17A contributes to paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain by modulating excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission signals of SOM+ neurons in the spinal dorsal horn3. Here, we demonstrate further the involvement of spinal astrocytic IL-17A in bone cancer pain without sex differences, suggesting an unconventional role for spinal astrocyte-derived IL-17A in our mouse model. We also found that activation of astrocytes, but not microglia, in the spinal dorsal horn, was necessary and sufficient for bone cancer-induced pain sensitization in both male and female mice. Moreover, IL-17RA, expressed by dorsal horn excitatory or inhibitory neurons rather than astrocytes, mediated the effect of IL-17A on bone cancer pain. These findings offer a new perspective on the astrocyte-neuron crosstalk in bone cancer pain, suggesting that targeting spinal astrocytes or IL-17A may provide a universal therapeutic approach for treating cancer pain in both sexes.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Adult male and female C57BL/6 mice (8–10 weeks) were purchased from Shanghai Experimental Animal Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Il17a tm1.1(icre)Stck/J (Il17aCre mutant mice, JAX016879), B6. Cg-Tg (Gfap-Cre/ERT2) 505Fmv/J (Gfap-Cre/ERT2, JAX012849), B6.Cg-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze/J (Ai14, JAX007914), Slc32a1tm2(cre)Lowl/J (Vgat-Ires-Cre, JAX016962), and Slc17a6tm2(cre)Lowl/J (Vglut2-Ires-Cre, JAX016963) mice were from the Jackson Laboratory. The source of animals is detailed in Supporting Information Table S1. All animals were housed in cages under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22 °C, ∼60% humidity, and ∼100 lux illumination, with food and water available ad libitum. We randomly assigned the body weight and sex of mice to experimental and control groups, and an investigator blinded to mouse genotypes/treatments and group assignments tested them. The Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee of Fudan University (Permit Number: SYXK2009-0082) approved all the animal experiments conducted by following the guidelines for pain research of the International Association for the Study of Pain. After the experiments, the animals were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation.

Mouse bone cancer model

Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells (C57BL/6 mouse-derived spontaneous lung carcinoma cells) were purchased from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell and Culture (Shanghai, China), and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Gibco, 11995065), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099141), penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 U/mL) (Gibco, 15140122) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 (The source detailed in Table S1).

The inoculation of LLC cells was performed as previously described27. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p.). The right leg was shaved, and the skin was disinfected with iodine tincture and 75% ethanol. A 27-gauge needle was inserted at the site of the intercondylar eminence of the right femur and was then replaced with a 10-μL microinjection syringe containing 5 μL suspension of tumor cells (1 × 106). The contents of the syringe were slowly injected into the femoral cavity. To prevent cell leakage outside the bone, the injection site was sealed with bone wax. For the sham group, 5 μL of PBS was injected into the femur instead of the carcinoma cells. At the end of the experiment, we performed radiological, postmortem, and histological evaluations. We excluded mice lacking tumor growth and bone destruction after the inoculation of tumor cells from the experiments.

Chronic constriction injury (CCI) of the sciatic nerve

The mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg i.p.), and we exposed the left sciatic nerve at the mid-thigh level by blunt dissection of the biceps femoris. For CCI, two ligatures with chromic gut (5–0) were tied loosely around the nerve at approximately 1 mm apart, proximal to its trifurcation, as published28. For sham surgery, we isolated the sciatic nerve but did not ligate it. After CCI or sham surgery, we closed the overlaying muscles and skin in layers with 5–0 silk sutures.

Behavioral tests

Von Frey test

Animals were habituated to the testing environment daily for at least 2 days before baseline testing. Room temperature and humidity were maintained throughout. The von Frey test for mechanical stimulation was performed by using paw withdrawal thresholds (PWTs) in response to a calibrated series of von Frey hairs (0.04–2 g, Stoelting Company). We placed mice in a chamber on an elevated metal mesh floor for 30 min before the test, then we applied von Frey filaments five times, maintained for 2 s with 15 s intervals, vertically to the central region of the hindpaw plantar surface in ascending order. Withdrawal from at least three out of five applications indicated the PWT in grams.

Hargreaves test

We performed Hargreaves tests for thermal pain by measuring paw withdrawal latencies (PWLs) in response to a radiant heat source (IITC Life Science Instruments). Mice were placed individually into Plexiglas chambers on an elevated glass surface and allowed to acclimate for 30 min. The heat was maintained at a constant intensity, producing a stable PWL of 10–14 s in baseline, with a 20 s cutoff to avoid tissue damage.

CatWalk gait analysis

We quantitatively assessed gait parameters and footfalls in mice using a CatWalk system (XT, Noldus Information Technology) as published27. We placed mice individually in an open end of an enclosed glass platform, in a darkened room with a red ceiling light-emitting diode light, a high-speed camera positioned underneath captured images of the illuminated area of each paw as each mouse walked across the glass floor and transferred the data to the CatWalk analysis software (CatWalk XT version 10.6, Noldus). We identified two available parameters to evaluate dynamic behaviors associated with bone cancer pain: (a) the stand, representing the duration of ground contact for a single paw; and, (b) the printarea as the surface of the complete print of a paw. Data were calculated as the percentage of ipsilateral/contralateral hindpaws.

Conditioned place preference (CPP)

We used a CPP procedure and place-conditioning apparatus, consisting of three opaque acrylic compartments, containing one neutral chamber (8 cm × 8 cm × 20 cm) and two conditioning chambers (20 cm × 20 cm × 20 cm) with distinct olfactory and visual cues. The experimental process comprised three distinct sessions: preconditioning (Days 1–3), conditioning (Day 4), and postconditioning (Day 5). On Days 1 and 2, all mice underwent habituation, in which they were exposed to the environment with full access to all chambers for 30 min. On Day 3 (preconditioning test day), the entrance to each conditioning compartment was opened after a mouse had habituated in the neutral compartment for 2 min. The mouse was then allowed to explore the two conditioning compartments freely for 10 min, with the time spent in each compartment being recorded blindly. Mice that spent >80% (480 s) on one side on that day were eliminated from the subsequent experiments. On the conditioning day (Day 4), the mice received no treatment in the morning and were randomly confined to one of the conditioning compartments for 60 min. After at least 4 h, they received an intrathecal (i.t.) injection of drugs (or vehicle as control) and were restrained in the opposite chamber for 60 min. On Day 5, the same procedure for Day 3 was followed, and the time animals spent in each compartment was measured. We determined the analgesic effect by the change of time spent in the treatment-paired compartment and the CPP score (the time spent in the treatment-paired compartment on the post-conditioning test minus the time spent in the same conditioning compartment on the pre-conditioning test).

Rotarod test

We placed mice on a rotarod apparatus (IITC Life Science) that accelerated from 5 to 20 rpm for 5 min per day for two consecutive days. On the third day, we increased the speed of the rod from 5 to 40 rpm and tested the mice with a maximum time of 300 s, recording the latency of animals to fall off.

Bone histology

Mice were anesthetized with an overdose of urethane (1.5 g/kg, i.p.), and we perfused them transcardially with 30 mL of 0.9% normal saline followed by 50 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde. The bilateral femur bones were removed and immersed in a decalcifying solution for 24 h, rinsed and dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, cut into 3-μm cross sections using a rotary microtome (Reichert-Jung 820, Cambridge Instruments), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin to visualize the extent of tumor infiltration and bone destruction.

Bone radiological detection

Mice were placed on a clear Plexiglas plane and exposed to an X-ray source under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia. Using the Digital Radiographer System (Philips, BV Pulsera C-arm), radiographs were taken from both hind limbs of sham and bone cancer mice.

Immunofluorescence staining and microglia morphological analysis

Mice were sacrificed with a urethane overdose and were perfused transcardially with warm normal saline followed by pre-cold 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (PA, pH 7.4). The L2–L5 segments of the spinal cord were removed, postfixed for 2 h at 4 °C, and then dehydrated in gradient sucrose at 4 °C. The spinal cord tissues were transected into 35-μm sections using a cryostat microtome (Leica Microsystems). The sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 2 h at room temperature (RT) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with corresponding primary antibodies. The sections were then incubated for 2 h at RT with secondary antibodies. For double immunofluorescence, we incubated the sections with a mixture of primary antibodies and a mixture of secondary antibodies. Table S1 lists the antibody sources. We verified the specificity of immunostaining and primary antibodies by omitting the primary antibodies, the corresponding blocking peptide pre-absorption, and gene knock-out mice (e.g., Cx3cr1 KO mice). The images were captured with a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Olympus, FV1000, Tokyo, Japan). We measured the optical density of immunoreactive staining for IBA-1, CX3CR1, GFAP, and IL-17A in the spinal dorsal horn of four to eight random sections for each animal using the Image J analysis system. Spinal IBA-1 immunopositive microglia were imaged with a confocal laser scanning microscope at 60 × magnification. The Imaris software (version 10.0.0, Oxford Instruments) was applied for the three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of microglia within the spinal dorsal horn. Custom settings for microglia surface reconstruction: Surfaces Detail 5 μm (smooth); Thresholding Background Subtraction (Local Contrast), Diameter of Largest Sphere, which fits into the object 5. Custom settings for microglia filament reconstruction: Detect New Starting Points: Largest Diameter 8 μm, Seed Points 0.3 μm; Remove Seed Points Around Starting Points: Diameter of Sphere Regions 10 μm. Seed points thresholds 1200. Notably, all the reconstructions were corrected manually if the Imaris algorithm autocalculated incorrectly. Sholl analysis was performed using Imaris in the filament reconstruction mode. And finally, all surface and filament parameters were exported into separate Excel files for data analysis.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed using the RNAscope system (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, ACD) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Sections were pretreated with hydrogen peroxide, target retrieval reagents, and Protease III, and then treated for 2 h with IL-17RA mRNA probes (ACDBio, 566151) followed by AMP1, AMP2, and AMP3 successively for 15–30 min. The sections were further incubated in HRP-C3 for 15 min and Opal 690 (1:1500, PerkinElmer) for 30 min to fluorescently label the probe. Finally, the HRP blocker was added for 15 min after fluorescent labeling of each channel. All hybridization and incubation steps were performed at 40 °C in the hybridization oven; moisture was maintained with the use of a wet box. EGFP immunohistochemical staining was continued after these procedures.

Western blotting

Mice were sacrificed with overdoses of urethane, and the L2–L5 spinal cord segments were rapidly removed. The dorsal horn tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing a mixture of protease inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics, 05892791001) and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics, 04906837001). We loaded equal amounts of protein onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Beyotime, P0012A) for separation and transferred them to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline (pH 7.6) with 0.1% Tween-20 for 2 h at RT, and we incubated them overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. GAPDH antibody was probed as a loading control. We visualized signals using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34095) and captured images with a ChemiDoc XRS system (Bio-Rad). Table S1 lists the antibody sources. All Western blot analyses were performed three to four times, and we obtained consistent results. A Bio-Rad image analysis system was then used to measure the integrated optic density of the specific bands.

Genotyping

Total DNA was extracted from mouse tail using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, 69506) following the manufacturer's protocol. PCR reaction mixture included specific primers (Sangon Biotech) for Il17aCre mutant mice, Genotyping Mix with dye (Sigma, KK7352) and 1 μL of DNA. Amplification products were examined by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels and visualized by ethidium bromide staining. The primer sequences were summarized in Table S1.

Reagents and intrathecal injection

d,l-Fluorocitric acid barium salt (FC, F9634) and minocycline hydrochloride (Mino, M9511) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. 2-[N-(2-hydroxyethyl)-N-(4-methoxybenzenesulfonyl)] amino-N-(4-chlorocinnamyl)-N-methylbenzylamine (KN93, 422708) and 2-[N-(4-methoxybenzenesulfonyl)] amino-N-(4-chlorocinnamyl)-N-methylbenzylamine (KN92, 422709) were obtained from Calbiochem. KN-93 is a cell-permeable, reversible, and competitive inhibitor of CaM kinase II (Ki = 370 nmol/L), which binds to the CaM binding site and prevents the association of CaM with CaMKII. KN-92 is useful as a negative control for KN-93. We purchased the mouse IL-17A antibody (sc-374218) and control IgG (sc-2025) from Santa Cruz, the mouse IL-17RA antibody (MAB4481) and control IgG (MAB006) from R&D system, and the rabbit CX3CR1 antibody (TP501) and control IgG (PP501P) from Torrey Pines Biolabs.

We delivered drugs, neutralizing antibodies, or vehicle into the spinal space of mice via a lumbar puncture made with a 30-gauge needle between the L5 and L6 vertebrae under isoflurane anesthesia (3% for induction and 1.5% for maintenance). Mice that showed any surgery-related neurological deficits were excluded from the following experiments. We injected 5 μL solution and confirmed successful spinal punctures by a brisk tail flick.

Adeno-associated virus and intra-dorsal horn injection

The design of IL-17R short-hairpin RNA sequence (5′-CCTACGTTGTTTGCTACTT-3′) referred to our previous work3. IL-17-shRNA (5′-acCGCAATGAAGACCCTGATA-3′) or shControl (the scrambled sequence) were designed and packaged completely by the Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd.

We used a published protocol for mouse intra-dorsal horn injection3. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p.), and ophthalmic ointment was applied to maintain eye lubrication. The skin was incised and then connective tissue and muscles were dissected to expose bony structures in the lumbar spinal cord region. The vertebral column was mounted in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting, USA) and the dura in intervertebral spaces was incised to expose the spinal cord at L2−L5. We performed no laminectomy to avoid trauma. A tilted micropipette was inserted into the dorsal spinal cord at a 45° angle and at 420 μm from the dura (equivalent to a vertical depth of 300 μm from the dura) to target the superficial dorsal horn. We injected AAVs into the right side of the spinal cord at 2 injection sites (400–600 nL of AAV were injected for each site at a rate of 40 nL/min) using an air pressure system (NANOLITER 2010 injecter, WPI, USA) connected to glass pipettes (tip diameter of 10–30 μm). Table S1 details the AAV sources. The micropipette was withdrawn 5 min after each viral injection for diffusion. The incision was closed with stitches, and the animals were allowed to recover from the anesthesia on a heating blanket before returning to their home cage. Antibiotics (ceftriaxone sodium, 0.1 g/kg) were injected intraperitoneally for three days after surgery to prevent infection. Behavioral tests were performed 2−3 weeks after viral inoculation to allow for maximal and stable expression. After all the experiments were completed, the mice injected with the AAV virus were sacrificed to confirm that the AAV virus expressed in the ipsilateral dorsal horn of the L2−L5 segments of the spinal cord, otherwise the results will be removed.

Chemogenetic and optogenetic experiments

For chemogenetic inhibition, we administered the designed clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 5 mg/kg, i.p., Sigma–Aldrich, C0832) 30 min before testing to Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice expressing AAV-EF1α-DIO-hM4D (Gi)-mCherry. For chemogenetic activation, we injected CNO (3 mg/kg, i.p.) into Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice expressing AAV-EF1α-DIO-hM3D (Gq)-mCherry 30 min before testing. Tamoxifen (100 mg/kg/day, i.p., Sigma–Aldrich, T5648) was injected into Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice for five successive days. Tamoxifen was dissolved in sunflower oil at a final concentration of 10 mg/mL at 37 °C, filter-sterilized, and stored for up to 7 days at 4 °C in the dark before injecting it (100 mg/kg/day, i.p.) into Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice for five successive days.

For optogenetic activation, we injected Cre-dependent AAV-expressing channelrhodopsin 2 (ChR2, AAV-EF1α-DIO-hChR2-mCherry) was injected into the dorsal horn of Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice, and we implanted an optical fiber on the spinal cord of each mouse. Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, i.p.), and the vertebral column was mounted in a stereotaxic frame. We used a dental drill to create a small hole without damaging the spinal cord and implanted a fiber-optic cannula (Ø 0.2 mm Core, 0.37 mm NA, 0.25 mm length; Hangzhou Newdoon Technology Co., Ltd.) above the spinal cord tissue. The cannula was secured in place using dental cement. The animals were allowed to recover for one week. Then tamoxifen (100 mg/kg/day, i.p.) was injected into Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice for five successive days. Behavioral tests were performed before, during, and after blue light stimulation (473 nm, 5.0 mW, 20 Hz, 25 ms, 20 min), respectively.

Statistical analysis

We present data as means ± standard error of mean (SEM); we gathered and analyzed them in a blinded manner (with no data excluded due to outlier status) using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The sample sizes and power calculations were based on our prior experience with similar experimental models and variables. We estimated sample sizes of at least four (n ≥ 4) to detect a significant effect (power, 0.75; alpha, 0.05) using G power. All data from the different groups were verified for normality and homogeneity of variance using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests (no data were transformed). Behavioral, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot data were analyzed using Student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test (nonparametric data) when comparing two groups, or one-way ANOVA or two-way Repeated Measures (RM) ANOVA, or Kruskal–Wallis H tests (nonparametric data) followed by post hoc Holm–Sidak or Student–Newman–Keuls test when comparing more than two groups. All analyses were two-tailed, and we considered P values lower than 0.05 as statistically significant. Sample size and replicates are shown in the figures or figure legends.

Results

Bone cancer induces persistent astrocyte activation in the spinal dorsal horn in both sexes

We examined the temporal progression of spinal microglial and astrocytic activation in a mouse model of bone cancer pain model induced by Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells in both sexes. After intrafemoral inoculation of LLC cells, we observed sustained elevation of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, astrocytic marker) immunoreactivity (GFAP-IR) from Days 3–28 post-tumor inoculation in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn of male and female mice (Fig. 1A). Activated astrocytes exhibited intense GFAP-IR and appeared hypertrophied thickened processes. A two-way ANOVA analysis revealed a statistically significant main effect of bone cancer on the GFAP-IR (Fig. 1B). By contrast, we did not detect any significant changes of microglia marker ionized calcium binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA-1) immunoreactivity (IBA-1-IR) in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn of bone cancer mice in (Fig. 1A and C). To confirm tumor growth, radiographs were taken and affected bones were collected. The radiographs showed soft tissue swelling adjacent to the tumor and signs of radiolucent lessions in the femur close to the injection site, especially at the distal femur near the epiphysis (Supporting Information Fig. S1A). Activation of spinal astrocytes, but not microglia, contributes to bone cancer pain

To explore the roles of astrocytes and microglia in advanced bone cancer pain, we injected mice with fluorocitrate (FC, an astrocyte metabolic inhibitor) or minocycline (an inhibitor of microglial activation) intrathecally on PTD 21. A single i.t. injection of FC (0.4 μg) significantly inhibited bone cancer-induced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia without sex difference (Fig. 2E and F). In addition, we used a conditioned place preference (CPP) test as an operant measurement of ongoing pain31, and we observed a distinct CPP after i.t. FC injection. After pairing FC treatment with a particular chamber in the place-conditioning apparatus, we observed that FC-treated bone cancer mice displayed a significantly increased CPP score, spending more time in the conditioning chamber on the postconditioning day than on the preconditioning day (Fig. 2G). By contrast, a single i.t. injection of minocycline (50 μg) did not affect the bone cancer-induced thermal hyperalgesia or mechanical allodynia on PTD21 in both male and female mice (Fig. 2H and I). The CPP scores in the vehicle and minocycline groups were similar (Fig. 2J). As a positive control, an i.t. injection of minocycline (50 μg) significantly suppressed neuropathic pain-induced mechanical allodynia (Fig. S2J). Considering that CX3CR1 is specifically expressed in spinal microglia, priming microglial activation in response to peripheral injury or inflammation32, we also examined the effects of blocking CX3CR1 in the spinal dorsal horn on bone cancer pain. In results similar to those obtained with the microglial inhibitor, i.t. injection of anti-CX3CR1 neutralizing antibody (3 μg) failed to reduce the bone cancer-induced thermal hyperalgesia, mechanical allodynia, or ongoing pain (Supporting Information Fig. S3A−S3C). Consistently, we observed bone cancer-induced mechanical allodynia, decreased stand phase, and reduced print area of the affected paw in Cx3cr1 knockout mice (Fig. S3D−S3F). Immunohistochemistry and Western blot analyses revealed no significant changes in CX3CR1 expression in the spinal dorsal horn during the development of bone cancer (Fig. S3G−S3K). These results suggest that spinal astrocytes, rather than microglia, are involved in LLC cells-induced mouse bone cancer pain, implying a unique mechanism of mouse bone cancer pain different from other chronic pain mechanisms.

Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice are an effective tool for cell- and time-specific manipulation of astrocytes33. To assess whether functional tamoxifen-induced Cre-recombined cells are restricted to astrocytes, we crossed Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice with Ai14 mice (tdTomato-loxP reporter mice expressing red fluorescent protein). Our results show that tdTomato-positive cells specifically express the astrocytic marker GFAP (Supporting Information Fig. S4A). The behavioral hyperalgesia, tumor growth, and femur bone destruction we observed in Gfap-Cre/ERT2 mice were consistent with the effects seen in wild-type mice (Fig. S4B−S4D). Astrocyte-derived IL-17A contributes to bone cancer pain

Studies from our laboratory and others have shown that spinalIL-17A is involved in inflammatory35 and paclitaxel chemotherapy-induced pain 3 in mice. To investigate whether bone cancer alters spinal IL-17A expression in mice, we collected spinal dorsal horns from mice with confirmed femur tumors and behavioral hypersensitivity development after LLC cells inoculation. Western blot analysis and immunohistochemistry results show elevated IL-17A protein levels and an increased IL-17A immunofluorescence intensity (Fig. 4A−C). Double immunofluorescence images show that IL-17A-IR was primarily colocalized with the astrocytic marker GFAP and ALDH1L1, but not with the microglia marker IBA-1 or the neuronal marker NeuN (Supporting Information Fig. S6A). Moreover IL-17A-IR was not colocalized with the CGRP+ (calcitonin gene-related peptide, a marker for peptidergic nociceptors) or IB4+ (isolectin B4, a marker for non-peptidergic nociceptors) primary afferent terminals (Fig. S6B), indicating that IL-17A may not be produced from nociceptor terminals in the spinal cord dorsal horn. We also did not detect CD4+ or CD8+ immune cells infiltration in the spinal cords of sham and bone cancer mice, suggesting that IL-17A in the spinal cord was produced from astrocytes rather than immune cells (Fig. S6C). To assess the spinal role of IL-17A in bone cancer pain, we injected IL-17A neutralizing antibody (2 μg) intrathecally on PTD 21. Thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia were significantly reversed in both male and female mice (Fig. 4D and E). We found similar effects of IL-17A neutralizing antibody in male and female mice. To examine the effect of spinal astrocyte-derived IL-17A on bone cancer pain, we injected an AAV expressing IL-17A-shRNA driven by the GFAP promoter (AAV-hGFAP-IL-17A shRNA-EGFP) into the spinal dorsal horn of mice to selectively knock down IL-17A in spinal astrocytes (Fig. 4F). The knockdown efficiency of IL-17A was confirmed by Western blot and immunohistochemistry experiments (Fig. 4G and H). IL-17A knockdown in spinal astrocytes delayed and suppressed the bone cancer-induced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia (Fig. 4I and J). These results indicate that spinal astrocyte-derived IL-17A is required for the development of bone cancer pain. Discussion

In the past decade, sex-specific susceptibility to chronic pain and sex differences in pain sensitivity and analgesic efficacy have been increasingly recognized. Findings show that spinal glia-mediated neuropathic and inflammatory pain mechanisms are sexually dimorphic13,15, suggesting the need for sex-specific pain research and analgesic strategies. Here, we report an unconventional role for spinal glia, including microglia and astrocytes, in a mouse model of femur bone cancer pain. Adopting pharmacological, chemogenetic, and optogenetic approaches, we show that spinal astrocytes but not microglia are required for thermal and mechanical pain hypersensitivity in the mouse bone cancer model without sex difference. Our previous study demonstrated thatIL-17A/IL-17RA mediates chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathic pain by regulating spinal SOM + neuron transmission3. Here, we further demonstrate IL-17A/IL-17RA signaling's involvement in LLC cells-induced mouse bone cancer pain. In particular, selective IL-17A knockdown in astrocytes and its receptor IL-17RA in Vglut2+ or Vgat+neurons effectively blocked bone cancer pain development in mice, providing potential targets for therapeutic cancer pain strategies.

Spinal astrocytes, but not microglia, sustain bone cancer pain in both male and female mice

Evidence has accumulated over the past two decades that spinal microglia and astrocytes are critical cellular mediators of pain hypersensitivity induced by peripheral nerve injury and peripheral tissue inflammation5,6. However, studies on the roles of these cells in the development and maintenance of cancer pain are inconclusive. Ducourneau et al.38 failed to observe glial marker (such as GFAP, S100β, and CD11b) changes in a bone cancer model induced by MRMT-1 cells in female SD rats, whereas astrocytic activation and astrogliosis have been proven in most rodent bone cancer models8, 9, 10, 11,37. Our studies on Walker 256 mammary gland carcinoma cell-induced bone cancer pain showed that the astrocytes in the spinal dorsal horn of female Wistar rats were rapidly and persistently activated4. Consistently, interference of proteinase-activated receptor 2, CXCL11, and JNK alleviated rat bone and mouse skin cancer pain by inhibiting astrocytic activation39,40. In this study, we showed further, using multiple approaches in a mouse bone cancer model, that activation of spinal astrocytes was necessary and sufficient for sex-independent bone cancer-induced pain hypersensitivity. Similarly, the role of sex-independent astrocytic signaling (JNK, ERK, Cx43 hemichannels, and CXCR2) has also been shown in neuropathic pain13.

Compared to astrocytic responses, microglia responses in the spinal cord seem to be less evident after bone cancer10. Studies have reported the absence of microglia activation in C3H/HeN mice inoculated with NCTC-2472 cells8, 9, 10, 11 and BALB/cJ mice inoculated with 4T1 mammary cancer cells41. Deletion of P2x7 receptor (primarily expressed by microglia in the spinal cord) did not affect the development of bone cancer pain both in BALB/cJ mice inoculated with 4T141 and C57BL/6 mice inoculated with LLC cells42. Our present study further demonstrates that spinal microglia is not required for mouse bone cancer pain in C57BL/6 mice inoculated with LLC cells in either sex. i.t. injection of anti-CX3CR1 neutralizing antibody to interfere CX3CR1, a chemokine receptor predominantly expressed by spinal microglia, did not alleviate bone cancer-induced mechanical allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and spontaneous pain. Inoculation of LLC cells in Cx3cr1 knockout mice resulted in pain hypersensitivity similar to those in wild-type mice, whereas Trpv1 knockout mice did not develop hyperalgesia at all despite a similar tumor growth27. These data support our point that spinal microglia are not necessary for the initiation and development of pain hypersensitivity in LLC-induced bone cancer model of C57BL/6 mice. Of course, some studies from different groups showed moderate microgliosis in the spinal dorsal horn of C3H/HeN mice inoculated with NCTC-2472 for bone cancer at certain time points24,43,44. It has been demonstrated that the peak of spinal microglial proliferation is at 3 days after spinal nerve transection45. As a positive control, we examined spinal microglial reaction in a mouse model of neuropathic pain induced by CCI. A significant increase in IBA-1-IR was detected in the ipsilateral spinal dorsal horn at 3 days after CCI, and microglia inhibitor minocycline reversed CCI-induced pain hypersensitivity. At present, we cannot explain clearly why different cell lines and animal species/strains, and even the same cell lines and strains have a different glial reaction pattern in the spinal dorsal horn, which highlights the complexity of the pathological process of bone cancer pain. Although it is generally believed that microglial activation drives astrocytic activation, our study showed that bone cancer-induced astrocytic activation can take place in microglial activity-independent manner. Increasing evidence suggests that spinal cord infiltrating T lymphocytes contributes to astrocytic activation and the development of chronic pain46,47. However, we did not detect CD4+ and CD8+ cells in the spinal dorsal horn of bone cancer mice, suggesting that peripheral immune cell infiltration was not an initiating factor for spinal astrocytic activation in bone cancer pain. Indeed, a link between neuronal activity and astrocytic activation has been proposed by several groups48, 49, 50.

Reports have shown a sex difference in the role of microglia in neuropathic pain. For example, in spinal nerve injury induced mouse neuropathic pain, spinal microglia mainly contribute to the mechanical hyperalgesia of male mice. Female mice achieved similar levels of pain hypersensitivity using adaptive immune cells, such as T lymphocytes15. Minocycline, a microglial inhibitor, also reduces neuropathic pain following spinal cord injury in female rats51. In chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy produced by paclitaxel, males have been found to utilize macrophage Toll-like receptor 452, whereas females utilize the IL-23/IL-17A/TRPV1 signaling axis in developing pain hypersensitivity25. We did not find the difference between male and female mice in pain hypersensitivity and spinal microglial responses caused by bone cancer, suggesting a sex-independent bone cancer pain mechanism in mice. Actually, a series of studies from Wu's laboratory find that sex difference of microglia in chronic pain is not always the case depending on the microglial manipulations. They found that peripheral nerve injury-induced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity can be completely abolished in Cx3cr1+ cell-depleted mice during the initiation phase (3 days after surgery) in both male and female. The gender differences in mechanical allodynia (but not thermal hyperalgesia) only occurred in the pain maintenance phase, suggesting that microglia equally participated in the mechanical pain development in male and female53. In vivo optogenetic activation of microglia in the spinal cord triggered chronic pain-like hypersensitivity in both male and female mice54. Chemogenetic inhibition of spinal microglia in spinal nerve transection mice effectively inhibited microglial activity and pain hypersensitivity without gender difference55. Taken together, our present study highlights the equivalence of spinal glia in bone cancer pain in male and female mice, providing universal targets for therapeutic strategies against bone cancer pain for both sexes.

Spinal astrocyte-produced IL-17A contributes to bone cancer pain via neuronal IL-17RA signaling

Th17 immune cells are a major source of IL-17A. Following peripheral inflammation and nerve injury, activated Th17 cells migrate to the injured nerve, the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), and the spinal cord, and interact with glia56. Recent studies have shown that T cell-derived IL-17A participates in various types of chronic pain with sex dimorphism. Female mice exhibit stronger proinflammatory T cell responses and higher IL-17A levels in the sciatic nerve and lumbar spinal cord than male mice after CCI57. The required dose of intraplanar IL-17A to induce allodynia in male mice was higher than in females25,58. Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy increased IL-17A in the DRG only in female mice, and a low IL-17A concentration (1 ng/mL) increased the action potential firing of DRG neurons in female but not in male mice25. In addition to Th17 cells, central astrocytes are another source of IL-17A. Astrocyte-produced IL-17A has been reported to be involved in stroke recovery59,60, neuroinflammation61, chemotherapy-induced neuropathic pain3, and inflammatory pain35. In this study, we found IL-17A-IR in the spinal dorsal horn of GFAP- and ALDH1L1-expressing astrocytes in bone cancer mice. IL-17A overexpression in spinal astrocytes was sufficient to induce pain hypersensitivity. Selective IL-17A knockdown in spinal astrocytes robustly blocked or delayed bone cancer-induced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia, whereas Il17a−/− mice still developed mechanical allodynia after bone cancer, suggesting a unique role for spinal astrocyte-derived IL-17A in pain hypersensitivity. Moreover, we showed that spinal administration of IL-17A-specific neutralizing antibody reversed the bone cancer-induced hyperalgesia and allodynia without sex differences. Lopes et al.62 reported similar findings, with sex-independent gene expression profiles of peripheral afferent neurons and spinal cord microglia after nerve injury, and sex differences only in DRG adaptive immune cell infiltration.

IL-17RA mediates the biological function of IL-17A signaling18. IL-17RA has been detected in most neurons of the dorsal root ganglion63 and the central nerve system3,64,65. IL-17A has neuromodulator-like properties and acts directly on neurons to increase their responsiveness to pre-synaptic input in Caenorhabditis elegans66. IL-17A can also disrupt hippocampal long-term potentiation through the activation of its receptor and p38/mitogen-activated protein kinase64. Moreover, IL-17A rescues sociability deficits by directly acting on IL-17RA+neurons of the primary somatosensory cortex dysgranular zone65. Our previous study demonstrated that IL-17A enhances excitatory postsynaptic currents and suppresses inhibitory postsynaptic currents in lamina IIo interneurons of the spinal cord3. Here, we reveal further that selective knockdown of IL-17RA in spinal excitatory and inhibitory neurons, but not in astrocytes, relieves bone cancer pain, highlighting the importance of IL-17A/IL-17RA signaling-mediated glial–neuron interaction for pain hypersensitivity. The small molecules inhibitors targeting the IL-17A/IL-17RA protein–protein interaction is a promising area of pain modulation67. Our results, showing that blockade of CaMKII reversed IL-17A overexpression-induced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia, are consistent with those of a report in which IL-17A-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia were absent in Camkii mutant mice68. IL-17RA receptor knockdown in bone cancer mice further reduced the phosphorylation of CaMKII, suggesting that IL-17A released from activated spinal astrocytes acts on IL-17RA+ neurons, and changes the nociceptive transmission of dorsal horn neurons through CaMKII intracellular signals, promoting pain hypersensitivity. Activated CaMKII can phosphorylate the NMDA receptor GluN2B69 and AMPA receptor GluR170, promoting their activation. Our previous study has implied that IL-17A increases NMDA activity via rapid posttranslational regulation3. In the present study, we observed that i.t. injection of KN93 rapidly improved bone cancer pain and reversed IL-17A overexpression-induced hyperalgesia, suggesting CaMKII-mediated posttranslational regulation.

Translational potential and limitations

Selective inhibition of spinal astrocytes, knockdown of astrocytes-produced IL-17A, or i.t. injection of IL-17A antibody effectively alleviates bone cancer pain. Our previous study also proved that spinal IL-17A levels were elevated in neuropathic pain induced by chemotherapy in mice, and blockade of IL-17RA in the spinal cord suppressed neuronal hyperexcitability3. Importantly, bone cancer resulted in similar sustained activation of spinal astrocytes and upregulation of IL-17A in both male and female mice. Therefore, targeting spinal astrocytes and their product, IL-17A may provide new therapies with sex universality for bone cancer pain. We are aware of the limitations of our study. For example, though the use of GFAP expression and IBA-1 expression as a measure of activation in these glial cells is widespread, we still lack other detailed examination of glial activation such as transcriptional profile and other biomarkers. Further studies are warranted to investigate the role of astrocytic IL-17A signaling in inflammatory and neuropathic pain of different sexes, including the potential sex difference in the role of microglia in other pain models. Technically, the red light-activated ReaChR developed in recent years is probably a better optogenetic approach. With deeper penetration, red light (625 nm)-activated ReaChR has been reported to be suitable for spinal activation without penetration of optic fiber into spinal parenchyma54. Therefore, in spinal research, ReaChR may be more advantageous than ChR2.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that spinal astrocytes, rather than microglia, are involved in mouse bone cancer pain without sex difference. Sustained activation of spinal astrocytes and astrocyte-derived interleukin IL-17A promotes the progression of mouse bone cancer pain. Neuronal IL-17RA–CaMKII signaling mediates nociceptive hypersensitivity. This study suggests that targeting spinal astrocytes and IL-17A/IL–17RA–CaMKII signaling may provide a new gender-inclusive therapeutic approach for pain management in bone cancer.
