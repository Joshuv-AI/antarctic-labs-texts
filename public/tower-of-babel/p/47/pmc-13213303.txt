Genotype and subependymal lesion burden influence volumetric response to everolimus in tuberous sclerosis complex-associated subependymal giant cell astrocytoma: A 10-year real-world study
Authors: Tzu-Hsuan Su, Steven Shinn-Forng Peng, Pei-Lung Chen, Shih-Hung Yang, Meng-Fai Kuo, Pi-Chuan Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEverolimus, a mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor, effectively reduces subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA) volume in patients with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC); however, biological determinants of therapeutic sensitivity remain poorly defined. In this 10-year real-world cohort, we retrospectively evaluated 43 patients (median age 12 years; 46.5% female) with TSC-associated SEGA treated with everolimus between 2013 and 2024. Over a median treatment duration of 79 months, SEGA volume significantly decreased and remained stable, with median reductions ranging from −21.6% to −26.6% from year one onward. Overall, 37.2% of patients achieved a ≥30% volume reduction, whereas 11.6% experienced SEGA progression; notably, none developed hydrocephalus or required surgical intervention during follow-up. Clinical and genetic analyses demonstrated that treatment responders, compared with non-responders, more frequently harbored TSC1 disease-causing variants (28.6% vs. 4.0%; p = 0.047) and were characterized by a significantly lower burden of baseline potential SEGAs—defined as non-growing subependymal lesions of 0.5–1 cm (12.5% vs. 55.6%; p = 0.018). These findings suggest genotype-dependent susceptibility to mTOR inhibition and highlight baseline imaging burden as key determinants of therapeutic response. Together, our results provide clinically relevant insights into treatment stratification and individualized response expectations for everolimus therapy in TSC-associated SEGA.Keywords: Tuberous sclerosis complex, Subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, Mechanistic target of rapamycin, Everolimus, mTOR inhibitor

Introduction

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by loss-of-function pathogenic variants in the TSC1 or TSC2 genes, leading to overactivation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, a key regulator of cell growth, differentiation, and proliferation [1]. As a result, TSC is characterized by the development of benign tumors in multiple organs, including the brain, skin, heart, lungs, and kidneys [2,3].

Common manifestations of tumor development in the central nervous system include subependymal nodules (SENs) and subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs). SENs are usually small, asymptomatic lesions located in the lateral ventricles and are present in more than 90% of patients with TSC [4,5]. In contrast, SEGAs are benign, slow-growing tumors arising in the periventricular region and are found in 8%–25% of patients with TSC [6]. Progressive SEGAs may lead to increased intracranial pressure and obstructive hydrocephalus, which represent major causes of morbidity and mortality among pediatric patients with TSC [7,8]. SEGAs are considered to arise from SENs [9,10], as they share indistinguishable histopathological features [11]. Although there is no universally accepted definition of SEGA [1], tumor growth is generally regarded as the principal feature distinguishing SEGAs from SENs [4,11].

The development of mTOR inhibitors, such as everolimus and sirolimus, represents a paradigm shift from symptomatic management toward a disease-modifying therapeutic strategy that targets the underlying pathogenic mechanism of TSC [12]. Clinical trials have established the efficacy of everolimus in reducing SEGA volume [13,14]. Nevertheless, data regarding long-term effectiveness, particularly in real-world clinical settings, remain limited [15,16]. Moreover, only few studies have investigated factors associated with treatment response [17].

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the long-term real-world effectiveness of everolimus in patients with TSC-associated SEGA and to identify factors associated with favorable treatment outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study subjects and design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the multidisciplinary TSC clinic of a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. Between September 2013 and December 2024, patients with confirmed TSC based on the 2012 International Tuberous Sclerosis Complex Diagnostic Criteria [3] were enrolled if they received everolimus treatment for SEGA. Patients who had been previously enrolled in everolimus-related clinical trials, had received rapamycin within 6 months prior to everolimus initiation, lacked radiologic assessment by at least two brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans, or had been treated with everolimus for less than 6 months were excluded. Patients previously enrolled in clinical trials were excluded to minimize potential confounding caused by protocol-driven dosing adjustment, imaging follow-up schedules, and treatment monitoring that may differ from routine real-world clinical practice. Demographic characteristics, medical history, everolimus serum concentrations, adverse events, brain imaging, and treatment responses were collected from medical records. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (Ref. No. 202511048RINA).

Dosage of everolimus

Everolimus was initiated once daily at doses of 2.5, 5, or 7.5 mg based on body surface area (0.5–1.2, 1.3–2.1, and ≥2.2 m2, respectively) and was titrated to achieve a target serum concentration of 5–15 ng/mL, unless not tolerated.

Definition of and volume measurement SEGA and potential SEGA

According to the diagnostic criteria of 2012 International Tuberous Sclerosis Complex Consensus Conference [4], a SEGA was defined as (1) a lesion at the caudothalamic groove with either a size of more than 1 cm in any direction, or (2) a subependymal lesion at any location that has shown serial growth on consecutive imaging regardless of size. Serial growth was defined as ≥25% increase in SEGA volume over time by this study. We also followed the definition described in a previous study and defined a potential SEGA as a tumor located at the caudothalamic groove with a maximum diameter greater than 0.5 cm but without serial growth [16].

Following methods used in previous studies [18,19], the volumes of SEGA and potential SEGA were approximated as ellipsoids using the formula 4/3×π×a×b×c, where a, b, and c represent the maximum tumor diameters measured in the axial, sagittal, and coronal planes, respectively. A physician to blinded patient information reviewed the imaging studies and recorded the measurements of SEGA diameters.

Effectiveness assessment of everolimus for SEGA

Changes in the total volume of all SEGA lesions in each patient from baseline were evaluated using brain MRI scans obtained at baseline, 6 months after initiation of everolimus treatment, and at least annually thereafter. Treatment effectiveness was expressed as the proportion of patients achieving a ≥20%, ≥30%, or ≥50% reduction in total SEGA volume; the median percentage change in SEGA volume compared with baseline; the cumulative probability of treatment response (defined as a ≥30% reduction in SEGA volume relative to baseline at some time point during the treatment period); and progression-free survival (PFS). SEGA progression was defined as an increase in SEGA volume of ≥25% from the lowest recorded value to a value greater than baseline, the appearance of new SEGA lesions, or new or worsening hydrocephalus, in accordance with previous clinical trials [20].

To evaluate the effectiveness of everolimus on potential SEGAs, the median percentage change in potential SEGA volume compared with baseline was also assessed.

Factors associated with treatment response

To evaluate factors associated with treatment response, we compared the clinical characteristics of responders (patients who achieved a ≥30% reduction in SEGA volume relative to baseline at any time point during the treatment period) and non-responders.

Adverse events

Adverse events (AEs) suspected to be related to everolimus over time (≤6 months, 7–12 months, and >12 months) were assessed and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 [21].

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were expressed as percentages, and quantitative variables were expressed as the median (interquartile range [IQR]). Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. For numerical variables, the Shapiro–Wilk test was first employed to assess the normality of data distribution; accordingly, between-group comparisons were performed using Student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate the cumulative probability of achieving treatment response and PFS. The log-rank test was applied to compare these outcomes across groups stratified by potential determinants, including genotype and subependymal lesion burden. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Demographic characteristics and medical history

Fig. 1 presents the flowchart of patient enrollment. A total of 67 patients who received everolimus for TSC-associated SEGA were reviewed. Of these, 24 were excluded because they were enrolled in everolimus-related clinical trials (n = 6), had received rapamycin within 6 months (n = 9), were evaluated only with brain computed tomography due to pacemaker implantation (n = 1), or had been treated with everolimus for less than 6 months (n = 8). Finally, 43 patients were included in the analysis.

The demographic characteristics and medical history of the study population is shown in Table 1. The median age was 12 years (IQR, 4 to 23), and 20 patients (46.5%) were female. The median duration of everolimus treatment was 79 months (IQR, 43 to 118). Five patients (11.6%) carried TSC1 disease-causing variants, 34 (79.1%) had TSC2 pathogenic variants, whereas 4 (9.3%) did not receive genetic testing. Twenty-three patients (53.5%) had 1 SEGA lesion, while 20 (46.5%) carried 2 lesions. The median SEGA volume was 0.38 cm3 (IQR, 0.27 to 0.79). Three patients (7.0%) had undergone surgery for SEGA before everolimus treatment. The median serum everolimus concentration at 1 year was 5.9 ng/mL (IQR, 2.9 to 8.8). Patients with serum everolimus concentration less than 5 ng/mL accounted for 44.2% (n = 19).

Treatment effectiveness for SEGA

Fig. 2a illustrates the proportions of patients achieving ≥20%, ≥30%, and ≥50% reductions in SEGA volume over time. During the 10-year follow-up period, approximately one-half to two-thirds of patients achieved a ≥20% reduction by 1 year after everolimus initiation, while about 20–30% achieved a ≥30% reduction. Fig. 2b shows the median percentage change in SEGA volume over time. During the 2-year period before everolimus initiation, SEGA volume progressively increased from −20.6% (IQR, −43.0 to −0.9) to 0% at baseline. After treatment initiation, SEGA volume decreased to −16.8% (IQR, −31.8 to −8.7) at 0.5 years and thereafter remained within a sustained range of approximately −21.6% to −26.6% relative to baseline throughout the 10-year follow-up period.

Overall, 16 patients (37.2%) achieved a ≥30% reduction in SEGA volume relative to baseline at any time point during the treatment period. Fig. 2c depicts the cumulative incidence of treatment response (≥30% reduction). The estimated cumulative probabilities of treatment response at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years were 26.7% (95% CI, 9.1 to 48.3), 35.5% (95% CI, 16.9 to 54.6), 38.9% (95% CI, 20.2 to 57.2), and 45.0% (95% CI, 24.9 to 63.2), respectively.

The Kaplan–Meier curve demonstrating PFS is shown in Fig. 2d. The estimated PFS at 1, 2, 5, and 10 years were 97.7% (95% CI, 84.6 to 99.7), 97.7% (95% CI, 84.6 to 99.7), 87.6% (95% CI, 69.9 to 95.2), and 83.2% (95% CI, 63.6 to 92.8), respectively. Five patients (11.6%) experienced SEGA progression at any time point during the treatment period (Fig. 3). Among these five patients, four exhibited growths of pre-existing SEGA lesions. One patient (black line) appeared to have an unchanged total SEGA volume over time; this was because the patient initially had one true SEGA and one small potential SEGA, and the potential SEGA subsequently showed serial growth and was reclassified as a true SEGA. Nevertheless, the growth of this lesion did not significantly affect the total tumor volume because of its small baseline size. Three patients demonstrated relatively rapid SEGA growth rates of 0.18, 0.05, and 0.03 cm3 per year, respectively. Two of these patients showed increases in SEGA volume exceeding 50% relative to baseline. All of the patients remained on everolimus treatment and continued regular imaging follow-up, without development of hydrocephalus or the need for surgical intervention during the observation period.

Treatment effectiveness for potential SEGA

Among the study population, 17 patients (39.5%) had at least one potential SEGA before treatment. The median volume of potential SEGAs was 0.16 cm3 (IQR, 0.10–0.18). Fig. 4 shows the median percentage change in potential SEGA volume over time. After everolimus initiation, the median potential SEGA volume decreased to a nadir of −17.4% (IQR, −29.5 to 6.1) at 2 years and subsequently increased toward baseline during the observation period. Among these patients, one had a potential SEGA that demonstrated volume progression throughout follow-up and was ultimately classified as a true SEGA.

Factors associated with treatment response

Comparisons of clinical characteristics and medical history between responders and non-responders are shown in Table 2. Responders had a significantly higher prevalence of disease-causing TSC1 variants (28.6% vs. 4.0%, p = 0.047) and a lower burden of potential SEGAs (12.5% vs. 55.6%, p = 0.018) than non-responders. There were no significant differences between the two groups in age, sex, number of SEGA, SEGA volume, the prevalence of epilepsy or TSC-associated neuropsychiatric disorders, or everolimus serum concentrations at 1 year.

We further performed an additional sensitivity analysis excluding patients with progression and comparing responders with patients with stable disease only, as patients with true SEGA progression may represent a clinically distinct subgroup from those with stable disease (Supplementary Table S1). While the burden of potential SEGAs remained significantly lower in responders, the association between TSC1/TSC2 genotype and volumetric response no longer reached statistical significance. However, the direction of effect remained consistent, with TSC1 disease-causing variants still being more frequent among responders. Other clinical variables also showed patterns similar to those observed in the primary analysis.

To further evaluate the potential impact of treatment adherence, we examined longitudinal everolimus serum concentrations across responders, patients with stable disease, and those with progression (Supplementary Fig. S1). No clear differences in serum concentration trends were observed among the three groups over time.

Adverse events

All patients experienced at least one AE during the treatment period (Table 3). The most common AEs throughout the treatment period were stomatitis (n = 33, 76.7%), hypercholesterolemia (n = 26, 60.5%), and upper respiratory tract infection (n = 13, 31.0%). Only five patients (11.6%) experienced grade 3 AEs, including pneumonia (n = 3), stomatitis (n = 1), and neutropenia (n = 1). No patients experienced grade 4 AEs. One patient died of septic shock associated with urinary tract infection and pneumonia 45 months after initiation of everolimus treatment. Four patients (9.3%) discontinued everolimus due to adverse events, with stomatitis (n = 3) being the most common reason for treatment discontinuation.

Discussion

This long-term study demonstrated a modest but sustained effectiveness of everolimus for TSC-associated SEGA in a real-world clinical setting. The majority of tumor reduction occurred within the first year, followed by a slower and more gradual improvement thereafter. None of the patients developed hydrocephalus or underwent surgical resection of SEGA during the observation period. Additionally, the present study revealed that TSC1 disease-causing variants and a lower burden of potential SEGA were associated with a favorable treatment response.

The responder rate of everolimus for TSC-associated SEGA in the present study was substantially lower than that reported in extension follow-up studies of previous clinical trials [20,22], in which 50–60% of patients achieved a ≥50% reduction in tumor volume. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, nearly half of the study population did not achieve therapeutic serum concentrations of everolimus, possibly due to dose reductions or temporary treatment interruptions related to adverse events. Nevertheless, SEGA volumes generally remained stable over time, and no patients experienced significant progression leading to hydrocephalus or the need for surgical intervention. These findings are consistent with the results of the EMINENTS study [23], which evaluated the efficacy of reduced-dose everolimus in patients with TSC-associated SEGA and demonstrated disease stability in the majority of patients, without any observed clinical symptoms of progression.

Second, the baseline SEGA volume in our study population (median, 0.38 cm3) was considerably smaller than that reported in previous studies, which ranged from 1.58 to 2.1 cm3 [16,20,23,24]. This finding was similar to data from another study also conducted in Taiwan, which reported a median SEGA volume of 0.24 cm3 [19]. The observed differences in tumor volume may be attributable to ethnic factors, which warrant further investigation. Overall, a smaller baseline SEGA volume may partially explain the lower degree of tumor reduction observed in the present study.

The present study revealed that TSC1 disease-causing variants were associated with a more favorable treatment response. Although statistical significance was attenuated in sensitivity analysis after excluding progression cases, the overall findings—including Kaplan–Meier analysis—support a clinically meaningful association between genotype and treatment response. This finding is consistent with the exploratory analysis of the EXIST-1 trial [23], which showed a lower response rate in patients with a TSC2 mutation. In contrast, the EMINENTS study reported no significant difference in SEGA outcomes between patients with TSC1 and TSC2 disease-causing variants treated with everolimus; however, the sample size was small (n = 14) [23]. A less effective treatment response in patients with TSC2 pathogenic variants may be attributable to a greater overall disease burden and more severe phenotypic manifestations compared with those observed in patients with TSC1 variants [25,26].

In addition, potential SEGAs were found to be less responsive to everolimus in the present study. According to definitions from various consensus meetings [4,11], a potential SEGA is actually a subependymal nodule (SEN) measuring 0.5–1 cm in diameter. The therapeutic efficacy of everolimus for SENs has been infrequently addressed in previous studies, and no definitive conclusions have been reached [24]. We further demonstrated that a lower burden of potential SEGAs was associated with a more favorable treatment outcome following everolimus therapy. To the best of our knowledge, this observation has not been previously reported. SENs are considered precursor lesions of SEGAs [9,10], as they share similar histopathological features [27]. Although the precise pathogenesis underlying the transformation of SENs into SEGAs remains unclear, several studies have demonstrated loss of a functional tuberin–hamartin complex, consistent with the two-hit model of tumorigenesis, resulting in subsequent activation of mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) [[28], [29], [30]]. The lower treatment response observed in patients with an additional burden of potential SEGAs (i.e., non-growing tumors) may reflect a lower degree of mTORC1 activation, rendering these patients less sensitive to mTOR inhibition.

In the present study, the most common AEs were stomatitis, hypercholesterolemia, and upper respiratory tract infection. Only a minority (11.6%) developed grade 3 AEs and none of the patients experienced grade 4 AEs. These results were comparable to those of the previous studies [20,22], suggesting that long-term use of everolimus is generally safe.

There are several limitations to the present study. The retrospective design precluded the establishment of causal relationships. In addition, there was no comparison group of patients with TSC-associated SEGA who did not receive everolimus treatment. Nevertheless, we had demonstrated a progressive increase in SEGA volume during the two years preceding everolimus initiation, followed by a reduction in tumor volume after treatment. Another limitation of this study is that detailed treatment adherence data, including missed doses and temporary treatment interruption, were not systematically recorded because of the retrospective design. Although longitudinal everolimus serum concentrations showed no clear differences among response groups, serum levels may not fully reflect real-world adherence or cumulative drug exposure. Therefore, the influence of treatment adherence on SEGA progression and treatment response cannot be completely excluded. Finally, the relatively small sample size limited further evaluation of genotype–phenotype correlations related to treatment response to everolimus.

In conclusion, everolimus provides modest but sustained volumetric reduction for TSC-associated SEGA in a real-world clinical setting. TSC1 disease-causing variants and a lower burden of potential SEGAs are significantly associated with a more favorable treatment response. These findings provide clinically relevant insights into treatment stratification and individualized response expectations for everolimus therapy in patients with TSC-associated SEGA.
