Effect of conspicuity level on dental students’ visual attention to impacted maxillary canines on panoramic radiographs: an eye-tracking study
Authors: Ozge Ozuturk Tokman, Furkan Ozbey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo quantitatively evaluate the visual attention patterns of dental students when examining panoramic radiographs containing impacted maxillary canines, and to determine how fixation-based metrics, visit-based metrics, and detection rates vary according to the conspicuity level of the impacted canine, defined as the degree to which the canine visually stands out from surrounding anatomical structures.Materials and methodsSixty-four dental students viewed nine panoramic radiographs categorized as high, moderate, or low according to the visual conspicuity of the impacted maxillary canine. Eye movements were recorded using a screen-based eye-tracking system. For polygon-defined areas of interest, fixation durations, fixation counts, visit metrics, and detection rates were calculated. Differences among conspicuity groups were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA, gender-based comparisons using independent-samples t-tests, and detection rates using chi-square tests.ResultsConspicuity level significantly affected the major eye-tracking metrics. High-conspicuity canines were associated with longer viewing times, greater total and mean fixation durations, higher fixation counts, and longer visit durations than moderate- and low-conspicuity canines (all p = 0.001; η²p = 0.42–0.58). Detection rates were 84.9% for high-conspicuity, 75.5% for moderate-conspicuity, and 59.3% for low-conspicuity canines (p = 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.23). Male students showed higher mean fixation duration and total fixation duration than female students (p = 0.019 and p = 0.005, respectively; Cohen’s d = 0.62 and 0.74).ConclusionsDental students’ visual attention patterns while evaluating impacted maxillary canines on panoramic radiographs differed according to target conspicuity. Low-conspicuity canines received less visual engagement and showed lower visual detection rates, indicating that conspicuity is an important determinant of attention allocation during panoramic image evaluation.

Introduction

Impacted maxillary canines are among the most clinically important eruption anomalies of the permanent dentition, with a reported prevalence of approximately 1–3% [1]. Their impaction may lead to significant consequences, including arch irregularities, root resorption of adjacent teeth, and dentigerous cyst formation; therefore, early detection and accurate localization are essential for appropriate treatment planning [2–4]. Panoramic radiography is widely used as the first-line imaging modality for evaluating the position of maxillary canines. However, the inherent limitations of two-dimensional imaging, including anatomical superimposition, distortion, and projection variability, may complicate interpretation, particularly for inexperienced observers [5, 6]. Consistent with this, dental students’ ability to detect impacted canines on panoramic radiographs has been shown to be heterogeneous. Subtle or less visually prominent cases may therefore be more easily overlooked [7–9].

In radiographic image interpretation, conspicuity refers to the extent to which a target structure stands out from its surrounding background and can therefore be more readily noticed during visual search [8]. In this sense, conspicuity is not merely a descriptive visual property, but a factor that may directly influence attentional allocation, search efficiency, and the likelihood that a lesion or anatomical finding will be detected. Eye-tracking technology provides an objective method for analyzing visual search behavior by recording eye movements, fixation locations, and the temporal distribution of visual attention [10, 11]. Accordingly, eye-tracking–derived metrics such as where students look, how long they inspect a region, and how often they return to it are important for clarifying the perceptual basis of diagnostic oversight [7, 8, 12, 13].

Eye-tracking has increasingly been applied in dental imaging research to investigate how observers inspect radiographic images and how expertise shapes examination behavior [7, 12]. Previous studies have shown that experienced observers tend to examine panoramic radiographs in a more systematic, efficient, and target-oriented manner, whereas students often concentrate disproportionately on dental structures, neglect peripheral regions, and miss relatively subtle findings [7, 14]. In addition, the visual conspicuity of a target has been reported to influence time to first fixation, fixation density, revisit behavior, and diagnostic performance in image interpretation tasks [15–17]. These findings suggest that radiographic performance depends not only on anatomical knowledge, but also on how visual search is organized during image examination.

The maxillary canine region is a particularly demanding area in panoramic interpretation because of its anatomical complexity, overlap with adjacent structures, and variable radiographic presentation [18, 19]. Despite this clinical relevance, no eye-tracking study has specifically examined how dental students evaluate impacted maxillary canines across different levels of conspicuity on panoramic radiographs. Existing studies have described general visual search behavior in panoramic interpretation, but it remains unclear how the conspicuity of an impacted canine affects students’ fixation behavior, revisit patterns, and likelihood of visual detection in this anatomically challenging region [7, 12, 20].

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to quantitatively evaluate the visual attention patterns of dental students while examining panoramic radiographs containing impacted maxillary canines with different levels of conspicuity. Specifically, the study assessed fixation-based parameters, including total fixation duration, mean fixation duration, and fixation count, together with visit-based parameters, including time to first fixation, first fixation duration, visit duration, and visit count, as well as visual detection rates. It can be hypothesized that these eye-tracking metrics and detection rates would differ significantly according to conspicuity level. Highly conspicuous impacted canines are expected to attract longer and more sustained visual attention and to show higher detection rates than low-conspicuity cases. As a secondary exploratory consideration, possible overall gender-related differences in visual attention metrics were also examined.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University Non-Interventional Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025/286). All participants were informed about the purpose, procedures, and confidentiality of the study prior to participation, and written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants. All methods were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional eye-tracking study was designed to evaluate the visual attention and scanning behaviors of dental students while examining panoramic radiographs containing impacted maxillary canines with different levels of conspicuity. A total of 64 volunteer students (32 male and 32 female) from the Faculty of Dentistry at Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University were included in the study.

The sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software to detect at least a medium effect size in fixation-based metrics. Assuming α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, and Cohen’s recommended medium effect size (d = 0.50), a minimum of 48 participants was required. To account for potential data loss, 64 students were ultimately included.

An a priori power analysis was used to determine the required participant sample size for the eye-tracking outcomes. In contrast, the number of radiographic stimuli was determined as a design decision. The stimulus set was intentionally limited to nine panoramic radiographs to preserve the feasibility of the eye-tracking session.

Participants with normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity who successfully completed the calibration procedure and provided informed voluntary consent were included. Individuals with strabismus, nystagmus, significant oculomotor or neurological disorders, or those who failed calibration were excluded.

Eye-tracking environment and equipment

Eye-tracking recordings were conducted at the Faculty of Dentistry of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University in a quiet room with controlled and stable lighting conditions. The ambient light level was maintained at approximately 300–350 lx, and background noise was kept below 40 dB. Participants were seated upright in an adjustable chair. A head stabilizer was not used because the eye-tracking system operated on a remote tracking principle.

Eye movements were recorded using the Tobii Pro Spark (Tobii AB, Sweden), a screen-based eye tracker with a 60 Hz sampling rate. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Tobii Pro Lab software (version 25.19). Fixations were identified using the software’s default I-VT algorithm with a velocity threshold of 30°/s and a minimum fixation duration of 60 ms. Digital panoramic radiographs were displayed on a LENOVO 16QSV34 16-inch IPS display/monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution, 24-bit color depth, 60 Hz refresh rate, 250 cd/m² brightness, and 1000:1 contrast ratio). Before each test session, the monitor was checked for brightness, contrast, and glare.

A nine-point calibration was performed for all participants, and calibration validity required a data loss rate of less than 20%. Prior to data collection, each participant underwent a standardized calibration procedure consisting of: (1) Participants were allowed to sit freely in a natural seating arrangement using a desktop monitor; however, during calibration, their eye movements were individually matched with the eye-tracking device to ensure recording accuracy. Artificial setups designed to fix the eye level were avoided to prevent influencing visual inspection behavior and to preserve natural viewing conditions. (2) a nine-point calibration sequence in which participants fixated on each calibration point as it appeared on screen; and (3) automatic validation by Tobii Pro Lab software, with successful calibration defined as achieving accuracy ≤ 1.0° visual angle and precision ≤ 0.5° for both eyes. Only participants with successful calibration were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Image material

The digital panoramic radiographs used in this study consisted of nine images selected from the archive of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology at Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University. All radiographs had been acquired as part of routine clinical care using the same digital panoramic unit model, Planmeca ProMax 2D (Planmeca Inc., Helsinki, Finland), and standard patient positioning procedures in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Because archived clinical panoramic radiographs were used, exposure parameters and overall image appearance were not fully identical across all cases, and some natural variation in contrast and display characteristics was present among images. These variations were not intentionally introduced for experimental manipulation; however, they may have contributed to the perceived conspicuity of the impacted canine. No additional study-specific post-processing or image enhancement was applied by the authors after image selection. Only images showing impacted maxillary canines with fully formed roots were included to ensure anatomical consistency across conspicuity groups.

The use of nine panoramic radiographs, with three images in each conspicuity category, was determined in accordance with the study design and the participant-based power analysis. In this eye-tracking design, the primary analytical unit was the participant rather than the image. In addition, limiting the number of radiographs helped reduce potential learning effects, repeated pattern recognition, and cumulative cognitive load that could arise if participants were exposed to a substantially larger number of similar panoramic images containing the same type of target finding. This approach was also consistent with previous eye-tracking studies in dental radiology that used relatively small but purposefully selected image sets to preserve interpretability and experimental control.

To determine the conspicuity level of the impacted maxillary canines, all radiographs were independently evaluated by two oral and maxillofacial radiologists. Interobserver agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.91). Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached.

The nine panoramic images were classified into three groups according to the conspicuity level of the impacted canine:

High conspicuity (n = 3): cases in which both the crown and root of the impacted canine exhibited clearly defined contours with minimal overlap from adjacent anatomical structures (Fig. 2a).Moderate conspicuity (n = 3): cases in which at least one component (crown or root) of the impacted canine was discernible, while the other was not clearly visible because of superimposition or relatively low contrast (Fig. 2b).Low conspicuity (n = 3): cases in which neither the crown nor the root of the impacted canine could be distinctly identified, with blurred contours or marked superimposition by surrounding structures (Fig. 2c).

Area of Interest (AOI) definitions

In each panoramic image, an AOI encompassing the impacted canine was defined. An AOI (area of interest) is defined as a pre-determined region of the image, specified by the researcher, within which visual attention metrics are calculated in eye-tracking analyses. AOIs were delineated using polygonal boundaries and standardized to fully encompass the impacted canine (Fig. 3). AOI boundaries were defined to represent comparable proportions of the visual field across all images. All patient information in the images was fully anonymized.

Procedure and stimulus presentation

Panoramic radiographs were presented to participants in a randomized order. A maximum viewing time of 120 s was assigned for each image; however, participants were allowed to proceed to the next image at any time once they felt that they had completed their examination. To minimize order effects, the presentation sequence was randomized across participants using the randomization module of Tobii Pro Lab software. All participants completed all nine images consecutively.

No response, decision-making task, or marking on the image was required. These requirements were intentionally avoided because they could alter natural scanning behavior, increase cognitive load, and artificially influence eye-movement patterns. This design was preferred to capture spontaneous visual search behavior under conditions that more closely resemble routine image viewing.

For operational purposes, an image was considered visually detected when the participant directed at least one fixation to the AOI. A fixation was defined according to the I-VT algorithm parameters described above (velocity threshold 30°/s; minimum duration 60 ms). This operational definition was used to quantify visual engagement with the target region rather than definitive diagnostic recognition.

Eye-tracking metrics

Eye-tracking analysis was conducted using fixation-based and visit-based parameters to characterize visual attention allocation and scanning behavior. Fixations were identified using the I-VT (velocity-threshold identification) algorithm (velocity threshold = 30°/s; minimum fixation duration = 60 ms).

A visit was defined as a continuous sequence of one or more fixations within the same AOI, bounded by fixations outside that AOI. Visit-based metrics capture the broader organizational structure of visual scanning behavior.

Together, fixation-based metrics describe micro-level attentional processing, whereas visit-based metrics reflect macro-level scanning organization and re-examination behavior.

Heat map and scan path analysis

To visualize visual attention across the participant cohort, aggregated heat maps were generated for each conspicuity level using Tobii Pro Lab software. Heat maps represent the spatial density of fixations, with warmer colors indicating areas of concentrated visual attention and cooler colors indicating lower fixation density.

In addition, individual scanpaths were generated for representative participants to illustrate the temporal sequence and spatial trajectory of eye movements. These scanpaths were included as qualitative examples to demonstrate how AOI-directed and non-AOI-directed viewing patterns appeared at the individual level and to complement the quantitative eye-tracking metrics.

Eye-tracking variables and data analysis

Key fixation-based metrics, including total fixation duration, average fixation duration, fixation count, time to first fixation, and first fixation duration, were calculated for each AOI. In addition, visit-based variables, including total visit duration, average visit duration, and visit count, were evaluated. Detection rate was defined as the proportion of observations in which at least one fixation was directed to the AOI. Heat maps and scanpaths were generated using Tobii Pro Lab software for qualitative support.

All quantitative data exported from the software were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0. Distribution characteristics were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics for normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Gender-based comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test, and Cohen’s d was calculated as the effect size. Differences among conspicuity levels (high, moderate, and low) were analyzed using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, and partial eta squared (η²p) was reported as the effect size. When significant differences were identified, Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons were performed. When significant differences were identified, pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction; for the three pairwise comparisons, the adjusted significance level was α = 0.0167. Detection rates across conspicuity levels were compared using the chi-square test, and Cramer’s V was calculated as a measure of effect size. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study provides eye-tracking-based evidence that the conspicuity level of impacted maxillary canines substantially influences how dental students inspect panoramic radiographs. Cases with high conspicuity were associated with longer viewing time, greater fixation-related engagement, longer visit durations, and higher visual detection rates, whereas low-conspicuity cases were associated with shorter and more limited visual exploration and were less frequently fixated within the area of interest. The consistency of this pattern across multiple fixation-based and visit-based metrics, together with the corresponding heat map findings, supports the interpretation that conspicuity is a major determinant of visual attention allocation during students’ panoramic image evaluation.

These findings, indicating that students allocate more visual resources to high-conspicuity images, are consistent with previous studies in the dental education literature. Botelho et al. reported that dental students tend to spend longer periods examining high-contrast or morphologically distinctive structures on panoramic images, whereas areas with lower visibility elicit more limited scanning behavior [21]. Similarly, studies by Gnanasekaran and Muthu have demonstrated that the visual conspicuity of a pathology directly influences students’ examination time and diagnostic performance [20, 22]. In this study, mean fixation duration and total visit duration were found to be significantly higher for highly conspicuous canines. This finding indicates that these images were processed in greater detail. In contrast, the shorter durations and lower detection rates observed in low-conspicuity cases suggest that weaker visual cues limit the depth of examination. The large effect sizes observed in our study (η²p = 0.42–0.58 for fixation metrics) further underscore the magnitude of this relationship and suggest that conspicuity is a primary determinant of visual attention allocation in this context. While longer fixation durations are often interpreted as indicators of deeper cognitive processing [23, 24], it is important to note that our study design—which did not include explicit diagnostic tasks—limits our ability to directly infer cognitive depth from fixation duration alone. Future studies incorporating think-aloud protocols or diagnostic accuracy measures would provide stronger evidence for the cognitive processes underlying these fixation patterns.

In addition, because some previous visual-search literature has suggested possible differences in spatial attention and scanning style between male and female observers, gender was included as a secondary exploratory variable rather than a primary study factor [7, 12, 20]. The modest gender-related differences observed in some metrics should also be interpreted cautiously. Although male students showed higher values for certain fixation-based parameters and a slightly higher overall detection rate, the effect sizes were limited, and these findings do not support a strong or clinically decisive gender-based distinction in panoramic image evaluation. The literature on gender-related differences in visual search is inconsistent: although some studies report that males demonstrate more focused scanning while females use a broader visual field, a meaningful advantage in dental image interpretation is seldom demonstrated [7, 12, 20]. The modest gender difference observed in detection rates (59.4% vs. 50.7%, Cramer’s V = 0.08) should be interpreted cautiously, as the effect size is small and may reflect sampling variability rather than a systematic cognitive or perceptual difference. Therefore, although the gender difference observed in our study was statistically significant, further research is needed to determine how this finding might translate into routine clinical performance.

The systematic pattern observed across both fixation-based and visit-based metrics in relation to conspicuity level suggests that greater attention to visual difficulty may be useful in dental radiology education. In particular, impacted maxillary canines with low conspicuity appeared to pose greater visual challenges for students and were associated with shorter and more limited examinations. This finding may have implications for curriculum design, suggesting that educational programs could benefit from including cases characterized by low contrast, pronounced anatomical superimposition, or poorly defined contours. Such integration could be explored through progressive case difficulty sequencing, instruction in systematic search strategies, and, where available, eye-tracking-based feedback to help students become aware of potentially overlooked regions. For example, future educational studies could examine whether showing students their own scan paths alongside those of expert radiologists improves awareness of overlooked or insufficiently examined regions. Additionally, simulation-based training modules that adaptively increase case difficulty based on individual performance could provide personalized learning experiences that address each student’s specific perceptual and cognitive weaknesses.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, conspicuity classification was based on expert assessment and consensus, although reproducibility was high, rather than on objective psychophysical image measures such as contrast-to-noise ratio or spatial frequency characteristics. Second, the operational definition of visual detection required only a single fixation within the AOI, and this may overestimate true diagnostic recognition, since fixation does not necessarily indicate correct interpretation. Third, the study included only fourth- and fifth-year dental students; therefore, the findings cannot be directly generalized to experienced clinicians. Fourth, the study was limited to impacted maxillary canines on panoramic radiographs, and the observed attention patterns may not be identical for other lesion types or imaging tasks.

A further limitation concerns the image set. Only nine panoramic radiographs were used, with three images in each conspicuity category. This choice was made to minimize potential learning effects, pattern-recognition bias after repeated exposure to impacted canine cases, and additional cognitive load associated with prolonged panoramic viewing. Similar eye-tracking studies in the literature have also employed limited image sets [11, 14, 22, 25].This design was selected in accordance with the participant-based power analysis and to preserve experimental control. It was also intended to minimize potential learning effects, repeated target familiarization, and cumulative cognitive load that could occur if participants were exposed to a substantially larger number of similar radiographs. Nevertheless, the limited number of images may reduce external validity, and individual image characteristics may have influenced the observed eye-tracking measures.In addition, all radiographs were acquired with the same panoramic unit model. However, exposure parameters and overall image appearance were not fully identical across archived clinical images, and such variation may have influenced perceived conspicuity. These differences were not intentionally introduced, but rather reflected the heterogeneity of routine clinical imaging. Rather than excluding such variation entirely, these naturally occurring differences were retained to better represent the variability encountered in routine clinical imaging. However, the independent contribution of image contrast to conspicuity was not quantified in the present study and should be investigated directly in future research.

Despite these limitations, the present study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that the conspicuity of impacted maxillary canines is closely associated with how dental students visually inspect panoramic radiographs. The combined use of fixation-based metrics, visit-based metrics, and qualitative visualizations offers a more comprehensive account of students’ search behavior in this clinically relevant task. Overall, the findings support the view that lower-conspicuity cases pose a greater attentional challenge and warrant particular consideration in future research and educational design.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that dental students’ visual attention patterns while evaluating panoramic radiographs containing impacted maxillary canines differ significantly according to the conspicuity level of the target. Highly conspicuous canines were associated with longer fixation and visit durations and higher visual detection rates, whereas low-conspicuity cases were associated with shorter and more limited visual exploration and were less frequently fixated within the area of interest. Although the gender difference observed in this study was statistically significant, further research is needed to determine how this finding may translate to routine clinical performance. These findings indicate that conspicuity is an important determinant of visual attention allocation during panoramic image evaluation.
