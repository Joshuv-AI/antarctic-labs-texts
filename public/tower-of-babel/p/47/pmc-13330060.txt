Stabilized mosaic hemagglutinin immunogens as novel universal influenza virus vaccines
Authors: Mitali Mishra, Eduard Puente-Massaguer, Andrew T Jones, Jose Martínez-Guevara, Tsoi Ying Lai, Adam Abdeljawad, Kevin Ding, Naiying Zheng, Stylianos Bournazos, Jeffrey V Ravetch, Florian Krammer, Patrick C Wilson, Peter Palese, Irene González-Domínguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfluenza virus undergoes frequent changes in its main surface glycoprotein, hemagglutinin (HA), evading pre-existing immunity acquired by a previous infection or vaccination. To overcome this challenge, we developed a novel vaccination strategy based on recombinant mosaic HAs (mHAs) that are stabilized without the use of an exogenous trimerization domain (foldon). These stabilized mHAs maintained high trimeric HA content and demonstrated enhanced stability at elevated temperatures and low pH. In mice, sequential immunization with stabilized mHAs redirected the antibody response toward conserved HA regions, resulting in broad protection against diverse influenza virus strains. Compared with foldon-containing constructs, stabilized mHAs improved epitope exposure while avoiding unwanted anti-foldon immune responses. We further compared the immune responses elicited by stabilized mHAs with those induced by conventional seasonal influenza vaccines. Notably, stabilized mHA vaccines conferred protection in the absence of hemagglutination inhibition antibodies, unlike seasonal influenza vaccines. This protection was mediated by a combination of serum neutralization and Fc effector functions predominantly targeting the HA stalk domain. Stabilized mHAs also provided broad cross-protection against homologous (pH1N1), heterologous (SwH1N2), and cross-subtype (H5N1) virus challenges in the mouse model. Overall, stabilized mHAs represent a promising alternative for the development of next-generation universal influenza virus vaccines.

INTRODUCTION

Influenza viruses represent a persistent global health threat, with seasonal epidemics causing significant morbidity and mortality every year.1 Vaccination remains the most effective method to prevent severe influenza illness.2 The influenza virus hemagglutinin (HA) has been the primary target for vaccine development. HA consists of a highly variable globular head domain and a more conserved, membrane-proximal stalk domain.3 The head domain contains the receptor-binding site (RBS), which allows influenza viruses to bind sialic acids and enter host cells.4 This domain also harbors the major antigenic regions that serve as the main target of neutralizing antibodies with hemagglutination inhibition (HI) activity, which are also the main sites of antigenic drift in influenza viruses.5 Since the establishment of the Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System (GISRS) in 1952, the World Health Organization has recommended the composition of influenza virus vaccines for each season.6 These vaccines are designed based on HI-active antibodies, making their protection largely strain specific, with poor induction of cross-reactive antibodies against other strains.7,8 Therefore, developing universal influenza vaccines capable of providing broad and lasting protection against diverse influenza strains remains a high priority.4,9–11

Mosaic hemagglutinins (mHAs) have shown great promise as universal influenza vaccine candidates.12–15 Mosaic HAs are engineered by replacing the major immunodominant antigenic sites in the head domain with those derived from exotic avian influenza viruses to which humans have no prior exposure.16 Sequential immunizations with these mHA vaccines aim to redirect the immune responses toward conserved, immuno-subdominant epitopes located in both the head and stalk domains.12 Recent data suggest that recombinant HA vaccines offer superior protection compared with classical egg-produced influenza vaccines.17,18 Moreover, egg-based vaccines depend on the egg supply chain for production,19 which may be disrupted during events such as avian influenza pandemic, as seen with the ongoing H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) outbreak.

Despite their promise, mHA protein-based vaccines face challenges, including low expression levels, instability, and improper folding of quaternary HA structures.20 These issues may hinder the presentation of conformational epitopes, which are critical for in vivo cross-protection.21 Because HA is a transmembrane protein, soluble forms require the removal of its transmembrane and cytoplasmic tails. However, the uncleaved HA ectodomain fails to assemble into a stable and homogeneous trimer, requiring additional protein engineering. Traditional approaches for stabilizing HA ectodomains involve fusion with exogenous trimerization domains, such as the bacteriophage T4 fibritin foldon20 or the leucine-rich GCN4 domain.22 However, the inclusion of such exogenous trimerization domains has been shown to induce undesired off-target immune responses,23–25 which could reduce influenza antigen-specific immunity, particularly after repeated immunization.25

To address these challenges, several innovative strategies have been developed to stabilize trimeric HA proteins. These include introducing interprotomer disulfide bonds,25,26 stabilizing the HA prefusion conformation,27 and disrupting HA-sialic acid binding.28 Each strategy has been independently tested to improve the stability of wild-type HA proteins. In the present study, we combined these novel approaches to stabilize mHA immunogens for universal influenza vaccine development. We developed novel stabilized mHA constructs and assessed their quaternary structure, stability, and immunogenicity in preclinical mouse models. Here, we report that our stabilized mHAs exhibited high trimeric HA stability and unique epitope display. In animals, stabilized mHAs induced broadly protective immune responses, even in the absence of HI-active antibodies. Overall, these findings support the potential of stabilized mHAs as protein-based universal influenza vaccines.

RESULTS

Design and characterization of stabilized mHA immunogens

Recombinant mHA immunogens were designed by replacing the major antigenic regions in the HA head domain with those of exotic avian species as described previously.16 Specifically, for H1, five major antigenic sites have been identified: the specific site a (Sa), the constant site a1 (Ca1), the constant site a2 (Ca2), the specific site b (Sb), and the constant site b (Cb) (Figure 1).16 Two different recombinant mosaic HAs (mH5/1 and mH13/1) were engineered in the H1 backbone of A/Michigan/45/2015, where the main immunodominant sites were replaced with sequences from H5 (A/Vietnam/1203/2004-H5N1) or H13 (A/black headed gull/Sweden/1/1999-H13N6) HAs respectively. Foldon mHAs were designed with a C-terminal T4 trimerization domain following the H1 ectodomain for expression (Figure 1B). In case of the stabilized mHAs, three sets of mutations (in H3 numbering) were introduced as compared with the foldon mHAs. These include a Y98F sialic acid-binding knock down mutation (shown in dark blue) in the RBS,28 two disulfide bond-forming mutations (HA1 L30C, HA2 G47C, shown in orange),26 and three prefusion-stabilizing mutations in the stalk domain (H26W, K51I and E103I shown in green),27 as depicted in the 3D structure (Figure 1A) and linear sequence (Figure 1B).

The protein quality, quaternary conformation, and stability of these mHA immunogens were studied by several techniques. To assess the protein production quality, reducing and non-reducing sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels were performed. Due to the introduction of interprotomer disulfide bonds, dimeric and trimeric HA structures were observed in the non-reducing gel for stabilized mHAs, as compared with foldon mHAs (Figure 2A). Partial disulfide formation (resulting in dimers) may reflect incomplete incorporation, warranting further investigation as this mutation combination is novel to our knowledge. In the reducing gel, the stabilized mHAs run at slightly higher molecular weight corresponding to foldon mHAs (Figure 2A). This could be attributed to glycosylation profiles generated by mammalian expression systems used to produce stabilized mHAs. On the other hand, in native gel electrophoresis, a single band of trimeric HA (~300 kDa) was observed for both foldon and stabilized mHA purified proteins (Figure S1A). Further assessment of mHA structures by electron microscopy confirmed the presence of homogenous trimeric structures in these preparations (Figure S1B).

Next, we studied the hemagglutination activity of the two sets of immunogens in an HA assay. Compared with foldon mHAs, stabilized mHAs presented no sialic-acid-binding activity, in agreement with the presence of the Y98F mutation (Figure 2B). The trimeric HA content was then investigated using size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Both the foldon and stabilized mHA presented trimeric HA conformations in comparison to a commercial H1 monomer (Figures 2C and 2D), but foldon mHA trimers run slightly higher than stabilized mHA trimers due to the presence of foldon domain. In addition, the foldon mH5/1 was observed to have more aggregates in native protein (Figure 2C) compared with foldon mH13/1.

Thermal and pH stability were studied by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) and dynamic light scattering (DLS). Raw relative fluorescence (RFU) data (Figure S2) were used to calculate the first derivative (Figures 2E and 2F). Stabilized mHAs presented superior melting temperatures (Tm50) at physiological pH (Figure 2E) with a two-peak transition at 58°C and 66°C compared with foldon mHAs (single peak at 55°C). Additional stability analyses at low pH (pH 5.5, Figure 2F) showed an earlier first transition peak in foldon mHAs (Tm1 at 44°C) compared with stabilized mHAs that translated into protein unfolding and aggregation observed by DLS (Figures 2G and 2H). Stabilized mH5/1 and mH13/1 proteins presented a uniform particle size of around 15 nm at both pHs tested. On the contrary, foldon mHAs formed multiple aggregates at low pH as indicated by 2- to 3-fold higher particle sizes (Figure 2H). Taken together, these results prove that the stabilized mHA proteins presented enhanced thermal and pH stability and high trimeric HA content in the absence of an exogenous trimerization domain.

Antigenicity of stabilized mHA immunogens

To determine the structural integrity and antigenicity of stabilized mHA immunogens, the binding activity to relevant epitopes was tested by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) against a panel of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting different regions of HA (Table S1). Stabilized mHAs showed 2-fold lower 50% binding concentration (EC50) values toward 1A01 (Figure 3A) than foldon mHAs, suggesting that these unique head epitopes are more accessible in stabilized mHAs. In case of the trimer interface FluA-20 mAb, both stabilized and foldon mH5/1 constructs maintained a similar binding activity (Figure 3B), unlike mH13/1 where stabilized mHA had 2-fold higher binding activity to FluA-20. We also validated that FluA-20 mAb binds only to trimeric HAs and not to monomeric HAs, as shown in Figure S3. Interestingly, stabilized mHAs had 3- to 4-fold higher binding activity toward anti-lateral patch mAb (047–09M 2F03), whereas foldon mHAs had very high EC50 values for this mAb (Figure 3C). This indicated that these lateral patch epitopes, somehow, are more exposed in stabilized mHAs structure. However, both foldon and stabilized mHAs retained a similar binding profile to central stalk mAbs CR9114 (Figure 3D), suggesting similar central stalk arrangements in both mHA constructs. While assessing the binding activity toward anti-RBS mAb (029–09 3A02), we noticed that stabilized mHAs still retained binding toward RBS site, even though these constructs had a Y98F point mutation in the RBS region (Figure 3E). Furthermore, the stabilized mHAs displayed greater binding to 029–09 3A02 than foldon mHAs, even 40-fold higher binding activity in case of stabilized mH13/1. This suggests that the overall RBS architecture remains the same in the stabilized mHAs, and only the sialic-acid-binding activity is diminished (Figure 2B). In addition, stabilized mHAs showed 4- to 6-fold higher binding activity compared with foldon mHAs for 047–4904 anti-anchor mAb (Figure 3F). In a similar fashion, for another anti-anchor mAb 350–132, stabilized mH13/1 demonstrated a 60-fold lower EC50 value than foldon mH13/1 (Figure 3G). This reiterates the higher accessibility of anti-anchor epitopes in case of stabilized mHAs than foldon mHAs. Interestingly, a 3-fold higher binding toward the foldon domain was observed with foldon mHAs using a polyclonal commercial anti-foldon Wac antibody (Figure 3H). Taken together, the ELISA binding data indicate that the stabilized mHA could better expose these lateral patch and anchor epitopes in their native prefusion conformation, while retaining the central stalk epitope of foldon mHA.

Immunogenicity of stabilized mHA in mice

Next, we investigated the immunogenicity of stabilized mHAs. To do so, we immunized 8-week-old female BALB/c mice in a prime-boost regimen as shown in Figure 4A. Briefly, the two groups of mice (n = 10/group) were vaccinated intramuscularly with two doses of 5 μg of stabilized or foldon mHA (mH5/1) followed by 5 μg of stabilized or foldon mHA (mH13/1) in a 4-week interval. Vaccines were administered in the presence of the oil-in-water emulsion adjuvant AddaVax at a 1:1 (v:v) ratio. The naive group of mice (n = 10) was left unvaccinated to compare the immune responses.

First, we determined the total serum IgG antibody responses toward a panel of homologous and heterologous group 1 influenza viruses and recombinant proteins by ELISA. Purified viruses were preferred in these assays since recombinant HA proteins contain the same foldon trimerization domain as the one provided in the foldon mHA vaccinated group. Firstly, serum IgG binding antibody titers against A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1) (Figure 4B), A/Netherlands/602/2009 (pH1N1) (Figure 4C), A/Victoria/2570/2019 (H1N1) (Figure 4D), A/Vietnam/1203/2004 (H5N1) (Figure 4E), and A/chicken/Hong Kong/G9/1997 (H9N2) (Figure 4F) were measured, and an irrelevant protein containing the T4 trimerization domain was used as foldon-binding control (Figure 4G). In all these assays, both stabilized mHA and foldon mHA vaccinations elicited equal levels of IgG binding antibody titers against all tested antigens (Figures 4B–4F). Notably, post-boost sera from stabilized mHA-immunized mice showed higher neutralizing antibody titers against the same three H1N1 viruses compared with foldon mHA recipients (Figures 4H–4J), consistent with superior presentation of broadly cross-reactive epitopes (Figures 3F and 3G). In a prime-boost-boost regimen (Figure S4), both vaccines induced similar total IgG titers, but neutralizing titer improvements were limited to the heterologous A/Victoria/2570/2019 virus, suggesting a potential ceiling effect in the three-dose vaccination model. Finally, foldon mHA vaccination triggered robust responses to the exogenous foldon domain after just two doses (Figure 4G), which intensified further after three doses (Figure S4). These findings underscore the importance of eliminating non-native trimerization domains to support clinical translation of recombinant HA-based vaccines.

Stabilized mHA vaccine protects mice from lethal challenge in the absence of HI-active antibodies

The in vivo protection of stabilized mHA vaccination was evaluated in mice. To do that, we compared stabilized mHA vaccination versus the standard of care quadrivalent inactivated vaccine (QIV with matched antigen A/Michigan/45/2015, from 2017 to 2018 season) and a naive group as negative control. Mice were vaccinated in a prime-boost scheme as mentioned earlier and 4 weeks after final immunization; mice were challenged intranasally with three times 50% murine lethal dose (3 mLD50) of matched A/Michigan/45/2015 virus (Figure 5A). Stabilized mHA and QIV vaccination provided complete protection against lethal challenge of matched A/Michigan/45/2015 virus (Figures 5B and 5C). To examine the protection in lungs, we challenged them with a sub-lethal dose of 0.1 mLD50 of A/Michigan/45/2015 (HIN1) 4 weeks post boost and harvested the lungs on day 3 for viral titer determination by plaque assay. The data revealed complete clearance of the virus from lungs upon day 3 post infection in both the stabilized mHA and QIV-vaccinated groups (Figure 5D).

Next, we investigated antibody functions and possible mechanisms of protection of these two vaccination regimens. Only QIV-vaccinated mice induced HI activity against the homologous virus, whereas no HI activity was observed in mHA-vaccinated animals (Figure 5E).29 This highlighted the protein engineering in our mHAs to get rid of the immunodominance of the HA head. We next performed an in vitro microneutralization assay against the homologous A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1) virus. We observed neutralizing activity of the sera antibodies with both stabilized mHA and QIV vaccines (Figure 5F).

Besides neutralizing antibodies, another mode of protection against influenza disease is through Fc-mediated effector functions driven by mostly non-neutralizing antibodies.30 To investigate the antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) activity of serum antibodies, we performed an ADCC reporter assay against A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1) virus-infected cells. Stabilized mHA produced moderate Fc-mediated functions for ADCC (Figure 5G) that correlated with the presence of IgG2a and IgG2b antibody subclasses in the sera (Figure 5H).31 Although, a general IgG1-skewed immune response was observed in both vaccinations, which could be attributed to the use of AddaVax adjuvant.32 Finally, binding antibody titers against different domains of the HA protein were compared for both vaccination regimens. IgG serum antibody titers against A/Michigan/45/2015 whole virus, recombinant HA1 globular head domain, and headless HA and cH8/1 proteins were measured (Figure 5I). QIV presented the highest antibody titers against the whole virion, which will encompass antibodies targeting the homologous Michigan/45/2015 HA but also the neuraminidase (NA) and nucleoprotein (NP), among others. Furthermore, we observed 25-fold higher binding antibody titers with QIV vaccination against the HA globular head of A/Michigan/45/2015, as compared with stabilized mHAs (Figure 5I). This is in agreement with the presence of HI-active antibodies that mainly target the major antigenic regions contained in that domain (Figure 5E). On the other hand, stabilized mHA vaccination induced 140-fold higher binding titers against the headless HA (stalk) and 8-fold higher toward cH8/1 recombinant proteins as compared with QIV (Figure 5I). This emphasized the generation of more stalk-directed antibodies in the serum with stabilized mHA vaccines, which was confirmed by competition assays against the CR9114 monoclonal antibody that binds the central stalk domain (Figure 5J).

Stabilized mHA vaccine protects mice from heterologous and heterosubtypic influenza A virus challenges

We next wanted to determine the breadth of protection provided by stabilized mHA vaccines. For this, 4 weeks post boost, mice were challenged with 3 mLD50 of a mismatched pandemic pH1N1 (A/Netherlands/602/2009), Swine H1N2 (A/swine/Germany/Bak50/2017), and heterosubtypic H5N1 (A/Vietnam/1203/2004) viruses (Figures 6A and 6B). All mice from the naive group succumbed to infection in the challenge studies. In case of distant pH1N1 and swine H1N2 virus challenge, both the stabilized and QIV-vaccinated mice showed complete protection. Interestingly, in a phylogenetically distant heterologous H5N1 lethal challenge, all QIV-vaccinated mice succumbed to infection by day 8, whereas only stabilized mHA-vaccinated animals provided sub-optimal cross-protection of 40%. The lack of protection of QIV could be correlated with the absence of HI-active antibodies against H5N1 viruses (Figure 6C). On the other hand, stabilized mHAs provided protection from infection even in the absence of HI-active antibodies. This confirmed that the protection elicited by seasonal influenza vaccination is strain specific and mostly HI dependent, whereas the stabilized mHA vaccines can induce broadly cross-protective immune responses.

DISCUSSION

Stabilizing multimeric glycoproteins is a promising strategy to enhance their immunogenicity as vaccine candidates.33–37 Two successful examples in currently licensed vaccines include the introduction of prolines in the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein33 and the prefusion stabilization of the respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) fusion (F) protein.36 For recombinant protein vaccines, the expression of high-quality antigens with correct conformation and optimal stability is essential.38 Here, we report the development of stabilized mHAs as universal influenza vaccine immunogens and demonstrate their efficacy in a preclinical animal model. Specifically, we engineered stabilized mHAs by introducing interprotomer disulfide bonds,26 prefusion-stabilizing mutations,27 and a sialic-acid-binding knockdown mutation28 into the backbone of our previously established mHA sequence.16 In the absence of a foldon trimerization domain, our stabilized mHAs produced higher levels of trimeric HA (Figures 2A, 2C, and 2D) and exhibited greater stability under external conditions, including elevated temperature and acidic pH (Figures 2E–2H). Low pH triggers structural transition of HA from the prefusion to the postfusion conformation.39 Thus, unlike foldon mHAs, the stabilized mHAs retained more trimeric HA in the prefusion state, likely due to mutations in the pH-switch regions of HA.27 Although both prefusion and postfusion conformations are immunogenic in nature, studies of RSV vaccines showed that the prefusion structure induced highly potent neutralizing antibodies,36 and a similar model has been proposed for HA-based influenza vaccines.27

Furthermore, compared with foldon mHAs, our stabilized mHAs demonstrated higher binding activity to unique head, lateral patch, and anchor-specific monoclonal antibodies (Figures 3A–3C, 3F, and 3G), some of which target broadly neutralizing epitopes.21,40 The increased binding activity of stabilized mHAs to these immuno-subdominant epitopes in the head and stem regions correlated with higher serum neutralizing antibody titers (in the absence of HI-active antibodies) in mice (Figures 4H–4J). This could be attributed to a better presentation of head and anchor epitopes in the stabilized mHAs. Previous studies have shown that membrane-proximal anchor epitope-targeting antibodies can be recalled in humans after seasonal influenza vaccination with an oil-in-water adjuvant, like AddaVax, which was also used here.40 Compared with foldon mHAs, stabilized mHAs demonstrated higher binding activity to anti-RBS monoclonal antibodies (Figure 3E), despite carrying a point mutation in the RBS that reduced sialic acid binding (Figure 2B). This suggests that stabilized mHA proteins not only preserved the overall RBS architecture but also exposed sub-dominant broadly neutralizing epitopes that may remain structurally buried in trimeric HA presented by foldon mHAs. Whether these differences between the two mHA designs are driven by trimeric HA conformation, epitope accessibility, or glycosylation patterns need to be determined. Additionally, the cumulative effects of these stabilizing mutations on overall trimer structure merit further investigation. Notably, we report for the first time (to our knowledge) incomplete incorporation of disulfide bonds within the stabilized trimeric structure after the incorporation of HA1 L30C and HA2 G47C mutations.26

We next assessed the immunogenicity of foldon and stabilized mHAs in mice. Both vaccines induced comparable serum binding antibody titers against diverse heterologous and heterosubtypic influenza viruses (Figure 4), consistent with our previous findings that sequential mHA immunizations broaden immune responses by targeting conserved HA regions.12,15 However, a key distinction was the strong induction of anti-foldon antibodies following foldon mHA immunization (Figures 4G and S4). Although we did not evaluate the long-term impact of these anti-foldon responses on HA-specific immunity, prior studies have shown that exogenous foldon trimerization domains generate unwanted anti-foldon antibodies upon repeated vaccination in both preclinical and clinical settings.24 Hence, novel scaffold-free designs may overcome one of the longstanding challenges of glycoprotein-based vaccines.23,24

The protective efficacy of stabilized mHAs was evaluated in a direct viral challenge in mice (Figure 6). Both QIV and stabilized mHAs provided complete protection against lethal challenge with the parental strain A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1). Stabilized mHAs limited viral replication in the lungs to levels comparable to QIV (Figure 5D). Importantly, unlike QIV-vaccinated mice, protection conferred by stabilized mHAs was independent of HI-active antibodies, indicating that immunity was mediated by antibodies targeting immuno-subdominant and conserved HA epitopes (Figure 5E). Stabilized mHAs also elicited serum neutralizing antibodies and Fc-mediated effector functions in mice. Fc-mediated protection correlated with the presence of IgG2a and IgG2b subclasses of antibodies against the A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1) virus (Figures 5G and 5H).

It is important to mention here that the QIV (split inactivated vaccine) used here has the identical H1N1 backbone and has also other viral antigen components, which is reflected in their high antibody binding titers against the whole A/Michigan/45/2015 virus. On the contrary, stabilized mHA vaccinations only have HA-specific antibody titers directed primarily toward immuno-subdominant epitopes in the stalk, as reflected in titers against headless HA and cH8/1 recombinant proteins (Figure 5I) and higher inhibition in competition ELISAs against CR9114 monoclonal antibody (Figure 5J).

When tested for the breadth of protection, stabilized mHA vaccination completely protected mice from a lethal dose of homologous and heterologous H1N1 infections with minimal morbidity. Furthermore, stabilized mHAs partially protected mice from phylogenetically distant heterosubtypic H5N1 infection, unlike the QIV group where all animals succumbed to infection. This suggested that the stabilized mHAs could induce a broadly protective immune response to antigenically drifted influenza viruses. Even though our stabilized mHA constructs have major antigenic sites from H5 origin, it did not elicit HI-active antibodies toward H5N1 virus (Figure 6C); rather the heterologous protection was driven by cross-reactive immunity. Compared with current seasonal influenza vaccines, which offer strain-specific immunity,14 the mHA approach offers broader protection12,16,41 to not only against seasonal H1N1 strains but also against pandemic influenza viruses, like the current highly HPAI H5N1 virus circulating in birds and cattle.42

Of note, we only tested the protection of this stabilized mHA vaccine in a prime-boost context. Furthermore, antibody response and breadth of protection can be boosted in a pre-immune animal model, as shown in our three-dose vaccination model (Figure S4), and as demonstrated for other influenza vaccines in previous works.43 Finally, stabilized mHAs could be adjuvanted to enhance a more balanced Th1/Th2 immune response43 or be presented in nanoparticle-like structures to improve their immunogenicity.44 New technologies in vaccine development, for example, Fc-fusion immuno-complexes with stabilized mHAs, could facilitate engagement of high-affinity IgG responses and T cell immunity.45 Future studies testing new advanced formulations will shed light on this matter. We are currently developing stabilized mHA vaccines for group 2 influenza viruses and influenza B viruses. The inclusion of group1, group2, and influenza B stabilized recombinant mHAs in a vaccine cocktail would ideally provide complete protection against all epidemiologically relevant influenza viruses for humans.39

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design and cloning of recombinant stabilized mosaic hemagglutinins

Mosaic H5/1 and mH13/1 sequences were constructed using the ectodomain of the A/Michigan/45/2015 H1 protein, in which the immunodominant head domains were replaced with those from exotic avian influenza viruses: H5 (A/Vietnam/1203/2004-H5N1) or H13 (A/black headed gull/Sweden/1/1999-H13N6), as reported previously.16 Foldon mH5/1 and mH13/1 were generated by fusing mosaic HA sequences with an N-terminal PR8 signal peptide and a C-terminal T4 trimerization domain (foldon), as described earlier.46 Stabilized mHAs (mH5/1 and mH13/1) without the foldon domain were engineered by introducing three types of mutations in the H1 protein (H3 numbering).

First, a sialic-acid-binding knockdown mutation was introduced by replacing tyrosine with phenylalanine at HA1 position 98 in the receptor binding site (Y98F), a modification shown to enhance the quality and durability of vaccine-induced immunity.28 Second, two cysteine residues were introduced into the HA stalk domain (HA1 Cys30 and HA2 Cys47) to form inter-protomer disulfide bonds.26 Finally, prefusion HA conformation was stabilized by replacing conserved lysine and glutamic acid residues in the pH-sensitive regions of the HA2 central helix to aliphatic isoleucines at positions H26W, K51I, and E103I.27 Stabilized mHA sequences were fused with a C-terminal 6xHis tag sequence and synthesized as g-block HiFi synthetic gene fragments (Integrated DNA Technologies).

In-fusion HD cloning plus kit (Takara Bio) was used for cloning mHA gene fragments into pCAGGs vector according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Recombinant constructs were transformed into Stbl2 max efficiency competent cells (Thermo Fisher), and positive clones were screened and expanded. Plasmid DNA was purified using the NucleoBond Xtra Maxi kit (Macharey Nagel), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Expression and purification of recombinant mosaic HA proteins

Stabilized mHAs were expressed using the Expi293F mammalian expression system.47 Briefly, Expi293F cells were cultured in Expi293F Expression medium with GlutaMAX (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and transiently transfected with the ExpiFectamine 293 Transfection Kit (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Filtered culture supernatants were incubated with Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen) overnight at 4°C with shaking at 120 rpm for efficient binding of his-tagged proteins. The next day, bound proteins were purified using gravity flow through 5-mL polypropylene columns (Qiagen). Eluted proteins were concentrated using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (30-kDa cutoff, Millipore Sigma) and buffer-exchanged into phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Protein concentrations were determined using Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad).

Reducing, non-reducing SDS-PAGE and native PAGE analysis

Protein purity and quality were assessed by SDS-PAGE under both non-reducing and reducing conditions.26 For reducing conditions, 10 μg of protein was mixed with 1x NuPAGE sample reducing agent (Fisher Scientific) and 2x Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad) containing 0.05% of β-Mercaptoethanol (β-ME) and then denatured at 95°C for 5 min. For non-reducing conditions, 10 μg of protein was mixed with 2x Laemmli sample buffer without β-ME, without denaturation. Protein samples were loaded onto 4%–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels (Bio-Rad). Native conformation of mHA proteins was examined by native PAGE. Foldon and stabilized mH5/1 proteins (10 μg each) were mixed with 1x native sample buffer (Bio-Rad) and separated on 4%–12% Novex Tris-Glycine precast gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) alongside NativeMark unstained protein standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. LC0725). Gels were stained with SimplyBlue SafeStain (Thermo Fisher scientific), and images were acquired using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Size exclusion chromatography analysis

The trimeric content of foldon and stabilized mHAs was analyzed by SEC using a Superdex 200 increase 10/300GL column (GE healthcare) on an AKTA Pure 25 HPLC system. Recombinant influenza hemagglutinin (HA) from A/California/04/2009 (Sino Biological) was used as a monomeric representative control. 200 μg of purified mHA protein was 0.22-μm filtered and manually loaded and eluted with equilibration buffer in the void volume at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. UV absorbance at 280 nm was recorded in real time in AKTA system, and data were later analyzed with Unicorn v6.3 software.

Differential scanning fluorimetry and dynamic light scattering

Thermal and pH stability of foldon and stabilized mHAs were determined by measuring their Tma (apparent melting temperature) in DSF, as described previously.48 The measurement was performed in a Real-Time PCR (Bio-Rad) with a temperature gradient from 25°C to 95°C (at 1°C increase/minute). For pH stability assays, mHA proteins were pre-incubated in sodium acetate buffer (20 mM NaAc, 75 mM NaCl, pH 5.5) for 30 min and stored at 4°C before addition of Sypro orange dye (Thermo Fisher) and DSF analysis. Raw relative fluorescence (RFU) data (Figure S2) obtained from the instrument were used to calculate the first derivative using the online DSFworld platform (https://gestwickilab.shinyapps.io/dsfworld/). The melting temperatures (Tm50) were determined as the lowest derivative value representing the Tm50 value, as described by Milder et al.27 Average particle size of foldon and stabilized mHAs was determined by DLS using a Litesizer 500 instrument.49 Samples were filtered through 0.22-μm syringe filters to reduce noise and measured in triplicate (11 runs/sample). Data were analyzed to calculate percent intensity over Z-average (particle diameter size in nm-d.nm). Low-pH (5.5) treated samples were also analyzed to assess HA aggregation.

Negative stain transmission electron microscopy analysis

To analyze the morphology of stabilized and foldon mHA proteins, negatively stained protein samples were visualized using a 100 kV HT7800 TEM (Hitachi, Japan). For negative staining, purified mHA proteins of mH5/1 and mH13/1 (both stabilized and foldon) were used. For each sample, 3 μL of protein solution (0.1 mg/mL) was applied to a plasma-cleaned (H2/O2 gas mixture for 30 s Solarus Plasma Cleaner 950 Gatan) continuous carbon film 300 Mesh, copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences). After 30 s, the excess solution was blotted up with a filter paper, followed by washing with 5 μL of deionized (Milli-Q) water. The grid was then rapidly blotted and briefly stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate. This step was repeated once and then stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate before final blotting. After being air-dried for 30 min, the stained grids were then loaded onto the sample holder in the transmission electron microscope operating at 200 Kev. Images were collected with a nominal magnification of 120,000x corresponding to a pixel size of 1.767 Å in a counting mode and a nominal defocus range of −2.0 to −3.0 μm. Then, the collected micrographs were visualized using ImageJ software.

Cells, viruses, and recombinant proteins

Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells, used in the plaque assay for viral titer determination, were maintained in modified Eagle’s medium (MEM, Gibco, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Invitrogen) and 100 U/ml penicillin-100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Invitrogen) in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2.

The influenza A viruses used included the following: A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1),16 A/Victoria/2570/2019 (H1N1),49 A/Netherlands/602/2009 (pH1N1),40 A/swine/Germany/Bak50/2017 (swH1N2),43 A/Vietnam/1203/2004 (H5N1),50 and A/chicken/Hong Kong/G9/1997 (H9N2).51

Recombinant proteins used in ELISA included Headless HA,52 cH8/1,53 HA1 protein (A/Michigan/45/2015; Sino biological, cat. no. 40567-H08H), and SARS-CoV2 spike with trimerization domain.54

Virus propagation in embryonated chicken eggs

Viruses were propagated in 8- to 10-day-old embryonated chicken eggs (Charles River Laboratories or AVS Biotech). Each egg was injected with 100 μL of freshly prepared, serially diluted virus stocks aseptically.55 The presence of virus was measured by hemagglutination assay, and the virus production was titered by 50% egg infectious dose as previously described.56 Clarified virus stocks were aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Purified virus stocks were prepared by purifying the allantoic fluid with ultracentrifugation as described previously.15

Hemagglutination assays

Using PBS pH 7.4 as a diluent, HA titer (HA units) of allantoic fluid samples/recombinant mHA proteins was determined as mentioned previously.12

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

To measure structural conformation of the stabilized mHA proteins, an ELISA was used. For this, a panel of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting different epitopes of hemagglutinin protein was used (Table S1), including head (1A01),57 central stalk (CR9114),52 lateral patch (047–09M 2F03),40 RBS (029–09 3A02),40 anchor (047–09-4F04, 350–132),21 and trimer interface (FluA20).58 Foldon chimeric cH5/1 protein, where the head domain is H5 and stalk is H1, was used as a control in the ELISA experiment. Additionally, FluA20 binding to trimeric HAs was determined using a commercially available monomeric HA protein (A/Michigan/45/2015, Sino biological, cat. no. 40567-V08H1) and purified A/Michigan/45/2015 virus (Figure S3). For checking the binding affinity toward T4 trimerization domain, a commercially available Wac Fibritin rabbit polyclonal antibody (Cusabio, cat. no. CSB-PA319157ZA01EDZ) was used.

Briefly, Immulon 4 HBX 96-well ELISA plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 2 μg/mL of protein or purified viruses (50 μL/well) in 1x KPL coating buffer (SeraCare Life Sciences) and were incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day, plates were washed 3 times with 220 μL of PBST (99.9% PBS, 0.1% Tween 20, v/v), and 220 μL of blocking solution (3% goat serum, 0.5% non-fat dried milk powder, 96.5% PBST) was added to each well and incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature. Each of the mAbs or mice polyclonal sera [(n = 5)/group] was then 3-fold serially diluted in blocking solution, at a starting concentration of either 10 μg/mL or 1:30 dilution respectively. They were added to the plates in duplicate in a final volume of 100 μL/well, and were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Plates were then washed 3 times with 220 μL of PBST, and 50 μL of anti-mouse IgG-horse-radish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibody from sheep (cat. no. NA931) was added at a dilution of 1:3,000 in blocking solution. For the Wac antibody-treated plates, anti-rabbit IgG-HRP-conjugated antibody from donkey (cat. no. NA9340V) was added at a dilution of 1:2,000 in blocking buffer. To determine IgG subclasses, goat polyclonal HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG1 (Abcam, ab97240), IgG2a (Abcam, ab97245), and IgG2b (Abcam, ab97250) were used at 1:3,000 dilution. Plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature followed by washing 4 times with 220 μL of PBST in a plate washer shaking between each wash. Later, O-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride (SigmaFast OPD, Sigma) substrate at 100 μL per well was added. After 10 min of incubation in the dark, 3 M HCl (50 μL/well) was added to stop the reaction. Immediately, the optical density (OD) was measured at 492 nm on a Synergy 4 plate reader (BioTek). Data from each ELISA assay were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software.

Competition ELISA

Immulon 4 HBX 96-well ELISA plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 50 μL/well of stabilized mH5/1 protein at a concentration of 2 μg/mL in 1X coating KPL solution (SeraCare Life Sciences)/PBS pH7.4 and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing the plates 3 times with PBST, 220 μL of blocking buffer (3% goat serum, 0.5% milk powder in PBST) was added to plates, and the plates were blocked for 1.5 h at room temperature. Post incubation, blocking solution was discarded, and serum samples (100 μL) were added (starting dilution of 1:30 in blocking solution) to the coated wells and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Plates were then washed three times with PBST, and biotinylated CR9114 (100 μL/well) at a concentration of 0.1 μg/ml, (2X IC50 value) was added. Plates were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Plates were washed three times with PBST and subsequently incubated with 100 μL/well of streptavidin conjugated to HRP (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1:3,000 dilution in blocking solution. Plates were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, plates were washed four times with PBST and then developed with 100 μL of SigmaFast OPD substrate per well. After 10 min of incubation, the reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of 3 M HCl. OD was measured on a Synergy H1 microplate reader. Antibody competition was defined as the percentage ratio between sample signal and the signal of CR9114 mAb binding diluted in PBS (no mouse serum) and calculated as [(OD sample/OD no serum control) × 100]. The data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software.

Mice immunizations and challenge studies

All animal experiments were approved and performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the Icahn School of Medicine at the Mount Sinai Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). 8- to 10-week-old female BALB/c mice (Jackson Laboratories) were used for all animal experiments. Mice were divided into three groups (n = 10/group) where each of them was vaccinated with either (1) stabilized mHA or (2) foldon mHA. The third group of mice was left unvaccinated and designated as (3) the naive group.

A prime-boost or prime-boost-boost regimen was used with a 4-week interval. For the prime-boost regimen, stabilized and foldon mHA group was primed with mH5/1 and boosted by mH13/1 of the corresponding protein. For the prime-boost-boost vaccination regimen, mice were immunized with H1 recombinant protein (A/California/04/2009) with foldon domain, followed by the two-boost dose of mH5/1 and mH13/1 proteins at a 4-week interval. Each mouse received a total volume of 100 μL of intramuscular injection containing 5 μg of H1/mHA protein combined with AddaVax adjuvant (InvivoGen) at 1:1 ratio, in both legs (50 μL per leg). 4 weeks post final immunization, mice were euthanized with a ketamine-xylazine cocktail, and blood was obtained by cardiac puncture. Sera were prepared by removing red blood cells by centrifugation and were stored at −20°C until further use.

To determine the in vivo protection efficacy of the stabilized mHAs, a direct challenge study was conducted, where mice were immunized in a prime-boost regimen. In this experiment, QIV at a final dose of 1 μg of HA/mouse was adjuvanted with AddaVax (1:1, v:v, InvivoGen). The QIV used here, Fluzone Quadrivalent Influenza Vaccine (2017–2018 formulation, Sanofi Pasteur), contained the following influenza virus strains: A/Michigan/45/2015 X-275 (H1N1), A/Hong Kong/4801/2014 X-263B (H3N2), B/Phuket/3073/2013 (B, Yamagata lineage), and B/Brisbane/60/2008 (B, Victoria lineage). 4 weeks post boost, mice (n = 5/group) were challenged intranasally with 3 mLD50 of viruses in 30 μL of sterile PBS in both nostrils, after sedation with 100 μL of ketamine/xylazine cocktail administered intraperitoneally. The viruses used in direct challenge include matched A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1), mismatched, pandemic A/Netherlands/602/2009 (pH1N1), heterologous A/swine/Germany/Bak50/2017 (swH1N2), and heterosubtypic A/Vietnam/1203/2004 (H5N1). Mice were monitored daily for weight loss and survival for 14 days post challenge, whereby mice that lost >25% of weight were sacrificed, as per IACUC recommendations.

Another set of mice (n = 5/group) received only a sub-lethal dose of A/Michigan/45/2015 (0.1 mLD50) and were kept for 3 days post infection, after which their lungs were harvested for determination of viral titers in lung homogenates.52

Hemagglutination inhibition assay

To test and quantify the presence of vaccine-induced HI-active antibodies in mice sera against A/Michigan/45/2015 (H1N1) virus, we performed a hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assay as previously described.41 Briefly, chicken red blood cells were washed in PBS and resuspended at a concentration of 0.5% hematocrit. Pooled mice sera from each group (n = 10) were treated with three volumes of receptor destroying enzyme (RDE from Vibrio cholerae, Denka Seiken, Japan) at 37°C for 18 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To the RDE-treated samples, three volumes of a 2.5% sodium citrate solution were added. After incubation of the samples at 56°C for 30 min, three volumes of PBS were added to reach a final dilution of 1:10. To perform HI assays, 50 μL of RDE-treated sera was added to the first well (in duplicate/group), which was serially diluted (1:2, in 25 μL of 1x PBS pH7.4) across a 96-well V-bottom plate. Two columns with only 25 μL of PBS were noted as negative control, and two columns with only 25 μL of virus were marked as no-sera control. Virus stocks were then diluted to 8 HA units (4 wells of HA) in PBS; 25 μL/well was then added, and plates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Chicken red blood cells (0.5%) were then added at 50 μL/well, and the plates were incubated further for 30 min at 4°C. HI titers were visually determined, and images were recorded.

Microneutralization assay

To quantify the serum neutralizing antibody titers, an in vitro microneutralization assay was done. Influenza virus (A/Michigan/45/2015) was first titered in MDCK cells by using a 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50).59 MDCK cells were plated in a 96-well plate at 1.8 × 104 cells per well and allowed to grow overnight at 37°C and 5% CO2, until they reached 90% confluence the next day. RDE-treated pooled mice sera, prepared as mentioned above, were 2-fold serially diluted. To one-half of the serum dilutions, virus at a TCID50 dose was added (1:1 v/v ratio). This mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature to allow serum antibodies to bind to virions. Later the serum-virus mixture was added to a monolayer of MDCK cells, after they were washed twice with sterile PBS and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. The remaining half of the serial serum dilutions was supplemented with Minimum Essential Medium (1x MEM, 1:1) with 2 μg/ml TPCK-treated trypsin. After 1 h of incubation, the virus-serum mixture was removed, and MDCK cells were washed again with sterile PBS. The serial serum dilutions (with TPCK) were added to the MDCK cells and incubated for 2 days at 37°C. An HA assay was used as the readout for the microneutralization assay using 0.5% chicken red blood cells. The microneutralization titer was determined as the last well in which HI occurs.

Antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity reporter assay

To assess the Fc-mediator functions of serum antibodies in immune response, ADCC reporter bioassay kit (Promega Life Sciences) was used. Briefly, MDCK cells were seeded on a flat-bottom, white, 96-well plate (Costar Corning) at 2 × 104 per well. After 18 h of incubation at 37°C, the MDCK cells were washed once with warm PBS and then infected with A/Michigan/45/2015 virus at a multiplicity of infection of 5 for single-cycle replication. The infected cells were then incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The next day, the culture medium was removed, and 25 μL of assay buffer (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 4% low-IgG FBS) was added to each well. Pooled mouse sera (n = 10/group) diluted at 1:3 (at a starting dilution of 1:10) in assay buffer were added in a volume of 25 μL/well in duplicates and were incubated with the infected MDCK cells for 30 min at 37°C. Genetically modified Jurkat cells (expressing the mouse FcγRIV with a luciferase reporter gene under transcriptional control of the nuclear factor-activated T cell promoter) were added at 7.5 × 104 cells in 25 μL/well (Promega). Cells were then incubated for another 6 h at 37°C. At the end of the incubation, 75 μL of Bio-Glo luciferase assay reagent (Promega Life Sciences) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 10 min before reading. Luminescence was quantified using a Synergy 4 microplate reader (BioTek). Fold induction was measured as relative light units (RLUs) and calculated as (RLUinduced − RLUbackground)/(RLUuninduced − RLUbackground) and plotted using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Statistics analysis

The ELISA data were analyzed using an in-built nonlinear regression method with setting parameters for Sigmoidal, 4PL, where x is log concentration. The observed EC50 value for each mAbs was tested in duplicate. For serum binding antibody titers, the baseline/cutoff was defined as the average OD value for blank wells plus three standard deviations across an individual plate in a single run, and the mean value for each serum tested in duplicate was calculated for each serum dilution. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the baseline as mentioned above, and where AUC could not be calculated, an arbitrary value of 1.0 was assigned. In the case of serum binding antibody titers, the groups were compared for statistical significance using one-way ANOVA non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction for multiple comparisons. The survival curve in challenge studies was determined using a Kaplan-Meier survival curve, and significance was calculated between test group vs. naive group using Mantel-Cox long rank test. All graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software.
