Emerging Risk of Campylobacter spp. Infection Associated With Anti-BCMA and Anti-GPRC5D Bispecific Antibodies
Authors: Idoia Bilbao, María Ansón, Sara Villar, Iñigo Pineda, Laura Gárriz, Manuel Pina-Sánchez, Luis-Esteban Tamariz-Amador, Paula Rodríguez-Otero, José Ramón Yuste, José L del Pozo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBispecific antibodies directed against BCMA and GPRC5D have shown remarkable efficacy in patients with refractory multiple myeloma; however, concerns regarding infection risk are emerging. In a cohort of 85 treated patients, we observed a notable incidence of Campylobacter infections, including 5 bloodstream and 5 gastrointestinal episodes, affecting 9 individuals. This unexpectedly high burden of infection highlights the vulnerability of this immunocompromised population and underscores the urgent need for studies evaluating targeted preventive and surveillance strategies.

Relapsed/Refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) remains a formidable therapeutic challenge in contemporary clinical practice. Among the most promising innovative approaches are bispecific antibodies (BiAbs) directed against B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and G protein–coupled receptor class C group 5 members D (GPRC5D), both of which are markedly overexpressed on malignant plasma cells. This aberrant expression profile renders these targets particularly amenable to selective immunotherapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing cytotoxicity against neoplastic clones [1, 2].

BiAbs directed against BCMA and GPRC5D are generally well tolerated, with cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and transient cytopenias typically being manageable adverse events [1, 3]. However, BCMA antigens are also physiologically expressed on normal plasma cells, which play a crucial role in humoral immunity; their targeted depletion frequently results in secondary hypogammaglobulinemia. Consequently, infections have emerged as a clinically significant complication associated with these therapies [3–5]. This infectious risk is further amplified by patient-related factors such as advanced age, frailty, comorbidities, and the baseline immunosuppression inherent to multiple myeloma. Although Campylobacter spp. infection is a recognized complication in immunocompromised hosts, including patients with multiple myeloma, robust quantitative data on its incidence in this population are lacking. Providing such context would help place our findings into perspective, as, to the best of our knowledge, no systematic estimates of Campylobacter infection rates in multiple myeloma have been reported to date.

Hypogammaglobulinemia is a well-established predisposing factor for infections caused by Campylobacter spp., particularly in the context of primary immunodeficiencies and secondary immunosuppression, including that induced by anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab [6, 7]. To date, one case has been reported of a patient with recurrent meningitis caused by Campylobacter jejuni in patients receiving BiAbs targeting BCMA and GPRC5D [8].

The present study aims to investigate the potential association between the use of anti-BCMA and anti-GPRC5D BiAbs and the development of Campylobacter spp. infection. Furthermore, it seeks to identify contributing risk factors that may guide the implementation of targeted preventive strategies.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

A retrospective observational study was conducted on a cohort of 85 patients diagnosed with RRMM who received BiAbs therapy at the Department of Hematology, Clinica Universidad de Navarra, between January 2021 and January 2025. Patients were categorized based on the presence or absence of Campylobacter spp. infection. The variables analyzed included: multiple myeloma subtype, age, history of autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), type of BiAbs, white blood cell differential, neutrophil and lymphocyte, serum albumin concentration prior to treatment, serum immunoglobulin (Ig) levels and concomitant intravenous immunoglobulin therapy (IVIG).

Immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM) were measured monthly. For each patient, baseline values and median levels within predefined time windows (months 1–6 and 1–12) were recorded. For patients who developed infection, measurements obtained after the date of infection were excluded. The primary analysis focused on the 1–6 month period.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (v.2023.12.1 + 402). Differences in age, leukocyte counts, albumin, number of prior chemotherapy lines, and serum Ig levels between groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Collective baseline Ig levels were compared with median values at 6 and 12 months; paired differences were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The median paired difference and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated by bootstrap with 10 000 resamples. Categorical variables, including sex, IVIG administration, and autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT), were compared using the chi-square test. The cumulative incidence of infection was estimated with the Aalen–Johansen method. A 2-sided P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample collection and processing protocols remained consistent throughout the study period. Blood cultures were obtained in at least 2 sets, each comprising aerobic and anaerobic bottles (BACT/ALERT FA Plus and FN Plus, bioMérieux), filled with 10 mL of blood per bottle and incubated in the BACT/ALERT 3D system (bioMérieux) for up to 7 days. Fecal specimens bacteriological culture included BD BBLTM Campylobacter agar plates (Becton Dickinson) incubated at 42°C under microaerophilic conditions for ≥48 hours. Alternatively, stool samples were tested using the FilmArray Gastrointestinal Panel (BioFire Diagnostics). In cases of Campylobacter spp., detection culture was performed for species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Colonies grown on solid media were identified by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF) using the VITEK MS system (bioMérieux).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed in accordance with European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines. Susceptibility to clarithromycin, levofloxacin, and tetracycline was assessed by disk diffusion on Mueller–Hinton agar supplemented with 5% horse blood and β-NAD (MH-F; bioMérieux), using commercial disks (Becton Dickinson). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of amoxicillin–clavulanic acid were determined by gradient diffusion (Etest®, bioMérieux) on MH-F agar. Results were interpreted following EUCAST clinical breakpoints (version 13.0), except for amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, which was interpreted according to the breakpoints defined by the Comité de l’antibiogramme de la Société Française de Microbiologie (version 2023, V1.0).

RESULTS

A total of 85 patients with multiple myeloma treated with BiAbs were included in the analysis. The overall characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. Among the cohort, 9 patients (10.5%) developed Campylobacter spp. infection, including 5 cases of bacteremia and 5 gastrointestinal infections. The total follow-up time was 87.9 person-years, corresponding to an incidence rate of 10.5 per 100 person-years (95% CI, 3.6–16.9). The median time to infection was 185 days. The cumulative incidence of infection was 4.9% (95% CI, 1.2–10.8) at 6 months, 6.2% (95% CI, 1.2–11.7) at 12 months, and 8.3% (95% CI, 2.4–15.0) at 18 months. Infected patients were older than non-infected patients (median age 75 vs 65 years; P = .06). No significant differences in sex distribution were observed between groups (P = .31). Before initiation of BiAbs therapy, 38 (44.7%), 26 (30.5%), and 56 (65.9%) patients had IgG, IgA, and IgM levels below 500, 7, and 19 mg/dL, respectively. Figure 1 shows collective Ig levels before and during the first year of BiAbs treatment. Significant reductions were observed in both IgA and IgG when comparing baseline with subsequent windows. For IgA, the median difference was −9.5 (95% CI, −17.5 to −3.5; P < .001) across both periods. For IgG, the median difference was −178.5 (95% CI, −354 to −68; P < .001) between baseline and the 1–6 month median values. In contrast, no meaningful differences could be assessed for IgM, as most patients had undetectable concentrations both before and during treatment.

No significant differences were observed in baseline immunoglobulin levels or in median values at 1–6 and 1–12 months between infected and non-infected patients, although slightly lower levels were noted in those who developed Campylobacter infection. These results are summarized in Table 1.

In contrast, no significant differences were detected, total leukocyte, neutrophil and lymphocyte count, or serum albumin concentration. IVIG therapy was more frequently administered in patients who developed infections (8/9, 88.9%) compared with those who remained infection-free (34/76, 44.7%; P = .012).

The distribution of multiple myeloma subtypes, history of ASCT and number of prior lines of treatment did not differ significantly between cohorts. Likewise, the specific BiAbs administered was not significantly associated with infection risk, although a higher frequency of infections was noted in patients treated with Elranatamab.

Of the 9 Campylobacter infections, 5 were bloodstream and 5 gastrointestinal. Four bacteremias were caused by C. jejuni and 1 to C. coli; among gastrointestinal cases, 5 were due to C. jejuni. Two patients experienced recurrent episodes. Among the 5 patients with bacteremia, 1 had positive stool cultures or multiplex gastrointestinal PCR. However, in patients who presented with gastrointestinal infection, blood cultures were not obtained; therefore, we were unable to assess the presence of concurrent bacteremia in these cases.

Gastrointestinal infections were diagnosed by positive stool culture and on 4 of them we also detected it via the FilmArray Gastrointestinal Panel. In contrast, bloodstream infections were identified by blood cultures, with no corresponding detection in stool specimens or multiplex PCR assays, except in 1. All isolates exhibited resistance to fluoroquinolones. Macrolide susceptibility was retained in all but 1 isolate, the C. coli strain.

DISCUSSION

A high incidence of Campylobacter spp. infections was observed in our cohort of RRMM patients receiving BiAbs. Notably, more than half of these infections were invasive, with 5 of 9 patients developing bacteremia. Most infections occurred within the first 6 months of therapy. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the incidence of Campylobacter infection in a cohort of RRMM patients.

Prior to BiAbs therapy IgM levels were low, while IgG and IgA decreased substantially during bispecific antibody therapy. These findings are aligned with prior evidence suggesting that deficiencies in IgA and IgM may impair mucosal immunity and promote gastrointestinal bacterial translocation [9]. The median time from the start of treatment to the onset of infection was 185 days, the prolonged duration may be attributed to the sustained hypogammaglobulinemia induced by BiAbs therapy since, according to Lancman G et al., IgA and IgM levels remain undetectable throughout the course of therapy and even for 3 or more months after treatment cessation, thereby maintaining an ongoing risk for infections such as the ones described [3].

Of note, gastrointestinal infections were consistently identified by stool samples, whereas most bacteremia was diagnosed solely by blood cultures, with negative results on both stool diagnostic platforms. This observation suggests that systemic dissemination may occur independently of detectable intestinal colonization, particularly in profoundly immunosuppressed hosts. Previous reports have described similar diagnostic discrepancies [6, 7, 10], and our data further reinforce the clinical importance of blood culture surveillance in febrile patients at risk of invasive Campylobacter disease, even in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms.

IVIG use was more frequent among patients who developed infections, likely reflecting that it was preferentially administered to those considered at higher risk. Our small sample limits firm conclusions, although other studies have suggested a protective effect [11].

Moreover, patients who developed Campylobacter infection generally had lower levels of IgA and IgM, and in those who received IVIG, these low levels persisted over time, likely due to the limited effect of IVIG on restoring IgA and IgM levels [3]. This finding suggests that IVIG alone may be insufficient to prevent infection in this high-risk cohort, possibly due to its limited IgA and IgM content or suboptimal mucosal penetration. Although the number of cases is small and no definitive conclusions can be drawn, this observation raises the possibility of exploring primary prophylaxis strategies beyond IVIG to prevent such infections in selected patients.

Moreover, all Campylobacter spp. isolates exhibited resistance to fluoroquinolones, while macrolide susceptibility was retained in all but 1 Campylobacter coli isolate. The overuse of quinolones in agriculture, particularly in poultry, has clearly contributed to the selection of fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter spp. strains, which can be transmitted to humans and complicate treatment [12]. The observed susceptibility profiles suggest that macrolide prophylaxis could be explored as a potential strategy in this patient population, provided that adverse effects such as QT interval prolongation, drug–drug interactions, and the risk of antimicrobial resistance are carefully evaluated and monitored. Nevertheless, given the limited number of cases and the lack of conclusive evidence, routine implementation of this approach cannot be recommended at present.

Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these associations and to determine whether immunoglobulin profiling could serve as a tool to stratify infectious risk in patients undergoing BiAbs therapy. Concurrently, the role of targeted antimicrobial prophylaxis should be prospectively evaluated as part of broader strategies aimed at reducing infectious complications in this vulnerable population.

CONCLUSION

In this cohort, the incidence of Campylobacter spp. infections among patients receiving BiAbs therapy targeting BCMA and GPRC5D was 10.5 per 100 person-years (95% CI, 3.6–16.9). Notably, 5 of 9 affected patients developed bacteremia. These findings highlight the vulnerability of this population and underscore the need for further studies to determine whether preventive strategies could be beneficial in this specific population of immunocompromised hosts.
