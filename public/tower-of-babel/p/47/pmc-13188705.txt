Development and external validation of a machine learning model for predicting chronic critical illness in ICU patients with acute pancreatitis
Authors: Zhikun Xu, Qinhua Yang, Yijing Su, Yichun Jiang, Dongting Peng, Boru Wu, Wenzhong Mo, Zhiming Chen, Jiayang Huang, Zhongji Jiang, Xueyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo develop and validate a machine learning (ML) model to assess the risk of chronic critical illness (CCI) in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with acute pancreatitis (AP).MethodsWe utilised two large, publicly available ICU datasets, MIMIC-IV (v3.1) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database (v2.0), as the development cohort for model construction. A single-centre dataset from China (SZICU) was used for external validation. Three feature selection methods—stepwise regression, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and the Boruta algorithm—were employed. Three ML methods—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—were used for model development. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and Brier score, in both internal and external validation.ResultsThe incidences of CCI were 7.00%, 9.89%, and 20.09% in the training, internal validation, and external validation sets, respectively. Eight predictors of CCI were identified: calcium level, body temperature, vasopressor use, urine output, Glasgow Coma Scale score, albumin level, haemoglobin level, and a history of cerebrovascular disease. In the internal validation set, the RF model achieved an AUROC of 0.85 (0.77–0.91), an AUPRC of 0.53 (0.39–0.69), and a Brier score of 0.07 (0.05–0.09). In the external validation set, the RF model achieved an AUROC of 0.73 (0.64–0.81), an AUPRC of 0.42 (0.30–0.56), and a Brier score of 0.16 (0.12–0.20). Feature importance analysis revealed that calcium level, body temperature, vasopressor use, and urine output were the most influential predictors of CCI.ConclusionsWe developed and validated an ML model using eight clinical variables to predict CCI risk in ICU patients with AP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03480-7.

Background

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common gastrointestinal disease that affects approximately 3 million patients worldwide annually [1]. Its global incidence is rising, making it one of the most common emergencies requiring hospitalisation [2]. While most patients present with mild interstitial oedema and recover within a few days of receiving supportive treatment, more than 10% develop severe AP [3]. This severe form is often complicated by pancreatic necrosis, infection, or organ failure, necessitating admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). These patients typically experience prolonged hospital stays, high complication rates, and increased mortality, with reported mortality rates as high as 30% [4]. Therefore, precise prognostic tools are needed to facilitate patient triage and personalised medicine. Given the complex and variable clinical course of ICU patients with AP, there is an urgent need for a precise prognostic model to inform clinical decision-making and patient counselling.

Despite the existence of several conventional AP prognostic models (e.g., the Ranson, Glasgow–Limrie, Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis, and Harmless Acute Pancreatitis Score), these models exhibit notable limitations in predictive performance and clinical utility [5–8]. Specifically, they often fail to account for the complex, nonlinear interactions inherent in AP progression. In recent years, machine learning (ML) has demonstrated considerable advantages in analysing complex, high-dimensional, and nonlinear data relationships and has become a powerful tool for prognostic modelling. Recent studies have demonstrated that artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML)-based models outperform traditional scoring systems such as APACHE and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) in predicting mortality among ICU patients [9, 10]. ML methods have been widely applied in prognostic prediction research for AP. Studies have developed ML models to predict ICU admission, in-hospital mortality, and mortality risk associated with specific complications such as acute kidney injury (AKI) [11–14]. These studies generally report that ML models (e.g., extreme gradient boosting [XGBoost] and random forest [RF]) perform better than traditional scoring systems. However, despite numerous studies developing ML models for AP, none have been widely adopted in clinical practice [15, 16]. This gap may stem from insufficient external validation, limited generalisability, or a narrow focus on outcomes such as severity or mortality rather than other clinically meaningful endpoints. Notably, chronic critical illness (CCI)—a prolonged and intricate clinical state associated with high complication rates, considerable healthcare resource use, and poor outcomes—has been understudied in AP research [17]. Despite its clinical significance, CCI remains understudied, and predictive models for this outcome are scarce.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and externally validate an ML-based model to assess CCI risk in ICU patients with AP. This work addresses several limitations of prior research in three key dimensions. First, it leverages multicentre data for model development and comprehensively evaluates generalisability through both internal and external validation. Second, it systematically compares a range of ML algorithms, including classical statistical methods (e.g., logistic regression [LR]) and high-performance ensemble learning techniques (e.g., RF and XGBoost), to examine the advantages and limitations of each approach. Third, performing feature importance analysis enhances model interpretability, thereby facilitating clinical comprehension and practical implementation. By addressing this unmet clinical need, we aim to provide a more comprehensive tool for clinical decision support and patient counselling in managing severe AP.

Methods

Data sources and study population

This study utilised two large, publicly available ICU datasets (the MIMIC-IV dataset (v3.1) and the eICU Collaborative Research Database [v2.0]), which were merged to form the development cohort for model construction. Data from a tertiary hospital in China (SZICU) were used as the external validation cohort. The MIMIC-IV dataset (2008–2022) included 94,458 ICU admissions from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, USA [18]. The eICU dataset contained 200,859 ICU admissions from 208 hospitals across the USA during 2014–2015, providing a broad and diverse sample [19]. The SZICU dataset encompassed 6,245 ICU admissions from Shenzhen People’s Hospital between 2017 and 2023. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (LL-KY-2025200-02). As the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases are publicly available and de-identified, additional ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board was not required.

The study population consisted of ICU patients diagnosed with AP. In the MIMIC-IV database, AP cases were identified using the first three International Classification of Diseases diagnosis codes because ICU admission diagnoses were unavailable. Conversely, AP cases in the eICU and SZICU databases were identified based on ICU admission diagnoses. The exclusion criteria were: 1) patients with multiple ICU admissions (only the first admission was included), 2) patients aged under 18 years, and 3) patients with an ICU stay of less than 24 h. A detailed patient inclusion flowchart for each dataset is shown in Fig. 1. No formal sample size estimation was performed; all eligible patients were included in model development and validation. This study adhered to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD)–AI guideline [20].Fig. 1Flowchart of the study

Flowchart of the study

Outcome and candidate predictors

The primary outcome was CCI, defined as an ICU length of stay ≥ 14 days accompanied by persistent organ dysfunction (a cerebrovascular SOFA score ≥ 1 or a SOFA score ≥ 2 in any other organ system) [21]. To determine the CCI outcome, we extracted data on the patient’s ICU length of stay and the components of the SOFA score on day 14 after ICU admission. Candidate predictors were selected based on a comprehensive literature review, data accessibility, and clinical consensus. These included demographic data, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory parameters, treatment measures, urine output, and the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score on the first day of ICU admission. The full list of candidate predictors was as follows: age, sex, cerebrovascular disease, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, cancer, minimum GCS score, urine output, minimum mean blood pressure, minimum oxygen saturation, maximum heart rate, maximum respiratory rate, maximum body temperature, maximum glucose, minimum albumin, maximum alkaline phosphatase, maximum alanine aminotransferase, maximum activated partial thromboplastin time, maximum aspartate aminotransferase, maximum blood urea nitrogen, minimum calcium, maximum creatinine, minimum haematocrit, minimum haemoglobin, minimum platelets, maximum red cell distribution width, maximum total bilirubin, maximum white blood cell count, mechanical ventilation use, vasopressor use, and renal replacement therapy (RRT) use. All vital signs, laboratory parameters, and treatment measures were collected within 24 h of ICU admission. Additionally, we extracted the SOFA score on day 1 and data on in-hospital mortality.

Data preprocessing and missing data imputation

For continuous variables such as laboratory results and vital signs, outliers were defined as values below the 1st percentile or above the 99th percentile of the observed distribution. These outliers were subsequently replaced with values corresponding to the 1st and 99th percentiles, respectively. All continuous variables were standardised by conversion to z-scores before incorporation into the model. Missing data were imputed using the missForest R package (v1.5) [22].

Model development and evaluation

Three feature selection methods were employed: stepwise regression, the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and the Boruta algorithm [23–25]. Final features were selected using at least two of these methods. Three ML models were developed: LR, RF, and XGBoost. These models were selected due to their extensive application in the medical field in previous studies, where they have demonstrated excellent performance and strong generalisation capabilities [26–28]. We did not employ deep learning models because they have limitations when processing tabular data, including the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning and relatively weaker generalisation performance [29]. Furthermore, in certain clinical prediction tasks, well-tuned traditional methods may achieve or even surpass the performance of deep learning approaches [30]. Hyperparameters were tuned via Bayesian optimisation to maximise the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) during five-fold cross-validation. The optimisation was implemented using Hyperopt v0.2.7 in Python, specifically employing the tree-structured Parzen estimator algorithm as the acquisition function to efficiently model and explore the hyperparameter space. The optimisation process was configured to run for a maximum of 1000 evaluations per model. The detailed hyperparameter search space is shown in Additional File 1. To mitigate potential bias from class imbalance due to the low incidence of CCI in the training set, we employed algorithm-specific class-weighting strategies during model training: for the LR model, the hyperparameter class_weight was set to ‘balanced’; for the RF model, class_weight was set to ‘balanced_subsample’; and for the XGBoost model, the scale_pos_weight parameter was calculated and applied accordingly. Youden’s index was used to determine the optimal probability threshold. Model performance was evaluated on both the internal and external validation sets. Receiver operating characteristic, precision–recall, and calibration curves were generated. Performance metrics included AUROC, area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the Brier score. Given the low incidence of CCI events in this study, a significant class imbalance was present. In such scenarios, both the AUROC and AUPRC are crucial for comprehensively assessing model performance, as they provide complementary clinical insights. Bootstrapping with 1000 iterations was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals. Since there is currently no dedicated scoring system for predicting CCI in ICU patients with AP, we selected the widely adopted and readily available SOFA score as the primary benchmark. This ensured consistent, feasible performance evaluation across all validation datasets. Statistical comparisons of AUROC between models were performed using DeLong’s test. Moreover, feature importance was assessed: for the RF model, it was calculated using the built-in feature importance function; for the LR model, it was calculated using the absolute values of the regression coefficients. The code for this study, including the procedures for feature selection, model training, and validation, is available on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.18724519) for model validation and application.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised using median and interquartile range (IQR), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data processing, feature selection, and statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.1.2), while ML model construction and evaluation were performed in Python (v3.11.9).

Feature selection

In the training set, eight factors were consistently selected by at least two of the three feature selection methods—stepwise regression, LASSO, and Boruta—and were significantly associated with CCI (Fig. 2). These features were calcium level, body temperature, vasopressor use, urine output, GCS score, albumin level, haemoglobin level, and a history of cerebrovascular disease (Additional File 5).Fig. 2The feature selection methods employed in this study

The feature selection methods employed in this study

Development and performance of the machine learning model

Three machine learning models (LR, RF, and XGBoost) were trained on the training set using the eight selected features to predict CCI. For each model, five-fold cross-validation was performed on the training set to determine the optimal hyperparameters (Additional File 1).

In the internal validation set, the RF model outperformed XGBoost and the SOFA score, achieving an AUROC of 0.85 (0.77–0.91), an AUPRC of 0.53 (0.39–0.69), and a Brier score of 0.07 (0.05–0.09). Despite no statistically significant difference in AUROC between the RF and LR models, the RF model exhibited a higher AUPRC and a lower Brier score than the LR model (Table 2, Figs. 3 and 4). Given the low incidence of CCI in this study, there is a significant class imbalance. In this context, the AUPRC provides a more clinically relevant performance assessment than the AUROC. The AUROC measures the model’s overall ability to distinguish between patients, while the AUPRC directly focuses on the model’s performance in identifying the positive class (i.e., CCI patients), balancing both precision (the proportion of patients predicted as high-risk who truly develop CCI) and recall (the proportion of all true CCI patients successfully identified by the model). Based on overall performance, the RF model was therefore selected for further evaluation. The optimal probability threshold for the RF model was 0.057, as determined by the Youden index. Subsequent assessment in the external validation set revealed that the RF model exhibited satisfactory performance, with an AUROC of 0.73 (0.64–0.81), an AUPRC of 0.42 (0.30–0.56), and a Brier score of 0.16 (0.12–0.20) (Table 2, Figs. 3 and 4). Discussion

This study utilised data collected within 24 h of ICU admission in patients with AP to develop and externally validate an ML model to predict CCI risk. In the internal validation, although LR and RF achieved identical AUROC values, the RF model demonstrated a significantly higher AUPRC. This indicates that, although the overall discriminatory ability was comparable, the RF model achieved a superior balance between precision and recall in identifying CCI events—capturing more true positives and generating fewer false positives. In external validation, the RF model maintained a relative advantage in AUPRC, with a smaller decline than other models, suggesting that the patterns it learned may be more generalisable. Consequently, we selected the RF model as the final model, based not only on its favourable AUROC but, more critically, on its excellent AUPRC, which has greater practical significance for clinical decision-making. The RF model exhibited robust discrimination and calibration in the internal validation cohort and demonstrated acceptable discrimination and calibration in the independent external cohort. Our analysis identified calcium level, body temperature, vasopressor use, and urine output as key predictors of CCI in this population.

Notably, our model exhibited a decline in predictive performance in the independent external validation cohort (SZICU), with the AUROC decreasing from 0.85 to 0.73. This expected phenomenon is not uncommon in validations across different healthcare systems and regions. We posit that the performance decline likely stems from heterogeneity between the development cohorts (MIMIC-IV/eICU) and the external validation cohort (SZICU) across several factors. First, there was a marked difference in the incidence of CCI: the external cohort had an incidence of approximately 20%, significantly higher than the <10% observed in the development cohorts. Correspondingly, the SZICU cohort had a higher baseline SOFA score (5 vs. 4) and a markedly higher rate of RRT utilisation (43% vs. 4%). These findings reflect differences in disease severity, baseline risk, and ICU admission criteria between populations. For instance, the higher incidence of CCI may indicate that the SZICU cohort included more patients with complex conditions prone to progressing to CCI. Second, systematic differences likely exist between healthcare system structures, ICU admission criteria, and management protocols for AP in China and the United States. Such differences can affect the distribution of key predictor variables in the model and the strength of their association with outcomes. Third, the inherent heterogeneity of retrospective data poses challenges for model transfer. The databases cover different time periods, with temporal gaps between the development databases (MIMIC-IV: 2008–2022; eICU: 2014–2015) and the validation database (SZICU: 2017–2023), during which clinical guidelines for managing AP may have changed. Furthermore, variations may exist in data collection procedures, coding practices (e.g., for recording comorbidities), and variable completeness (e.g., frequency of laboratory tests) across institutions and ICUs.

Compared with previous studies, our study identified a more clinically significant prognostic indicator: CCI, defined as an ICU stay of ≥14 days with persistent organ dysfunction [21]. This indicator not only reflects the severity of the patient’s condition but also provides clinicians with an early window for intervention, potentially improving patient outcomes. ML has demonstrated potential in predicting various outcomes in AP, yet a critical gap remains: no ML model has been specifically designed to predict CCI in patients with AP admitted to the ICU [15]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and externally validate an ML model for this purpose. The prediction model developed in this study holds significant clinical implications. It enables early identification of high-risk patients, guides the rational allocation of intensive care resources and translates key predictive factors—such as serum calcium levels, body temperature, and fluid management—into actionable clinical interventions. Furthermore, the model can serve as an effective tool for clinician–patient communication, helping patients and their families understand potential disease progression and long-term treatment trajectories. This helps establish realistic expectations and promotes shared decision-making. Integrating the model into clinical workflows could further amplify its value. It could assist in screening high-risk patients, prompting clinical teams to implement closer monitoring and proactive interventions, thereby optimising the use of limited resources. Clinicians could also use the objective prediction results to initiate earlier discussions with families regarding treatment plans, rehabilitation prospects, or palliative care options, fostering greater consensus in the clinician–patient relationship.

Following feature selection, eight variables were identified as key predictors of CCI: calcium level, body temperature, vasopressor use, urine output, GCS score, albumin level, haemoglobin level, and a history of cerebrovascular disease [15, 31–33]. These factors align with previously identified prognostic indicators in AP; however, our study is the first to systematically integrate them for predicting CCI. Among them, calcium level, body temperature, vasopressor use, and urine output emerged as the four most important predictors, all closely related to AP pathophysiology. Serum calcium level is a well-established prognostic marker [5, 6, 34]. The elevated body temperature observed in patients with CCI may reflect severe systemic inflammatory response syndrome in early-stage AP or may be exacerbated by secondary infections or infected necrosis [35, 36]. The strong association with vasopressor use is consistent with previous findings and reflects intravascular volume depletion due to reduced intake (anorexia, vomiting) and increased vascular permeability from inflammatory mediators, leading to third-space fluid accumulation, hypoperfusion, organ ischaemia, and dysfunction [37–39]. In the early stages, aggressive and precise fluid management is critical for mitigating these effects. Urine output is a sensitive indicator of renal function and is often impaired in severe AP [11]. Inflammatory cascades and hypoperfusion can trigger AKI, a known predictor of mortality and a contributor to persistent organ dysfunction [40].

This study has several strengths. First, external validation of the RF model using data from different institutions enhances its generalisability. Second, identifying the most relevant predictors through multiple feature selection techniques reinforces the biological plausibility of the model. Third, feature importance analysis provides insight into the key drivers of CCI risk and potential treatment strategies. However, this study has certain limitations. First, both the development and validation cohorts were derived from retrospective datasets. Future prospective multicentre studies are needed to validate the model’s performance and generalisability across different healthcare settings. Second, the absence of key inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and interleukin-6) may limit the model’s predictive ability, as these biomarkers are crucial for comprehensively assessing inflammation and infection in AP [41–43]. Incorporating these biomarkers in future iterations could enhance the model’s predictive accuracy. Finally, relying solely on data collected within the first 24 h of admission may not adequately capture the evolving nature of the patient’s condition. Future studies should consider incorporating time-series data [44].

Conclusions

The RF model developed in this study, based on routinely available ICU data collected within the first 24 h, demonstrated good performance in predicting CCI risk in patients with AP. This provides a novel decision-support tool for clinical practice, enabling early and accurate identification of high-risk patients and the implementation of timely, proactive interventions. This could optimise the allocation of intensive care resources and, ultimately, significantly improve the clinical prognosis of ICU patients with AP.
