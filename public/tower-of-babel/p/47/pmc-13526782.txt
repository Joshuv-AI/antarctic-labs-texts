High-performance absorption and regeneration of water vapor by choline chloride-based hydrophilic deep eutectic solvents
Authors: Zeynab Rezaeiyan, Peyman Keshavarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Moisture removal is essential in natural gas processing to prevent hydrate formation, corrosion, and operational issues in pipelines. In this work, a series of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) were synthesized by combining choline chloride with various hydrogen-bond donors: ethylene glycol (EG), triethylene glycol (TEG), tetraethylene glycol (TTEG), glycerol, and urea. Using a bubble-column system, their water vapor absorption capacities were evaluated and compared to TEG under controlled laboratory conditions. All synthesized DESs reduced the relative humidity (RH) of the outlet gas stream. At a gas flow rate of 100 mL·min⁻¹, the outlet RH was reduced more than 90% to less than 3%, confirming their effective dehydration performance. Among the systems studied, the 1:2 molar ratio choline chloride–ethylene glycol DES (Ethaline) exhibited the highest water vapor absorption capacity. This superior performance was attributed to its low viscosity and high hydrophilicity. For the highly viscous choline chloride–urea DES, the addition of approximately 2 wt% water was found to substantially reduce its viscosity while maintaining effective moisture absorption. Thermal regeneration effectively removed absorbed water, with approximately 80% of the absorbed water desorbed after 2 h of heating. Furthermore, repeated absorption–regeneration cycles showed negligible loss in absorption capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57957-6.

Introduction

Natural gas wells contain contaminants and impurities including hydrogen sulfide (H2S), water vapor and carbon dioxide (CO2). Prior to pipeline transmission, natural gas must be processed to remove such impurities in order to comply with standard specifications1,2. Gas purification involves several processes, such as heavy hydrocarbon removal, acid gas treatment, and dehydration1.

Near-saturated Natural Gas (NG) entering gas processing plants often contains a considerable amount of water vapor. Therefore, to reduce water content in natural gas, it is important to remove water vapor or dehydrate it3. There are several issues associated with natural gas containing water vapor, such as the formation of hydrates, corrosion, especially when CO2 and H2S are present, and environmental risks during transportation. Consequently, the removal of water vapor via dehydration is an essential processing step prior to transmission4.

In industry, common methods of dehydrating are liquid desiccants (e.g., TEG), cooling (methanol/propanol), and solid desiccants (molecular sieves, alumina, silica gel)5,6. The predominant absorption method relies on, the use of glycols as a liquid absorbent, with TEG currently considered the standard method for dehydrating natural gas in industrial processes7. However, TEG has certain limitations, including high volatility, high viscosity and flammability8; this has created a significant challenge for the chemical industry the need to switch to more efficient, non-polluting, green and environmentally-friendly solvents. In this context, the application of green solvents, such as DESs and ionic liquids (ILs), has attracted increasing attention in recent years9,10. Although ILs are frequently referred to as substitutes for volatile organic solvents, their large-scale use is limited by their poor biocompatibility, bio sustainability, and biodegradability, along with the widespread use of organic solvents in their synthesis. Consequently, the significance of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) has recently been highlighted11. DES refers to a mix of hydrogen bond donor (HBD) and hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) whit a melting point below 100 °C and physicochemical properties similar to those of ionic liquids (ILs)12.

Recent years have witnessed a greater interest in deep eutectic solvents (DESs) as a substitute for conventional organic solvents used in industry due to their outstanding properties, such as biodegradability, low or non-existent toxicity, low volatility (close to zero), and low preparation costs. DESs are inexpensive and environmentally-friendly ionic solvents having a variety of chemical and industrial applications13. These include CO2 capture, extraction solvents in industry, catalysis, synthesis and polymerization, among others13–18.

Choosing suitable HBA: HBD pairs considering various molecules and their mole ratios enabled DESs to be tailored for specific tasks whose characteristics can be chosen and adjusted for every application. For example, adding OH-functional groups to HBD would decrease the values of viscosity in the DES. Moreover, the carboxylic group could influence the thermal characteristics and hydrophilicity of the resulting DES. Nonetheless, the amine group via HBA, can improve the hygroscopic nature of DES19.

Most choline chloride-based DESs exhibit high hydrophilic behavior due to the presence of hydrophilic groups within the molecule20–23. Aside from being hygroscopic, these DESs tend to absorb moisture from the environment when exposed for prolonged periods of time. For example, Choline chloride-urea DES; is capable of absorbing atmospheric moisture up to 40 wt%24. Water contamination can have significant effects on their properties; impact their chemical structure, reduce kinematic viscosity, increases their electrical conductivity, and significantly decrease their electrochemical window25. The high volatility of glycols poses a major drawback in natural gas dehydration; however, when glycols are combined with ChCl to form DESs, their volatility decreases26.

Several studies have investigated the water absorption potential of DESs19,20,27,28. In an early study, Yu Chen et al. demonstrated that DESs were capable of absorbing moisture from the atmosphere under static conditions20. In subsequent studies, such as those conducted by Temidayo et al. and Raymund Brusas et al., their moisture-absorbing properties for use in liquid drying systems and plating baths were confirmed19,27. Moreover, theoretical studies using models such as COSMO-RS predicted that DESs can be effective for natural gas dehydration, and that they can be reused28. In our previous work, we showed that choline chloride–urea DESs outperformed conventional absorbents such as TEG and lithium chloride in a bubble column29.

Although choline chloride-based deep eutectic solvents (DESs) have been previously investigated for water vapor absorption, most existing studies have been conducted under static or equilibrium conditions, or have relied on the theoretical or thermodynamic modeling approaches. In contrast, experimental evaluations of continuous gas–liquid absorption systems, especially those involving direct side-by-side comparisons of multiple DESs under identical operating conditions, remain scarce in the literature.

In this work, a systematic comparison of five choline chloride (ChCl)-based DESs was presented using a continuous absorption setup enabling a direct performance evaluation against TEG under identical operating conditions. This experimental framework was designed to elucidate the effects of key parameters—including molecular structure, hydrophilic functional groups, viscosity, and initial water content—in a consistent manner. Furthermore, the thermal regeneration of the highest-performing DESs was investigated to gain a better understanding of its potential reusability under cyclic operation.

Accordingly, we discuss the rationale behind the design and the selection of the investigated choline chloride-based DES systems in the following section.

Background and design rationale for choline chloride–based deep eutectic solvents

Based on the established physicochemical principles and prior experimental evidence, the choline chloride–based DESs for water vapor absorption were selected. Choline chloride is a widely-used hydrogen bond acceptor with negligible vapor pressure and strong affinity toward polar molecules due to the presence of the chloride anion20–23. Previous studies have shown that choline chloride–based DES systems form extensive hydrogen-bond networks with suitable donors, resulting in significant moisture affinity20,27. These characteristics make them logical candidates for vapor-phase dehydration applications.

In this study, to systematically compare different hydrogen bond donors within the same ionic framework, choline chloride was intentionally kept constant as the hydrogen bond acceptor. This strategy enables direct assessment of donor chemistry on moisture absorption and mass-transfer-related properties without introducing additional structural variables.

The molar ratios employed in this study were selected based on compositions formed stable eutectic liquids under ambient conditions19,24,30–32, ensuring phase stability and consistent comparison among systems.

The selected hydrogen bond donors represented systems with substantially different viscosities. The reported viscosities for these systems ranged from approximately 50 mPa·s to above 1000 mPa·s, depending on the donor structure30,31,33. This variation was considered intentionally due to the key role of viscosity in liquid-phase mass transfer. The investigation of systems with a wide range of viscosities, it is possible to analyze the relation of structure-viscosity-performance and potential considerations of scale-up.

The present work focuses on water vapor removal under controlled laboratory conditions and near-ambient pressure. Experimental investigation of interactions with hydrocarbon components was beyond this study scope. The literature indicates that methane solubility in choline chloride–based DES systems is pressure-dependent and remains very limited at near-ambient pressures34.

Regarding regeneration and long-term performance, previous studies have demonstrated that absorbed water can be removed from DES systems through mild heating, allowing recovery of functional properties19,27. In this work, cyclic regeneration was experimentally evaluated for the system showed the most favorable balance between absorption capacity and viscosity, and its short-term stability was assessed under laboratory conditions. However, extended multi-cycle durability and comprehensive life-cycle assessment—particularly for DESs incorporating longer-chain donors—remains understudied, requiring further systematic investigation.

Experimental section

Materials and characterizations

The chemicals used for DES synthesis were obtained from commercial suppliers with a minimum purity of 99.0 wt% and were used without further purification. These materials comprised choline chloride (ChCl), ethylene glycol (EG), triethylene glycol (TEG), tetraethylene glycol (TTEG), and glycerol, sourced from Merck KGaA (Germany), and urea, sourced from Samchun Chemical Co., Ltd. (South Korea). The molecular structures of these constituents are provided in Table 1. ChCl, EG, TEG, TTEG, and glycerol were mixed in the molar ratios listed in Table 2 to prepare the various binary DESs.

TEG employed in this study was a laboratory-grade product (Merck, ≥ 99 wt%). Although this purity was comparable to that of industrial lean TEG, the solvent did not replicate the operational environment of industrial dehydration units, which typically included continuous regeneration. Consequently, TEG was used solely as a benchmark absorbent under identical laboratory conditions to enable controlled comparison with the synthesized DESs.

To determine whether DESs were suitable for water-vapor absorption, several factors were examined, including moisture absorption, thermophysical, and physiochemical properties. The Fourier Transform Spectroscopy (FTIR) analyzer (Bruker Tensor II) was used investigate the effect of water addition on the structure of the absorbent and to identify the functional group. Over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1, the attenuated total reflection ATR-FTIR method was utilized. Additionally, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Avance DPX400 MHz Bruker) was used to detect the presence of water in the hydrogen bonding.

The density of each DES sample was measured at 30 °C using a DA-645 density meter (Kyoto Electronics Manufacturing Co., Japan). The kinematic viscosity was determined at 25 °C using a rotational viscometer (MCR-302, Anton Paar, Austria). Water content (wt%) was determined using a volumetric Karl Fischer titrator (Metrohm 758KFD Titrinio, Switzerland). The sample solution was homogenized manually before titrating. Three drops of sample or 0.08–0.1 g were added to a dry methanol solvent (Merck) and titrated with a suitable titrant (Merck).

The experimental uncertainties were estimated as ± 1.5% RH for relative humidity (KIMO TH300 hygrometer), ± 0.0001 g·cm⁻³ for density (DA-645 m), ± 1% of reading for viscosity (Anton Paar MCR-302 viscometer), ± 0.5 °C for temperature, and ± 3% for gas flow rate.

Beyond the evaluation of DES’ inherent functional groups, experimental studies were conducted on the absorption of humidity from gas flow, dehumidification and regeneration capabilities of the solutions, as well as comparisons with the conventional absorption capabilities of TEG.

Preparation of DESs

Prior to synthesis, ChCl was dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for approximately 6 h. Mixtures with the specified molar ratios were transferred into a beaker, heated to 70 °C, and stirred until a homogeneous and clear liquid was formed. To minimize moisture absorption, the beaker was sealed with aluminum foil. Following synthesis, the DESs were further dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for approximately 12 h before characterization. All DESs were prepared using the same procedure.

The water absorption experiment

The water vapor absorption performance of the DESs was evaluated using a continuous gas–liquid setup, depicted schematically in Fig. 1. All experiments were performed under near-ambient conditions at a controlled temperature of 25–30 °C. Compressed ambient air (HAILEA ACO-328 compressor) was first regulated to the desired dry-gas flow rate (100, 400, or 800 mL·min⁻¹) using a flow meter. This air stream was then fed into a glass bubbling humidifier containing deionized water. By bubbling upward through the water column, the gas became saturated with water vapor. The relative humidity at the humidifier outlet was continuously monitored, and the system was allowed to equilibrate until a stable baseline of approximately 95% RH was achieved prior to each absorption test.

The humidified air stream exiting the humidifier then passed through a second flow meter to confirm the wet-gas flow rate prior entering the absorption system. Subsequently, the gas was directed to the first inlet line of a KIMO TH300 hygrometer for the measurement of the inlet relative humidity (RH_in).

Following the RH_in measurement, the first inlet line was isolated from the gas stream, and the hygrometer’s measurement chamber was evacuated with a vacuum pump to purge any residual moisture. This procedure eliminated potential memory effects from the instrument, ensuring that the subsequent outlet humidity (RH_out) measurement was not influenced by traces of water vapor from the previous reading.

The humid air stream was then introduced at the bottom of a jacketed glass bubble column containing 20 mL of the DES under test. The column was equipped with a porous plate sparger to disperse the gas into fine bubbles upon entry into the liquid phase. This configuration provided direct, intimate gas–liquid contact, facilitating efficient water vapor absorption.

The dehydrated gas stream exiting the top of the bubble column was then directed to the second inlet line of the same KIMO TH300 hygrometer for determination of the outlet relative humidity (RH_out). The use of a single instrument equipped with two independent, selectable inlet lines allowed for separate measurements of RH_in and RH_out, thereby eliminating inter-instrument calibration errors and ensuring direct comparability of the humidity readings.

Each absorption experiment was conducted for 360 min at a constant gas flow rate. Both the bubbling humidifier and the bubble absorption column were custom-designed and fabricated in our laboratory. Their key their geometrical characteristics are summarized in Table 3.

Result and discussion

DES characterization and physicochemical properties (Viscosity, Density, FTIR, NMR)

One of the most important properties of liquids is their viscosity, as it is directly related to the mass transfer rate and has a considerable impact on their application in industrial processes. The viscosity of DES is affected by a number of factors, including the type and structure of the components, molar ratios, temperature, water, and impurities, with temperature proving to be the most important33.

An illustration of the viscosity of five DESs as a function of temperature is shown in Fig. 2. Overall, the results demonstrated that temperature significantly affected the viscosity of DESs, and as temperature increased, the viscosity decreased. To explain why viscosity decreased with increasing temperature in a fluid, the molecular level must be considered: a substance’s temperature is directly proportional to the average speed of its molecules. As the temperature increases, the amount of free space among molecules increases. Therefore, the fluid density decreases, and as a result of the increased mutual distances among molecules, intermolecular interactions become weaker33.

As shown in Fig. 2, the Reline exhibited the highest viscosity among all the DESs, whereas the Ethaline showed the lowest. This correlation was attributed to the variations in the number of hydrogen bonds among the liquids under study. Reline exhibits this behavior due to its urea content forming four hydrogen bonds in comparison to glycerol and EG forming three and two bonds, respectively. Thus, Reline’s denser hydrogen-bond network, as reported in previous molecular-level analyses, restricts molecular mobility and is the primary contributor to its significantly higher viscosity relative to the other DESs studied35.

The relatively low viscosity of Ethaline (50.014 mPa·s), which is comparable to that of TEG (47.423 mPa·s), indicates that mass-transfer limitations typically associated with highly viscous DESs may be less pronounced in this system. This aspect is relevant when considering potential scale-up and practical process implementation.

Water vapor absorption by DESs at different gas flow rates

Figure 4 shows, the relative humidity of inlet and outlet air for five different DESs at three flow rates (100, 400, and 800 mL per minute). Figure 4a shows that for the five selected DESs, the relative humidity at the initial times has decreased from more than 90% to less than 3% as a result of the disparity between the humidity of the inlet and the outlet air flows. These results indicate that DES solvents effectively absorbed water vapor as an initial result. It is also evident from this figure that the output relative humidity increased with time for all three flow rates, which suggests that water vapor absorption diminished while the water vapor concentration in the DESs increased.

Figure 5 presents, the recovery factor over time at three gas flow rates of five selected DESs. Initially, times, the recovery factor was greater than 90%, but decreased with increasing liquid water vapor concentration.

As shown in Figs. 4 and 5, increasing the inlet gas flow rate causes the relative humidity of the outlet air to increase and the recovery factor to decrease. These findings suggest that the variation in gas flow rate markedly influenced the water vapor absorption for these reasons:

Figure 4 shows that ChCl: EG performed best at all three flow rates, and the results also indicate that the order in which the five selected DESs absorbed water can be arranged as follows:

ChCl: EG > ChCl: glycerol > ChCl: urea > ChCl: TEG > ChCl: TTEG.

Furthermore, water absorption measurements of ChCl–EG at different molar ratios (1:2, 1:1, and 1:3) confirmed that the 1:2 ratio provides the best performance (see Supplementary Fig. S6 and details information).

A number of factors influenced water absorption by DESs, such as the characteristics of the components (HBD and HBA), the molar proportions among the components, the hydrogen bonding potential of the DES, and its viscosity. According to Chen et al.20, water absorption was influenced by a number of factors, including methyl groups (e.g. ChCl: methylurea compared to ChCl: Urea), hydroxyl groups, and the length of the chain.

An investigation was conducted into why ChCl: EG absorbed so much water vapor.1HNMR spectroscopy was employed to identify the functional groups in ChCl-EG that were responsible for the absorption of moisture. A series of measurements were conducted before and after moisture absorption for six hours. Our 1HNMR spectra, presented in Fig. 3; Table 5, showed characteristic chemical shifts that are consistent with the established structure of the Ethaline complex39–41.

Following water vapor absorption, a clear increase in peak intensity was observed at chemical shifts corresponding to 3.07–3.14 and 3.54–3.63 ppm (see Table 5), regions primarily associated with the methylene (–CH₂–) groups of the DES constituents. It is important to note that the proton signal from absorbed water typically resonated between 3.3 and 3.8 ppm, depending on its concentration, leading to potential spectral overlap with these regions. Consequently, the observed increase in peak intensity was caused by both the contribution of water protons and the changes in the local chemical environment of the DES following water absorption, such as hydrogen bonding and/or dipole-dipole interactions. This perturbation likely involves the formation of new hydrogen bonds and/or dipole–dipole interactions between water molecules and the DES components. This interpretation aligns with the literature, where the high moisture absorption capacity of Ethaline has been linked to its constituent hydrophilic groups, such as Cl⁻, CH₃, and OH23,42, The role of such functional groups in promoting water absorption is well established25,27,43.

At the molecular level, the high hygroscopicity of Ethaline can be attributed not only to the presence of hydrophilic functional groups but also to the specific hydrogen-bonding environment formed between the chloride anion and ethylene glycol. The small molecular size and high conformational flexibility of ethylene glycol facilitate the formation of an extended and dynamic hydrogen-bond network with Cl⁻, creating accessible interaction sites for incoming water molecules. This hydrogen-bonding environment facilitates interactions between water molecules and the DES components, contributing to enhanced moisture uptake. This molecular arrangement provides a plausible mechanistic basis for the enhanced water-vapor absorption behavior observed for ChCl: EG.

Figure 6 illustrates the complex mechanism between ChCl and EG. As indicated, hydrogen bonding among EG and the chloride ion (Cl−) hydroxyl groups (O-H), as well as those of choline and ethylene glycol, led to the complex; known as ChCl-EG being formed. The hydrogen bonds formed in DES resulted in an ordered structure and stronger interactions among components, which ultimately impacted its chemical and physical properties.

In this molecular structure, two hydroxyl (–OH) groups and a central chloride ion (Cl⁻) are clearly present20,27,43. Based on the ¹H NMR results and the interaction scheme illustrated in Fig. 6, the pronounced hygroscopic behavior of Ethaline can be attributed to the presence of hydrophilic Cl⁻ and –OH groups.

Absorption efficiency of DESs compared to TEG

The performance of the five DESs was compared with TEG under identical operating conditions. As demonstrated in Figs. 7 and 8, all DESs achieved a more pronounced reduction in outlet relative humidity and higher calculated recovery factors than TEG across the entire range of flow rates tested. This result demonstrated the superior water vapor affinity of the DESs under dynamic, continuous gas–liquid contacting conditions.

This behavior was further supported by a quantitative comparison based on Karl Fischer measurements. After 6 h of continuous absorption at a flow rate of 400 mL·min⁻¹, the DESs exhibited final water contents ranging from 5.68 to 9.03 wt% (equivalent to 60.2–99.2 g H₂O per kg DES). In contrast, TEG reached a significantly lower water content of 4.81 wt% (50.6 g/kg). The choline chloride–ethylene glycol (ChCl–EG) DES achieved the highest absorption capacity, which aligns with its favorable combination of lower viscosity and greater hydrophilic character.

The superior water absorption capacity of the DESs was attributed to their extensive hydrogen-bonding networks and the abundance of hydrophilic functional groups (e.g., –OH, Cl⁻), which facilitated strong interactions with water molecules. In comparison, TEG offered a more limited array of accessible interaction sites, leading to its lower absorption capacity under identical experimental conditions.

It is important to state that the results presented herein characterized the performance of the DESs and TEG under controlled, non-regenerative laboratory conditions. Consequently, the superior absorption observed for the DESs reflects their behavior within this specific experimental framework and is not intended to replicate industrial-scale dehydration performance.

Influence of water on the physicochemical properties and absorption performance of ChCl-urea

In addition to the high viscosity of DESs, their practical application in a number of fields is limited by their slow mass transfer. Diluting DES with water was found to be a useful method to reduce its viscosity44. It has been reported that DES formation involves the establishment of an intermolecular hydrogen-bond network among its components, accompanied by modification of the original hydrogen-bond interactions of the individual constituents. When water enters the DES system, it disrupts the DES–DES hydrogen bonds and forms new interactions with both ionic species and hydrogen-bond donors, particularly at low water concentrations. As a result of the high-water concentration, strong interactions were created among the DES components, resulting in decreased intermolecular and intramolecular interactions in the liquids and a reduction in their interactions. Consequently, the physicochemical properties of DES were greatly altered under these conditions25,44.Then, ChCl-urea diluted with varying water percentages was measured at 25 °C for viscosity. Based on the results presented in Fig. 9a; Table 4, adding water to ChCl-urea significantly reduced the viscosity. When DES was diluted with water, the interactions of hydrogen bonding among the components slowly diminished, leading to a significant decrease in viscosity. Furthermore, after adding about 2% water, the viscosity value decreased by approximately 60%. This pronounced initial decrease suggests that the effect of water on DES viscosity is non-linear, as small amounts of water disrupt hydrogen-bond interactions within the eutectic system.

ChCl-urea-Water systems were measured from 298 to 373 K in terms of viscosity. Based on the findings presented in Fig. 9b for the ChCl-urea-Water systems, it is evident that the viscosity of the eutectic aqueous solutions was highly dependent on temperature, and it has been demonstrated that viscosity decreased considerably with increasing temperature. This finding is consistent with previous findings that revealed viscosity changes were temperature-dependent45.

For the purpose of determining whether water presence had an impact on the interactions among various terminal groups in ChCl-urea, the FTIR spectrum of the ChCl-urea was recorded before and after adding 8 wt% water.

Figure 10 illustrates the effect of adding water on DES. Before adding water (Fig. 10a), the sample had the major bands, including a N-H stretching band in the 3100–3500 cm− 1 range, a C = O band in the range of 700–900 cm− 1, a C-N band in the range of 1200–1400 cm− 1, a C-N band relating to the quaternary ammonium group of choline in the range of 1000–1100 cm− 1, and a C-O band in the 1050–1100 cm− 1 range. A FTIR analysis of the sample after adding water revealed that adding water produced changes in the FTIR bands, primarily as a result of the formation of new hydrogen bonds24. Due to the formation of new hydrogen bonds with the water molecules, the N-H and C = O bands became broader and shifted to lower wavenumbers, while a broad and strong band appeared in the range of 3000–3600 cm⁻¹ corresponding to the presence of the water OH band24(these changes are shown in Fig. 10b).

These findings indicated that diluted DES resulted in the weakening of powerful hydrogen bonds between HBD and HBA24,46, but the intermolecular interaction network within the DES remained unchanged until 8% mass fractions of water were added, and water molecules were integrated into the network46. Furthermore, it was revealed that as a result of adding water, the number of hydrogen bonds formed between urea molecules and between the urea and the anion decreased46. This is clearly because the anion tends to bind water instead of urea. Finally, it was found that the presence of water resulted in a significant increase in Reline ion diffusion46.

Effect of water content on DES absorption performance

As previously mentioned, the DESs were found to be powerful water-vapor absorbents. However, as illustrated in Fig. 2, at room temperature, most of the DESs are highly viscous, indicating that hydrogen bonds are likely to occur among the components. Eutectic mixture viscosity is influenced by the nature of its composition, the HBA/HBD molar ratio, temperature, and water content47. Due to their high viscosity, DESs pose a challenge to practical applications in industries. Figure 9 and the findings of the conducted research indicate that water can greatly reduce DES viscosity, which is a crucial factor when applying viscous DESs, and thereby enhancing their performance48.

In this study, to investigate the effect of water on viscosity and absorption capacity, Reline (choline chloride and urea 1:2) was chosen due to its high viscosity; 2,4,6,8 wt% liquid water was added to it. Numerous studies have explored the impact of the addition of water to Reline25,33. Based on the findings, adding water to the system reduced the number of hydrogen bonds among the components of DES and formed new bonds between them and water25. It is worth mentioning that at low water concentrations (≤ 1 mol fraction), water slowly enhanced the hydrogen bonds between choline and urea25. Furthermore, it was found that the DES system resisted hydration between 2 and 10 water mole fractions by maintaining its original structure, but at 15 mol fractions (51% water), the structural motifs of DES were no longer evident, and an aqueous solution containing DES components was obtained25.

Figure 10 illustrates how water was incorporated into the hydrogen bond network of DES and how the DES structure was maintained up to 8 wt% water. Furthermore, Table 4 indicates that the addition of water to DES decreased its viscosity noticeably, and the presence of 2%, 4%, 6% and 8% of water decreased the viscosity to more than 60%, 87%, 92%, and 95% of the pure viscosity, respectively. These results indicate that the presence of water greatly influenced viscosity and entirely resolved the issue of fluids with high viscosity. Next, water was examined with regard to its impact on the water vapor absorption efficiency of ChCl-urea-Water. Figure 9 shows how water impacted the absorption efficiency of ChCl-urea. All experiments were carried out at a flow rate of 400 mL/min for inlet air. Based on the results of this study and in line with expectations, in the presence of water, the driving force for water vapor absorption is weakened, decreasing the recovery coefficient. The recovery coefficient decreased from 94.84% to 93.63%, 91.89% and 85.99% in the presence of 2%, 4% and 6% water. Consequently, after adding a small content of water (around 2% wt.), although the viscosity of the Reline reduced noticeably, DES aqueous solution still exhibited a greater moisture absorption capacity than TEG’s. This indicates that DESs can absorb moisture more effectively than TEG. However, due to the noticeable decrease in viscosity and the slight decrease in absorption efficiency, the aqueous solution of DESs is thought to be a preferred choice where complete dehydration is not desired, such as in air conditioning systems (Fig. 11).

To place the present findings in the context of existing literature, the observed absorption behavior can be compared with previously reported studies on choline chloride–based DESs. Earlier investigations have mainly focused on moisture uptake under static or equilibrium conditions, or have relied on thermodynamic and modeling approaches. The absorption trends observed in this work are consistent with those reports, confirming the strong affinity of hydrophilic ChCl-based DESs toward water vapor. However, in the present study, water vapor absorption is examined in a continuous gas–liquid contact system, and multiple DESs are directly compared under identical operating conditions, allowing their performance to be assessed under conditions closer to practical applications.

Regeneration of DES through heating

Considering the hygroscopic nature of deep eutectic solvents (DESs) and the substantial influence of absorbed water on their physical and chemical properties, implementing reliable regeneration strategies was essential to restoring their original characteristics. The water content of the DESs and TEG after six hours of absorption was quantified by volumetric Karl Fischer titration (Table 6). Given its superior absorption performance, the thermal regenerability of the most effective DES, Ethaline, was investigated. Water-loaded Ethaline samples were regenerated in a vacuum oven at two temperatures (50 and 80 °C), with the desorption progress tracked gravimetrically by measuring mass loss at two-hour intervals.

The evolution of water content in Ethaline as a function of heating time is shown in Fig. 12. The findings demonstrate that desorption at 80 °C is significantly more efficient than at 50 °C. At the higher temperature, approximately 80% of the absorbed water was desorbed within the initial two hours. After eight hours of heating, the water content reached approximately 0.6 wt%, representing the removal of nearly 94% of the initial absorbed water. Prolonged heating beyond this time resulted in a plateau in the water content, indicating that a small fraction of strongly bound water molecules remained within the DES’s hydrogen-bonded network.

Changes in the bulk properties of ethaline are consistent with these observations. Following vacuum heating at 80 °C, the viscosity and density of the water-loaded sample increased from 43.145 mPa·s and 1.0812 g·cm⁻³ to 49.032 mPa·s and 1.1013 g·cm⁻³, respectively (Table 4). These regenerated values corresponded to approximately 98% of the viscosity and density of freshly prepared Ethaline (50.014 mPa·s and 1.1291 g·cm⁻³), demonstrating that the regeneration process effectively removed absorbed water and restored the DES’s key physicochemical properties. These results indicate that, under the applied laboratory regeneration conditions, the system maintains short-term physicochemical stability within the tested cycle range.

Furthermore, FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 13) confirms the preservation of Ethaline’s characteristic supramolecular structure post-regeneration, affirming its thermal and chemical stability under the employed conditions.

While Ethaline demonstrated effective dehydration and high property recovery under the mild regeneration conditions applied in this work, these conditions were notably less severe than those in industrial TEG units, where temperatures of 190–205 °C, with vacuum or stripping gas, were used to achieve final water contents below 0.01 wt%. In contrast, the lower temperature used here resulted in a residual fraction of strongly bound water remaining in the DES. Acknowledging this key difference was critical for interpreting the present results and for realistically evaluating the feasibility of implementing such DESs in industrial gas dehydration processes.

Thermal regeneration and reusability of ChCl-EG

To evaluate the practical potential for repeated use, the cyclic absorption–regeneration stability of Ethaline was investigated. Following each absorption experiment, the water-loaded DES was regenerated under vacuum at 80 °C and then reused. In Fig. 14, the outlet relative humidity (RH) profiles of freshly prepared Ethaline (ChCl-EG) are compared with those obtained after one and two regeneration cycles.

The absorption profiles of the regenerated Ethaline samples are closely aligned with that of the fresh DES. In each cycle, the outlet RH remained low initially and then rose progressively as the absorbent approached saturation, with only minimal variation between cycles. These slight discrepancies were likely due to minor differences in residual water content after regeneration and inherent experimental measurement uncertainty. Overall, the findings demonstrate that low-temperature vacuum regeneration at 80 °C effectively restored the water vapor absorption performance of the ChCl–EG DES, supporting its potential for repeated use.

Following regeneration, viscosity, density, and hydrogen-bond structure were recovered, consistent with physicochemical and spectroscopic observations reported previously. Although these findings demonstrate that ChCl–EG withstood repeated regeneration under the mild conditions applied here, it is important to acknowledge that such performance reflected behavior under laboratory-scale regeneration. It would be necessary to evaluate these results under more rigorous regeneration conditions before attempting to apply them to industrial dehydration.

Conclusions

In this study, a continuous absorption method was employed to investigate how five kinds of synthesized DESs based on choline chloride (ChCl-EG, ChCl-TTEG, ChCl-TEG, ChCl-glycerol, and ChCl-urea) absorbed water vapor from air. A number of effective parameters were investigated, including the rate at which air enters the contactor tower, the amount of water in the absorbing liquid, various ratios of DES, and the regeneration of DES through thermal energy for the purpose of reabsorbing.

First, the effect of inlet gas flow rate on water vapor absorption was evaluated at three flow rates of 100, 400, and 800 mL/min. In all experiments, the relative humidity of the outlet gas increased over time as the absorbent gradually became saturated. This decline in performance at higher flow rates is primarily attributed to the shorter residence time of gas bubbles in the liquid phase, which reduces the effective contact between gas and absorbent. Consequently, mass transfer limitations become more pronounced, lowering the overall uptake capacity.

This study also studied the impact of water on viscosity and it was found that water, one of the most environmentally friendly solvents, can be utilized as a co-solvent to modify the physicochemical characteristics of DESs efficiently by lowering their viscosity, which is one of the primary limitations of DESs in practical applications.

Finally, DES thermal regeneration demonstrated that 80% of the water was removed within two hours by heating it at 80 °C, restoring the water vapor absorption rate, density, and viscosity to values close to the original values. These results indicate that heating not only facilitates water removal, but it also provides an opportunity to successfully recover DES properties. Consequently, DES solvents are highly absorbent and cost-effective, making them efficient and effective solvents for water vapor absorbers in industry, with the potential for being reused multiple times.

It is important to recognize that the conclusions of this work were derived from controlled laboratory-scale experiments. A comprehensive evaluation of the practical viability of DESs for industrial natural gas dehydration will require further investigation under conditions that mimic full-scale operations, including performance benchmarking against continuously regenerated, lean TEG systems.
