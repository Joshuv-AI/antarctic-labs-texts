Associations of oxidative balance score and sleep duration with cognitive impairment in aging adults: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Botang Guo, Yihao Shu, Kexin Li, Yuying Wu, Xueru Fu, Yimeng Ge, Weifeng Huo, Liuding Wen, Haocheng Yang, Ying Fu, Yu Liu, Ping Tang, Ming Zhang, Dongsheng Hu, Lei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress, reflecting the balance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant influences from diet and lifestyle, has been implicated in cognitive decline. Sleep duration is another modifiable lifestyle factor associated with cognitive health, but its joint relationship with oxidative balance remains insufficiently understood.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted in Henan Province, China, based on the Rural Chinese Cohort Study. A total of 4,988 eligible participants aged 60 years or older at the 10-year follow-up were included in the final analysis. The Oxidative Balance Score (OBS) was constructed from dietary and lifestyle components, and cognitive impairment was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination at the 10-year follow-up. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to examine the associations of OBS and sleep duration with cognitive impairment. Restricted cubic splines were applied to assess nonlinear associations. Joint and interaction analyses were further conducted to evaluate the combined associations of OBS and sleep duration.ResultsAt the 10-year follow-up, 760 participants (15.2%) had cognitive impairment. In the fully adjusted model, compared with the lowest OBS tertile, the highest OBS tertile was associated with lower odds of cognitive impairment (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.63−0.96), whereas the association for the moderate OBS tertile was not statistically significant. Restricted cubic spline analysis suggested a nonlinear association between OBS and cognitive impairment (P for nonlinearity = 0.023). Compared with normal sleep duration, both short and long sleep durations were associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment. Joint analyses showed that certain unfavorable combinations of OBS and sleep duration were associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment, particularly short sleep with moderate OBS and long sleep with low OBS. Interaction analysis based on OBS and sleep categories also suggested evidence of interaction (P for interaction = 0.048). Results remained broadly consistent in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsHigher OBS was associated with lower odds of cognitive impairment, whereas both short and long sleep duration were associated with higher odds. Certain unfavorable combinations of oxidative balance and sleep duration were also associated with greater odds of cognitive impairment. These findings support the relevance of considering oxidative balance and sleep duration together in relation to cognitive health in older adults.Keywords: Oxidative balance score, Cognitive impairment, Sleep duration, Neurodegeneration, Cohort study

Introduction

As the global population ages, neurodegenerative processes pose a significant health challenge, characterized by the gradual dysfunction of neurons, leading to cognitive decline, impaired functionality, and a lower quality of life [1]. Neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease are advanced stages of a spectrum, while cognitive impairment in the general population shows earlier and more common signs [2,3]. Identifying modifiable factors associated with impaired cognitive function is crucial for developing preventive strategies aimed at maintaining long-term brain health.

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a biological pathway underlying neurodegenerative processes, leading to neuronal damage, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation [4,5]. Accumulating experimental and clinical evidence suggests that antioxidants may play a protective role by mitigating oxidative damage and supporting neuronal integrity [6,7]. However, population-based evidence linking antioxidant-related exposures to cognitive outcomes remains inconsistent [[8], [9], [10], [11]], partly due to challenges in capturing long-term oxidative status in real-world settings. Focusing on individual nutrients might not accurately represent real-world exposure, given that the cumulative interaction of diet and lifestyle determines oxidative and antioxidative balance across time.

The Oxidative Balance Score (OBS) serves as an integrative metric capturing the overall equilibrium between oxidative stress and endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms. This score is derived from a combination of dietary exposures and lifestyle behaviors that collectively shape redox status [12]. By simultaneously incorporating pro-oxidant and antioxidant components, OBS provides a broad characterization of behaviors and diets connected to systemic oxidative stress and inflammation regulation [13,14]. Higher OBS values typically indicate elevated levels of antioxidant exposure, suggesting a relatively favorable oxidative-antioxidant balance in the body [15]. Research has indicated that imbalances in oxidative stress are crucial in the development of various diseases and may underlie cognitive decline [9,10]. Earlier research has demonstrated that higher levels of OBS are associated with a reduced risk of diabetes and heart diseases, suggesting that enhancing oxidative balance might help in preserving homeostasis [[16], [17], [18]]. Despite growing interest in oxidative balance, the association between OBS and cognitive function has not been adequately investigated in prospective cohort settings.

Sleep duration represents another important lifestyle factor closely linked to brain health and oxidative regulation [19,20]. Sufficient sleep is necessary for neuron repair, waste removal, and oxidative balance whereas both too little and too much sleep are connected to higher oxidative stress and cognitive decline [21,22]. These findings imply that the amount of sleep may act as a biological context that influences the relationship between oxidative balance and cognitive decline. Nevertheless, few population-based studies have investigated whether sleep duration modifies the association between oxidative balance and cognitive outcomes.

With this in mind, the present study examined the association between OBS and cognitive impairment in a prospective Chinese cohort and assessed whether this association differed by sleep duration. By jointly considering antioxidant-related lifestyle patterns and sleep behavior within a longitudinal framework, this study might improve insights into the possible impact of oxidative balance on cognitive impairment in the context of neurodegeneration.

Methods

Study design and participants

The present analysis was conducted within the Rural Chinese Cohort Study (RCCS), a prospective cohort established in two townships of Xin’an County, Henan Province, China, using natural villages as sampling units [23]. The RCCS enrolled 20,194 participants at baseline during 2007−2008. According to the RCCS entry criteria, participants were required to have no evident cognitive or communication impairment and no major neurologic disease at enrollment. Cognitive assessment in the RCCS was performed only at the 10-year follow-up and only among participants aged 60 years or older, the eligible population for the present study was restricted to those who had reached 60 years of age or older by the 10-year follow-up (n = 11,016). Of these, 688 were lost to follow-up, 2,297 died before the 10-year examination, 3,011 did not complete the cognitive assessment because of non-participation or inability to perform the test, and 32 had missing key baseline variables. The final analytic sample therefore included 4,988 participants. The detailed selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University on April 10, 2014. The research was performed in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was secured prior to participation.

Flowchart of participant selection.

Data collection

Data collection was performed by trained investigators employing standardized protocols, which included face-to-face structured questionnaires, physical examinations, and the collection of fasting blood samples. The questionnaire was used to gather sociodemographic data (age, sex, education, marital status, and household income), lifestyle behaviors (smoking, drinking, physical exercise, and sleep), and dietary intake. The physical exams involved taking measurements of height, weight, and blood pressure, all conducted according to standardized methods. Participants had their height and weight measured while wearing light clothing and no shoes. Venous blood samples, after fasting for 8 h or more, were collected in the morning, and biochemical indicators were evaluated using automated analyzers based on standardized lab methods.

Definition and assessment of study variables

Cognitive impairment

The Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was used to evaluate cognitive function in participants aged 60 and older during the 2018–2020 follow-up [24]. The MMSE is a well-known assessment tool that measures different cognitive domains, including orientation, memory, attention and calculation, recall, and language, with scores that can range from 0 to 30. Given the well-documented influence of educational attainment on MMSE performance, education-specific cutoff values validated in Chinese older populations were applied. Cognitive impairment was characterized by an MMSE score of 17 or lower for those without formal education, 20 or lower for individuals with primary education, and 24 or lower for participants with secondary education or higher [25]. Participants whose scores fell below the corresponding threshold at follow-up were classified as having cognitive impairment.

Oxidative Balance Score (OBS)

Based on established methods, OBS was designed as a composite index to show the balance between antioxidant and pro-oxidant exposures [26,27]. The OBS was made up of 20 components, 16 of which were dietary and 4 were lifestyle factors, including 15 antioxidants and 5 pro-oxidants. Dietary components, including dietary fiber, carotenoids, riboflavin, niacin, vitamin B6, total folate, vitamin B12, vitamin C, vitamin E, calcium, magnesium, zinc, copper, selenium, total fat, and iron, were assessed using a semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire administered by trained interviewers. This questionnaire has been previously used in dietary epidemiologic analyses within the RCCS [28], and baseline dietary intake was used in the present analysis. The lifestyle factors considered were physical activity, body mass index, alcohol intake, and smoking habits. Each component was categorized into sex-specific tertiles and assigned a score of 0, 1, or 2 [29]. For antioxidant components, participants in the lowest, middle, and highest tertiles were assigned scores of 0, 1, and 2, respectively; for pro-oxidant components, the scoring was reversed. The total OBS was obtained by summing all the scores, with higher numbers reflecting a more favorable oxidative balance [30]. OBS was further categorized into tertiles according to its distribution in the analytic sample, corresponding to <15, 15 to <17, and ≥17. The detailed scoring scheme for all OBS components is presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Sleep duration

The assessment of sleep duration was based on a self-reported question regarding the average amount of sleep per day, including both night and day sleep. Baseline sleep duration was used in the present analysis. Based on previous research, sleep duration was classified as less than 6 h per day (short sleep), 6–8 h per day (normal sleep), and more than 8 h per day (long sleep) [31,32]. This categorization was adopted in accordance with commonly used classifications in epidemiologic studies, with 6−8 h/day serving as the reference group.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on previous studies and biological plausibility. The adjusted covariates included sociodemographic characteristics and baseline health conditions. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, education level, marital status, and personal monthly income. Baseline health conditions included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Type 2 diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of glucose-lowering medication. Hyperlipidemia was defined as self-reported physician diagnosis or current use of lipid-lowering medication.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 from SAS Institute Inc. in Cary, NC, USA, and R version 4.4.1 from the R Foundation for Statistical Computing in Vienna, Austria. Baseline characteristics were summarized according to cognitive status. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) because several variables were not normally distributed, and group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were shown as numbers and percentages and compared using the χ2 test. To assess potential selection bias related to selective survival, attrition, non-participation, and missing data, baseline characteristics were compared between participants included in the final analysis and those excluded during the selection process.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of OBS and sleep duration with cognitive impairment. In the primary analyses, OBS was categorized into tertiles according to its distribution in the analytic sample. Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 further adjusted for education level, marital status, and personal monthly income; and Model 3 additionally adjusted for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and type 2 diabetes.

Restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to examine the potential nonlinear association between OBS and cognitive impairment. To further examine the joint associations of OBS and sleep duration, combined exposure categories were created using OBS tertiles and sleep-duration categories, with participants having high OBS and normal sleep duration (6−8 h/day) serving as the reference group.

Multiplicative interaction between OBS and sleep duration was assessed by including an product term of OBS tertiles and sleep duration category in the fully adjusted logistic regression model. Exploratory additive interaction measures were further calculated for selected combined exposure categories, by using the relative excess risk due to interaction, attributable proportion, and synergy index, and their coresponding 95% CIs.

To assess whether the association between OBS and cognitive impairment was broadly consistent across subgroups, subgroup analyses were conducted with OBS modeled as a continuous variable. For each subgroup analysis, the corresponding interaction term between continuous OBS and the stratifying variable was included to obtain the P value for interaction.

Sensitivity analyses included inverse probability weighting (IPW) to account for potential bias due to differential inclusion in the final analytic sample, repeated analyses excluding participants with type 2 diabetes at baseline, and modified Poisson regression with robust variance to assess the robustness of the findings. Statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Among 4,988 participants, 760 participants (15.2%) had cognitive impairment. Baseline characteristics stratified by cognitive status are presented in Table 1. Participants with cognitive impairment were older and more likely to be women compared with cognitively normal participants (P < 0.001). They also had lower educational attainment, were less likely to be married or cohabiting, and reported lower physical activity levels and longer sedentary time (all P < 0.001). In terms of lifestyle behaviors, participants with cognitive impairment were less likely to report current smoking or alcohol consumption (both P < 0.001). The prevalence of hypertension and type 2 diabetes was significantly higher in the cognitive impairment group (both P < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed for hyperlipidemia. Participants with cognitive impairment also had a slightly lower body mass index than those without cognitive impairment (P = 0.021). Participants with cognitive impairment had significantly lower OBS compared to those without cognitive impairment (P < 0.001). The distribution of sleep duration also differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.001). Among all participants eligible for the 10-year cognitive assessment, those included in the present analysis differed from excluded individuals in several sociodemographic and health-related characteristics, including age, sex, education, lifestyle behaviors, and cardiometabolic conditions (Supplementary Table S2), supporting the need to consider potential selection bias in sensitivity analyses.

Association of OBS and sleep duration with cognitive impairment

As shown in Table 2, higher OBS was associated with lower odds of cognitive impairment. In the fully adjusted model, compared with participants in the lowest OBS category, those in the highest OBS category had lower odds of cognitive impairment (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.63−0.96), whereas the association for the moderate OBS category was attenuated and did not reach statistical significance (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.67−1.02). When OBS was analyzed as a continuous variable, each one-standard deviation increase in OBS was associated with a 3% lower odds of cognitive impairment (OR = 0.97, 95% CI:0.94−1.00). Restricted cubic spline analysis further suggested a nonlinear association between OBS and cognitive impairment (P for nonlinearity = 0.023), with the estimated odds decreasing at lower OBS levels and then tending to plateau at higher levels in Fig. 2. Sleep duration was also associated with cognitive impairment. Compared with participants sleeping 6−8 h/day, both short sleep duration (<6 h/day) and long sleep duration (>8 h/day) were associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment in the fully adjusted model (short sleep: OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.01−2.35; long sleep: OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.14−1.62).

Table 3 presents the joint associations of OBS and sleep duration with cognitive impairment. Participants with normal sleep duration (6−8 h/day) and high OBS were used as the reference group. Compared with this group, short sleep combined with moderate OBS was associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment after full adjustment (OR = 2.79, 95%CI: 1.36−5.51). Short sleep combined with low OBS showed elevated but non-significant odds (OR = 1.85, 95%CI: 0.85−3.77). Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether the association between OBS and cognitive impairment was consistent across sleep duration and other participant characteristics, with OBS modeled as a continuous variable (Fig. 3). Higher OBS showed an inverse association with cognitive impairment across most subgroups, although several stratum-specific estimates did not reach statistical significance. Among sleep duration strata, the inverse association was statistically significant only among participants with long sleep duration. However, when OBS was modeled continuously, the interaction test between OBS and sleep duration did not reach statistical significance (P for interaction = 0.138), suggesting limited evidence of heterogeneity across sleep-duration strata in the subgroup analysis. For other participant characteristics, the direction of association was generally consistent across strata. Evidence of heterogeneity was observed only for type 2 diabetes mellitus (P for interaction = 0.037), whereas interaction tests for age, sex, marital status, personal monthly income, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses

After excluding participants with type 2 diabetes at baseline, the associations of OBS and sleep duration with cognitive impairment remained broadly consistent with the primary analyses (Table 5). Higher OBS was consistently associated with lower odds of cognitive impairment, and long sleep duration remained associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment. Short sleep duration showed an elevated but non-significant association after full adjustment. Additional sensitivity analyses using inverse probability weighting and modified Poisson regression with robust variance yielded generally similar findings (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). In both analyses, the direction of association for OBS and long sleep duration was consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the main findings.

Discussion

The findings showed that a higher OBS was associated with lower odds of cognitive impairment, and this inverse association was generally robust after multivariable adjustment and sensitivity analyses. Both short and long sleep durations were independently associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment. Joint analyses further suggested that certain unfavorable combined exposure profiles, particularly short sleep with moderate OBS and long sleep with low OBS, were associated with greater odds of cognitive impairment. Interaction analysis based on OBS tertiles and sleep categories provided statistical evidence of interaction, although subgroup analyses and additive interaction measures indicated that the interaction pattern was not uniform across exposure combinations.

The observed association between OBS and cognitive impairment is consistent with the growing body of evidence linking oxidative stress to cognitive decline and neurodegenerative processes [33]. Accumulating studies suggest that persistent oxidative stress may contribute to cognitive deterioration through multiple biological pathways, including neuroinflammation [34], mitochondrial dysfunction [35], synaptic damage [36], and neuronal apoptosis [37]. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on single antioxidant nutrients or isolated dietary components, OBS integrates multiple pro-oxidant and antioxidant dietary and lifestyle factors, thereby reflecting the overall oxidative-antioxidative balance [38,39]. This integrative approach may better capture cumulative exposure patterns under real-world conditions and may therefore be useful for understanding the association between complex lifestyle profiles and cognitive function.

In the present study, both short and long sleep durations were associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment in the fully adjusted primary analysis, with the association for long sleep duration appearing more consistent across sensitivity analyses. This pattern is broadly in line with previous epidemiological evidence, although the sleep-cognition relationship remains heterogeneous. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that short sleep duration was associated with a higher risk of future dementia mainly in studies with shorter follow-up, whereas this association was not statistically significant in studies with longer follow-up, suggesting that short sleep may partly reflect prodromal changes rather than a stable long-term causal risk factor [40]. By contrast, extended sleep duration was associated with higher dementia risk in both shorter and longer follow-up studies, although substantial between-study heterogeneity was noted. Experimental and observational evidence further suggests that abnormal sleep duration may be related to cognitive dysfunction through circadian disruption [41], impaired neuroplasticity, and reduced clearance of metabolic waste products from the brain [42]. At the same time, prolonged sleep duration may reflect underlying health conditions, frailty, or early neurological dysfunction [43]. Therefore, the associations between sleep duration and cognitive impairment should be understood in the context of heterogeneous prior evidence and the possibility that abnormal sleep duration may represent early manifestations of neurodegenerative processes rather than independent causal exposures.

The joint and interaction analyses extend these observations by suggesting that oxidative balance and sleep duration may be jointly related to cognitive impairment. The higher odds observed for short sleep with moderate OBS may be partly biologically plausible, as sleep deprivation has been linked to increased reactive oxygen species production [44], impaired antioxidant defense [45], and reduced oxidative damage repair [46], which may intensify the adverse consequences of an unfavorable oxidative balance. The higher odds observed for long sleep combined with low OBS may reflect a different pattern, potentially involving accumulated health vulnerability, frailty, or early neurodegenerative changes. Taken together, these results support the relevance of considering oxidative balance and sleep duration together when examining lifestyle-related correlates of cognitive health in older adults. However, because the interaction pattern varied across exposure combinations and analytic scales, these interaction findings should be interpreted cautiously.

This study has several strengths, including its community-based sample, the use of an integrative OBS measure combining multiple dietary and lifestyle-related pro-oxidant and antioxidant factors, and the application of joint analyses, interaction analyses, and multiple sensitivity analyses. Several limitations should also be considered. Substantial attrition occurred during follow-up, and the final analytic sample may represent a relatively healthier surviving subset of the original cohort. Although IPW analyses yielded broadly similar results, residual selection bias and survivor bias cannot be excluded. In addition, although individuals with evident cognitive or communication impairment and major neurologic disease were excluded at cohort enrollment, baseline cognitive function was not assessed using a standardized instrument. As a result, baseline cognitive status could not be more precisely characterized or adjusted for, and residual confounding related to baseline cognitive status cannot be completely excluded. Sleep duration and several lifestyle-related factors were self-reported and may have been subject to measurement error. Moreover, the present analyses were based on baseline OBS and baseline sleep duration and did not account for changes in these exposures during follow-up, which may have influenced the magnitude of the observed associations. Oxidative balance was assessed using a composite score rather than direct biomarkers, which limits biological interpretation. Additive interaction estimates should also be interpreted cautiously because they were derived from odds ratios and the outcome was not rare. Finally, cognitive function was evaluated using a screening tool and may not fully capture domain-specific changes. Future studies incorporating objective biomarkers, repeated exposure assessments, and more detailed cognitive evaluations are warranted.

Conclusion

Higher OBS was associated with lower odds of cognitive impairment, whereas both short and long sleep durations were associated with higher odds. Certain unfavorable combinations of oxidative balance and sleep duration were also associated with greater odds of cognitive impairment. These findings support the relevance of considering oxidative balance and sleep duration together in relation to cognitive health in older adults.
