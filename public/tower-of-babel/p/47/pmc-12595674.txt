Sequential PD-1 inhibitors as consolidative therapy post-CD19 CART in relapsed/refractory NHL: a propensity score matching cohort study
Authors: Bin Xue, Jie Zhou, Xi Chen, Yifan Liu, Huina Lu, Zeyu Zhu, Wenjun Zhang, Li Zhang, Aibin Liang, Ping Li, Xiu Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CART) has improved outcomes for relapsed/refractory non-Hodgkin lymphoma (R/R NHL). However, patients with high-risk molecular features, such as double-expressor lymphoma (DEL+), characterized by concurrent MYC and BCL-2 protein overexpression, and TP53 alterations (TP53+), experience dismal outcomes with CART alone.MethodsThis single-center, propensity score matching (PSM) cohort study analyzed 44 R/R NHL patients. 22 patients received consolidative PD-1 inhibitors (Sintilimab, iPD-1) after CART, matched 1:1 with 22 patients receiving CART alone based on 10 baseline characteristics.ResultsThe CART + iPD-1 group showed significantly improved progression-free survival (PFS, median 42.9 months vs. 3.0 months; HR = 0.32, P = 0.003) and overall survival (OS, median not reached vs. 21.5 months; HR = 0.24, P = 0.017). Disease recurrence (40.9% vs. 81.8%, P = 0.013) and progression-related mortality (13.6% vs. 54.5%, P = 0.011) were markedly reduced. The DEL+TP53+ subgroup benefited most (PFS P < 0.001, OS P = 0.010). Adverse events, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), were comparable between groups.ConclusionConsolidative PD-1 inhibitors following CD19 CART infusion could improves survival outcomes in R/R NHL, particularly for the high-risk DEL+TP53+ population, without adding significant toxicity. This suggests a molecularly-defined approach to consolidation therapy and emphasizes the importance of strategic timing in combination immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07281-w.Keywords: CD19, CART/CAR-T, PD-1 inhibitors, NHL, PSM

Introduction

The therapeutic landscape for relapsed or refractory (R/R) non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) has undergone a revolutionary transformation with the advent of CD19-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy. Pioneering trials, including ZUMA-1 (axi-cel), JULIET (tisa-cel), and TRANSCEND-NHL-001(liso-cel), demonstrated exceptional efficacy, achieving durable remissions in patients who had failed first-line therapies [1–3]. Despite the superior effectiveness of CD19 CAR T-cell therapy (CART) compared to other conventional second/third line methods, a substantial proportion of patients experience disease progression within the initial one year following CAR T-cell infusion [4]. For aggressive subtypes such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), long-term progression-free survival (PFS) remains dismally low, sustaining below 40% [5] and worsening catastrophically in molecularly defined high-risk subgroups harboring double-expressor phenotypes (MYC, BCL-2) or TP53 alterations [6–8].

Landmark genomic studies have established that double-expressor lymphoma (DEL), characterized by concurrent overexpression of MYC and BCL-2, displays aggressive biological behavior driven by enhanced proliferation and survival signals [9, 10]. TP53 functional loss promotes profound genomic instability and increased tumor neoantigen burden while simultaneously impairing critical interferon signaling pathways [11, 12]. The combination of double expression and TP53 alterations disrupts key apoptotic and DNA repair pathways, resulting in a poor prognosis for patients [13]. Recent multi-center real-world data demonstrate that the combined presence of DEL and TP53 altered defines a subgroup with poorer CAR T-cell therapy outcomes, and the 1-year PFS and overall survival (OS) rates were close to 0 despite salvage therapies [14].

Immunophenotypic analysis of pre-and post-treatment biopsy samples from ZUMA-1 revealed that CAR T-cells rely on cytokine-mediated crosstalk with the tumor microenvironment (TME) to achieve optimal activity [15]. Preclinical models demonstrate that CAR T-cells upregulate programmed death-1(PD-1) upon antigen exposure, while lymphoma cells counterexpress programmed death-ligand-1(PD-L1), engaging a molecular “brake” that extinguishes CART cytotoxicity and persistence. Lymphomas use this axis to induce T-cell exhaustion and create a protective shield. PD-1 inhibitors can rescue CAR T-cell exhaustion and activate endogenous T cell function, indicating their potential for synergistic effects in combination therapy [16–19]. Early-phase trials explored differences in efficacy and cytokine release syndrome (CRS) response caused by different infusion times between PD-1 antagonist and CAR T-cells infusion [20, 21]. Although initiating PD-1 blockade one day before CAR T-cells infusion delayed the onset and shortened the duration of CRS, post-infusion PD-1 inhibition was associated with superior long-term efficacy [22]. In a previous study, we initially explored the therapeutic potential of CD19 CART combined with PD-1 inhibitors in DLBCL patients with TP53 alterations. Among the 9 patients, 8 achieved the overall response (OR) rate, with 6 achieving a complete response (CR), and the median PFS and OS were not reached (NR), showing the potential benefits of combining CART and PD-1 inhibitors [23].

On these bases, we conducted a rigorous propensity score matching cohort analysis in the R/R NHL cohort to address the pivotal question regarding the efficacy and safety of consolidative PD-1 inhibition administered after CD19 CART. Our findings demonstrate that PD-1 blockade following CAR-T therapy could improves survival. And as a consolidation strategy, it enhances long-term prognosis in DEL+TP53+ lymphoma patients refractory to CART monotherapy. The findings discuss the importance of strategic timing in immunotherapy combinations and explore a molecularly defined approach to CART consolidation therapy.

Methods

Study design and population

This study reviewed 127 R/R NHL patients who received CD19 CART for the first time in the Shanghai Tongji Hospital from July 2017 to December 2024. Clinical data were collected from medical records and electronic patient files, the follow-up period ended on June 30, 2025, and 6 patients lost to follow-up were excluded. The study protocols, approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tongji Hospital, adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

The PD-1 inhibitor (iPD-1) Sintilimab was administered every 3 weeks after CAR T-cell infusion, 3 mg/kg for patients with a weight of < 60 kg or 200 mg for patients with a weight of ≥ 60 kg. Patients who used Sintilimab when the disease had not progressed after CART were included in the CART + iPD-1 group. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed at a 1:1 ratio to generate a matching cohort. The variable included gender, age at enrollment, Hans classification, lymphoma stage at enrollment, extra-nodal disease, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score at enrollment, International Prognostic Index (IPI) score, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level elevated, bulky disease (maximum tumor length ≥ 10cm‌), and TP53 alterations (mutation or deletion). Caliper value is 0.03 and absolute standardized difference (ASD) ≤ 10% (Supplementary Fig. 1). This included 22 patients as the CART + iPD-1 group and 22 patients as the matching control group (CART group), and a final comparative analysis was conducted (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart illustrating patient selection and propensity score matching. A total of 127 patients with relapsed/refractory non-Hodgkin lymphoma who received CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy were initially screened. After excluding patients lost to follow-up and applying PSM based on 10 baseline variables, 44 patients were included in the final analysis: 22 in the CART + iPD-1 group and 22 in the CART group

Diagnosis and CART teartment

The diagnosis of NHL (DLBCL, FL, tFL) based on pathological evaluation according to the 2016 World Health Organization classification. The classification of double-expression lymphoma (DEL, overexpression of MYC and BCL-2 proteins) and double/triple-hit lymphoma (MYC and BCL2 and/or BCL6 rearrangements) followed standard diagnostic criteria. The Hans algorithm was used to determine the classification of origin cells (COO) to distinguish between germinal center B cells (GCB) and non-germinal center B cell subtypes (non-GCB) [24]. TP53 alterations were identified through mutations detected by next-generation sequencing (NGS) [25] or deletions observed via fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis by the most recent pathological test conducted before CART [26].

All CAR T-cell infusions were done during hospitalization. Prior to the infusion of CART therapy (day 0), FC (Fludarabine 25–30 mg/m²/day + Cyclophosphamide 250–500 mg/m²/day, day − 5 to -3) chemotherapy regimen was administered as preconditioning therapy [1, 2]. And FC chemotherapy was given after the drug washout period of bridging therapy (if had). The drug-eluting period between bridging therapy and initiating lymphodepletion for CAR-T therapy strictly adhered to the practice recommendations [27]. All cases of CRS and ICANS were managed according to the consensus guidelines established by the American Society of Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) [28] and 2022 Chinese consensus guidelines [29].

Study endpoint

The treatment responses were evaluated using positron emission tomography and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (PET-CT) from the Lugano classification [30], categorizing them as complete response, partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). The overall response rate was determined based on the best response (CR or PR) achieved within 3 months after CAR T-cell infusion. PFS was defined as the time from CAR T-cell infusion to disease progression, death, or last follow-up, whichever occurred first. OS was calculated from the time of CAR T-cell infusion until death or last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis in this study was conducted primarily via R software (version 4.3.2) [31], Python software (version: 3.11.x), and GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1). This study employed different statistical tests based on the type of variable. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was first used to assess the normality of their distribution. For variables that met the assumption of normal distribution (P > 0.05), parametric tests were used for between-group comparisons: the independent samples t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three or more groups. If the variable distribution did not meet the assumption of normality, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to assess whether the differences in medians between groups were statistically significant. For categorical variables, we used Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) test to determine the significance of their associations; when the expected frequency of any cell was less than 5, we used Fisher’s exact test as an alternative to ensure the validity of the statistical results. PFS and OS were visualized via Kaplan‒Meier curves. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses on the PSM cohort using Cox proportional hazards models to assess the impact of the treatment on PFS and OS. The reported P values were two-sided, P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant result.

Taking 3 months after CART as the time point to evaluate the patients’ responses, the CR rate of the CART + iPD-1 group was 45.5%, and that of the CART group was 31.8% (P = 0.536). The OR rate (CR + PR) at 3 months in the CART + iPD-1 group was 68.2%, and that in the CART group was 40.9% (P = 0.130). During the entire follow-up period, the disease recurrence rate in the CART + iPD-1 group was 40.9%, and that in the CART group was 81.8% (P = 0.013). The mortality rate due to disease progression was 13.6% in the CART + iPD-1 group and 54.5% in the CART group (P = 0.011). In the CART + iPD-1 group, the median PFS was 42.9 months (95% CI: 16.0-NR), with the median OS not reached (95% CI: NR-NR). In the CART group, the median PFS was 3.0 months (95% CI: 2.0–26.0), and the median OS was 21.5 months (95% CI: 13.0-NR). Significant differences were observed in the K-M curves for PFS (HR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.15–0.70, P = 0.003) and OS (HR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.09–0.67, P = 0.017) between the two groups (Fig. 2B-C).

In addition, it must be mentioned that there are differences in the selection of CAR-T products between the two groups. In the CART group, 2 cases used commercial CAR-T products (Axi-cel, Relma-cel), and 20 cases used clinical trial CAR-T products (NCT03154775, NCT02537977). Univariate Cox analysis further confirmed that product type was not prognostic for PFS or OS (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Previous research has shown that tumor cells can promote the upregulation of PD-L1 on CAR T-cells, leading to PD-1/PD-L1 engagement and subsequent immunosuppressive signaling that compromises T-cell functionality [19]. Additionally, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly M2-polarized subsets, and tumor-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) contribute to elevated PD-L1 expression [17, 32]. It further inhibiting CAR-T cell activity and tumor infiltration via the same pathway. Such suppression is especially evident in solid tumors, where it exacerbates T-cell exhaustion and diminishes cytotoxic efficacy [33]. The combination of CAR T-cell therapy with PD-1 inhibitors aims to overcome this exhausted phenotype by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, thereby rejuvenating effector function, prolonging persistence, and potentiating synergistic antitumor responses when integrated with genetic engineering strategies [34, 35].

This propensity score-matched cohort study provides evidence that consolidative PD-1 inhibitors following CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy improves clinical outcomes in patients with R/R NHL. The robustness of these findings is strengthened by rigorous propensity score matching that balanced 10 critical baseline characteristics between cohorts, including established prognostic factors such as TP53 alteration status, disease stage, extranodal involvement, and tumor burden. The strict caliper value of 0.03 and absolute standardized difference threshold below 10% ensured highly comparable patient groups, minimizing confounding from variables known to influence CART outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that patients receiving sequential CART + iPD-1 therapy exhibited substantially superior progression-free survival (median PFS: 42.9 months vs. 3.0 months; HR = 0.32, P = 0.003) and overall survival (median OS: not reached vs. 21.5 months; HR = 0.24, P = 0.017) compared to those receiving CART alone. The profound reduction in disease recurrence (40.9% vs. 81.8%, P = 0.013) and progression-related mortality (13.6% vs. 54.5%, P = 0.011) emphasizes the potential of this combinatorial approach to overcome the limitations of CART monotherapy.

A pivotal finding of this investigation is the potential differential treatment effect observed across subgroups, particularly in high-risk molecular subgroups. Statistical interaction analysis revealed that double-expressor status significantly modified the treatment effect for progression-free survival (P for interaction = 0.033). Subgroup analysis indicated a trend toward improved PFS with consolidative iPD-1 therapy in the DEL + cohort (HR = 0.56), although the wide 95% confidence interval (0.22–1.43) that includes unity indicates substantial uncertainty and necessitates cautious interpretation. It is noteworthy that DEL status itself was not independently prognostic for survival outcomes in univariate analysis (Table 2), suggesting its role may be primarily predictive rather than prognostic in this context. Despite its strong ability to predict treatment response, DE status itself was not independently associated with either PFS or OS in univariate Cox models (Table 2). In a previous CART study, we found that the coexistence of double expression (DEL+) and TP53 altered (TP53+) leads to a poorer prognosis compared to a single factor [14]. DEL combined with TP53 abnormalities increases the difficulty of treatment by enhancing replication stress and DNA damage accumulation, escape from apoptosis and enhanced pro-proliferation signals, and coordination of drug resistance [36–38]. Some research has established that MYC overexpression transcriptionally upregulates PD-L1 to orchestrate an immune-evasive TME [39, 40]. The comparatively shorter PFS in the matched CART monotherapy group highlights the enrichment for ultra-high-risk molecular features (TP53 alterations) in our cohort, a population with a known predisposition for early progression despite CD19 CART. Recently, TP53 alterations have also been considered an important feature in R/R NHL patients who are insensitive to CART [41, 42]. In malignant DLBCL, TP53 deficiency up-regulates the expression of PD-L1 on the lymphoma cell surface, promotes T cell dysfunction in the TME and contributes to immune evasion, which is one of the reasons for disease relapse [43, 44]. This creates a compelling biological rationale for targeting DEL+TP53+ patients with PD-1 blockade after CAR-T therapy. Although the overall log-rank test for heterogeneity among subgroups did not reach statistical significance (PFS P = 0.366, OS P = 0.073, Fig. 4A-B), likely reflecting limited power for this analysis, a clinically meaningful trend was evident. In the CART + iPD-1 group, DEL-TP53- patients were absent, mainly due to the bias in the selection of clinicians who tend to include high-risk patients in PD-1 inhibitor consolidation therapy (Fig. 4C-D). Even so, the DEL+TP53+ subgroup still improved prognosis after receiving PD-1 inhibitor consolidation therapy after CART (PFS P < 0.001, OS P = 0.010, Fig. 4E-F).

It is worth noting that the rates of CRS, ICANS, cytopenia, and infections were comparable between groups. While one case of grade 4 CRS occurred in the combination group, no new or unexpected safety signals emerged. The single case of immune-related hypothyroidism leading to PD-1 discontinuation was manageable and consistent with the known PD-1 inhibitors’ toxicity profiles. Long-term follow-up revealed no secondary malignancies in either group. These findings suggest that consolidative PD-1 inhibition, when initiated after resolution of acute CART toxicities (median ~ 3 weeks post-infusion), does not substantially exacerbate the immunotherapy toxicity profile.

This study has several limitations. Its non-randomized, single-center design may affect generalizability due to institution-specific patient demographics, treatment protocols, and clinical expertise. Future multi-center studies with broader inclusion criteria are needed to enhance the external validity of the findings. Although PSM was applied to balance known prognostic factors including refractoriness to last therapy, it cannot account for unmeasured variables such as treatment adherence or individual biological differences. The non-randomized assignment to consolidative PD-1 inhibitors introduces the potential for indication bias, and residual confounding may remain. In addition, the limited sample size of 22 subjects per cohort increases the risk of Type I error, where differences may be detected by chance without true underlying effects, and reduces statistical power in subgroup analyses. Our results should be interpreted as comparing a strategy of consolidation with anti-PD-1 against a strategy of observation in patients who are progression-free at that early time point, rather than as a pure measure of the drug’s effect. Expanding the cohort in future prospective studies would improve the reliability of the results and allow for more robust adjustment for confounding. Furthermore, uneven use of CAR-T products across groups and real-world variation in Sintilimab dosing may influence outcomes and complicate dose-response interpretation.

Our findings resonate with emerging scientific literature on immune checkpoint modulation in the post-CART setting [22]. Contemporary investigations exploring PD-1 inhibition concomitant with or after CAR T-cell infusion have yielded inconsistent efficacy outcomes. The sequential approach implemented in this study appears advantageous for both safety and efficacy. The timing potentially allows PD-1 inhibitors to intervene during the critical transition phase when early CAR T-cells functional exhaustion develops, thereby extending the duration of effective tumor immunosurveillance [17, 45]. The effective enhancement of CART efficacy by sequential PD-1 inhibitors may be through delaying CAR T-cells exhaustion caused by persistent antigen stimulation. Our data suggest that DEL and TP53 status may help identify a patient subset most likely to benefit from consolidative PD-1 inhibitors. We propose that these biomarkers should be incorporated into future prospective, randomized trials to validate their predictive value. If confirmed, this strategy could potentially become a new standard for this high-risk population, but current evidence is insufficient to recommend it for routine clinical practice. Future prospective trials are urgently needed to validate our findings in randomized, multi-center settings and define the optimal PD-1 inhibitors dosing schedule and duration.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that consolidative PD-1 inhibitors following CD19 CART improves survival outcomes in relapsed or refractory NHL, particularly in the high-risk DEL+TP53+ subgroup, without introducing new safety concerns. The findings highlight the importance of molecular stratification and strategic timing in immunotherapy combinations. In the future, multi-center prospective randomized trials need to further support the conclusions of the single-center study.
