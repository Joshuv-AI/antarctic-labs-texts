Application of visualization three-dimensional medical imaging technology based on mobile platform in surgery of giant thyroid tumors: a case report
Authors: Zhen Wu, Hailin Sun, Zhao Li, Shen Chen, Lin Han, Yongkui Wang, Wenchao Duan, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical treatment for giant thyroid tumors is complex and carries a high risk of complications. Visualization of the tumor shape and its associations with the surrounding tissue are difficult with two-dimensional imaging techniques. It is thus important to explore more effective approaches for preoperative evaluation and surgical assistance.Cases presentationsThree patients with giant thyroid tumors underwent successful surgery with no complications at Liaocheng People’s Hospital, between January and December 2024. One patient was a 58-year-old male, while the other two were 60-year-old female patients. All the patients are Chinese, both in nationality and ethnicity. We combined tumor imaging information with three-dimensional medical imaging to enable visualization for preoperative assessment, guiding successful surgical planning.ConclusionsThe application of three-dimensional medical imaging during surgery for giant thyroid tumors significantly enhanced the accuracy, safety, and efficiency of surgery, resulting in improved patient outcomes and prognosis.Keywords: Three-dimensional, Medical imaging technology, Case reports

Introduction

The definition of a giant thyroid tumor is a mass greater than 500 g and a diameter of more than 10 cm, with the upper pole potentially reaching the mandibular angle, the lower side potentially reaching the sternum, and the sides potentially reaching the posterior margin of the sternocleidomastoid muscle [1]. Giant thyroid tumors pose significant challenges for anesthesia and surgical treatment due to their large size and potential complications [2]. Patients with giant thyroid tumors may show symptoms such as tracheal compression [3], malignant transformation of tumors, extrinsic glandular invasion [4], and local or systemic metastasis. Comprehensive preoperative evaluation is crucial, with special emphasis on airway assessment and evaluation of disease extension in the cervical and thoracic areas. Computerized tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans of the neck and chest can provide sufficient information about the tumor. Here, we combined tumor imaging information with three-dimensional (3D) medical imaging to enable visualization for preoperative assessment, guiding successful surgical planning. The report describes three cases of giant thyroid tumor resection performed at Liaocheng People’s Hospital from January 2024 to December 2024 using this technology.

Case reports

Case 1

A 58-year-old Chinese man was admitted to the hospital with dyspnea and a neck mass that had enlarged progressively over 17 years. The patient had a history of “encephalitis” as a child, after which he developed a speech disorder and difficulty moving the right leg. Physical examination showed a large mass on the right side of the neck, with compression of the trachea to the left. The mass was firm, with a smooth surface and had reasonable motion. The size of the mass was about 12 × 10 × 10 cm, with no obvious enlargement of lymph nodes in the neck or clavicle region (Fig. 1A). Cervicothoracic enhanced CT showed a large, mostly cystic, low-density mass on the right neck, with a macular high-density lesion within, and uneven enhancement after contrast enhancement. The mass, approximately 10.6 × 9.5 × 12.1 cm in size that showed calcification of the envelope and margin and was closely associated with the right thyroid gland, with compression and displacement of the right neck (Fig. 1B). Before surgery, 3D images of the patient’s neck were obtained with a 3D visualization simulation system (Fig. 2) to determine the blood supply of the tumor and compression and displacement of the trachea and to perform preoperative airway assessment. During the operation, the blood vessels nourishing the tumor were found using the 3D images and were severed to reduce bleeding. The right lobe of the thyroid was removed completely (Fig. 3). After removal of the tumor, the tracheal rebound occurred without tracheal malacia, and the tracheal intubation was removed smoothly. The postoperative pathologic diagnosis was a benign thyroid tumor with cystic changes and flaky necrosis. The patient was discharged from the hospital 3 days after surgery and was treated with oral Euthyrox® replacement therapy.

Case 2

The patient was a 60-year-old Chinese female who had been diagnosed with a giant goiter 2 years before, with the development of dyspnea after movement 1 year later. Physical examination showed asymmetry of the neck, with the presence of a hard mass (7 × 6 × 4 cm in size) on the right lobe of the thyroid gland. The mass had no clear boundary, the surface was not smooth, and the movement of the mass was poor. Hard nodules were apparent in the middle and lower parts of the left lobe of the thyroid, with sizes of approximately 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 cm. The boundaries of the nodules were not clear, the surfaces were smooth, and the motion was reasonable. Neck and chest enhanced CT showed an enlarged nodule in the right lobe of the thyroid, measuring approximately 6.8 × 6.4 × 5.9 cm. Local calcification was observed, the enhanced scan was uneven, and the trachea was compressed, with narrowing and displacement (Fig. 4). Bilateral thyroid nodules were pathologically examined by fine-needle aspiration before surgery, and the results indicated bilateral papillary thyroid carcinoma. Three-dimensional visualization images were made of the CT data (Fig. 5) to determine the relationships between the tumor and the surrounding organs, the presence of tracheal and esophageal invasion, and the condition of the cervical lymph nodes, and to guide and plan the surgery. Total thyroidectomy was performed during the operation to remove the central lymph nodes. The pathological results confirmed the diagnosis of papillary thyroid carcinoma in the left lobe of the thyroid, with a tumor size of 1.1 cm × 0.9 × 0.5 cm. The papillary thyroid carcinoma in the right lobe was of the follicular subtype, with a tumor size of 2.8 × 2.5 × 1.8 cm, no capsule involvement, and no typical nerve invasion. Metastatic cancer was found in the central lymph nodes, with metastasis in two of eight lymph nodes. The patient was discharged from the hospital 3 days after surgery. During the follow-up period, oral Euthyrox® replacement therapy was given, and the thyroid function index was good.

Case 3

The patient was a 60-year-old Chinese female who had undergone a chest CT examination due to dyspnea after exercise, leading to the identification of a giant goiter more than 2 months before. Neck and chest CT showed a slight shift of the upper mediastinum to the right, and the trachea and esophagus were also compressed to the right. The left thyroid gland was significantly enlarged, with a low-density focus and a lower margin reaching the thoracic cavity. The tumor had dimensions of 4.4 × 3.6 × 9.6 cm and was located in the middle mediastinum, with several of the lower pole glands located in the anterior mediastinum and sized approximately 3.0 × 2.3 cm (Fig. 6). The thyroid nodule underwent pathological examination after fine-needle aspiration before surgery; the results showed that it was benign and consistent with chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis. Three-dimensional visualization images were made of the CT data (Fig. 7) to determine the status of the tumors located behind the sternum and guide the surgery. During the operation, a low-neck arc incision was made to expose the left thyroid gland, and the substernal struma was found to be divided into cystic structures. After the extraction of 60 mL of dark red fluid, the volume of the substernal struma was significantly reduced. The preoperative 3D images were compared with the actual situation during the surgery to confirm that the tumor and the left thyroid gland had been completely removed (Fig. 8). Three days after surgery, the patient was discharged from the hospital and received oral Euthyrox® replacement therapy with good thyroid function during follow-up.

Discussion

Giant thyroid tumors have become relatively rare due to advancements in both society and medical care. Most of these tumors are found in patients with long-standing disease who have neglected to seek prompt medical treatment, resulting in necrosis of the tumor tissue, bleeding, malignancy, and the development of obvious symptoms of compression, leading to hospital admission. Of the three patients with giant thyroid tumors described here, one had chronic illness associated with a degree of mental deficiency, one had a malignant tumor, and one was a patient with substernal struma, all being typical cases of giant thyroid tumors.

Surgery is the primary treatment for giant thyroid tumors and can relieve the compression and displacement of the trachea and esophagus. However, this surgery is difficult [5]. On the one hand, the very large size of the tumor makes it difficult to expose during the operation, and damage to surrounding organs can easily occur. On the other hand, the tumor typically has a good blood supply and bleeds easily, adversely affecting the clarity of the operating field. Third, the tumor compresses the trachea, complicating the smooth progress of tracheal intubation. Fourth, due to tracheal compression and softening, postoperative extubation is difficult, and tracheotomy and tracheostomy may be necessary. Fifth, malignant transformation of the tumor, as well as adhesion to surrounding organs and tissues, increases the probability of damage to other organs.

Therefore, detailed disease assessment before surgery is necessary for surgical safety. Preoperative fine needle aspiration followed by cytological assessment is helpful for disease diagnosis. In addition, preoperative neck and chest CT can provide detailed information on the disease; however, CT is a tomographic imaging modality that requires the reader to have good spatial imagination. The use of 3D medical imaging can make up for the lack of spatial imagination in the film readers, as well as having many significant advantages in surgery for giant thyroid tumors, as described below.

Simulation of the surgical process to improve surgical safety

Three-dimensional images enable surgeons to perform accurate preoperative planning, allowing clear visualization of the 3D structures hidden in the bones and soft tissues to avoid damage to vital organs. Although traditional two-dimensional (2D) imaging techniques (such as CT, MRI, and ultrasound) can provide basic anatomical information, it is difficult for them to provide a the visual presentation of the actual shape of the tumor, together with invasion of the boundary and surrounding tissues [6]. For example, in our three cases, we could understand the relationships between the tumors and the trachea and esophagus before surgery, thereby reducing the likelihood of damage to vital organs. At the same time, we could determine the source of the tumor’s blood supply, thereby reducing intraoperative bleeding and ensuring the smooth completion of the operation.

Provision of real-time monitoring during surgery and shortening of the duration of the operation

The application of the mobile platform enables viewing of 3D images of the patient on a mobile phone, allowing the doctor to adjust the surgical strategy according to the actual situation. For example, in the patient with substernal struma, puncture of the cystic mass behind the sternum and fluid withdrawal led to a significant reduction of the tumor volume and lifting of the tumor tissue of the middle mediastinum, enabling removal of the tumor tissue of the anterior mediastinum and avoiding splitting of the sternum. The reconstruction of 3D images and the simulation of the surgical operation enhance the skill of the surgeon and reduce the length of the operation.

Improvement of patient outcomes and quality of life

Due to the more precise surgery and reduced recovery time, patients recover more quickly and remain in the hospital for less time. Furthermore, the use of 3D imaging technology provides better protection for normal tissue and reduces postoperative complications, thereby improving the patients’ quality of life.

Facilitation of doctor–patient communication

Doctors can provide a visual representation of the surgical site and surgical plan through three-dimensional images, assisting patients to understand the surgical process and enhancing their confidence in the operation.

The three cases of giant thyroid tumors described in this paper represent individual cases where 3D medical imaging technology was utilized. We believe that, with the establishment of a Big Data platform and the development of artificial intelligence (AI) technology, 3D medical imaging technology will be used effectively in the field of thyroid surgery. For example, before surgery, through Big Data analysis, AI can provide a preliminary surgical proposal based on the 3D image, including the location of the lesion and the surgical path, enabling manual adjustment of the surgical path and resection scope, and the formation of a personalized surgical plan. During surgery, the 3D medical imaging technology can be combined with current robotic thyroid surgery [7] or endoscopic thyroid surgery [8]. By analyzing surgical videos, AI can identify surgical steps, assess the technical level of the operator, and provide feedback in real-time to improve the accuracy and safety of the surgery. After surgery, AI can evaluate the surgical effect, the degree of lesion resection, the extent of surgical trauma, and other factors, by comparing the preoperative and postoperative 3D images, as well as generating surgical reports and completing postoperative monitoring and evaluation [9].

In addition, to obtain the optimal neck CT image information, we used neck enhancement scans for all patients requiring 3D reconstruction, enabling accurate collection of image information of the lesion area through dynamic monitoring of the horizontal level in the middle of the neck. During the scanning process, the phase control was set to simultaneous development of the neck arteries and veins to ensure clear contrast between the tumor and the surrounding vascular structure at the same time point. This imaging method could effectively evaluate the blood supply of the tumor and the anatomical relationships between the tumor and important organs of the neck and provide accurate image guidance for both preoperative planning and intraoperative performance, improving the safety and success rate of the surgery.

Conclusion

In summary, the application of 3D medical imaging technology to the surgery of giant thyroid tumors can markedly enhance the accuracy, safety, and efficiency of the operation, as well as improve patient outcomes and prognosis. However, it was also found that CT images are unable to show the locations of the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands. Therefore, the effects of these methods on reducing loss of the recurrent laryngeal nerve and the non-resection rate of the parathyroid glands require verification in a larger number of cases. It is expected that, in the near future, 3D medical imaging technology can be combined with Big Data platforms and AI technology to enhance the effectiveness of thyroid surgery.
