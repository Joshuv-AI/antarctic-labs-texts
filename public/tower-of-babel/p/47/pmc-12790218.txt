Eclipsed Severe Mitral Regurgitation Without Obstructive Coronary Artery Disease
Authors: D.R. Tomassoni, T. Yeung, A.W. Teh, C. Ford
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIschemic secondary mitral regurgitation (MR) from left ventricular remodeling and geometric mitral valve changes is well recognized. Rarely, severe MR occurs with preserved left ventricular systolic function.Case SummaryA 70-year-old man presented with chest pain, global ischemic electrocardiography changes, and pulmonary congestion. During an anginal episode, transthoracic echocardiography revealed transient posterior-wall hypokinesis and torrential MR, both resolving as symptoms subsided. Invasive coronary angiography demonstrated moderate coronary artery disease in the left anterior descending artery, with negative resting full-cycle ratio. Intracoronary acetylcholine provocation testing confirmed coronary vasospasm; microvascular dysfunction was excluded. Beta-blocker therapy was ceased, and calcium-channel blocker therapy was up-titrated with good effect, improving the patient's angina and dyspnea.DiscussionOnly 6 previous studies have described cases of severe secondary MR from vasospastic angina resulting in leaflet tenting.Take-Home MessagesEclipsed MR is rare and may be under-recognized. In the current case, treatment of coronary vasospasm resulted in marked symptomatic improvement.

History of Presentation

A 70-year-old man presented with 1 week of progressively worsening, nitrate-responsive chest pain and dyspnea, accompanied by fluid overload with biventricular heart failure signs. Lower limb edema predominated, with additional signs of interstitial edema on chest x-ray accompanied by intermittent oxygen requirement. Salient electrocardiographic features: global ST-segment depression with aVR ST elevation.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, hypertension, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, peripheral vascular disease, gout, and right-sided below-knee amputation after a motor vehicle accident.

Differential Diagnosis

At this point, the differential diagnosis included acute coronary syndrome, coronary artery vasospasm, or microvascular angina.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography as work-up for pulmonary edema coincided with a further episode of recurrent chest pain and dyspnea. This revealed torrential mitral regurgitation (MR) with a visual coaptation defect, posterior wall hypokinesis, and severe pulmonary hypertension (right ventricle systolic pressure: 95 mm Hg), which resolved spontaneously with the resolution of chest pain (Figure 2). The episode was self-limiting, and no nitrates were administered. Repeat echocardiography 5-minutes after chest pain resolution demonstrated only mild MR. Initial coronary angiography (Figure 3) showed moderate nonobstructive coronary artery disease in the left anterior descending and right coronary arteries. Subsequent to this, repeat angiography and functional assessment demonstrated negative resting full-cycle ratios (0.95-1.00), with positive acetylcholine provocation testing for coronary vasospasm. Management

Initial therapy involved intravenous heparin infusion during transfer to a tertiary hospital with a cardiology service. After assessment and heart team discussion, beta-blockers were discontinued in favor of intensified antispasmodic therapy given demonstrable vasoreactivity, which included amlodipine 10 mg twice daily, isosorbide mononitrate 120 mg daily, and nicorandil 10 mg twice daily. Other pre-existing therapies, including rivaroxaban, aspirin, furosemide, pantoprazole, and allopurinol, were continued.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient achieved significant clinical improvement with the adjusted therapeutic regimen. He was discharged with cardiology follow-up and ongoing medical management. At the 8-month follow-up, he remained well (Canadian Cardiovascular Society score: 1 and NYHA functional class I).

Discussion

Eclipsed MR associated with transient myocardial ischemia due to coronary vasospasm was first described in 2008, and it represents a rare clinical entity that can manifest dramatically despite normal left ventricular systolic function.1 It is a subtype of secondary MR (also known as type 3 functional MR) described by Breen et al2 whereby intermittent coronary artery vasospasm causes global myocardial ischemia and apical valvular tenting, resulting in incomplete leaflet coaption. The MR resolves parallel to resolution of coronary vasospasm. This is contrasted to other subtypes that can develop concurrent with left bundle branch block, causing desynchrony (type 1) or a left ventricular load-dependent mechanism (type 2).2 Within the literature, there have been 6 reports on clinically similar cases.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 There is only 1 other published case of vasospasm-associated transient paroxysmal mitral regurgitation seen outside of the postmenopausal female population.1

Unique aspects of this case include the dynamic echocardiographic finding of torrential MR occurring exclusively during symptomatic episodes of myocardial ischemia and completely resolving with symptom relief. The transient nature of this hemodynamic change can lead to diagnostic uncertainty and inappropriate treatment if not correctly identified, thus highlighting the need for prompt and thorough echocardiographic assessment of patients presenting with chest pain and pulmonary edema. This may serve as a reminder that echocardiography and invasive coronary angiography are complementary diagnostic modalities. Similar to previous studies,2 dynamic echocardiography during episodes of chest pain was crucial for the diagnosis, as standard imaging performed in asymptomatic intervals may fail to reveal the transient severity of the MR. Particular signs on echocardiography to prompt consideration of eclipsed MR include regional left ventricular hypokinesis, restricted mitral leaflet opening, triangular MR continuous-wave Doppler, and markedly elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, which are all dynamic with symptoms.

Additionally, this case underscores the importance of a thorough evaluation for coronary vasospasm, including provocative testing in symptomatic patients without significant obstructive coronary disease. These investigations serve to identify a trigger event, which should be considered alongside other factors involved in the multifaceted eclipsed MR pathogenesis. Further to the vasospastic trigger, contributions for eclipsed MR in this case included severe left atrial enlargement7 and mitral annulus dilatation the latter resulting in a small coaption reserve.8

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of recognition of eclipsed MR due to coronary vasospasm, which is essential to guide appropriate management. Transthoracic echocardiography during angina episodes, acetylcholine provocation testing, and vasodilator antianginal therapy are all useful in this setting.
