Deep learning-based artificial intelligence can improve the diagnosis of small bowel obstruction: stratified comparison study and hierarchical Bayesian model
Authors: Aitaro Takimoto, Yuichiro Hayashi, Kazuki Nishida, Liu Jiahui, Chiyoe Shirota, Masahiro Oda, Akinari Hinoki, Kojiro Suzuki, Kensaku Mori, Hiroo Uchida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate diagnosis of small bowel obstruction (SBO) is critical to patient outcomes, particularly in the emergency department (ED). To enhance diagnostic precision, we developed an artificial intelligence (AI) technology that automatically extracts dilated intestinal segments from contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) images. 158 contrast-enhanced CT examinations containing 5,200 annotated images were used for deep learning, and the potential utility of AI in improving SBO diagnosis was subsequently evaluated by residents and surgeons. CT images from 30 patients with suspected SBO in the ED were used as a test set. Seventeen residents and ten surgeons were divided into two groups, one interpreting images with AI support and the other without AI. Participants assessed the presence of SBO and identified the obstruction location, and diagnostic time was recorded. A hierarchical Bayesian model was applied for analysis. The median precision, recall, and Dice score of the AI model were 0.98, 0.63, and 0.77, respectively. For both residents and surgeons, the correct diagnosis rate of the obstruction location was significantly higher in the AI-assisted group compared with the non-AI group (74.1% vs. 56.7% and 88.2% vs. 66.2%, respectively; P < 0.0001 and P = 0.0001). Among residents, AI support significantly improved the diagnosis of obstruction location (odds ratio: 4.20; 95% credible interval: 2.14–8.26) and reduced reading time by 26.84 s per case (95% credible interval: −50.37 to − 2.83). These findings indicate that AI technology is clinically feasible and can improve diagnostic accuracy while reducing the time required for diagnosing bowel obstruction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57999-w.Keywords: Small bowel obstruction, Emergency department, Artificial intelligence, Computed tomographySubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Small bowel obstruction (SBO) is a time-sensitive and complex condition requiring diagnosis and treatment. SBO constitutes a predominant abdominal emergency, with approximately 15–20% of surgical hospitalizations attributed to bowel obstruction1,2. A substantial proportion of patients with SBO seek care in the emergency department (ED), with a considerable proportion (approximately 93.5%) requiring hospitalization and 20–45% require surgical intervention3–6. Furthermore, mortality rates among patients with ED-presenting SBO are documented at 1.9–6.5%3,7. Multiple studies have shown that the primary management of patients from the ED to admission affects outcomes. Delayed treatment is correlated with increased mortality and complication rates8. Hwang et al. reported a median time of 45 min from ED admission to initial assessment and 265 min to surgical consultation for patients with SBO9. Aquina et al. demonstrated that primary management by medical service was a risk factor for postoperative outcomes when compared with surgical service10. They attributed this finding to differences in postgraduate training in SBO training. These publications also revealed that many patients with SBO presenting to the ED were initially managed by physicians who were unfamiliar with SBO. For these physicians, interpreting computed tomography (CT) images can be a significant burden. The management of SBO is largely determined by the accuracy of imaging-based diagnosis, especially in CT11. In this context, the imaging diagnosis of SBO involves more than just the identification of the presence of a dilated bowel. A precise diagnosis also involves the evaluation of the underlying cause of the obstruction. Specifically, it is crucial to localize and assess the site of obstruction in order to guide appropriate management.

Novel technology has been devised to enhance CT imaging diagnostics for bowel obstruction12–14. This technology employs artificial intelligence (AI) to autonomously extract the intestinal lumen from CT images and generate a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction that facilitates interpretation. Diagnostic assistive technologies utilizing CT images have shown substantial progress in recent years. However, no reports have documented assistive technologies that specifically cater to the diagnosing of intestinal obstruction15,16. This study was a comparative analysis to measure the efficacy of assistive diagnostic imaging for bowel obstruction. The central hypothesis is that employing AI technology leads to an increased rate of accurate determination of the obstruction location and reduced reading time among residents.

Methods

The institutional ethics committee reviewed and approved the study protocol (Ref No. 2022 − 0188). Written informed consent was obtained from all the participating physicians. This study followed the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD). This study conforms to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki17.

Development of AI to assist diagnostic imaging and quantitative evaluation

We constructed an AI to segment the intestinal lumen region by using contrast-enhanced CT images. The AI extracted the intestinal luminal regions from the CT image and colored them with a rainbow color (red to blue) gradation from the proximal to the distal sides. The AI then reconstructed the extracted intestinal region into a 3D image (Fig. 1). The utilization of AI technology is detailed in Video 1.

We previously reported this technique12–14. The details of our AI structure are shown in Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplemental Methods.

The CT data of the SBO cases were collected independently from the test sampled from two tertiary referral hospitals. CT data were obtained using two CT systems (Canon Medical Systems Aquilion 64-TSX-101 A/NA and SIEMENS Somatom Definition AS+). Two surgeons with more than 10 years of experience annotated the intestinal lumen region in the images and generated ground-truth images from 158 contrast-enhanced CT examinations containing 5,200 annotated images. The annotation protocol and consensus criteria were standardized through repeated discussions with AI engineers. Among 600 images from ten cases of SBO, precision, recall, and the Dice score were measured for the quantitative evaluation of AI performance after training. The details of the quantitative evaluation are described in Supplemental Methods.

Study design

All participants completed both the preliminary and main tests on the same day, at the same location, using the same computer. Participants were recruited through an open call for volunteers. The participants included residents and surgeons with more than 100 cases of experience, who received guidance on how to complete the test and use the device. The residents and surgeons were divided into two groups: one group read the images using AI technology (AI group), and the other group read the images without AI technology (manual group). The residents took a preliminary test and were divided into two groups with equal reading ability based on the results. Surgeons were randomly assigned to two groups.

Preliminary test

Residents read contrast-enhanced CT images of eight cases of bowel obstruction, each indicating the location of the obstruction and the time required for diagnosis. The time limit for each case was set to 3 min. The number of correct answers for obstruction location and reading time was ranked, and the residents were divided into two groups such that the rank sum was equal.

Main test

CT image data were obtained from patients who visited the ED of two tertiary referral centers between June 6 and July 26, 2018, and were suspected of having SBO based on medical history, physical examination, and imaging studies such as abdominal radiography and non-contrast CT, and for whom contrast CT was performed. The AI group read all the cases using the new technology, whereas the manual group read all the cases without it. The same test sets were used for both the AI and manual groups. The participants indicated whether they had identified bowel obstruction, the location of the obstruction, and the reading time. The time limit for each case was set to 3 min. The primary endpoint was the correct response rate for the obstruction location, whereas the secondary endpoints were the reading time, correct diagnosis rate (bowel obstruction or not), and correct response rate for the obstruction location in cases of bowel obstruction. The diagnosis of small bowel obstruction and the location of the obstruction were independently determined based on contrast-enhanced CT images by two surgeons with experience in managing more than 300 cases of bowel obstruction. In cases of disagreement, the final diagnosis was determined by an additional experienced surgeon. In surgically treated patients, operative findings were reviewed when available to confirm the obstruction location. The details of the test protocol are summarized in Supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

Our primary analysis uses a hierarchical Bayesian model. However, for sample size determination, we adopted a conventional method to compare the group proportions. With an α of 0.05 and a power of 0.90, 454 readings (227 per group) were needed. Detailed methods of sample size calculation are described in Supplemental Methods.

Participant characteristics and performance in both the preliminary and main tests are summarized as follows: continuous variables are presented using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables are described by frequencies and proportions. For comparisons, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, and the Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables.

In our primary analysis, to explore the influence of AI support on the diagnostic accuracy among residents, we adopted a hierarchical Bayesian logistic regression model. This model incorporated the fixed effect of AI support, the fixed effect of preliminary test scores, random effects of the individual diagnostic ability of each resident, and random effects of the inherent difficulty of each image. For secondary analysis, we utilized a hierarchical Bayesian linear regression model to investigate the impact of AI support on the time taken for image diagnosis among residents. This model incorporated the fixed effect of AI support, the fixed effect of preliminary test scores, random effects accounting for variability in reading time across different residents, and random effects based on image difficulty. Beyond the primary and secondary analyses of residents, we also examined the impact of AI support on the diagnostic accuracy and reading time of surgeons.

All the models were constructed using the “rstanarm” package in R. The reliability of the models was verified by assessing their convergence using trace plots. Further details and the R code for the hierarchical Bayesian models are provided in the Supplementary Material. A coefficient was considered significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not include zero. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.0.

Results

Quantitative evaluation of AI performance

To assess the feasibility of this AI model, the Dice score was calculated (Supplementary Table S1). Among the ten cases of SBO, the median Dice score was 0.77 (range: 0.71–0.81). Notably, the median precision was high at 0.98 (range: 0.96–0.99) compared to the median recall of 0.63 (range: 0.55–0.70). Subsequently, the potential utility of AI in improving bowel obstruction diagnosis was examined.

Participant overview and preliminary study

Seventeen emergency medicine residents and 10 surgeons participated in this study. The affiliations of residents and surgeons are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Among the surgeons, five had 5–10 years of experience, while the remaining five had more than 10 years of experience. A flowchart of the study is presented in Fig. 2. The 17 residents were divided into an AI group of nine participants and a manual group of eight based on the results of the preliminary study (Table 1). In the preliminary test, the percentage of correct obstruction locations was similar between the two groups (55.6% vs. 64.1%; P = 0.382). The median reading time for the preliminary test was 107.0 s/case in the AI group and 113.0 s/case in the manual group (P = 0.892).

Case selection

Contrast-enhanced CT imaging data for 33 cases of suspected bowel obstruction, obtained between June 6 and July 26, 2018, were collected from two tertiary referral centers. Three cases without sufficient clinical findings to suspect bowel obstruction were excluded by the case acceptance/rejection panel, leaving a test sample of 30 cases. Among the 30 cases, 27 had small bowel obstruction, including 5 cases that required surgical intervention, while 3 cases did not have small bowel obstruction. Of the three patients without bowel obstruction, two had small bowel inflammation and one had cholecystitis.

Basic performance outcomes

Residents’ performance

Among the 17 emergency medicine residents, the diagnostic accuracy for bowel obstruction was comparable between the AI and manual groups (90.7% vs. 92.9%; P = 0.422) (Table 1). In the non-obstructed cases, the AI group correctly diagnosed 63.0% of the cases, whereas the manual group correctly had a higher percentage (75.0%). For identification of the obstruction location, the AI group outperformed the manual group across all cases (74.1% vs. 56.7%, P < 0.0001) and when limited to 27 cases of bowel obstruction (75.3% vs. 54.6%, P < 0.0001). Additionally, the AI-assisted residents had a significantly shorter median reading time of 75.0 s/case compared to 104.5 s/case in the manual group (P < 0.0001).

To investigate how baseline diagnostic ability influences the performance-enhancing effect of AI, we examined the progression of results between the preliminary test and main test among residents in the AI group (summarized in Supplementary Table S3). Among the nine residents assigned to the AI group, eight showed improved accuracy in identifying the obstruction location compared with their pretest performance. In contrast, the resident with the highest pretest score did not demonstrate further improvement with AI assistance. The effect of AI was particularly pronounced among residents who were less experienced in interpreting images for bowel obstruction.

Surgeons’ performance

Ten surgeons were randomly divided into two groups. The diagnostic accuracy was slightly lower in the AI group (93.3% vs. 98%; P = 0.085), although the difference was not significant. The percentage of correctly diagnosed non-obstructed cases was 93.3% in the AI and manual groups. Among the ten incorrect diagnoses in the AI group, nine involved cases of bowel obstruction were misdiagnosed as non-obstructions (compared to two in the manual group). Of these nine errors, five were made by a single surgeon. A case-by-case analysis revealed that in one particular instance, three out of five surgeons in the AI group misdiagnosed the condition, whereas no such errors were observed in the manual group. This specific case exhibited marked fecal retention in the colon and rectum in addition to small bowel dilation proximal to the obstruction. The AI correctly depicted all the dilated intestinal segments. The percentage of correct obstruction locations in all cases and the percentage of correct obstruction locations limited to bowel obstruction cases were significantly higher in the AI group than in the manual group (P = 0.002, 0.001). There was no significant difference in reading time (median 53.0 s/case vs. 61.5 s/case; P = 0.095).

Impact of AI on resident diagnostics

Notably, the influence of AI support is significant (Fig. 3). The coefficient for AI support stood at 1.43 with a 95% CI of 0.76–2.11, which translated to an odds ratio (OR) of 4.20 with a 95% CI of 2.14–8.31 (Fig. 4). This robust evidence underscores the marked improvement in diagnostic accuracy owing to AI assistance. When juxtaposed with the coefficient for Pretest_Score (0.415), the magnitude of the effect of AI support was akin to a boost in Pretest_Score for approximately three questions. Additionally, the time for diagnosis of residents was curtailed by 26.68 s/case, with a 95% CI of −50.29 - −3.03, indicating a significant time-saving advantage.

Impact of AI on surgeon diagnostics

Although the primary emphasis of our study was residents, it is worth noting that surgeons also benefited from AI support, albeit to a lesser extent (Supplementary Figure S2). The coefficient of AI support was 0.973 with a 95% CI of 0.25–1.72, yielding an OR of 2.64 with a 95% CI of 1.27–5.65 (Figure S3). However, the difference in reading time was a reduction of 8.74 s/case, with a 95% CI of −29.07–11.36, which was not statistically significant (Supplementary Figure S3).

The convergence of all models was ascertained via a trace plot and is omitted here for brevity (Supplementary Figure S4).

Discussion

This study represents a pioneering effort to validate the utility of AI-based imaging technology for SBO diagnosis. This novel technology has demonstrated a certain level of effectiveness in improving both the accuracy and efficiency of bowel obstruction diagnosis. The development of AI-based imaging technologies is progressing worldwide18–25. However, there are few studies on intestinal segmentation, which remains a central topic of interest. This study uniquely targeted “intestinal fluid retention and dilatation,” the hallmark characteristics of intestinal obstruction, and successfully segmented the intestinal tract by isolating the intestinal contents. A notable advantage of this approach is its resistance to the extensibility of the intestinal wall, contrast variance, and presence of surrounding ascites. Simultaneously, this method is useful in diagnosis because the endpoint of intestinal dilation serves as an indicator of the location of the obstruction. An exceptional feature of this technology is its ability to reconstruct an extracted intestinal tract in 3D images by employing graduated colors for visualization. This enables immediate comprehension of the overall arrangement of the intestinal tract, facilitating rapid identification of obstruction locations based on color differentiation. Rather than navigating blindly, as is often the case, residents can now follow the dilated intestinal tract to its endpoint to pinpoint the location of the obstruction.

The current model demonstrates a strong performance in terms of precision and the Dice scores; however, recall remains suboptimal. Upon further investigation of instances where the model failed to make predictions on intestinal segments, it appears that it was unable to detect non-dilated intestines. Given that this model was designed to address intestinal obstruction, it was primarily tailored to identify dilated intestinal segments, which explains this limitation to some extent. Because precision and recall often exhibit a trade-off, the current settings were considered acceptable within the context of this study. Nonetheless, to improve the overall performance, future iterations of the program should be refined to accurately detect non-dilated intestines. Enhanced training on non-dilated intestines may improve the adaptability of AI technology to applications beyond SBO.

As a preliminary step toward implementing AI technology in the emergency room, this study was designed using a dataset of patients who visited the emergency room. The dataset consisted of patients who visited the emergency departments of two tertiary referral centers during a fixed study period, were clinically suspected of having SBO, and underwent contrast-enhanced CT. Therefore, the diagnostic performance reported in this study does not reflect only obvious SBO cases, but rather reader performance in a clinically relevant population of patients suspected of having SBO in the emergency setting. Acknowledging the varied reading proficiencies of the residents, a preliminary test categorized them into two groups with equivalent abilities. Hierarchical Bayesian modeling was used to assess the impact of AI within this intricate study of design.

Remarkably, this technique showed dual improvements in both the reading time and diagnostic accuracy. Although originally aimed at aiding residents unfamiliar with CT interpretation, this study effectively demonstrated the relevance of this technology not only for residents but also for experienced surgeons. The rate at which residents correctly diagnose SBO was the same with or without AI. However, considering that they often failed to accurately diagnose the location of the obstruction, it’s uncertain if the residents’ diagnoses were truly reliable. The same can be said for surgeons. To diagnose SBO accurately, the exact location of the obstruction must be identified. Although participants in the manual group showed a decrease in the accuracy of the obstruction location diagnosis from the preliminary test to the main test, diagnostic accuracy improved in the AI group. This suggests that the implementation of AI technology enhances the diagnostic capabilities of users. Therefore, AI imaging seems to be very useful in this regard.

Although the study sample size was fair, it did not include all bowel obstruction patterns. The study sample did not include images of bowel obstruction with necrosis, which is associated with a high mortality rate that requires emergency surgery. Five cases (17%) of bowel obstruction with closed loops, which also required surgical indication, were included, and the proportion was similar to that reported in previous studies3,8,9.

In this evaluation, the effect of the AI was not constant from sample to sample. In most samples, AI had a positive effect; however, it misled the users in a few cases. Although there were no significant differences, there were scattered cases in which trainees diagnosed a non-SBO as an obstruction; the AI extracted the lumen even if it was not dilated. In cases of non-SBO, residents may have mistakenly identified the normal intestinal tract as dilated, even though there was no obviously dilated intestinal lumen. Because this tendency was not observed in surgical specialists, it was thought to be overcome by user experience and learning. Users must be fully aware of the characteristics of AI technology. Residents should limit the indications to patients with suspected bowel obstructions.

Another unique challenge is that there were scattered instances of surgical specialists diagnosing obvious bowel obstruction as a non-bowel obstruction when using AI. Detailed analysis revealed two key findings. First, AI does not necessarily provide intuitive benefits to all surgeons. Second, in cases of significant fecal retention or intestinal dilation in the colon or rectum, AI-driven extraction of the intestinal tract may lead to confusion. The phenomenon in which residents over-identify non-dilated intestinal segments and the tendency of surgeons to focus on dilation in the colon or rectum may share a common cause: the AI system equally extracted regions that physicians would typically deprioritize unconsciously. In the context of this study, where no patient physical findings or additional test results were provided, this characteristic of AI manifested as a negative influence.

This study had several limitations. The performance of the manual group declined from the preliminary test to the main test. This decline may have been influenced by decreased motivation or fatigue, and therefore the possibility of residual bias cannot be fully excluded. Furthermore, the design of the main test differed from routine clinical practice, which may have affected participant performance and the generalizability of the findings. Participants were not provided with any patient data other than the imaging data used in this study. Additionally, no time limit exists in practice. The most severe cases of SBO, such as necrosis, were not included in the test sample. Furthermore, the impact of the AI system on non-SBO cases was not adequately assessed. And well-experienced radiologists and emergency physicians were not included in this study. Given these limitations, further studies involving multidisciplinary participants and more diverse clinical cases are necessary to validate the utility of this AI system across a broad spectrum of clinical settings. It was also found that there is room for improvement in AI technology. The low recall rate, while partly attributable to its tuning specifically for the diagnosis of bowel obstruction, may be improved through further advancements in machine learning. The relatively low recall may increase the risk of missing mildly dilated bowel segments and atypical presentations of small bowel obstruction. This concern is further compounded by the limited representation of non-SBO cases in the test dataset. The current clinical applicability of this AI system may be limited primarily to typical SBO cases with evident bowel dilatation. Therefore, this AI system should not be used as a screening tool for all acute abdominal conditions. Additionally, although infrequent, there were certain cases where AI appeared to cause confusion among physicians. This issue is not related to the accuracy of the AI itself. However, it has the potential to enhance usability by refining the methods of image rendering and color coding.

This study focuses on the process of diagnosing bowel obstruction by residents. As this study did not extend to patient outcome evaluation, cautious interpretation of the findings is warranted. While AI facilitates accurate diagnosis of the location of the obstruction, further research is necessary to evaluate its impact on decisions regarding surgical or non-surgical management of patients, surgical planning, time to operating room entry, and the necessity of bowel resection.

In conclusion, AI-based imaging technology for bowel obstruction is useful for helping residents and surgeons accurately diagnose the location of bowel obstruction. It also reduced the resident reading time for diagnostic imaging of bowel obstruction. While AI has shown significant benefits for residents and surgeons, it occasionally leads to errors due to over-reliance or misinterpretation. Further refinement in AI training and usability is needed to optimize performance and adaptability to broader clinical scenarios, including decision-making and surgical planning.
