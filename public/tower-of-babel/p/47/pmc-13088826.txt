Molecular mechanisms of ABA-mediated cold stress response in Dendrobium officinale
Authors: Jian Wang, Rong Wang, Qingxia Liu, Yan Zhang, Mingyang Zhou, Sha Yang, Tao Yang, Ronghui Gu, Deqiang Ren, Qingwen Sun, Jie Zou, Xia Meng, Jingzhong Chen, Yuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDendrobium officinale, a valuable medicinal plant, is highly susceptible to cold stress, which particularly affects the accumulation of secondary metabolites. Abscisic acid (ABA) is a key hormone regulating plant responses to cold stress; however, the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying exogenous ABA-mediated cold tolerance in D. officinale remain largely unknown. In this study, we investigated the effects of exogenous ABA application under cold stress on physiological and biochemical traits, gene expression profiles, and the accumulation of major medicinal components in D. officinale seedlings.ResultsAt the physiological and biochemical level, exogenous ABA treatment significantly increased biomass accumulation and relative water content (RWC) in D. officinale under cold stress. It also elevated endogenous ABA levels, reduced stomatal conductance, and thereby enhanced water retention capacity. Compared with cold stress alone, plants subjected to combined ABA and cold treatments exhibited significantly lower malondialdehyde (MDA) content and electrolyte leakage, indicating reduced membrane lipid peroxidation. In addition, proline accumulation was promoted and chlorophyll degradation was alleviated. At the molecular level, transcriptomic analysis revealed the differential expression of multiple cold-responsive genes (e.g., DREB1, SKIP30, AP2-3, APRR9, Ubc12) as well as key genes involved in ABA biosynthesis and signaling pathways (e.g., NCED1, NCED2, PP2C50, SnRK1, PYR1-2). Furthermore, both cold stress and exogenous ABA treatment significantly modulated the expression of genes associated with polysaccharide and alkaloid biosynthesis, thereby promoting the accumulation of these medicinal compounds.ConclusionsExogenous ABA application effectively alleviates cold stress-induced growth inhibition in D. officinale by enhancing endogenous ABA accumulation and inducing stomatal closure. Moreover, ABA treatment mitigates cold-induced membrane damage and regulates osmotic adjustment through the modulation of compatible solutes and secondary metabolites. These findings provide new insights into ABA-mediated signal transduction under cold stress and offer a theoretical basis for the development of cold-tolerant D. officinale cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08477-y.

Introduction

Dendrobium officinale is a perennial herbaceous plant belonging to the genus Dendrobium in the family Orchidaceae and is mainly distributed in southern China [1]. Modern research has shown that D. officinale contains a variety of bioactive compounds, such as polysaccharide, alkaloid, and flavonoid. These components exhibit diverse pharmacological activities, including antitumor, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and blood pressure-regulating effects, and play a pivotal role in pharmacology research and clinical applications [2, 3]. However, excessive harvesting of wild D. officinale, combined with its low reproductive capacity and slow growth rate, has resulted in wild resources being insufficient to meet increasing market demand. Consequently, commercial demand is now largely satisfied through wild-simulated cultivation of D. officinale [4]. Nevertheless, D. officinale has stringent environmental requirements, and its quality and yield are highly susceptible to adverse environmental stress, particularly cold stress. This sensitivity poses significant challenges to wild-simulated cultivation practices [5]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the cold stress response of D. officinale is essential for guiding molecular breeding strategies aimed at improving cold tolerance.

Cold stress is a major abiotic factor that severely restricts plant growth and development. With the increasing frequency of extreme climate event, cold stress has profoundly affected plant geographical distribution and growth patterns worldwide [6, 7]. To adapt to low-temperature environments, plants have evolved complex physiological and molecular regulatory network. Under cold stress, plants initiate a cascade of physiological responses, including activation of the antioxidant defense system, which leads to enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes to scavenge excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8]. Meanwhile, cell membrane integrity is compromised, as reflected by increased relative electrical conductivity and MDA accumulation, both of which intensify with increasing cold injury severity. In addition, plants enhance cold tolerance by accumulating osmolytes, such as proline and soluble proteins, which help maintain cellular osmotic balance and structural stability [9, 10]. At the molecular level, the ICE-CBF-COR signaling cascade plays a central role in plant cold stress responses [9]. In this pathway, the C-repeat binding factor (CBF), a member of the dehydration-responsive element binding factor (DREB) subfamily, is activated by the upstream transcription factor (TF) inducer of CBF expression (ICE) under cold stress conditions. Activated CBF subsequently induces the expression of downstream cold-responsive (COR) genes, thereby enhancing plant cold tolerance [11].

ABA is a crucial hormone for cold stress resistance in plants, with both its biosynthetic and signal transduction pathways responding to cold stress. The ABA biosynthesis pathway mainly involves enzymes such as zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP), aldehyde oxidase (AAO), and 9-cis-epoxy-carotenoid dioxygenase (NCED), among which NCED is considered the rate-limiting enzyme [12–14]. Cold stress has been shown to significantly induce the expression of OsNCED4 in rice, mutation of OsNCED4 results in reduced cold tolerance, likely due to decreased ABA accumulation and excessive ROS production under cold stress [15]. ABA signaling transduction under cold stress primarily operates through the PYL-ABA-PP2C signal module. Upon ABA perception, PYL receptors binds ABA and interacts with protein phosphatase 2 C (PP2C), thereby inhibiting PP2C activity and releasing sucrose non-fermenting-1-related protein kinase 2 (SnRK2) from repression. Activated SnRK2 subsequently regulates downstream TFs, such as ABA-responsive element-binding proteins (AREB) and ABA-binding factors (ABF), thereby enhancing cold tolerance [16]. Additionally, ABA signaling activates SnRK2.6, which phosphorylates TFs (such as AREB, ABF and RAV1), and ion channels (such as SLAC1), promoting stomatal closure to reduce water loss and alleviate cold-induce damage [17–19]. Previous studies have shown that exogenous ABA application increases endogenous ABA content and suppressing cold-induced MDA accumulation, thereby mitigating cellular damage [20, 21]. Moreover, exogenous ABA has been reported to upregulate the expression of key cold-responsive genes, including SnRK2.6, ICE2, and CBF1, under cold stress conditions [22]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which exogenous ABA regulates cold stress responses in D. officinale remain largely unexplored.

Secondary metabolites play a crucial role in enhancing plant resistance to cold stress and are also important indicators of medicinal plant quality [23]. Cold stress can significantly alter the accumulation of secondary metabolites, including polysaccharide, alkaloid, and flavonoid [24]. For instance, polysaccharide content in rice has been shown to increase significantly after 6 days of cold stress, accompanied by differential expression of key genes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis, such as ARF3, ARF11, ARF15, GH3.8, HXK7, SPS1, and SUS2 [23, 24]. In Dendrobium nobile, polysaccharide content increase by 56.18% after 6 days of exposure to 0 °C, with genes such as TPP6, PGMP, and BAM3, as well as TFs including bHLH, MYB, and WRKY, playing regulatory roles [25]. Similarly, Dendrobium huoshanense incubated at 4 °C for 7 days exhibited increased polysaccharide accumulation, with multiple differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in polysaccharide synthesis and metabolism [26]. Polysaccharides are a key active ingredient of D. officinale, and previous studies have reported significant increases in polysaccharide content in both stems and leaves under cold stress at 4 °C [27]. Alkaloids represent another important class of bioactive component in D. officinale and their composition and content have been systematically characterized, mainly including pyrrole, terpenoid, indole, and quinazoline alkaloids [28, 29]. Alkaloid biosynthesis primarily involves the shikimate, mevalonate (MVA), and methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathways, with key regulatory genes such as AACT, HMGR, DXS, and DXR playing critical roles [30, 31]. Environmental factors, including cold stress, have been shown to promote alkaloid accumulation, accompanied by the upregulation of genes such as TDC and STR [32, 33]. In addition, hormonal regulation, particularly by exogenous ABA, has been demonstrated to significantly influences secondary metabolites biosynthesis, including alkaloid [34]. However, the combined regulatory effects of cold stress and exogenous ABA on polysaccharide and alkaloid biosynthesis in D. officinale remain poorly understood.

Transcriptomics analysis provides a powerful approach for elucidating molecular mechanisms, and previous studies have successfully identified numerous genes and regulatory networks associated with plant responses to abiotic stresses and exogenous hormone treatments [30, 35–38]. Nevertheless, the transcriptome-level regulatory mechanism underlying exogenous ABA-mediated cold stress response in D. officinale have not yet been reported. Therefore, in this study, we systematically investigated the role of exogenous ABA in regulating cold stress response of D. officinale at physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic levels. We identified key genes associated with cold stress and ABA signaling, as well as critical genes and regulatory pathways involved in polysaccharide and alkaloid biosynthesis, which are directly linked to the medicinal value of D. officinale. These findings provide a solid theoretical basis for cold-tolerant breeding and deepen our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying cold stress adaptation in D. officinale.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The D. officinale cultivar used in this study was obtained from the germplasm resource nursery of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Tissue culture seedlings (8 months old) maintained in tissue culture bottles were transplanted to plastic pots filled with granulated pine bark and acclimatized in an artificial climatic chamber for one month. The growth conditions in the artificial climatic chamber were set as follows 22 ℃, a 12/12 h light/dark photoperiod, photosynthetic photon flux density of 60 µmol·m-2·s-1, and relative humidity of 60%-70%. After acclimatization, uniform and vigorously growing seedlings were selected for cold stress and exogenous ABA treatment experiments. These seedlings were subjected to the following four treatments: (1) control (22 °C), (2) cold stress (4 °C), (3) exogenous ABA spraying (100 µmol·L- 1), and (4) combined cold stress (4 °C) and exogenous ABA spraying (100 µmol·L- 1). For the control and cold stress-only treatment, plants were sprayed with distilled water, whereas ABA-treated plants were sprayed with 100 µmol·L- 1 ABA solution. All treatments were maintained for 5 days. The control and ABA-only treatment groups were maintained in the artificial climatic chamber under the conditions described above, while the cold stress-only and the combined cold stress and ABA treatment groups were placed in a chamber set at 4 °C, with all other environmental parameters kept identical. Leaves and stems were collected from seedlings in each treatment group and designated as follows: control (leaf: CK-L; stem: CK-S); cold stress (leaf: Cold-L; stem: Cold-S); ABA treatment (leaf: ABA-L; stem: ABA-S); combined treatment (leaf: Cold-ABA-L; stem: Cold-ABA-S). All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analyses. Three independent biological replicates were performed for each treatment.

Determination of physiological and biochemical indicators

Stomatal conductance of D. officinale leaves at the same developmental position was measured under different treatments using a Li-6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) between 8:00 to 11:00 a.m. Measurements were conducted under standardized conditions: light intensity of 800 µmol·m-2·s- 1, atmospheric CO2 concentration of 400 µmol·mol-1, leaf chamber temperature of 22 °C, and relative humidity of 65%.

Endogenous ABA content was measured as previously described [39]. Approximately 300 mg of fresh seedlings tissue was collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground to powder. ABA content was analyzed using UHPLC-MS/MS.

Biomass and RWC of leaf and stem samples were measured with slight modifications to a previously described method [40]. To determine biomass, samples were dried in a 70 °C oven to constant weight. RWC was calculated using the saturated water weight method according to the following formula: (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{RWC=}\frac{\mathrm{(FW-DW)}}{\mathrm{(TW-DW)}}\times\mathrm{100}$$\end{document}%), where FW, DW, and TW represent fresh weight, dry weight, and turgid weight, respectively.

MDA and proline contents were quantified using the thiobarbituric acid method and the acidic ninhydrin method, respectively. Measurements were performed using a multifunctional microplate reader (Synergy 2, BioTek, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions (Boxbio, Beijing, China).

Electrolyte leakage was measured following a previously described method with minor modifications [39]. Chlorophyll content was determined using an ethanol-acetone (1:2, v/v) extraction method. Absorbance at 645 nm and 663 nm was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader (Synergy 2, BioTek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Boxbio, Beijing, China).

Determination of polysaccharide and total alkaloid content

Polysaccharide and alkaloid contents in leaves and stems of D. officinale under different treatments were determined using the phenol-sulfuric acid method and bromocresol green method, respectively. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm for polysaccharides and 416 nm for alkaloids following the manufacturer’s instructions (Boxbio, Beijing, China).

RNA extraction and sequencing

Frozen leaf and stem samples stored at -80 °C were used for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using 2×CTAB extraction buffer (Zhongke Ruitai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity, concentration, purity, and RQN values were assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and Agilent 5300 (Agilent, USA). The mRNA was isolated using oligo (dT) magnetic beads (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). RNA fragmentation, library construction, and PCR amplification were performed according to the RNA-seq protocol [41]. Sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (PE150) at Beijing Majorbio using the NovaSeq Reagent Kit.

Sequence data analysis

Raw paired-end reads were processed using fastp software with default parameters to remove low-quality reads and adapters, generating high-quality clean data (BioProject accession: PRJNA1262384) [42]. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 [43]. Based on the reference genome, mapped reads for each sample were assembled using StringTie [44]. The resulting unigenes were annotated by BLASTX alignment (E < 0.00001) against multiple databases, including NCBI non-redundant protein sequences (Nr), Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Swiss-Pro, Pfam 32., and String 10.0 databases.

Differential expression analysis and functional enrichment

Transcript abundance was calculated according to the TPM (the transcripts per million reads) method, and gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM [45]. Differential expression analysis was performed using DEGseq, with genes satisfying |log2 fold change (FC)|≥1 and FDR < 0.001 considered as significantly DEGs [46]. In addition, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using Goatools and Python SciPy, respectively, with Bonferroni-corrected P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant [47, 48].

RT-qPCR validation

Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green I (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a CG-05 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Heal Force, Shanghai, China). Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer Premier 6.0. D. officinale Actin was used as an internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Supplementary Table 1) [3].

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Statistics, USA). Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on three biological replicates.

Results

Exogenous ABA treatment alleviated cold stress phenotype in D. officinale

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying ABA-mediated responses to cold stress, D. officinale seedling were subjected to cold and exogenous ABA treatments. Phenotypic observations showed that cold stress caused visible leaf wilting in some seedlings, whereas exogenous ABA treatment alone induced leaf yellowing and wilting. In contrast, plants subjected to the combined cold and exogenous ABA treatment exhibited no obvious phenotypic differences compared with the control group (Fig. 1A). Biomass measurements showed that both cold stress and ABA treatments alone significantly reduced leaf and stem biomass relative control, whereas no significant difference was observed between the Cold-ABA treatment and the control (Fig. 1B). Although RWC decreased under all three treatments compared with in the control, the Cold-ABA treatment maintained significantly higher RWC than either cold stress or ABA treatment alone in both leaves and stems (Fig. 1C).

Exogenous ABA regulates physiological characteristics associated with cold stress resistance

Endogenous ABA content increased moderately under cold stress (36.9 ng/g) compared with the control (13.7 ng/g), but remained significantly lower than that observed in the ABA-only (5201.9 ng/g) and Cold-ABA (4751.7 ng/g) treatments (Fig. 2A). Consistent with these changes in hormone levels, stomatal conductance decreased significantly under all three treatments, with the greatest reduction observed in the Cold-ABA treatment (Fig. 2B).

To evaluate cellular membrane damage, MDA content was measured as an indicator of lipid peroxidation. MDA content increased significantly in both leaves and stems under all three treatments (Fig. 2C). However, MDA levels in the Cold-ABA treatment were significantly lower than those observed under cold stress or ABA treatments alone, indicating reduced membrane lipid peroxidation. A similar trend was observed for electrolyte leakage: although electrolyte leakage increased under all stress conditions, it was lowest in the Cold-ABA treatment and highest in the cold stress treatment (Fig. 2D). Proline, an important osmotic regulator, accumulates rapidly in both leaves and stems under cold stress, and exogenous ABA application further enhanced proline accumulation under cold conditions (Fig. 2E). Chlorophyll content declined significantly under all treatments compared the control, with the lowest chlorophyll content observed in the ABA-only treatment. Notably, the Cold-ABA treatment alleviated chlorophyll loss, resulting in significantly higher chlorophyll content than that observed under cold treatment alone (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these results demonstrated that exogenous ABA enhances cold stress tolerance in D. officinale by coordinately regulating stomatal conductance, membrane stability, osmolyte accumulation and chloroplast protection, thereby mitigating cold-induced physiological damage.

Significant accumulation of polysaccharides and alkaloids in D. officinale

Polysaccharides and alkaloids are major bioactive constituents of D. officinale (Fig. 3A). Measurements revealed that polysaccharide and alkaloid contents in both leaves and stems were significantly higher under all three treatments compared with the control (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, the combined cold and ABA treatment exhibited a strong synergistic effect, resulting in significantly greater accumulation of both polysaccharides and alkaloids than either cold stress or ABA treatment alone. In addition, alkaloid accumulation displayed clear tissue specificity, with higher levels detected in leaves than in stems, whereas polysaccharide content showed no significant tissue-dependent difference.

GO and KEGG classification of DEGs

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying exogenous ABA-mediated cold stress responses, transcriptome analyses were conducted across all treatment group, and detailed sequencing and assembly information is provided in Supplementary Table 2. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation among the four treatments in both leaves and stems, while biological replicates clustered closely together, indicating high reproducibility and reliability of the transcriptomic data (Fig. 4A, C). Using stringent criteria (|log2FC|≥1 and FDR) < 0.001), a total of 48,647 DEGs were identified, including 23,520 known genes and 25,127 novel genes. In leaves, the four treatments collectively exhibited 20,457 DEGs (52.96%), with Cold-L exhibiting the highest number of unique DEGs (2,384; 6.17%), while Cold-ABA-L contained the fewest (1,541; 3.99%). In stems, the four treatments collectively exhibited 22,381 DEGs (55.76%), among which Cold-S showed the greatest number of unique DEGs (2,252; 5.61%), whereas Cold-ABA-S exhibited the fewest (1,371; 3.42%) (Fig. 4B, D).

TF analysis revealed that the identified DEGs were predominantly enriched in the WRKY, NAC, MYB, ERF, and bHLH families (Supplementary Table 3). Comparative analysis across 10 pairwise comparison groups revealed that, in five leaf comparison groups, the number of downregulated genes generally exceeded that of upregulated genes. However, in the Cold-ABA-L vs. Cold-L comparison, upregulated genes outnumbered downregulated genes. Across all leaf comparisons, significantly upregulated genes (e.g., NAC, ADO3, APRR9, KLP, RGA2, FEN, ASR1, LHT1, and SRO3) were more numerous than significantly downregulated genes (e.g., CHLM, EP3, and GDH2) (Fig. 4E-I). In the five stems comparison group, no significantly difference was observed between the total numbers of upregulated and downregulated genes. However, significantly downregulated genes were numerically predominant, including MAG2, DEAD-box, RGA2, KLP, TTC, CHK, RSE1, MFP, UGT, REVEILLE1, COL9, DHN4, RLK, and HPT (Fig. 4J-N).

GO enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs from all ten comparison groups were significantly enriched in cellular component and molecular function categories. The most overrepresented GO terms included “cellular anatomical entity,” “intrinsic component of membrane,” “integral component of membrane,” “protein serine kinase activity,” “purine ribonucleoside triphosphate binding,” “nucleotide binding,” and “ADP binding” (Supplementary Fig. 1).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further identified key signaling and metabolic pathways involved in cold stress and ABA responses. “Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism” and “phosphatidylinositol signaling system” were enriched in nine out of ten comparison groups, with Cold-ABA-S vs. Cold-S as the only exception. Additional significantly enriched pathways included “plant hormone signal transduction,” “MAPK signaling pathway-plant,” “plant-pathogen interaction,” and “ribosome.” Comparative analysis of enriched DEGs revealed that, in leaves, Cold-ABA-L vs. CK-L showed the highest number of enriched pathways, significantly surpassing those observed in Cold-L vs. CK-L and ABA-L vs. CK-L. Among all leaf comparisons, Cold-ABA-L vs. ABA-L also exhibited relatively high enrichment, whereas Cold-ABA-L vs. Cold-L displayed the lowest enrichment. A similar trend was observed in stems, where Cold-ABA-S vs. CK-S contained significantly more enriched DEGs than Cold-S vs. CK-S and ABA-S vs. CK-S. Moreover, Cold-ABA-S vs. Cold-S exhibited more enriched DEGs than ABA-S vs. CK-S, while Cold-ABA-S vs. ABA-S showed fewer enriched DEGs (Fig. 5).

Expression characteristics and interaction network of cold-resistance genes

Cold-responsive signaling pathways play a crucial role in responses to abiotic stress. In this study, key DEGs associated with cold stress response pathways in D. officinale were identified, including DREB1/2/3, SKIP30, HSF, MYB44/73, ERF109, and COLD1. Notably, SKIP30, MYB44, and ERF109 were significantly induced by cold stress in the Cold-L and Cold-ABA-L groups of D. officinale leaves, whereas the expression levels of DREB1/2 and MYB73 were markedly upregulated in the Cold-S and Cold-ABA-S groups of stems (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. 2). Genes with similar functions or those involved in the same signaling pathways often form complex interaction networks. Therefore, interaction network analyses were performed for DREB1 and SKIP30. The results showed that DREB1 interacted with AP2-3, SBT1.3, SBT2.5, MYBP, NFYB5, and VP1 (Fig. 6B), whereas SKIP30 interacted with BHLH49, CUL1, CUL3A, CUL3A-2, HSK1, HSK2, APRR9, RBCMT-2, RBX1A, and Ubc12 (Fig. 6C). Correlation analysis further revealed that DREB1 exhibited a strongly positive correlation with AP2-3, while SKIP30 was strongly correlated with APRR9 and Ubc12 (Fig. 6D, E; Supplementary Fig. 3, 4). Collectively, these findings suggest that DREB1, AP2-3, SKIP30, APRR9, and Ubc12 may coordinately participate in key signaling pathways mediating underlying cold stress responses in D. officinale.

Expression characteristics and interaction network of genes in ABA biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways

ABA is a key hormone regulating cold stress resistance in D. officinale. DEGs involved in ABA biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways were identified, including NCED1/2, P450, PYL8-3, SAPK7, SnRK, PP2C, and ABF3. In leaves, PYL8-3, SnRK3, and PP2C8 were significantly induced by both cold stress and exogenous ABA in the Cold-L, ABA-L, and Cold-ABA-L groups, whereas in stems, SnRK1, SnRK3, SnRK5, SnRK6, PP2C50, and PP2C8 were markedly upregulated in the Cold-S, ABA-S, and Cold-ABA-S groups (Fig. 7A; Supplementary Fig. 5). Interaction network analyses were conducted for NCED1, PP2C50, and SnRK1. The results revealed that NCED1 interacted with NCED2, NCED3, ZEP-4, AAO3, ABA2, Dhn4, PP2C6, PSY-2, PSY, and PP2C5, and strongly correlations were observed between NCED1 and NCED2, as well as Dhn4 (Fig. 7B, E; Supplementary Fig. 6). PP2C50 interacted with multiple ABA receptors and kinases, including PYL1, PYL2, PYL3, PYL8, PYL8-3, PYR1, PYR1-2, SAPK3, SAPK10-2, and SAPK10-3, with a strong correlation detected between PP2C50 and PYR1-2 (Fig. 7C, F; Supplementary Fig. 7). SnRK1 interacted with MAPKKK18, PDPK1, ATG1, and UVR8, and exhibited a strongly correlation with UVR8 (Fig. 7D, G; Supplementary Fig. 8). Taken together, these results indicated that NCED1, NCED2, and Dhn4 are key genes involved in ABA biosynthesis, while PYR1-2, PP2C50, and SnRK1 play critical roles in ABA signal transduction during cold stress responses in D. officinale.

Differential expression and interaction network of genes in alkaloid biosynthetic pathways

Alkaloid biosynthesis in D. officinale involves the shikimate, MVA, and MEP pathway, as well as multiple alkaloid-specific branching pathways, ultimately leading to the production of tropane, isoquinoline, indole and terpenoid alkaloid (Fig. 9). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that genes in these pathways exhibited pronounced tissue-specific expression patterns under different treatment. In the shikimate pathway, key genes such as DHS, DHQS, SKDH, and CS showed no significant changes in leaves, whereas in stems, DHS, DHQS, and CS were upregulated, with the highest expression observed under Cold-ABA-S treatment. In the MVA pathway, genes such as HMGS, HMGR, and PMK were downregulated in leaves but significantly upregulated in stems treated with exogenous ABA, with PMK-1 showing the most pronounced expression in exogenous ABA-treated stems. Within the MEP pathway, genes such as DXS, CMK, MCS, HDS, HDR, IPI, and FPS exhibited diverse regulatory patterns. In leaves, MCS and HDS were upregulation across all treatments, with DXS and MCS significantly induced by cold stress, while HDS was significantly upregulated under ABA-L and Cold-ABA-L conditions. Although HDR, IPI, and FPS were generally downregulated, IPI showed significant induction under ABA-L treatment. In stems, CMK and HDR were upregulated under ABA-S and Cold-ABA-S treatments, with CMK showing significantly higher expression in Cold-ABA-S. Within alkaloid-specific biosynthesis branches, genes such as AOC3-1, TDC, AnPRT, and IGPS were upregulated in leaves; whereas genes including AOC3-3, TR-I1, TDC, AS, and PAI were predominantly upregulated in stems. These results indicate that alkaloid biosynthesis in D. officinale is coordinately regulated by cold stress and ABA in a tissue-specific manner.

Discussion

D. officinale is an important medicinal plant that is widely recognized for its therapeutic value [1]. However, cold stress severely inhibits the growth and development of D. officinale, thereby triggering a series of physiological and biochemical responses that ultimately affect the accumulation of its active components [49]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the phytohormone ABA plays a key role in regulating plant growth, development, and responses to abiotic stresses. Exogenous ABA treatment can strengthen plant tolerance to cold stress by regulating endogenous ABA levels and associated physiological and biochemical processes [50, 51]. Cold stress directly suppresses plant growth by reducing biomass accumulation and RWC, whereas exogenous ABA application can mitigate these detrimental effects [52–54]. In this study, cold stress caused substantial physiological damage to D. officinale, as evidenced by significant decreases in biomass and RWC; however, these reductions were effectively mitigated by exogenous ABA application (Fig. 1B, C). A rapid increase in endogenous ABA levels under cold stress represents a critical adaptive response, acting as a “stress messenger,” that initiates plant defense programs. This accumulation of ABA leads to reduced stomatal conductance, thereby limiting transpiration and water loss and maintaining tissue hydration [55–57]. Consistent with this mechanism, endogenous ABA content increased and stomatal conductance decreased in D. officinale under cold stress, and these effects were further amplified by exogenous ABA application (Fig. 2A, B). This result is consistent with previous studies, indicating that exogenous ABA promotes the accumulate of endogenous ABA, induce stomatal closure, and enhances cold stress tolerance. Such responses form a positive feedback loop, whereby enhanced stress signaling further promotes ABA accumulation, resulting in more pronounced stomatal closure [58, 59].

Concurrently, cold stress induced a series of additional physiological changes, including the accumulation of membrane damage indicators such as MDA and electrolyte leakage, as well as osmotic regulators such as proline [60]. ABA has been shown to reduce MDA and electrolyte leakage while promoting proline accumulation, thereby protecting membrane integrity and increasing osmotic adjustment under cold stress [55, 61]. In agreement with these findings, cold stress elevated MDA content and electrolyte leakage in D. officinale, indicating membrane damage, whereas exogenous ABA application effectively attenuated these increases (Fig. 2C, D), consistent with previous studies [62]. Additionally, cold stress induced proline accumulation in D. officinale, and proline contents were further elevated under combined cold and ABA treatment (Fig. 2E). This finding aligns with previous studies showing that exogenous ABA enhances proline biosynthesis under cold stress, thereby improving stress tolerance [63]. Chlorophyll content, a key indicator of photosynthetic capacity, is also critical for plant survival under cold stress [64]. In this study, cold stress significantly accelerated chlorophyll degradation in D. officinale leaves, whereas exogenous ABA effectively alleviated this degradation (Fig. 2F), thereby protecting photosynthetic capacity. Taken together, D. officinale responds to cold stress by regulating stomatal movement, accumulating osmoregulatory substances, and modulating membrane permeability. Exogenous ABA enhances cold tolerance through multiple mechanisms, including the promotion of stomatal closure, attenuation of lipid peroxidation-induced membrane damage, and maintenance of osmotic equilibrium, thereby preserving membrane integrity. These findings provide a physiological foundation for further investigation of ABA-mediated regulatory mechanisms underlying cold stress responses in D. officinale.

Cold stress responses in D. officinale are regulated through the coordinated action of functional and regulatory genes, including DREB, ICE, CBF, bZIP, MYB, and HSF [35]. In this study, transcriptomic analysis identified multiple DEGs involved in cold stress signaling pathways, including DREB1/2/3, SKIP30, HSF, MYB44/73, ERF109, and COLD1 (Fig. 6A). Among these genes, DREB1 exhibited particularly strong induction under cold stress, with the most pronounced upregulation observed in leaves (Fig. 6A). This result is consistent with previous findings showing that 21 out of 24 LpDREB1 genes in Lolium perenne were significantly upregulated under cold stress [65]. Heterologous overexpression of LpDREB1H2 enhanced cold tolerance in Arabidopsis, and overexpression of DREB1 genes similarly improved cold tolerance in rice and Arabidopsis [66, 67]. DREB genes expression is also significantly influenced by ABA signaling. For example, several SiDREB genes in Setaria italica and DREB genes in tomatoes were significantly induced by exogenous ABA treatment [68]. In D. officinale, exogenous ABA upregulated DREB1 expression; however, under combined cold ang ABA treatment, DREB1 expression was lower than that observed under cold stress alone. Under intensified stress conditions, DREB-related signaling pathways may become suppressed, resulting in reducing signal generation and transmission [69]. The AP2/ERF TF family, which includes the AP2, ERF, DREB, and RAV subfamilies, play a central role in plant responses to cold, drought, and salt stresses. AP2 and DREB members share high functional similarity and often exhibited coordinated expression under abiotic stress conditions [70, 71]. Protein interaction analysis identified AP2-3 as a gene highly correlated with DoDREB1 (Fig. 6B, D), and both genes were upregulated under cold stress and ABA-mediated cold responses, suggesting coordinated regulatory roles.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the SKIP gene family participates in ABA-mediated responses to multiple abiotic stresses. For instance, the SKIP17 gene in Dendrobium is induced by cold stress, SKIP genes in tobacco enhance tolerance to mannitol and NaCl, and rice SKIP genes are upregulated under drought and salt stress [72–74]. In this study, SKIP30 expression was induced by cold stress, but this induction was attenuated by exogenous ABA application, indicating that SKIP30 is a key component of the cold stress response whose expression is finely regulated by the ABA signaling.

Cytokinin signaling also plays an important role in cold stress responses. The APRR gene family regulates cytokinin signaling and is induced by cold stress [75, 76]. Ubc gene encode ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes that are essential for plant growth, development, and stresses responses [77]. Previous studies have reported cold-induced upregulation of Ubc genes in peanut, maize, and Arabidopsis [78, 79]. Consistent with these finding, APRR9 and Ubc12 were significantly upregulated under cold stress in D. officinale (Fig. 6G). Both genes were strongly correlated with SKIP30 and jointly participated in the cold stress response network (Fig. 6E). Overall, these results indicate that key regulatory genes, including DREB1, AP2-3, SKIP30, APRR9, and Ubc12, form an integrated molecular network that play a central role in mediating cold stress responses in D. officinale.

ABA biosynthesis and signaling pathways are critical for plant adaptation to cold stress [80, 81]. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that genes involved in ABA biosynthesis and signaling, including PP2C, SnRK, NCED, ABF, and P450 were induced by cold stress and exogenous ABA, with notably strong induction of NCED1, PP2C50, and SnRK1 (Fig. 7A). NCED is the key rate-limiting enzyme in ABA biosynthesis and plays a pivotal role in cold stress responses [82]. Cold stress and exogenous ABA treatment have been shown to induce NCED expression in rice, raspberry, and sweet osmanthus, while overexpression of NCED genes enhances cold tolerance and ABA accumulation in Arabidopsis [15, 83, 84]. In D. officinale, cold stress significantly upregulated NCED1 and NCED2 expression; however, under combined cold and ABA treatment, their expression levels were lower than those under cold stress alone. This reduction may reflect a negative feedback mechanism in which plants suppress endogenous ABA biosynthesis to prevent excessive ABA accumulation under high ABA conditions [85].

The PYR/PYL-PP2C-SnRK signaling module constitutes the core ABA signal transduction pathway in response to cold stress. Cold-induced ABA accumulation promotes ABA binding to PYR/PYL receptors, which inhibits PP2C activity and activates SnRK2 kinases, thereby regulating downstream TFs such as ABF, AREB, and ABI [16, 86]. In D. officinale, PP2C50 and SnRK1 were upregulated under cold stress and exogenous ABA treatment, with stronger induction observed in stems than in leaves. However, combined treatment attenuated their expression under cold stress, suggesting feedback regulation of ABA signaling under high ABA condition [85]. A strong correlation between PP2C50 and PYR1-2 was observed, indicating coordinated regulation within the ABA signaling pathway. Similar regulatory patterns have been reported in Eutrema, where cold stress and ABA treatment downregulated PYR expression while upregulating PP2C [87]. Collectively, these findings confirm that ABA is a central regulator of cold stress resistance in D. officinale, and that genes such as PYR, PP2C, SnRK, and NCED play essential roles in mediating ABA-dependent cold tolerance.

Polysaccharides are key bioactive compounds in D. officinale, conferring antitumor, antioxidant, and anti-aging effects [88]. Plant polysaccharide accumulation is highly responsive to cold stress, and significant increases have been reported in rice and tea plants under low-temperature conditions [89, 90]. In the present study, cold stress and exogenous ABA treatment promoted polysaccharide accumulation in D. officinale (Fig. 3B). Genes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis, including SUS, SPS, UGP, NI, and GTs, play crucial regulatory roles in D. officinale [91, 92]. Transcriptomic analysis identified multiple polysaccharide-related genes (SUS, SPS, UGP, NI, GTs, PMM, and GMPP) that were differentially expressed under various treatments (Fig. 8). Among these genes, NI4 and GT6 showed particularly strong expression responses (Supplementary Fig. 9A). NI catalyzes sucrose hydrolysis, increasing glucose and fructose availability for polysaccharide biosynthesis. Previous studies have shown that the NI1 gene is closely associated with polysaccharide accumulation in D. officinale stems, which contain high polysaccharide levels [93, 94], and similar expression patterns have been reported in polysaccharide-rich rhizomes of Polygonatum cyrtonema [95]. In this study, NI4 exhibited high expression in stems under control conditions and was further induced under cold stress and ABA treatment.

GTs catalyze the transfer of monosaccharides from sugar nucleotide donors to polysaccharide backbones, ultimately forming complex polysaccharide structures [96]. GT gene expression was higher in stems than in leaves, consistent with higher polysaccharide content in stems [97]. Notably, GT6 expression was strongly induced by cold stress and ABA treatment, particularly in polysaccharide-rich stems. SUS is a key enzyme in sucrose metabolism and catalyzes reversible reactions between sucrose and UDP. Most SUS genes in D. officinale exhibit higher expression in stems than in leaves, and in this study, SUS4 showed strong stem-specific expression and significant induction under abiotic stress conditions [98]. Collectively, these results suggest that SUS4, NI4, and GT6 are key genes regulating polysaccharide biosynthesis in D. officinale under cold stress, likely through ABA-mediated pathways.

Alkaloids are important medicinal constituents of D. officinale, with significantly higher content in leaves than in stems, a pattern likely associated with tissue-specific expression of key biosynthetic genes [31, 91]. Consistent with previous findings, alkaloid content was higher in leaves than in stems in this study. Genes such as MVK, HDS, CMK, and HDR were predominantly expressed in leaves, whereas genes including DXS, DHS, DHQS, and SKDH showed higher expression in stems, indicating functional specialization of alkaloid biosynthesis between tissues (Fig. 9). Abiotic stresses and exogenous hormones are known to influence alkaloid accumulation, and both cold stress and ABA treatment have been shown to promote alkaloid biosynthesis [99–102]. Consistent with these findings, cold stress and exogenous ABA particularly in combination significantly increased alkaloid content in D. officinale (Fig. 3C), suggesting that enhanced alkaloid accumulation contributes to stress adaptation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that alkaloid biosynthesis-related genes, including DHS, DXS, DMGS, CS, MVK, UGTs, DDC, ALDH, and PPO, respond dynamically to abiotic stress and hormonal regulation [30, 103–107]. Our study further reveals that several of these genes, including DHS, DHQS, SKDH, AS, PPO, MVK, DXS, HDS, and CMK exhibited distinct expression patterns under different treatments, highlighting complex regulatory control of alkaloid biosynthesis under cold stress. In summary, D. officinale likely enhances cold stress tolerance by regulating the expression of key genes involved in alkaloid biosynthesis, thereby promoting alkaloid accumulation. The ABA signaling pathway appears to play a central role in coordinating this response under cold stress conditions.

Conclusions

In summary, this study combined physiological analyses with transcriptomics profiling to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which exogenous ABA mediates the response of D. officinale to cold stress. Physiologically, exogenous ABA protects the plant by reducing water loss through decreased stomatal conductance and alleviating cell membrane damage, as evidence by lower MDA content and reduced electrolyte leakage, thereby maintaining biomass. In addition, exogenous ABA enhances osmotic adjustment and photosynthetic performance, as indicated by regulated proline accumulation and mitigated chlorophyll degradation. At the molecular level, both cold stress and exogenous ABA activate cold and ABA responsive signaling pathways, including genes involved in ABA biosynthesis. Simultaneously, they promote polysaccharide and alkaloid accumulation by upregulating key genes involved in their respective biosynthetic pathways.
