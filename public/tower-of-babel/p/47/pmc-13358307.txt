Knowledge and misconceptions regarding choking and first-aid procedures among the general population in the West Bank, Palestine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ahmad Hamammdi, Mohammad F Zhour, Fares Ahmad Melhem, Shaymaa N S Tamimi, Qusai Belbaisi, Mohanad S O Jaber, Bashar Yaser Hasan Awad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess the level of knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding choking first aid among the general population in the West Bank.DesignA descriptive cross-sectional study.SettingWest Bank, Palestine (web-based survey).Participants454 Palestinian adults aged 18 years and above.Primary outcome measuresGeneral knowledge concerning signs of choking, misconceptions and proper first-aid manoeuvres (such as the Heimlich manoeuvre and back blows) was assessed. Analysis was performed using SPSS V.26.ResultsA cumulative total of 454 participants took the online survey. Although 55.7% of the participants defined choking accurately, there was still room for improvement in the actual management of this condition. Fewer participants (46.7%) could recognise the picture of the Heimlich manoeuvre and only 14.8% could state the appropriate management for vulnerable groups such as pregnant patients. Harmful misunderstandings existed, with 26% of participants endorsing the blind finger sweep and 32.8% offering water to a choking victim. Multivariable analysis revealed that higher knowledge scores were independently predicted by having a medical background (p=0.007) and previous formal training in first aid (p=0.019).ConclusionsThe general public in this region has shown moderately good theoretical knowledge about choking but lacks competence in practical first aid training and has a remarkably low level of safe practices. There is an urgent need for organised community-based training programmes for first aid in an attempt to bring down morbidity and mortality from choking.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation, Health Education, Health Literacy, PUBLIC HEALTH, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Background

Choking, alternatively referred to as foreign body airway obstruction (FBAO), is a medical emergency in which hypoxic brain injury or death can result within minutes unless the problem is quickly and correctly treated by a bystander.1 FBAO has been considered to be one of the leading causes of accidental deaths worldwide, across all age groups, but young children and older adults are especially vulnerable due to various anatomical and physiological factors that predispose these age groups to aspiration events.1,3 On the international scene, global estimates continue to indicate choking as one of the leading preventable causes of unintentional injury-related deaths, especially among children under 5 years of age.3 4

In children, about 80% of cases of choking are attributed to those who are under 3 years due to under-developed eating and swallowing techniques, the size of their airway, curiosity and mobility.5 The most common causes are:

Food items such as nuts, seeds, grapes, hard candies and chunks of fruit or vegetables.3 5 These items are retained in the teeth.

Small things, including toys, balloons, marbles and other household articles, which are quite common causes of aspiration in children.3

In adults, choking is frequently associated with eating and particularly high-risk foods, such as meat, being the typical causative factor,1 6 with the elderly, particularly the chronically ill, being significantly at risk. US statistics demonstrate that choking was the causative factor in 21.6% of deaths in people aged ≥65 years in large numbers, particularly in relation to neurological and psychiatric illness, such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, schizophrenia, stroke and mood disorder.7 In psychiatric inpatient groups, the incidence of choking is even greater, with the relative risk of choking being 3.4 times higher, and the re-choking incidence rate up to 40%.8

The result of a choking incident is extremely dependent on the first few minutes of management, and the intervention of laypersons—who usually are the only witnesses in most instances—is literally a matter of life and death.2 Studies indicate that early institution of correct manoeuvres like back blows, abdominal thrusts or Heimlich manoeuvre, chest thrusts for infants and undertaking of cardiopulmonary resuscitation when indicated can vastly improve the outcome.9 10 Conversely, incorrect or late interventions may cause significant worsening of obstruction and diminish the hope for a successful outcome.10

Multiple studies worldwide have demonstrated substantial gaps between perceived and actual first-aid competence among the general population. Research from Saudi Arabia showed that knowledge of choking first aid was inconsistent, with only 61% of adults correctly identifying the steps for relieving infant choking and less than two-thirds demonstrating adequate knowledge overall.11 12

Similarly, Syrian adults were found to have largely insufficient knowledge concerning the management of choking, despite most having passed through earlier training on first aid concepts.13 School-based studies also indicate that among adolescents, knowledge of the signs of choking and the pertinent manoeuvres is still poor, with many unable to appreciate partial from complete airway obstruction or identify age-appropriate interventions correctly.4

In the Middle Eastern region, although some studies have examined the general level of first-aid knowledge among the population, very few studies specifically focus on choking first-aid. In Palestine, training in first aid is offered by some non-governmental organisations (NGOs) like the Palestinian Red Crescent Society; nonetheless, no study has been conducted to evaluate the level of public awareness regarding the management of a choking situation. This absence is a notable gap within the Palestinian public health field. Understanding population-level knowledge is essential because choking response skills are simple, teachable and easily disseminated through structured programmes such as school curricula, community workshops and digital campaigns.9 10 14

Given the significant preventability of choking fatalities and the consistently modifiable state of public knowledge, a thorough assessment of awareness and preparedness in Palestinian society is essential. In this particular investigation, the objective is to assess the knowledge levels of choking first-aid among the general public in the West Bank and to explore the determinants associated with good knowledge levels. Population research has the capacity to generate significant information regarding the extent of knowledge of the subject and current disparities. In addition to discovering which regions require intervention regarding choking episodes, and the morbidity and mortality rates that follow.

Materials and methods

Study design, period and setting

This descriptive cross-sectional study evaluated the general population’s knowledge of choking episodes and first-aid care in the West Bank, Palestine. The questionnaire was sent over WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram and email, media commonly used in Palestine, to achieve high reach and response rates. The media were selected because of their availability, popularity, and potential for accessing various groups of the population immediately. Participants were requested to share the link of the questionnaire on social media and with their networks, a snowball sampling, to facilitate a broader scope and involvement than from the immediate social network of the research team. This was found to be especially useful concerning the logistical and political limitations of gathering data in the field within the region, as well as the need to provide a diverse sample from localities. All responses were made anonymously via an online questionnaire.

Data collection took place over 1 week, from 29 November to 5 December 2025. The study included Palestinians living in the West Bank. We employed a quota sampling method to ensure geographic representativeness. Participant quotas were established for each governorate proportional to its population size based on the latest census data. We actively monitored response rates and targeted underrepresented areas to align the sample distribution closely with the actual population demographics. Recruitment covered urban areas, villages and refugee camps throughout the West Bank. The Gaza Strip was excluded due to accessibility constraints, so the findings represent only the West Bank population.

Multiple procedures were employed to ensure data quality and integrity. To prevent duplicate submissions, the ‘limit to one response’ feature was activated, and responses were examined for matching timestamps and demographic patterns. All survey items were set as mandatory fields to eliminate missing data, ensuring that only complete questionnaires were submitted.

Participation in the study was voluntary. Each participant gave electronic informed consent after learning about possible benefits for themselves and the community. Confidentiality was carefully protected, and no personal details like names or IP addresses were collected. The data were only used for statistical analysis. All 454 participants finished the questionnaire, and their answers were included in the final analysis.

Inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were Palestinian residents living in the West Bank and capable of independently completing the online questionnaire.

Individuals from a wide age range were included to ensure diversity of responses. Exclusion criteria included individuals who were living outside of Palestine, those unable to complete the questionnaire in Arabic, or those unwilling to provide informed consent to ensure data quality and integrity and to guarantee the confidentiality of the collected data. Questionnaires that were incomplete, duplicated or showed inconsistent demographic patterns were also excluded.

Study instruments

The sample was estimated through the single-proportion formula for a population proportion test. The desired confidence level was 95%, the margin error was set at 5% and the estimated proportion was conservatively set at 50% due to the absence of any study in our locality to base our study on. The estimated minimum sample for this study was 384. There was a total of 454 subjects in our final sample. The study adopted a structured online-self-administered questionnaire based on an established one that targeted a similar environment in previous research.13 Although the format was retained, adjustments were necessary to ensure that it is compatible with the Palestinian context. The initial format is in English, which requires Arabic translation following a standard reverse translation technique for validation. The questionnaire was translated into Arabic using a standard forward–backward translation technique and subsequently reviewed by healthcare professionals to assess linguistic accuracy, content validity and cultural appropriateness within the Palestinian context.

The pilot test study was conducted among 15 non-medical students to evaluate the clarity, understanding and effectiveness of the questionnaire. These students were selected because lacking a healthcare background, they were an excellent proxy to provide useful feedback on whether the questions were clear, free of medical jargon and easy to understand for the general population. The pilot phase was carried out only to refine and validate the questionnaire before starting the main study. Data obtained during the pilot phase were excluded from the final analysis. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a value of 0.668, which was considered acceptable given the exploratory nature and multidimensional structure of the instrument.

A complete English-translated version of the used questionnaire is provided in online supplemental file 1. The original study protocol is provided in online supplemental file 2.

The final questionnaire comprised 37 questions and they were distributed into four sections. The first section, sociodemographic variables, consisted of 11 questions to obtain data on respondent age, gender, location of residence (city, village and camp), level of education, work experience in a medical setting, family members’ monthly income, past experiences in attending courses on first aid and personal experiences with choking events. The second section, general knowledge about choking, included questions about the concept of choking, recognition of susceptible groups (children and seniors), susceptible factors (such as laughter during meal times) and recognition of choking signs and emergency medical contact numbers in Palestine. The third section, common misconceptions, assessed common misconceptions related to choking first-aid management, including the value of water during choking episodes, the inversion manoeuvre and the blind finger sweep technique. The fourth section, knowledge of accurate first aid procedure, assessed specific skills in the back blow technique, the number of needed blows and performing the Heimlich manoeuvre (abdominal thrusts) correctly concerning positioning of the hands in general and special cases in pregnant females or obese persons.

Practical knowledge was assessed theoretically through scenario-based and procedure-oriented questions rather than direct observation of participants’ real-life first-aid performance.

Data management and analysis

The collected data were exported from Google Forms, coded and organised using Microsoft Excel, and subsequently imported into the SPSS software V.26 for statistical analysis.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and questionnaire responses. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

A composite knowledge score was calculated by assigning 1 point for each correct response and 0 points for incorrect or ‘I do not know’ responses. Participants scoring more than 16 points were categorised as having a high level of choking first-aid knowledge, whereas those scoring 16 points or less were classified as having a low level of knowledge.

Associations between knowledge level and sociodemographic variables were initially assessed using the χ2 test. Variables that demonstrated statistical significance in the bivariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of high choking first-aid knowledge. The dependent variable in the regression analysis was the level of choking first-aid knowledge (high vs low).

Adjusted ORs (AORs) with corresponding 95% CIs were calculated. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed prior to model construction using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with all analyses conducted using a 95% confidence level.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A cumulative total of 454 eligible respondents successfully completed the online questionnaire. The fully refined and restructured findings are detailed below across descriptive, bivariate, and multivariable domains.

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

As summarised in table 1, more than half of the analysed sample were female (n=247, 54.4%), and the predominant age cohort fell within the 18–25 years bracket (n=279, 61.5%). In terms of geographic and community distribution, 52.0% (n=236) of the participants resided in urban areas (cities), with the largest regional representation coming from the Hebron governorate (n=188, 41.4%). Regarding educational and professional backgrounds, a substantial majority held a bachelor’s degree (n=299, 65.9%), whereas 59.9% (n=272) reported studying or working in non-medical fields. Furthermore, 46.9% (n=213) reported a monthly household income ranging between 1880 and 5000 New Israeli Shekels (NIS). Notably, a large proportion of the study population had never undergone formal first-aid training (n=310, 68.3%; figure 1). Additionally, while 70.7% (n=321) reported having children at home, approximately half of the sample had never personally witnessed a real-life choking incident (n=231, 50.9%).

Public knowledge regarding choking recognition and emergency response

The assessment of baseline awareness indicated that while participants demonstrated high general recognition of choking as an emergency, severe gaps remained regarding specific medical comorbidities and initial contact protocols (online supplemental table 1). More than half of the respondents (n=253, 55.7%) accurately defined choking as the entry of a foreign body into the airways. The vast majority recognised FBAO as a critical, potentially fatal emergency demanding immediate bystander intervention (n=418, 92.1%). Regarding behavioural risk factors, 96.5% (n=438) recognised that movement or laughter during meals elevates choking risks, and 93.4% (n=424) identified small toys as prominent hazards for young children. However, underlying clinical risks were less recognised; only 53.1% (n=241) identified neurological or psychiatric conditions (eg, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease) as predisposing factors for FBAO.

The classic clinical signs of choking (coughing, wheezing, facial redness and speech impairment) were correctly identified by 72.0% (n=327) of the sample. Children (n=435, 95.8%) and the elderly (n=256, 56.4%) were perceived as the most vulnerable cohorts. When contemplating an intervention, 73.8% (n=335) recognised that the victim’s age heavily dictates the choice of first-aid management. Interestingly, digital infrastructure served as the primary vehicle for first-aid knowledge acquisition, led by social media platforms (n=163, 35.9%) and YouTube videos (n=131, 28.9%).

Public misconceptions and attitudes toward choking management

Evaluation of safety attitudes revealed alarming, potentially life-threatening misconceptions deeply rooted among the general population, alongside positive awareness of certain hazardous practices (table 2). A substantial majority of respondents correctly rejected highly instinctive yet unsafe procedures, identifying the execution of a blind finger sweep (n=336, 74.0%) and offering a glass of water to an actively choking casualty (n=305, 67.2%) as wrong practices. Encouraging the casualty to cough was correctly recognised as an appropriate response by 57.5% (n=261) of participants. Conversely, critical deficits in respiratory mechanics emerged: 80.0% (n=363) of participants incorrectly believed that bystanders should actively prevent the casualty from inhaling due to fear of pushing the foreign body deeper. Furthermore, less than half of the sample (n=219, 48.2%) correctly recognised that turning a choking child upside down and shaking them is an inappropriate and harmful manoeuvre.

Regarding the abdominal thrust manoeuvre, fewer than half of the respondents (n=212, 46.7%) could correctly identify the visual depiction of the Heimlich manoeuvre. Technical knowledge regarding the spatial application of force was profoundly low: 85.5% (n=388) incorrectly identified the proper technique, with a striking minority of only 14.5% (n=66) knowing that thrusts must be applied specifically above the navel in an inward and upward direction. Additionally, only 29.5% (n=134) correctly identified that the manoeuvre should be repeated 5–10 times until the obstruction is cleared. Crucially, a profound knowledge deficit was observed concerning special demographics; only 14.8% (n=67) of the entire sample correctly recognised that chest thrusts—rather than abdominal thrusts—must be exclusively used for pregnant women, infants under 1 year of age and obese individuals.

Overall knowledge levels and bivariate associations

Based on the composite knowledge score (where scoring >16 points denoted a high level of choking first-aid knowledge), 60.1% (n=273) of the participants achieved a high overall knowledge level, while 39.9% (n=181) were classified as possessing a low knowledge level (figure 2).

Distribution of participants based on level of first-aid knowledge. A total of 273 participants demonstrated a high level of knowledge, while 181 participants were classified as having a low level of knowledge.

Bivariate analysis via χ2 testing (online supplemental table 2) revealed that overall knowledge levels were completely independent of gender (p=0.389), personal experience witnessing a prior choking event (p=0.088), the presence of children in the household (p=0.677), residential community type (p=0.654) or age (p=0.202). In contrast, significantly higher proportions of high knowledge were observed among individuals working or studying within a medical field compared with non-medical peers (74.2% vs 50.7%, p<0.001), and among those who had received formal first-aid training compared with untrained individuals (73.6% vs 53.9%, p<0.001). Furthermore, overall knowledge varied significantly across geographic governorates (p=0.006), with Salfit exhibiting the highest proportion of high knowledge (81.8%) and Tulkarm the lowest (32.1%). Higher educational attainment (p=0.010) and greater monthly household income (p=0.045) were also significantly linked to superior knowledge scores, with the highest scores found among bachelor’s degree holders (64.9%) and households earning more than 5000 NIS (70.6%).

The multivariable regression analysis identified prior formal first-aid training and professional background as the strongest independent predictors of choking first-aid knowledge. Specifically, individuals who had not received formal first-aid training were 44% less likely to possess high knowledge compared with those who had received training (AOR=0.56; 95% CI 0.34 to 0.91; p=0.019). Similarly, not working or studying in a medical field was a robust independent predictor of lower knowledge levels, with non-medical participants being 48% less likely to achieve high knowledge scores (AOR=0.52; 95% CI 0.33 to 0.84; p=0.007). Although educational level retained an overall significant association with knowledge within the model (p=0.031), individual pairwise comparisons against the reference group (graduate studies) did not yield statistically significant differences. Monthly household income demonstrated a borderline overall association with knowledge levels (p=0.077); however, participants earning less than 1880 NIS were significantly less likely to demonstrate high knowledge levels compared with those with no monthly income (AOR=0.47; 95% CI 0.23 to 0.98; p=0.043).

Discussion

Acquiring first aid knowledge is crucial for quickly responding to health issues, especially when someone is choking, which is a life-threatening condition.

Choking results from acute airway obstruction caused by foreign body aspiration, which can be quite dangerous if not dealt with quickly. Our present cross-sectional study on awareness, knowledge and misconceptions about first-aid practices for choking, conducted on the general population of the West Bank, Palestine, sheds light on the preparedness and capacity of the community to respond appropriately to choking, which is a high-risk emergency care scenario, while also revealing knowledge gaps and other factors impacting choking response capacity.

The present study evaluated not only theoretical knowledge regarding choking, but also misconceptions, attitudes and practical first-aid awareness among the general population in the West Bank.

Of the 454 surveyed, the findings of our study showed moderate knowledge about choking, as 253 (55.7%) of the participants correctly identified choking as the entry of a foreign body into the respiratory tract. These findings are consistent with a similar study conducted among Saudi parents, in which 55% had a moderate level of knowledge and 39% had a low level of knowledge.12 Similarly, a different study performed in Saudi Arabia found that 65.4% of parents in Makkah and 61.3% of parents in the Al Qassim region had insufficient knowledge regarding choking.15 16 Furthermore, a study conducted in Addis Ababa reported that only 37.6% of kindergarten teachers exhibited proficiency in choking first-aid procedures,17 while a study involving Turkish mothers found that only 50% demonstrated an adequate level of knowledge.18 In addition, a study of adults in Syria found that 60.6% of participants demonstrated low knowledge regarding choking.13 These findings collectively highlight significant differences in choking-related knowledge between communities, revealing critical gaps in awareness of a potentially fatal emergency that can often be easily treated with basic first-aid techniques.

An interesting finding in our study was the lack of a significant association between gender and choking knowledge (p=0.389). This contrasts sharply with previous regional studies, which often reported pronounced, although contradictory, gender disparities. For instance, studies in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, indicated that males achieved higher accuracy scores,4 whereas other investigations in Makkah and Al Qassim reported superior knowledge among females.15 16 Our findings suggest a more homogenous distribution of basic awareness across genders within the Palestinian context, likely driven by shared exposure to digital information rather than traditional gender roles in caregiving.

Our study demonstrated that higher educational attainment, including university or postgraduate degrees, employment in the medical field and prior formal first-aid training were all associated with significantly greater knowledge regarding choking. These findings are consistent with studies from Turkey, Syria and Saudi Arabia, which also reported higher awareness among individuals with advanced education and professional exposure.12 13 15 19 However, a study in Dammam, Saudi Arabia, showed no prominent difference in response between high school and college participants, showing that context matters.20

One of the most important findings of this study was the considerable deficiency in practical choking first-aid knowledge, particularly regarding Heimlich manoeuvre performance and the management of choking in special populations such as pregnant women, obese individuals and infants. As only 14.8% of the population correctly identified chest thrusts as the appropriate technique for pregnant women, obese individuals and infants. Participants without medical backgrounds, those with lower educational attainment, and individuals who had not received previous first-aid training demonstrated lower awareness levels and therefore represent priority target groups for future educational interventions.

Besides education and training, other sociodemographic factors were significantly associated with knowledge levels. While initial unadjusted analysis suggested a positive correlation between household income and awareness, our multivariable regression model revealed that income was not an independent predictor (p=0.077) when adjusting for other variables. This indicates that the initially observed effect of income might be confounded by underlying factors such as educational attainment and employment in the medical field. Geographic differences were also noteworthy, with those from Tulkarm and Qalqilya showing the lowest knowledge levels (32.1% and 34.6%, respectively), while Salfit had the highest. This regional discrepancy could be due to variations in local health awareness campaigns, educational opportunities or access to healthcare facilities. Collectively, these results underscore the importance of education, professional experience, formal training, socioeconomic status and geographic context in public knowledge of choking and first-aid management.

The present study incorporated several misconceptions and inappropriate behaviours to assess their occurrence among the sample. One such misdirected intervention is the finger sweep technique, which is an instinctive yet unsafe response. This intervention may exacerbate airway obstruction by pushing the foreign body deeper or may result in injury to either the victim or the rescuer, as documented in previous studies.21 22 In our study, 26% of participants were unaware that inserting fingers into the victim’s mouth to remove the foreign body is contraindicated. Comparable findings were reported among adults in Syria, where 29.1% of participants demonstrated the same misconception.13 Notably, higher proportions were reported in Saudi Arabia, where 50.0% of parents in Riyadh, 77.1% in the Al Qassim region and 79.4% in Makkah intended to perform the finger sweep technique.12 15 16

Many people think that giving water will help someone who is choking. In our study, we found that 32.8% of individuals were unaware that water should not be offered, as it is regarded as an inappropriate and ineffective remedy. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting similar gaps in knowledge, including 55.1% of parents in Riyadh, 43.8% of kindergarten teachers in Addis Ababa and 34.5% of adults in Syria who indicated a likelihood of offering water to a choking child.12 13 17

Unlike previous studies that often highlight healthcare professionals or school education as primary knowledge sources, a unique finding in our cohort was the profound reliance on digital platforms. Social media (35.9%) and YouTube (28.9%) were identified as the absolute primary sources of first-aid information, far exceeding traditional educational or healthcare sources. This reflects a significant contemporary shift in health-information-seeking behaviour within Palestinian society. While these platforms offer unprecedented opportunities for the rapid, widespread dissemination of educational content, their unregulated nature poses a substantial risk for amplifying the very misconceptions (such as the blind finger sweep) that this study identified.

For a choking patient with complete airway obstruction who is unable to cough, back blows are a recommended first-aid intervention.23 Nonetheless, evidence from Saudi Arabia shows that misperceptions are common, with 90.6% in Al Qassim and 68.4% of parents in Makkah intending to give back blows even when the patient was able to speak.15 16 In our study, only 66.3% correctly recognised the need to lean the patient forward before administering back blows. According to these findings, it is essential to provide practical, accessible first-aid training that teaches proper techniques and addresses misunderstandings. Possible reasons for these gaps include limited public access to training, insufficient first-aid education in schools and inadequate training in medical schools. A striking and somewhat paradoxical finding was that a substantial proportion (26.4%) of participants who had previously completed formal first-aid courses still demonstrated low levels of practical knowledge. While formal training is generally protective, this specific finding highlights a critical decay of knowledge over time or a failure of conventional courses to instil long-term practical competence. This observation aligns with findings from Madinah City, Saudi Arabia, where only 12.8% of mothers exhibited adequate choking management skills despite over one-third having prior first-aid certification.24 This clearly underscores that singular, theoretical first-aid education is insufficient; continuous, practical refresher training is mandatory to maintain emergency readiness.

To rigorously assess the representativeness of our quota sampling, we cross-referenced the demographic characteristics of our sample with the latest official census data from the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics. Our sample’s gender distribution (45.6% male, 54.4% female) aligns closely with the national demographic averages for the adult population, although with a slight female predominance. Furthermore, the age distribution within our cohort accurately reflects the predominantly young demographic structure of Palestinian society.

However, as expected with web-based survey methodologies, the younger adult cohort (18–25 years) was somewhat over-represented compared with national census figures. Overall, this comparison confirms that our quota sampling successfully captured a reasonably representative cross-section of the target general population in the West Bank.

The current study has a relatively large sample size and includes participants from different areas of the West Bank, which adds to its strength and credibility. However, several limitations should be considered. Causal inference is limited by the cross-sectional design, and reporting or recall bias may have been introduced by the use of self-reported data. In addition, younger and more educated individuals may have been overrepresented due to the web-based distribution method and reliance on social media platforms for participant recruitment. Additionally, the assessment prioritised knowledge over actual first-aid performance, which may differ in real choking scenarios.

Based on our findings, translating this data into targeted public health interventions is crucial. The specific areas requiring the most urgent improvement are distinctly practical; primarily the correct anatomical execution of the Heimlich manoeuvre, the management of special populations (such as pregnant women and obese individuals) and the eradication of hazardous misconceptions like the blind finger sweep.

To be effective, intervention measures must be specifically tailored to the priority groups identified in this study: individuals without medical backgrounds, those with lower educational and socioeconomic statuses, and residents of underperforming geographic regions (specifically Tulkarm and Qalqilya).

We propose two specific, tailored programmes to reach these demographics. First, since social media and YouTube were identified as the primary sources of information, the most feasible strategy for the general and lower-income public is the dissemination of short, localised Arabic instructional videos demonstrating precise practical skills (eg, Heimlich thrusts and back blows). Second, to ensure structured, long-term practical competence among younger generations before they enter the workforce, we recommend integrating mandatory, hands-on first-aid modules into school curricula and university graduation requirements. This measure would be highly applicable if executed through strategic collaboration between the Ministry of Health, educational institutions and established NGOs like the Palestinian Red Crescent Society.

Together, these targeted measures will transition the population from fragmented theoretical knowledge to practical lifesaving competence, ultimately reducing preventable choking mortality.

The feasibility and applicability of these recommendations lie primarily in their cost-effectiveness and reliance on existing local infrastructure. For instance, leveraging social media platforms for instructional videos requires minimal financial investment while offering maximal, rapid reach, directly aligning with the population’s current information-seeking habits. Furthermore, integrating first-aid modules into school and university curricula is practically attainable through administrative policy adjustments rather than extensive resource allocation. This is especially feasible when coordinated with established local NGOs, such as the Palestinian Red Crescent Society, which already possesses the necessary training expertise, facilities and community trust to facilitate such widespread educational initiatives.

Furthermore, these findings serve as crucial baseline data for developing countries and resource-limited settings, providing a solid foundation for evidence-based health policies and targeted community interventions from a broader global health perspective.

Conclusions

This paper brings to the fore an important chasm that exists between theoretical knowledge and actual skills for the management of choke situations in the West Bank population. The gravity of the consequences that could result from choking has long since been realised, and yet the prevalence of potentially life-threatening misinformation, such as the back-of-the-hand sweep in the dark or the administration of water, remains an important safety concern. The results bring to the fore the urgent need for comprehensive programmes for training people in general first aid. Future community training programmes must shift from broad theoretical education to targeted practical skills, emphasising correct manoeuvre mechanics and the avoidance of dangerous misconceptions. Special emphasis and tailored measures—such as digital visual campaigns and integrated school curricula—must be explicitly directed towards lower socioeconomic strata, non-medical individuals and underperforming geographic regions to ensure equitable dissemination of life-saving competencies.
