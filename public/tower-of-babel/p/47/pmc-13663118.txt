Chromosome-scale genome of Ormosia hosiei provides insights into legume tree genome evolution
Authors: Yi Wang, Minhao Liu, Liang Shi, Binhao Yang, Linli Dai, Hanbo Yang, Jinwu Li, Huimin Zhao, Xingcui Xiao, Hongying Guo, Yunjie Gu, Jian Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrmosia hosiei, commonly known as the “Chinese love bean tree” for its traditional symbolic association with love and longing in Chinese literature, is an economically and ecologically important leguminous tree species endemic to China, prized for its high-density hardwood and its vital ecological functions in subtropical forests. However, the limited availability of genomic resources has hindered in-depth molecular studies, constraining both genetic improvement efforts—such as those targeting wood formation—and conservation strategies.ResultsIn this study, we present a high-quality genome assembly of O. hosiei with a total size of 2.14 Gb and a scaffold N50 of 255.85 Mb, and annotated 39,309 protein-coding genes. These genes likely contribute to wood formation and the development of straight trunk architecture.ConclusionsOverall, the O. hosiei reference genome provides a valuable genomic resource for understanding legume evolution and offers essential support for functional genomics studies, molecular breeding, and conservation of this precious timber species endemic to China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13195-8.Keywords: Ormosia hosiei, Whole-genome duplication, Genome evolution, Transcription factors

Background

Ormosia hosiei, commonly known as the “Chinese love bean tree”, is a rare and economically important leguminous tree species endemic to China [1]. As a flagship timber species within the Fabaceae family, O. hosiei is highly valued for its impressive height, straight and sturdy trunk, and exceptionally dense, richly colored heartwood, which is widely used in the manufacture of high-end furniture, musical instruments, and finely crafted ornamental items [2]. Beyond its timber utility, the species is also notable for its brilliant red seeds, which carry cultural and symbolic meaning in Chinese folklore and are extensively used in traditional handicrafts. Various plant parts—including roots, stems, leaves, and seeds—have been traditionally used to treat rheumatism, trauma, and inflammatory disorders, highlighting its medicinal significance [3, 4].

Ormosia hosiei primarily inhabits low-elevation evergreen broadleaved forests in southeastern and central China [5]. Mature trees can reach heights of up to 30 m and often serve as dominant canopy species, playing vital roles in forest structure and biodiversity maintenance. Compared to many tropical Fabaceae species, O. hosiei exhibits notable cold tolerance, making it a promising candidate for afforestation and ecological restoration programs in the central and northern subtropical zones of China. However, over the past several decades, natural populations have sharply declined due to unsustainable logging and habitat fragmentation [6]. Its natural regeneration is further constrained by poor seed dispersal, low germination rates, and anthropogenic disturbances limiting seedling establishment. These factors have led to small, isolated, and fragmented populations that are vulnerable to demographic fluctuations and genetic erosion. Recent genetic studies have revealed low levels of genetic diversity and evidence of inbreeding within some populations [7, 8], raising concerns about its long-term survival and adaptability. Currently, O. hosiei is listed as “Near Threatened” on the IUCN Red List [9] and is designated as a “Second-Class Nationally Protected Plant” in China [10].

Although previous research has addressed morphology, silviculture, and genetic diversity in O. hosiei [11, 12], the scarcity of genomic resources restricts molecular investigations critical to both breeding, including wood development, and conservation genetics. Here, we present a high-quality, chromosome-level genome assembly of O. hosiei, based on PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing and Hi-C scaffolding. The assembled genome spans approximately 2.14 Gb with a scaffold N50 of 255.85 Mb. We performed comprehensive annotation of protein-coding genes and repetitive elements, analyzed gene family expansion and contraction, and identified whole-genome duplication events within the lineage. In particular, we characterized the NAC and MYB transcription factor families and examined their phylogenetic relationships with homologs from Arabidopsis thaliana and Populus trichocarpa. This genomic resource provides a valuable platform for future studies on the biology, evolution, and conservation of this important leguminous hardwood species.

Results

De novo assembly of O. hosiei genome

The genome size of O. hosiei was estimated to be ~ 2.07 Gb based on K-mer analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1), which was consistent with the results from the flow cytometry test (Supplementary Table S1). To obtain a high-quality chromosome-level genome, we integrated multiple sequencing technologies and assembly strategies. Specifically, we generated a total of 64.66 Gb of PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) long-read data, representing approximately 30× genome coverage, and 212.52 Gb of Hi-C paired-end reads to assist with chromosome scaffolding (Supplementary Table S2). For the preliminary assembly, only high-quality filtered long reads were used. This initial assembly yielded 558 contigs with a contig N50 of 64.38 Mb (Supplementary Table S3). After assembly cleaning, 53 contigs were subjected to Hi-C scaffolding, enabling 99.48% of the assembled sequences to be anchored and oriented onto eight pseudochromosomes (Table 1; Fig. 1A and B). The final chromosome-level assembly spanned 2.14 Gb, with individual chromosomes ranging from 173.23 Mb to 383.14 Mb in length and a scaffold N50 of 255.85 Mb (Table 1, Supplementary Table S4; the assembly is not telomere-to-telomere and contains multiple gaps).

To evaluate the quality and completeness of the assembly, we performed several assessments. Mapping of Illumina short reads to the assembled genome resulted in a high alignment rate of 99.96% (Supplementary Table S4), indicating excellent base-level accuracy. Furthermore, the completeness of the O. hosiei genome assembly and the predicted gene set was assessed using BUSCO with the Eudicots odb10 database (n = 2,326) (Supplementary Table S5). For the genome assembly, 2,307 BUSCOs (99.20%) were complete, including 2,063 (88.70%) single-copy and 244 (10.50%) duplicated, while only 5 (0.20%) were fragmented and 14 (0.60%) were missing. For the annotated genes, 2,249 BUSCOs (96.70%) were complete, including 2,029 (87.20%) single-copy and 220 (9.50%) duplicated, with 39 (1.70%) fragmented and 38 (1.60%) missing, indicating a high completeness of both the assembly and the gene predictions. In addition, the LTR Assembly Index (LAI) was calculated as 10.69 on average (Fig. S2), reflecting moderate assembly continuity and completeness of long-terminal repeat retrotransposons. Collectively, these metrics indicate that the O. hosiei genome assembly is highly accurate in gene regions while achieving moderate completeness in repetitive elements, providing a solid foundation for downstream genomic and evolutionary analyses.

Genome annotation

Using a combination of de novo and homology-based methods, up to 85.35% of the assembled sequences were found to be repeated sequences comprising transposable elements and simple repeats (Table 1, Supplementary Table S6). Long terminal repeat-retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) comprised the most abundant fraction of transposable elements (72.40% of the assembled sequences, Supplementary Table S6), with the Gypsy (41.04%) superfamily being more abundant than the Copia superfamily (9.12%). Estimates of sequence divergence between the 5′ and 3′ LTRs of individual elements suggest that Gypsy elements experienced a burst of activity within the last ~ 2 million years (Mya), whereas Copia elements show evidence of more gradual accumulation beginning around 5 Mya (Supplementary Fig. S3). To investigate the diversity and evolutionary relationships of LTR retrotransposons in O. hosiei, we constructed phylogenetic trees based on the reverse transcriptase (RT) domains of intact elements. For Gypsy elements (Fig. 2A), six major clades were identified, including Athila, Retand, Ogre, Reina, CRM, and Tekay. For Copia elements (Fig. 2B), eight clades were recovered: SIRE, Ivana, Ale, Tork, TAR, Bianca, Ikeros, and Angela. There were more members of the Gypsy (5,336 members) than the Copia (2,338 members), consistent with a more extensive amplification of Gypsy in the O. hosiei genome. More specifically, the Athila clade within Gypsy and the Ale clade within Copia contributed most substantially to genome expansion in O. hosiei.

We masked repetitive regions and proceeded to annotate the genome using a pipeline combining RNA-seq data-based, ab initio, and homology-based evidence. In total, 39,309 gene models were identified, with total genic length of 227.16 Mb (Table 1, Supplementary Table S7). The average length of these protein-coding genes was 5,778 bp, and the average CDS length was 1,404 bp. Overall, functions were assigned to 37,417 genes (95.19%, Supplementary Table S8) based on homology to annotated proteins in multiple databases, including 37,382 in the Non-Redundant (NR) protein database, 28,215 in Swiss-Prot, 14,830 in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), 18,118 assigned Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and 34,541 annotated in the Evolutionary Genealogy of Genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database. We also predicted a variety of non-coding RNAs in the genome (Supplementary Table S9), including 1,906 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), 182 microRNAs (miRNAs), 1,194 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), 313 small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and 11,794 small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs).

Whole-genome duplication events

To elucidate the whole-genome duplication (WGD) history of Ormosia hosiei, we analyzed the distribution of synonymous substitution rates (Ks) and compared it with those of O. semicastrata, Calophaca sinica, and Glycine max. The Ks distribution of O. hosiei revealed two distinct peaks, indicating that its genome has experienced two rounds of WGD (Fig. 2C). The older peak (Ks ≈ 1.75) corresponds to an ancient WGD event shared among Fabaceae species, while the more recent peak (Ks ≈ 0.38) suggests a lineage-specific duplication event in O. hosiei. This inference is further supported by strong chromosomal collinearity within the O. hosiei genome, characterized by a 1:1 collinearity ratio between homologous chromosome pairs (Figs. 1A and 2D). Gene duplication analysis identified a total of 32,125 duplicated genes, of which 11,861 (36.9%) were derived from WGD. The remaining duplicates were categorized as tandem (1,874; 5.8%), proximal (1,155; 3.6%), transposed (8,039; 25.0%), and dispersed (9,196; 28.6%) duplicates (Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings collectively provide robust evidence for a recent WGD event in O. hosiei.

Phylogenetic and comparative genomic analysis

To clarify the evolutionary position of O. hosiei and gain deeper insights into its phylogenetic relationships within eudicots, we conducted a comparative phylogenomic analysis using the genomes of 10 other eudicot species (Supplementary Table S10). A total of 39,309 genes were clustered into 17,517 gene families, which included 10,151 gene families shared by all 11 species and 705 O. hosiei-specific families (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S10). Our GO term enrichment analysis (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table S11) revealed that those genes unique to O. hosiei were involved in external encapsulating structure organization (GO:0045229), cell wall organization (GO:0071555), supramolecular fiber organization (GO:0097435), regulation of Rho protein signal transduction (GO:0035023), and response to stimulus (GO:0050896).

We identified 204 strictly single-copy orthologues among 11 species to construct a time-calibrated phylogenetic tree, which showed that Ormosia hosiei occupies a relatively basal position within the Papilionoideae clade of the Leguminosae family (Fig. 3B). It forms a sister lineage with Sophora flavescens, and the two species diverged from each other approximately 30.98 Mya. Together, they split from other core Papilionoideae species, such as Robinia pseudoacacia and Lotus japonicus, around 40.84 Mya. The expansion and contraction of orthologue gene families were determined based on stochastic birth–death model implemented in CAFE (Fig. 3B). A total of 3,056 and 2,571 gene families had expanded and contracted in O. hosiei genome, respectively. GO enrichment analysis revealed that the expanded gene families were primarily associated with defense responses (e.g., defense response GO:0006952, jasmonic acid-mediated signaling GO:0009861), secondary metabolism (including phenylpropanoid biosynthetic process GO:0009699 and lignan metabolic process GO:0009806), and detoxification pathways such as glutathione metabolic process (GO:0006749) and sulfur compound metabolic process (GO:0006790). Transport-related processes, including phloem transport (GO:0010233), were also enriched (Supplementary Table S12).

Discovery of NAC and MYB transcription factors related to wood formation

The NAC and MYB transcription factor families play critical roles in plant growth and development, particularly in regulating secondary cell wall biosynthesis and wood formation [13, 14]. A total of 117 NAC and 176 MYB transcription factors were identified in the O. hosiei genome based on conserved domain detection and sequence homology (Fig. 4A and B). To investigate their evolutionary relationships and potential functional conservation, phylogenetic trees were constructed using homologous sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana and Populus trichocarpa. The analysis revealed that more than 20 O. hosiei genes clustered with well-characterized regulators of secondary wall development in A. thaliana and P. trichocarpa. Notably, these functionally conserved NAC and MYB homologs were all located in more derived clades of the phylogenetic trees, whereas the basal branches lacked such wood-associated regulators.

Among the NAC transcription factor family, approximately 17 genes from O. hosiei—including OsChr05.g31889, OsChr01.g06809, OsChr04.g20580, OsChr05.g28186, OsChr02.g10581, OsChr01.g03403, OsChr06.g37066, OsChr01.g01099, OsChr02.g09676, OsChr04.g21276, OsChr05.g32848, OsChr02.g13305, OsChr05.g29798, OsChr03.g17601, OsChr02.g13770, OsChr08.g49288, and OsChr03.g16140—were phylogenetically clustered with members of the well-characterized secondary wall–associated NAC (SWN NAC) subgroup in A. thaliana. This subgroup includes key regulators such as SND1 (At1G32770), SND2 (At4G28500), SND3 (At1G28470), NST1 (At2G46770), NST2 (At3G61910), XND1 (At5G64530) as well as a suite of VND (VASCULAR-RELATED NAC-DOMAIN) proteins including VND1 to VND7 (At2G18060, At4G36160, At5G66300, At1G12260, At1G62770, At5G62380, and At1G71930, respectively). These A. thaliana transcription factors are recognized as master regulators of secondary cell wall biosynthesis, particularly in promoting lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose deposition in xylem vessels and fibers [13].

Similarly, in the MYB transcription factor family, around 12 O. hosiei genes—such as OsChr01.g03878, OsChr05.g30903, OsChr05.g29013, OsChr01.g00636, OsChr02.g11788, OsChr04.g23428, OsChr05.g28665, OsChr08.g51411, OsChr05.g30497, OsChr01.g04211, OsChr04.g27748, and OsChr04.g22280—were found to group closely with the A. thaliana transcription factors MYB46 (At5G12870) and MYB83 (At3G08500), along with MYB46/83-like genes including At1G16490, At1G79180, and At4G22680. These MYB proteins function downstream of the SWN NACs and are critical components of the hierarchical transcriptional network that activates the expression of genes responsible for secondary cell wall biosynthesis [14].

Discussion

The successful chromosome-level assembly of the O. hosiei genome marks a significant advancement in the genomic study of this valuable legume tree species. By integrating PacBio HiFi long reads with Hi-C chromatin conformation data, a high-quality genome assembly was generated. The assembled genome spans approximately 2.14 Gb (Table 1), positioning O. hosiei among legumes with relatively large genomes, substantially exceeding the sizes reported for several representative species such as Glycine max (~ 1.1 Gb) [15], Phaseolus vulgaris (~ 560 Mb) [16], Cajanus cajan (~ 813 Mb) [17], and Medicago truncatula (~ 481 Mb) [18]. Notably, the O. hosiei genome also exceeds the sizes reported for other sequenced Ormosia species (1.42–1.64 Gb) [19, 20], but is smaller than that of O. henryi (2.69 Gb) [21]. The considerable genome size of O. hosiei is predominantly attributed to the massive accumulation of repetitive sequences, particularly LTR-RTs, which constitute over 70% of the assembly. Among these, the Gypsy superfamily exhibited a pronounced expansion (41.04%) compared to Copia elements (9.12%). Divergence time estimates suggest that Gypsy elements underwent a relatively recent burst of activity approximately 2 Mya, while Copia elements accumulated more gradually starting around 5 Mya, indicating differing transpositional dynamics between these two lineages. Phylogenetic analyses identified multiple clades within both Gypsy (including Athila, Retand, Ogre) and Copia (including Ale, Angela), with the Athila and Ale clades being particularly enriched. This suggests that specific LTR subfamilies disproportionately contributed to genome expansion. The predominance of intact Gypsy elements further corroborates a burst-driven expansion model. Such extensive LTR-RT amplification is a common characteristic of plant genomes undergoing recent size increases and may be linked to reduced efficiency of transposable element removal, stress-induced activation, or relaxed epigenetic repression mechanisms [22–24].

Although the genome annotation identified 39,309 protein-coding genes in O. hosiei—fewer than those reported in O. purpureiflora (55,061 genes), O. emarginata (50,517 genes), O. semicastrata (51,220 genes), O. boluoensis (51,822 genes) and O. henryi (42,260 genes) [19–21]—this difference likely reflects variations in annotation pipelines and the limited transcriptomic evidence derived from a restricted set of tissues in our study, which may have led to the underrepresentation of lowly expressed or tissue-specific genes, while lineage-specific gene loss cannot be excluded [25, 26]. Yet, synteny analyses revealed that O. hosiei maintains high structural conservation with O. purpureiflora, O. emarginata, and O. semicastrata, whereas O. henryi shows more structural variation, possibly due to chromosomal rearrangements or transposon activity (Supplementary Fig. S5). Overall, these observations suggest that despite differences in genome size and annotated gene number, the genus Ormosia retains a largely conserved chromosomal architecture, with genome expansion driven predominantly by repeat sequence proliferation rather than extensive gene duplication or chromosomal reorganization.

Whole-genome duplications (WGDs) are widespread across land plants and are frequently associated with lineage diversification, phenotypic innovation, and enhanced adaptability to environmental fluctuations [27]. In the O. hosiei genome, Ks-based analyses revealed two distinct peaks, consistent with two separate WGD events. The older Ks peak (~ 1.75) corresponds to an ancient polyploidy event broadly shared across the Fabaceae family, in agreement with previously reported legume-wide duplication signals [28–30], and likely represents a foundational genomic event in early legume evolution. In contrast, a second, more recent Ks peak (~ 0.38), consistently detected in both O. hosiei and O. semicastrata but absent in outgroup species such as Calophaca sinica and Glycine max, suggests a more recent WGD event within the Ormosia clade. However, comparative genomic evidence from O. purpureiflora and Lupinus albus indicates that this duplication may not be restricted to Ormosia, but instead shared among members of the Genistoid lineage within Fabaceae [19]. This view is further supported by conserved WGD signals across multiple Ormosia species, pointing to a shared genomic duplication event likely occurring after divergence from other Papilionoideae but before diversification within Genistoids. These findings underscore the value of clade-focused genome comparisons in refining WGD histories and illustrate the complex dynamics of polyploidy across legume evolution.

As a leguminous hardwood species prized for its dense, finely grained timber, Ormosia hosiei holds considerable economic value, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying its wood formation remain poorly understood. To address this gap, we combined phylogenomic, gene family, and transcription factor analyses to investigate regulatory pathways involved in secondary cell wall biosynthesis. Gene family expansion analyses revealed significant enrichment in categories related to secondary metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and defense responses—processes closely associated with lignification and vascular development (Fig. 3C). Moreover, O. hosiei-specific gene families were notably enriched in terms linked to cell wall organization and supramolecular fiber assembly, suggesting adaptive innovations in wood structural formation. Focusing on transcriptional regulation, we analyzed the NAC and MYB transcription factor families, which form the core of a conserved hierarchical network controlling secondary cell wall biosynthesis [13, 14]. Within this network, secondary wall–associated NAC (SWN NAC) proteins—such as SND, NST, and VND family members—function as master regulators initiating secondary wall formation programs in xylem vessels and fibers. These NAC factors subsequently activate downstream MYB transcription factors, primarily MYB46 and MYB83, which directly upregulate genes responsible for lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose synthesis [31]. In this study, 17 O. hosiei NAC genes clustered with well-characterized A. thaliana SWN NACs including SND1, NST1/2, and VND1–7, while 12 MYB genes grouped with MYB46, MYB83, and their close homologs. Notably, we observed copy number expansions in several key regulators. For example, the A. thaliana gene XND1 (Xylem NAC Domain 1)—a transcription factor known to influence xylem differentiation and secondary cell wall formation by modulating tracheary element development [32]—exists as a single copy in A. thaliana but as four homologs in both O. hosiei and P. trichocarpa. Similarly, A. thaliana genes MYB46 and MYB83, typically single-copy, are each present as two copies in O. hosiei. This lineage-specific expansion likely increases transcriptional regulatory complexity or facilitates functional divergence, thereby enhancing the fine-tuned control of wood formation in O. hosiei.

Conclusion

This study reports a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly for Ormosia hosiei, a valuable leguminous hardwood species. The large genome size of O. hosiei is primarily attributable to the massive expansion of LTR-RTs, particularly Gypsy elements, which underwent a recent burst of amplification. Ks-based analyses uncovered evidence for two WGD events: an ancient duplication shared broadly across the Fabaceae family and a more recent event likely common to members of the Genistoid clade. In addition, we identified a suite of NAC and MYB transcription factors, including multiple homologs of well-characterized regulators of secondary cell wall biosynthesis, offering important insights into the genetic basis of wood formation in this species. Together, these genomic resources lay a solid foundation for future studies on the evolution, adaptation, and molecular regulation of wood development in O. hosiei and related leguminous trees.

Methods

Plant materials and genome sequencing

One-year-old seedlings of Ormosia hosiei were selected for genome sequencing. Plant materials were obtained from the germplasm collection conserved at the National Long-term Research Base for Phoebe Cultivation, located in Yuchan Mountain, Luzhou, Sichuan Province, China (105°23’ E, 29°09’ N). High-quality genomic DNA was extracted from newly expanded young leaves using a modified CTAB method [33]. The quality and quantity of the isolated DNA were determined using (i) NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific), (ii) gel electrophoresis and (iii) Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen). Total DNA was purified by AMPure PB beads (PacBio 100-265-900). For HiFi sequencing, PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) bell library was constructed using SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, PN 101-853-100) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. High-quality genomic DNA (> 40 kb) was sheared to > 15 kb using a Megaruptor system, followed by damage repair, end-repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection (SageELF or BluePippin), and sequencing on the PacBio Sequel II/IIe platform. To obtain a chromosome-level genome assembly, a Hi-C library was constructed using DpnII and sequenced with 150 bp paired-end reads on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform following standard protocols. In addition, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on four different tissues, including leaf, stem, root and seed. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, 150 bp paired-end libraries were constructed and sequenced on the MGI-2000 platform.

De novo genome assembly

Raw polymerase reads were filtered using SMRT Link v5.0 (https://www.pacb.com). Reads shorter than 50 bp, with a quality score below 0.8, or containing self-ligated adaptors were removed, and adaptor sequences were trimmed. Hifiasm v0.19.5 was employed to generate the draft genome assembly using clean HiFi reads [34]. To ensure high-quality assembly, a series of filtering steps were performed to remove low-quality and potentially contaminant sequences. First, Purge_dups v1.2.5 was used to eliminate redundant sequences (https://github.com/dfguan/purge_dups). Second, assembled contigs were aligned against the NCBI NT database to identify and remove potential contaminants. Third, contig depth and GC content were assessed, and sequences with a coverage depth below 5× or a GC content exceeding 50% were excluded. To evaluate the accuracy of the assembled genome, HiFi reads were aligned to the draft assembly using Minimap2 v2.26 [35]. Based on the alignment results, Samtools v1.17 [36] and BEDTools v2.2.29 [37] were employed to calculate sequencing depth and GC content across 10 kb sliding windows. For Hi-C scaffolding, raw reads were first subjected to quality filtering as follows: (i) paired-end reads containing more than 3 ambiguous bases (N) in either end were removed; (ii) paired-end reads with over 20% low-quality bases (quality score < 5) in either read were discarded; (iii) reads containing adapter sequences were filtered out. The filtered Hi-C reads were then aligned to the draft genome assembly using HiC-Pro v2.11.4 software, which filters and corrects the alignment results, retaining only read pairs for which both ends uniquely map to the genome for downstream analysis [38]. Subsequently, 3D-DNA v180922 was employed for Hi-C-assisted scaffolding and generation of interactive contact maps [39]. For scaffolding purposes, the chromosome number of O. hosiei was set to 2n = 16, in accordance with reports from related Ormosia species [19–21]. Post-assembly, manual correction of mis-ordered or mis-oriented sequences was performed using Juicebox v1.11.08 based on the Hi-C interaction heatmaps to produce the final chromosome-scale assembly [40]. Additionally, HiCExplorer v3.7.2 was used to generate high-resolution Hi-C contact maps for visualizing genome-wide chromatin interactions [41]. The numbering of the assembled chromosomes was assigned by comparison with chromosome-scale genomes of closely related Ormosia species, following their conserved syntenic relationships [19]. Based on this workflow, a primary assembly of the O. hosiei genome was generated (haplotype-resolved assemblies were not produced). Genome completeness was further assessed using the BUSCO v5.6.0 framework with the Eudicots odb10 database, which includes 2,326 conserved single-copy orthologs [42]. The LTR Assembly Index (LAI) was additionally calculated using LTR_retriever v2.9.0 [43] to evaluate the continuity and completeness of long-terminal repeat retrotransposon regions within the genome.

Repeat annotation

Repetitive sequences in the Ormosia hosiei genome were annotated using a combination of homology-based and de novo approaches. For homolog evidence, RepeatMasker v4.1.1 [44] was used to search the genome against the RepBase database (http://www.girinst.org/repbase) to identify known repetitive elements. For de novo detection, RepeatModeler v2.0.2a [45] was applied to the masked genome to identify novel repetitive sequences, and MITE-Hunter v1.0 [46] was used to detect miniature inverted-repeat transposable elements (MITEs). Long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) were further annotated using a structure-based pipeline. Specifically, LTRharvest v1.2 [47] and LTR_FINDER v1.07 [48] were used to detect candidate LTR-RTs, and the results were integrated and filtered with LTR_retriever v2.9.0 [43] to obtain high-confidence intact LTR elements by removing non-LTRs and those with excessive tandem repeats or gaps. To investigate the evolutionary dynamics of LTR-RTs, intact elements were classified into Copia and Gypsy superfamilies using TEsorter v1.4.7 [49] based on the REXdb database, and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using their reverse transcriptase (RT) domain sequences. The insertion time (T) of each intact LTR-RT was estimated using the formula T = K / (2µ), where K is the divergence between the two LTRs and µ is the nucleotide substitution rate. For O. hosiei, we adopted a substitution rate of 6.1 × 10⁻⁹ substitutions per site per year, as previously proposed for leguminous species [50].

Gene prediction and functional annotation

A comprehensive gene annotation strategy was employed by integrating ab initio prediction, protein homology-based annotation, and RNA sequencing data. Ab initio prediction was performed using AUGUSTUS v3.5.0 [51], Snap [52], GlimmerHMM v3.0.4 [53], and GeneMark-ES Suite v4.32 [54] on the repeat-masked genome. GeMoMa v1.9 was applied for homology-based prediction, providing exon–intron boundary information [55]. RNA-seq data were used to assist in transcript assembly through HISAT2 v2.2.1 [56]. Gene predictions were integrated using EvidenceModeler (EVM) [57], in which different evidence sources were assigned different weights: transcriptome-based evidence was assigned the highest weight (10), protein homology evidence was assigned an intermediate weight (5), and ab initio gene predictions were assigned lower weights (1–2). The resulting gene models were further refined using PASA v2.5.2 [58] to annotate untranslated regions (UTRs) and alternative splicing events, allowing the retention of multiple high-confidence transcript isoforms per gene. Consequently, the total number of predicted mRNA transcripts exceeds the number of annotated gene models, reflecting alternative splicing events supported by RNA-seq and homology evidence.

For functional annotation, protein-coding genes were aligned against the Non-Redundant (NR) protein database, Swiss-Prot, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO) terms, and Evolutionary Genealogy of Genes: Non-supervised Orthologous Groups (eggNOG) database using BLASTP v2.2.25 (best hit with E < 10 − 5) [59]. Protein domains were identified via InterProScan v5.59-91.0 [60], GO terms were assigned using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.12 [61]. All annotation results were integrated to generate final gene functions.

Whole-genome duplication analysis

Four representative legume genomes—Ormosia hosiei, O. semicastrata [19], Calophaca sinica [22], and Glycine max [15]—were selected for whole-genome duplication (WGD) analysis. Colinear genes within and between genomes were identified using the icl module in WGDI v0.75 [62], which detects syntenic blocks by integrating gene sequence similarity and conserved gene order. This approach enables the identification of homologous gene pairs resulting from both recent and ancient duplication events. Synonymous substitution rates (Ks) between colinear gene pairs were calculated using the ks module in WGDI. Within this pipeline, protein sequences were aligned, codon-level alignments were generated, and Ks values were estimated using the Nei–Gojobori method as implemented in YN00. The resulting Ks distributions were used to infer the evolutionary history of gene duplication events across the four genomes. To visualize intra-genomic synteny, Circos v0.69.9 was employed to illustrate the positional relationships among syntenic blocks within the O. hosiei genome [63]. In addition, gene duplication modes were classified into whole-genome duplication (WGD), tandem duplication (TD), proximal duplication (PD), transposed duplication (TRD), and dispersed duplication (DSD) using DupGen_finder v1.0.0 with default parameters [64].

Gene family and phylogenetic analysis

As references, protein sequences from 10 species were selected, including 9 species of Fabaceae (Bauhinia variegata [65], Arachis duranensis [66], Sophora flavescens [67], Robinia pseudoacacia [68], Lotus japonicus [69], Cajanus cajan [17], Phaseolus vulgaris [16], Amphicarpaea edgeworthii [70], and Glycine max [15]) and one species of Vitaceae (Vitis vinifera [71]). For genes with alternative splicing variants, the longest transcript in each case was selected to represent the gene. Orthologous gene families in O. hosiei and these 10 species were identified by using OrthoFinder v3.1.1 [72]. In total, 204 high confidence single-copy orthologous genes were extracted, aligned with MAFFT v7.525 [73], and analyzed phylogenetically using IQ-TREE v3.0.1 with 1000 bootstraps [74]. Divergence time was estimated using MCMCtree from the PAML v4.10.6 software package [75], with calibration based on the divergence between G. max and A. edgeworthii (9.8–24.0 million years ago [Mya]) as retrieved from the TimeTree database (http://www.timetree.org/). After a burn-in of 1,000,000 iterations, MCMC chains were run for 50,000 generations, with a sampling frequency of 1,000. To explore the expansion and contraction of gene families in O. hosiei, CAFE v5.1.0 was used with the p-value set to 0.01, and auto searching for the λ value (lambda, representing the average gene birth and death rate) [76]. Subsequently, GO enrichment analyses were performed using R package topGO to identify significantly enriched terms [77]. Fisher’s exact test was applied to calculate the statistical significance of enrichment.

Ormosia hosiei transcription factors

The candidate NAC and MYB protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana were downloaded from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles of the NAM (PF02365) and MYB (PF00249) domains were obtained from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). HMMER (http://hmmer.org) was used to search for putative NAC and MYB genes in the O. hosiei and Populus trichocarpa genomes with default parameters. In parallel, BLASTP searches (E-value < 1e − 5) were performed against the predicted O. hosiei and P. trichocarpa protein sequences using the A. thaliana NAC and MYB proteins as queries. Arabidopsis and Populus were selected as reference species because they are among the best-studied models for secondary cell wall formation and wood development, facilitating identification of functionally conserved homologs in O. hosiei. The candidate protein sequences were then aligned using MAFFT v7.525 [73], and the alignments were trimmed using trimAl [78]. Phylogenetic trees of the NAC and MYB families were constructed using the maximum likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE v3.0.1 [74] with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, and the best-fit substitution model was automatically selected. The resulting trees were visualized with iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).
