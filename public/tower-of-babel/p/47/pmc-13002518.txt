History of Presentation

A 74-year-old woman was referred to the cardio-oncology team after detection of a cardiac mass. She initially presented to her local emergency department with an episode of atrial fibrillation. Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) suggested a possible left atrial (LA) appendage thrombus, prompting anticoagulation switch from direct oral anticoagulant to low–molecular weight heparin. Follow-up TTE suggested thrombus reduction, although with limited views. After a further episode of atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, pleuritic pain, and breathlessness, a computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiogram ruled out an acute pulmonary embolism but detected a mixed-density lesion (4.9 × 4.1 × 3.4 cm) inferior to the pulmonary trunk and superior to the LA.

While awaiting further evaluation, the patient developed cardiac tamponade requiring urgent pericardiocentesis, which drained serosanguineous fluid. Three weeks later, she presented with a collapse. A CT of the head ruled out acute pathology, but a CT aortogram revealed 2 LA masses, one of them infiltrating the myocardium, concerning for neoplasia.

In light of her rapidly changing clinical course, she was transferred to our tertiary cardiothoracic hospital for further expert cardio-oncology assessment. On arrival, she was hemodynamically stable, in sinus rhythm, without signs of heart failure.

Past Medical History

The patient's background included hypertension, type 2 diabetes, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, obstructive sleep apnea, and Sjogren syndrome.

Differential Diagnosis

At that point, the differential diagnosis was a cardiac metastasis from another malignancy or a primary cardiac tumor.

Investigations

TTE confirmed a large, heterogeneous extracardiac mass in the pericardial space, extending adjacent to the lateral left ventricle wall, pulmonary artery, and LA, with heterogeneous perfusion (Figure 1).

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated a large, heterogeneous mass with a significant vascular component occupying the left pericardial space, invading the LA and giving rise to 2 intracavity masses. The mass exhibited heterogeneous T1 and T2 signal with marked first-pass perfusion enhancement, suggesting a high degree of vascularity, and heterogeneous enhancement on early and late contrast phases (Figure 2, Video 1). These findings were highly suggestive of angiosarcoma.

Cardiac CT confirmed a large vascularized heterogenous extracardiac mass with 2 spherical opacities in the left atrium. A CT coronary angiogram suggested possible flow-limiting disease in the left anterior descending artery (Figure 3). However, invasive angiography showed normal coronary arteries, without any significant epicardial coronary stenosis. Intriguingly, numerous small vessels supplying the tumor originated from the circumflex and the right coronary artery, with a large feeder vessel from the proximal left anterior descending artery (Figure 4).

Management

The case was reviewed in an urgent multidisciplinary team meeting with cardiologists, cardiac imaging specialists, cardiac surgeons, and sarcoma oncologists. A probable diagnosis of cardiac angiosarcoma was made based on the patient's clinical history and multimodality imaging. Three management options were explored: tumor resection (deemed unfeasible owing to extensive infiltration), debulking of atrial masses (technically possible but with unclear clinical benefit), and surgical biopsy using direct visualization, with chemotherapy planned based on histopathology.

After extensive discussion, surgical biopsy was recommended. However, given the tumor's high vascularity, hemorrhage risk was a major concern. Embolization of the feeding vessels was explored to reduce vascularity and improve biopsy safety. However, a review of the angiogram revealed diffuse tumor vascularization via multiple small vessels, excluding embolization as an option.

A minimally invasive surgical biopsy was attempted via mini-sternotomy, revealing a highly vascularized and pulsatile tumor encasing the pulmonary artery. Given the high risk of uncontrollable bleeding, the procedure was abandoned. During a postsurgical meeting with the oncology team, it was reinforced that histopathological confirmation was ideally required before initiating treatment. Positron emission tomography (PET)–CT was performed to identify any metastatic disease for a potential biopsy target, but no extracardiac disease was found (Figure 5).

At this point, a percutaneous biopsy of the LA mass through a transseptal approach was considered. However, a preprocedural transesophageal echocardiogram revealed heterogeneous, vascularized atrial masses, with the septal mass being highly mobile, raising concerns of embolization risk. Consequently, percutaneous endovascular biopsy was deemed not possible.

After further discussions with oncology, cardiac surgery, and cardiology, it was concluded that any attempt to obtain tissue diagnosis carried an unacceptably high risk. Surgical biopsy was unfeasible owing to the tumor's extreme vascularity and potential for uncontrolled bleeding with a fatal outcome. Similarly, a percutaneous biopsy posed significant dangers, including embolization with potential stroke and hemorrhage. Given these risks, a definitive histopathological diagnosis was considered not possible to be obtained safely. The option of testing for circulating tumor cells or circulating DNA was discussed, but these are not routinely available for sarcoma.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Recognizing the strong clinical and imaging-based suspicion of cardiac angiosarcoma, the oncology team agreed to the initiation of empirical chemotherapy. The patient was initiated on paclitaxel, which was switched to abraxane after 3 infusion reactions. Unfortunately, after starting chemotherapy, the patient was admitted owing to infection and fluid overload, with a significant clinical deterioration. She was considered unfit for more chemotherapy, and she was referred for palliative care.

Discussion

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, whereas cardiac metastases of systemic tumors are more frequent.1 The majority of primary cardiac tumors are benign, with only 10% reported to be malignant.2 Multimodality imaging plays a key role in the evaluation of cardiac masses, although histopathology remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis. However, obtaining tissue can be challenging or even impossible in some cases.

We present a patient with a rapidly growing, highly vascularized cardiac mass, raising strong suspicion for cardiac angiosarcoma. Multimodality imaging played a crucial role in refining the differential diagnosis, but a definitive tissue diagnosis was required before initiating targeted therapy. The case was particularly challenging given the significant hemorrhagic risk associated with the tumor's location and extensive vascularization, which limited options for obtaining tissue diagnosis.

Despite their rarity, primary cardiac tumors should always be considered a differential diagnosis of cardiac masses. Their clinical manifestations and trajectory can be variable, from incidental findings to severe symptoms such as cardiac tamponade or collapse. Their prognosis and treatment differ according to the type of tumor, which makes histopathology fundamental to guide management.3, 4, 5 Different techniques for tissue diagnosis include cytological evaluation of pericardial fluid, thoracoscopic approach, percutaneous cardiac biopsy, and cardiac surgery. However, tissue diagnosis can be challenging in some cardiac tumors or even not possible depending on anatomical localization and degree of vascularization, as was evident in this case.

Multimodality imaging has a crucial role in cardiac tumors assessment, and it is fundamental for guiding diagnosis and treatment, especially when tissue diagnosis cannot be obtained.6, 7, 8 Echocardiography is usually the primary diagnostic modality and is useful for understanding the hemodynamic significance of the mass. Furthermore, contrast agents can be used to assess its perfusion and rule out thrombi.9 Cardiac CT and CMR can provide more detailed assessment of morphology, size, invasiveness, and vascularity. CMR is useful in providing a noninvasive tissue characterization given the ability to undertake repeated image plans using multiparametric tissue characterization.8 PET-CT provides information about the metabolic activity of the tumor, and it can be helpful in the identification of malignancy and detecting metastases.10

Conclusions

This case highlights the complexity of the diagnosis and management of cardiac tumors, where both the tumor's histopathology and anatomical considerations posed significant challenges. It underscores the critical role of a multidisciplinary team—including clinical cardiologists, imaging specialists, interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and oncologists—in navigating these complexities and optimizing patient outcomes. Moreover, it demonstrates the need for personalized decision-making in rare and aggressive malignancies where standard diagnostic pathways may not always be feasible.
