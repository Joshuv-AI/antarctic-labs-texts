Genome-wide identification, characterization and expression analysis of the CesA/Csl superfamily in Boehmeria nivea and its feral species
Authors: Duqing Wu, Ping Chen, Gang Gao, Kunmei Chen, Jia Chen, Xiaofei Wang, Aiguo Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cellulose synthase (CesA) and cellulose synthase-like (Csl) superfamily functions as the core enzymatic system for plant cell wall biosynthesis and is pivotal for bast fiber development. Boehmeria nivea (ramie) is a economically vital bast fiber crop, yet systematic analysis of its CesA/Csl superfamily remains lacking.ResultsIn this study, a total of 9 CesA and 22 Csl genes was identified in cultivated ramie B. nivea and 7 CesA and 20 Csl genes in feral ramie B. nivea var. tenacissima. Phylogenetic analysis clustered these genes into one CesA subfamily and six Csl subfamilies, with CesA genes further divided into primary cell wall and secondary cell wall subgroups. Chromosomal localization showed scattered distribution and tandem duplication events. Syntenic analysis revealed high conservation of CesA/Csl loci between the two varieties, although a CesA8, CesA10 and several Csl orthologs were not detected in the current feral genome assembly. Promoter analysis identified abundant light-, phytohormone-, and MYB-responsive cis-elements. Expression profiling demonstrated high expression of BnCesA4/7/8 in stems and middle bark tissues, where bast fibers underwent active tertiary cell wall thickening, suggesting their potential involvement in tertiary cell wall biosynthesis during fiber development. Protein structural modeling verified that BnCesA4 contained conserved catalytic residues and formed a canonical homotrimeric complex.ConclusionsThis study revealed evolutionary conservation and expression divergence of the CesA/Csl superfamily in ramie, and identified BnCesA4/7/8 as potential key candidate genes regulating G-layer cellulose biosynthesis. These results established a foundational genomic resource for further functional studies of fiber development and cell wall biosynthesis in ramie.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09424-7.

Introduction

As core structural elements of plant cells, cell walls (CWs) are not only essential for maintaining cellular integrity but also serve as pivotal regulators of multiple physiological processes, including mechanical support, intercellular communication, environmental signal transduction, and the coordination of growth and differentiation [1, 2]. Plant cell walls are classified into primary cell walls (PCW), secondary cell walls (SCW), and specialized tertiary cell walls (G-layers), each with distinct composition, ultrastructure, and physiological function [2–4]. PCWs are formed during cell expansion, relatively thin, and highly flexible; dicot PCWs are rich in pectin and xyloglucan, whereas monocot PCWs contain abundant mixed-linkage glucans. SCWs are deposited after cell elongation stops, providing mechanical strength with high cellulose and lignin content. G-layers, typical in many bast fiber cells, are specialized tertiary cell walls mainly composed of pure cellulose with negligible lignin [2–4].

Composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin, and lignin, the proportion of these polymers varies drastically across different wall types. In most plant cell walls, cellulose and hemicellulose constitute 60–90% of the dry weight [4], but this ratio is extremely high in G-layers of fiber cells, where cellulose can account for up to 90% of the wall mass [5]. Given that the CesA/Csl superfamily encodes functionally specialized enzymes for PCW, SCW, or G-layer biosynthesis, clarifying these differences is essential for understanding gene function and fiber development [6].

Cellulose acts as the structural backbone to modulate cell expansion and differentiation, while hemicellulose forms a cross-linked network with cellulose microfibrils via hydrogen bonding, an interaction that is indispensable for preserving wall stability [7]. The cellulose synthase superfamily, a large clade of glycosyltransferase family 2 (GT2) enzymes, stands as the central mediator of cellulose and hemicellulose biosynthesis [8].

Conserved across all land plant lineages, the CesA superfamily is phylogenetically divided into two core subfamilies: CesA proper and cellulose synthase-like (Csl) genes [9]. This superfamily encompasses 12 distinct gene families in total, with the Csl subfamily further subdivided into 11 subgroups (CslA to CslM) [9]. As the founding members of this superfamily, CesA genes form a monophyletic clade that originated from cyanobacteria via horizontal gene transfer in ancestral algae, and subsequently underwent lineage-specific expansion, functional divergence, and subfunctionalization during the evolution of land plants [8]. Angiosperms generally possess 10–20 CesA paralogs, which encode isoforms specifically dedicated to cellulose synthesis in primary or secondary cell walls [10]. Functional CesA proteins assemble into hetero-multimeric cellulose synthase complexes (CSCs) at the plasma membrane, with distinct combinations of CesA isoforms dictating the formation of primary versus secondary cell walls [11–13]. In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, the functional differentiation of CesA members has been clearly defined. CesA1, CesA3, and one of four CesA6-like proteins (CesA2, CesA5, CesA6 and CesA9) are involved in synthesis of primary wall cellulose whereas CesA4, CesA7 and CesA8 are crucial isoforms for synthesis of secondary wall cellulose [13] and mutation often caused dwarfed, shortened hypocotyl length or gametophytic lethal phenotypes [14–16].

Csl genes are primarily responsible for the biosynthesis of diverse hemicellulosic polysaccharides, and their evolutionary relationships and functional differentiation exhibit remarkable complexity [8, 10]. Phylogenetically, the CslB/D/E/F/G/H/J/M families cluster into a monophyletic branch with CesA genes, whereas the CslA/C/K families form a separate clade that arose from independent endosymbiotic events in green algae [10]. Within these lineages, CslD and CslF clades share the closest evolutionary affinity with CesA genes: CslD genes form a sister group to CesAs, while CslF genes represent a Poaceae-specific clade derived from CslD duplication, though the temporal dynamics and molecular mechanisms underlying this duplication event remain unresolved [8, 9]. Functionally, several Csl subfamilies have been well characterized: CslF, CslH, and CslJ catalyze the synthesis of (1,3;1,4)-β-glucans (mixed-linkage glucans, MLG) in Poaceae, yet recent studies have revealed that certain barley CslF homologs can also produce (1,4)-β-linked glucoxylans, challenging the traditional view of their substrate specificity [17, 18]. CslA genes are involved in mannan and glucomannan biosynthesis, CslC isoforms mediate xyloglucan synthesis, and CslD genes are hypothesized to synthesize non-crystalline cellulose in tip-growing cells and may also participate in mannan production [19–22]. In contrast, the biological functions of the CslB, CslE, CslG, and CslM subfamilies remain largely uncharacterized [9]. Beyond their canonical role in cell wall biosynthesis, CesA/Csl genes exert pleiotropic effects on plant growth, development, and stress adaptation.[23]. Alterations in CesA/Csl expression or function via mutation or genetic regulation can modulate a range of phenotypic traits, including pollen tube elongation, root system architecture, vegetative organ morphogenesis, and grain yield across diverse plant species [24–26]. In terms of stress response, high-temperature stress regulates rice CslF6 expression through kinase-dependent phosphorylation, while CslA and CslD subfamilies exhibit divergent functions in mediating drought and freezing tolerance in orchids [27]. For economically important crops, CesA/Csl genes are tightly linked to key agronomic traits: in cotton, over 94% of raw fiber is composed of cellulose, and CesA/Csl-mediated cellulose biosynthesis directly determines fiber strength, length, and elasticity. In Eucalyptus grandis, a fast-growing tree species with substantial industrial applications, the lack of systematic identification of the CesA/Csl family has hindered progress in molecular breeding for improved wood quality [28].

Despite significant advancements in dissecting the molecular biology of CesA/Csl genes, several critical knowledge gaps remain to be addressed. First, the evolutionary pathways driving the diversification of the CesA superfamily-including the timing of key gene duplication events, the contribution of genomic structural variations to family expansion, and the evolutionary constraints linking phylogenomic patterns to functional divergence-are not fully understood [9]. Second, functional characterization of Csl subfamilies is incomplete: the molecular mechanisms bridging gene mutations to phenotypic variation, as well as the potential multifunctional roles of Csl proteins in polysaccharide biosynthesis, remain elusive. Third, genome-wide analyses of the CesA superfamily, encompassing syntenic relationships, selection pressure dynamics, and spatiotemporal expression profiles, are lacking in many fiber-producing crops such as Boehmeria nivea [9, 29].

Boehmeria nivea, one of the most important fiber-producing crops in China, has significant economic, ecological and social values, and is widely used in texture and other industrial fields [30]. With the release of high-quality genome assemblies for cultivated ramie B. nivea and its feral relatives Boehmeria nivea var. tenacissima [31, 32], comparative genomic analysis has become feasible. Notably, B. nivea var. tenacissima is a bona fide feral variety of cultivated ramie, rather than an independent species [32, 33]. It shows distinct morphological and agronomic characteristics, including weaker plant vigor, thinner stems, shorter and coarser fibers, and lower cellulose content compared with cultivated ramie [32]. During domestication, ramie has undergone strong selection for enhanced fiber yield and quality, which are closely associated with secondary cell wall cellulose biosynthesis. Therefore, comparative analysis of the CesA/Csl superfamily between cultivated and feral ramie can reveal key genes and evolutionary changes underlying fiber improvement.

To date, existing studies on CesA/Csl superfamilies have mainly focused on model plants and major fiber crops such as cotton and flax, while systematic research on bast fiber crops represented by ramie (B. nivea) is largely blank. Cotton fibers are single-celled seed trichomes with extremely high cellulose content, and its CesA/Csl family exhibits massive expansion and seed-specific high expression [34]. Distinct from cotton seed fibers, flax fibers originate from plant phloem, in which members of the CesA/Csl superfamily coordinate fiber elongation and secondary cell wall deposition [35, 36]. As another representative phloem bast fiber crop, ramie fibers originate from stem phloem parenchyma cells and undergo unique G-layer thickening shaped by long-term domestication [32]. However, flax fibers consist of dozens of elementary fibers bonded together into bundles, while ramie fiber exists as individual single fibers [37]. At present, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding whether ramie CesA/Csl genes differ evolutionarily in copy number dynamics, functional divergence, and transcriptional regulatory patterns relative to homologs in cotton, flax, and other fiber crops. Furthermore, the pivotal genes governing ramie bast fiber developmental pathways have yet to be identified. This key scientific question has not been answered, which seriously hinders the molecular improvement of ramie fiber quality.

Bast fiber crops produce long, high-quality bast phloem fibers in stem bark, which undergo a two-phase developmental program. In the early developmental stage, fiber cells elongate rapidly and assemble thin primary cell walls to support cell expansion [35]. After elongation ceases, fibers initiate deposition of a thick, cellulose-rich tertiary cell wall universally termed the G-layer, which largely determines fiber tensile strength and yield [3]. Unlike lignified secondary cell walls found in xylem tissue, bast fiber G-layers contain minimal lignin and are dominated by highly crystalline cellulose synthesized by specialized SCW-type CesA enzymes [3]. Genes governing G-layer biosynthesis are therefore central regulators of agronomic fiber traits, motivating our genome-wide comparative analysis of the CesA/Csl superfamily between cultivated ramie and its wild progenitor.

In view of the important roles of CesA and Csl genes in plant growth and development, we integrated phylogenomic, syntenic, and functional analyses of the CesA superfamily across a broad phylogenetic range of plant species, from herbal plants to woody plants, with a focus on an economically valuable bast fiber crop B. nivea and its feral species. By identifying CesA/Csl family members, dissecting their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, cis-acting regulatory elements, expression patterns, and selection pressures, we aimed to clarify the evolutionary mechanisms underlying the diversification of the CesA/Csl superfamily, fill gaps in functional characterization, and establish a theoretical framework for genetic improvement of plant traits related to cell wall synthesis and stress tolerance. These findings will advance our understanding of plant cell wall evolution and provide novel strategies for manipulating CesA/Csl genes to enhance crop productivity and industrial utility.

Similar with CesA genes identified in other plants, all BnCesA and BoteCesA contains both CeS domain and Zinc-binding RING-finger domains. BnCesA proteins varied from 1063 to 1359 amino acids, while BoteCesA proteins ranged from 700 to 1090 amino acids (Table S2). The relatively short sequence of BoteCesA4 was attributed to incomplete gene prediction from the existing feral ramie genome assembly. By contrast, Csl proteins exhibited a wide range of sequence lengths from 540 to 1875 aa. This variation was primarily attributed to the differences in non-conserved sequences and terminal domains among distinct Csl subfamilies, implying potential structural differentiation among subfamilies. All BnCesA/Csl physiological and biochemical characters and subcellular location predictions were listed in Table S2 and Table S3.

Most BnCesA proteins showed negative hydropathicity (GRAVY) values (Table S2), representing a hydrophilic nature, a conserved trait widely observed in plant CesA proteins. Combined with predicted plasma membrane localization, the hydrophilic cytoplasmic regions and hydrophobic transmembrane domains constitute the basic structural basis for the assembly of cellulose synthase complexes. BnCesA proteins also showed high structural stability (instability index < 50) and typical isoelectric point characteristics consistent with canonical CesA proteins [38], which were conducive to maintaining normal cellulose synthesis in ramie fiber cells.

To explore the structural characteristics and evolutionary patterns of CesA/Csl genes, the exon/intron structure of each member of the cellulose synthase family was analyzed (Fig. 2C). Evolutionary dynamics of specific CesA/Csl gene families

To further understand CesA superfamily characteristics during fiber crop evolution, CesA/Csl members were also identified in numerous plants, with ramie and its feral species (B. nivea and B. nivea var. tenacissima), hemp (Cannabis sativa), jute (Corchorus capsularis), kenaf (Hibiscus cannabinus), flax (Linum usitatissimum) to represent bast fiber crop, and cotton (Gossypium raimondii) and kapok tree (Bombax ceiba) to represent seed fiber crop, and pineapple (Ananas comosus), coconut (Cocos nucifera), walnut (Juglans regia), black cottonwood (Populus trichocarpa), field elm (Ulmus minor) to represent woody plants, and Arabidopsis thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa), maize (Zea mays), herbaceous bamboo (Raddia guianensis), soybean (Glycine max), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) to represent herbal plants (Table 1). Fiber crops typically accumulated much higher levels of cellulose than herbaceous plants, while they did not have a larger CesA/Csl gene repertoire than A. thaliana and other non-fiber herbaceous species (Table 1). This finding further confirmed that the CesA/Csl gene copy number was not positively correlated with cellular cellulose accumulation. Combined with previous studies, the high cellulose content in bast fibers was mainly achieved via elevated expression of key functional genes and coordinated regulation of cell wall biosynthetic pathways, rather than gene family expansion. Furthermore, slightly more CesA/Csl genes were identified in cultivated ramie (B. nivea) compared with feral ramie (B. nivea var. tenacissima). This numerical difference was most likely attributable to incomplete genome assembly and annotation discrepancies between the two assemblies, rather than gene duplication events occurring during domestication. Validating potential gene duplication associated with domestication will require high-quality, gap-free genome assemblies and further comparative genomic analysis in follow-up studies.

In order to further characterize CesA/Csl superfamily evolution and function relevance, a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using the CesA/Csl protein sequences of B. nivea, B. nivea var. tenacissima and Arabidopsis. CesA/Csl superfamily were divided into seven major clades, namely one CesA subfamily and six Csl subfamilies (CslA, B, C, D, E and G) (Fig. 3). Overall, these seven CesA/Csl superfamily phylogenomic clades are genomically independent from each other, with seldom interclade syntenic connections (Fig. 3). Considering that Arabidopsis CesA1/2/3/5/6/9 were reported to be responsible for primary CW formation, whereas Arabidopsis CesA4/7/8 played a role in secondary CW formation [13], CesA clade was clearly grouped into two subclades, which elucidated the subfunctions of CesA superfamily (Fig. 3). Notably, orthologs of BnCesA8, BnCesA10 and a few Csl members were not identified in the current genome assembly of B. nivea var. tenacissima genome. This absence was most likely attributable to incomplete genome assembly, sequencing gaps, or annotation limitations at these complex loci, rather than true biological gene loss, given the essential and conserved function of these genes in cell wall synthesis.

To further clarify the evolutionary conservation and phylogenetic relatedness of the CesA/Csl superfamily in ramie, comparative syntenic maps were constructed using B. nivea, B. nivea var. tenacissima and model plant A. thaliana (Fig. 4B). A total of 22 orthologous CesA/Csl gene pairs were detected between B. nivea and A. thaliana, and 23 orthologous pairs between B. nivea var. tenacissima and A. thaliana. In addition, substantial conserved collinear gene pairs were identified between B. nivea and a broad range of representative plant species, including major bast fiber crop (C. sativa, L. usitatissimum, H. cannabinus, C. capsularis)(Figure S4).These widespread cross-species homologous relationships indicated that the CesA/Csl superfamily has maintained strong evolutionary conservation across dicot and monocot lineages.Extensive collinear regions are commonly observed for this gene family across different plant species [27, 34].

To investigate the selective pressure of these homologous gene pairs, their Ka/Ks values were calculated. The Ka/Ks ratio of most homologous CesA pairs and all tandem duplicated gene pairs were less than 1 (Tables S4). This pattern was typical for functionally essential gene families, reflecting prevailing purifying selection that acted to conserve the core biological functions associated with cell wall biosynthesis.

Analysis of cis-acting elements in B. nivea and its feral species

To further characterize the regulated mechanism of CesA/Csl genes in B. nivea, we analyzed the cis-acting elements in their 2000 bp upstream promoter regions in B. nivea and its feral species. Among 31 B. nivea and 27 B. nivea var. tenacissima CesA/Csl members, a total of 52 response elements were predicted (Fig. 5). The identified cis-acting elements were associated with five types, including environment response elements, phytohormone response elements, life stage elements, MYB elements and tissue specific elements (Fig. 5, Figure S1). Remarkably, a large number of light responsive elements were identified in CesA/Csl superfamily of both B. nivea and its feral species, indicating light may play an essential role in regulation of cellulose biosynthesis. As illustrated in Fig. 6A and 6B, nearly all CesA genes displayed abundant transcription in the three tested organs of both ramie materials, with the exception of CesA9. Highly expressed CesA genes were enriched specifically in stems, matching the location of bast fiber and secondary cell wall development (Fig. 6A&B). For the Csl gene family, BnCslA2, BnCslA3, BnCslA7, BnCslA9, BnCslC4, BnCslC5 and BnCslC12 showed dominant expression in stems, while other Csl members were preferentially expressed in leaves or roots. This distinct expression divergence demonstrated functional differentiation within the Csl subfamily, and suggested that CslA and CslC subfamilies may participate in bast fiber development (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, tandem duplicated gene pairs shared highly similar expression profiles (Fig. 6A), which indicated that these duplicate genes are governed by identical transcriptional regulatory networks and may possess functional redundancy.

We further compared the organ-specific expression patterns of CesA and Csl family genes between B. nivea and its variety B. nivea var. tenacissima. The universal expression of most CesA/Csl genes and the stem-preference of key fiber-related CesA members were fully conserved (Fig. 6B). To further investigate the roles of the CesA/Csl superfamily in ramie fiber development, we compared gene expression levels in different ramie bark organs. BnCesA1/2/3/6/10 expressed higher in the apical bark region where the bast fiber cells remained immature without tertiary cell walls thickening, whereas BnCesA4/7/8 expressed higher in the middle bark region where the fiber cells underwent active tertiary cell wall deposition [41] (Fig. 6A), further supporting the hypothesis that BnCesA4/7/8 may contribute to bast fiber development. Remarkably, BnCslA3 expressed higher in middle part of the bark, indicating BnCslA3 was probably involved in bast fiber development (Fig. 6A).

To verify the reliability of RNA-seq data and clarify key genes regulating bast fiber development, we selected representative CesA/Csl genes for qRT-PCR validation based on stem-specific high expression, and secondary cell wall association. We analyzed the expressional levels between BnCesA4/7/8, BnCslA2/A3/A7/A9/C4/C5/C12 in B. nivea and B. nivea var. tenacissima cultivars (Fig. 6B).CesA8 and CslA7 orthologs of feral ramie could not be recovered from the current genome assembly or by PCR, but its potential presence cannot be ruled out without long-read sequencing or Southern blot. Moreover, as shown in the Fig. 6B and Table S6, qRT-PCR analysis revealed tissue expression trends consistent with RNA-seq transcriptome profiles. BnCesA4/7/8, BnCslA2/A3/A7/A9/C4/C5/C12 expressed highly in stems but low in both leaves and roots, indicating these genes may play an essential role in bast fiber formation.

Previous cryo-EM study verified that poplar CesA8 and cotton CesA7 proteins formed a functional homomultimeric cellulose synthase complex (PDB Entry: 6WLB and 8VHZ) on plasma membrane to play a role in secondary CW formation [42–44]. Combined with phylogenetic classification and spatiotemporal expression patterns, BnCesA4 was supposed as a core SCW-type cellulose synthase participating in ramie bast fiber G-layer thickening. Therefore, BnCesA4 was selected as a representative SCW-type CesA for in-depth structural analysis. Here, we exploited AlphaFold2 to respectively resolve a monomer and homotrimer complex of BnCesA4 (Fig. 7A and C).

The monomer model of BnCesA4 displayed a typical CesA fold, with a soluble cytoplasmic catalytic domain and seven transmembrane helices (TMHs) forming a potential glucan translocation channel (Fig. 7A), which was the prerequisite for its catalytic function on the plasma membrane. Multiple sequence alignment revealed that the catalytic residues (D360, D526, D763, W805) as well as the plant-conserved region (PCR) and class-specific region (CSR) were fully conserved in BnCesA4, BnCesA7, poplar PttCesA8, and cotton GhCesA7 (Fig. 7D), supporting its conserved catalytic function in cellulose biosynthesis of SCW-type CesA.

Homotrimeric models have been widely used as a validated structural paradigm in plant CesA studies, providing a simplified framework for analyzing conserved catalytic residues, transmembrane topology, and domain organization across species. In this study, the BnCesA4 homotrimer model served to confirm that BnCesA4 retaind the canonical C3 symmetrical CesA fold and all key catalytic residues (Fig. 7B, C). This structural model illustrated the conserved molecular architecture of a ramie SCW-type CesA protein and supported the notion that BnCesA4 functioned as a canonical SCW-type cellulose synthase, likely contributing to G-layer cellulose deposition in ramie bast fibers. However, it remaind important to note that homotrimeric assembly has been documented primarily under in vitro conditions and may not fully recapitulate the in vivo architecture of plant cellulose synthase complexes in ramie.

Discussion

The CesA/Csl superfamily constitutes the core enzymatic machinery for cellulose and hemicellulose biosynthesis in plant cell walls [10]. While this gene family has been extensively characterized in model plants and several fiber crops (cotton, flax) [12, 35], systematic analysis in ramie—a perennial bast fiber crop with unique G-layer thickening—has been lacking.This study conducted a genome-wide identification and comparative analysis of the CesA/Csl superfamily in cultivated ramie (B. nivea) and its feral relative (B. nivea var. tenacissima). The results revealed the evolutionary conservation and expression patterns of this gene family, providing new insights into their poteintial roles during bast fiber development.

The number of CesA genes in cultivated ramie (n = 9) is comparable to that in Arabidopsis (10), tomato (10), and rice (10), and lower than in polyploid or recently duplicated genomes such as soybean (29) and cotton (18) (Table 1). Pancaldi et al. (2022) demonstrated that CesA/Csl copy number correlates positively with whole-genome duplication events across 242 plant genomes, but also noted that secondary cell wall CesA clades tend to have fewer copies than primary cell wall clades, reflecting their more specialized and constrained functions [45]. Ramie’s modest CesA copy number, despite its high cellulose content (> 80% in fiber G-layers), supports the view that transcriptional upregulation and post-translational regulation, rather than gene family expansion, drive cellulose hyperaccumulation in bast fibers—a pattern also observed in flax and hemp. The slightly lower CesA count in feral ramie (7 vs. 9) was most likely due to incomplete genome assembly (e.g., the CesA8 locus is absent in the current wild genome assembly) rather than true gene loss during domestication.

Phylogenetic analysis clearly separated ramie CesA proteins into PCW-type (BnCesA1/2/3/6/9/10) and SCW-type (BnCesA4/7/8) clades, mirroring the deeply conserved functional dichotomy established in Arabidopsis and land plants. Pancaldi et al. (2022) showed that primary CesA sequences appeared before secondary CesAs during land plant evolution, with bryophyte and lycophyte CesAs clustering only within PCW clades [45]. This evolutionary primacy of PCW CesAs was consistent with their roles in cell expansion, whereas SCW CesAs emerged later with the evolution of vascular tissues and mechanical support. In ramie, the SCW-type BnCesA4/7/8 were expressed specifically in stems and middle bark, where G-layer thickening occurs, suggesting that these ancient SCW isoforms have been co-opted for tertiary cell wall synthesis in phloem fibers—a role analogous to their function in xylem secondary cell walls but without lignin deposition. The structural conservation of BnCesA4, as revealed by AlphaFold2 modeling, retained all catalytic residues (D,D,D,QxxRW motif), the plant-conserved region, and the class-specific region that were essential for CSC assembly and glucan chain elongation [43, 44]. The homotrimeric model, while not representing the native heteromeric CSC (which likely contained three distinct SCW-type isoforms: CesA4, CesA7, and CesA8), served as a useful template for sequence-structure–function comparisons.

In addition, we did not recover a CesA8 ortholog from the current feral ramie genome assembly. This absence could reflect (i) incomplete genome assembly or annotation gaps (common in repetitive regions of wild relatives), (ii) genuine gene loss during domestication, or (iii) divergence such that the ortholog is not recognizable by homology. Given that functional secondary cell wall CSC in all angiosperms studied required three distinct SCW-type CesA isoforms (CesA4, CesA7, and CesA8), true biological loss would be expected to cause severe cell wall defects. We therefore considered assembly incompleteness the most parsimonious explanation. Resolution of this uncertainty will require long-read sequencing, targeted PCR, or Southern blot analysis of the wild accession.

The 22 Csl genes in cultivated ramie span six subfamilies (CslA, B, C, D, E, G). Among these, CslA and CslC genes–known to mediate mannan/glucomannan and xyloglucan biosynthesis, respectively [45]–showed preferential expression in stems, suggesting they cooperated with SCW-type CesAs to build the hemicellulose network that crosslinked cellulose microfibrils in the G-layer. This was consistent with observations in eucalyptus, where CslA and CslC are co-expressed with SCW-type CesAs during xylem differentiation [46]. Notably, several Csl genes exhibited root- or leaves-specific expression, reminiscent of the role of CslD1 and CslD4 in Arabidopsis root hair and pollen tube tip growth [23]. The Csl subfamilies remained poorly characterized. Their scattered expression patterns in ramie (some leaf or roots-preferential, others stem-enriched) suggested diverse but as-yet-unidentified roles in cell wall remodeling.

Promoter analysis revealed abundant light-responsive, phytohormone-responsive (auxin, gibberellin, MeJA), and MYB-binding cis-elements. MYB transcription factors were master regulators of secondary cell wall biosynthesis, directly activating SCW-type CesA genes by binding to AC-rich elements [47]. The presence of MYB sites in ramie CesA promoters was consistent with this conserved regulatory logic. The high density of light-responsive elements was noteworthy, as light penetration through stem bark may influence fiber cell wall deposition, though direct evidence was lacking. The abundance of anaerobic response elements (ARE) may reflect adaptation to waterlogged soils—a common stress in ramie cultivation.

Several important caveats should be acknowledged. First, our expression data were derived from whole-stem or whole-bark tissues, which contain multiple cell types (phloem fibers, xylem vessels, cambium, cortex, pith). Therefore, stem-enriched expression did not prove fiber-specific expression; cell-type resolution (e.g., laser microdissection, in situ hybridization) will be required to confirm the precise spatial expression of BnCesA4/7/8 in bast fibers. Second,the feral nature of B. nivea var. tenacissima limited the robustness of our conclusions regarding domestication-associated evolution.Future research should incorporate authentic conspecific wild populations, where available, or adopt population genomic approaches using multiple feral and cultivated accessions to disentangle domestication effects from post-escape evolutionary changes. This clarification did not invalidate our core findings identifying candidate genes governing fiber development, but established a proper contextual framework for the comparative analyses presented in this study. Third, our structural modeling of BnCesA4 as a homotrimer was a computational prediction; native CSCs are hetero-oligomeric, and the precise subunit composition in ramie fiber cells remained unknown. Finally, we did not perform functional validation (e.g., virus-induced gene silencing or CRISPR-Cas9 editing) to test the necessity of BnCesA4/7/8 for fiber development, and such experiments are a priority for future work.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Two accessions were used in this study: Cultivated ramie (Boehmeria nivea, Zhongzhu No.1) and feral ramie (Boehmeria nivea var. Tenacissima, Qingyezhuma) – a sympatric feral variety of B. nivea. Feral ramie exhibits significant phenotypic differentiation from cultivated ramie, including lower plant height, smaller stem diameter, shorter fiber length, coarser fiber diameter, and lower cellulose content, as reported in previous comparative genomic and phenotypic studies [29]. Anatomical studies have documented reduced fiber cell wall thickness in this feral variety [30]. These two accessions were closely related genetically but showed significant divergence in fiber development and cell wall composition due to long-term domestication.

The seeds of cultivated ramie “Zhongzhu 1” and feral ramie “Qingyezhuma” were surface sterilized, placed on wet filter paper, and germinated in an incubator at 25 ℃. After 7 days, the germinated seeds were transplanted to potting soil and grown in a greenhouse at the Institute of Bast Fiber Crops, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Changsha, Hunan, China) under 16-h-light/8-h-dark cycle at 25 ℃. Three-month-old plants at the vigorous vegetative growth stage (stem elongation stage, ~ 80 cm in height) were used for sampling.

For bark tissue sampling: Apical bark, bark tissues were dissected from the upper 10 cm segment of the main stem, where fiber cells were in the elongation stage without tertiary cell wall thickening. Middle bark: Bark tissues were dissected from the middle 10 cm segment (30–40 cm from the shoot apex) of the main stem, where fiber cells undergo active tertiary cell wall thickening. All tissues were peeled carefully to avoid xylem contamination, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until use.

For leaves sampling: fully expanded, healthy mature functional leaves located on the middle part of ramie stalks; senescent basal leaves and newly emerging top young leaves were excluded.

For roots sampling: only active lateral root zones with vigorous absorption and growth were collected; aged lignified taproot sections were discarded.

For stems sampling: whole intact middle stalk segments (excluding tender apical stem tips and heavily lignified basal stem parts close to soil) were harvested for total stem tissue samples.

Three independent biological replicates were collected for each sample.

Genome-wide identification of CesA/Csl genes

To identify CesA/Csl family genes across diverse plant species, genome and protein sequences were downloaded from the following public databases: Phytozome 13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov), NCBI RefSeq (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq), Ensembl Plants (https://plants.ensembl.org). The hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the cellulose synthase domain (PF00535 and PF13632) was downloaded from the Pfam database (V36.0). HMMER 3.3.2 was used to search the whole-genome protein sequences with an E-value threshold of 1e−5 and a minimum domain coverage of 70%. All candidate sequences were then verified by BLASTP against the NCBI non-redundant protein database using known CesA/Csl genes from Arabidopsis thaliana (http://www.arabidopsis.org) as queries. Only sequences containing complete conserved cellulose synthase domains were retained. Redundant and incomplete sequences were manually removed to generate the final non-redundant CesA/Csl gene set. The ExPASy databases (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/) were exploited to obtain basic protein information such as peptide length, isoelectric point and molecular weight. The full-length coding sequences of all identified CesA/Csl genes were provided in File S1 for sequence comparison between B. nivea and B. nivea var. tenacissima.

Chromosomal localization of CesA/Csl genes

Genome annotation files of cultivated ramie (B. nivea) and feral ramie (B. nivea var. tenacissima) were obtained from the reference genome under NCBI SRA accession PRJNA663425 and PRJNA663427. Physical chromosomal coordinates of CesA/Csl genes were extracted and mapped to corresponding chromosomes. The chromosomal distribution map (Fig. 1) was generated and visualized using TBtools-II (v1.120) with the default chromosome visualization module. Genes were placed according to their physical positions, and the scale bar was set to represent chromosome length in megabases (Mb).

Sequential and phylogenetic analysis

Gene Structure Display Server v2.0 (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/index. php) was employed to analyze the exon and intron structure of the identified genes. Protein subcellular location was predicted by WoLF PSORT (http://psort.nibb.ac.jp). MEME program (http://meme.sdsc. edu/meme/cgi-bin/meme.cgi) was used to identify the motif of these genes. InterProScan (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/InterProScan/) was used to annotated the motifs. Syntenic relationships of all CesA/Csl were analyzed using Circoletto.

Muscle (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/muscle) was used to perform alignments of CesA/Csl amino acid sequences with default parameters. A phylogenetic tree for relevant CesA/Csl gene groups was constructed using MEGA5 software with the Jones–Taylor–Thornton (JTT) substitution model. Gaps were treated by partial deletion, and branch support was evaluated with 5000 bootstrap replicates.

Analysis of cis-acting elements

A 2000 bp sequence upstream of the transcriptional start site of CesA/Csl was retrieved using "GXF Sequence Extraction" tool integrated in TBtools-II software. Subsequently, these extracted sequences were subjected to comparative analysis against the PlantCare online database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to identify the cis-acting elements of this gene family. The location of cis elements was annotated and visualized using the "Basic BioSequence View" tool in TBtools-II.

For transcription factor binding sites prediction, the corresponding promoter sequences were submitted to the PlantRegMap online platform (http://plantregmap.gao-lab.org/) with the parameter set to a p-value < 1e−7.

RNA-seq expression analysis

Publicly available RNA-seq data generated from ramie B. nivea and its feral species was exploited to analyze the expression of CesA/Csl genes [32, 41]. Raw sequencing data were obtained from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA666067, and SRR8997219 to SRR8997224 [32, 41]. Three independent biological replicates were included for each tissue type, including leaves, roots, stems, apical bark, and middle bark [32, 41]. Gene expression levels were calculated as transcripts per million (TPM) and normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method. Expression heatmaps were generated using log2-transformed and normalized TPM values for unified comparison.

Quantitative RT-PCR

The total RNA was isolated from consistent tissue samples using the RNAiso Plus Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturers' instruction.), followed by being transcribed into cDNA using a HiScript IV All-in-One Ultra RT SuperMix (Vazyme, China). The cDNA was subsequently used as a template for RT-qPCR analysis using primers based on the reference B. nivea and B. nivea var. tenacissima genes sequences. qRT-PCR was performed on a real-time PCR system with gene-specific primers. The primers were designed based on conserved coding regions of orthologous CesA/Csl sequences from cultivated and feral ramie (Table S7) by Primer Premier 5.0, with 40%−60% GC content, appropriate fragment length, and no predicted off-target amplification. A standard curve (90–110%, R2 > 0.99) dilution method was used to detect and verify the amplification efficiency of each primer pair. The actin gene was used as the single internal reference gene for normalization. Leaf tissue of cultivated ramie Zhongzhu 1 was used as the calibrator sample. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Values are presented as mean ± SD. All qRT-PCR experiments were conducted with three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

Identification of orthologous and paralogous gene pairs

Syntenic CesA/Csl gene pairs between B. nivea and B. nivea var. tenacissima, as well as tandem duplicated pairs within each genome, were identified using MCScanX with default parameters. The protein sequences of each pair were aligned using MUSCLE (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/muscle) with default settings, and the corresponding CDS alignments were back-translated using the ‘pal2nal’ script (v14).

Calculation of Ka/Ks

Nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates were calculated using the YN00 model in the KaKs_Calculator 2.0 package. The Ka/Ks ratio was used to infer selection pressure: Ka/Ks > 1 indicates positive selection, Ka/Ks = 1 neutral evolution, and Ka/Ks < 1 purifying selection. Only gene pairs with Ks ≤ 2 (to avoid saturation) were retained for analysis.

Protein structure prediction

Alphafold (https://alphafoldserver.com) was exploited to predict the protein structures with the default multimer prediction pipeline. The reliability of the predicted models was evaluated based on the predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) score and template modeling (TM) score. Additionally, BnCesA4 was predicted to form a homo-multimeric cellulose synthase complex, which were potentially implicated in the synthesis of secondary walls. The assembly mode of homologous complexes was further inferred in combination with typical structural characteristics of plant cellulose synthase complexes reported in previous studies.
