Macrophages: pivotal orchestrators and emerging therapeutic targets in osteoarthritis pathogenesis
Authors: Jia Hu, Jing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is a heterogeneous total joint disease that can cause disability, affecting over 500 million people worldwide. Macrophages play an important regulatory role in OA progression through orchestrating synovial inflammation and degradation of the cartilage matrix. The polarization and inflammatory functions of macrophages are coordinately regulated by multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, mTORC1, Wnt/β-catenin, and MAPK. At present, the pathogenesis of OA is not fully understood, and effective treatment methods are lacking. Therefore, clarifying the regulatory mechanism and therapeutic potential of macrophages in OA provides direction for the development of more effective pharmacological interventions. In this review, we summarize the epidemiology and pathogenesis of OA, highlights the role of macrophages in the development of OA and related molecular targets, and discusses the latest drug research progress in targeting macrophage-mediated pathways in OA.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Macrophage, Polarization, Molecular targets

Introduction

Osteoarthritis is a serious chronic disease characterized by synovitis, cartilage degeneration, and subchondral bone sclerosis, resulting in pain, joint narrowing, and irreversible loss of function (Fig. 1). In 2021, more than 22% of adults over 40 were suffering from knee osteoarthritis (KOA) [1]. At present, OA affects about 5% of people worldwide and causes serious socio-economic burden [2]. However, due to the poorly understood pathogenesis, few effective therapeutic options currently exist to prevent the progression of OA. Joint replacement surgery is a critical method for advanced OA, but prosthetic joints has finite longevity and postoperative complications [3]. Multiple studies have shown that immune cell infiltration, especially macrophages, is crucial in OA inflammation and structural remodeling.

In 1882, Russian immunologist Élie Metchnikoff first identified phagocytic cells through microscopic observation of starfish larval development. His seminal work revealed specialized cells capable of engulfing foreign particles, which he designated as “macrophages”. This discovery enabled Metchnikoff to formulate a hypothesis: these phagocytic cells play an important role of sterile inflammatory responses and organismal homeostasis, opening the prelude to the study of macrophages in inflammation [4]. Macrophages can be classified into tissue-resident macrophages and monocyte-derived macrophages based on their origin. Early studies proposed that macrophages are components of the mononuclear phagocyte system and that tissue-resident macrophages are replenished by circulating blood monocytes. However, subsequent observations in animal models of monocytopenia and in human patients with monocytopenia revealed that the number of tissue-resident macrophages remains largely unaffected, challenging the notion of their continuous dependence on monocyte-derived replenishment [5–7]. According to the latest research, tissue-resident macrophage precursors are derived from erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMPs) and are widely distributed across various tissues throughout the body [8, 9]. During colonization, macrophage precursors are influenced by various cytokines within different tissues, which promote the upregulation of lineage-determining factors (LDFs) and ultimately drive their differentiation into tissue-specific resident macrophages [10–14]. In OA, mechanical injury can trigger tissue-resident macrophages to enter an inflammatory state. Meanwhile, this process leads to the recruitment and activation of monocyte-derived macrophages, which secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), contributing to synovitis progression [15–18]. Macrophage activation phenotypes are broadly classified into pro-inflammatory M1 (classically induced by TLR ligands and IFN-γ) and anti-inflammatory M2 (alternatively activated by Th2 cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13). In the progression of inflammatory diseases, macrophages polarize toward a classically activated M1 phenotype, releasing pro-inflammatory mediators that amplify tissue inflammation. Conversely, during the resolution phase, alternatively activated M2 macrophages become predominant, secreting anti-inflammatory factors that promote inflammation resolution and tissue repair. These findings collectively demonstrate that the dynamic phenotypic transition of macrophages across different disease stages represents a critical regulatory mechanism in inflammatory diseases [19–21].

In addition to functioning as sensors for both humoral and cellular immunity and regulating these processes through autocrine signaling, macrophages also interact with chondrocytes via paracrine secretion, thereby contributing to the maintenance of joint homeostasis [16, 22, 23]. In mice models of arthritis with synovial macrophage depletion, both synovitis and cartilage destruction are exacerbated, indicating that macrophages play a crucial role in regulating inflammation and cartilage remodeling [24–26]. Multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, mTORC1, Wnt, and MAPK, are involved in the regulation of OA and promote macrophage polarization and cartilage degradation [27–30]. (Fig. 2). Therefore, understanding the mechanisms underlying the interactions between macrophage regulatory pathways and chondrocytes in OA is crucial for advancing therapeutic strategies for the disease. In this review, we highlight the regulatory role of macrophages in the OA pathogenesis and provide an overview of current pharmacological approaches targeting macrophages for therapy.

Osteoarthritis

The pathogenesis of OA remains incompletely understood. Current evidence suggests that OA progression is influenced by a multifactorial network involving synovial inflammation, mechanical stress, metabolic dysregulation, and age-related degeneration [31, 32]. These pathogenic factors contribute to pathological features within the joint microenvironment, including enhanced inflammatory responses, aberrant angiogenesis, and imbalanced extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism, collectively creating a complex effect in body. In subsequent research, Hunter and Deveza consolidated the phenotype-endotype-treatment axis, establishing a roadmap for precision-guided standardized management of OA. (Fig. 3).

It is notable that although aging does not directly cause osteoarthritis, it remains the most significant risk factor for the disorder [34]. A 2014 Spanish study including over 3 million participants demonstrated that the incidence of OA in women peaked between the ages of 60 and 64 and continued to rise with advancing age [35]. Elevated pro-inflammatory mediators, which are hallmarks of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), may constitute a key mechanism in OA pathogenesis. SASP factors recruit immune cells, such as macrophages, neutrophils, and natural killer cells, and promote inflammation, with macrophages regarded as central to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators [35]. Moreover, aging can influence M1/M2 activation and polarization [36, 37]. SASP factors secreted by hepatic stellate cells drive a shift in macrophage polarization from M2 to M1, suggesting that SASP may participate in the regulation of macrophage activation programs [38].

It is widely accepted that proinflammatory mediators, such as IL-6 and matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13), secreted by the synovial tissue, play an important role in promoting ECM degradation in articular cartilage [39–41]. Using dual-labeled vascular imaging techniques, Walsh and colleagues demonstrated spatial heterogeneity in angiogenesis within osteochondral and synovial regions during OA progression. Specifically, vascular density in the osteochondral region was positively correlated with the extent of histological cartilage damage and clinical symptom severity, whereas angiogenesis in the synovial region was primarily associated with the degree of synovial inflammatory infiltration [42].

Further studies have revealed that both innate immune cells (e.g., dendritic cells and macrophages) and adaptive immune cells (e.g., T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells) are actively involved in OA pathogenesis by releasing a variety of inflammatory mediators and cytokines. (Fig. 4). These immune components modulate key signaling pathways—such as autophagy, ferroptosis, HIF-1, PI3K-Akt, and NF-κB—ultimately contributing to cartilage degradation and abnormal bone remodeling. Bioinformatics and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses have identified macrophages and T cells as the predominant infiltrating immune populations within OA-affected joints [43, 44]. Therefore, targeting immune cell activity and modulating their downstream signaling pathways hold great promise for the development of novel therapeutic strategies for OA.

Histology of OA articular cartilage. Articular cartilage is composed of chondrocytes and the ECM. Cartilage is stratified into the superficial layer, middle layer, and deep layer (calcified cartilage). In OA cartilage, both chondrocytes and the ECM undergo significant alterations. Under stimulation by inflammatory factors and reactive oxygen species (ROS), chondrocytes exhibit cellular hypertrophy and increased apoptosis. The ECM displays enhanced catabolism and diminished anabolism. B cells, T cells, tissue-resident macrophages, and dendritic cells are activated, secreting inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1, thereby participating in joint immune responses. Stimulated by inflammatory chemokines within OA joints, monocyte-derived macrophages are recruited from the blood into the joint. Concurrently, synovial hyperplasia and fibrosis are observed in OA joints, accompanied by vascular invasion of the subchondral bone

During the progression of OA, the expression of numerous genes is upregulated, many of which may serve as potential therapeutic or diagnostic biomarkers. Recent studies have identified several key proteins—colony-stimulating factor 1 Receptor (CSF1R), CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein Beta (CEBPB), CX3C chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), and toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7)—as potential biomarkers for OA diagnosis [45–47]. Notably, CSF1R (a tyrosine kinase receptor) and CEBPB (a transcription factor) have been shown to play critical roles in regulating macrophage polarization during OA development, thereby influencing local inflammation and tissue remodeling. Yang et al. reported IL-6 and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) are key pathogenic genes in KOA, and demonstrated their strong diagnostic performance, particularly in advanced stages of the disease [48]. Additionally, Liu et al. constructed a macrophage-associated gene diagnostic signature (MAGDS), comprising IL-1β, complement component 5a receptor 1 (C5AR1, a G protein-coupled receptor), Fc fragment of IgG receptor IIb (FCGR2B, a protein coding gene), IL-10, IL-6, and TYRO protein tyrosine kinase binding protein (TYROBP), using protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis combined with machine learning approaches. Patients exhibiting higher MAGDS scores were found to have elevated levels of immune cell infiltration and MMP expression, which are associated with more severe disease progression and poorer prognosis [49].

Macrophage polarization: concepts and mechanisms

Macrophage polarization refers to the process by which the functional state of macrophage activation is determined at a given point in space and time. Due to the high plasticity of macrophages, which enables them to integrate signals from pathogens, damaged tissues, and normal tissue environments, polarization is not static [50]. To link the pathways involved in polarization to the pathophysiological processes of disease, the macrophage field has reached partial consensus on describing macrophage activation phenotypes. For example, M1 macrophages primarily arise in inflammatory environments mediated by TLRs and interferons, where they secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and COX-2, which are typically associated with responses against intracellular pathogens. M2 macrophages, also known as wound-healing cells, exist in a TH2-mediated environment and secrete cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-10, which have been widely demonstrated to have anti-inflammatory and tissue-repairing effects [19, 21].

The regulatory mechanisms governing macrophage polarization are highly sophisticated. Numerous signaling pathways have been shown to participate in macrophage metabolic reprogramming, which regulates the progression of inflammatory diseases. Studies indicate that during M1 polarization, IFN-γ, GM-CSF, and TNF-α activate the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, whereas LPS triggers the MAPK signaling pathway. Conversely, IL-4/IL-13 activates the JAK/STAT6 signaling pathway and regulates transcription factors such as PPARγ and IRF4 to promote M2 macrophage polarization [51, 52]. Additionally, multiple pathways including TGF-β, PI3K/Akt, and Notch have been shown to regulate the phenotypic switch between M1 and M2 macrophages [53].

Macrophages and osteoarthritis

Macrophages play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of OA. Clinical evidence from SPECT-CT imaging in knee OA patients receiving etarfolatide injection revealed macrophage activation in 76% of cases, with a positive correlation between macrophage infiltration and both the degree of joint degeneration and pain severity [54]. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that the CRP neoepitope (CPRM), generated by MMP-mediated cleavage, serves as a biomarker for cartilage degradation and may provide a quantitative alternative to etarfolatide imaging in assessing synovial inflammation [55].

The M1/M2 polarization delineates the bipolar extremes of macrophage functional plasticity. While this framework remains instrumental in dissecting inflammation activation mechanisms, emerging evidence highlights its limitations in summarizing the spectrum of in vivo macrophage phenotypic. Within tissue microenvironments, macrophage phenotypes are dynamically influenced by cells pathways, exhibiting continuous phenotypic transitions that defy rigid M1/M2 categorization [56–58]. Despite these complexities, M1 and M2 macrophages retain pathophysiological significance in inflammation-associated disorders, serving as critical investigational anchors [59]. Clinical-pathological correlations in non-traumatic osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) demonstrate an elevation in the bone marrow M1/M2 ratio compared to healthy controls, with this imbalance positively correlating with necrotic lesion volume. Meanwhile, mechanistic insights from the arthritis-resistant MRL/MPJ murine model revealed a predominant synovial M2 macrophage enrichment post-traumatic joint injury. Notably, these M2 subsets exhibited robust triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2, a transmembrane receptor) expression, functionally associated with clearance of inflammatory debris and apoptotic cells. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that within OA, M1 macrophages function as a pro-inflammatory phenotype, while M2 macrophages exhibit an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and their dynamic interplay drives disease progression [60].

Synovial macrophages exhibit catabolic activity, wherein their secretion of inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α) upregulates the proteolytic activities of a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS) and MMPs, driving cartilage matrix destruction in OA [61–63]. In the STR/Ort hyperlipidemia model—a murine model of obesity-associated OA—this pathogenic process is linked to the expansion of CD11c (+) F4/80 (+) CD11b (+) macrophage subpopulations within synovial tissue (ST) and adipose tissue (AT). These macrophages exhibit dual pathological features: hypersecretion of TNF-α and MMP-3 overexpression in the local microenvironment. Macrophage ablation experiments revealed elevated expression of TNF-α and MMP-3 in ST and AT of OA mice, which decreased following macrophage depletion. These findings have established macrophage causal role in cartilage degradation [64]. Mechanistically, TNF-α orchestrates a cross-tissue inflammatory axis by enhancing MMP-3 transcriptional activation in synovial fibroblasts (SFs) and adipocytes through NF-κB p65 pathway [43]. This process is further amplified by IL-1 family cytokines (e.g., IL-1β), which synergize with TNF-α through convergent activation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways. Such interaction causes the activation of cartilage-degrading enzymatic networks [63]. Collectively, these findings delineate animmunometabolic regulatory framework in OA, providing novel therapeutic targets for metabolic OA subtypes.

Emerging evidence supports the synovium as a dynamically regulated estrogen-responsive microenvironment. Estrogen receptors alpha (ERα) high expression within the proliferative synovial lining layer and CD68⁺ macrophage subpopulations, coupled with aberrant endogenous estradiol accumulation, implicates autocrine estrogen signaling in modulating both synoviocytes and macrophage-like cells to regulate local inflammatory homeostasis [65]. Intriguingly, this metabolic-immune interaction extends multidimensionally in obesity-associated OA. While high-fat diet (HFD) alone fails to directly induce cartilage degradation, it exacerbates post-traumatic cartilage degeneration by driving pro-inflammatory phenotypic shifts in synovial CD68⁺ macrophages and establishing an inflammation-primed microenvironment. Further investigations uncover a metabolo-inflammatory pathways in obese OA synovium, characterized by activation of pro-inflammatory cascades (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) alongside suppressed protective adipokine signaling (leptin, lipocalin). These alterations coincide with pathological synovial fat pad atrophy and disrupted metabolic plasticity of adipose progenitor cells [66, 67]. Collectively, these discoveries defining an adipose-immune-cartilage axis that drives OA progression via extracellular matrix remodeling, inflammatory amplification, and metabolic reprogramming.

The chondrocyte-macrophage crosstalk constitutes a pivotal driver of OA initiation and progression. Emerging evidence demonstrates that chondrocyte-derived SRGN (a protein coding gene) facilitates THP-1 macrophage recruitment via C-C motif chemokine ligand 3 (CCL3, a macrophage inflammatory protein) upregulation, thereby exacerbating localized inflammatory responses through paracrine mechanisms [68]. Additionally, Zhang et al. utilized public databases to predict and construct an endogenous RNA network centered on S100A12 (a protein coding gene) within the lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA axis. Through in vitro validation, they demonstrated that S100A12 promotes M1 macrophage polarization via the NF-κB pathway. Concurrently, LINC00894 (a long intergenic non-protein coding RNA) was significantly upregulated in OA and showed a strong correlation with S100A12. The study revealed that LINC00894 likely modulates S100A12 expression through miRNA interactions, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory process in OA. This research highlights the therapeutic promise of LINC00894 as a potential target for OA treatment [69]. Studies demonstrate that multiple genes can restore immune balance in OA by regulating macrophage polarization, particularly the transition from M1-type to M2-type macrophages. Studies have shown that calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP, a neuropeptide) positive sensory neurons act on receptor activity modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) expressed by neutrophils and macrophages via thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) mediation, which inhibits immune cell aggregation, accelerates immune cell apoptosis, and promotes macrophage polarization toward the pro-repair M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing the repair of damaged tissues [70, 71]. Concurrently, CSF1R and CEBPB play crucial roles in macrophage polarization during OA, emerging as potential novel drug targets. Research by Hu et al. demonstrated that CSF1R expression was markedly elevated and CEBPB expression was markedly reduced in synovium of OA patients compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, CSF1R expression was positively associated with M1 macrophage polarization, while CEBPB expression was positively associated with M2 macrophage polarization [45]. Toh et al. also reported increased CSF1R expression in collagen-induced arthritis and serum-transfer arthritis mouse models, and found that blocking CSF1R with the specific monoclonal antibody AFS98 mitigated cartilage destruction [72]. Moreover, CSF1R inhibitors such as imatinib, Ki20227, and PLX3397 have shown promising therapeutic effects in animal arthritis models [17, 73, 74]. Additionally, aspirin was shown to promote CEBPB–IL-4 binding and reduce cartilage loss in OA, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent, though further clinical trials are required for confirmation [75, 76]. Interestingly, Li et al. found that microgel carriers effectively regulate macrophage phenotypic transformation by activating Yes-associated protein (YAP) protein, offering a novel immunomodulatory strategy for OA treatment [77]. Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of macrophages in OA pathogenesis and suggest that targeting macrophage polarization or related signaling pathways may provide innovative therapeutic approaches for OA management.

Molecular targets in immune regulation of osteoarthritis and macrophage polarization

In studies on OA, multiple molecular mechanisms have been found to be involved in disease progression, particularly signaling pathways associated with immune responses and macrophage polarization. Glycoprotein M6B (GPM6B) emerges as a novel OA-associated mediator, demonstrating marked upregulation in fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) and macrophage-like synoviocytes from OA patients. Functional studies show that GPM6B knockdown significantly suppresses inflammatory cytokine production (IL-6, TNF-α) and MMP-3/13 expression in primary FLS cultures, effectively curtailing their proliferative capacity. Preclinical validation in murine OA models further establishes GPM6B expression levels as strongly correlated with disease severity, solidifying its therapeutic target potential [78]. Similarly, pentraxin 3 (PTX3), a long pentraxin secreted by immune cells, has been demonstrated to play an important role in osteoarthritis (OA) in mouse models. Deficiency of PTX3 significantly alleviated joint cartilage degradation and reduced systemic inflammation by inducing M2-type macrophage polarization, further suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for OA [79].

Calcium-binding protein 39 (CAB39) ameliorates chondrocyte injury in OA by activating the AMPK/Sirt1 pathway and promotes the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, indicating that CAB39 plays a pivotal role in the immunomodulation of OA [80]. Meanwhile, reports have indicated that sirtuin 6 (SIRT6, a NAD⁺ dependent deacetylase) expression in M1 and M2 macrophages is decreased and increased, respectively. Inhibiting SIRT6 can promote synovial macrophages to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, induce macrophage M1 polarization and inhibit M2 polarization, showing the hope of SIRT6 as a potential therapeutic target [81]. T cells and macrophages engage in reciprocal regulation and play a critical role in the pathogenesis of OA. Research has demonstrated that nuclear factor of activated T cells 5 (NFAT5, a protein coding gene) is another key factor. NFAT5 was significantly upregulated in OA mouse models, and its deletion alleviated synovial inflammation and osteoarthritic changes in cartilage and subchondral bone, highlighting the immunoregulatory role of NFAT5 in OA, particularly its importance in macrophage recruitment and chondrocyte responses [82]. Additionally, G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2 (GRK2) is a key regulator of inflammatory responses and pathological macrophage phenotypes in various diseases. Studies have shown that early intervention by inhibiting the G beta-gamma- G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2 (Gβγ-GRK2) pathway significantly improved OA therapeutic outcomes by reducing initial inflammatory responses to prevent disease progression [83].

Milk fat globule-epidermal growth factor (EGF) factor 8 (MFG-E8) is an essential bridging molecule between apoptotic cells. In OA mice, the expression of MFG-E8 is downregulated, leading to cartilage damage and excessive osteophyte formation. Further studies confirm that miR-99b-5p inhibits MFG-E8 and activates the NF-κB pathway, thereby exacerbating OA progression [84]. Recent studies reveal that 14-3-3ε, a novel mediator, binds to TLR2 and TLR4 to promote M1 macrophage polarization and enhance the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, while facilitating cartilage inflammation [85]. It may serve as an alarmin in OA and represents a potential therapeutic and prognostic target.

It is noteworthy that in a recent study by Xu et al., increased expression of the proviral integration of Moloney virus 2 (Pim2) kinase was observed in macrophages from patients with inflammatory arthritis and collagen-induced arthritis mice. Their research demonstrated that Pim2 promotes glycolytic reprogramming in macrophages by activating multiple metabolic enzymes, thereby further inducing polarization toward pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages. In this study, Xu and colleagues developed bexarotene (a Pim2 inhibitor)-loaded PLGA nanoparticles. These nanoparticles alleviated inflammatory arthritis by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization, demonstrating significant therapeutic efficacy and confirming Pim2’s potential as a therapeutic target [86].

In summary, these studies highlight the immunomodulatory roles of multiple molecular mechanisms in OA, including macrophage polarization, inflammatory responses, and metabolic regulation of chondrocytes. Future therapeutic strategies targeting these molecules, particularly interventions focused on GPM6B, PTX3, CAB39, NFAT5, Pim2, and other key targets, may offer novel directions for the clinical management of OA (Table 1).

Emerging therapeutic targets for osteoarthritis and macrophage polarization

Application of macrophage polarization in the treatment of osteoarthritis

New drugs therapy

With the increasing elucidation of the pathogenesis of OA, therapeutic strategies are progressively transitioning from traditional symptom-oriented approaches to more comprehensive and targeted intervention models. Resveratrol has been shown to significantly attenuate chondrocyte ferroptosis by regulating the glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), transferrin receptor protein 1 (TFRC), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), thereby decelerating OA progression [44]. Similarly, the natural compound Xibining (XBN) effectively suppresses M1 macrophage polarization via inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, resulting in synergistic alleviation of synovial inflammation and pain sensitization [27]. Further mechanistic investigations have revealed that chrysin mitigates macrophage pyroptosis by modulating the HMGB1/RAGE/PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, thereby relieving neuropathic pain induced by aberrant activation of neuronal ion channels [87]. Tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ) is an inhibitor of oxidative stress-induced injury and apoptosis of human neural stem cells. The current study found that TBHQ inhibited LPS induced activation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, inhibited ROS production, and significantly reduced cartilage destruction in mice with OA [88]. Additionally, carveol exerts dual regulatory effects by promoting macrophage phenotypic switching and restoring reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis. This is achieved through activation of the NRF2/HO-1/NQO1 antioxidant pathway in parallel with inhibition of the NF-κB/MAPK pro-inflammatory signaling axis [89].

Recent advances in the understanding of the inflammasome regulatory network have provided new insights into OA therapy. The hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) donor morpholin-4-ium-4-methoxyphenyl(morpholino) phosphinodithioate (GYY4137) has been shown to effectively suppress NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3, an inflammatory corpuscle protein) inflammasome activation by inducing persulfidation of NF-κB p65, reducing ROS production, improving mitochondrial function, and inhibiting IL-1β release [90]. Compounds such as holomycin and resatorvid target the NLRP3–caspase-1 axis and the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, respectively, demonstrating distinct advantages in disrupting the vicious cycle between chondrocyte pyroptosis and macrophage-mediated inflammation [91, 92].

With regard to the dynamic regulation of macrophage phenotypes, Songorine facilitates M2 polarization by reprogramming cellular metabolic pathways—specifically, by inhibiting glycolysis and enhancing oxidative phosphorylation [93]. Similarly, Kongensin A induces macrophage phenotypic switching via modulation of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling cascade [94]. Notably, oxytocin-stimulated infrapatellar fat pad-derived mesenchymal stem cells (IFP-MSCs) contribute to the reconstruction of an M2-dominant immune microenvironment through enhanced immunomodulatory activity [95]. In addition, squid-derived type II collagen and high molecular weight hyaluronic acid (HMW-HA) regulate macrophage chemotaxis and polarization through the TCPTP–STAT1 and GRP78–NF-κB signaling pathways, respectively [96, 97]. Similarly, hyaluronic acid viscous supplement reduced the expression level of macrophage inflammatory factors in vitro, exhibiting favorable anti-inflammatory effect. Further studies showed that it played a protective role by partially inhibiting the MAPK pathway in chondrocytes and p65/NF-κB signaling in macrophages [28].

Importantly, various bioactive components derived from traditional Chinese medicine have also demonstrated multi-target synergistic regulatory effects in OA management. This is exemplified by three representative components. Studies have revealed that 2,5-dihydroxyacetophenone (DHAP), an extract from Rehmannia glutinosa, suppresses the production of inflammatory factors in macrophages by blocking the ERK1/2 and NF-κB pathways [98]. Additionally, Rehmanniae Radix Praeparata modulates osteoclastic bone resorption by regulating SRC proto-oncogene (SRC) expression, and it likely exerts systemic therapeutic effects in OA by targeting key molecules, including signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) and RELA proto-oncogene (RELA), as well as pathways such as PI3K/AKT and IL-17 signaling [99]. Similarly, formononetin, a phytoestrogen derived from herbal plants, exerts potential therapeutic effects on osteoarthritis by reducing MMP expression, downregulating oxidative stress mediators, pro-inflammatory factors, and chemokines, thereby inhibiting IL-1β-induced osteochondral catabolism and osteoarthritis progression [100]. Moreover, rhein is an anthraquinone drug, directly interferes with the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex, thus inhibiting caspase-1 protease activity and IL-1β production, exhibiting promising therapeutic potential in NLRP3-mediated arthritis [101]. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of natural products for precision-targeted interventions in OA.

Precision therapeutic strategies informed by single-cell omics have seen notable advances and are gradually reaching translational maturity [102]. For instance, the dimethyl fumarate/monomethyl fumarate (DMF/MMF) system ameliorates OA progression by modulating the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Similarly, the novel immunoregulatory factor truncated IK cytokine (tIK) exerts its effects by inhibiting the MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby attenuating macrophage activation and suppressing Th17 cell differentiation—offering a promising avenue for personalized immune modulation [103, 104].

Looking forward, future studies should seek to integrate advanced multi-omics technologies—such as metabolomics and spatial transcriptomics—to comprehensively dissect the interaction networks among key signaling pathways, including ferroptosis, autophagy, and Wnt signaling [105]. In parallel, exploration of synergistic therapeutic strategies involving existing pharmacological agents, such as FK506 (which maintains extracellular matrix integrity) and rapamycin (which modulates mTORC1-mediated fibrotic responses), may offer new opportunities to enhance the efficacy of systemic OA treatment [29, 105] (Table 2).

metabolism

Anti-inflammatory

Biological therapy

In the treatment of OA, increasing attention has been directed toward the roles of cellular polarization and microenvironmental regulation. Recent studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning of cell volume can effectively induce the polarization of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, a process likely mediated via the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. This finding suggests a novel immunomodulatory strategy for OA therapy. Preliminary evidence indicates that polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated regulation of cell volume can alleviate early-stage synovitis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic intervention [40].

Moreover, it has been observed that matrix stiffness plays a critical role in modulating chondrocyte behavior. Optimal matrix stiffness promotes chondrocyte proliferation and enhances exosome secretion, with these exosomes facilitating OA treatment by modulating the proliferation, migration, and inflammatory responses of both chondrocytes and macrophages [77].

In addition, biological components such as the buffy coat—rich in cytokines and growth factors—have demonstrated the ability to support M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization, promote tissue repair, and facilitate the resolution of inflammation, thereby offering new therapeutic avenues for musculoskeletal disorders [106]. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has similarly been shown to suppress M1 macrophage polarization while enhancing M2 polarization, effectively slowing OA progression [107, 108].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUC-MSCs) and human amniotic membrane-derived stromal cells (hAMSCs) have also shown great promise. These EVs appear to mediate therapeutic effects by regulating proteins involved in immune responses and cartilage repair, further underscoring their potential in OA treatment [109, 110]. Notably, elevated levels of microRNA-146a (miR-146a) have been detected in synovial exosomes from OA rat models compared to healthy controls. In vitro studies reveal that miR-146a overexpression reduces chondrocyte apoptosis, while its downregulation leads to the opposite effect. In vivo, exosomes derived from FLS overexpressing miR-146a (miR-146a-FLS-Exos) attenuate articular cartilage damage, reduce chondrocyte apoptosis, and promote M2 macrophage polarization. Mechanistically, miR-146a regulates the TLR4/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling pathway by modulating tumor necrosis factor receptor associated factor 6 (TRAF6) expression, highlighting its central role in innate immune modulation [111].

Furthermore, adipose-derived regenerative cells (ADRCs) have been shown to induce M2 macrophage polarization, effectively delaying OA progression and further affirming the therapeutic value of targeting macrophage plasticity in OA [112].

In summary, these studies collectively highlight a diverse array of immunomodulatory strategies for OA treatment. Approaches that target macrophage polarization, exosome-mediated signaling, and the optimization of biology microenvironments offer substantial promise in achieving more precise and effective clinical interventions for OA in the future.

Biomaterials-based applications

In the treatment of OA, the use of nanomaterials, advanced drug delivery systems, and modulation of macrophage polarization has emerged as a set of promising and increasingly significant research directions. Recent studies have demonstrated that ROS-responsive nanodrug formulations—specifically, polymeric nanoparticles (NPs) coated with fibronectin (FN) and loaded with azabisdimethylphosphate-terminated phosphorus dendrimers (G4-TBP)—can be synthesized into G4-TBP NPs-FN complexes. These nanocomposites not only exhibit potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities, but also effectively scavenge ROS, mitigate hypoxic conditions, and promote M2 macrophage polarization. As a result, they protect macrophages from apoptosis and facilitate osteogenic differentiation of stem cells, collectively contributing to the amelioration of OA-related pathological changes [113].

EVs have demonstrated therapeutic potential in nanotherapy for OA, though their large-scale production remains a barrier to clinical translation. To address this, researchers have employed electromagnetic field (EMF) stimulation and ultra-small superparamagnetic iron oxide (USPIO) nanoparticles to activate mesenchymal stem cells, thereby generating EMF-USPIO-sEVs in high yield. These engineered EVs suppress catabolic processes, promote M2 macrophage polarization, and contribute to cartilage regeneration and immune modulation [114].

Similarly, mesenchymal stem cell-derived nanovesicles (MSC-NVs) encapsulated within a gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel (GelMA) matrix—termed GelMA-NVs—enable the sustained and controlled release of therapeutic vesicles. This system has been shown to reduce the secretion of catabolic factors, enhance ECM synthesis, and induce M2 macrophage polarization, thereby demonstrating marked therapeutic efficacy in murine OA models induced by medial meniscal destabilization [115].

In addition, targeted delivery systems for miRNAs and small-molecule therapeutics have demonstrated unique therapeutic advantages. For example, modulation of miR-146a expression inhibits TRAF6, thereby regulating the TLR4/TRAF6/NF-κB signaling axis, ultimately reducing cartilage degradation and favoring macrophage polarization [111]. In another approach, Bai et al. developed a nano-drug delivery platform using porous microspheres to co-encapsulate platelet-derived growth factor-AB, nucleopoietin, and metformin (PMs@Met). This system facilitates the recruitment of endogenous MSCs and regulates synovial macrophage polarization to promote osteochondrogenic repair. Similarly, Yang et al. constructed M1 macrophage membrane-camouflaged PLGA nanoparticles loaded with verteporfin (MVPs), which attenuated the progression of diabetic osteoarthritis (DOA) by modulating the YAP1/TXNIP signaling axis, thereby inhibiting synovial glycolysis and M1 macrophage infiltration [116, 117].

Furthermore, skeletal stem cells (SSCs) combined with microgel carriers have been shown to induce the emergence of a Pdpn(+) Grem1(+) Ptgs2(+) SSC subpopulation via YAP-mediated nuclear translocation. This subpopulation suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, promotes M2 macrophage polarization, and enhances IL-10 expression, collectively exerting anti-inflammatory effects in OA [118]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of macrophage signaling and immune regulation in bone tissue repair, offering novel therapeutic insights into OA pathogenesis and treatment.

In summary, the modulation of macrophage polarization and immune responses, combined with nanodrug delivery systems and immune microenvironment regulation, offers multiple promising avenues for OA therapy. Future research is anticipated to refine these strategies, facilitating their clinical translation and maximizing therapeutic efficacy.

Surgical therapy

Surgical interventions and histological analyses have offered valuable perspectives on both the treatment and underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of OA. For instance, high tibial osteotomy (HTO), beyond its mechanical benefits in correcting lower limb malalignment, has been shown to attenuate synovial inflammation and promote macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. This indicates a substantial improvement in the intra-articular immune microenvironment of the osteoarthritic knee, underscoring the immunomodulatory potential of surgical interventions in OA management [119].

Additionally, histological analyses following ankle arthroplasty have revealed distinct osteolytic regions characterized by an abundance of polyethylene wear particles, located both intracellularly and extracellularly. These regions are infiltrated by CD163(+) macrophages. Polyethylene debris is known to elicit a foreign body reaction, wherein macrophages engulf the particles via phagocytosis—highlighting a pivotal role for wear particles in the development of osteolysis [120].

Collectively, these findings advance our understanding of OA-related joint damage by elucidating the interplay between surgical techniques, implant materials, and the joint immune environment. They also emphasize the importance of integrating immunological considerations into surgical decision-making and material design for OA treatment.

Physical therapy

Recent studies have highlighted the therapeutic potential of quantum molecular resonance (QMR) technology in the management of OA. QMR treatment has been shown to significantly downregulate the expression of NLRP3 and activated caspase-1 proteins, as well as reduce the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-18 and IL-1β—key mediators of OA-associated inflammation. These anti-inflammatory effects are accompanied by a shift in macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby promoting tissue repair and restoring joint homeostasis [121].

Similarly, chiropractic manipulation (CM) therapy has demonstrated efficacy in an OA model in New Zealand rabbits, where it markedly reduced macrophage infiltration and suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory, fibrotic, and angiogenic factors. These effects contributed to the improvement of subchondral bone quality, cartilage integrity, and synovial condition, ultimately attenuating disease progression [122].

Collectively, these findings suggest that both QMR and CM represent promising non-pharmacological strategies for OA treatment, particularly through their roles in modulating immune responses and enhancing joint tissue regeneration.

Conclusion

Contemporary consensus establishes OA as a multifactorial disease. Synovial inflammation, mechanical stress, metabolic disorders, and age-related degenerative changes interact with each other, affecting the development process of OA. Macrophages play a bridging role in OA pathological processes such as inflammation, loss of extracellular matrix in chondrocytes, and cartilage degradation. M1/M2 macrophage polarization is only one type of macrophage phenotype, but it plays an important role in inflammation research. At present, research has observed that macrophage polarization plays a joint role in regulating therapeutic targets and designing emerging drug mechanisms for OA. Although the inflammatory factors secreted by M1 macrophages play an important role in the pathogenesis of OA, complete depletion of macrophages promotes synovitis and cartilage destruction, and the mutual regulatory mechanism between macrophages and chondrocytes needs further clarification. In HFD model mice, elevated estrogen levels correlate with increased pro-inflammatory macrophages, exacerbating joint inflammation. Notably, this effect does not directly initiate cartilage destruction but accelerates degradation in mechanically damaged cartilage. Additionally, upregulated ERα expression is observed in OA synovium. Therefore, investigating the synergism between estrogen-regulated macrophage pathways and mechanical stress provides critical insights for elucidating OA pathogenesis.

Bone joints and their surrounding structures collectively facilitate movement generation. During the progression of OA, macrophages play a pivotal role in mediating synovial inflammation and articular cartilage damage. However, the influence of macrophages on other periarticular structures, such as muscles, ligaments, and the infrapatellar fat pad, has been relatively understudied. Therefore, investigating the mechanisms underlying macrophage interactions with periarticular tissues is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of osteoarthritis pathogenesis. Moreover, given the complexity of osteoarthritis pathology, single-target therapies are likely to be inadequate, multiple targets and pathways can affect macrophage polarization and cartilage degeneration. Further studies are needed to elucidate the relationship between them and help develop more effective therapeutic drugs to halt or reverse OA in the future.
