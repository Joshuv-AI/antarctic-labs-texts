Targeting PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways for synergic effects on improving features of peripheral diabetic neuropathy
Authors: Vuong M Pham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic neuropathy is one of the most serious and common complications of diabetes with a wide spectrum, affecting 30–50% of diabetic patients. However, the current treatments of this disorder, mainly based on controlling blood glucose level, show an inadequate clinical outcome. Better approaches are needed. In this fashion, it is noted that promoting nerve regeneration and preventing nerve degeneration should be focused on equally and appropriately. In this mini review, how more effective approaches are in targeting PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways in the treatment of peripheral diabetic neuropathy is discussed. Future treatment of peripheral diabetic neuropathy should consider these approaches.Keywords: Combined approaches, Diabetic neuropathy, Insulin

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy is one of the most common complications of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes 1 , affecting 30–50% of diabetic patients 2 . Hyperglycemia is supposed to be the main cause of diabetic neuropathy via four associated pathways including the polyol pathway, the advanced glycation end‐product (AGE) pathway, the protein kinase C pathway, and the hexosamine pathway 3 . These pathways lead to changes in the expression of multiple intrinsic factors such as growth suppressors and extrinsic factors such as neurotrophic factors 3 . It is reported that diabetic neuropathy affects all peripheral nerves including sensory, motor, and autonomic fibers 4 . In humans, diabetic peripheral neuropathy is described as a ‘stocking‐and‐glove’ neuropathy with sensory fibers mainly affected 5 . Schwann cells (SCs) are also affected, ultimately resulting in impaired support to axon regeneration 6 , 7 . In addition to hyperglycemia, other contributors such as hypertension, age, and dyslipidemia contribute to the pathogenesis of diabetic neuropathy 8 .

The current approaches for the treatment of diabetic neuropathy such as blood glucose level control or pain reducing drugs show an inadequate clinical outcome 9 . A previous study showed that once diabetic neuropathy is established, glycemic control can only improve in 25% of patients 10 . It is noted that preventing nerve degeneration and enhancing nerve regeneration should be considered equally for improving the treatment outcome of this complication 11 . Thus, multiple‐target approaches have gained attention. In this mini review, the discussion is focused on how combined intervention is more effective in the treatment of peripheral diabetic neuropathy with specific attention to the crosstalk between PI3K/Akt and MEK/ERK pathways. PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted in chromosome ten) and insulin, respectively, are representative molecules of these pathways.

COMMITTED RELATIONSHIP OF SCHWANN CELLS AND NEURONS

There are some differences in the biological properties of Schwann cells and neurons. However, they are dependent on each other during development as well as in nerve regeneration after an injury.

By forming myelin, SCs play a critical role in homeostasis maintenance, myelination, and providing support and protection for peripheral neurons 4 . Schwann cells are the source of trophic support including nerve growth factor (NGF), brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and glial‐derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) to axons. These growth factors promote axon growth and prevent neuronal apoptosis to maintain the proper function and morphology of peripheral nerves 12 , 13 . In addition, myelin plays a critical role in saltatory conduction 14 .

Interactions between Schwann cells and neurons play a crucial role in nerve regeneration. A delay in these critical interactions may result in impaired nerve regeneration and chronic nerve damage 15 . After an injury, a set of molecular and cellular events occurs at the distal site of the injured nerve known as Wallerian degeneration 16 ; one of the very first events is the degeneration of both axon and myelin at the distal nerve stump. As a natural response to the injury, Schwann cells proliferate and are involved in multiple actions to nerve regeneration. Schwann cells down‐regulate protein zero, myelin basic protein, and myelin‐associated glycoprotein and concurrently up‐regulate cell adhesion molecules, neural CAM, and sets of growth factors such as NGF, BDNF, and GDNF. In addition, together with macrophages, Schwann cells clear cellular debris to facilitate nerve regeneration under the effects of growth factors 17 , 18 . In addition, neurotrophic molecules released by SCs provide directional guidance to regenerating axons to reach their targets 18 . Schwann cells and neurons both independently as well as their interaction are affected in diabetes 19 . Although whether Schwannopathy or axonopathy develops first in diabetic neuropathy is still controversial, the injury of SCs is a critical factor in the pathogenesis and progression of diabetic neuropathy 19 , 20 , 21 . A series of events in diabetes results in morphological changes and diminished neurotrophic release that leads to impaired neuronal function through disruption in myelination and axonal conduction. In addition, the Schwann cell dysfunction impairs axonal regeneration 22 , 23 . However, approaches targeting SCs in treating diabetic neuropathy have not been investigated sufficiently. Thus, even though it is certain that impaired SCs are only part of the pathogenesis and development of diabetic neuropathy, preserving healthy Schwann cells is vitally important both in peripheral nerve biology and in the recovery of diabetic peripheral neuropathy 24 .

UNLEASHING BRAKES TO PERIPHERAL DIABETIC NEUROPATHY

The cell cycle consists of four phases including G1, S, G2, and M. During the G1 phase, the cells prepare all required conditions and monitor the cellular environment for DNA replication in the S phase. Proceeding to the S phase from the G1 phase is an irreversible process and is tightly controlled by various intrinsic molecules and signals ranging from growth factors to DNA damage and developmental cues 25 . The cell cycle is controlled by cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs) and CDK inhibitors (CKIs). In mammalian cells, CDK4/6 and CDK2 play a role in controlling G1. Both cyclins and CKIs are regulated at various levels such as mRNA stability, translation, and subcellular localization. However, transcriptional regulation and ubiquitin–proteasome are two main mechanisms. The former way can be carried out via the transcriptional regulation of cyclin proteins or CKIs and post‐transcriptional regulation of CDKs. There are two CKI families playing a pivotal role in prohibiting the cell cycle transition from the G1 to the S phase: the p21 family includes p21, p27, and p57; the INK4 family includes p15, p16, p18, and p19. Likewise, the ubiquitin–proteasome system can regulate both cyclin and CKIs to control the G1 cell cycle through mechanisms with the involvement of extracellular signals 25 .

Initiation of cell division is tightly controlled and is dependent on cell type, differentiation stage, cell context, and growth factor receptor (GFR) activation. Phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K), a group of downstream signaling proteins of GFR activation, plays a critical role in controlling early and late events in mammalian cell division 26 . In this fashion, PTEN is well noted as a molecule that acts to oppose PI3K activity. In particular, an elevated expression of PTEN in peripheral neurons of diabetic mice was found by us 2 and others 27 , 28 , 29 . We then showed that the introduction of PTEN inhibitor to the sciatic nerve transection–regeneration model applied to type‐2 diabetic mice promoted functional recovery after sciatic nerve regeneration in both diabetic and normal mice. Furthermore, the number of DRG sensory neurons in the group treated by the PTEN inhibitor was significantly higher than that in the vehicle‐treated groups, suggesting that PTEN inhibitor may promote the axonal transport in the axon regardless of diabetes 2 . In addition to PTEN, other tumor suppressors have been recognized as internal brakes to peripheral nerve regeneration and have been discussed extensively 3 , 30 . These molecules share common pathways of cell division and cell growth or nerve regeneration. Thus, future studies need to identify potential molecules that show a strong impact in peripheral nerve regeneration, then inhibition of these molecules will unleash ‘brakes’ to improving peripheral diabetic neuropathy.

SYNERGIC EFFECTS ON RESERVING FEATURES OF PERIPHERAL DIABETIC NEUROPATHY

Given that many complicated aspects affect nerve regeneration after peripheral injury, strategies approaching multiple targets need to be considered for effective treatments. Specifically, to achieve the best outcome in treating diabetic neuropathy, preventing nerve degeneration, and enhancing nerve regeneration should be focused on equally 11 . Therefore, targeting Schwann cells must also be considered appropriately in addition to promoting axonal regeneration. In this regard, neurotrophic factors, although having been used widely as an approach for dual targets including Schwann cells and neurons, will not be discussed in this review. Instead, approaches using more than one molecule that interact with each other and target both Schwann cells and neurons for synergic effects on an improvement of diabetic neuropathy are discussed.

Insulin has been well recognized as a metabolic hormone that plays an important role in controlling the blood glucose level. By binding to its receptors on skeletal muscle, adipose, and liver cells, insulin stimulates glucose transport into these cells via glucose transporters 31 . Glucose is the main energy source for neurons, however, unlike muscle or adipose cells, neurons take up glucose in an insulin‐independent way 32 . Interestingly, the expression of insulin receptors was found in neurons. Furthermore, some studies showed that SCs also express the insulin receptor, especially during the development of the myelin sheath 33 , suggesting that insulin crucially contributes to peripheral nerve support via SCs. Studies using a cell culture system showed that insulin supplementation to the media culture supports the proliferation of Schwann cells 34 , 35 . Unfortunately, after reaching the maximum proliferation at 1 mmol/L, the proliferation of Schwann cells was diminished, although it was still higher than that in the control group 34 . In addition, insulin significantly increased the expression of myelin protein zero (MPZ) and myelin basic protein (MBP) in Schwann cells 33 , 35 . As MPZ and MBP are major structural proteins playing a crucial role in the compaction and maintenance of peripheral myelin protein, the results suggest that insulin is involved in PNS regulation 33 . Interestingly, the effect of insulin on P0 expression was both time‐ and dose‐ dependent manner. In particular, ranging from 0.5 to 80 nM, insulin promoted P0 mRNA expression. However, at a concentration greater than 80 nM, insulin decreased the P0 mRNA expression of SCs 33 . In addition to hyperglycemia that is widely accepted as a main cause of diabetic neuropathy, disruption in the insulin signaling pathway is supposed to be another critical mediator contributing to the progression of the complication of the disorder 36 .

As insulin mediates its role via two main pathways including AKT/mTOR and ERK/MAPK pathways 37 , we hypothesized that insulin may interact with other molecules in these pathways and ultimately lead to the promotion of neurite extension and myelination. To address our questions, we first confirmed the expression of insulin receptor and PTEN in both neurons and Schwann cells. Then, in in vitro culture, insulin promoted neurite extension and the survival of both embryonic and adult rat neurons and supported Schwann cell proliferation 34 , 38 . Surprisingly, these effects of insulin were enhanced by PTEN inhibitor/knockdown. In particular, the combination of PTEN siRNA and insulin showed the strongest support on neurite extension and on the proliferation rate of Schwann cells compared with the groups treated by PTEN inhibitor or insulin alone. Furthermore, PTEN inhibitor enhanced the effects of insulin on sensory axon myelination. Moreover, combined local insulin and PTEN siRNA hind paw injections improved several features of chronic experimental diabetic peripheral neuropathy, including impaired sensation, motor conduction velocities slowing, myelin thinning, and epidermal denervation 34 (Figure 1).

IGF‐1, another member of the insulin‐like super family, has also been recognized itself as a promising therapeutic molecule for peripheral diabetic neuropathy. It showed various effects on many cell targets including neurons, myocytes, and Schwann cells in both in vitro and in vivo studies 39 . Previous studies showed that IGF‐1 stimulated protein synthesis and proliferation in oligodendrocytes via PI3K/mTOR/Akt and MEK/ERK pathways 40 , 41 . More recently, De Paula et al. showed that PTEN inhibitor enhances IGF‐1 effects on the proliferation and myelination of oligodendrocytes 42 . As insulin and IGF‐1 can act through each other's cognate receptors 43 , suggesting that similar effects in SCs as well as in neurons can be achieved. Further studies are needed to discover the synergic effects of PTEN inhibitors and IGF‐1 in diabetic neuropathy treatment. In addition, other growth factors such as NGF, FGF, and neuregulin play important roles in neuronal development, migration, and myelination via pathways with the downstream involvement of PI3K/Akt and MEK/ERK stimulation. Therefore, it suggests that the combinations of these growth factors and inhibition of PI3K/Akt components may also have synergic effects on improving hallmarks of peripheral diabetic neuropathy.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Peripheral diabetic neuropathy has a wide spectrum, affecting both Schwann cells and all types of neurons with many signaling pathways disrupted. Schwann cells and axons have a tight interaction for maintaining their vitality and cellular phenotype. When an injury occurs, their timely interactions is critical for guiding regenerating axons to their correct target and for minimizing nerve degeneration 15 . Therefore, approaches targeting both neurons and Schwann cells should be included in the treatment of diabetic neuropathy.

Whereas to internal “brakes”, neurotrophic factors are considered as extrinsic factors that can be used to support regeneration to impaired axons 3 . Schwann cells supply neurotrophic factors to axons to enhance axon growth, prevent neurons from apoptosis, and guide regenerating axons to their targets. It is noted that not only neurons, but Schwann cells are also seriously affected in diabetic neuropathy. As a result, many neurotrophic factors are altered or impaired during the progression of diabetic neuropathy. Several neurotrophic factors have been investigated for promoting nerve regeneration and improving diabetic neuropathy 44 , 45 . Insulin, in addition to being well recognized as a metabolic hormone that plays an important role in controlling the blood glucose level, has recently been noted as a neurotrophic factor 46 . We have recently shown that by targeting both neurons and Schwann cells, insulin performs dual effects on improving diabetic neuropathy: preventing nerve degeneration and enhancing nerve regeneration 34 , 38 . Importantly, at the investigated doses (very low), the introduction of insulin did not result in hypoglycemia 34 , 47 , 48 .

Strikingly, the combination of insulin and PTEN inhibitor shows a stronger improvement than the effects of each single molecule. It is noted that both PI3K/Akt/mTOR and MEK/ERK signaling pathways are involved in cell proliferation 41 . It is found that both neurons and Schwann cells express receptors for other growth factors 49 , 50 , 51 and components in the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway 52 , 53 , 54 . Therefore, it suggests that there is crosstalk between these molecules in their actions resulting in potentiated effects on improving diabetic neuropathy. Future studies may consider this approach for exploring more robust intervention to treat this complication effectively.

In summary, the aim of this review is to introduce an approach that is called combined intervention to support the concept that targeting Schwann cells and targeting neurons should be considered equally for an effective treatment of diabetic neuropathy. Insulin and PTEN inhibitor are two representative molecules in our approach showing a remarkable outcome due to: (1) in the single form, they have dual effects on both SCs and neurons, (2) in their combination, they interact with each other and show synergic effects. Furthermore, we draw attention to the local introduction of these molecules by strategies without viral carriers. However, further investigations on these combined interventions are clearly needed, especially in type 2 diabetes with obesity and insulin resistance (IR) being the most well documented. The signaling pathway of insulin is disrupted by insulin deficiency and hyperinsulinemia in type 1 and type 2 diabetes, respectively 55 . Recently, it was proposed that neurons develop resistance to insulin and finally result in impaired neuronal functions and the disease state. However, the contributors to the development of neuronal insulin resistance and the involvement of insulin resistance in the progression of diabetic neuropathy are still unclear. Pham et al. recently showed that after reaching a maximum promotion, neurite extension of both sensory and motor neurons was impaired in media culture supplemented with a high dose of insulin 38 , reinforcing the knowledge that insulin resistance in neurons can be induced by hyperinsulinemia. Insulin resistance results in an inappropriate response of the peripheral neurons to the growth factor signal, making them more vulnerable to diabetic insults such as hyperglycemia which subsequently leads to the development of diabetic neuropathy 56 , 57 . Interestingly, even though insulin plays its role via both AKT/mTOR and ERK/MAPK pathways 37 , hyperinsulinemia‐induced insulin resistance has no effect on the MAPK‐mediated signaling pathways 56 . In addition to neurons, insulin resistance is also noted in Schwann cells in Peripheral diabetic neuropathy. Insulin resistance disrupts lipid metabolism in Schwann cells via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway leading to many sensory impairments such as deficits in nerve conduction velocity, reduced sensory nerve action potential amplitude, and an impaired g‐ratio 58 . In addition, the metabolism and functions of adipose tissue such as enhanced release of free fatty acids, hormones, and induced macrophage accumulation, are changed in the obese state 59 . These accumulated macrophages in target tissues are the source of elevated circulating proinflammatory cytokines leading to insulin resistance 60 , 61 , 62 . Some papers showed that obesity induces insulin resistance in neurons in the brain 63 , 64 , 65 , however, little is known about insulin resistance induced by obesity in peripheral neurons. In addition, there is currently no idea about the impact of the combination of insulin (and other molecules in the MEK/ERK pathway) with inhibition of key molecules in the PI3K/Akt pathway on insulin resistance. Thus, it is worth to trying to explore further potential approaches targeting PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways in future studies on the management of diabetic neuropathy (Figure 2).
