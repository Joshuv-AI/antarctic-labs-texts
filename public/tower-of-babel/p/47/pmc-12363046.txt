Evaluating the operational and financial performance of Community-Based visiting nursing center for elderly with the balanced scorecard model
Authors: Ji Young Lim, Seong Kwang Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAging populations have increased demand for long-term care, raising concerns about quality, efficiency, and sustainability. While the Balanced Scorecard (BSC) is widely used for performance evaluation, it is underutilized in Community-Based Elderly Visiting Nursing Centers (CB-EVNC). This study applies the BSC model to evaluate performance across four perspectives—learning and growth, internal processes, customer, and financial—to identify key performance indicators (KPIs) and enhance operational efficiency.MethodsA secondary data analysis was conducted using simulation data from the 3 S Business Intelligence System (3 S-BIS) program, reflecting operational data from a home-based long-term care facility in Seoul (January–December 2021). Pearson correlation and path analysis were performed using IBM AMOS 25.0. Model fit was evaluated using chi-square tests, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).ResultsTotal pay correlated positively with visit frequency (r =.938, p <.001) and staff count (r =.931, p <.001), while visit frequency negatively correlated with beneficiary status (r =–.330, p <.001). Path analysis showed that visit frequency significantly increased with staff count (β = 0.917, p <.001), but total performance rate decreased with higher staff numbers (β = − 0.466, p <.001). Beneficiary status was positively influenced by visit frequency (β = 1.328, p <.001) and performance rate (β = 0.273, p <.001) but negatively affected by staff count (β = − 1.439, p <.001). Financial performance was primarily driven by visit frequency (β = 0.977, p <.001). The final path model demonstrated acceptable model fit, supporting the interconnected nature of operational efficiency and service quality.ConclusionsThe study confirms that staffing levels and process efficiency significantly influence financial sustainability and service quality in home-based long-term care facilities. While increased staffing improves service delivery, it may reduce efficiency, highlighting the need for strategic workforce management and continuous professional training. Future research should expand to multiple institutions and adopt longitudinal designs to assess long-term impacts. Standardized performance management systems using the BSC model could enhance service quality and support long-term care policy development.Keywords: Community health services, Aged, Balanced scorecard model, Financial performance

Introduction

Research necessity

Currently, home-based long-term care facilities in South Korea operate as semi-public medical and welfare institutions based on the Long-Term Care Insurance Act for the Elderly, receiving financial support from the National Health Insurance Corporation and the government [1]. This operational structure differentiates them from typical for-profit enterprises, forming a unique revenue model where maximum revenue is explicitly defined by long-term care benefit fees [1, 2]. In this context, the sustainable operation of home-based long-term care facilities necessitates efficient utilization of limited resources and systematic management, rather than focusing on revenue growth [3].

Home-based long-term care facilities provide integrated medical and welfare care services, requiring the collaboration of various professionals, such as nurses, nursing assistants, and care workers [2, 3]. These facilities must comply with statutory staffing standards and maintain professionalism through continuous education and training. Furthermore, they need to provide differentiated care based on the health and functional status of elderly residents while balancing the quality of medical services and cost-efficiency [1–3].

Globally, the growing elderly population and the increasing costs of managing end-of-life illnesses have become top priorities in national healthcare policies [4]. Particularly in South Korea, where healthcare expenditures for the elderly have significantly increased as a proportion of total healthcare costs following the transition to a super-aged society, the active utilization of community-based home care facilities has been promoted as part of efforts to establish cost-effective healthcare infrastructure [1–3]. Since the introduction of the Long-Term Care Insurance System for Elderly in 2008, various policies and studies have been implemented to revitalize home care nursing and home care service centers [5–7]. Additionally, with the enactment of the Act on Integrated Support for Community Care including Medical and Nursing Services in 2024, a shift from a traditional hospital-centered end-of-life care model to a community-centered end-of-life care model has been actively pursued [8].

Despite these advancements, many home-based long-term care facilities still lack systematic management tools to comprehensively evaluate their financial stability and operational efficiency. This limitation may result in inefficient resource allocation, ultimately degrading the quality of care services. Addressing complex operational challenges, such as individualized care planning and execution, requires the introduction of systematic and scientific performance management tools.

The Balanced Scorecard (BSC) model presents a viable framework for assessing and improving the performance of home-based long-term care facilities in a multidimensional manner [9–11]. The BSC model evaluates organizational performance across four perspectives: financial performance, customer satisfaction, internal process efficiency, and learning and growth [9]. Its effectiveness has been demonstrated in both non-profit organizations and public institutions, making it well-suited to the unique operational environment of home-based long-term care facilities, where optimal efficiency must be achieved with limited resources [10, 11]. Furthermore, with the rapid expansion of elderly care facilities driven by an aging population, societal expectations for higher service quality continue to increase [1–3]. Although the number of care facilities has grown significantly since the introduction of the Long-Term Care Insurance System in 2008 [1], disparities in service quality and operational inefficiencies remain pressing challenges.

In the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, interest in Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems and Business Intelligence (BI) programs has surged, highlighting the importance of real-time task processing and rapid decision-making in the healthcare and welfare sectors [12]. The 3 S (Smart, Simple & Speed) Business Intelligence System (3 S-BIS), an ERP simulation program designed specifically for non-management professionals like nurses, has gained attention as a tool for enhancing productivity and efficiency in nursing organizations [12]. Integrating management support solutions such as 3 S-BIS with the BSC model may enable home-based long-term care facilities to execute strategic operational decisions more effectively.

This study seeks to explore and analyze the application of the BSC model in enhancing the operational efficiency of home-based long-term care facilities. The research aims to identify practical strategies for simultaneously improving medical service quality and operational efficiency in a balanced manner. Additionally, it endeavors to establish a standardized performance management system tailored to these facilities, providing empirical evidence to support the advancement of the Long-Term Care Insurance System.

Research objectives

This study applies the BSC to develop a performancemanagement framework that simultaneously improves efficiency and service quality in homebased longtermcare facilities, using 3SBIS simulation data. Specifically, it (1) selects key performance indicators (KPIs) for the BSC’s four perspectives and examines their interrelationships, and (2) derives practical guidelines for field implementation to support sustainable facility management.

Methodology

Study design

This study employs a secondary data analysis approach using simulation data from the 3 S-BIS program to explore the operational efficiency and service quality of home-based long-term care facilities across the four perspectives of the BSC model: financial, customer, internal processes, and learning and growth. Given the limited prior research in this area, this study does not explicitly assume causal relationships but focuses on identifying potential pathways among variables.

Theoretical framework

The BSC was first articulated by Kaplan and Norton in their landmark 1992 Harvard Business Review article, “The Balanced Scorecard—Measures that Drive Performance” [13]. They later refined the concept in the 1996 monograph The Balanced Scorecard: Translating Strategy into Action [9]. The BSC is defined as a strategic performancemanagement system that translates an organization’s vision and strategy into a coherent set of indicators across four mutually reinforcing perspectives—learning & growth, internal processes, customer, and financial.

Because homebased longterm care facilities operate under capped reimbursements yet must demonstrate high service quality, a multidimensional tool is required to balance efficiency with nonfinancial outcomes. Prior systematic reviews confirm the BSC’s suitability for healthcare and nonprofit settings facing similar constraints [14, 15]. Accordingly, applying the BSC is both appropriate and necessary for performance management in semipublic Korean longterm care facilities.

A key characteristic of this model is the interconnected nature of these perspectives, where performance improvements in lower stages can cascade upward to higher stages [9]. For instance, enhanced employee education and knowledge sharing in the learning and growth perspective can lead to increased efficiency in internal processes, improved service quality, higher customer satisfaction, and ultimately, positive impacts on financial performance [9].

The BSC model has been effectively applied in healthcare organizations, as demonstrated by several prior studies [14–18]. These studies highlight the following key performance indicators (KPIs) commonly used for each perspective of the BSC model in healthcare settings:

Learning and Growth: Employee satisfaction [18–20], staff competency [20, 21], and education [20, 21].

Learning and Growth: Employee satisfaction [18–20], staff competency [20, 21], and education [20, 21].

Internal Processes: Operational efficiency [19, 20], length of stay [18, 20], bed occupancy rate [18, 20], and bed turnover rate [18, 20].

Internal Processes: Operational efficiency [19, 20], length of stay [18, 20], bed occupancy rate [18, 20], and bed turnover rate [18, 20].

Customer: Patient satisfaction [18–20], family satisfaction [20], and patient volume [18, 20].

Customer: Patient satisfaction [18–20], family satisfaction [20], and patient volume [18, 20].

Financial: Profitability [19, 20], cost reduction [19, 20], and cost efficiency [18, 20].

Financial: Profitability [19, 20], cost reduction [19, 20], and cost efficiency [18, 20].

It is important to note that these KPIs may vary depending on the specific characteristics and strategies of the organization. The BSC model emphasizes not only measuring financial performance but also achieving a balance between financial and non-financial indicators. Additionally, understanding the causal relationships among these indicators and making continuous improvement efforts are critical components of the model [9, 13–18].

Based on the BSC model, this study established an initial pathway. Specifically, it hypothesizes that learning and growth influence the efficiency of internal processes, internal processes affect customer satisfaction, and customer satisfaction ultimately leads to financial performance. Using this framework (Fig. 1), the study aims to explore the impact of the BSC model on performance improvement in home-based long-term care facilities.

Data collection

This study retrospectively analyzed management performance data integrated into the 3 S-BIS program, utilizing daily operational records from a community-based visiting nursing center for the elderly located in Seoul. Data collection covered the entire period from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2021 (N = 365 days). The facility adheres to standardized data management protocols under the Long-Term Care Insurance Act for the Elderly, ensuring data reliability, completeness, and consistency throughout the study period.

The original dataset comprised a wide range of operational variables, including financial transactions for nursing and home care services, workforce deployment details, service utilization records, and inventories of medical supplies and consumables. To align with the study objectives and the BSC framework, variables were selectively organized based on their relevance to performance monitoring. Variables irrelevant to the BSC framework, such as granular details of clinical supplies or administrative notes, were not individually analyzed but systematically consolidated into broader accounting categories, particularly within overall operational expenses, in accordance with the facility’s standard financial management practices.

Furthermore, as the facility manages integrated accounting for both home nursing and home care services, related variables—such as revenues, expenditures, beneficiary counts, staffing levels, and service records—were aggregated to reflect the center’s overall operational performance rather than disaggregated by specific service types. This approach aligns with the study’s focus on assessing the comprehensive operational efficiency of the facility, rather than conducting segmented analyses by occupation or service line.

Notably, there were no missing data points in the dataset, as the facility maintained continuous operations throughout the study period, including weekends and public holidays. Daily data entries were recorded systematically following institutional data governance protocols, ensuring the completeness and integrity of the data used in this analysis.

Study variables

Grounded in the conceptual framework of our study, which is informed by Kaplan and Norton [9, 18] and supported by multiple systematic reviews [18–21], we defined the following KPIs:

Learning and Growth: Total Staff Count, Employee Training Completion.

Learning and Growth: Total Staff Count, Employee Training Completion.

Internal Processes: Visit Frequency, Total Performance Rate (%).

Internal Processes: Visit Frequency, Total Performance Rate (%).

Customer: Beneficiary Status (Client Numbers).

Customer: Beneficiary Status (Client Numbers).

Financial: Total Pay, Total Operating Expenses.

Financial: Total Pay, Total Operating Expenses.

These variables were selected to reflect the legal staffing standards, restricted revenue structures, and service quality management requirements of home-based long-term care facilities. By integrating financial and non-financial indicators, this study aimed to explore pathways between operational efficiency and service quality.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Python and IBM AMOS 25.0 statistical software. Descriptive statistics (e.g., means, standard deviations) and normality tests (skewness, kurtosis) were performed for each variable. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine linear relationships between variables.

To normalize distributions and address outliers, log transformation was applied to Total Pay and Total Operating Expenses, while the remaining variables were standardized using Z-scores. Path analysis was performed using the maximum likelihood estimation method, and model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including Chi-square (χ²) test, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA).

Considering the complexity of the model and sample characteristics, potential over- or under-estimation of χ² and RMSEA values was acknowledged. Accordingly, these indices were reported as reference measures, while alternative fit indices such as GFI, TLI, and CFI were prioritized for model interpretation. This approach aligns with prior studies [22, 23], which highlight the sensitivity of the χ² test to large sample sizes and complex models (low degrees of freedom), as well as the potential distortion of RMSEA in models with low degrees of freedom or highly correlated variables. Lastly, indirect effects among variables were tested using the bootstrapping method.

Ethical considerations

This study involved secondary data analysis and was conducted with prior approval for data usage. The study received an exemption from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB). All personal information was anonymized, and data use was strictly limited to research purposes.

Path analysis

The initial model showed inadequate fit (χ² = 177.348, p <.001, CFI = 0.91, GFI = 0.907, AGFI = 0.732, NFI = 0.90, TLI = 0.74, RMSEA = 0.297, AIC = 38.52, and BIC = 108.13). After trimming nonsignificant paths, fit improved markedly (χ² = 26.624, p <.001, CFI = 0.992, GFI = 0.991, AGFI = 0.956, NFI = 0.991, TLI = 0.964, RMSEA = 0.109, AIC = 45.85, and BIC = 135.550. Despite the chi-square test’s significance and the RMSEA value being slightly elevated at 0.109, these indices are sensitive to model complexity. The overall model fit was deemed acceptable based on superior indices such as CFI, GFI, and TLI [22, 23].

The revised path analysis revealed the following key findings:

Visit Frequency rose with Total Staff Count (β = 0.917, p <.001), whereas Total Performance Rate fell as staffing increased (β = − 0.466, p <.001). Beneficiary Status grew with Visit Frequency (β = 1.328, p <.001) and Performance Rate (β = 0.273, p <.001) but declined with higher staffing (β = − 1.439, p <.001) and greater Employee Training (β = − 0.466, p <.001). Total Pay was most strongly predicted by Visit Frequency (β = 0.977, p <.001), with Total Staff Count also having a significant indirect effect via Visit Frequency (β = 0.926, p <.001). Finally, Total Operating Expenses decreased as Beneficiary Status expanded (β = − 0.382, p <.001) and Performance Rate improved (β = − 0.287, p <.001), reflecting economies of scale and process efficiency.

The squared multiple correlations (SMC) showed the highest explanatory power for Visit Frequency (88.8%) and Total Pay (87.2%) (Fig. 2; Table 3).

Discussion

This study applied the BSC model to explore ways to enhance the operational efficiency of home-based long-term care facilities in South Korea, addressing the inherent trade-offs between human and financial resources and service quality. Home-based long-term care facilities often face challenges in balancing the provision of high-quality nursing services with achieving financial sustainability. By empirically validating the effectiveness of managing the four perspectives of the BSC model—learning and growth, internal processes, customer, and financial—this study demonstrated the potential of the BSC framework as a strategic tool to resolve these challenges and improve institutional performance [9, 13, 14, 18].

Aligned with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) “Aging in Place” policy framework [24] and through comparative analysis with long-term care service models in leading countries like Japan and Germany, this study also provided insights into the development of sustainable operational strategies tailored to South Korea’s specific needs. Within the context of the Long-Term Care Insurance Act for the Elderly, South Korea’s home-based long-term care facilities operate as semi-public organizations. These facilities are tasked with delivering integrated medical and welfare services while navigating constraints such as capped service fees, statutory staffing requirements, and the demand for public accountability [1–3]. This dual challenge underscores the necessity of balancing financial constraints with non-financial outcomes to ensure the quality of care and operational sustainability.

In this study, the selected KPIs for the BSC model included total staff count and employee training for the learning and growth perspective, visit frequency and performance rate against work plans for the internal processes perspective, beneficiary status (client numbers) for the customer perspective, and total pay and operating expenses for the financial perspective. These indicators reflect the multi-dimensional nature of home-based long-term care facilities, where financial and non-financial factors must be integrated for comprehensive performance evaluation. The BSC framework proved particularly effective for these institutions, allowing for balanced monitoring and management of both financial indicators (e.g., total pay, operating expenses) and non-financial indicators (e.g., staff capabilities, visit frequency, and client base). This balance enables facilities to address their operational challenges while meeting the increasing societal demand for high-quality elderly care services [9, 16–18].

By applying the BSC model, the study demonstrated that it is possible to develop strategic approaches that enhance the sustainability of operations and improve service quality simultaneously. The findings highlight the potential of the BSC model as a practical tool to help home-based long-term care facilities navigate their complex operational environments, delivering both effective resource utilization and superior care outcomes for the elderly population. This research contributes to advancing the understanding of performance management in semi-public healthcare organizations and underscores the importance of integrating financial and non-financial metrics for achieving long-term operational success [9, 13–18].

This study applied the BSC model to analyze the interplay between human and financial resources and service quality in home-based long-term care facilities in South Korea. The results revealed a positive correlation between staff size and visit frequency, showing that increased staffing directly contributed to the higher frequency of service delivery, which in turn enhanced financial outcomes such as total pay. However, an increase in staff size was also linked to a decrease in the Total Performance Rate, indicating that a larger workforce might lead to reduced average efficiency due to factors such as lower proficiency among new employees or higher costs associated with task coordination [25]. Furthermore, while education, as a component of the learning and growth perspective, did not show statistically significant direct effects on short-term indicators like visit frequency or performance rate, this may suggest that the benefits of education take time to materialize or that the training programs lacked effectiveness [26].

Path analysis highlighted that staffing levels increased visit frequency, which subsequently boosted financial outcomes like total pay. At the same time, increased staffing was associated with a decline in performance rates, underscoring the need for strategies to balance service volume with efficiency. Additionally, the finding that an increase in beneficiary (client) numbers reduced operating expenses suggests that achieving economies of scale can enhance cost efficiency once a certain scale of operations is reached [27]. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in environments where small-scale facilities are prevalent, offering practical and policy-level implications for achieving economies of scale in long-term care services.

The study also reaffirmed that, while the BSC model was originally designed for corporate performance management, its application to non-profit and semi-public organizations like home-based long-term care facilities reveals unique challenges. Given the capped fee structure under the Long-Term Care Insurance System, the key task for such facilities is to enhance cost efficiency rather than unlimited revenue growth. In this context, non-financial indicators such as service quality, client satisfaction, and staff expertise become far more critical [28, 29]. The finding that increasing staff size does not necessarily improve service quality highlights the importance of considering workforce management and process innovation strategies. Therefore, adopting the BSC model in home-based long-term care facilities necessitates balancing staffing levels with operational process efficiency, staff training, and client satisfaction.

Comparisons with Japan and Germany offer valuable insights. In Japan, which has already entered a super-aged society, community-based care systems actively promote home nursing stations, emphasizing team approaches among healthcare professionals and leveraging ICT to enhance efficiency [30–34]. Japan’s strategies suggest that simply increasing staff numbers is less effective than optimizing team coordination, retaining skilled professionals, and utilizing digital technologies to reduce redundant tasks and administrative burdens. These approaches align with the study’s findings, indicating that education programs should be closely integrated with practical tasks and supplemented with simulation-based training.

Our path analysis revealed complex dynamics between staffing levels, service frequency, and performance outcomes. Specifically, while increased staffing significantly enhanced visit frequency (β = 0.917, p <.001), suggesting greater service volume, it simultaneously decreased the total performance rate (β=–0.466, p <.001). This finding indicates a potential trade-off where quantitative service expansion may occur at the expense of average service quality or efficiency. This tension resonates with quality management approaches in established systems like Germany’s long-term care sector. There, institutional guarantees for home care services are complemented by robust quality assurance mechanisms, including strict certification and evaluation [35]. German providers often focus on qualitative indicators, such as process compliance and client satisfaction, alongside trust-building, to maintain standards and foster competition [36]. Such qualitative monitoring offers a strategy to potentially mitigate the performance declines observed in our study when service frequency increases due solely to staffing expansion. Therefore, achieving operational efficiency that genuinely reflects high-quality care in home-based long-term care settings necessitates complementing quantitative measures (e.g., visit frequency) with detailed monitoring and transparent reporting of qualitative service dimensions (e.g., performance rates and adherence to care protocols). Adopting this balanced approach is particularly critical for Korean long-term care facilities, aligning closely with the empirical findings from our research.

These findings align with the WHO’s “Aging in Place” policy direction [24], as well as the effective systems established in Japan [30–32] and Germany [33, 34]. Both countries demonstrate the importance of providing continuous medical and social support to the elderly within community-based frameworks. Such integrated approaches not only enhance the quality of life for elderly individuals but also strengthen the overall healthcare and welfare system of a nation. As South Korea prepares for a super-aged society, establishing a system where medical and care services are seamlessly provided within the community, centered around home-based long-term care facilities, becomes essential.

The study’s findings emphasize the potential for quantitative expansion of community-based care through increased staffing, as higher visit frequencies contributed to financial performance. However, the paradox of reduced performance rates associated with staff increases highlights the challenges of workforce efficiency, team-based approaches, and the integration of ICT solutions [37, 38]. Addressing these challenges requires a focus not merely on increasing staff numbers but also on fostering highly skilled professionals, implementing systematic training programs, and innovating internal processes. Only by pursuing these qualitative improvements can home-based long-term care facilities achieve the dual objectives of “high-quality nursing services” and “cost efficiency.”

The study’s limitations include the use of retrospective secondary data from a single home-based long-term care facility, necessitating caution in generalizing the results. The cross-sectional design also limited the ability to capture the long-term effects of staff training or expertise development. Additionally, the BSC indicators used in this study did not fully reflect clinical service quality, such as client functional status, readmission rates, or satisfaction levels. Although the data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, the facility continued its operation without interruption as an essential service provider. Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that pandemic conditions may have influenced service delivery patterns to some extent. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate post-pandemic data to validate and generalize the findings under normalized operational conditions. Future longitudinal studies incorporating more refined quality assessment indicators will be crucial.

Despite these limitations, this study holds significant value in its application of the BSC model to analyze the causal pathways among multidimensional factors, such as staffing, visit frequency, and financial efficiency, in home-based long-term care facilities. While the model fit indices, such as χ² and RMSEA, showed some shortcomings, these are likely attributable to their sensitivity to sample size and model complexity [19, 20]. Considering that χ² tends to yield significant results with sample sizes over 200 and RMSEA can be overestimated in models with low degrees of freedom, alternative indices like CFI, GFI, and TLI, which all exceeded 0.90, were prioritized for interpretation, confirming the model’s adequacy in explaining the data.

In conclusion, this study provides empirical evidence for the efficient operation of community-based care systems in aging societies by integrating human resource, financial, and service quality indicators using the BSC model. While the importance of increasing staffing to enhance visit frequency and financial outcomes is emphasized, the observed paradox of reduced performance rates due to staff increases highlights the need for efficient workforce management and internal process innovation. These findings align with the strategies of Japan and Germany and the WHO’s recommendations, underscoring the importance of quality management for staff, the adoption of ICT solutions, and investments in systematic training. Future research that tracks the long-term interaction between operational performance and service quality will contribute further to the effective development of the Long-Term Care Insurance System and the realization of the “Aging in Place” philosophy.

Conclusion

This study explored the operational efficiency of home-based long-term care facilities by applying the BSC model, aiming to identify effective strategies for utilizing human and financial resources. The findings confirmed that the four perspectives of the BSC model—learning and growth, internal processes, customer, and financial—are organically interconnected, with staffing levels and visit frequency closely linked to financial outcomes. While increased staffing positively influenced visit frequency, it was also associated with a decline in performance rates, highlighting the need for efficient internal process management and professional development rather than merely expanding the workforce.

A limitation of this study lies in its reliance on data from a single facility, requiring caution when generalizing the results. Additionally, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to assess long-term impacts. Future research should incorporate data from multiple institutions and adopt longitudinal designs to further investigate the effects of training and process improvements on operational performance.

In conclusion, this study empirically validated the applicability of the BSC model in enhancing the operational efficiency of home-based long-term care facilities, providing strategic insights for achieving both sustainability and improved service quality. Based on these findings, future studies should focus on developing standardized performance management systems and analyzing the interactions between long-term operational outcomes and service quality. Such efforts will contribute to improving the health and quality of life for the elderly while fostering the advancement of the long-term care insurance system.
