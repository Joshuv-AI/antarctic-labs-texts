Classification of neurodevelopmental disorders and typical development using deep learning and a portable patch-type electroencephalography device
Authors: Byambadorj Nyamradnaa, Maya Izumoto, Yoshiko Iwatani, Shusuke Yoshimoto, Kotaro Ezure, Azusa Ono, Yoko Kato, Kyoko Makita, Ikuko Hirata, Masaya Tachibana, Ikuko Mohri, Shuichi Hiroyama, Sotaro Momosaki, Takufumi Yanagisawa, Kuriko Kagitani-Shimono, Masako Taniike
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are common neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) in children and often co-occur (ASD + ADHD), complicating the diagnosis. The diagnostic process is lengthy and subjective, relying heavily on expert knowledge, which limits accessibility. Electroencephalography (EEG) offers potential as a biomarker but requires skilled technicians for measurements and can be stressful for children with NDDs. This study aimed to provide a more accessible diagnostic support tool. We used a portable EEG device with a low participant burden and a deep learning model to distinguish between the typical development group (TD) and the NDD group, comprising children with ASD, ADHD, and ASD + ADHD. Resting-state EEG data were recorded for 5 min using the portable HARU-2 device, which features three channels placed on the forehead of 163 participants (87 TD, 76 NDD). A deep learning model combining a one-dimensional convolutional neural network and a transformer encoder was developed to analyze the EEG data. In 5-fold cross-validation, the model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.713 and a balanced accuracy (bACC) of 67.5% for classifying NDD and TD. Exploratory evaluation of out-of-fold predictions stratified by clinical phenotype showed an AUC of 0.793 and bACC of 75.0% for ASD vs. TD, 0.817 and 79.5% for ADHD vs. TD, and 0.667 and 62.7% for ASD + ADHD vs. TD. These findings suggest that portable EEG devices combined with deep learning models may serve as accessible adjunctive tools for NDD screening in children.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) are among the most common neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) characterized by deficits in personal, social, academic, and occupational functioning (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). With an increasing global trend, the prevalence of ASD and ADHD in children is reported to reach approximately 2% and 10%, respectively (Bougeard et al., 2024; Danielson et al., 2024). Additionally, ASD and ADHD are commonly comorbid: 30–75% of children with ASD exhibit ADHD symptoms, and 20–60% of those with ADHD display ASD-like social difficulties (Grzadzinski et al., 2016). Early diagnosis, environmental modification, therapeutic interventions, and sometimes medical treatment are essential to provide adequate support for NDDs.

NDD diagnosis primarily relies on behavioral observations by experienced doctors and psychologists and detailed interviews based on symptom criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Symptom assessment often utilizes parent- or caregiver-reported screening questionnaires such as the Social Responsiveness Scale, Second Edition (SRS-2) (Constantino and Gruber, 2012), ADHD Rating Scale (ADHD-RS) (DuPaul et al., 1998), and Conners-3 (Conners et al., 2008). The Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2), is a semi-structured, standardized observational ASD assessment tool. ADOS-2 can be administered and interpreted by appropriately credentialed professionals in 40–60 min, with approximately 1 h for scoring (Lord et al., 2012). However, the limited number of facilities that can administer this assessment leads to extended waiting times for consultations and delayed diagnoses. These critical issues in today's healthcare system underscore a strong need for objective and quantifiable markers for early screening.

Extensive research has investigated molecular and non-molecular biomarkers, utilizing modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography (EEG), event-related potential, and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (Parellada et al., 2023; Cortese et al., 2023). Among these, EEG is a non-invasive technique widely used in numerous studies.

EEG in children with ASD has consistently shown a U-shaped abnormal pattern characterized by increased power in low-frequency (delta and theta) and high-frequency bands (gamma), accompanied by reduced power in the mid-frequency alpha band (Wang et al., 2013). In children with ADHD, absolute theta and delta power, including in the frontal regions, is increased compared with that in children with typical development (TD) (Markovska-Simoska and Pop-Jordanova, 2017).

These findings highlight EEG as a potential supportive tool for ASD and ADHD diagnosis. However, as children with NDDs dislike novel environments, measuring EEG in specialized hospital examination rooms is sometimes stressful and difficult. Moreover, traditional multichannel clinical EEG systems are expensive and bulky and require operation by skilled medical personnel. The process of electrode placement using a conductive paste can impose a significant physical burden on children with NDD exhibiting sensory abnormalities. These factors represent substantial barriers to large-scale clinical screening, limiting the widespread use of EEG in ASD and ADHD diagnosis.

The advancement and adoption of portable EEG devices may help address these challenges. Here, we used a portable EEG device with only three channels placed on the forehead. The device is easy to apply and does not require conductive paste. Additionally, measurements can be performed in clinics without technicians or at home. We also developed a deep learning model to classify NDD and TD using resting-state EEG data. We hypothesized that a convolutional neural network (CNN) can discriminate between NDD and TD with higher accuracy than traditional approaches using discriminative frequency power features. We primarily aimed to evaluate whether the classification accuracy of the model exceeded the chance level. Additionally, we directly compared our model with an approach that uses frequency power features in terms of performance. We aimed to facilitate accessible and efficient diagnostic support for NDD in a broader healthcare setting. Finally, we assessed the model's performance in distinguishing ASD, ADHD, and ASD + ADHD from TD.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and local statutory requirements. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Osaka University Hospital (Assurance No. K22213). After a complete explanation of the study, written informed consent was obtained from all the participants and their parents.

Study population

We recruited children aged 6–10 years diagnosed with ASD, ADHD, or both by pediatric neurologists based on the DSM-5 criteria at Osaka University Hospital. ASD diagnosis was confirmed using the ADOS-2 administered by expert psychologists, and participants were classified as having autism or ASD based on the assessment. ADHD was identified through interviews conducted by a clinical psychologist or pediatric neurologist using the Conners-3. Participants scoring above the cutoff were regarded as having ADHD. Exclusion criteria included a Full-Scale IQ (FSIQ) < 70 on the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition (WISC-IV); daily use of sleep aids, anti-ADHD drugs, antipsychotics, or antiepileptic drugs within 3 months; and failure to meet the diagnostic criteria for either ASD or ADHD, as assessed by the ADOS-2 and Conners-3, respectively. Children with ASD, ADHD, or both were classified into the NDD group.

Age-matched participants with TD were recruited as controls from the local community through advertisements. They had no history of neurodevelopmental or psychiatric disorders and had not received special support education. Girls who had reached menarche, children with an FSIQ score ≤85, and children scoring above the cutoff on the SRS-2 or ADHD-RS were excluded.

Two pediatric neurologists performed the EEG measurements using a standardized protocol. All EEG recordings were conducted in a quiet room, with each participant seated in the same chair for 5 min. EEG was recorded under eyes-open, resting-state conditions. For those who had difficulty remaining still, verbal reminders were provided to encourage them to stay calm, and a simple sand timer was displayed.

We recruited 91 and 100 children with NDD and TD, respectively. Of these, 12 participants with TD and one with NDD were excluded based on the exclusion criteria. Additionally, resting-state EEG data could not be obtained for three participants with NDD owing to scheduling conflicts, refusal to wear the EEG device, and improper device application. Statistical analyses of demographic data

To compare age, FSIQ, SRS-2 scores, and ADHD-RS scores between the NDD and TD groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. Sex distribution was assessed using the chi-squared test for independence. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using chi-squared tests for each pair of groups, with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons, using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Based on a two-tailed test with an assumed effect size of 0.5, the power for the sample sizes of the NDD and TD groups was calculated to be 88.6%. Statistical significance was defined as a corrected p-value <0.05. All analyses were performed using Python, specifically the scipy.stats module (version 1.14.1) for statistical computations (Virtanen et al., 2020). Calculations were conducted using appropriate methods and libraries to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

A patch-type portable EEG sensor

EEG measurements were conducted using a portable patch-type EEG device, HARU-2 (PGV Inc., Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 2), and its dedicated electrode sheet, HA-M2. HARU-2 has received medical approval from Japan's Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) and meets the same evaluation standards as traditional clinical EEGs (Certification Number: 304 AFBZX00012000, Class II). Similarly, the disposable HA-M2 electrode sheets have received medical approval from the PMDA (Certification Number: 13B2X10421000002, Class I). The device is lightweight (42 g) and features a curved design that ensures a comfortable fit on the user's forehead. With compact dimensions of W67 × L42 × H22 mm, it is designed for portability and ease of use.

HA-M2 sheets have a thickness of <50 μm, stretchability of up to 200%, and a moisture permeability of 2700 g/m2/day. These sheets were manufactured using a screen-printing process with a biocompatible gel on an elastic base and silver-based material. The biocompatibility of the conductive and nonconductive gels used in HA-M2 sheets was assessed in accordance with ISO 10993 standards for skin sensitization, irritation, and in vitro cytotoxicity.

EEG preprocessing

When analyzing resting-state EEG data, a bandpass filter (0.5–90 Hz) was applied to remove low-frequency drifts and high-frequency noise, and notch filters at 60 Hz were utilized to remove power-line interference.

To address artifacts, an amplitude threshold of ±200 μV was adopted. If the amplitude in any one of the three channels exceeded this threshold, the corresponding sample, along with 0.5 s of data before and after the event, was excluded across all three channels. This threshold was determined based on clinical evaluations by EEG specialists who visually identified artifacts such as muscle movements and eye blinks. After artifact removal, the total length of artifact-free EEG data varied across participants. To standardize input dimensions and avoid biases that could arise from variable-length data, we selected a fixed 1-min segment for each participant for further analysis. The 1-min duration was chosen because it represented the longest interval that could be consistently obtained across all participants after applying the artifact-rejection criteria. For participants whose artifact-free data exceeded 1 min, the last 1-min segment was used, because the later portion of the 5-min recording was expected to better reflect a stable resting state after participant acclimation to the recording environment. The use of short-duration resting-state EEG segments is consistent with prior deep-learning studies in pediatric EEG classification (Chen et al., 2019). Participants whose artifact-free data did not reach 1 min in total were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Deep learning model

To perform the classification task, we developed a deep learning model specifically designed to extract features from the EEG data (Fig. 3). This model combines seven parallel deep one-dimensional (1D) CNNs (Gehring et al., 2017) with a transformer block (Vaswani et al., 2017), leveraging the strengths of both convolutional and self-attention mechanisms to effectively capture temporal and spatial dependencies. The model was implemented using TensorFlow 2.13, which is a reliable and widely adopted version that ensures compatibility with state-of-the-art tools and libraries for efficient training and evaluation (Abadi et al., 2016). The deep 1D CNN component consisted of seven parallel convolutional branches, each designed to capture features over varying temporal resolutions. These branches use strides of 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128, enabling the model to process time-series data across multiple temporal scales. Each branch consists of a convolution, followed by pooling, batch normalization, and dropout.

The concatenated feature tensor is then passed through a transformer block that applies self-attention mechanisms to model the complex dependencies between the extracted features. The output from the transformer block retains the temporal structure while enhancing the representation using global contextual information.

After processing through the transformer block, the temporal dimension is reduced using global average pooling. The pooled features are passed through a dense layer with 512 units, followed by a final classification layer with two output units for binary classification.

This architecture was optimized for EEG data by combining local feature extraction via CNN branches and global feature refinement through a transformer, ensuring robust performance on the classification task.

Model validation

We employed a 5-fold cross-validation approach to evaluate model performance (Kohavi, 1995). This method involves partitioning a dataset into five equal subsets, sequentially using one as the validation set and the remaining four as the training set, ensuring that each subset is utilized once for validation. After cross-validation, we adopted an out-of-fold (OOF) evaluation strategy, where predictions are aggregated from the validation folds to form a comprehensive OOF prediction set. This enables performance assessment across the entire dataset without bias from any single fold.

NDD vs. TD was designated as the primary classification task. ASD vs. TD, ADHD vs. TD, and ASD + ADHD vs. TD comparisons were conducted as exploratory, hypothesis-generating analyses by subsetting the fixed OOF predictions from fold-specific models trained exclusively for the primary NDD vs. TD cross-validation. No subgroup-specific classifiers were trained, and no subgroup-specific threshold re-optimization was performed. These analyses were intended to characterize the behavior of the primary NDD classifier across clinically diagnosed phenotypes, rather than establish disorder-specific diagnostic classifiers.

We computed the area under the curve (AUC) (Powers, 2020; Fawcett, 2006) to further evaluate model performance. For threshold-dependent metrics, we employed the Youden Index (Youden, 1950) to identify the point that maximizes the balanced accuracy (bACC). This index is defined as J = Sensitivity + Specificity – 1. bACC was calculated as the average of sensitivity and specificity, and was particularly effective for addressing imbalanced datasets (Brodersen et al., 2010).BalancedAccuracy=Sensitivity+Specificity2

For threshold-dependent metrics (bACC, sensitivity, and specificity), we used a single shared operating point derived from the Youden Index of the primary NDD vs. TD comparison rather than re-deriving an optimal threshold for each subgroup.

To assess statistical significance, we performed subject-level label-permutation tests for AUC and bACC (Good, 2005). For each comparison, class labels were randomly shuffled 1000 times, and the empirical null distributions of AUC and bACC were constructed. The one-sided empirical p-value was estimated as the proportion of permutations yielding a metric greater than or equal to the observed value, with a standard small-sample correction applied. This non-parametric approach is particularly appropriate for the exploratory subgroup analyses, where small sample sizes may limit the reliability of parametric assumptions. As a complementary assessment of overall classification accuracy, a binomial test (Zar, 2010) was performed by assuming a baseline success probability of 0.5, with the significance threshold set at 0.05. For subgroup comparisons (ASD vs. TD, ADHD vs. TD, and ASD + ADHD vs. TD), binomial p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction (Weisstein, n.d.).

To quantify uncertainty in performance metrics, we computed 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for AUC, bACC, sensitivity, and specificity using subject-level bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations (Efron and Tibshirani, 1993). For each iteration, subjects were resampled with replacement, the metrics were recomputed, and the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of the bootstrap distribution were reported as 95% CIs.

To examine whether classification performance was confounded by sex imbalance between groups, sex-stratified metrics were computed separately for male and female participants using the OOF predictions for each comparison.

Classification using frequency power

In an additional experiment, we extracted the mean relative power spectral density (PSD) for each 2-s epoch from 1-min EEG data. The PSD was calculated for six frequency bands (delta: 0.5–4 Hz, theta: 4–8 Hz, alpha: 8–13 Hz, beta: 13–26 Hz, low gamma: 26–45 Hz, high gamma: 65–90 Hz) and across three channels, resulting in 18 features. These features were used as inputs for a light-gradient boosting machine (LGBM) (Ke et al., 2017) model, which was trained and validated using the same 5-fold cross-validation method as in the primary experiment.

Age did not differ significantly between the NDD and TD groups (p = 0.863), but a significant difference was observed in sex distribution (male-to-female ratio; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis of age revealed significant differences between the TD and ASD + ADHD groups (p < 0.001), while no significant differences were observed among the other groups. In the primary analysis, the deep learning model achieved an AUC of 0.713 (95% CI: 0.635–0.790) for the classification of NDD vs. TD, with a bACC of 67.5% (95% CI: 60.5–74.4%), sensitivity of 68.4% (95% CI: 58.5–78.6%), and specificity of 66.7% (95% CI: 56.7–76.1%). The classification performance significantly exceeded chance level under the label-permutation test (p = 0.001 for both AUC and bACC), with consistent results from the binomial test on overall classification accuracy (p = 4.73 × 10−6).

The conventional PSD-LightGBM approach achieved an AUC of 0.51 (95% CI: 0.421–0.613) and a bACC of 55.3% (95% CI: 48.2–63.7%) for the same NDD vs. TD classification. This did not reach statistical significance under the label-permutation test (permutation p ≥ 0.134 for AUC) or the binomial test (p = 0.136). Notably, the 95% CIs for AUC of the PSD-LightGBM approach and the deep learning model did not overlap for the primary NDD vs. TD comparison, supporting improved classification performance of the deep learning approach over the conventional spectral-feature-based approach beyond what would be expected from statistical uncertainty alone.

Given the significant difference in sex distribution between groups (mainly driven by the ASD + ADHD subgroup; Table 1), we further evaluated whether classification performance was confounded by sex. The primary NDD vs. TD classification remained above chance in both sexes (males: AUC = 0.702, bACC = 64.9%; females: AUC = 0.737, bACC = 73.8%), suggesting that the overall model performance was unlikely to be explained solely by sex distribution.

For exploratory evaluation of OOF predictions stratified by clinical phenotype, the deep learning model yielded AUCs of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.635–0.920) for ASD vs. TD, 0.817 (95% CI: 0.718–0.899) for ADHD vs. TD, and 0.667 (95% CI: 0.573–0.761) for ASD + ADHD vs. TD. Sensitivity was higher in the ASD and ADHD subgroups (83.3% and 92.3%, respectively) than in the ASD + ADHD subgroup (58.8%), whereas specificity was 66.7% across all subgroup analyses because the operating point was shared with the primary NDD vs. TD analysis.

As exploratory subgroup analyses are appropriately evaluated using non-parametric methods, all comparisons remained significantly above chance level under the label-permutation test (all permutation p-values ≤0.002 for AUC and ≤0.004 for bACC), with consistent results from the Bonferroni-adjusted binomial test (p = 3.83 × 10−4, 1.18 × 10−4, and 2.31 × 10−3 for ASD, ADHD, and ASD + ADHD vs. TD, respectively). The agreement between parametric and non-parametric significance tests was particularly relevant for the ASD and ADHD subgroups, where small positive-class sample sizes (n = 12 and n = 13) rendered parametric assumptions less reliable. However, the 95% CIs for AUC in the ASD and ADHD subgroups (widths of 0.285 and 0.181, respectively) were substantially wider than that in the primary NDD vs. TD analysis (width of 0.155), reflecting the small positive-class sample sizes. These exploratory point estimates should therefore be interpreted as characterizing how the single NDD classifier behaves across phenotypes rather than estimates of disorder-specific diagnostic performance.

The PSD-LightGBM approach yielded the following exploratory subgroup results: ASD vs. TD (AUC = 0.298, 95% CI: 0.154–0.444; bACC = 31.3%, 95% CI: 27.6–47.1%), ADHD vs. TD (AUC = 0.553, 95% CI: 0.370–0.715; bACC = 57.6%, 95% CI: 47.4–76.3%), and ASD + ADHD vs. TD (AUC = 0.555, 95% CI: 0.449–0.661; bACC = 60.2%, 95% CI: 50.3–67.9%). None of these comparisons reached statistical significance under the label-permutation test (all permutation p-values ≥0.134 for AUC) or the binomial test (ASD vs. TD: p = 1.00; ADHD vs. TD: p = 1.00; ASD + ADHD vs. TD: p = 0.221).

Sex-stratified analyses were also conducted for each exploratory subgroup comparison. Above-chance performance was preserved in both sexes across all comparisons. In the ASD vs. TD comparison, point estimates were higher in males (AUC = 0.800, bACC = 76.0%) than in females (AUC = 0.748, bACC = 68.7%). Similarly, in the ADHD vs. TD comparison, point estimates were higher in males (AUC = 0.875, bACC = 81.5%) than in females (AUC = 0.732, bACC = 75.4%), whereas the opposite pattern was observed in the ASD + ADHD vs. TD comparison (males: AUC = 0.652, bACC = 59.8%; females: AUC = 0.737, bACC = 75.4%). However, female subgroup sizes were very small in the ASD (n = 3), ADHD (n = 5), and ASD + ADHD (n = 5) comparisons; therefore, these stratified estimates should be interpreted as descriptive only.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated whether EEG data recorded using a portable patch-type device applied to the forehead could be used to classify children with NDD and TD. The device was easy and quick to apply, and only a few participants complained of discomfort. Although the characteristics of ASD (e.g., social impairment and sensory abnormality) and ADHD (e.g., attention disorder and impulsiveness) differ, difficulties in daily life are often related to executive dysfunction, which is a frontal lobe function (Townes et al., 2023). Therefore, we employed a portable EEG device specifically designed to measure frontal lobe activity.

In the primary analysis, our model achieved an AUC of 0.713 and a bACC of 67.5% in distinguishing NDD from TD, with the label-permutation test confirming above-chance performance for both AUC and bACC, with consistent results from the binomial test on overall classification accuracy. Exploratory analyses on clinically diagnosed subgroups, in which the fixed OOF predictions from the primary NDD vs. TD cross-validation procedure were subset according to phenotype without subgroup-specific retraining, yielded AUCs of 0.667–0.817 and bACCs of 62.7–79.5%. While all comparisons exceeded chance level, the relatively wide CIs in the ASD and ADHD subgroups, reflecting their small positive-class sample sizes, indicate that these subgroup estimates should be regarded as exploratory characterizations of how the NDD classifier behaves across clinically diagnosed phenotypes, rather than as estimates of disorder-specific diagnostic performance.

A significant sex imbalance was observed between the NDD and TD groups, primarily driven by the higher male-to-female ratio in the ASD + ADHD subgroup (Table 1). In sex-stratified analyses to examine whether this imbalance confounded classification performance, above-chance discrimination was preserved in both sexes for the primary NDD vs. TD task: AUCs were 0.702 and 0.737 in males and females, respectively, suggesting that overall model performance was not driven by sex distribution alone. However, divergent patterns were observed in some exploratory subgroup analyses: performance was higher in males than females for the ADHD vs. TD comparison, whereas the opposite was observed for the ASD + ADHD vs. TD comparison. These sex-stratified estimates should be interpreted with caution, as the female subgroup sizes were very small (n = 3–5) in the exploratory comparisons, limiting statistical reliability. Recruitment of larger, sex-balanced cohorts will be needed to clarify potential sex-specific neurophysiological patterns.

In contrast, the PSD-LightGBM model demonstrated substantially lower performance, with an AUC of 0.51 and a bACC of 55.3% for NDD vs. TD. When applied to clinically diagnosed subgroups, the model yielded AUCs ranging from 0.298 to 0.555 and bACCs from 31.3% to 60.2%. These values are below chance level, underscoring the inability of conventional methods to effectively classify NDD and TD, as well as NDD subgroups. These findings likely reflect the limited ability of the frequency power features from only three frontal EEG channels to capture the distributed and dynamic neural activity associated with NDDs. In contrast, the deep learning model effectively extracted informative spatiotemporal patterns directly from raw waveforms, despite the restricted spatial coverage. These results highlight the suitability of end-to-end learning approaches for simplified EEG systems with limited channels, such as patch-type devices.

Among previous studies focused on ASD vs. TD classification, Xu et al. (2024) reported an accuracy of 81.8% using EEG signals to create time series maps of brain functional connectivity analyzed using a CNN–Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model. Similarly, Peng et al. (2021) demonstrated accuracies ranging from 85.32% to 87.12% using EEG signal feature selection with L1-norm regularization. While these studies achieved higher classification accuracies than the bACC of 75% in our study, it is important to note that both used high-density EEG systems with 125–128 channels, whereas our model achieved competitive performance using only three channels. This demonstrates the potential of portable EEG systems to achieve clinically meaningful classification with significantly fewer electrodes, enhancing their practicality for real-world applications. Furthermore, the application of high-density EEG is time-consuming (Chu, 2015). Achieving a certain level of accuracy with portable EEG systems, which allow shorter and simpler setup, is particularly significant for children with developmental disorders.

For ADHD vs. TD classification, Alim and Imtiaz (2023) achieved accuracies of 93.2% and 94.2% using a Gaussian support vector machine (SVM) model trained on EEG data, while Chen et al. (2019) obtained an accuracy of 90.29% using a CNN-based approach that leveraged spatial-frequency abnormalities. Both studies used EEG devices with 19 channels. In comparison, our study achieved a sensitivity of 92.3% and a bACC of 79.5% for ADHD vs. TD using only three EEG channels. This result suggests that ADHD-related neurophysiological features may be reflected in frontal attention-network activity, which was targeted by our portable EEG device.

However, a study investigating the EEG power spectrum in the ASD + ADHD group suggested that, despite overlapping behavioral characteristics with the ADHD group, the underlying psychophysiological mechanisms may differ (Bink et al., 2015). In addition, Shephard et al. (2018) reported that resting-state EEG in children with ASD is characterized by reduced theta and alpha power, whereas children with ADHD exhibit lower delta power. In the comorbid ASD + ADHD group, these characteristics appeared to be combined. Given the substantial overlap in EEG features between ASD and ASD + ADHD, distinguishing between these groups has been challenging. However, the use of the RO-MLT model has been reported to achieve a high accuracy of 85.7% in classification, addressing this issue effectively (Dong et al., 2024). Unlike these previous studies, we achieved a lower bACC of 62.7% for ASD + ADHD vs. TD classification. This suggests that, despite the overlapping neuropsychological features, the neurophysiological patterns of this subgroup may be more heterogeneous/complex and may not be fully captured by frontal lobe EEG measurements alone.

Distinguishing clinically defined NDD categories has also been challenging with other non-invasive physiological measures. Constable et al. (2025) used light-adapted electroretinography with machine-learning methods to classify ASD, ADHD, ASD + ADHD, and TD groups. Although relatively high balanced accuracy was reported for binary classifications of ASD or ADHD, performance was lower in the four-group model that included ASD + ADHD as a separate diagnostic category. Specifically, balanced accuracy decreased from 0.70 in a three-group model to 0.53 when ASD + ADHD was added as a fourth group, likely reflecting the small subgroup size and phenotypic overlap. These findings are consistent with the broader difficulty of aligning physiological biomarkers directly with behaviorally defined DSM categories. Thus, EEG-based and other physiological biomarkers may be useful as adjunctive screening tools to identify children who may require further clinical evaluation for NDDs rather than as stand-alone tools for clear-cut categorical diagnoses.

In the present study, the classification performance for ADHD vs. TD was superior to that for ASD or ASD + ADHD vs. TD. This may be partly because individuals with ASD exhibit network abnormalities in the temporal and parietal regions in addition to the frontal regions (Wang et al., 2020). Our portable EEG system limited to measuring frontal brain activity may have been insufficient to capture broader neural abnormalities associated with ASD.

Current NDD diagnostic pathways remain resource-intensive and largely symptom-based, often limiting access and leading to long waiting times. While validated instruments such as ADOS—considered the gold standard for ASD diagnosis—show high diagnostic performance (e.g., sensitivity 87% and specificity 75%) (Santos et al., 2024), their use requires trained specialists and may therefore limit access, particularly for patients living far from specialized centers. Therefore, simplified adjunctive tools have been developed, such as Gazefinder for ASD (Tsuchiya et al., 2021) and QbTest for ADHD (Bellato et al., 2024), which provide clinically useful objective measures based on behavior during short-term tasks (e.g., gaze patterns, inattention, impulsivity, and hyperactivity). In this context, we focused on portable frontal EEG as a minimally invasive and rapidly deployable approach that may provide objective adjunctive information to support further clinical evaluation, particularly for children with ADHD-related features. In real-world screening settings, the sensitivity and specificity observed in this study require cautious interpretation because false negatives may delay further evaluation, whereas false positives may increase unnecessary referrals, particularly in lower-prevalence populations such as community-based screening or general pediatric practice. The present findings suggest that portable EEG may provide adjunctive information for identifying children requiring further evaluation for neurodevelopmental difficulties, including co-occurring ASD and ADHD features. However, it should not be interpreted as a stand-alone diagnostic test or a tool that directly determines treatment selection. In particular, identifying an ASD + ADHD phenotype may not necessarily alter management, because medications for ADHD-related symptoms primarily target inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, whereas social-communication difficulties in ASD require comprehensive developmental and behavioral assessment and support. Therefore, this approach is best positioned as an adjunctive screening tool to guide further evaluation rather than as a replacement for standard clinical diagnosis.

This study has several limitations. First, the ASD and ADHD groups had smaller sample sizes than the TD and ASD + ADHD subgroups. This concern has two components: the impact on model training and that on performance evaluation. The first was mitigated by the analytical design, in which fold-specific deep learning models were trained exclusively within the primary binary NDD vs. TD cross-validation procedure, and the resulting fixed OOF predictions were then subsetted according to clinical diagnosis. No subgroup-specific classifiers were trained, mitigating the principal source of optimistic bias associated with re-fitting models on small positive classes. The second remains a legitimate limitation, as small test samples necessarily produce wide CIs around the estimated metrics. We addressed this by reporting 95% bootstrap CIs and label-permutation tests, both of which are non-parametric and robust for small sample sizes, and by repositioning the subgroup analyses as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Nonetheless, larger prospectively recruited cohorts will be required to provide reliable disorder-specific performance estimates.

Second, as discussed above, the sex distribution imbalance between groups was examined through sex-stratified analyses. Although male predominance is expected in ASD and ADHD, the male-to-female ratios in this cohort, particularly in the ASD + ADHD subgroup, appeared higher than those reported in Japanese epidemiological data (Saito et al., 2020; Okada et al., 2024). This suggests that cohort-specific recruitment, referral, or diagnostic-ascertainment factors may have contributed to the imbalance. Larger sex-balanced cohorts are needed to clarify potential sex-specific neurophysiological patterns and generalizability.

Third, the deep learning architecture used in this study, which combines parallel 1D CNN branches with a transformer encoder operating on raw EEG waveforms, limits direct feature-level interpretability. Although general feature-attribution methods such as SHAP can in principle be applied to deep learning models, their outputs are not straightforward to translate into conventional EEG features, such as frequency-band power, channel-level spatial patterns, or physiologically interpretable temporal components, in an end-to-end raw-waveform architecture. Therefore, we could not directly identify which spectral or spatial EEG features drove the model's decisions. This is a known limitation of end-to-end deep learning approaches for EEG classification. Nevertheless, the pattern of performance across clinically diagnosed subgroups is consistent with the existing neurophysiological literature on frontal EEG abnormalities in NDDs. Classification performance was strongest for ADHD vs. TD, in which frontal-midline theta abnormalities and elevated theta/beta ratio are well-established neurophysiological signatures (Markovska-Simoska and Pop-Jordanova, 2017; Snyder et al., 2008), localized to the regions covered by our forehead electrodes. ASD-related EEG abnormalities have been reported to be more spatially distributed across frontal, temporal, and parietal regions (Wang et al., 2013, 2020), which may partly explain the more modest performance for ASD vs. TD. For the ASD + ADHD subgroup, prior work has shown that the comorbid phenotype manifests as a largely additive combination of ASD and ADHD EEG features with additional unique markers (Shephard et al., 2018; Bink et al., 2015), and this neurophysiological complexity may have contributed to the lower classification performance in this group. Future work should explore interpretable deep learning architectures such as prototype-based networks (Barnett et al., 2024) and concept bottleneck models to provide direct feature-level explanations.

Fourth, although the model was evaluated using cross-validation to ensure robustness, external validation with a holdout dataset is needed to confirm the generalizability of the results. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Our findings demonstrate the feasibility of distinguishing between NDD and TD using EEG data recorded from a portable patch-type device applied to the forehead. We also exploratorily evaluated this model's classification performance for ADHD vs. TD, ASD vs. TD, and ASD + ADHD vs. TD. In these exploratory subgroup analyses, the model achieved relatively high performance in distinguishing ADHD from TD (sensitivity: 92.3%, bACC: 79.5%), suggesting that ADHD-related neurophysiological features may be captured by frontal EEG even with a limited number of electrodes. In contrast, the classification performances for ASD vs. TD (bACC: 75%) and ASD + ADHD vs. TD (bACC: 62.7%) were moderate to low, reflecting the complexity of these classifications. Despite these challenges, the results underscore the potential of portable EEG systems as adjunctive screening tools for NDDs in clinical and health checkup settings. Future efforts should focus on improving classification accuracy and expanding the application of portable EEG systems in diverse clinical contexts.
