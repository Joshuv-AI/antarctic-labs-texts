Isolation of CD63‐positive epididymosomes from human semen and its application in improving sperm function
Authors: Jingwen Luo, Shiqing Zhu, Yafei Kang, Xinyu Liu, Xia Tan, Jieyi Zhao, Xiaofang Ding, Honggang Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are highly heterogeneous, and different EV subpopulations from various origins mediate different biological effects. The separation of different subpopulations of EVs from mixtures is critical but challenging. Epididymosomes are secreted by the epididymal epithelium and play a key role in sperm maturation and function. However, limited access to human epididymal tissue and epididymal fluid has hampered further study of epididymosomes and their potential clinical applications. Here, we established a novel strategy based on flow cytometry sorting to isolate human CD63‐positive epididymosomes from ejaculate. We identified CD52, a membrane‐located protein expressed exclusively in the epididymis, as the sorting marker for human epididymosomes. Then, CD63‐positive epididymosomes were isolated from human semen using a flow cytometry sorting instrument and concentrated. Additionally, we observed that isolated CD63‐positive epididymosomes improved sperm function more than other CD63‐positive seminal EV subpopulations did, demonstrating the successful isolation of a subpopulation of epididymosomes from human semen and their potential application in the clinic.Keywords: asthenozoospermia, epididymosomes, EV isolation methods, extracellular vesicles, sperm function

INTRODUCTION

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanometre‐sized particles surrounded by phospholipid bilayers (Nieuwland et al., 2018) that play important roles in intercellular communication and the regulation of numerous physiological and pathological processes (Yu et al., 2022). It is now widely accepted that EVs comprise different subpopulations with different cargoes derived from their cells of origin (Willms et al., 2018; Zaborowski et al., 2015). The cargoes of EVs reflect the statuses of their parental cells and can deliver information to recipient cells (Kormelink et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2022). Each subpopulation of EVs from a different origin has its own specific biological role (Tian et al., 2018). However, the mixture of EVs in laboratory preparations prevents a clear understanding of the specific functional properties of each (Kowal et al., 2016). On the other hand, the use of EVs as disease diagnostic markers has significantly progressed over recent years (Roy et al., 2018). Recently, many studies have shown that the compositions and functions of EVs change in disease states (Nieuwland et al., 2018). For example, there are differences between EVs produced by normal and heterogeneous populations of cells in the cancer microenvironment (Moeng et al., 2020), and EV cargoes from inflammatory cells are altered compared to those from normal cells (Yin et al., 2022). These findings also suggest that EV subpopulations of abnormal origin require isolation from their normal counterparts so that the disease mechanisms can be investigated. Likewise, for diseases in which only complex fluids from multiple sources are clinically available, isolating EV subpopulations may be a noninvasive diagnostic tool for determining the location and type of infection (Yu et al., 2022). Therefore, there is a critical need for a method of separating EV subpopulations from various origins.

The current common methods for isolating EVs, such as differential ultracentrifugation (dUC), size exclusion chromatography (SEC) and polymer‐mediated precipitation (Singh et al., 2021), can isolate only a total pool of EVs and are unable to assess inter‐EV variability. Recently, methods for separating various subpopulations of EVs, such as an Iodixanol/Optiprep gradient and sucrose‐based step‐gradient separation, have begun to emerge, mainly based on different sizes and densities (Crescitelli et al., 2021; D'Acunzo et al., 2022; Kowal et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2017). However, the physical properties of tissue‐ or cell‐specific EV subpopulations are often similar. How to separate them remains a challenge (George et al., 2021).

As EV subpopulations from different origins exhibit different levels of specific protein expression (Boriachek et al., 2018), this approach allows us to capture target EV subpopulations with a high degree of specificity through an immunoaffinity approach (Koliha et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2021). In particular, in recent years, the resolution and sensitivity of conventional flow cytometry have steadily improved with technological advances (Zhu et al., 2014), allowing this technology to no longer be limited to the cell field but also to be applied to the detection of nanoparticles (Higginbotham et al., 2016; Koliha et al., 2016). Unfortunately, the absence of well‐defined subpopulation‐specific markers has hampered the study of flow cytometry for sorting target EVs (Willms et al., 2018). Determination of the exclusive biophysical and molecular compositions and characterisation of each EV subpopulation are major challenges (Vader et al., 2016). In addition, increasing the concentration of the collected target EV subpopulations for downstream experiments is also a problem. Overall, flow cytometry‐based sorting of EVs still needs further improvement, such as how to improve the detection sensitivity of nanoparticles, how to address the “swarming” phenomenon (Van Der Pol et al., 2012), how to address the steric hindrance caused by multiple antibodies (Tian et al., 2018), and how to accurately separate and sort positive and negative populations.

Sperm are transcriptionally and translationally quiescent and, therefore, rely on the epididymal microenvironment for sperm maturation. Epididymosomes are EVs secreted by the epididymal epithelium and are a major component of the epididymal microenvironment (Nixon et al., 2019). Previous studies have confirmed that epididymosomes deliver proteins and lipids to sperm; this is the primary mechanism for establishing sperm competence. Recently, the importance of epididymosomes has become increasingly appreciated. Many reports (Bohacek &Rassoulzadegan, 2020; Donkin & Barrès, 2018; Tamessar et al., 2021) have demonstrated that small noncoding RNAs (sncRNAs) in epididymosomes can be affected by environmental factors and subsequently be delivered into sperm and play a role in embryonic development and offspring health by acting as a cargo to communicate between somatic cells and germline cells; that is, they break the Weismann barrier and support Darwin's pangenesis hypothesis (Nejabati et al., 2021). In summary, given the important role of epididymosomes in male reproduction, a more in‐depth study of these proteins is essential. However, robust human studies of epididymosomes are limited. It is difficult to obtain epididymal tissue and epididymal fluid without prostate or seminal vesicle gland fluid (Goss et al., 2023). The ability to isolate epididymosomes directly from human semen will contribute greatly to research in the field of male reproduction. Additionally, we can use epididymosomes as a noninvasive clinical diagnostic marker.

The present study represents the first report to establish a method for isolating tissue‐specific EV subpopulations by using flow cytometry sorting technology. In this study, we identified an epididymal‐specific protein present in epididymosomes located on the membrane surface as a sorting marker. We subsequently attempted to isolate epididymosomes subpopulation directly from human semen and investigated the effects of different seminal EV subpopulations on sperm function. Increasing the concentration and validation were also major focuses of our work. Taken together, these findings suggest that this approach can also be applied to isolate target EV subpopulations from other mixtures if specific markers can be determined.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection

Human semen samples were collected at the Union Hospital of Huazhong University of Science and Technology and Wuhan Huake Reproductive Hospital from volunteers aged 25–40 years. Samples were obtained by masturbation after 3–5 days of abstinence and underwent semen analysis after liquefaction at 37°C for 30 min. Semen evaluation was performed in accordance with the Fifth Edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen (World Health Organization, 2010). All the experiments were performed in accordance with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from each of the participants.

We collected data from a total of 153 men with normozoospermia (NSP), 56 men with obstructive azoospermia (OASP) (including postvasectomy azoospermia), and 60 men with severe asthenozoospermia (SASP). NSP individuals: all semen parameters are normal. OASP patients: no sperm were found in the semen, and the semen test was performed three times. Then diagnosed with OASP through sex hormone examination, ultrasound, seminal biochemical examination and testicular biopsy (postvasectomy azoospermia patients: more than 3 months after vasectomy). Complete obstruction in men with OASP was confirmed by the absence of DDX4 mRNA in seminal plasma, as described in our previous report (Li et al., 2012). SASP patients: had normal semen parameters except for the percentage of progressive sperm motility, which was less than 10%, and the semen test was performed three times. The exclusion criteria were as follows: varicocele, genitourinary inflammation, seminal tract infections and smoking. Epididymal fluid was derived from vaso‐epididymal anastomosis in patients with OASP by siphon aspiration. After exclusion, totally 193 individuals were included: 123 men with NSP, 16 men with OASP and 54 men with SASP. Six NSP and 13 OASP patients were used to validate CD52 as a specific marker for epididymosomes. Three NSP individuals were used to estimate the purity of EVs isolated by dUC. Eighteen NSP and 9 SASP patients were used for flow cytometry analysis, in which seminal EVs from every three individuals were mixed into one sample, and three replications were performed for both NSP and SASP groups. Thirty‐three NSP and 3 OASP patients were used to validate successful flow cytometry sorting of epididymosomes. Three individuals’ seminal EVs were mixed into one sample for each flow cytometry sorting. Three NSP individuals were used to observe the entry of EV subpopulations into sperm. Sixty NSP and 45 SASP patients were used to explore the effect of different seminal EV subpopulations on sperm function. Among them, semen samples of 15 NSP individuals were used to isolate sperm while others were used to sort EV subpopulations. Each sperm function test was repeated at least three times.

Isolation of EVs from seminal plasma

After liquefaction, the semen samples were centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 25°C to remove sperm. The seminal plasma was then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cell debris and possible apoptotic bodies. Next, the supernatant was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C to remove any other contaminants. Finally, the EVs were precipitated by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4°C, washed in PBS, and ultracentrifuged again (Figure 1a). The pellets were resuspended in PBS and stored at −80°C.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis

The size distribution and particle concentration of the EVs were measured via a light scattering‐based Zetaview (Particle Metrix, Germany). Before measurement, the equipment was calibrated with 100 nm polystyrene standard beads. All the samples were then diluted in PBS before being loaded into the equipment to determine the ideal particle count per frame. Captured video clips from ZetaView were analysed by ZetaView software (version 8.05.14 SP7).

Transmission electron microscopy

The resuspended EVs were added dropwise to 200‐mesh grids and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Then, the grids were negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 3 min, and the remaining liquid was removed with filter paper. The morphology of the EVs was subsequently observed via an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, Japan).

Western blot analysis

Sperm and EVs were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (PMSF, 1 mM) on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, after which the protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0012S; Beyotime). The samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer and boiled for 5 min at 100°C. Equal amounts of each group protein were loaded on the blots. Proteins were separated by SDS‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (IPVH00010; EMD Millipore). The PVDF membranes were blocked in QuickBlock blocking buffer (P0252; Beyotime) for 30 min at 37°C. The membranes were subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with the following antibodies: anti‐CD9 (1:1,000; #13174; Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐CD63 (1:1,000; A19023; ABclonal), anti‐Alix (1:1,000; A2215; ABclonal), anti‐Calnexin (1:1,000; A15631; ABclonal), anti‐CD52 (1:1,000; A16815; ABclonal), anti‐ADAM7 (1:500; EM1701‐53; HUABIO), anti‐α‐Tubulin (1:5,000; AC012; ABclonal), and anti‐phosphotyrosine antibody clone 4G10 (1:1,000; 05–321; Millipore), anti‐PSCA (1:500; A5614; ABclonal). After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5,000; Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature. After the membranes were washed again three times with TBST, the blots were treated with enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (C510045‐0100; Sangon Biotech) and imaged using ChemiDoc XRS+ (Bio‐Rad, USA).

Treatment of EVs with Triton X‐100

EVs of 5 × 109 particles were incubated with 1% Triton X‐100 (Sigma‐Aldrich, #T9284‐100ML) for 1 h at 4°C. The purity level was estimated by comparing the relative particle counts before and after treatment. Semen from three NSP individuals was collected for three independent experiments. The reduction in particle numbers with the addition of Triton X‐100 indicates disruption of lipid particles (Lau et al., 2024).

Immunofluorescence microscopy

EVs and epididymal fluids were incubated with PKH26 staining solution for 10 min at room temperature in the dark and then centrifuged at 120,000 × g for 90 min to remove free working solution. EVs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and the precipitated EVs were suspended in PBS for smearing. Then, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum at 37°C for 30 min. Primary antibodies were added to the sections, and the sections were incubated at 4°C overnight. After washing with PBS 3–5 times, the EVs were incubated with fluorescence‐conjugated secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 h. The samples were then washed with PBS, followed by slide mounting. Finally, images were taken with a laser confocal microscope (UltraVIEW VoX & IX81, Perkin Elmer & Olympus, Germany).

Flow cytometry analysis

For particle size estimation, standard fluorescence microspheres 100, 200, 300, 500 and 1000 nm in length (Invitrogen, USA) were run on a CytoFLEX Daily QC Fluorospheres (Beckman Coulter, Pasadena, CA, USA) using a violet side scatter (VSSC) trigger.

Ten microlitres of EVs were incubated with antibodies for 30 min in the dark on ice. The concentration of EVs measured by the BCA method was approximately 0.17 µg/µL. The details of the antibodies used and the concentrations used can be found in Table S1. EVs were diluted 1:30 in 300 µL of PBS, and the samples were subsequently run on the CytoFLEX system (Beckman Coulter, Pasadena, CA, USA). With the advantage of light‐splitting Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM) used by CytoFLEX SRT, we exchanged the position of 405 and 450 nm filters to obtain the VSSC channel, which has higher sensitivity to nanoparticles according to the Mie scattering theory. Because there is no overlap in the wavelength range of phycoerythrin (PE) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) fluorescence excitation in this instrument, there is no need to adjust compensation. The detailed parameters used were as follows: trigger on 405 nm VSSC, violet side scatter detection set = 416 and gain of the VSSC signal in the acquisition settings set to 68. Samples were run at 10 µL per min for 30 s until the event/second rate became stable and then collected for 60 s for analysis. Manual gating was performed on populations of the target according to the isotype control. Gain settings: VSSC: 68. forward scatter (FSC): 247. side scatter (SSC): 221. FITC: 196. PE: 129.

Flow cytometry sorting and ultrafiltration

The most appropriate antibody concentration was selected based on flow cytometry analysis. Seminal EVs from three men were mixed into one sample for each flow cytometry sorting. Then, 300 µL of EVs was incubated with antibodies on ice for 30 min in the dark and diluted with PBS. After manual gating according to the isotype control, CD63/CD52 double‐positive and CD63‐positive/CD52‐negative EVs were isolated using the CytoFLEX system and collected separately. The detailed sorting settings are as follows: Sort logic: P3/P4, Sort mode: Purity 1–2, Target count: 1,000,000, Tube size: 5 mL, Initial volume: 1 mL (to prevent rupture of the sorted EVs by hitting the tube, a receiving solution, such as PBS, should be reserved with a volume of 1/5 of the collection tube). The consistent concentration of antibodies and EVs was ensured in each sperm function experiment.

The collected EVs from each group were concentrated separately using the 10 kDa MWCO Amicon concentrator (Millipore, USA), and the resulting EVs were used for downstream analyses.

In vitro binding of epididymosomes and sperm

A concentration of 0.02 mM Dil dye (C1036; Beyotime) in ethanol absolute was added to EV subpopulation suspensions and incubated for 30 min at 37°C with regular mixing. Excess dye is removed by ultrafiltration. Sperm from NSP individuals were separated from semen samples using a discontinuous Percoll gradient (VitaVitro, China) and then resuspended in Ham's F10 (12390035; Thermo Fisher Scientific) medium. Subsequently, Dil‐labelled EV subpopulations were added to sperm. After coincubation, the combination of them was observed under a fluorescence microscope (NIB‐630FL, Nexcope, China) using 40× magnification (Mahdavinezhad et al., 2022).

Sperm function examination

Sperm from NSP individuals were coincubated with epididymosomes from NSP individuals, seminal EVs without epididymosomes from NSP individuals, and epididymosomes from SASP patients. All the treatments were performed in Biggers‐Whitten‐Whittingham (BWW) “capacitating” medium (Solarbio, China). This medium is mainly composed of 120 mM NaCl, 4.6 mM KCl, 1.7 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 5.6 mM D‐glucose, 0.27 mM sodium pyruvate, 44 mM sodium lactate, 5 U/mL penicillin, 5 mg/mL streptomycin, 20 mM HEPES, 3 mg/mL bovine serum (BSA), and 25 mM NaHCO3, at a pH of 7.4 and an osmolarity of 300 mOsm. It is a common nutrition solution widely used in the treatment of animal and human sperm for capacitation (Shi et al., 2024). The above groups were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 in air for 15 min, 1 or 4 h. Each experiment was repeated in three different semen samples.

At each time point, the sperm samples were mixed with swelling solution (Solarbio, China), and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37°C in 5% CO2. Sperm viability and progressive motility were examined under an optical microscope (Olympus CX21, Japan) at a total magnification of 400×. The mean of three consecutive assessments under double‐blind conditions was recorded as the final result (%). To detect sperm capacitation, sperm protein tyrosine phosphorylation was assessed by Western blotting using the mouse anti‐phosphotyrosine antibody clone 4G10 (Millipore, USA). To visualize the acrosome reaction, sperm were stained with FITC‐PNA (30 min) and DAPI (5 min) under dark conditions. The state of the acrosome was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). The sperm with intact acrosomes were stained fluorescent green in the acrosome region but staining was not observed in the sperm in which the acrosome reaction occurred. The nuclei of all the sperm were stained blue. The number of acrosomes was subtracted from the number of nuclei and divided by the number of nuclei; this represented the incidence of acrosome reaction.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed by the two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post tests. A p < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference (GraphPad Prism, Version 9.5.0).

RESULTS

Identification of CD52 as a specific marker for human epididymosomes

We investigated epididymis‐specific proteins, such as CD36, CD52, ADAM7, CRISP1, BSPH1, ELSPBP1 and DEFB121, by comparing seminal plasma proteomics of NSP and OASP patients (Batruch et al., 2011) or examining epididymis‐related articles (Liu, 2016; Thimon et al., 2008) and subsequently searching for them on the Human Protein Atlas website (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). However, proteins such as CRISP1, BSPH1, ELSPBP1 and DEFB121 are not expressed on the membrane. No commercial antibodies against ADAM7 are available for flow cytometry sorting. There is no evidence that CD36 is expressed on epididymosomes, and we found that CD36 could not bind to EVs by flow cytometry analysis (data not shown). Therefore, these proteins were excluded. Previous studies have demonstrated that CD52, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)‐anchored protein, is expressed only in the epididymis and in blood lymphocytes and monocytes in males (Kirchhoff & Hale, 1996). The presence of CD52 protein in the seminal plasma of NSP individuals was verified by Western blotting, while CD52 was not detected in the seminal plasma of OASP patients (Rooney et al., 1996; Yeung et al., 1998). Moreover, CD52 is associated with EVs in epididymal luminal fluid and appears to be involved in the transfer of EVs to sperm (Liu, 2016). Therefore, we speculated that CD52 might be a marker for epididymosomes.

Since the ejaculate of OASP patients does not contain secretions from the epididymis, an ideal model for identifying specific markers of human epididymosomes would be identified by comparing candidate markers between EVs from NSP and OASP patients. EVs were successfully isolated from the seminal plasma of NSP and OASP patients by dUC (Figure 1a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed classic cup‐shaped double‐layer membrane‐bound vesicles (Figure 1b). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that the mean diameter of seminal EVs from NSP individuals was 147.2 nm, whereas it was 154.7 nm from OASP patients (Figure 1c). Moreover, there were no differences in the size or morphology of EVs between NSP and OASP patients. The presence of the EV‐specific markers CD9, CD63 and Alix was verified by Western blotting. The endoplasmic reticulum marker calnexin was undetected in all EVs but was found in the sperm, which served as a positive control (Figure 1d).

The expression of the CD52 protein in EVs from NSP and OASP patients was further analysed. CD52 protein was not detected in seminal EVs from three different OASP patients but was detected in NSP individuals by Western blotting (Figure 1d). Consistent with these findings, the laser confocal microscopy results of the NSP‐derived EVs revealed the presence of CD52 (green fluorescence) and colocalisation with PKH26 (red fluorescence, an EV‐specific dye), whereas CD52 was not detected in the EVs from OASP patients (Figure 1e). These results suggest that CD52 is present in seminal EVs of testicular and epididymal origin, but it has been reported that the testis does not express the CD52 protein (Hale et al., 1993). Following this, epididymal fluids were used for further validation. We used immunofluorescence microscopy to visualise the collected epididymal fluid. Additionally, large amounts of CD52 were detected, and CD52 was colocalised with EVs (Figure 1e). Therefore, we speculate that the CD52 protein is a specific marker for epididymosomes and can be used in the isolation of epididymosomes.

Isolation of CD63/CD52 double‐positive EVs from human seminal EVs

We estimated the purity of EVs obtained from dUC by measuring the number of particles before (Figure S1A) and after (Figure S1B) treatment with Triton‐X 100 using NTA. The results showed that the number of particles after treatment was 61.89 % of that before treatment (average of three measurements) (Figure S1C). Triton X‐100 can dissolve the lipid layer and disrupt membrane structure and semen is a very complex component. Therefore, the purity of EVs should be less than 61.89%. To ensure that the CytoFLEX system produced a low background signal, the PBS and EVs were run separately (Figure S2A,B). To ensure that the antibodies bound to the EVs and did not aggregate, the antibodies were tested individually (Figure S2C,D). To ensure that the primary antibodies bound to the specific target, isotype controls for each antibody were incubated with EVs (Figure S3B).

Given that CD52 is located on the surface of seminal EVs and is specifically expressed in the epididymis, we sought to determine whether CD52‐positive EVs could be detected by flow cytometry to determine the percentage of seminal EVs. To detect epididymosomes in seminal EVs, EVs were incubated with antibodies against CD63 and CD52. First, nanoparticles with a size range of 0 to 1000 nm were set as P1 gates (Figure S3A). Considering that CD63 is considered a classic marker of EVs (Andreu & Yáñez‐Mó, 2014), we decided to use CD63‐positive EVs as P2 gates (Figure S3B). We subsequently selected CD52‐positive EVs among the P2 gates (Figure S3C). A distinct population of nanosized CD52‐positive particles was observed in seminal EVs (Figure S3).

A series of titration studies was performed to reduce nonspecific signals and to determine the appropriate antibody concentrations. The determined antibody concentrations were used in subsequent experiments (Figure 2a,b). The final quantified proportion of CD63‐positive EVs was approximately 15% (Figure 2c), among which CD52‐positive EVs accounted for approximately 30% (Figure 2d). However, we cannot exclude the possibility that some antibodies did not bind sufficiently, causing the expression of CD63 and CD52 to be below the detection limit. Another potential problem could be the aggregation of neighbouring EVs into clusters, resulting in the selection of some non‐CD63/CD52 double‐positive EVs. Therefore, it is important to note that the 15% and 30% data concluded from the titration studies are only rough estimates. The purpose of this analysis is to determine the antibody concentrations to be used for subsequent insolating experiments.

Once the antibody concentration was optimised, the concentration was selected as the sorting criterion for EVs (Figure 2e). The P3 gate included CD63‐positive/CD52‐negative EVs, which were considered seminal EVs without epididymosomes. P4 gates (CD63/CD52 double‐positive EVs) were thought to be epididymosomes. We backtested the collected EV subpopulation fluids (Figure S4).

Validation of CD63/CD52 double‐positive EVs as epididymosomes

The CD63/CD52 double‐positive EVs obtained by flow cytometry sorting and ultrafiltration were considered epididymosomes. We selected the ADAM7 protein as a marker to verify that epididymosomes were successfully isolated. ADAM7 belongs to the disintegrin and metalloprotease (ADAM) family, a family of multidomain membrane proteins (Cho, 2005). It is exclusively expressed in the epididymis and transferred directly to sperm through epididymosomes during epididymal transit (Han et al., 2011; Oh et al., 2009). We verified this by detecting the presence of ADAM7 in seminal EVs from NSP individuals but not from OASP patients by Western blotting (Figure 3a). We also attempted to use CRISP1, which has been reported to be specifically expressed in the epididymis and present in epididymosomes (Gibbs et al., 2008; Thimon et al., 2008), as a validation protein to show the successful sorting of epididymosomes. However, we detected CRISP1 expression in EVs from OASP patients by Western blotting (data not shown). Recent reports have shown that it is not specifically present in epididymosomes (Goss et al., 2023).

After successful sorting of the EV subpopulations, we performed subsequent validation. As shown in Figure 3b, Western blot analysis was used to detect ADAM7 expression in CD63/CD52 double‐positive (CD52‐positive) EVs but not in CD63‐positive/CD52‐negative (CD52‐negative) EVs. Then, the CD52‐positive and CD52‐negative EVs were first incubated with CD63 (green fluorescence) and stained with ADAM7 (red fluorescence). ADAM7 was identified to be active by super‐resolution imaging with colocalisation of CD63 in CD52‐positive EVs (Figure 3d). In contrast, almost no red fluorescence was detected in the CD52‐negative EVs (Figure 3d). Additionally, we detected prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA), a prostate‐specific marker (García‐Rodríguez et al., 2018), expressed in the CD52‐negative EVs but absent in the CD52‐positive EVs (Figure 3c,e). Therefore, we concluded that epididymosomes were successfully isolated.

Effects of different subpopulations of seminal EVs on sperm function

Since sperm progressive motility is acquired during passage through the epididymis and ascribed to epididymosomes (Candenas & Chianese, 2020), we collected semen samples from patients with SASP, whose sperm showed only poor progressive motility and whose other semen parameters were normal. For seminal plasma EVs from SASP patients, a series of titration studies was also performed to determine the appropriate antibody concentrations (Figure S5A,B). The final quantified seminal plasma contained approximately 15% CD63‐positive EVs, while CD52‐positive EVs accounted for approximately 28% of them (Figure S5C,D). Then, the epididymosomes of SASP patients were isolated using a CytoFLEX system (Figure S5E).

To investigate the functions of (CD63‐positive) epididymosomes and compare the functional differences between epididymosomes and other (CD63‐positive) seminal EVs, we incubated NSP individuals’ sperm with epididymosomes isolated from NSP (NSP+) and SASP (SASP+) patients and seminal EVs without epididymosomes from NSP (NSP−) individuals separately at the indicated time points. After a coincubation period, we observed that Dil‐labelled EV subpopulations were able to enter the sperm (Figure S6). And we found that different subpopulations of seminal EVs have different effects on sperm function. The information of individuals whose sperm was used for sperm function test is shown in Table S2.

Progressive motility and viability of sperm

Epididymosomes from NSP individuals had no significant effect on the progressive motility of NSP sperm. Seminal EVs without epididymosomes derived from NSP individuals significantly increased the percentage of progressive motility sperm and displayed better performance than epididymosomes at 1 h but impaired sperm progressive motility at 4 h. In contrast, instead of exerting any positive impact, epididymosomes from SASP patients exacerbated the decrease in the percentage of progressive sperm (Figure 4a). When the viability of the sperm was assessed in each group using the hypoosmotic swelling (HOS) test, we found no statistically significant differences at any time point (Figure 4b).

Sperm capacitation

Sperm tyrosine phosphorylation was increased over time and peaked at 4 h. We quantified the results of three Western blot results at 15 min, 1 and 4 h. The results showed that compared to controls, a significant increase in the degree of sperm capacitation in coincubation with epididymosomes from NSP individuals at 4 h. Seminal EVs without epididymosomes from NSP individuals also activated sperm tyrosine phosphorylation, but to a lesser extent. Conversely, treatment with epididymosomes from SASP patients had a very limited effect (Figure 4c).

Acrosome reaction

At 15 min, the acrosome reaction could already be observed in the sperm coincubated with epididymosomes from NSP individuals. At 1 h, the number of sperm undergoing acrosome reaction increased in all groups. At 4 h, the acrosome reaction was significantly increased in co‐incubation groups of epididymosomes from NSP individuals, followed by the seminal EVs without epididymosomes groups, while epididymosomes from SASP patients could not stimulate the reaction (Figure 4d,e).

DISCUSSION

The origin of EVs in seminal plasma is heterogeneous and includes the epididymis, prostate and seminal vesicle gland (Aalberts et al., 2012). Among them, epididymosomes play an important role (Barrachina et al., 2022) but are difficult to obtain (Goss et al., 2023). Here, we developed an emerging method, flow cytometry‐based sorting of EVs, to isolate CD63‐positive epididymosomes directly from human seminal plasma. Briefly, we identified the protein CD52, which is exclusively expressed in the epididymis and is present on the membranes of epididymosomes and used as a sorting marker. Antibody concentration titration experiments were then designed to analyse the proportion of CD63/CD52 double‐positive EVs among the total seminal EVs obtained by dUC using a CytoFLEX system. Following flow cytometry sorting, the proteins were concentrated via ultrafiltration for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, we observed that different seminal EV subpopulations have different effects on sperm functions. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to isolate human epididymosomes subpopulation from semen, and this is the first time that tissue‐specific EV subpopulations have been isolated from body fluids. The isolation of human epididymosomes could be used for functional studies or noninvasive diagnosis. Moreover, determining a sorting marker could lead to a novel approach for isolating other EV subpopulations of specific origin from mixtures.

The principle of flow cytometry sorting is to capture target EVs through binding interactions between specific antibodies labelled with fluorescent dyes and EV surface‐specific proteins (Singh et al., 2021). Although this technology was developed many years ago, it has not been applied in the EV field due to their nanoscale sizes, complex compositions, and other problems (Higginbotham et al., 2016). In particular, the determination of specific markers for EV subpopulations is a significant challenge (Willms et al., 2018). First, the specific marker proteins must be exclusively expressed at their origin, from which EV‐carried cargoes are derived (Zaborowski et al., 2015). A great deal of work is needed to identify tissue‐specific protein pools. Second, the biomarkers required for flow cytometry sorting should be localised on the surfaces of EVs to increase the likelihood of antibody binding and to ensure that the functional integrity of the EVs is not disrupted (Paul et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2021). Our study is the first to describe CD52 as a marker of epididymosomes. In the male reproductive system, CD52 is expressed exclusively in the epididymis (Hale et al., 1993; Kirchhoff et al., 1993; Yeung et al., 1998) and has been confirmed to be carried in EVs by Western blotting (Rooney et al., 1996). Because it is a GPI‐anchored protein located on the cell membrane (Kirchhoff & Hale, 1996), membrane‐associated proteins could also be present on the EV surface (Salmond et al., 2021). In this work, we confirmed that CD52 is expressed in the seminal EVs of NSP individuals and is absent in OASP patients (Figure 1d,e). Moreover, it can bind to the EV surface and be detected by flow cytometry analysis (Figure S3).

A previous study reported a new type of soluble CD52 without GPI anchor (Flori et al., 2008). This soluble CD52 is generated during semen liquefaction and is found in prostasome‐free seminal plasma, indicating that it is present as a soluble protein in seminal plasma not associated with EVs. There is also other evidence demonstrating that the soluble CD52 cannot insert into cell membranes (Giovampaola et al., 2001). In our results, as shown in Figure 3c,e, the CD52‐positive EVs did not express PSCA, which suggested that CD52‐positive EVs do not contain prostate‐derived substances, that is, no prostasomes. These provide support for excluding the possibility that soluble CD52 proteins can bind to other seminal EV subpopulations.

A series of concentration titration experiments were designed to determine the appropriate concentrations of CD63 and CD52 antibodies for binding to EVs using flow cytometry analysis (Figure S3). We observed that the percentage of fluorescence‐labelled EVs increased as the antibody concentration increased but decreased above the optimal concentration. This is because a concentration that is too low cannot reach the triggering threshold for fluorescence detection, while a concentration that is too high can lead to a “swarm” phenomenon (Libregts et al., 2018). CD63‐positive EVs accounted for only 15% of the P1 gates. This may be because EVs are isolated by dUC from biological fluids based on size, density and molecular weight (Singh et al., 2021), so some soluble protein aggregates and lipoproteins cosegregate (Yuana et al., 2014). There are also some EVs belonging to the CD9‐ or CD81‐ positive subpopulation (Tian et al., 2019). In addition, high concentrations of EVs and other particles that lack fluorescence labelling can hide the presence of target EV subpopulations, and smaller EVs are difficult to detect above this threshold (Libregts et al., 2018; Salmond et al., 2021). Moreover, immunofluorescence labelling of two proteins together is inefficient due to steric hindrance (Tian et al., 2018). Furthermore, antibody batch‐to‐batch variation and different instrument configurations (such as optical setups and analytical sensitivities) could also lead to discrepancies in results (Greening et al., 2015; Singh et al., 2021). Additionally, in our results, CD52‐positive EVs accounted for 30% of CD63‐positive EVs, which seems to be inconsistent with the report that epididymal fluid is a small portion of seminal plasma. It may be that the result of our analysis represents only the proportion of the CD63‐positive EVs, not the percentage of all seminal EVs. Moreover, the mean population of EV‐antibody binding is displaced compared to EV‐isotype antibody binding in Figure 2c,e. This may be because flow cytometry techniques cannot detect nanoparticles with high enough resolution. Similar results can be seen in other articles on flow cytometry analysis of EVs (Choi et al., 2019). The analysis and sorting of smaller nanoparticles by flow cytometry remain challenging.

There is another problem regarding low concentrations that we also confronted. To avoid the “swarm” phenomenon (Van Der Pol et al., 2012), EV preparations were performed at low sample concentrations (Kormelink et al., 2016). Moreover, the sheath fluid entered the collection tube during the sorting process, further diluting the collected EV fluid. Thus, solutions must be found to ensure that downstream experiments proceed successfully. First, we used the dUC method, but the losses were significant, resulting in low yields of EVs (Willms et al., 2018). We then tried to concentrate the EVs from PBS by polymer precipitation. However, polymeric contaminants may be toxic to sperm (Patel et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020), causing massive sperm death. Eventually, we chose the ultrafiltration strategy, which isolates particles based on their size and molecular weight with minimal structural damage to maintain functional integrity (Singh et al., 2021). However, the concentration efficiency is still not fully satisfactory, and further exploration of the methods is needed.

Previous studies have focused on elucidating the content and role of total EVs in human semen (Foot & Kumar, 2021; Kowalczyk et al., 2022). Our study reports new findings regarding the functional diversity of different subpopulations of seminal EVs. Moreover, we compared the functions of (CD63‐positive) epididymosomes from NSP and SASP patients. Both epididymosomes and (CD63‐positive) seminal EVs without epididymosomes derived from NSP individuals improved sperm functions. However, these two subpopulations of EVs do not have identical roles. After coincubation with seminal EVs without epididymosomes, the percentage of progressive motility peaked at 1 h, and this value was significantly lower than that of the controls at 4 h. However, the progressive motility of sperm coincubated with epididymosomes was not impaired at 4 h. Epididymosomes may facilitate sperm acquiring motility and fertilizing ability as well as inhibit excessive motility to keep sperm in a stable active state, just like the function of epididymis. But prostasomes may not perform as well in maintaining sperm activity. This interesting phenomenon was shown in previous studies. During coincubation with EVs from OASP patients, the sperm progressive motility peaked rapidly and then decreased over time (Murdica et al., 2019). Moreover, treatment with epididymosomes and seminal EVs without epididymosomes from NSP individuals triggered sperm capacitation and acrosome reactions, but these effects were more evident with epididymosomes at 4 h. This difference may be related to the fact that different seminal EV subpopulations contain different proteomes and have different functions (Murdica et al., 2019). Human epididymosomes and prostasomes have been confirmed to contain many proteins in different functional categories (Martin‐DeLeon, 2015; Ronquist, 2012; Thimon et al., 2008). Many of these genes are thought to be involved in the regulation of sperm maturation and fertility (Murdica et al., 2019). However, it is worth noting that the epididymosomes we isolated were CD63‐positive, and there may exist CD81‐ or CD9‐positive epididymosomes. What we have isolated currently may be a specific subpopulation of epididymosomes.

It is highly anticipated that complex EV populations will be accurately isolated and characterised into different subpopulations based on their functional diversity and specificity. The results of our study have established a novel strategy for isolating tissue‐specific EV subpopulations. Similarly, this strategy can be extended to other separations of specific EVs from complex origins. Here, we also provide a requirement and a way to identify specific sorting markers. However, there are still several challenges that need to be addressed, such as further purification and concentration of EV subpopulations.
