A Rare Presentation of A Common Dermatosis; Unilateral Blaschko-Linear Erythema Multiforme
Authors: Birgul Ozkesici Kurt, Ilknur Kıvanc Altunay, Aysegul Oz, Seyhan Ozakkoyunlu Hascicek, Pinar Ozdemir Cetinkaya, Asli Aksu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Erythema multiforme (EM) is a self-limiting, immune-mediated condition characterized by target-like lesions, often triggered by infections, with herpes simplex virus (HSV) being the most common agent. In rare cases, EM may present following Blaschko’s lines, a pattern of cutaneous mosaicism. We report a unique case of EM in a 17-year-old male with lesions distributed along Blaschko’s lines across his arm, trunk, and leg, marking the most extensive presentation of EM in this pattern to date. The patient had no history of HSV infection or drug use, except for cannabis consumption, which was considered a probable trigger based on the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of EM, and the lesions resolved with topical clobetasol propionate. This case highlights the rare occurrence of EM along Blaschko’s lines in a young patient and suggests cannabis as a potential trigger, contributing to the understanding of atypical presentations and etiology in EM.

Erythema multiforme (EM) is an acute, immune-mediated, self-limiting condition that presents with characteristic target-like lesions that usually involve the extensor surfaces of the acral extremities symmetrically. Various factors, including infections, medications, vaccination, menstruation, and autoimmune diseases, may play a role in EM. However, infections play the main role in the etiology, being present in 90% of cases, and herpes simplex virus (HSV) is the most commonly identified agent among all. It follows a centripetal pattern and usually resolves spontaneously within a few weeks.[1] The lines of Blaschko as a distribution pattern for EM are exceptionally infrequent, and to the best of our knowledge, only three patients have been described previously in the literature (Suppl 1).[2–4]

Case Report

A 17-year-old male patient consulted our clinic from the emergency room with the complaint of a sudden, widespread rash for a week. He had a history of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, and he had no previous history of drug use or infection, including HSV infection, and no history of a similar skin rash. Later, he informed us about his cannabis usage, which was two weeks before the rash, and alcohol consumption on the previous night. However, he reported that he had used them before and had no rash. The lesions first appeared on his hand and spread to his arm, trunk, and leg. On dermatological examination, linear, erythematous, edematous papules and plaques following Blaschko’s lines, extending from the right hand and arm to the shoulder, and the ipsilateral trunk and ipsilateral leg were observed (Fig. 1a, 1b, 1c). The lesions consisted of target lesions distributed in narrow bands following Blaschko’s lines (Fig. 1d). The mucosal examination was normal. There was no sign of any infection including HSV. In laboratory examination, hematological, biochemical, and immunological tests were within the normal range. The histopathology of the skin biopsy taken from a lesion on the trunk was compatible with EM, showing basal vacuolar degeneration, necrotic keratinocytes, papillary dermal edema, lymphocyte exocytosis, and dermal lymphocytic infiltrate (Fig. 2). A diagnosis of EM was made based on clinical and histopathological findings, and the patient was started on clobetasol propionate cream once daily. The lesions resolved within a week with the treatment. No recurrence was observed at the 1-year follow-up. Based on the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale, the association between cannabis abuse and the adverse drug reaction was determined to be ‘probable’, with a score of 5.[5] Informed consent forms were obtained from the patient and his mother.

Discussion

Blaschko’s lines, described by Blaschko in 1901, follow a pattern represented by many congenital, genetic, or acquired skin diseases. It is a pattern of cutaneous mosaicism, and the cause of these lines is not fully elucidated.[6] EM following Blaschko’s lines is an extremely rare presentation.[2–4]

Considering the triggering conditions, while recurrent herpes labialis was reported as an etiological factor in two of the previous three cases, one did not have a history of active or previous HSV, similar to our case. As in our case, no drug history has been reported in the other three cases in the literature. Cannabis is one of the most popular illicit drugs used around the world and has been associated with several skin diseases.[7,8] Only one case of cannabis-associated EM has been reported in the literature, and this may be the second case with the possible association between EM and cannabis.[9] Cannabis-associated EM may be mediated through a type IV hypersensitivity reaction.

All previous cases in the literature were localized to the arm and trunk. However, our case, to the best of our knowledge, is the first one presented with leg involvement in addition to arm and trunk distribution.[2–4] Therefore, our case differs in the involvement of both unilateral extremities and trunk. As far as we know, it is the most widespread EM following Blaschko’s lines in the literature. EM frequently occurs in young adults. However, our case is the youngest EM case presented with lesions following Blaschko’s lines.

The main differential diagnosis is adult blaschkitis, which always follows ipsilateral Blaschko’s lines and presents with relapsing, pruritic papulovesicular eruption.[10] Due to the target-like skin lesions, histopathological features, and lack of recurrence, we excluded adult blaschkitis and diagnosed our patient with EM.

Conclusion

We report this case because it is an extremely rare presentation of EM with a rash on one-half of the body, following Blaschko’s lines in an adolescent patient. Although it is one of the common and well-known dermatological diseases, some rare presentations may be difficult to diagnose. It should be kept in mind that common and well-known dermatological diseases may also have rare presentations, may occur at younger ages, and the etiology cannot always be determined.
