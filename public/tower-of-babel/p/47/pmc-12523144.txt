Development and implementation of a multiple stage emergency care training program in Kono, Sierra Leone: a clinician-educator curriculum
Authors: Michael Shen, Fenty Benk Sankoh, Pranali Sheth, Shada A Rouhani, Madison Bangert, Eleyias Tebeje, Remy Nkwiro Bitwayiki, Emaudu Deen, Keegan Checkett, Marta Patiño Rodriguez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face a disproportionate burden of acute diseases exacerbated by limited emergency care infrastructure and severe deficiencies in emergency care education. To address this critical need in Sierra Leone, we implemented an emergency care training program at Koidu Government Hospital (KGH) targeted at Clinical Health Officers (CHOs) and nurses. We further developed an educator training curriculum aimed at transitioning program graduates into clinician-educators.MethodsAlumni of an emergency care course previously conducted at this site were eligible for inclusion in the study. Participants underwent a multidimensional knowledge and skills assessment; those that passed were selected to participate in the Emergency Medical Educator Development (EMED) course. Participants completing the EMED course were evaluated using a knowledge and skills assessment. The cohort’s teaching self-efficacy was then compared to that of a baseline group, and student feedback on the cohort’s teaching performance in an Advanced Emergency Training (AET) course was compared to consultant instructors. All materials including the preliminary multidimensional assessment, EMED course, and EMED student assessment were developed by the authors with incorporation of key stakeholder input and consideration of contextual factors. Data analysis was performed using SPSS.ResultsFour CHOs and three nurses completed the EMED course. All but one student passed the final knowledge assessment. Evaluation of didactic and clinical teaching skills demonstrated adequate to good performance. Self-efficacy scores in both didactic and clinical teaching were significantly higher in the EMED cohort compared to a baseline group. Although students in the AET course scored lower compared to consultant instructors, subjective reviews were positive overall.ConclusionsDespite significant technological barriers and limited prior educational experience, we successfully trained a cohort of educators who demonstrated markedly improved self-efficacy and received strong reviews from students. Moving forward, our goal is to transition the cohort to more autonomous roles while reducing reliance on international assistance. We believe this model offers a promising approach to sustainable emergency care development in resource-limited settings by building local educational capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07808-1.Keywords: Emergency care, Low-middle income country (LMIC), Sierra leone, Training of trainers (ToT), Curriculum development, Clinician-educator

Introduction

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear a disproportionate burden of mortality and morbidity from acute illness including infectious and non-communicable diseases, pregnancy complications, and traumatic injuries [1, 2]. This burden is exacerbated by limited primary care, delayed treatment, and a paucity of out-of-hospital emergency services [3]. In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) has identified the development of emergency care systems as a key priority for developing healthcare systems, with the potential to substantially impact mortality [4]. Effective emergency care systems require a trained, skilled workforce. Many LMICs face extreme shortages of trained emergency care providers, with limited educational pathways for providers to learn emergency care [4].

Among LMIC regions, Sub-Saharan Africa endures conspicuously high mortality rates in emergency care settings [5]. Historical, socioeconomic, and infrastructural factors, including the dearth of physicians with emergency training, contribute to this current state of adversity. Sierra Leone, a low-income country in West Sub-Saharan Africa, exemplifies the confluence of these factors and the subsequent critical need for emergency care development. Over the past three decades, the Ebola outbreak and civil war have severely impacted the country’s health workforce and medical system [6]. With only 0.425 doctors per 10,000 people, Sierra Leone has one of the lowest doctor-to-population ratios globally [7]. Needs assessments highlight significant gaps in emergency care systems, workforce, education, and infrastructure [8]. In Freetown, the capital city, studies note serious deficiencies in emergency care education, skills, and resources across public and private hospitals [8, 9]. The situation is likely more challenging outside of Freetown as most doctors are concentrated in the capital [10]. In rural settings, mid-level providers known as Clinical Health Officers (CHOs) are often the only medical providers available and have limited or no training in emergency care.

Emergency care education efforts can be broadly divided into two models. The first is a resource-intensive, physician-targeted approach, such as emergency medicine residency programs, which typically last six months to six years [11–13]. These programs assume access to trained doctors, academic settings, and technological resources, and aim to develop highly educated clinicians who can also serve as educators [11–14]. Unfortunately, these are not yet practical in settings like rural Sierra Leone. These regions often suffer from a lack of doctors, limited technological facilities, and lack of academic resources, which makes physician-led educational development unfeasible. “Brain drain” of highly trained providers further limits the sustainability of such efforts.

The second and less resource-intensive model is short, focused courses that target a wider range of providers. Students can include nurses, mid-level providers, physicians, or even community members. Within emergency care, this model is best and most widely exemplified by the WHO-Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course, a five-day intensive course focused on a systems-based approach to emergency care [15]. This course has a two-day “Training of Trainers” (ToT) component for passing students, where students learn to teach subsequent cohorts pre-developed, structured lessons [16]. This model theorizes that educational materials can be disseminated through a “cascading” series of teaching interactions, where initial students teach subsequent cohorts [17]. This course has been implemented with success in LMICs and demonstrated substantial mortality benefit in Uganda [18]. However, the ToT program associated with the WHO-BEC is limited in scope, as participants are taught to deliver specific material, neglecting to develop critical independent teaching skills [16].

Our program, the Emergency Medical Educator Development (EMED) Course, aimed to bridge the gap between resource-intensive physician-focused programs and short-term interventions in a setting with limited doctors, technology, and academic resources. Koidu Government Hospital in Sierra Leone previously implemented two emergency care courses targeting CHOs, clinical officers, medical officers, and nurses. To make these courses sustainable, we aimed to develop graduates of these initial courses into clinician educators through the EMED program.

Methods

Study aim

The aim of this study was to develop, implement, and assess an emergency care clinician-educator pilot program for mid-level providers and nursing staff.

Study design

This was a single-site educational development and implementation study that utilized within-site comparisons. The overall schema of the study may be found in Fig. 1, which attempted to incorporate Mormina & Pinter’s (2018) framework for a sustainable ToT educational cascade [19]. Briefly, graduates of the previously conducted Emergency Medicine Training Course were invited to participate in a multidimensional assessment through which a cohort of CHOs and nurses was selected to complete the Emergency Medical Educator Development (EMED) Course. The EMED Course was designed in line with behavioral, cognitive, constructivist, and social learning approaches to medical education. Our model of student development pulled heavily from novice to expert schemas of professional development [20]. Given the emphasis on training trainers, the curriculum particularly pulled from social learning theory (role performance in situ) and cognitive learning theory (educational theory lectures and discussions) [21, 22].

The effectiveness of the EMED Course was evaluated through a multi-pronged end-of-course exam and a self-efficcacy survey. Additionally, an assessment of cohort students’ teaching skills was conducted in a subsequent Advanced Emergency Trainer (AET) Course.

Study setting

The program was implemented at Koidu Government Hospital (KGH), a rural district hospital in Kono, Sierra Leone that treats medical, surgical, pediatric, and maternity cases. The hospital is managed by the Sierra Leone Ministry of Health and Sanitation (MOHS) with support from Partners In Health (PIH), a Boston-based non-governmental organization (NGO). Acute care is provided in a seven-bed emergency room staffed primarily by nurses and CHOs, with supervision from specialist consultants (residency-trained physicians). CHOs are mid-level providers who have completed a three-to-four-year diploma in general clinical medicine. They are trained primarily to manage peripheral/rural health units, although some work in hospitals; their training focuses on diagnosing and treating commonly encountered diseases under the supervision of physicians [23].

Participant eligibility

Prior to this study, two emergency care training courses of eight and twelve-weeks’ duration had been conducted at KGH, graduating seven CHOs, four clinical officers, two medical officers, and six nurses (Fig. 1) These graduates formed the potential participant pool. Since clinical officer and medical officer staff frequently turn over, we limited the EMED cohort to nurses and CHOs to better propagate education in the long-term. Potential participants completed an assessment (described below) to verify their baseline knowledge and determine which candidates were qualified to be trained as educators. The aim was to select a cohort of approximately five nurses and CHOs.

Preliminary multidimensional assessment

The assessment took place from August-September 2023 (Fig. 1). Enrollment was opened voluntarily to graduates of the previous emergency curricula. Five nurses and five CHOs chose to participate. Core competencies were adapted from the framework of the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) [24]. Patient Care (PC), Medical Knowledge (MK), Professionalism (P), and Communication Skills (CS) were included, as these domains were relevant to the educator role for the selected cohort and setting. Systems Based Practice (SBP) and Practice Based Learning and Improvement (PBLI) were excluded as these competencies were beyond the scope of the cohort’s previous education and clinical experience.

We evaluated these competencies multidimensionally, using direct clinical observation, multiple-choice tests, and simulation assessments. We a priori decided that if most students performed poorly in any topics, we would conduct remedial clinical education early in the EMED curriculum.

Direct clinical observation (CS, MK, PC, P)

Each student was directly observed practicing clinically in the emergency ward for half of a shift (four hours) by a consultant physician. An assessment rubric was created, assessing aspects of patient care, medical knowledge, professionalism, and communication skills. Students were scored on a one-to-five scale, with one indicating that the student needed improvement in competency, three indicating average performance relative to the student’s role, and five indicating excellent performance relative to the student’s role.

Multiple choice test (MK)

Two multiple-choice tests were created, one targeted at nurses and the other at CHOs. The content was based on the curriculum of the previous emergency training course, and a passing score was set at 70% or above.

Simulation assessment (PC, MK, CS)

Students were assigned into teamsof one CHO and one nurse. One team consisted of two CHOs and one nurse. Teams underwent an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) simulation evaluation. The OSCE consisted of four clinical cases, covering pneumonia/tuberculosis, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), trauma, and status epilepticus. Cases were chosen based on common syndromes seen in the emergency department. The structure of the simulation assessment was based on research detailed in Boulet (2008). Students were evaluated both holistically and using an “essential action” checklist [25]. By utilizing a summative approach for the simulation assessment, students were able to receive effective feedback of patient care, medical knowledge, and communication skills core competencies.

To pass the multidimensional assessment, participants had to receive at least a satisfactory rating in each of these separate evaluations and in each competency. One nurse did not complete the simulation assessment after performing poorly in the knowledge assessment. One CHO failed the multiple-choice and simulation assessment. Finally, one nurse was unable to proceed with the curriculum due to scheduling conflicts after passing the assessment. The final EMED Educator Cohort was thus established, consisting of four CHOs and three nurses. Certain knowledge deficiencies were noted across the cohort, specifically regarding trauma, and remedial lectures were developed and incorporated into the following curriculum.

Emergency medical educator development (EMED) course

To create the EMED curriculum, we reviewed syllabi of various Master of Education in Health Profession (MEHP) programs, referenced the Friedman et al. (2019) review of Clinician-Educator tracks, and pulled from with the BEC Training of Trainers (ToT) and other published ToT courses [16, 26–28]. Curriculum drafts were edited with feedback from local leadership and medical educators with clinical and educational experience in LMICs.

Localizing our curriculum and addressing the specific needs of our cohort and setting necessitated incorporating a variety of learning theories and approaches [21, 22]. The final EMED curriculum pulled from behavioral, cognitive, social learning, and constructivist learning theories, with social learning the most impactful given key curricular strategies of role modeling, collaborative learning, and teaching with case studies. The developed curriculum covered essential aspects of medical education including learning theory, clinical precepting, the one-minute-preceptor model, effective feedback, instructional methods, morbidity and mortality conferences, and assessment [29–31]. The seminar schedule is outlined in Fig. 2. Emphasis was placed on subjects that directly linked to practice. Given the time constraints of the course and the current level of the students, we omitted modules that discussed curriculum development and conducting simulations.

6-week Emergency Medical Educator Development (EMED) Course Schedule

Training in teaching pedagogy and skills-based teaching were emphasized, based on previous research [27]. We pursued a blended approach based upon established practices of ToT programs [28]. Students attended a weekly seminar featuring lectures on educational topics. In addition, students worked with faculty to develop an hour-long lecture on a selected medical topic using a flipped classroom approach [32]. They then presented the lecture to the class, receiving feedback from peers and the instructor. Instructors evaluated students’ lecture presentations via rubric on a 5-point scale in organization, use of visuals, personal presentation, and content. Students were additionally scheduled for a four-hour supervised teaching shift in the emergency ward, where they were expected to precept lower-level providers and present a short, focused clinical lecture to learners in the clinical setting.

The EMED Course was conducted over 6 weeks in January/February 2024, with the EMED Education Cohort of four CHOs and three nurses. Course activities occurred one day a week and utilized multiple teaching modalities (Fig. 2).

EMED End-of-Course assessment

At the end of the course, knowledge assessment was performed using a thirty-point multiple choice exam covering education topics. Rubric-based assessments were used to evaluate the clinical teaching shift and the mentored lecture components throughout the course.

Students also completed a self-efficacy assessment immediately following the course, which we adapted from a previously validated self-efficacy assessment tool to assess medical communication skills [33]. The assessment consisted of two scenarios, each with four questions. The first scenario covered student confidence in a lecture setting. The second addressed student confidence in a clinical teaching setting. The survey can be found in the Supplementary Materials section.

Advanced emergency training (AET) course

The final stage of our project was to integrate the EMED Education Cohort into the ongoing emergency care training program (Fig. 1). In May/June 2024, an 8-week Advanced Emergency Training (AET) Course aimed at CHOs and a separate 8-week course aimed at nurses were conducted. As part of these courses, EMED students were assigned a lecture topic overlapping with their mentored lecture from the EMED Course. Nurses taught within the nursing curriculum, and CHOs within the CHO curriculum. Four of the CHOs and two of the nurses from the EMED Education Cohort participated in this stage; one nurse was unable to complete the presentation due to scheduling conflicts.

At the end of each day of the AET course, students assessed each of their lecturers using an original six-question Likert-scale survey with two questions for subjective feedback. This survey can be found in Supplemental Materials. In total, four consultant lecturers were evaluated as part of the CHO course, alongside four CHO lecturers from the EMED Education Cohort. Two trained nurse educators were evaluated as part of the nurse training course, as well as the two nurses from the EMED Education Cohort. Average survey scores were obtained for each lecturer.

Passing students from the AET curricula were given the same self-efficacy survey as the EMED Education Cohort. This group of passing emergency care trained students served as the baseline group for the self-efficacy scale.

Data collection

All data and evaluations, including the lecturer evaluations, were initially collected on paper and subsequently transcribed into Excel. For assessments involving direct clinical observation or simulation, each assessor was provided with one score sheet per participant. All multiple-choice exams were completed by participants on paper in a supervised setting.

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was performed on SPSS. Descriptive data were reported as percentages or averages. Comparisons were performed through the independent t-test. Justification of this choice may be found in the work of Sullivan, which argues that parametric tests are appropriate for Likert-scale data [34].

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Partners in Health Sierra Leone IRB. Exemption was received from the University of Chicago IRB.

Results

Pre-Course Multidimensional Assessment Results

Results for the multidimensional assessment conducted on a cohort of five CHOs and five nurses considered candidates for the EMED Course are presented in Table1. Four out of five nurses passed the multiple-choice assessment, with an average passing score of 76% and a cutoff score of 70%. One nurse did not pass, with a score of 59%. Analysis of the test questions noted low scores in trauma, CPR, and shortness of breath. All five CHOs passed the knowledge assessment, with an average score of 85%. Scores were lowest in questions related to trauma, CPR, and shock (Table1).

Direct clinical observation of the four passing nurses demonstrated strong scores in communication skills and professionalism, and relative weaknesses in patient care and nursing care competencies. Teaching skills were not adequately assessed during direct observation and were excluded. Notable deficiencies among the nurse cohort were related to nursing re-evaluation, independent intervention, and the titration of infusion medications.

CHO performance was more uniform across competencies, with relative weaknesses in Medical Knowledge (3.75 out of 5) and Patient Care (3.88 out of 5). Performance was strongest in Professionalism (4.20 of 5). Deficiencies in patient care were most prominent in the areas of re-evaluation and initiating interventions. Medical knowledge deficiencies were noted in students’ abilities to create broad, prioritized differentials and complete, independent plans. There was a deficiency in sign-out communication in both cohorts.

All groups performed well on the simulation cases related to DKA and status epilepticus. Performance in the trauma case was poor, with two out of four groups failing. These results correlated strongly with the knowledge exam.

EMED End-of-Course Assessment

At the conclusion of the EMED course, the average score for students on the multiple-choice knowledge assessment was 77%, with all but one student passing the threshold of 70%.

On the supervised lectures, students performed well at organization (avg: 4.14), use of visuals (avg: 4.57), and personal presentation (avg score: 4.29), but needed improvement related to content and clarity (avg: 3.41).

On clinical teaching shifts, evaluation showed adequate to good performance. Rubric evaluation showed students successfully implemented the one-minute preceptor model. Feedback was generally noted to be clear and respectful, with one exception. Students appeared to struggle with giving actionable and specific feedback. Students also demonstrated poorer performance at giving focused clinical lectures and “teaching a skill” relative to other proficiencies.

Overall, six of the seven students passed the combined assessment, and were considered graduates of the EMED Course.

EMED self-efficacy assessment

Compared to the baseline group, the EMED Educator Cohort reported significantly higher total self-efficacy (MD = 1.64, p< .001), lecture self-efficacy (MD 1.55, p <.001) and clinical teaching self-efficacy (MD 1.73, p < .001), with the greatest difference in clinical teaching self-efficacy (Fig. 3).

Independent t-test analysis of this data demonstrated a significant difference in the mean rating between the EMED Educator Cohort and their consultant counterparts (p < .001). The mean score for outside lecturers was 4.67 (SD .131), whereas the mean score for the EMED Educator Cohort was 4.03 (SD .238).

Advanced Emergency Training (AET) Course Assessment

Feedback forms at the conclusion of the AET course indicated that the course was well-received (average rating 8.63/9). Despite the lower average teaching ratings for the EMED Educator Cohort, these instructors were received positively by students, with students indicating they believed their inclusion contributed positively to their learning. (+1.71, on a scale from -4 to 4) Subjectively, students noted improved confidence when dealing with critically ill patients, an improved ability to systematically assess patients, and strong relevance to their clinical practice. In short-form feedback, students requested more bedside lectures, practical application components, and simulations.

Discussion

Our program aimed to develop emergency care educators in a rural district hospital in Sierra Leone. Our results show the EMED program trained a cohort of mid-level providers and nurses into effective educators and successfully integrated them as teachers into an existing emergency care training program. Our data revealed significantly higher total self-efficacy, lecture self-efficacy, and clinical self-efficacy scores among the EMED Educator Cohort compared to the baseline group, and noted good student feedback for students’ lecturer performance. The EMED program marks progress towards the eventual transition of leadership in an emergency care curriculum to trained local providers.

Based on the existing literature, many training-of-trainers programs focus on smaller-scale interventions, where participants are taught to deliver specific skills or curricula using pre-developed materials [16]. Our results suggest a way to extend the scope of these programs by developing participant teaching skills to support broader locally led medical education. This is aligned with current efforts to decolonize global health and medical education which emphasize the importance of training local educators who can develop independent, locally led, and context-specific educational programs [35, 36]. While instructors from our cohort still required substantial support in their teaching roles, our initial outcomes are promising. Furthermore, students in the AET course found the introduction of local lecturers beneficial, indicating a desire for locally led education.

Our program's multi-stage design was crucial to its success as it allowed the curriculum to be tailored to local needs. Barteit et al. (2019) emphasized that the successful implementation of medical education interventions, particularly in low-resource countries, pivots on the ability of implementers to integrate the intervention into the local educational setting, including by using an initial comprehensive needs assessment [37]. Our multidimensional assessment identified specific deficiencies in medical knowledge and patient care, which were then addressed by the student’s mentored lectures. Direct observation also provided insight into local communication patterns and common clinical errors. Although this extended the program timeline, these steps allowed the education team to familiarize themselves with local needs and educational strengths and weaknesses, a process we strongly recommend for future similar programs. A single consistent course director’s presence throughout each phase helped with cohort engagement, program development, and ongoing student support.

Student short-answer feedback also highlighted the value of direct 1:1 supervision in lecturing and clinical teaching, particularly in overcoming technological and social barriers. Out of all the students, only one had consistent access to a computer; others were able to access the internet only through their phones using pay-as-you-go data plans. Similar limitations can be seen in other settings [38, 39]. Given these limitations, students had very little experience in PowerPoint presentations, research analysis, or lecture development. In response, we emphasized direct 1:1 mentoring in both operating technology and developing teaching materials, which was well-received based on class feedback. Tailored 1:1 mentorship is recognized as a core component of many medical education models as a tool not only for developing professional competencies but also for building community and influencing personal development [40, 41]. Various subsequent benefits may be observed, such as increased workforce retention [42]. Future programs should utilize similar mentorship combined with physical resources when confronting technological and social barriers.

While at first glance the performance gap between our cohort and the consultants may be concerning, this finding was expected given the discrepancy in the years of former schooling and higher education between the two groups. Healthcare educators in high-income countries undergo years of formal education and training that cannot be replicated in a 6-week intervention [43]. Despite the score differences between international lecturers and the EMED Education Cohort, the EMED Education Cohort received ratings correlating to “very good” from their students - a satisfactory peer rating and promising outcome given the gross differences in preceding education.

Future efforts should note that there was substantial heterogeneity within the EMED Education Cohort. Results of assessments concerning clinical teaching and lecturing were often disparate, most noticeably between nurses and CHOs. In supervised settings, nurses were much more comfortable and competent with clinical teaching than CHOs, whereas the opposite was true for lecturing. These results likely reflect the prior experience in each cohort; nurses at KGH often teach nursing students in the clinical setting and are trained primarily clinically. Conversely, CHOs have a more extensive background in lecture-based education and have higher technological familiarity, but do not often occupy a clinical teaching role. Although advancement of each cohort in both lecturing and clinical teaching is ideal, individuation of the cohort and adaptation of their roles to specific strengths may be beneficial for future programs.

Limitations

First, the research was conducted at a single site which, while representative of a low-resourced hospital in a LMIC undergoing educational development, may limit the generalizability of the results. While we hypothesize that the findings could be applicable to similar settings, further studies are needed to test whether these results are transferable, including across other medical specialties.

Second, the study sample size was small. Although statistically significant differences were observed between our cohort and a baseline group, replicating this study in other settings, with larger and more diverse cohorts, would help to validate our findings and strengthen our conclusions.

A third limitation is the longitudinal nature of educational development. This study focused on short-term outcomes, but the long-term impact of the intervention remains unknown. Educational development is often a slow and ongoing process, requiring sustained attention to ensure that students continue to develop their teaching skills and retain their knowledge. Without ongoing opportunities for teaching practice and engagement, skills and interest deteriorate over time. Continued mentorship, support, and structural interventions will be essential to ensure the sustainability of this educational program.

Finally, due to time constraints, the scope of the Emergency Medicine Education Development (EMED) program was limited, and deliberately did not include subjects such as curriculum development, simulation, and assessment. These aspects, though critical for fully developed independent medical educators, were beyond the scope of the current program. Future interventions should expand to include these elements to better prepare students for independent teaching.

Conclusions

Our program successfully trained a cohort of mid-level providers and nurses, marking the first step towards a long-term goal of transitioning leadership of an emergency medicine curriculum to locally trained staff. The results demonstrate significant improvements in teaching self-efficacy and strong teacher ratings among our cohort. In healthcare settings without pre-existing standardized training pathways, successful development, expansion and dissemination of this category of clinician-educator programs has the potential to elevate the level of provider training in a financially feasible, contextually adaptive and longitudinal manner. Next steps at KGH will focus on continuing educator development among the cohort, with incremental advancement in responsibility and independence, and easing of direct supervision and mentorship. Further systemized teaching on conducting simulations, creating assessments, and developing curricula have been proposed and may be implemented in the future. Similar programs and interventions should be considered and evaluated in other settings with limited doctors, technology, and academic resources to determine the feasibility of program implementation in other low-resource settings.
