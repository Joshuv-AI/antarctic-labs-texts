Prevalence and drug resistance profiles of nontuberculous mycobacteria isolated from Iranian fish pond waters
Authors: Xiaoqian Cui, Yu Zhang, Yancui Cai, Sale Zhang, Ying Li, Haifang Wang, Fatemeh Sakhaee, Seyed Davar Siadat, Abolfazl Fateh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infection is considered one of the most severe waterborne diseases. NTMs are widespread in fish pond water, which may serve as a significant exposure pathway for NTM infections, raising substantial concerns for public health. Consequently, the purpose of this study was to investigate the presence of NTM and determine their drug resistance in 120 fish pond waters (FPW). Totally, 120 water samples were gathered from FPW. After being filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, the samples were decontaminated with 0.005% cetylpyridinium chloride and subsequently cultured on two Lowenstein-Jensen media. The cultures were incubated at 25 °C and 37 °C for a period of 8 weeks. The identification of different NTM species was conducted using molecular tests targeting the hsp65, rpoB, and 16 S rDNA genes. The identification of NTM isolates included several species: M. marinum, M. chelonae, M. fortuitum, M. mucogenicum, M. lentiflavum, M. abscessus, M. gordonae, M. porcinum, M. kansasii, M. conceptionense, M. simiae, M. phocaicum, M. xenopi, and M. neoaurum. The NTM counts across the 110 water samples ranged from 10 to over 1,000 CFU/1,000 mL, with a median of 400 CFU/1000 mL. Furthermore, we documented two cases of skin infections attributed to M. marinum and M. chelonae, with the source of infection traced back to NTM-contaminated FPW. In conclusions, the study revealed that a significant proportion of FPWs were contaminated with NTM, presenting a potential health risk to humans. This study highlights the critical importance of implementing rigorous water quality control measures in fish breeding ponds, with a particular focus on ensuring the absence of NTM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20286-1.Keywords: Nontuberculous mycobacteria, Fish pond waters, CFUSubject terms: Microbiology, Antimicrobials, Bacteriology, Clinical microbiology

Introduction

In contrast to the Mycobacterium tuberculous complex, such as M. tuberculosis or M. leprae, which possess exclusively human or animal reservoirs, the atypical or nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) species are frequently located in a wide variety of aquatic environments, encompassing freshwater, brackish, and marine ecosystems. Exposure to NTM species through contact, inhalation, or ingestion of contaminated water can lead to a variety of diseases affecting the skeletal system, lymph nodes, skin and soft tissues, as well as the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts1,2.

Since NTM naturally reside in soil and water, specific environmental conditions may promote their abundance, increasing exposure and the risk of infection. Previous research has identified several environmental factors linked to NTM, such as evapotranspiration and geochemical soil properties. Additionally, recent studies have highlighted strong and consistent associations between trace metals in environmental water sources and a heightened risk of NTM infection3–5. NTM infections have become one of the most severe waterborne diseases globally, with the estimated number of deaths and associated healthcare costs surpassing those of other waterborne illnesses6. However, from 1996 to 2020, there has been a notable rise in publications addressing NTM-related issues, particularly concerning ‘drinking water,’ ‘water supply,’ ‘water distribution,’ and ‘water use’. Various species of waterborne NTM are widespread throughout drinking water treatment systems, distribution networks, premise plumbing, and point-of-use devices. Despite this, NTM are not routinely monitored, and limited data exist to clarify transmission routes and infection sources7–10.

The frequent detection of pathogenic NTM species, such as M. fortuitum, M. avium, M. lentiflavum, M. intracellulare, and M. gordonae in water supply systems suggests that water may serve as a potential exposure route for NTM infections. These bacteria are resistant to water treatment processes and can proliferate in distribution systems and premise plumbing. However, the behavior and fate of NTM within water supply systems, from the source to the point of use, remain unclear due to insufficient monitoring data10.

Fish mycobacteriosis is a chronic, progressive illness induced by NTM. NTMs are classified into two groups: slowly growing (SGM) and rapidly growing mycobacteria (RGM). M. marinum belongs to the slowly growing category. M. marinum, M. fortuitum, and M. chelonae are the most commonly identified NTM species linked to fish mycobacteriosis. Piscine mycobacteriosis is a widespread disease affecting over 200 species of marine, brackish, and freshwater fish, with its impact observed across a wide geographical range from subarctic to tropical regions. This disease also affects tropical aquarium fish and is regarded as a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in free-living fish populations11,12.

The zoonotic potential and significant impact of mycobacteriosis on fish breeders underscore the importance of characterizing the diversity of mycobacteria present in fish pond water (FPW). Although studies have investigated the presence of mycobacteria in aquariums, there is no research discussing their occurrence in FPW in Iran. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of NTM in FPW and assess the drug resistance patterns of the identified isolates.

Materials and methods

Samples collection and culture

This study was conducted in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulations, following approval by the Pasteur Institute of Iran Ethics Committee (IR.PII.REC.1402.027). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

A total of 120 fish pond water samples were collected between June and October 2023. This sample set comprised 86 fish breeding ponds and 34 home ponds containing fish. The numbers reflected the relative availability of each pond type in the study area. Ponds were chosen using random sampling from among those accessible and whose owners consented to participate, to ensure both representativeness and feasibility of sampling.

All samples were processed and decontaminated according to the procedures previously described2. In brief, one liter of water was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter (Millipore, Bedford, USA). The filtered samples were subsequently decontaminated with 0.005% cetylpyridinium chloride for 30 min at room temperature. The membranes were then washed with sterile distilled water to remove any remaining decontaminants. Afterward, they were placed on two Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) media and incubated at 25 °C and 37 °C for duration of 8 weeks. The phenotypic tests for evaluating NTM isolates were macroscopic and microscopic morphological features, growth rate, niacin production, nitrate reduction, Tween-80 hydrolysis, tellurite reduction, urease production, salt tolerance, arylsulfatase activity, and semi-quantitative catalase production, following the standard protocols outlined by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) procedures13.

Assigning isolates to NTM based on molecular analysis

Bacterial DNA extraction was carried out using the PREP-NA-S DNA/RNA Extraction Kit (DNA Technologies Ltd, Russian Federation).

NTM species were detected using three housekeeping genes: rpoB, 16 S rDNA, and heat shock protein-65 (hsp65). Amplification of a 441-bp fragment of the hsp65 gene was accomplished with the primers TB11 (5’-ACCAACGATGGTGTGTCCAT-3’) and TB12 (5’-CTTGTCGAACCGCATACCCT-3’). The resulting PCR products were then sequenced for further analysis14–16.

For amplification of a specific 750-bp segment for rpoB gene, we used two sets of primers: F-(5’-GGCAAGGTCACCCCGAAGGG-3’) and R-(5’-AGCGGCTGCTGGGTGATCATC-3’). Two primers pA (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and pI (5’-TGCACACAGGCCACAAGGGA-3’) were used to amplify 16 S rDNA gene, as previously reported16.

For the interpretation of sequencing results, the obtained gene sequences were aligned with reference sequences from the NCBI GenBank database, revealing a sequence similarity of 99–100%.

Drug susceptibility test

The broth microdilution method was used to determine each drug’s minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs), with interpretation based on the guidelines provided by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)17. The antibiotics examined in this study included amikacin, capreomycin, ciprofloxacin, vancomycin, ethionamide, isoniazid, ofloxacin, cefoxitin, ethambutol, levofloxacin, clarithromycin, imipenem, doxycycline, minocycline, rifampicin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

Two-fold serial dilutions of the antibiotics were prepared in the range of 0.06 to 512 mg/L. The NTMs were cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 supplement enriched with 5% albumin-dextrose-catalase under aerobic conditions at 25 °C and 37 °C after inoculation. The breakpoints for susceptibility, intermediate susceptibility, and resistance were established following the recommendations of the CLSI.

Results

Biochemical and molecular tests for detection of NTM species

Of the 120 water samples analyzed, 110 (91.6%) were confirmed to contain NTM species, while 9 (7.5%) tested negative. Additionally, 4 (3.3%) samples were deemed contaminated and could not be assessed. The isolated NTM species were classified based on their growth rates into slow-growing mycobacteria (SGM) and rapid-growing mycobacteria (RGM). Among the 110 isolates, 48 (43.6%) were identified as SGM, while 62 (56.4%) were categorized as RGM. Additionally, 22 (20.0%) of the isolates exhibited scotochromogenic characteristics, and 28 (25.5%) were photochromogenic. Additionally, 58 (52.7%) and 52 (47.3%) of the strains thrived at 25 °C and 37 °C, respectively. The predominant species identified through biochemical testing included Mycobacterium marinum (16 isolates), M. chelonae (12 isolates), M. fortuitum (11 isolates), M. abscessus (9 isolates), M. gordonae (7 isolates), M. simiae (6 isolates), and M. kansasii (6 isolates). Among the 110 isolates analyzed, only 67 (60.9%) were successfully identified using biochemical methods.

Colony-forming units (CFU)

The CFU were quantified in all samples infected with various NTM strains. NTM concentrations ranged from 10 to over 1000 CFU per 1000 mL across the 110 samples, with a median value of 400 CFU per 1000 mL. Notably, the CFU count was higher in FPW from home water ponds containing fish compared to those from fish breeding ponds (Supplementary Table 1).

Introduction of two patients with skin infections caused by M. marinum and M. chelonae

Case 1

A 51-year-old man, who was a fish farmers and had direct contact with fish farming ponds, reported an asymptomatic lesion on his hand and fingers (Fig. 1A). The lesion initially appeared as small papules, which later progressed to scaling. The patient did not report any other skin or mucosal lesions, lymphadenopathy, changes in sensation, paresthesia, or itching. There were no associated systemic symptoms. After visiting the doctor, the patient was initially prescribed topical corticosteroids, but there was no evidence of improvement. The results of clinical parameters were normal. A sample from the patient was sent to our center by the attending physician for further analysis. After processing the sample and preparing the smear, the tissue was cultured on LJ medium. The result of smear staining was negative for acid fast bacilli. After 4 weeks of incubation, smooth or medium-sized white or beige colonies developed on the LJ medium. Upon exposure to light, these colonies turned yellow to orange in color. Molecular analysis confirmed that the ulceration in the patient was caused by M. marinum. Following the diagnosis, the patient was treated with clarithromycin and amikacin for three months, leading to significant regression of the lesion.

(A) The hand lesion caused by M. marinum infection; (B) the right leg lesion caused by M. chelonae.

Case2

A 45-year-old woman employed at one of the fish breeding ponds studied in this research presented to the doctor with a complaint of a lesion on her right leg (Fig. 1B). The lesion appeared red with a central white area. Initially, the doctor prescribed topical treatments, including zinc ointment and corticosteroids; however, these proved ineffective. All clinical laboratory parameters were within normal limits, except for an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate. After collecting samples from the lesion and pus, they were sent to our laboratory for analysis. The smear staining yielded a positive result for mycobacterial infection. After five days of incubation at 25 °C, the culture on LJ media confirmed the presence of mycobacteria. Molecular testing subsequently identified the isolate as M. chelonae. After treatment with clarithromycin and ciprofloxacin, the small lesion became pale and scaly, leading to a successful recovery.

Discussion

Several studies have shown that aquaculture organisms can serve as sources of bacterial infections in humans, with Mycobacterium species being particularly significant18,19. In this study, a substantial portion of FPW samples were contaminated with NTM, including M. marinum, M. chelonae, M. fortuitum, M. mucogenicum, M. lentiflavum, M. abscessus, M. gordonae, M. porcinum, M. kansasii, M. conceptionense, M. simiae, M. phocaicum, M. xenopi, and M. neoaurum. To our knowledge, no previous studies in Iran have investigated NTMs in FPW; earlier research focused mainly on ornamental aquarium fish. For example, M. fortuitum was reported as the predominant species in ornamental aquaria, and another study of 50 aquarium water samples identified 13 NTM species, with M. marinum and M. fortuitum most frequently detected20,21. The wider variety of species observed in our study likely reflects the larger sample size and the different types of water reservoirs examined.

NTM species, including M. avium, M. fortuitum, M. chelonae, M. kansasii, M. gordonae, and M. xenopi, are commonly found in natural and man-made water systems. Early identification methods, such as hybridization probes and multiplex 16 S rRNA PCR, initially classified some household and potable water isolates as M. intracellulare, but subsequent reanalysis revealed them to be M. chimaera, now widely recognized in water biofilms22,23.

NTM distribution in aquatic environments—including marine, freshwater, and drinking water systems—is influenced by environmental factors such as temperature, pH, and organic matter. Marine species, such as M. marinum and M. abscessus, tolerate brackish and high-salinity waters, while freshwater species like M. avium, M. intracellulare, and M. gordonae are common in reservoirs and distribution systems. Biofilms, elevated water temperatures, and low chlorine residuals further enhance NTM persistence24–26.

Thermal waters, such as natural hot springs, can harbor thermophilic NTM species like M. thermoresistibile, posing potential exposure risks through recreational activities. NTM can also survive wastewater and industrial water systems, contaminating effluents and re-entering natural water bodies. Aerosolization from contaminated sources further increases inhalation risks, particularly in individuals with underlying lung conditions27.

The presence of NTM in aquatic environments has important public health implications, including waterborne pulmonary infections, skin and soft tissue infections, and outbreaks in healthcare and recreational settings. Understanding their environmental reservoirs and persistence is essential for effective water quality management and minimizing exposure28.

Several Mycobacterium species have been reported in sediments from fish breeding pools in Iran, including some RGM from soil and water sources29. Contamination of water by Mycobacterium in fish ponds—especially those with muddy rims and floors—poses a potential public health risk to individuals in contact with the water or fish, including fish workers, fishermen, aquarium owners, and household members20.

In this study, M. marinum was the predominant species in FPW. Previous studies in Iran have also reported M. marinum in environmental samples, with prevalence rates of 7.2% in soil from Mazandaran, 1.6% in breeding pools from Gilan, and 1.2% in soil from Golestan Province21,30,31.

In this study, two patients presented with skin infections caused by M. marinum and M. chelonae. Molecular analyses of FPW samples also detected these species. Although the same species were identified in both water and patient samples, no strain-level typing was performed, so direct attribution of infections to these water sources remains speculative. Historically, the most frequently reported Mycobacterium species in fish are M. marinum, M. fortuitum, and M. chelonae, which are commonly associated with fish infections and can pose zoonotic risks21.

Piscine mycobacteriosis caused by M. marinum and M. chelonae can adversely affect fish in freshwater, marine, and brackish environments and poses notable zoonotic risks. This disease threatens both fish populations and human health, particularly for individuals in contact with infected fish or contaminated water, such as fish breeders, fishermen, and aquarium owners. These risks highlight the need for improved surveillance and control measures in aquatic environments to reduce potential transmission between fish and humans11.

Several studies worldwide have reported diverse Mycobacterium species in fish pond water and ornamental fish, highlighting regional differences in prevalence. In India, Shukla et al. found NTMs in 25% of 60 aquarium fish samples, with M. gordonae and M. abscessus most frequently isolated32. In Sweden, Hongslo et al. detected NTMs in 23% of 120 aquarium fish samples, primarily M. marinum, M. chelonae, and M. gordonae33. In the Czech Republic, Beran et al. identified NTMs in 75.4% of 65 samples, with M. fortuitum and M. flavescens predominating34. Variability in NTM prevalence may reflect differences in fish suppliers, cleaning and filtration practices, handling protocols, and the susceptibility of different fish species35. Many species of fish have been documented to suffer from mycobacterial infections; however, certain species seem to exhibit greater susceptibility, leading to a higher incidence of these infections19.

In our study, NTM concentrations in FPW ranged from 10 to over 1000 CFU per 1000 mL across 110 samples, with a median of 400 CFU per 1000 mL. CFU counts were higher in home water ponds than in fish breeding ponds. These differences are likely influenced by environmental and management factors, including nutrient availability, organic matter, water quality (temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen), microbial community diversity, and pond maintenance practices22,24–28. FPW typically have higher nutrient levels and diverse microbial communities due to fish waste, whereas home ponds experience lower nutrient loads and periodic water replacement. Water source and replenishment frequency may also contribute to observed variations in NTM concentrations24. Although many mycobacteria are considered highly infectious, data on the minimum infectious dose for NTM are limited. For example, 1–10 CFU of M. bovis can cause tuberculosis in cattle, but no established infectious dose exists for NTM, and virulence likely varies by species. Nonetheless, fewer than 10 CFU of M. abscessus from contaminated water has been associated with soft tissue infections in individuals receiving mesotherapy or subcutaneous injections2,36,37.

In this study, RGM and SGM showed varying levels of drug resistance, highlighting the need to distinguish between these groups when selecting treatment strategies. Among SGM, M. marinum and M. kansasii were the most susceptible, whereas M. simiae and M. lentiflavum exhibited broader resistance. Among RGM, M. abscessus and M. chelonae displayed the highest resistance. Assessing drug resistance in environmental NTM strains is important for guiding species-specific treatment approaches, informing public health policies, and improving management of potential infections2.

Drug susceptibility testing is essential for clinical isolates, but data on resistance genes in environmental NTM remain limited. Species such as M. chelonae and M. abscessus exhibit intrinsic resistance to many antibiotics. Horizontal gene transfer, via plasmids and prophages, can spread virulence and resistance genes between NTM species, complicating treatment and highlighting the need for further research on environmental isolates38. For example, genomic analyses of M. abscessus have identified virulence genes likely acquired from pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Burkholderia cenocepacia39,40demonstrating its adaptability and the complex interactions between environmental and clinical bacteria.

This study has several limitations. Sampling was conducted from June to October 2023 and may not reflect seasonal variations in NTM prevalence and species composition throughout the year in Iran, potentially affecting the overall representation of species in FPW. Additionally, the mechanisms underlying antimicrobial resistance in environmental NTM were not investigated, limiting understanding of how resistance develops and spreads. Further research is needed to address these gaps and provide a more comprehensive analysis.

In conclusion, this study revealed that the majority of FPW samples contained high microbial loads of NTM. These findings highlight the importance of implementing routine water quality monitoring and basic hygiene practices to mitigate potential health risks associated with NTM in aquatic environments, particularly for individuals involved in fish farming and handling. While specific disinfection protocols were not evaluated in this study, maintaining strict water quality control measures remains a key precautionary strategy.
