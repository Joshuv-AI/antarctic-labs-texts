A Research Classification for Long COVID: Symptom Frequency and Severity Improve Accuracy of Machine Learning Models
Authors: Leonard A. Jason, Lauren Ruesink, Jacob Furst, Ben Z. Katz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Long COVID definitions based solely on symptom occurrence may reduce diagnostic specificity.Machine‐learning models incorporating symptom frequency and severity outperformed occurrence‐only models.Composite scoring achieved 90.12% accuracy compared with 88.73% for occurrence‐based scoring.Composite models required fewer predictive symptoms, indicating greater efficiency.Measuring symptom burden improves the precision of research classification for Long COVID.

A critical step in advancing research on Long COVID is the establishment of a reliable and valid case definition; however, to date, numerous competing definitions have been proposed, complicating the identification and comparison of affected individuals versus controls across studies. As an example, according to the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine [1], Long COVID is diagnosed following a confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and requires the presence of at least one symptom from a list of over 200, persisting for a minimum of 3 months post‐infection. While this broad definition is inclusive and captures a substantial proportion of individuals affected by the condition, it does not account for symptom severity and may result in the misclassification of individuals who do not, in fact, meet a clinically meaningful threshold for illness [2].

An alternative method using machine learning was employed by Thaweethai et al. [3] to identify symptoms that most effectively distinguished individuals with Long COVID from uninfected, healthy controls. Their model assigned scores from 1 to 8 across eight key symptoms, with a total score exceeding 12 indicating a probable case. Yet, among 2231 participants first infected on or after December 1, 2021, and enrolled within 30 days of infection, 10% met the criteria for Long COVID after 6 months, but 4.6% of uninfected controls were also classified as positive. This relatively high rate of false positives among the control group raises concerns about the specificity of their symptom‐based case definition, particularly given its basis solely on symptom occurrence.

The integration of both symptom frequency and severity may yield greater specificity and diagnostic utility. Such an approach has been incorporated into the diagnostic criteria for Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS) as defined by the Institute of Medicine (2015). Applying similar thresholds in Long COVID research has proven useful for characterizing patients and tracking illness trajectories [4].

The present study seeks to evaluate the comparative efficacy of two case classification approaches for Long COVID: one relying solely on binary symptom presence (i.e., whether or not a symptom occurred within the past 6 months), and the other incorporating scale ratings of both frequency and severity. We hypothesized that models incorporating symptom frequency and severity would more accurately distinguish Long COVID cases from controls.

This study included two participant groups: one that self‐described as having Long COVID and a second that consisted of people who were well and were never exposed to COVID‐19, since their data were collected prior to 2020. There were 648 total participants (292 Long COVID and 356 Controls).

The Long COVID group was recruited through social media forums in August 2020. International participants were included in this sample. While originally this group had 299 individuals, 7 were removed from our analyses due to incomplete demographic information. The control group included three subgroups. The first was a sample collected from BioBank (n = 53), which was curated by Solve ME/CFS. The second was a sample of students from DePaul University in Chicago (n = 93). The third and largest group was recruited from first‐time blood donors at a Norwegian outpatient clinic, who used a Norwegian validated translation of our study measures (n = 210).

Participants in each group completed the DePaul Symptom Questionnaire (DSQ) [5] or its Norwegian translation (2022). This is a 54‐item self‐report measure of symptoms related to ME/CFS. This measure has participants record the frequency and severity of symptoms on five‐point Likert scales. Frequency refers to how often a symptom is experienced (0 = symptom experienced none of the time; 1 = symptom experienced a little of the time; 2 = symptom experienced about half of the time; 3 = symptom experienced most of the time; 4 = symptom experienced all of the time). Severity is concerned with the impact of the symptom on daily life (0 = symptom not present; 1 = mild; 2 = moderate; 3 = severe; 4 = very severe). The questionnaire demonstrates strong reliability, sensitivity, specificity, and validity. Ohanian et al. [6] used the DSQ to distinguish ME/CFS from multiple sclerosis based on key symptoms. It was also used to compare symptoms in people with ME/CFS versus people with Long COVID [7].

We wanted to compare the composite with the occurrence outcomes. Therefore, we first used the DSQ to take the sum of the frequency and severity of each symptom, so that symptoms were given composite scores from 0 to 8, which indicated their impact upon an individual. In contrast, binary occurrence scores were calculated for each symptom such that any symptom that was reported to have a frequency of 1 or greater was given an occurrence of 1 (meaning the symptom was present), and any symptom that was reported to have a frequency of 0 was given an occurrence of 0. This occurrence variable reflects the approach of many previous research and Long COVID studies, which tend to identify symptoms in terms of whether they are present, without regard to their frequency or severity (e.g., [1]). Thus, the range of scores for a participant using composite variables was 0–8, whereas the scores for occurrence was either 0 or 1.

For participants who were missing frequency and/or severity of a particular item, but had more than 90% of the DSQ complete, we replaced the missing values with the median frequency and severity of the entire group of controls or cases.

We evaluated whether the occurrence or composite measure of symptoms was better able to distinguish cases of Long COVID from controls using LASSO regression. To develop models, the data were split using leave one out cross validation analysis (LOOCV), where one case out of 648 was left out so that the machine learning model could be trained. Each case was left out of either the composite or occurrence machine learning model's training set once, so that 648 models were produced for each symptom conceptualization. LASSO assigns coefficients for each input variable, the composite or occurrence symptom scores, that are an indicator of their perceived importance to the machine learning model in predicting whether a given individual does or does not have Long COVID. We averaged the coefficients of each symptom variable across the 648 models to finalize a linear model based on composite or occurrence scores for each symptom. These linear models predicted whether someone had Long COVID based on either the occurrence of symptoms or the composite scores of their symptoms, multiplied by the average coefficient of each symptom across the original 648 respective models. The initial LASSO classification model based on composite symptom scores had 26 symptoms with coefficients > 0, and the model based on occurrence symptom scores had 27 coefficients > 0. Coefficients at or below zero were not included because LASSO procedure tends to shrink less important predictors to zero, thus eliminating them from the system, and we chose not to include negative predictors of Long COVID, to make our classification system more inclusive. Since the composite models were scored from 0 to 8 and occurrence models could be scored from 0 to 1, the coefficients of occurrence models tended to be higher.

Once the initial composite and occurrence linear models were created, additional linear models were developed based on a narrower set of symptoms defined by the coefficients of their corresponding original models, to produce simpler predictive algorithms. The final composite model had just 15 variables pared down through LASSO, since all other coefficients were reduced to 0 or below. The final occurrence model had 19 symptoms with coefficients above 0. For the succeeding linear models, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was then used to find the optimal cut‐off score for each model to classify Long COVID cases. We chose the cutoff that would maximize the accuracy of the classifying linear model. The performance metrics of linear models were recorded, including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Lastly, we did a McNemar test to see if there was a significant difference in classification accuracy between the models.

Group comparisons indicated significant differences in sex and age, but not race. The Long COVID group included a higher proportion of females (84%) compared to the control group (69%), χ² (1, N = 648) = 18.35, p < 0.01. Participants with Long COVID were also significantly older (mean = 54.79, SD = 12.03) than the healthy controls (mean = 31.95, SD = 14.92). An independent‐samples t‐test using Welch's correction indicated a large and statistically significant age difference between groups, t (645.82) = 21.57, p < 0.01. In contrast, the racial composition of the two groups was identical, with both samples consisting of 87% White participants, χ² (1, N = 648) = 0.00, p = 1.00. The demographic differences our study noted align with previous research demonstrating that women and older patients are diagnosed more frequently with Long COVID [8, 9].

Table 1 presents the variables from both the composite and occurrence models with coefficients greater than zero. The sizes of coefficients are based on a model's prediction of their importance in classifying Long COVID. The higher the coefficient, the more important a model found it to be for classification. These 15 composite variables and 19 occurrence variables came from LASSO analysis results, where all coefficients greater than 0 were included. Despite using fewer predictors, the composite model significantly outperformed the occurrence model. The composite model achieved 90.12% accuracy, 89.04% sensitivity, and 91.01% specificity, whereas the occurrence model achieved 88.73% accuracy, 87.33% sensitivity, and 89.89% specificity. To compare classification accuracy between the composite and occurrence models, a McNemar test was performed. The test indicated a significant difference in performance, χ² (1) = 4.65, p = 0.03, with the composite model yielding more correct classifications than the occurrence‐based model. Because LOOCV was applied at each modeling stage, additional rounds of model selection were not expected to improve generalizability and were therefore not pursued, in order to reduce the risk of overfitting through repeated optimization on the same dataset.

Our results indicate that symptom frequency and severity contribute meaningfully to the identification of Long COVID. Many symptoms relevant to Long COVID are common in the general population, but they become diagnostically informative when their intensity, persistence, and functional impact are captured. Occurrence‐only approaches necessarily compress this clinically important variation into binary indicators, limiting their discriminative capacity. Composite scoring preserves gradations of symptom burden, allowing classification models to better distinguish pathological symptom patterns from background noise.

Comparison of composite and occurrence scoring approaches revealed both convergence and divergence in the symptom patterns that best distinguished individuals with Long COVID from healthy controls. Across both models, exertion‐related fatigue emerged as a central discriminating feature, indicating a shared core symptom profile. However, the composite model concentrated greater predictive weight on exertion‐sensitive and cognitively fatiguing symptoms and retained a broader representation of sleep‐related disturbances, whereas the occurrence model more frequently elevated broadly endorsed physical symptoms such as weakness, joint pain, and weight change. These differences suggest that composite scoring emphasizes sustained symptom burden, while occurrence scoring is more influenced by symptom prevalence.

These inferences were based on three complementary observations. First, the composite model ranked exertion‐sensitive fatigue items among the strongest predictors, including symptoms reflecting both physical and mental exhaustion following minimal activity. A closely related cognitive‐exertional item also ranked higher in the composite model than in the occurrence model, indicating increased discriminative value when symptom frequency and severity were incorporated.

Second, although sleep‐related symptoms were not among the top‐ranked predictors in either model, the composite approach retained a greater number of sleep disturbance items among the selected predictors. This suggests that composite scoring may better distinguish clinically meaningful sleep disruption by down‐weighting mild or occasional sleep problems that are common in the general population.

Third, the occurrence model more frequently selected diffuse physical symptoms that are commonly reported across a range of conditions. When assessed as binary indicators, such symptoms may appear predictive due to their high prevalence, whereas composite scoring reduces their influence unless they are persistent and severe. Together, these patterns indicate that composite scoring reshapes the classification signal by emphasizing symptom domains characterized by sustained intensity and post‐exertional worsening, rather than simply the presence or absence of individual symptoms.

The present findings also align with recent work emphasizing the value of composite symptom measurement for defining Long COVID [10]. The current study demonstrated that machine‐learning models incorporating symptom frequency and severity achieved high diagnostic accuracy and identified a symptom profile that differed meaningfully from occurrence‐based definitions. Together with the current results, this convergence suggests that composite scoring systems better capture symptom burden, fluctuation, and functional impact.
