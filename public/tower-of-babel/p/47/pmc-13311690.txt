Reducing unnecessary bed days to optimise flow and improve demand-capacity mismatch: learning from a system-wide patient flow collaborative
Authors: ­Kenneth Jun Logrono, Mohamed Salem Khalil, Mohammed Abuqamar, Elizabeth Tamara Biong, Luay Alsmadi, Juliet Jalmasco, Pradeep Radhakrishnan, Khadija Al-Shukaili, Belal Salem Mufadi Zubi, Esmat Swallmeh, Emad Mraweh Mohammed Mustafa, Muna Al-Maslamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDischarge delays and unnecessary bed days (UBDs) are known to cause increasing Emergency Department (ED) boarding and account for over 20% of inappropriate hospital bed use. The aim of this project is to reduce the number of UBDs by 25% (from 1500 days to 1100 days) and increase bed availability at 7 a.m. by 15% (from 0 to 1-2 beds) for medical inpatient beds over a year.MethodsThe implementation of change was facilitated by the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI) Model for Improvement, with iterative testing through PDSA cycles. Quality improvement (QI) tools such as fishbone diagram, Pareto chart, and high-level flow diagrams were used to understand system drivers of delay and prioritise change ideas. Shewhart charts were used to distinguish between common-cause and special-cause variations.ResultsThe reasons for UBD and flow failures were classified as lack of capability, lack of capacity, lack of payment, patient refusal, and delayed planning. Within a year of initiating the QI project, UBDs decreased by 68% (from 1522.7 to 493.4 days), increased the availability of staffed beds at 7 a.m. by 87% (from 1.5 to 2.8 beds) and reduced ED pending admissions by 23% (from 12.1 to 9.3 patients). These improvements temporarily coincided with the implementation of several change ideas—integrated early discharge planning, the use of a CPS tool, and early involvement of social workers.ConclusionSeveral systems and throughput factors, in addition to staff capacity, may contribute to increasing the availability of hospital beds and reducing UBDs. QI methods and tools can rapidly uncover, test, and implement opportunities for improving patient flow from ED to inpatient units.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This QI study shows how a system-wide collaborative approach can implement targeted, multi-level interventions addressing both internal and external factors to improve patient flow from the ED to inpatient units. It highlights that sustained improvements depend on data-driven decisions, iterative testing, multidisciplinary collaboration, external expertise, and active staff engagement to standardise and scale effective practices.

Problem

Patient flow through a complex health system is affected by multiple causative factors. It is clear that prolonged wait times in the Emergency Department (ED) are a result of poor patient flow processes.1 The subsequent delays in services and unavailability of beds have become increasingly prevalent within the healthcare sector.2 ED crowding or boarding is an indicator of poor patient flow1 2 and is associated with increased mortality and longer length of stay (LoS).2 Consequently, most interventions focused only on improving flow within the ED since this is where the problem is most visible.2 3

Boarded patients, on the other hand, are those who have been admitted to the hospital but are forced to stay in the ED because inpatient beds are not available. These patients typically remain in the ED for extended periods, often beyond four hours, while waiting for an available bed. In 2023, an average of 45% of admitted patients were boarded, with an average boarding time of 15–16 hours. The issue arises due to a mismatch between the daily demand for admissions and the available staffed beds, while inpatient areas are routinely operating at 90–95% occupancy. This leads to backlogs in the admission process.

Multiple studies have identified teaching hospitals, trauma centers, and metropolitan healthcare facilities as being more susceptible to hospital congestion and capacity challenges,4 such as the Hamad General Hospital (HGH). Our ED is one of the most crowded facilities in the Middle East and North African (MENA) region, often serving at full capacity.5 HGH, which is in the heart of the city, is considered one of the main healthcare providers in the country, operating an inpatient bed capacity of over 700 staffed beds. Daily attendances often reached as high as 1200-1500 patients daily, which created a busy and highly pressured working environment.5 Bed shortages and pending admissions in the ED, nevertheless, were a persistent concern for the organisation, and these confinements often negatively affect the quality and patient safety.35

Overcrowding in the ED leads to more boarded patients, which results in a reduction in the quality of care provided.24 This domino effect culminates in various adverse outcomes, including medical errors, delayed interventions, longer hospital stays, increased operational costs, higher morbidity and mortality rates, and ambulance diversions.24

Discharge delays and unnecessary bed days (UBDs) were known phenomena to cause the increasing ED boarding.4 6 7 UBDs, or avoidable days, as defined, resulted when patients remained in the hospital for one or more days after meeting medical-readiness criteria for discharge to home or transfer to a community-based setting of care.6 7 The utilisation of different quality improvement (QI) tools, such as the high-level flow diagram, showed that UBDs and delays were apparent in the admission process from ED to the medical inpatient units, as shown in (online supplemental figure 1).

UBDs account for over 20% of inappropriate hospital bed use, implying a waste of resources and increased safety risk for patients and healthcare providers.7 Long-stay patients, on the other hand, spend most of these UBDs. They stay 30 or more days in the hospital from the time they are deemed medically fit for discharge or transfer. The key contributing factors or system drivers contributing to the increasing number of UBDs are often the result of non-medical or complex psychosocial barriers to discharge (online supplemental figure 2), and the specific reasons were quantified using the Pareto Chart in (online supplemental figure 3).

Moreover, patient flow is not only an ED issue but also a system-level concern where the inpatient discharge delays, UBDs, and unnecessary wait times can also contribute to ED boarding.4 6 7 Hence, it is recommended that hospitals develop a systems-based approach to understanding patient flow1 2 4 and utilise a collaborative model to demonstrate sustainable, system-wide patient flow improvements.8

The system-wide patient flow collaborative united the expertise of the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (IHI), the Hamad Healthcare Quality Institute (HHQI), and our clinical teams at HGH. IHI provided advanced improvement knowledge—including the use of Shewhart charts, waste wheels, Pareto analysis, etc.—while HHQI supported the collaboration through resource provision, coordination, and stakeholder communication. Our multidisciplinary team (MDT) contributed to deep systems knowledge and subject matter experience, applying newly acquired improvement skills to test and implement changes effectively.

To ensure capability building and knowledge transfer, the collaborative employed a variety of learning formats, including workshops, webinars, quarterly virtual meetings, and local learning sessions. These venues not only reinforced technical expertise but also created opportunities for our members to share progress, exchange outcomes with other participating facilities, and embed improvement methods into our daily practice. With this, our project set the following aims, which included:

reduce the number of UBDs by 25% (from 1500 days to 1100 days) in the medical inpatient over a year and;

reduce the number of UBDs by 25% (from 1500 days to 1100 days) in the medical inpatient over a year and;

increase inpatient bed capacity (staffed beds available every 7 a.m.) by 15% (from 0 to 1-2 beds) for medical inpatient beds over a year.

increase inpatient bed capacity (staffed beds available every 7 a.m.) by 15% (from 0 to 1-2 beds) for medical inpatient beds over a year.

We proposed to achieve these aims by improving the inpatient throughput process through optimal utilisation of our hospital resources, thereby avoiding the bed costs from unnecessary overstay or avoidable days.

Methods

Context

Our QI initiative was implemented at a large tertiary care hospital in Doha, Qatar, in response to rising patient volumes and a growing number of pending admissions in the ED. To address these challenges, we commissioned an MDT, nominated by each department for their expertise and experience, to join the system-wide patient flow collaborative to collaborate through structured workstreams, meetings, and workshops. With executive sponsorship and protected time, we leveraged QI knowledge and frontline engagement to implement and sustain patient flow improvements across our units and services within HGH.

Medical inpatient department

With rising medical bed demand and frequent bed crises, the Medical Inpatient Department is a key focus due to its role in providing acute care to complex patients. Our department consists of 11 inpatient units, including three acute units, three specialty units, and five general medical units, each with 20-30 beds, and LoS ranges from 3-5 days for acute units and more than five days (>5 days) for general medical units.

Patient flow team

The Medical Patient Flow Department oversees patient movements within and between facilities. The team includes Bed Managers (BM), who allocate beds and coordinate with external facilities, and Case Managers (CM), who facilitate discharges and transfers. While Social Workers (SW) assist by addressing barriers and leveraging resources to resolve risks for delayed discharges or transfers.

Complex Patients

Our study targeted complex patients categorized as long-stay or long-term patients in general medical units. These patients often have complex care needs arising from chronic conditions, social barriers, repatriation requirements, or the need for transfer to other facilities. Long-stay patients were defined as those who remained hospitalized for 30 or more days after being deemed medically fit for discharge or transfer. Long-term patients were defined as adults awaiting bed availability in long-term care facilities, regardless of their LoS. These categories of patients frequently contribute to UBDs by blocking beds needed for new admissions, and the key causes of UBDs include capacity limits, capability gaps, financial issues, patient refusal of transfer, and delayed planning (online supplemental figure 4).

Interventions

The implementation of change was guided by the IHI Model for Improvement, with iterative testing through PDSA cycles, allowing structured testing and refinement of multiple strategies (online supplemental figure 5). Our team tested interventions iteratively and studied their impact over time. Successful changes were scaled for broader application, ensuring continuous improvement. Patients and the public were not involved in this study.

An MDT consisting of executives, administrators, champion physicians, pharmacists, quality experts, clinical nurses, CM, BM, and SW focused on identifying root causes of increasing UBDs. Workflow processes were reviewed through focus group discussions (FGDs), brainstorming, and post-learning sessions. Each root cause was linked to specific interventions with rationale drawn from frontline subject matter experience while using recommendations from IHI and HHQI experts.

Key solutions included flow huddles, bedside rounds, revised screening tools, improved discharge planning, and streamlined communication supported by dashboard tracking of bed capacity and demand. These interventions effectively reduced UBDs and optimised bed utilisation and aligned with the project driver diagram adopted from the IHI (online supplemental figure 6). They were refined and prioritised through collaborative MDT decisions, ensuring feasibility, alignment with clinical priorities, and frontline ownership. This structured approach simplified our workflow operations, eliminated unnecessary steps, and addressed the root causes directly.

Change Idea #1: Complex Patient Screening Tool

Audits into discharge planning revealed delays due to poor execution, including late or non-documented discharge plans and inadequate involvement from SW. To address this, the Complex Patient Screening (CPS) tool was developed to classify patients at risk for complex discharges, such as those requiring support with housing, transportation, or extended caregiver support. Derived from existing checklists and the Blaylock Risk Assessment Tool9 (online supplemental figure 7), the CPS tool identified and reassessed patients within 24–48 hours of admission and weekly thereafter.

Early testing revealed that 70–80% of Intensive Care Unit (ICU) step-down patients were dischargeable without complex interventions, prompting a strategy shift to rescreen these patients on admission to medical inpatient wards. The tool’s scoring system, refined through testing phases, set a cut-off score of six; patients scoring above were referred to SW, ensuring improved discharge planning and minimised delays.

Change Idea #2: Complex Care Team (CCT)

A CCT is an MDT that was formed to identify patients at risk for complex discharges. This included healthcare leaders and executives, clinicians, and CM, who initiated discharge plans within 24–48 hours of patient admission. While effective initially, the team faced sustainability challenges due to time constraints. To address this, daily huddles with department leaders, physician champions, and CM/SW were introduced, enabling proactive planning and minimising discharge delays for complex patients.

Change Idea #3: Early Involvement of SW

Patients with prolonged hospital stays often present with complex psychosocial needs (10), necessitating early involvement of SW to address discharge barriers. Our initiative focused on improving SW referrals and escalations to external organisations (i.e., local charities and community support services) to streamline discharge planning.

The ‘L.A.V.E. Interventions’ approach was adopted to ensure readiness for discharge or transfer through multidisciplinary collaboration.6 10 11 Key components included:

Leverage to External Offices10: SW coordinated with external organisations such as social services, embassies, and charities to resolve financial and logistical challenges.

Assessments10: Psychological, environmental, and additional needs assessments were conducted to support patients’ clinical recovery and discharge readiness.

This framework improved discharge planning and reduced UBDs through evidence-based practices and targeted interventions.

Change Idea #4: Electronic Dashboards to Track Flow Performance over time

Real-time dashboards have improved bed capacity management by providing instant access to critical metrics for informed decision-making.14 Drawing on performance data, these dashboards allow proactive monitoring of trends, enhancing operational efficiency and patient flow.15 16 Initially implemented in the bed management office, the success led to broader use among leadership to optimise resource allocation and strategic planning. By offering real-time data on bed occupancy, discharge status, and unit allocations, they facilitate timely responses to bottlenecks, improving patient care and resource utilisation.

To enhance executive engagement, the integration of Value Improvement Methodology (VIM)17 was tested, providing a structured, data-driven framework for leadership and MDT participation. Regular huddles were introduced as a platform to review performance metrics, address patient flow barriers, and implement rapid-cycle improvements. The approach was piloted for a month, with refinement aimed at enabling team independence and maintaining huddle duration under 15 minutes through guided rehearsals.

Change Idea #5: Embedding VIM into the Patient Flow Collaborative

Patient flow huddles, supported by a visual management board,17 display key metrics such as bed occupancy, discharge delays, and pending admissions, enhancing transparency and team alignment. Leaders utilised control charts to identify issues and box scores to ensure clear communication of our performance data, enabling timely decision-making. Following the test phase, bi-weekly huddles commenced, fostering a culture of continuous improvement and sustainable processes.

Measures

Outcome measures focused on reducing UBDs and enhancing morning bed availability. Process measures tracked the percentage of newly admitted patients screened for discharge needs, while balance measures monitored pending ED admissions. These metrics were reported monthly to project teams, facility leaders, and stakeholders through action period reports shared with IHI and HHQI under the system-wide patient flow collaborative program.

Outcome measures

Outcome measures in the Flow Collaborative Project included the prevalence of UBDs and number of patients affected, identified through monthly audits with defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Morning bed availability is tracked daily at 7 a.m. for acute and medical inpatient departments.

Process measure

A key process measure tracks the percentage of medical patient admissions screened within 24–48 hours, with data compiled daily and reported weekly for accurate evaluation.

Balance measure

Balance measures focused on the weekly average of pending ED admissions, monitored through the Capacity Management system, as a formal measurement of patient satisfaction and readmission rates was not yet feasible. Nevertheless, other potential unintended effects were monitored through routine MDT and operational oversight, although not captured as formal quantitative balance measures.

Analysis

Patients with UBDs were identified daily by CM, who updated lists of long-term, long-stay, and complex patients, specifying reasons for unnecessary stays, such as repatriation, capacity constraints, financial issues, and refusal. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged 14 and above, admitted to general medical inpatient units, with a LoS under six months.

Shewhart charts were used to analyze process changes over time and distinguish between common-cause and special-cause variation. Data were plotted chronologically using QI Macros software. The project team applied the IHI rules embedded within QI Macros to identify signals of special-cause variation and assess shifts in performance. This enabled comparison of baseline and post-intervention data to evaluate the effectiveness and sustainability of the interventions while continuously monitoring process performance throughout the project.

Results

During a data collection period spanning 79 weeks, a significant 68% reduction of UBDs was observed, decreasing from a baseline average of 1522.7 days (lower control limit (LCL): 1405.6; upper control limit (UCL): 1639.7) to an average of 493.4 days (LCL: 426.8; UCL: 560.0) (Figure 1). The reduction began in June 2023, corresponding to the initiation of two critical interventions: the testing of the CCT in February 2023 and the implementation of the CPS Tool in March 2023. Furthermore, a sustained improvement was achieved in July 2023 with the addition of SW bedside visits for identified complex patients, marking a pivotal enhancement in patient care strategies.

C-chart for the total number of UBDs. (UBDs, unnecessary bed days; LCL, lower control limit; UCL, upper control limit).

The reduction in UBDs continued consistently through July 2024, demonstrating the long-term sustainability of these measures. Additionally, the number of patients experiencing UBDs decreased by 53%, with figures declining from a baseline average of 29.0 patients (LCL: 12.8; UCL: 45.2) to an average of 13.6 patients (LCL: 2.53; UCL: 24.7) (online supplemental figure 8).

Patients classified as complex or at risk for complex discharges demonstrated sustained reductions in UBDs over a 10-month period, as evidenced by c-chart analysis (online supplemental figure 6). This trend coincided with the introduction of complex patient huddles within the CCT, involving MDT members and executive leaders. A temporary increase in complex patient numbers in December 2023 prompted reorientation sessions for nursing and case management teams, improving adherence to screening protocols and early discharge planning. These measures further reduced UBDs and minimised prolonged LoS.

Inpatient bed capacity, measured daily at 7 a.m., initially decreased from an average of x̄ = 1.5 (LCL: 0.1; UCL: 2.9) to x̄ = 1.0 (LCL: 0.3; UCL: 1.7) during the early testing phase, indicating frequent bed shortages (figure 2). To address this, a strategic reallocation of a medical unit to geriatric care was implemented in early 2024, which increased average bed availability to x̄ = 2.8 (LCL: 1.3; UCL: 4.3), stabilising capacity near this level by the end of the period. This change aimed to meet the growing demand for geriatric services and optimise overall resource distribution.

X-chart for Medical Staffed Beds Available every 7 a.m. (LCL, lower control limit; UCL, upper control limit).

However, this reallocation also contributed to an unintended rise in pending medical admissions in the ED during the same period. The number of pending admissions rose sharply from x̄ = 9.3 patients (LCL: 4.4; UCL: 14.2) to x̄ = 19.5 patients (LCL: 14.5; UCL: 24.5), reversing a prior 23% improvement that had reduced pending cases from x̄ = 12.1 (LCL: 6.8; UCL: 17.4) to x̄ = 9.3 (figure 3).

X-chart for ED Pending Medical Admissions every 7 a.m. (LCL, lower control limit; UCL, upper control limit).

Although overall bed availability increased following the reallocation of a medical unit to geriatric care, the increase was specific to geriatric-designated beds, not general medical inpatient beds. At the same time, general medical bed capacity effectively decreased, limiting the hospital’s ability to admit non-geriatric patients from the ED. As a result, pending medical admissions in the ED increased, even though total bed numbers improved. Additionally, external pressures—like admission surges from peripheral hospitals and new referral patterns—further strained the remaining medical bed supply. Further analysis revealed morning bed availability influenced admission delays, with notable fluctuations observed when staffed bed capacity dropped to one during peak hours (July to October 2023).

Moreover, screening and assessing newly admitted patients were monitored weekly using p-charts, with the CPS Tool significantly reducing UBDs and inpatient stays (online supplemental figure 9). Initial screening success averaged x̄ = 75% (LCL: 55%; UCL: 85%) within 24–48 hours of admission. Testing from March to December 2023 improved this rate to x̄ = 84% (LCL: 77%; UCL: 93%). To avoid duplication of tasks, the team adapted the electronic health record (EHR)-embedded checklist in January 2024, further enhancing the screening rate to x̄ = 89% (LCL: 77%; UCL: 100%). These adjustments, alongside complex care huddles, improved screening reliability and proactive discharge planning.

Discussion

Summary

Our study evaluates the outcomes of a system-wide patient flow collaborative implemented in a tertiary hospital in Qatar. Findings highlight critical factors influencing patient flow improvement, including shaping or reducing demand, addressing capacity-demand mismatches, systemic redesign, fostering collaboration, and engaging leadership. The research emphasises the holistic integration of professional, cultural, organizational, and technical elements to enhance person-centered healthcare delivery in complex systems.

Shape and reduce demand by reducing UBDs

A sustainable 68% reduction in UBDs and a 53% decrease in patients accumulating UBDs were achieved through comprehensive patient assessments and proactive screening on admission. Interdisciplinary discharge planning, centred on patient and family needs, emerged as best practice.

Studies emphasise that effective discharge planning, incorporating evaluations of patients’ home environments, caregiver support, and access to follow-up care, significantly lowers 30-day readmission rates.12 18 19Additionally, integrating ‘what matters to you’ conversations into discharge processes enhances alignment with patient values and improves adherence to post-discharge regimens.12 13 These conversations, informed by flow collaborative workshops, emphasise tailoring care to individual preferences, which have been associated with better outcomes and improved satisfaction.20 While broader data measures are limited,21 this approach proved beneficial within the study’s context.

Data-driven learning system to match capacity and demand

Before initiating the project, inpatient capacity limitations led to unpredictable patient flow processes. Using QI tools such as fishbone diagrams, waste wheels, flow maps, and Pareto charts, our team identified bottlenecks caused by UBDs awaiting beds in peripheral facilities. These bottlenecks restricted ED admissions and created significant daily variations in bed availability.

Shewhart control charts revealed inefficiencies in discharge planning, which often began late in the process. Early discharge planning was emphasised in subsequent PDSA cycles, addressing these inefficiencies. Additionally, real-time dashboards were proposed to accelerate data collection and enhance team awareness of patient flow. Dashboards demonstrated effectiveness in tracking capacity and throughput data14, reducing bottlenecks and optimising inpatient and ED flow by providing actionable insights.

Real-time monitoring highlighted key trends, such as peak discharge times between 12 p.m. and 2 p.m., guiding adjustments to target earlier discharges before 11 a.m. These interventions demonstrated significant improvements in discharge efficiency and situational awareness14, aligning patient flow with our institutional capacity and operational demands.

Redesigning the system

Redesigning the system was essential to improving discharge and admission processes. Implementing standardised patient assessments, such as the CPS Tool, enabled proactive identification of high-risk patients and streamlined discharge planning.10 18 Screening for social risks, including housing and financial instability, facilitated early referrals to support services like physiotherapy and charities, enhancing efficiency and alignment with patient needs.10 13 18

The study identified non-clinical barriers, including transportation and payment issues, as primary causes of discharge delays. Initiating discharge planning at admission improved information readiness for care transitions.11 SW played a pivotal role in addressing logistical concerns, coordinating post-hospital care and reducing readmissions, a result consistent with findings demonstrating average patient stays reduced by 30 days.10 Optimising their roles through interventions, such as L.A.V.E., demonstrated measurable impacts on inpatient throughput and patient flow efficiency.

Collaboration and teamwork

Collaboration within interorganizational QI initiatives like the patient flow collaboratives proved instrumental in driving innovation and efficiency22. Key features such as change packages, learning sessions, monthly conference calls, site visits, and PDSA cycles facilitated knowledge sharing, timely data reporting, and sustainable strategy implementation. Peer interactions during learning sessions enabled facilities to benefit from shared successes and lessons learned19, while MDT teamwork fostered problem-solving and a culture of continuous improvement.21

Leadership and sponsor engagement emerged as critical for sustained patient flow improvements23 Tools from VIM, including visual management boards, regular huddles, and box scores, enhanced transparency, accountability, and structured communication, aligning efforts with organisational goals17 18 23. These strategies promoted timely problem-solving and sustainable outcomes, as evidenced by improved team coordination and reduced delays.

Interpretation

Analysis of patient flow inefficiencies highlighted a mismatch between hospital bed capacity and demand, impacting transitions from the ED to inpatient units. Targeted strategies reduced UBDs and reduced discharge delays, yielding measurable improvements in bed capacity despite external constraints on ED boarding times.

Key interventions included early initiation of discharge planning at admission, proactive identification of long-stay patients using the CPS tool, and systematic resource utilisation. These measures reduced UBD by 68% and improved inpatient bed availability, aligning with similar findings in tertiary facilities across the MENA region.

UBD reasons were classified into two categories: localised issues requiring swift departmental-level changes (e.g., delays in medical clearance, payment issues, and patient refusals) and systemic challenges necessitating broader interventions (e.g., legal barriers, community limitations, and lack of post-acute care capacity). Addressing both categories through multidisciplinary approaches significantly improved patient flow and inpatient throughput, demonstrating effectiveness across multiple PDSA cycles.

Limitations

Limitations included time constraints, which hindered comprehensive planning and implementation, and the narrow scope of interventions, limited to a single medical inpatient department of a tertiary hospital. This localisation affects the generalisability of findings across other facilities and specialties. Additionally, reliance on data from the EHR may limit applicability to organisations without electronic systems.

Intensive training on advanced quality tools, offered by IHI and HHQI, was initially provided to selected team members, resulting in project activities being limited to those individuals. This challenge was subsequently addressed by establishing a follow-up mechanism to ensure continuity in their absence, such as during annual leave.

Operational challenges included insufficient conference room availability for discharge huddles, necessitating the use of virtual meeting platforms. Patient involvement was also restricted during the planning phase due to cultural and procedural barriers, as well as organisational approval delays. These limitations underscore the need for broader testing and inclusive strategies in future projects.

Sustainability plan

To sustain the gains achieved from our system-wide patient flow collaborative, we integrated key process improvements—such as multidisciplinary discharge huddles, visual management boards, and standardised workflows—into our routine operations. We maintained leadership engagement through regular reporting to executive sponsors, ensuring continued visibility and accountability. We also monitored progress using a Patient Flow Visual Management Board tracking indicators such as UBDs and discharge timeliness, reviewed monthly in unit quality meetings.

To build staff capability, we conducted refresher training sessions and designated unit-based flow champions to promote continuous improvement. Sustained learning was supported through ongoing PDSA cycles and quarterly collaborative meetings to share best practices. As interventions demonstrated success, we spread them to additional units under the same framework. Finally, we reinforced motivation through recognition programmes and integration of flow metrics into departmental goals, embedding the initiative into our facility’s culture of quality and operational excellence.

Conclusions

The reduction of the UBDs temporally coincided with the implementation of several change ideas. By integrating early discharge planning, the use of a CPS tool, L.A.V.E. interventions, real-time dashboards, and consistent patient flow huddles with leaders and sponsors, the project successfully reduced UBDs by 68%, increased the availability of staffed hospital beds by 87%, and reduced pending admissions in the ED by 23%. These improvements have led to enhanced patient care, cost savings, and reduced ED overcrowding.

This project demonstrated that a multidisciplinary approach, with collaboration among CM and BM and early involvement of SW and medical and nursing teams, is essential to sustaining improvements in patient flow. Furthermore, the data-driven approach and systematic tracking of process and outcome measures provided valuable insights into addressing the root causes of UBDs and flow failures.

While the results are promising, there are limitations to the generalisability of these findings due to the project’s focus on a single facility department. Future effort processes and expanding these interventions to other facilities and departments, engaging patients and patient representatives in the planning process, and ensuring long-term sustainability of the improvements. Despite these challenges, this project offers a valuable framework for improving patient flow in high-demand healthcare facility settings and serves as a model for similar healthcare environments globally.
