Compact and informative representation learning for scRNA-seq data clustering with masked information bottleneck
Authors: Xiaoqiang Yan, Fengshou Han, Yunpeng Wu, Zhen Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides unprecedented opportunities to characterize cellular heterogeneity. However, the high sparsity, noise, and redundancy inherent in gene expression data often obscure biologically meaningful signals and hinder accurate cell clustering. Although highly variable genes are commonly used to reduce dimensionality, they may still contain redundant or noisy information that degrades clustering performance.ResultsHere, we propose scMIB, a masked information bottleneck framework for robust representation learning in scRNA-seq data. The method introduces a masking-based denoising strategy that perturbs gene expression patterns and trains the model to recover informative structures while suppressing noise. Background

Single-cell ribonucleic acid sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides measurement of cellular heterogeneity at single-cell resolution, which is particularly useful for revealing the heterogeneity and diversity among cell subpopulations. By exploiting high-throughput sequencing data, clustering has become a fundamental step for identifying cell types and characterizing subpopulation structures [1–5]. As a core problem in machine learning, clustering analysis has witnessed extensive investigation throughout recent decades, such as K-means [6], spectral clustering [7, 8] and density-based spatial clustering [9]. However, directly applying traditional clustering algorithms to scRNA-seq data often yields suboptimal results. This is mainly because scRNA-seq data exhibits extreme sparsity compared with bulk RNA-seq data, in which most measurements are zeros caused by low RNA capture rate. Besides, the high dimensionality of scRNA-seq profiles often involves tens of thousands of genes measured across individual cells, which introduces significant computational complexity.

To overcome the aforementioned challenges, diverse clustering algorithms have been systematically investigated for scRNA-seq analysis. For instance, pcaReduce [10] employs principal component analysis (PCA) to map scRNA-seq data into a low-dimensional representation, followed by a hierarchical clustering strategy to derive cell subpopulations. SC3 [11], SIMLR [12], SMSC [13], SAME [14], and RCSL [15] utilize ensemble learning methods to combine multiple base clusterings of scRNA-seq data. Seurat [16] uncovers the transcriptomic subtypes by constructing cell similarity networks through graph partitioning. However, a critical limitation of these traditional clustering lies in their linear separability assumption, which fundamentally conflicts with the intrinsic nonlinear manifolds embedded in complex scRNA-seq data.

Due to its powerful nonlinear modeling capabilities, deep learning has enabled encouraging advances in scRNA-seq clustering in recent years, such as deep embedded clustering [17], deep graph neural networks [18], and contrastive learning-based methods [19]. Recent studies have further explored graph neural networks and multi-view contrastive learning strategies to improve cell representation learning and clustering robustness [20, 21]. Despite their ability to handle nonlinear relationships, these methods still remain susceptible due to the following issues: (1) Intrinsic stochasticity of gene expression. Owing to the inherent randomness of gene expression in biological systems, even similar cells in the same tissue often exhibits divergent expression patterns, which increases the difficulty of accurate cell type identification. (2) Technical variations during sequencing. For instance, the low efficiency of RNA capture may cause expressed genes to be undetected, leading to a “false” zero count observation. This phenomenon is commonly referred to as a dropout event. Additionally, variability across sequencing platforms often causes differences in read counts, especially with low sequencing depth. These technical variations reduce the discriminative resolution between cell populations, which hinders accurate identification of cell subpopulations.

To effectively deal with the highly noisy scRNA-seq data caused by intrinsic stochasticity of gene expression and technical variations during sequencing, lots of imputation and denoising approaches have been developed [22–30], which leverage inter-cellular relationships to estimate gene expression values. In the literature, existing imputation and denoising methods are usually classified into four categories: probabilistic generative model [22, 23], neighborhood inference [24], matrix factorization [26, 27], and deep representation learning [28, 29]. Although existing imputation and denoising methods have achieved encouraging results, they predominantly rely on the predefined assumptions about data structures, such as neighborhood locality, low-rank linear features, or specific probability distributions. These assumptions often fail to capture the complex gene interactions and cellular heterogeneity in scRNA-seq data, which leads to the loss of genuine biological signals.

Recently, mask estimation approaches address these limitations by randomly masking subsets of data features [31, 32], which compels models to dynamically learn intrinsic data relationships rather than depending on prior assumptions. Stochastic masking of scRNA-seq inputs enables model training through context-aware imputation of omitted values, thus effectively preserving critical biological information. For example, scNAME [31] enhances scRNA-seq data denoising through a hierarchical masking strategy and neighborhood contrastive learning, where noises are reduced by encouraging the model to learn nonlinear dependencies across genes. scMAE [32] employs a masked autoencoder framework to capture gene correlations by randomly shuffling gene expression values and using a mask predictor to guide accurate reconstruction. However, as an unsupervised data reduction method, the masked encoder often preserves irrelevant correlations between technical factors (such as batch effects and dropout effects) and cellular features when learning low-dimensional representations of the masked data, which introduces noises into the learned representations. In addition, random gene masking may distort cell type-specific expression signatures, which could hinder the ability of the model to learn discriminative representations and compromise the separability of cell clusters.

We therefore propose a novel masked information bottleneck method for clustering scRNA-seq data (scMIB), which enhances the representation compactness and cluster separability by designing noise-aware mask estimation and task-oriented information compression. The model architecture of scMIB is provided in Fig. 1. Firstly, scMIB generates a masked version of the original scRNA-seq data by randomly shuffling gene expression values across cells and applying a Bernoulli-sampled mask matrix to combine the original and shuffled data. Then, an information bottleneck compression mechanism is implemented through a constrained encoder to extract compact cell representations. Besides, to mitigate the impact of perturbation on cell identity-specific gene expression, we devise a perturbation-resistant semantic alignment module, which aligns the semantic labels derived from the original and masked representations. Finally, we construct a perturbation predictor to estimate the probability whether the gene expression values have been masked, in which the predicted mask and the learned cell representation are integrated into the reconstruction process, yielding a denoised gene expression profile. Extensive evaluations on various scRNA-seq datasets, combined with biological analyses, demonstrate that the proposed scMIB achieves superior clustering performance, denoising capability and biological applicability compared with state-of-the-art baselines. In summary, the proposed scMIB framework offers three key contributions:

A mask estimation strategy is proposed to predict perturbed gene positions, which addresses the stochasticity and technical noise inherent in scRNA-seq data by enabling the model to capture intrinsic gene-gene dependencies and learn perturbation-robust representations without modeling any specific types of noises.

Overview of the scMIB framework. a Given the original scRNA-seq expression matrix X, scMIB constructs perturbed inputs by first randomly shuffling cells to obtain X′ and then sampling a binary mask matrix M from a Bernoulli distribution. The masked data X~ is generated by selectively combining X and X′ according to M. b The masked input X~ is encoded into a compact latent representation Z~ through an encoder constrained by the information bottleneck compression loss. To ensure robustness under perturbations, scMIB jointly optimizes multiple loss components: the information bottleneck compression loss Lcompress, the semantic alignment loss Lsemantic that enforces consistency between representations derived from original and masked data, the mask estimation loss Lmask for predicting the applied perturbations, and the reconstruction loss Lreconstruction for noise-aware recovery of gene expression profiles. The learned compact representation serves as the basis for the final clustering step. c The final latent representations produced by scMIB can be directly used for clustering-based cell-type discovery and further support downstream analyses, including differential expression analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, and trajectory inference

Results

scMIB achieves state-of-the-art clustering performance

To evaluate the clustering performance of scMIB, we included ten benchmark methods in the comparison: CLEAR [33], contrastive-sc [19], scGNN [18], Graph-sc [34], scGPCL [35], scDFN [36], scNAME [31], scMAE [32], DCA [28], and AutoClass [29]. CLEAR and contrastive-sc are two contrastive clustering approaches for scRNA-seq data. scGNN, Graph-sc, scGPCL, and scDFN employ graph neural network model to learn the cellular topology. scNAME and scMAE adopt masked encoders to learn cell subpopulations. DCA and AutoClass denoise scRNA-seq data via deep generative model and Bayesian model, respectively. The performance of these methods was assessed on fifteen real scRNA-seq datasets generated from diverse sequencing platforms, as summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1 [37–49]. Clustering performance was evaluated using seven commonly adopted metrics, including normalized mutual information (NMI) [50], adjusted rand index (ARI) [51], clustering accuracy (ACC) [52], purity (PUR) [53], adjusted mutual information (AMI) [54], Jaccard index (JI) [55], and average silhouette width (ASW) [56].

Figure 2 showed the clustering results of scMIB and competing methods on the fifteen real-world scRNA-seq datasets. From Fig. 2a, we found that scMIB had superior performance in terms of NMI and ARI metrics on eleven datasets, while showing comparable clustering performance to the leading baselines on the remaining four datasets. The corresponding numerical NMI and ARI values are summarized in Additional file 1: Tables S2–S3. Since both scMIB and the competing algorithms rely on random initialization during gradient-based optimization, their clustering performance may vary across different random seeds. To validate the robustness of these methods on different initializations, we compared their average clustering performance over ten different random seeds. From Fig. 2b, we observed that scMIB consistently outperformed other baselines across different random seeds, demonstrating superior stability under diverse initialization conditions.

In summary, the evaluation results verify that scMIB method has superior and stable performance on a wide range of scRNA-seq datasets.

Evaluating denoising ability of scMIB on simulated scRNA-seq data

scMIB synergizes noise-aware mask estimation with task-oriented information compression to learn compact representation. To evaluate the denoising ability of scMIB under different scenarios, we conducted four simulation experiments under different dropout effects and noise distributions by following the experimental setup of AutoClass [29]: (1) raw gene count data with dropout effects; (2) raw gene count by adding Gaussian noises; (3) raw gene count by adding Uniform noises; (4) raw gene count by adding Gamma noise. The simulated data were generated using the Splatter [57] package, consisting of 3000 cells and 500 genes across six cell types.

We compared scMIB with three popular and widely-used single-cell denoising algorithms: DCA [28], scImpute [23], and AutoClass [29]. Figure 3a presented t-SNE visualizations of scMIB and other three denoising methods on the four simulated datasets. The visualization revealed that the raw noisy data exhibited scattered low-dimensional embeddings that distorted the underlying biological structure. Besides, scMIB consistently achieved more compact and clearly separable cluster structures than competing algorithms under various noise and dropout scenarios. Remarkably, in cases of Gaussian, Uniform, and Gamma noise contamination, the representations learned by scMIB demonstrated even more compact and distinct cluster separation compared with the ground truth labels. This observation supports our hypothesis that scMIB’s gene-level perturbation discriminator can distinguish true biological signals from spurious variations by simulating biological and technical noises.

The first column shows raw data with varying noise types: dropout effects, Gaussian noise, Uniform noise, and Gamma noise. The second column presents the true gene count data, while the subsequent columns display the denoised results from DCA, scImpute, AutoClass, and scMIB. b Average silhouette width (ASW) scores under different noisy conditions are reported for raw data, ground truth, and denoised outputs from different methods. c ASW scores under dropout conditions are reported for raw data, ground truth, and denoised outputs from different methods. To quantitatively assess the clustering quality of scMIB under different dropout effects and noise distributions, we computed the average silhouette width (ASW) values, which ranged from −1 to 1 and higher values indicated well-defined and distinct cluster structures. From Fig. 3b, we found that the ASW values on the raw data with noises dropped significantly compared with true gene count data without technical noises. This is mainly because the noises in scRNA-seq data greatly compromise the data quality and obscure the differences between different cell types. Besides, the data denoised by scMIB clearly recovered the original cell type with a significant improvement (0.4–0.8.4.8) in ASW metrics. For the other imputation and denoising approaches (DCA, scImpute and AutoClass), their ASW values were significantly lower than scMIB. Notably, scMIB achieved superior ASW value even compared with the ground-truth data under Gaussian, Uniform, and Gamma noise conditions. This unexpected improvement stems from the combination of noise-aware mask estimation with task-oriented information compression in our scMIB method, which not only removes technical noises, but also filters out the irrelevant biological signals to enhance cluster separation.

With the increase of cell type diversity in simulated data (e.g., from 6 to 10 types), subtle transcriptional differences among heterogeneous cell populations are more likely obscured by dropout events, making it more challenging to extract meaningful biological signals. To assess scMIB’s performance across datasets with varying numbers of cell types, we generated five simulated scRNA-seq datasets containing between 6 and 10 cell types using Splatter. Figure 3c and Additional file 2: Fig. S2 displayed the ASW values and t-SNE visualizations of scMIB compared with the ground-truth and other three benchmark methods on datasets with different numbers of cell types. From this figure, we observed that scMIB exhibited lower ASW values than the ground-truth, but it significantly outperformed the other three competing algorithms. The above observation further validates the robustness of scMIB method in identifying different numbers of cell types.

Differential expression and enrichment analysis

Differential expression (DE) is a fundamental scRNA-seq application, which identifies genes or biomarkers with significantly altered expression levels. In this part, we investigated the applicability of scMIB to DE analysis on Limb Muscle data, which consists of six gold-standard cell type labels: endothelial cell, B cell, mesenchymal stem cell (MSCs), skeletal muscle satellite cell, T cell and macrophage.

First, we adopted UMAP approach to visualize the learned cell embeddings of scMIB on Limb Muscle data, in which each cell cluster was colored according to the gold-standard labels and the predicted labels, respectively. From Fig. 4a, we observed that scMIB clearly divided all cell types in the learned cell embeddings. Then, we quantified the biological consistency by computing the overlap degree between the top 50 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each cluster and those of gold-standard cell types. Specifically, Fig. 4b showed the heatmap visualization of the identified clusters (rows) against the gold-standard cell types (columns), in which the color intensity scaled with the extent of DEG overlap. From this figure, we found that scMIB demonstrated superior overlap scores, enabling explicit assignment of predicted clusters to gold-standard cell types. The comparative results of DEG overlap with other baselines further validate the effectiveness of scMIB in identifying highly expressed genes (Additional file 2: Figs. S3–S4).

Then, we performed DE analysis based on the cell clusters learned by scMIB to systematically select top five DEGs within each cluster, which were subsequently designated as cell-type-specific genes. As shown in Fig. 4c, the DE analysis of scMIB revealed many validated marker genes in each cell type, such as Ly6c1, Aqp1, and Slc9a3r2 (highly expressed in endothelial cells); Cd74, Cd79a, H2Ab1, H2-Aa, and Cd79b (B cell markers); Tryobp, Fcer1g, Lyz2, Ctss, and Lyz1 (macrophage markers). Next, we generated the ranked gene lists for each cluster based on log fold-change values, which were subsequently used as input for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify hallmark pathway enrichments.

Figure 4d presented GSEA results for mouse-ortholog hallmark pathways. As shown in this figure, the apoptosis pathway was significantly enriched in mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), macrophages, and endothelial cells, consistent with their established roles in programmed cell death regulation [58–60]. Besides, the inflammatory response regulators were enriched in macrophages and endothelial cells. The macrophages are central regulators of the inflammatory response and that endothelial cells play an important barrier and signaling role in the early stages of inflammation [61, 62]. Moreover, the angiogenesis pathway had the highest enrichment score in endothelial cells. Behaviors such as proliferation, migration, and lumen formation of endothelial cells have been shown to underlie angiogenesis [63, 64]. In addition, the coagulation pathway was also highly enriched in endothelial cells. The role of endothelial cells in the coagulation process has been demonstrated in many studies [65, 66]. These analyses demonstrate the biological applicability of the clustering results of scMIB.

Notably, competing methods erroneously split RBCs into two or more smaller clusters, whereas scMIB maintained cell type consistency despite significant data imbalance. We further assessed the biological significance of the identified clusters by benchmarking scMIB against competing methods using the Bach dataset. The Bach dataset consists of 23,184 cells capturing the temporal cellular heterogeneity of murine mammary epithelial cells during developmental stages: hormone sensing cells (Hs), luminal progenitor (Lp), alveolar cells (Av), basalcells (Bsl), myoepithelial cells (Myo), and procr+basalcells (Prc). Among them, the Hs cells are further divided into hormone sensing progenitors (Hsp) and hormone sensing differentiated (Hsd), and the Av cells are further divided into alveolar differentiated cells (Avd) and alveoloar progenitor cells (Avp). Figure 5b and Additional file 2: Fig. S9 presented the Sankey plots of scMIB and other baselines, where the left panels displayed the clustering labels generated by different methods, and the right panels showed the ground truth cell labels. From this figure, we observed that scMIB exhibited thicker connecting lines with the ground truth, which indicated a higher number of matched labels. In contrast, scGNN produced more clusters than the real number of cell types, and other baselines erroneously divided the Hsd cells into two distinctive cell clusters. This phenomenon validates that scMIB achieves superior accuracy in preserving cell type integrity and biological consistency.

To evaluate the performance of learned representations of scMIB method in downstream tasks, we applied them to cell trajectory inference task. Following the scCRT [67], we conducted cell trajectory inference on the synthetic dataset binary_tree_8 (8,429 cells and 531 genes), and estimated cellular pseudotime based on the inferred trajectories. The discrepancies between inferred and ground-truth trajectories were quantified through the following key metrics. Hamming-Ipsen-Mikhailov (HIM) score quantifies the topological similarity between predicted and reference trajectory structures; F1 branches evaluates accuracy of cellular ordering along trajectory bifurcations; F1 milestone assesses correctness of milestone progression sequencing. Pseudotime estimation quality is determined by Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), which captures the linear correlation between inferred pseudotime and the true temporal ordering. Figure 5c and Additional file 2: Figs. S10–S12 demonstrated the cell trajectory inference results of scMIB versus all benchmark methods. scMIB exhibited superior performance in both trajectory topology inference and pseudotime estimation accuracy, validating its ability to capture dynamic cellular state transitions and reconstruct authentic developmental trajectories through learned representations.

Figure 5d presented the representation compactness, separability, and cluster purity across varying random seeds, following the evaluation metrics proposed in MetaQ [68]. As shown in this figure, scMIB consistently maintained stable performance under diverse stochastic initializations, demonstrating its algorithmic reliability in single-cell representation learning.

To evaluate the informativeness and generalizability of the representations learned by scMIB, we employed them as the input features of mainstream clustering algorithms and assessed the resulting performance gains. Figure 5e and Additional file 2: Fig. S13 illustrated that the clustering performance of other scRNA-seq data clustering approaches could be improved by adopting the compact representation learned by scMIB. This observation highlights two critical properties of scMIB-derived low-dimensional embeddings: (1) Algorithm-independent separability: The representations learned by scMIB maintain clear cluster boundaries between cell types, which is independent of the downstream clustering algorithms. (2) Biological information preservation: scMIB effectively preserves discriminative biological signals while removing noisy and redundant features, thereby improving clustering performance.

Hyperparameter tuning, ablation study and time complexity

In the loss function of scMIB, there are two key hyperparameters α and β that control the contributions of semantic alignment and information compression, respectively. To investigate the sensitivity of these two hyperparameters on scMIB, we conducted experiments with various combinations of α and β, where α∈{0.001,0.01,0.1,0.2,1,10,100} and β∈{0.001,0.01,0.1,0.5,1,10,100}. Figure 6a illustrated the average NMI and ARI values across the used fifteen scRNA-seq datasets. From this figure, we observed that scMIB exhibited robustness to the most combinations of these two hyperparameters. However, when either α or β exceeded 10, there was a sharp decline in terms of NMI and ARI metrics. The decline in clustering performance indicates that excessive focus on either semantic alignment or information compression imposes limitations on the scMIB model. When the α parameter is set too high, the scMIB model overemphasizes semantic alignment, resulting in redundant information unrelated to cell clustering persisting in its learned representations of scRNA-seq data. Conversely, a large β value drives the model to prioritize information compression excessively, which simultaneously removes biologically significant information from scRNA-seq data. The experimental results of parameter sensitivity analysis on individual datasets are provided in Additional file 2: Figs. S14–S17. Based on the aforementioned experimental observations, we set α=0.2 and β=0.5 for all used scRNA-seq datasets.

To comprehensively evaluate the contribution of each component in scMIB, we conducted an extended ablation study that includes both single-component removal and pairwise ablations. Specifically, we first examined four single-component variants by removing the reconstruction loss (scMIB-R), mask estimation loss (scMIB-M), semantic alignment loss (scMIB-S), and information bottleneck compression loss (scMIB-T), respectively. Since the reconstruction loss is required to ensure stable representation learning, we restrict the pairwise ablations to interactions among the remaining three core components. As shown in Fig. 6b, removing the reconstruction loss results in the most severe performance degradation, confirming its essential role in preserving the global structure of the gene expression space and stabilizing representation learning. Removing any of the other three core modules also leads to a substantial and comparable decline, indicating that each component is indispensable for optimal performance. Importantly, the pairwise ablation results reveal a clear synergistic effect, particularly between the mask estimation and information bottleneck modules. While removing either module alone degrades performance, jointly removing both causes a more pronounced decline, demonstrating that their contributions are not simply additive. The masking operation introduces controlled perturbations, while the information bottleneck encourages the encoder to learn a compact and robust representation for cell-type discrimination. Furthermore, the perturbation-resistant semantic alignment module provides complementary benefits by organizing the compressed representations into a semantically coherent and well-separated latent space. Its joint ablation with the information bottleneck leads to additional performance loss, highlighting their cooperative roles. Overall, these results indicate that the superior performance of scMIB arises from a coherent pipeline in which masking, information bottleneck compression, and semantic alignment work together to produce discriminative representations for effective clustering.

We also performed sensitivity analyses on other model parameters. As shown in Fig. 6c, we examined the effects of different learning rates, reconstruction weights, masking probabilities for the original data, and the number of selected HVGs on clustering performance. We found that scMIB maintains relatively stable performance across a wide range of learning rates and reconstruction weights. In contrast, the masking probability has a more pronounced effect: clustering performance degrades when the masking ratio is either too low or too high. A low masking probability introduces insufficient perturbation, limiting the model’s ability to learn denoised representations, whereas an excessively high masking probability removes too much informative content, hindering effective reconstruction and semantic alignment. Regarding the input dimensionality, we evaluated five commonly used HVG settings (500, 1000, 1500, 2000, and 2500) across 15 scRNA-seq datasets. The results indicate that scMIB is generally robust to the choice of HVG number, with consistently stable performance across a wide range of settings. Notably, using only 500 HVGs leads to a decline in ARI performance, likely due to insufficient biological information for reliable cell-type discrimination. Based on the robustness across larger HVG counts and considering the trade-off between clustering performance and computational efficiency, we adopt 1,000 HVGs as the default setting in our experiments. Detailed NMI and ACC results are reported in Additional file 2: Fig. S18.

To evaluate the running time of scMIB, we simulated datasets with 1000 to 100,000 cells derived from a large-scale dataset of ∼900,000 human non small cell lung cancer and normal adjacent cells (detailed information can be referred to “Scalability analysis”). Specifically, we employed linear regression with ordinary least squares fitting (OLS) to quantify the linear relationship between running time of scMIB and dataset volume. Figure 6d demonstrated the linear dependence of running time on cell numbers under both K-means (R2 = 0.9705) and Leiden (R2 = 0.9748) clustering paradigms, where R2 was the coefficient of determination, the higher its value, the better the model’s fit. This linear time complexity highlights efficient algorithmic implementation and its practical applicability to large-scale single-cell data analysis. Additional file 2: Fig. S19 presented the comparisons of running time and memory consumption between scMIB and other baselines on real-world datasets. All results were obtained on a computer with RTX 4090, 24 Gb memory. Figure 6e provided the convergence analysis of scMIB on Guo, Young, Baron and Plasschaert datasets. The total loss decreased rapidly during the initial iterations and subsequently stabilized at a fixed value, highlighting the excellent convergence performance of the scMIB algorithm. Similar results on other datasets can be found in Additional file 2: Fig. S20.

The practical utility of scMIB extends beyond clustering accuracy and depends on whether the learned representations preserve biologically meaningful structure that supports downstream cell-type analysis. Discussion

Single-cell RNA-seq data are inherently noisy due to stochastic gene expression and technical artifacts, which poses significant challenges for learning reliable cellular representations. In this context, scMIB provides a unified framework that jointly addresses noise perturbation, representation compression, and semantic stability. By combining noise-aware mask estimation, information bottleneck–based compression, and semantic alignment, scMIB is designed to learn representations that are both robust to perturbations and informative for cell-type discovery.

A central characteristic that distinguishes scMIB from existing masking-based self-supervised methods is its information-theoretic learning objective. Unlike approaches that primarily rely on reconstruction fidelity or contrastive similarity, scMIB explicitly constrains the latent representation to function as a minimal sufficient statistic for clustering. This task-oriented formulation encourages the model to discard task-irrelevant variability while preserving biologically meaningful structure, thereby providing a principled explanation for its improved robustness and discriminative power.

An important practical consideration concerns the biological plausibility of the masking strategy. Rather than attempting to explicitly model a particular noise distribution, scMIB adopts a distribution-agnostic perturbation mechanism. This design choice avoids imposing potentially incorrect assumptions about noise characteristics, such as specific dropout or count-based models, and instead promotes the learning of stable gene–gene dependencies that remain invariant under diverse and unknown sources of variation. Consequently, the learned representations emphasize robust biological signals rather than noise-specific artifacts, contributing to improved generalizability across heterogeneous scRNA-seq datasets. The information bottleneck further reinforces this effect by filtering redundant or irrelevant features, while semantic alignment ensures consistency of cluster-level representations under perturbation.

Beyond its clustering capabilities, scMIB demonstrates strong biological applicability. The differential expression analysis of detected clusters reveals well-established marker genes, confirming that the method captures biologically meaningful signals. GSEA validates functional pathways associated with these clusters, further highlighting their biological relevance. Moreover, when applied to trajectory inference, scMIB successfully reconstructs branching developmental processes consistent with established biological knowledge, highlighting its ability to characterize dynamic cell state transitions.

Finally, scalability is an essential requirement for large-scale single-cell analysis. Linear regression fitting indicates that scMIB achieves linear time complexity with respect to the number of cells, ensuring its applicability to datasets of increasing size and heterogeneity.

In summary, scMIB not only achieves state-of-the-art clustering accuracy but also provides biologically meaningful, noise-robust, and scalable representations. These results underscore its potential as a versatile tool for diverse downstream analyses in single-cell transcriptomics.

Conclusions

The intrinsic stochasticity of gene expression and technical variations during sequencing processes often introduce substantial noises irrelevant to clustering tasks. To deal with these challenges, we have proposed a novel masked information bottleneck approach for scRNA-seq data clustering, named scMIB, which synergizes noise-aware mask estimation with task-oriented information bottleneck compression mechanism to learn compact representation. Comprehensive evaluations demonstrate that scMIB significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines on both simulated and real-world datasets. Besides, scMIB is capable of removing diverse types of noises (e.g., Gaussian, Uniform, Gamma) and technical artifacts (e.g., dropout events) in scRNA-seq data compared with denoising baselines. It is noteworthy that under various types of noise interference (including Gaussian, Uniform, and Gamma), the learned representations of scMIB exhibit superior representation compactness and cluster separability compared with the original data. This finding demonstrates that the representation space of scMIB not only possesses strong noise robustness but also maintains discriminative features between cell types. Moreover, the compact and informative representations learned by scMIB are crucial for the downstream tasks in scRNA-seq data analysis.

Despite the promising results achieved by scMIB, several limitations should be acknowledged. The current framework still involves several hyperparameters whose optimal settings may vary across datasets, and further investigation into adaptive parameter selection strategies may improve its usability in practical applications. In addition, although our experiments demonstrate consistent improvements in clustering performance, further biological validation and applications to more diverse datasets would help to better assess the generalizability and interpretability of the proposed framework.

Methods

This section details the proposed methodology and its implementation. We first describe the preprocessing steps applied to raw single-cell RNA-seq count data. Next, we present the overall architecture of the scMIB framework, designed to learn compact and informative representations through mask estimation and information bottleneck compression mechanism. Subsequent subsections elaborate on each key component: masked data generation, the information bottleneck constrained-encoder, perturbation-resistant semantic alignment, gene-level perturbation prediction, and noise-aware reconstruction. Finally, we specify the implementation details for model training and evaluation.

Raw read count data preprocessing

We first performed quality control on the raw scRNA-seq data by preserving genes that were detected in at least one cell and cells that expressed more than one gene. After quality control, the filtered scRNA-seq data were preprocessed and normalized by the Python toolkit SCANPY [74] (version 1.9.5). Specifically, the gene expression counts of each cell were first scaled by a size factor corresponding to the total expression in that cell. Then, the normalized gene counts were log-transformed to stabilize variance. Next, we select 1000 highly variable genes (HVGs) according to their expression dispersions. Finally, the scRNA-seq data was scaled so that each gene has a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across all cells.

Framework of scMIB

Denote the preprocessed scRNA-seq data as X∈RC×G, where C and G are the number of cells and genes, respectively. Cell clustering aims to partition the X cells into K distinct clusters or subpopulations. However, the complex perturbations of gene expression stochasticity and technical variations often results in noisy single-cell expression profiles, which introduce spurious correlations in representations. To deal with these limitations, we devise a scMIB framework to enhance the representation compactness and cluster separability. The scMIB framework is organized in a hierarchical manner. Specifically, the mask generation module defines a perturbation space to simulate stochastic and technical variations; within this space, the information bottleneck serves as the primary regularization to enforce representation compactness by filtering irrelevant factors; finally, semantic alignment and reconstruction act as consistency constraints to preserve cell identity and biological signals under perturbations. The framework of scMIB consists of five components:

1) Masked scRNA-seq data generation simulates complex noise effects. We randomly shuffle gene expression values across cells and use a Bernoulli-sampled mask matrix M∈RC×G to combine the original and shuffled data, which generates the masked version X~ of original scRNA-seq data X.

2) Information bottleneck constrained-encoder learns compact and informative masked representations Z~ of the scRNA-seq data X~ by filtering out the noises and irrelevant factors.

3) Perturbation-resistant semantic alignment improves the cluster separability by aligning the semantic labels S~∈RC×K and S∈RC×K derived from the masked representations and original scRNA-seq data, respectively.

4) Gene-level perturbation predictor learns the predicted mask matrix M^∈RC×G by utilizing a two-layer neural network with a Swish activation function, which introduces nonlinearity and facilitates accurate mask predictions for each gene in response to perturbations.

5) Noise-aware reconstruction is achieved by concatenating the masked representations Z~ with the predicted mask M^, allowing the model to recover biological signals while mitigating noise effect.

In scRNA-seq data analysis, a compact yet informative representation is crucial for effectively performing downstream tasks. Inspired by information bottleneck (IB) theory [75], scMIB is designed to learn representations that are both sufficient for preserving task-relevant biological signals and minimal in data complexity. In other words, the learned representation approximates a minimal sufficient statistic of the input, capturing essential cellular identity information while filtering out irrelevant information. The compression Lcompress of the representation can be achieved by minimizing the mutual information between the masked data X~ and its compressed representation Z~. The preservation of relevant information is realized through three aspects: mask consistency Lmask, semantic consistency Lsemantic, and reconstruction consistency Lreconstruct. Perturbation-resistant semantic alignment

The mask estimation strategy simulates potential noise interference during sequencing and enhances the adaptability of the model to data variability. However, the randomness of the perturbation process may distort cell-type-specific gene expression patterns, making it challenging to accurately recover cellular identities when directly applying masked representations to clustering.

To address these issues, we propose a perturbation-resistant semantic alignment module to capture the semantic consistency between original and masked scRNA-seq data, which focuses on aligning global semantic cluster labels rather than matching detailed feature patterns. The global semantic alignment approach is less sensitive to local distortions introduced by masking and prevents perturbed representations from being incorrectly aligned to unrelated cell types. In addition, by enhancing the consistency of representations within the same cell type and increasing the semantic differences between different cell types, aligning semantic consistency labels between original and masked scRNA-seq data can also improve the separability of clusters.

Specifically, we stack two separate label multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) on the masked representations Z~ and the original data representations Z∈RC×G to generate cluster assignments S~={s~ij} and S={sij}, respectively. Clustering procedure

After obtaining the latent representations learned by scMIB, we apply an external clustering algorithm to derive cell cluster assignments. Specifically, we adopt two widely used clustering approaches: K-means [6] and Leiden [77] clustering. To balance computational efficiency and clustering robustness, the choice of clustering algorithm is determined by dataset size. For datasets with fewer than 10,000 cells, we use K-means, while for larger datasets, Leiden clustering is employed due to its superior performance and scalability on large single-cell datasets.
