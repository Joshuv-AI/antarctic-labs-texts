History of Presentation

An 80-year-old man presented with progressive dyspnea with an NYHA functional class III. He underwent a combined surgical aortic valve replacement with a 29-mm ATS 3f model 1000 stentless aortic bioprosthesis (Medtronic) + coronary artery bypass graft with a saphenous vein graft to the first obtuse marginal + ascending aorta replacement with a Dacron graft 11 years prior. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a severe bioprosthetic failure of the 3f surgical valve, especially of the noncoronary leaflet, with a severe intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (pressure half-time 290 ms), a dominant excentric jet toward the interventricular septum, a mean transvalvular gradient of 9 mm Hg, and an area of 1.9 cm2 (Video 1). The estimated left ventricular ejection fraction was 62%, and there was moderate functional mitral regurgitation with severe pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary artery systolic pressure 54 mm Hg).

Past Medical History

Additional relevant medical history included persistent atrial fibrillation and bifascicular block (right bundle branch block + left anterior fascicular block) with an unsuccessful ablation attempt, a persistent hemolytic anemia (hemoglobin 109 g/L, haptoglobin 0.21 g/L, lactate dehydrogenase 493 U/L), chronic kidney failure (clearance 52 mL/min/m2), colectomy for colon cancer, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage 2 years prior with no residual neurological deficits, and an early-stage interstitial pulmonary fibrosis. Coronary angiography 1 year prior showed a 40% (mild) stenosis of saphenous vein graft and a 60% (moderate) stenosis of the circumflex artery (Figure 1). Furthermore, the patient was on the waiting list for a shoulder operation. Differential Diagnosis

The patient's dyspnea in the context of a dysfunctional bioprosthesis warranted consideration of both cardiac etiologies—including structural valve deterioration, paravalvular leak, endocarditis, atrial fibrillation, and progressive ventricular dysfunction—and noncardiac causes such as anemia or pulmonary fibrosis. After comprehensive investigation, severe intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (AR) secondary to structural valve deterioration was identified as the predominant cause of the patient's heart failure symptoms. All other potential differentials were systematically excluded.

Given a Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality of 4.4% and a morbidity rate of 19.2%, the patient was considered a poor candidate for a surgical redo and Heart Team consensus was for a valve-in-valve (ViV) transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) strategy.

Management

Based on the CT-based preplanning, we decided to perform a ViV TAVR via transfemoral access with a 27-mm JenaValve Trilogy System (JenaValve Technology GmbH). The procedure was performed under conscious sedation using fluoroscopic guidance via the right femoral artery as primary access (with a delivery sheath of 20 F) and the left femoral artery and the right radial artery as secondary accesses. After crossing the valve with the aid of a pigtail catheter and positioning the Safari X-small wire in the left ventricle, the Trilogy valve delivery system was advanced into the ascending aorta. During the attempt to engage the THV locators within the coronary cusps under rapid ventricular pacing (180 beats/min), the patient developed rapid and severe hemodynamic compromise, that progressed to cardiorespiratory arrest. Angiography revealed massive aortic regurgitation. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation and emergency endotracheal intubation were initiated; meanwhile, the correct positioning of the THV allocators in the nadirs of the 3 cusps was confirmed using pigtail catheters. The valve was deployed (Video 2), and spontaneous circulation reemerged. Postimplant aortography showed no AR, confirmed by the postprocedural echocardiography (Video 3).

A leadless pacemaker (Micra) for a third-degree atrioventricular block was implanted on the same day of the procedure. The postprocedural course was uncomplicated and the patient was discharged after 2 days.

Follow-Up

At 2-month follow-up, the patient is in NYHA functional class I, and transthoracic echocardiography showed good functioning of the aortic THV with a mean transvalvular gradient of 3 mm Hg, an area of 3.4 cm2 with no intra- or paravalvular leak (PVL), and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55% with a mild to moderate mitral regurgitation (Video 4). Hemolytic anemia also resolved, with follow-up hemoglobin normalizing to 147 g/L.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported experience of the use of a TrilogyValve for a ViV TAVR for a degenerated 3f stentless valve with severe pure aortic regurgitation.

Stentless surgical aortic valves, such as the ATS 3f model 1000, were developed to optimize hemodynamics and facilitate implantation in complex surgical settings, including concomitant aortic procedures. In contrast, ViV TAVR in stentless surgical valves is associated with a higher risk of malposition, embolization, and coronary obstruction, underscoring the need for meticulous imaging-based planning and device selection.1, 2, 3 Unlike stented or true sutureless valves, the ATS 3f stentless bioprosthesis is characterized by absence of a rigid frame and commissural fixation directly to the aortic wall at the sinotubular junction, using 3 sutures.

This design creates unique challenges during ViV TAVR. In particular, the lack of a defined landing zone and the supra-annular attachment of the commissures substantially increase the risk of coronary obstruction and sinus sequestration, as the degenerated leaflets may be displaced outward and form a tubular structure extending to the sinotubular junction, effectively isolating the coronary sinuses.

Dedicated TAVR devices for pure aortic insufficiency are needed and currently undergoing clinical investigation. At present, the JenaValve Trilogy is the only THV with CE mark approval (European Conformity) for patients with severe AR at high surgical risk. The Trilogy THV features a self-expanding nitinol frame with porcine pericardial leaflets. It has 3 locators that clip onto the native leaflets to improve stability and facilitate commissural alignment. This mechanism is particularly advantageous in stentless valves, where anchoring cannot rely on a rigid surgical frame. Three large open cells promote ease of coronary access. The valve is available in 3 sizes (23, 25, and 27 mm), allowing treatment of aortic annular perimeters between 66 and 90 mm.4

The ALIGN-AR (Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation with the Trilogy Valve for Symptomatic Native Aortic Regurgitation) study, a multicenter, nonblinded, single-arm evaluation of high-risk patients with symptomatic (≥3+) aortic regurgitation undergoing TAVR with the JenaValve Trilogy System, demonstrated a high rate of device success alongside acceptable complication rates, including a 0% procedural death rate, 1.4% 30-day mortality, 1.6% valve embolization, and 0.8% 30-day disabling stroke.5

In a meta-analysis, dedicated THVs for aortic regurgitation significantly outperformed off-label new-generation devices, yielding higher procedural success rates and lower rates of permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI), greater than mild PVL, and 30-day mortality.6

In our case, the use of a dedicated THV allowed favorable procedural and hemodynamic outcomes through several mechanisms. The clipping system secured the prosthesis to the leaflets of the degenerated valve, which reduced the risk of embolization and, by pulling the valve leaflets inward, created a sealing cushion that minimized the risk of PVL. Concurrently, this traction of the valve leaflets prevented their outward prolapse, mitigating the risk of coronary obstruction. Furthermore, the delivery system's capability for horizontal rotation allowed for the optimization of commissural alignment and coaxial deployment, avoiding misalignment, a factor known to alter fluid flow patterns, increase leaflet stress, and generate nonphysiological vorticity that can promote blood stagnation within the sinus of Valsalva, improving coronary flow physiology and long-term valve durability.7

Our patient developed a third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating PPI. This can be considered the Achilles’ heel of dedicated devices for pure AR.5 We should emphasize that patients with pure AR often present with preexisting conduction disturbances, as ours did. The need for PPI is a recognized challenge in treating pure AR, irrespective of the modality, due to intrinsic changes in the heart conduction system induced by the pathology. The recent meta-analysis showed comparable PPI rates after transcatheter therapy with dedicated devices, comparable to surgical aortic valve replacement.6

Compared with conventional off-label TAVR devices designed for aortic stenosis (AS), the JenaValve Trilogy System offers fundamental anchoring advantages in pure AR and stentless ViV. AS-designed valves rely on annular calcium and radial force; in pure AR, their absence predisposes to embolization, paravalvular leak, and need for a second valve—risks compounded in stentless valves by the lack of a rigid prosthetic scaffold. The PANTHEON (Performance of Currently Available traNscaTHEter Aortic Valve ON Platforms in Inoperable Patients With Pure Aortic RegurgitatiON of a Native Valve) registry reported a device embolization rate of 13.6% and device success of only 83.6% with off-label valves in native AR.8 The Trilogy's locator-based leaflet-clipping mechanism provides annular-independent anchoring uniquely suited to this anatomy. A meta-analysis of 2,162 patients confirmed superior device success (93% vs 82%), lower 30-day mortality (3% vs 9%), fewer embolizations (2% vs 8%), and lower residual AR (2% vs 5%) with dedicated vs off-label devices.6 Furthermore, integrated delivery system rotation enables commissural alignment unavailable with AS valves,7 and annulus-perimeter–based sizing avoids the aggressive oversizing often required with AS devices, reducing conduction system injury.

Based on our experience and published technical guidance,9 we propose the following key technical considerations for the JenaValve Trilogy System implantation within a stentless bioprosthesis. First, locators must be advanced with extreme caution—stentless leaflets are thin and unsupported, making malpositioning and leaflet injury genuine risks; fluoroscopic confirmation in orthogonal views with pigtail catheters marking the cusp nadirs is strongly recommended before deployment. Second, meticulous CT preplanning is essential to define implant depth, as the absence of a radiopaque frame removes the usual fluoroscopic landmarks. Third, careful coaxial centering before locator exposure is critical, because root kinking or dilatation may deviate the catheter off-axis. Fourth, the team must rehearse emergent deployment capability, as abrupt hemodynamic collapse during locator engagement is a realistic risk in severe AR without calcific anchoring, as illustrated in our case. Finally, rapid ventricular pacing duration should be minimized to avoid precipitating cardiovascular collapse in a hemodynamically compromised patient.

Conclusions

This case provides the first evidence that a dedicated transcatheter valve (JenaValve) can successfully treat pure regurgitation in a failing ATS 3f (model 1000) stentless aortic bioprosthesis, a scenario associated with a high risk of coronary obstruction and device instability when treated with conventional ViV strategies.
