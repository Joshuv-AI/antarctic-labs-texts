Magnetic resonance imaging-based biodistribution of theranostic AGuIX nanoparticles in the NANORAD 2 clinical trial for brain metastases
Authors: Fabien Boux, Alexis Mercier, Daniela Talba, Jean-Yves Giraud, Sandrine Dufort, Khalide Seddik, Sylvie Grand, Mélanie Gataleta, Alexandre Leboucher, Johan Pietras, Olivier De Beaumont, Géraldine Le Duc, Camille Verry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAGuIX nanoparticles are promising theranostic agents that selectively accumulate in brain metastases via the enhanced permeability and retention effect, potentially improving radiotherapy efficacy. This ancillary analysis of the NANORAD2 trial investigates AGuIX biodistribution in patients with multiple brain metastases undergoing whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT).MethodsSignal enhancement (SE) maps, quantifying the relative increase in T1-weighted MRI signal intensity due to AGuIX accumulation, were generated from pre- and post-injection scans of 11 patients receiving three weekly AGuIX injections (100 mg/kg) prior to radiotherapy. SE values were measured in 14 regions of interest at one hour post-injection in all patients (n = 11), at four hours in a subset (n = 4) to assess early clearance, and at one hour after the third injection (n = 7) to evaluate long-term accumulation.ResultsAt one hour post-first injection, full-volume brain metastases showed marked SE elevation to 54.1 ± 29.5% (mean and standard deviation, n = 208 metastases), while surrounding organs exhibited transient SE (especially pituitary gland) that declined at four hours (all SE values < limit of detection, n = 11). At four hours, mean metastatic SE decreased from 33.8 ± 18.4% to 18.8 ± 16.6% (n = 54, p < 10− 10), indicating partial clearance. After three weekly injections, mean metastatic SE at one hour remained stable comparing to first injection (from 63.0% to 64.8%, n = 132), demonstrating complete inter-dose clearance with no cumulative accumulation detectable by MRI.ConclusionThis study confirms selective AGuIX accumulation in brain metastases with favorable clearance kinetics, supporting radiotherapy delivery four hours post-injection to optimize therapeutic ratio while minimizing off-target exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11060-026-05468-9.

Introduction

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone of cancer treatment, used with curative or palliative intent in a large proportion of patients. Technological advances, including image-guided and intensity-modulated techniques, have improved dose conformity and reduced toxicities [1]. Nevertheless, when tumors lie close to radiosensitive organs or in reirradiation settings, dose constraints may limit the prescribed dose and compromise local control, especially in radioresistant tumors [2].

Two complementary strategies have emerged to improve the therapeutic ratio of radiotherapy: (i) technological innovation to better target the tumor, such as MRI-guided radiotherapy and robust treatment planning [1, 3], and (ii) biological optimization using radiosensitizers to increase tumor response to X-rays [4, 5]. Metallic nanoparticles are attractive radiosensitizers because their high atomic number increases local dose deposition via the photoelectric effect, generating secondary photons and electrons that enhance tumor cell kill [2, 6]. It is well known that under clinical megavoltage (MV) photon beams (commonly used in external beam radiotherapy and whole-brain radiotherapy, WBRT) Compton interactions prevail and the magnitude of purely physical macroscopic dose enhancement is substantially reduced. However, several studies have shown efficacy of nanoparticles although measurable radiosensitization is not systematically observed under MV beams [7]. Gold nanoparticles were the first to demonstrate this concept in vivo [8]. Among nanoparticles currently in clinical development, NBTXR3 (hafnium-based, ~ 50 nm) is injected intratumorally [9], whereas AGuIX for “Activated guided irradiation by X-ray” (gadolinium-based, ~ 5 nm) is administered intravenously and is being evaluated in several phase II trials [10–12].

AGuIX nanoparticles combine radiosensitizing and imaging properties, defining a theranostic platform. Their small size promotes tumor accumulation within the interstitium and at the cellular and subcellular levels [10, 13], mainly through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [12]. Their gadolinium core provides T1-weighted MRI contrast, enabling non-invasive monitoring of biodistribution. Early-phase trials have shown that AGuIX is safe and can produce marked MRI enhancement of brain metastases and other tumors [10–12, 14, 15]. A first-in-human trial in brain metastases (NANO-RAD) reported strong and persistent contrast in metastases up to several days post-injection in a subset of patients [10, 11]. More recently, AGuIX has been investigated in additional indications, including glioblastoma in the NANO-GBM trial [12].

NANORAD2 (NCT03818386) is a randomized multicenter phase II trial evaluating WBRT with or without AGuIX in patients with multiple brain metastases. In this setting, the large irradiated volume raises concern about neurocognitive toxicity, and any off-target nanoparticle retention is particularly relevant. Precise knowledge of AGuIX biodistribution in the brain and surrounding organs is therefore essential to optimize scheduling, maximize tumor radiosensitization and limit adverse effects.

Previous AGuIX studies mainly focused on qualitative MRI assessment or on individual metastases [10, 12, 15]. The present ancillary study of NANORAD2 provides a quantitative and regional analysis of AGuIX biodistribution using SE maps derived from T1-weighted MRI. Our objectives were: (i) to characterize AGuIX distribution across 14 predefined ROIs encompassing brain metastases, healthy brain regions and organs adjacent to the irradiated volume; (ii) to describe early kinetic changes between 1 and 4 h after the first injection; and (iii) to evaluate potential accumulation after three weekly injections. This work aims to refine our understanding of AGuIX pharmacokinetics in patients with brain metastases and to inform optimal timing of WBRT.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This ancillary imaging study was embedded in the NANORAD2 trial and approved by the appropriate ethics committee and health authorities. All patients provided written informed consent. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Eleven patients with multiple brain metastases enrolled at the coordinating center (University Hospital of Grenoble) were included. The cohort exhibited clinical heterogeneity typical of patients with brain metastases, with primary tumors including mainly lung cancer (n = 8), breast cancer, melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma (n = 1 each), and metastasis counts ranging from 3 to 69 per patient (detailed characteristics in Supplementary Table S1). These patients were treated with WBRT to 30 Gy in 10 fractions over 2 weeks combined with three intravenous injections of AGuIX at 100 mg/kg.

The first AGuIX injection was administered 2–7 days before the start of WBRT. The second and third injections were given at the beginning of weeks 1 and 2 of WBRT, 3–5 h before the first and sixth fractions, respectively, according to the trial protocol. A schematic of the treatment and imaging schedule is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

MRI acquisition schedule

All patients underwent a baseline MRI without contrast within 2 weeks before treatment start. To characterize AGuIX biodistribution and clearance dynamics, MRI acquisitions were performed at strategic timepoints: all eleven patients (n = 11) were imaged at 1 h post-first injection to assess overall biodistribution patterns across brain regions and metastases. A subset of four patients (n = 4) underwent additional imaging at 4 h post-first injection to investigate early clearance kinetics. To evaluate potential long-term accumulation after repeated weekly administrations, seven patients (n = 7) were imaged at 1 h post-third injection (approximately 2 weeks after the first injection), in line with earlier observations of prolonged detectability of AGuIX on MRI [10]. The differential patient numbers at each timepoint reflect the ancillary nature of this biodistribution study, with imaging sessions added to the main trial protocol based on patient consent and logistical feasibility at the coordinating center.

The recorded delay corresponds to the interval between the end of AGuIX injection and the start of the MRI sequence. Median delays were 29 min for the first post-injection MRI (“1 h post first injection”), 249 min (4.1 h) for the second session (“4 h post first injection”) and 23 min for the third session (“1 h post third injection”). Individual timing details are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

MRI protocol and SE maps

MRI was performed on a 3-Tesla Philips scanner using a 3D turbo spin-echo T1-weighted sequence acquired with identical parameters at all time points (repetition time 600 ms, echo time 29.3 ms, echo train length 22, flip angle 90°, field of view 240 × 240 mm², slice thickness 1 mm, matrix 480 × 480 × 350 and voxel size 0.5 × 0.5 × 1 mm³).

Before quantification, all images were co-registered using rigid transformation via Advanced Normalization Tools software (https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTs), with the MRI used for ROI delineation serving as the reference. This procedure allows the same ROIs to be applied across all images. A key aspect of this approach is the normalization process. Normalization is necessary to correct for potential differences in MRI acquisitions that could affect signal intensity (several days separate the acquisitions). By normalizing images before calculating the relative error, we ensure that observed differences are genuinely attributable to the effect of the contrast agent, rather than technical or methodological variations. Typically, this normalization is performed using a reference region or tissue that is not expected to show enhancement. In this study, the choice of white matter as a normalization reference is supported by converging physiological and preclinical evidence. Healthy white matter combines the lowest vascular volume fraction among brain regions (2.7% vs. 5.2% for gray matter) with the lowest cerebral blood flow (~ 20 vs. ~ 80 mL/100 g/min) [17], minimizing even transient intravascular signal contribution. Additionally, the intact blood–brain barrier prevents extravasation of AGuIX nanoparticles into the parenchyma. Consistent with this dual mechanism, preclinical biodistribution studies using radiolabeled AGuIX showed less than 0.15% of the injected dose in organs other than kidneys and bladder, with gadolinium undetectable in healthy brain tissue [16, 18].

Regions of interest

AGuIX distribution was assessed in 14 ROIs: carotid artery, venous sinus, scalp, bone, vitreous humor, muscle, pituitary gland, basal ganglia, brainstem, ventricle, gray matter, white matter, hippocampus and brain metastasis (the one with the most extensive signal enhancement). ROIs were delineated on the 1-hour post-first-injection T1-weighted images by an experienced physician and validated by a senior radiologist. For each anatomical structure, regions with homogeneous and representative signal characteristics were selected to ensure reliable quantification. The number of MRI slices included in each ROI varied across patients based on operator judgment and anatomical coverage. Volumes depended on anatomical size; mean volumes and standard deviations are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

To enable comprehensive lesion-level analysis, additional contours encompassing the complete volume of each individual brain metastasis were independently delineated by an experienced scientist using semi-automated segmentation tools. These metastasis-specific contours allowed detailed characterization of intra-lesional nanoparticle distribution heterogeneity.

SE quantification and statistics

For each ROI and each time point, mean SE was extracted. To distinguish true enhancement from noise, a limit of detection (LOD) was defined. Noise standard deviation was estimated in a region outside the patient on both reference and post-contrast images, assuming Gaussian noise. Propagation of noise through SE computation yielded an SE uncertainty value. The threshold for the LOD was set at two times the propagated standard deviation, ensuring that any signal change exceeding this threshold could be confidently attributed to the presence of AGuIX nanoparticles. Conversely, changes below this threshold were considered likely due to acquisition noise, which, under the assumption of Gaussian noise distribution, corresponds to a probability of less than 5%. For further interpretation, contrast gain was defined as occurring when the first quartile (Q1) of SE values exceeded the LOD, while no contrast gain was assumed when the third quartile (Q3) fell below the LOD.

Paired comparisons of SE between 1 and 4 h after the first injection and between 1 h after the first and third injections were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses focused on descriptive distributions and within-patient changes due to the limited sample size.

Across patients, SE maps consistently showed high enhancement in brain metastases and low or undetectable enhancement in most healthy brain regions. Outside the brain, several tissues exhibited visible enhancement, consistent with the systemic distribution of AGuIX during its plasma circulation phase (mean half-life about 1.4 h [12]), with enhancement magnitude reflecting regional blood volume.

AGuIX distribution in brain regions and metastases

Quantitative SE analysis in brain ROIs is summarized in Fig. 3 (11 patients). One hour after the first injection, SE in all healthy brain regions remained below the LOD. In contrast, brain metastases showed marked enhancement, with a median SE of 78.1% (IQR 51.5–108.7; n = 11, p = 0.12), indicating substantial nanoparticle accumulation driven by the EPR effect [11]. At 4 h post-injection, median SE in metastases decreased from 57.3% to 30.9% (IQR 27.0–35.6; n = 4), corresponding to approximately half of the initial SE. This decline suggests partial clearance or redistribution of nanoparticles while maintaining detectable levels several hours after injection. SE in healthy brain regions remained below the LOD at 4 h. One hour after the third injection, SE in healthy brain regions was again below the LOD. Median SE in metastases was similar to that observed after the first injection (from 78.1 to 79.6%; n = 7, p = 0.69).

At 1 h after the first injection, mean SE across metastases was 54.1 ± 29.5% (n = 208). This lower mean value compared to the patient-level median (78.1%) reflects the inclusion of complete lesion volumes, encompassing regions with heterogeneous enhancement including areas of lower uptake, whereas the patient-level analysis focused on smaller, highly enhancing representative regions within each metastasis. The distribution was broad, reflecting inter-lesion variability in nanoparticle uptake. Visual inspection of SE maps revealed heterogeneous intra-tumoral patterns, including ring-like enhancement with central minima or, less frequently, central maxima, consistent with previous MRI-based analyses of AGuIX uptake in brain metastases [15]. At 4 h post-injection, mean SE decreased from 33.8 ± 18.4% in paired lesions to 18.8 ± 16.6% (n = 54), about half of the initial value, confirming substantial yet incomplete clearance over this interval (p < 10− 10). After three injections, the distribution of SE remained very close to that observed after a single injection (from 63.0% to 64.8%; n = 132), with no significant difference (p = 0.14), indicating absence of measurable cumulative accumulation at the tissue scale with the weekly administration schedule used in NANORAD2.

No clear relationship between lesion size and SE was identified.

The pituitary gland, one of the most highly vascularized endocrine organs, displayed particularly high enhancement with a median SE of 73.1% (n = 11 patients). Vascular regions also showed elevated SE (median 75.0% in the carotid artery and 178.3% in the venous sinus).

In the subset of 4 patients imaged at 4 h, SE decreased markedly in surrounding organs. In the pituitary gland, median SE dropped from 59.3% to 15.5%, and similar declines were observed in other tissues, indicating rapid clearance or redistribution. These findings are consistent with the reported plasma half-life of AGuIX of approximately 1.4 h [12]. By the time of standard WBRT delivery (around 4 h post-injection), off-target enhancement in most non-tumor tissues was therefore substantially reduced.

After the third injection, SE in surrounding organs at 1 h remained comparable to that observed after the first injection, and no progressive increase was detected. This suggests efficient systemic elimination of AGuIX between weekly administrations and supports a favorable safety profile with respect to off-target accumulation.

Discussion

This ancillary analysis of NANORAD2 provides a quantitative description of AGuIX biodistribution in patients with multiple brain metastases treated with WBRT. Using SE maps derived from standardized T1-weighted MRI, we show that AGuIX selectively accumulates in brain metastases, with minimal enhancement in healthy brain structures and transient uptake in surrounding organs.

A first key finding is the high SE in metastases 1 h after injection, followed by a substantial but incomplete decrease at 4 h. This kinetic profile suggests that nanoparticles rapidly reach metastatic tissue and then progressively clear or redistribute while remaining detectable several hours after administration. These observations align with preclinical data and earlier clinical reports that described early and intense enhancement in brain metastases and other tumors after AGuIX administration [10–12, 15]. In the context of NANORAD2, they support the current practice of delivering WBRT approximately 4 h after AGuIX injection to coincide with persistent tumor enhancement and reduced systemic exposure.

A second important observation is the limited enhancement of healthy brain regions at all time points, reflecting preservation of the blood–brain barrier and confirming tumor specificity driven by the EPR effect [19]. Surrounding organs such as the pituitary gland and vasculature showed strong but transient SE, with rapid decline between 1 and 4 h. This pattern is consistent with the short plasma half-life of AGuIX reported in NANO-GBM and other studies [12] and suggests that off-target radiosensitization should be limited when radiotherapy is delivered at 4 h.

Contrary to previous smaller studies that suggested cumulative tissue retention after repeated AGuIX administrations [10], we did not observe a significant increase in SE after three weekly injections. Several factors may explain this discrepancy, including differences in imaging schedules, small sample sizes in both studies and challenges in detecting small residual enhancements near the LOD. While preclinical and translational work has demonstrated cellular and subcellular internalization of AGuIX and associated biological effects [10, 13], our results indicate that, at the tissue scale and with the dosing schedule used here, any long-term accumulation is below the sensitivity of our MRI-based approach. Further studies combining imaging with biological assays are needed to clarify the relationship between intracellular retention, MRI signal and radiosensitizing efficacy.

Our study also highlights marked inter- and intra-lesion heterogeneity in SE, in line with previous descriptions of diverse enhancement patterns in brain metastases [15]. Such heterogeneity may reflect differences in vascular permeability, interstitial pressure, necrosis or prior treatments. Understanding how these factors influence nanoparticle uptake and radiosensitization could help select patients most likely to benefit from AGuIX-based approaches and potentially guide adaptive treatment strategies.

Standard gadolinium-based contrast agents are designed for diagnostic imaging with rapid diffusion and renal clearance. They do not exhibit tumor retention at time scales relevant for radiotherapy delivery. In contrast, our results confirm that AGuIX nanoparticles selectively accumulate in brain metastases and remain detectable several hours after injection—a key prerequisite for theranostic application. Importantly, MRI-visible accumulation enables non-invasive quantification of nanoparticle distribution, potentially supporting individualized radiotherapy scheduling, patient stratification, and correlation between nanoparticle uptake and treatment response.

This work has limitations. The cohort was small, particularly for the 4-hour and third-injection time points, limiting statistical power and precluding robust subgroup analyses. Variability in exact imaging delays, despite being carefully recorded, may have introduced additional dispersion in SE measurements. ROI delineation and image registration are subject to minor uncertainties, especially in small structures such as vascular ROIs. Finally, the choice of white matter as a normalization reference assumes absence of enhancement in this region; while supported by our results, small undetected changes cannot be entirely excluded, and no phantom-based validation with known gadolinium concentrations was performed to further anchor absolute LOD values. Moreover, our study should not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinically meaningful physical dose amplification under MV irradiation. Rather, it demonstrates selective tumor accumulation and favorable clearance kinetics—essential prerequisites for potential radioenhancement effects. Recent preclinical and clinical investigations suggest that mechanisms beyond macroscopic physical dose enhancement, including nanoscale energy deposition and biological amplification processes, may contribute to AGuIX efficacy under MV irradiation [11, 12, 20]. Further correlative studies integrating dosimetry, biological endpoints, and clinical outcomes are needed to fully characterize radioenhancement mechanisms in MV settings.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide clinically relevant insights into AGuIX pharmacokinetics in patients with brain metastases and support ongoing development of this theranostic agent. In particular, the significant decline in SE observed in surrounding organs at 4 h, while metastatic enhancement persisted, suggests that delayed radiotherapy delivery could optimize the therapeutic ratio. Future work should include larger cohorts, standardized imaging schedules and integration of dosimetric and clinical outcome data to fully assess the impact of AGuIX on tumor control, neurocognitive function and survival. Trials exploring alternative schedules or additional injections, such as NANO-GBM [12], will be particularly informative.

Conclusion

AGuIX nanoparticles show preferential and robust accumulation in brain metastases with minimal enhancement of healthy brain tissue and transient uptake in surrounding organs. Metastatic SE decreases but remains detectable 4 h after injection, and no MRI-visible accumulation is observed after three weekly administrations. These results support the use of AGuIX as a theranostic radioenhancer in WBRT and validate delivering radiotherapy around 4 h post-injection to optimize the balance between tumor targeting and safety.
