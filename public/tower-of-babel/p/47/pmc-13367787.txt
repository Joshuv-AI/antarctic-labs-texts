Late Pleistocene Clovis atlatl hunting fails a chronological modeling test
Authors: Metin I. Eren, Michelle R. Bebber, Robert S. Walker, C. Reagan Johnson, Briggs Buchanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Late Pleistocene North American foragers assigned to the Clovis culture have long been assumed to have hunted megafauna with the atlatl, which would have provided several advantages. Yet, we chronologically modeled radiocarbon ages from preserved Holocene atlatls and show that Clovis atlatl use is not supported. If Clovis hunters did not use atlatl technology, then its emergence in the Americas during the early Holocene represents a case of technological convergent evolution analogous to atlatls in Late Pleistocene Europe. A further implication is if Clovis hunters used spears, javelins, or bow technology then models concerning megafaunal hunting need to be rethought given the distinct effectiveness, hunting risk, and tactics associated with these weapons.

Late Pleistocene human foragers assigned to the Clovis culture [13,340-12,710 calBP (calibrated years before present)] likely preyed upon a variety of species, including large mammals (1, 2). Clovis-style artifacts have been found in association with the bones of proboscideans (Mammuthus columbi, Mammut americanum, Cuvieronius sp.), camel (Camelops hesternus), horse (Equus conversidens), and bison (Bison antiquus) (3, 4). While current archaeological evidence cannot distinguish whether any single site with an animal–artifact association is due to hunting or scavenging (3), most would agree that at least some of these animals were hunted.

Archaeologists have long assumed that Clovis foragers used the atlatl and dart to hunt megafauna (5) and “most now consider atlatls the primary projectile weapon Paleoindians used in the Americas” (6). This weapon system consists of a handheld, rod-shaped device with a “hook” at one end, which is used to propel a lightweight, flexible spear (i.e., the dart) via leverage (7). Relative to the presumed weapon systems from which it was derived—the thrusting spear and javelin—an atlatl-launched dart achieves faster velocities and longer ranges, which would have increased Clovis hunter safety and decreased the chance of injury while hunting big game (8–11). Atlatl use would also have served as an “equalizer,” or a way for weaker individuals and those at great risk to bolster their throwing ability and thus partake in hunting activities (8, 12), increasing both tactical options and chances of hitting the animal.

While atlatl use would have given Clovis individuals those advantages, there is no hard evidence of Clovis atlatl use, such as preserved specimens (13). A few alleged atlatl “hooks” have been found and claimed to be of that antiquity (14), but they are in poor context, remain undated, and may have nothing to do with either atlatl use or Clovis culture.

Here, we address whether atlatls were used in Clovis times by applying optimal linear estimation (OLE) to radiocarbon ages from preserved atlatls (Dataset S1). OLE is a statistical technique used to infer the timing of species’ arrival and extinctions from the distribution of observed occurrences and is now regularly used to reconstruct origination and cessation dates for artifact phenomena (15).

Results

The 10 oldest directly dated atlatl weapons systems range in age from about 9,300 to 6,100 y ago (Fig. 1). These samples are mostly found in the Yukon with two specimens found in Texas and Coahuila. The bootstrapped OLE for the first appearance of the atlatl is 9,996 y ago. The latest date for Clovis is 12,710 calBP, and several dated Clovis megafaunal sites date earlier (Fig. 2). Thus, while the archaeological association of Clovis artifacts with megafauna skeletal remains is consistent with the hypothesis that megafauna were occasionally targeted by Clovis hunters, our results do not support the long-held assumption that they used the atlatl and dart to accomplish that task.

Discussion

Our model concerns the probability of observing preserved atlatl evidence within the Clovis age range. Our results demonstrate the improbability of making that observation. When these are taken together with the current lack of archaeological evidence for Clovis atlatls, we conclude that Clovis megafauna hunting with atlatls should not be assumed, and Holocene use of the atlatl cannot currently be used to infer Clovis atlatl-use. Barring the empirical discovery of a directly dated, unequivocal atlatl in clear Clovis contexts, our results highlight the possibility that Clovis hunters did not use the atlatl. Given prevailing archaeological conventional wisdom, this latter suggestion may at first seem unorthodox, but less so when considering that, to our knowledge, there is currently no direct evidence for the atlatl in the form of preserved specimens in Pleistocene Asia, Africa, or Australia. One interpretation of our results and current archaeological evidence is that atlatl technology was not carried over from the Old World to the New during the Peopling of the Americas. Instead, the atlatl and dart weapon system in the Americas represents a case of technological convergent evolution emerging during the Early Holocene that is analogous to, not derived from, atlatl technology in Upper Paleolithic Europe (16).

By questioning the long-standing presumption that Clovis hunters used the atlatl, or only used the atlatl, to hunt megafauna, our results emphasize that archaeologists should more regularly consider other possible weapon systems, such as thrusting spears, javelins, or bow technology, which would have substantially altered weapon effectiveness, hunting risk, and tactics (8–12, 16, 17). Thrusting spear and javelin use would have made Clovis big game hunting more dangerous but also more effective in some ways. Javelins and especially thrusting spears would have required closer hunting distances, increasing the risk of hunter injury or death by frightened megafauna. For small Clovis forager bands, the risk of losing even one individual may have tempered the frequency of hunting megafauna. But modern experiments suggest that javelins and thrusting spears can deliver kinetic impact energies much higher than those of atlatl darts (17). This additional power may have facilitated a kill when megafaunal hunting was deemed necessary enough to risk it. And these weapons are less complex to produce than atlatl technology.

Archaeological evidence suggests bow technology was converged upon several times in prehistory (18) and thus would have been within Clovis forager capacity. Clovis bow use could have offered benefits such as increased projectile velocity, accuracy, and distance, but costs in terms of more complex production and less frequent fatal shots requiring megafauna prey tracking (16, 17). Yet, in western North America, archaeological evidence suggests the bow replaces the atlatl around 1,400 BP (18). This does not preclude the possibility of Clovis bow-use but entails a scenario in which bow technology was lost post-Clovis and then reinvented in late prehistory.

Finally, our chronological modeling not only fails to support Clovis atlatl-use but also atlatl-use by post-Clovis Pleistocene Paleoindian cultures, including Folsom [12,780 to 12,380 calBP (1, 19)]. This may come as a surprise given that small, thin, full-faced fluted Folsom points appear to “fit” narrow, flexible atlatl darts. But it is possible that Folsom points were hafted onto thrusting spears, javelins, or arrows (20). And Folsom forager javelin or bow use may help explain why many (the majority) of Folsom bison kills are in topographic settings where the prey were located below the hunters (Dataset S1). Unlike atlatl darts, which experiments suggest may not function optimally when a hunter shoots downward, javelins increase in kinetic impact energy as launch height increases (21). Shooting downward with a bow would not likely be problematic (16). Moreover, javelin-launching and arrow-shooting “from above” could have partially offset the inherent risks of big game hunting.

Materials and Methods

OLE infers the first occurrence time from a temporal distribution of sightings or, in our case, the first appearance of atlatls in western North America. OLE is also known as the Weibull extreme-value model. This is because OLE assumes that the joint distribution of the tail of the distribution (commonly the 10 most extreme points in a distribution) follows a Weibull extreme value distribution. This assumption is used to estimate the distribution’s shape parameter from the temporal spread of the observed dates, making the observed times between dated events crucial to the estimation.

We collected radiocarbon dates with errors for 66 directly dated archaeological atlatl or dart specimens and calibrated them using the rcarbon package in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team) with the IntCal20 calibration curve (20). We sample from the probability density functions of each specimen’s calibrated years before present (cal.BP) for the OLE analysis. The dated samples come from western North America, primarily from a small region in the Yukon and Alaska and in a larger area covering northern Mexico, the Southwest, and California (Fig. 3). The full dataset is available in the Supporting Datasets and the R-script is available in SI Appendix.

Locations for the 66 atlatl and dart radiocarbon-dated weapon systems used in this study. The size of each circle corresponds to the calibrated calendar date in years before present (cal.BP).

We ran the OLE function in the sExtinct package in R and bootstrapped 10,000 OLE estimates for the origin of the atlatl. We randomly sampled a single calendar date from each calibrated probability distribution for each bootstrap. Hence, the 66 atlatl specimens in the sample would yield 66 sampled calendar dates per run. Of these, the 10 oldest calendar dates are used to generate an OLE date, and this experiment is repeated 10,000 times, always taking the 10 oldest specimens in the sample. The OLE function generates a distribution of median appearance dates. This bootstrapped distribution is approximately normal, and we use its 95% prediction interval to represent the range of calendar dates for the first appearance of the atlatl in North America.
