Network meta-analysis of exercise modalities for their effects on micro-inflammation in patients undergoing hemodialysis
Authors: LongKe Xu, XinYu Wang, XiaoXia Cai, YuanFeng Lv, LiXiang Ye, XiaoShan Wang, Li Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsExercise can effectively ameliorate the micro-inflammatory state in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), but the optimal intervention modality remains unclear. This study systematically compared the anti-inflammatory efficacy of different exercise modalities by focusing on evaluating the levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).MethodsA systematic search was conducted across the CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, China Biology Medicine disc (CBMdisc), PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases to identify randomized controlled trials investigating exercise interventions for micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients, from database inception to November 16, 2024. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was employed to assess the methodological quality of the included studies. Data were analyzed using Stata 16.0 and Review Manager 5.3 software.ResultsA total of 41 RCTs involving 12 intervention modalities and 2939 patients were ultimately included. The network meta-analysis results demonstrated that, compared with usual care (UC), intradialytic resistance exercise combined with non-dialytic aerobic exercise (IRE + NAE) [SMD = − 1.78, 95% CI (− 3.06, − 0.5)] showed the most promising effect in improving CRP levels (SUCRA = 89.1%). Intradialytic aerobic exercise combined with non-dialytic aerobic exercise (IAE + NAE) [SMD = − 1.43, 95% CI (− 2.71, − 0.14)] exhibited the most promising efficacy in reducing IL-6 levels (SUCRA = 85.9%). Non-dialytic aerobic exercise (NAE) [SMD = − 2.77, 95% CI (− 4.37, − 1.16)] was most promising in lowering TNF-α levels (SUCRA = 95.8%).ConclusionsDifferent exercise interventions showed varying effects on ameliorating the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients. Based on comprehensive evaluation of three biomarkers (CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α), intradialytic aerobic exercise combined with non-dialytic aerobic exercise (IAE + NAE) demonstrated the most promising anti-inflammatory effects, with non-dialytic exercise modalities generally superior to intradialytic interventions. These findings require further validation through higher-quality studies. This network meta-analysis has been registered on PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420250653330).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03280-1.

Introduction

The micro-inflammatory state is defined as a condition in which patients exhibit no obvious clinical manifestations of infection, but demonstrate low-grade yet persistent elevation of inflammatory proteins and inflammatory cytokines in systemic circulation [1]. This condition is not caused by pathogenic microorganisms but represents an immune-mediated inflammation. Its characteristic features include sustained elevation of inflammatory cytokines, such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), C-reactive protein (CRP), serum amyloid A, interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [2]. Among patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), the prevalence of this condition ranges from 54 to 75% [3]. It serves as a critical risk factor for severe complications including cardiovascular diseases, anemia, malnutrition, and sleep-cognitive disorders, significantly impairing patients’ quality of life and long-term survival rates [4, 5].

The decline in glomerular filtration rate in end-stage renal disease leads to the accumulation of metabolic waste products, including endotoxins, pro-inflammatory cytokines, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and oxidized protein products [6]. These substances persistently stimulate the secretion of inflammatory cytokines. Concurrently, the elevated inflammatory cytokines further promote the liver to synthesize large amounts of acute-phase reactive proteins, increasing the renal clearance burden and forming a vicious cycle that is difficult to break [7]. Among various inflammatory markers, CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α have become key indicators for assessing micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients due to their high correlation with the degree of inflammation, and are widely used in clinical research [8]. Notably, the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients is closely associated with Th1/Th2 inflammatory cytokine imbalance. Studies have shown that these patients exhibit a predominant Th1-type immune response, characterized by excessive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-α), while Th2-type anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-4 and IL-10) are relatively insufficient [9].

Given the significant negative impact of the micro-inflammatory state on patients’ prognosis, exploring effective interventions to break this vicious cycle is of urgent clinical importance. Effectively alleviating the micro-inflammatory state cannot only directly reduce the risk of related complications, but also significantly improve patients’ quality of life and overall prognosis.

Among various intervention strategies, exercise therapy has attracted widespread attention due to its accessibility and efficacy [10, 11]. The mechanisms by which exercise alleviates micro-inflammation are multifaceted. To be specific, exercise can: effectively reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine levels while elevating anti-inflammatory cytokine levels [12]; enhance antioxidant enzyme activity, mitigate oxidative stress damage, and help regulate immune function balance, thereby reducing chronic immune activation [13]; improve insulin resistance, reduce visceral fat accumulation, and promote vascular endothelial function, synergistically alleviating micro-inflammation through metabolic and vascular pathways [14]. Furthermore, exercise has been demonstrated to modulate Th1/Th2 balance: moderate-intensity exercise can suppress hyperactive Th1 responses (decreasing the IFN-γ/IL-4 ratio) while stimulating Th2 cell differentiation through mechanisms such as myokine IL-6, thereby promoting the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 and ultimately correcting immune imbalance [15].

In recent years, virtual reality (VR)-based exercise interventions have been increasingly applied in MHD patients. VR technology effectively improves patient exercise adherence through immersive experiences and engaging designs [15]. Preliminary studies suggest that VR exercise not only modulates autonomic nervous function and reduces oxidative stress, but also decreases levels of inflammatory markers, providing a novel intervention option for MHD patients with mobility limitations or low exercise motivation [16, 17]. Currently, the primary exercise modalities for MHD patients include aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and combined aerobic-resistance exercise. Based on the timing of implementation, these can be categorized into intradialytic exercise (performed at the bedside/chairside during the first 2–3 h of dialysis treatment) and non-dialytic exercise (performed on non-hemodialysis days). Notably, in related populations such as type 2 diabetes patients, concurrent exercise training—defined as performing both aerobic and resistance exercises within the same session—has demonstrated significant benefits for inflammation control, metabolic regulation, and cardiopulmonary fitness [18]. These findings support the potential utility of multimodal exercise approaches for populations with systemic inflammation, particularly MHD patients.

However, existing studies are mostly limited to pairwise comparisons between a few exercise modalities. Traditional meta-analysis methods are inadequate for systematically evaluating and comparing the relative efficacy of diverse exercise interventions in ameliorating the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients within a unified analytical framework, nor can they rank the therapeutic effectiveness of these interventions. Consequently, the lack of high-quality evidence regarding the “optimal exercise regimen” hinders the ability to provide precise guidance for developing individualized exercise prescriptions. To address this gap, the present study aims to systematically review existing literature and employ network meta-analysis (NMA) to directly and indirectly compare the effects of different exercise interventions on micro-inflammatory markers in MHD patients within a single framework. This approach will assess their relative efficacy and generate rankings. The findings are expected to provide high-quality evidence for clinicians to identify the optimal exercise regimen for ameliorating the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients, thereby guiding the formulation of individualized exercise prescriptions and ultimately enhancing patients’ long-term health outcomes.

Methods

Search strategy

Two investigators (Xu Longke and Wang Xinyu) independently conducted a systematic literature search, with data extraction performed through cross-verification. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third investigator (Lyu Yuanfeng). The search covered the following databases: CNKI, VIP, Wanfang, China Biology Medicine disc (CBMdisc), PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library, with the search period spanning from the inception of each database to November 16, 2024. The search strategy was constructed using a combination of subject headings and free-text words related to hemodialysis, exercise, and micro-inflammation (see Supplementary Materials, Methods S1 and S2).

Study selection

Inclusion criteria were based on the PICOS (Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes, and Study Design) framework: (a) participants: MHD patients aged ≥ 18 years, receiving hemodialysis for ≥ 3 months with a regular dialysis schedule of 2–3 sessions per week; (b) interventions: the intervention group receiving any form of exercise intervention; (c) comparators: the control group receiving either UC or a distinct exercise intervention regimen from the intervention group; (d) outcomes: assessment of pre- to post-intervention changes in at least one inflammatory cytokine (CRP, IL-6, or TNF-α); (e) study design: randomized controlled trials. The exclusion criteria included: (a) studies without full-text availability (e.g., dissertations, conference abstracts); (b) studies with irrecoverable outcome data after contacting the original authors; (c) duplicate publications; (d) non-English/Chinese publications; (e) studies rated as “Grade C” in the final quality assessment.

Data selection and extraction

All searched literature records were imported into NoteExpress (Chinese reference management software) for organization, with duplicate records removed initially. Two investigators (Xu Longke and Wang Xinyu) independently screened the studies according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, followed by data extraction from the included studies. The extracted data encompassed: (1) first author, publication year, and country; (2) baseline characteristics of the participants, interventions, and control conditions; (3) assessment results of relevant inflammatory cytokines; and (4) methodological quality information of the studies. Any discrepancies during the screening and extraction process were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third investigator (Lyu Yuanfeng).

Quality assessment

Two investigators (Xu Longke and Wang Xinyu) independently applied the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool to assess the risk of bias in included studies. The assessment covered seven domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other bias. Each item was rated as “high risk”, “low risk”, or “unclear risk”. Given the nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants and intervention providers is generally infeasible; thus, this domain was anticipated to have a high risk of performance bias that might lead to overestimated effect sizes. The overall study quality grading criteria referenced established methods [19]. To be specific, studies were classified as: high quality, if no domains were rated as high risk and the number of unclear risks was ≤ 3; moderate quality, if one domain was high risk, or if no domains were high risk but unclear risks numbered ≥ 4; and low quality in all other scenarios. Discrepancies in ratings were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third investigator (Lyu Yuanfeng). Due to the inherent nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants and intervention providers was generally infeasible, resulting in a high risk of performance bias for all studies in the domain of “blinding of participants and personnel”. However, all primary outcomes in this study were objective laboratory-measured data, the quantification of which was unaffected by investigators’ or participants’ subjective judgments. Consequently, although performance bias existed, its impact on the evaluation of objective inflammatory markers was considered limited. Under this premise, studies maintaining low risk across all other bias domains with ≤ 3 unclear risks could still be classified as high quality.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata 16.0 (StataCorp LP, USA) and Review Manager 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, UK). To minimize the impact of baseline differences, this analysis primarily pooled the mean changes and standard deviations (SDs) from pre- to post-intervention values. The transformation of SD changes was calculated using the formulas specified in the Cochrane Handbook (version 6.3) [20]. The statistical procedures adhered to the PRISMA guidelines for network meta-analysis (NMA) [21]. Under the frequentist framework, a random-effects model was employed to pool effect sizes, with standardized mean differences (SMDs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated (given potential inconsistencies in inflammatory cytokine units across studies). A network geometry plot was generated to visually present the evidence relationships among different exercise interventions through direct comparisons. If closed loops were present in the network, consistency tests were performed [22]. Global and local inconsistency tests were conducted to compare the consistency between direct and indirect comparisons. A consistency model was applied when P > 0.05 (indicating good global consistency between direct and indirect comparisons), otherwise an inconsistency model was used [23]. Node-splitting analysis was employed for local inconsistency tests, with P < 0.05 suggesting local inconsistency between direct and indirect comparisons. The surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated to rank and compare the efficacy of different exercise interventions in improving target inflammatory markers [24]. SUCRA values range from 0 to 100%, where 100% represents the optimal intervention effect and 0 indicates the worst effect [25]. To verify the robustness of primary findings, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed while simultaneously exploring potential sources of heterogeneity. Finally, publication bias and small-study effects were assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots complemented by Egger’s test.

Results

Literature search results

The initial search identified 4697 potentially relevant articles. After screening titles and abstracts, 3023 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Following full-text review of the remaining articles, additional 108 articles were excluded, resulting in 41 articles of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) eligible for network meta-analysis. Characteristics of included studies

The 41 RCTs included 12 distinct exercise interventions, involving a total of 2939 MHD patients (57.87% male, 42.13% female). Among these studies, CRP outcomes were reported in 29 studies (intervention group: n = 1106; control group: n = 973); IL-6 outcomes in 27 studies (intervention group: n = 1031; control group: n = 983); and TNF-α outcomes in 21 studies (intervention group: n = 849; control group: n = 801. The basic characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1, and the definitions of the 12 exercise interventions are provided in Table 2.

Risk of bias

All included studies had a low risk of selective reporting bias. Thirty-one studies specified randomization methods, while only 4 reported adequate allocation concealment, with the remainder categorized as “unclear risk”. Blinding procedures for participants and investigators were inadequately reported in most studies. To address this limitation, we explicitly documented the prevalence of non-blinding and corresponding risk of bias levels in quality assessments, emphasized this issue in the discussion of limitations and its potential to overestimate effect sizes, interpreted results cautiously regarding effect sizes and rankings, stressed relative rather than absolute efficacy, and identified blinding implementation as a key methodological priority for future studies. Since all outcomes were objective laboratory-measured data, the risk of detection bias was generally low. Seven studies showed high risk of bias for incomplete outcome data due to a > 20% dropout rate. Quality assessment results indicated 30 studies as Grade A (high quality) and 11 as Grade B (moderate quality), with detailed assessment results presented in Fig. 2. Consistency test results

Global inconsistency tests (for closed loops) and node-splitting analyses were performed to evaluate consistency between direct and indirect evidence. The loop inconsistency test results demonstrated that all closed loops for CRP showed statistically insignificant inconsistency (P > 0.05), therefore the consistency model was applied for analysis (see Supplementary Materials, Figure S1). For IL-6, the closed loop originated exclusively from a single three-arm clinical trial, which inherently precluded the need for formal inconsistency tests; consequently, the consistency model was adopted directly for analysis. No closed loops existed for TNF-α, thereby allowing direct application of the consistency model for analysis. Node-splitting analysis results demonstrated high consistency between direct and indirect evidence for all three outcomes (P > 0.05), indicating robust result reliability (see Supplementary Materials, Table S1).

Pooled effect size analysis and ranking results

The SUCRA-based rankings of different exercise interventions for improving CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α levels are summarized in Table 3. For CRP levels, all nine exercise intervention modalities demonstrated statistically significant differences compared with UC (Fig. 4a). Among them, IRE + NAE (SUCRA = 89.1%) and IAE + NAE (SUCRA = 77.1%) exhibited the most promising effects. Regarding IL-6 levels, nine exercise intervention modalities showed statistically significant improvements over UC (Fig. 4b). The most promising modalities were IAE + NAE (SUCRA = 85.9%) and NRE (SUCRA = 70.1%). Notably, IVR (SUCRA = 50.7%) outperformed UC but was less effective than combined exercise regimens. For TNF-α levels, six exercise intervention modalities achieved statistical significance versus UC (Fig. 4c), with NAE (SUCRA = 95.8%) and IAE + NAE (SUCRA = 81.9%) ranking as the two most promising modalities. The SUCRA values reflect the relative ranking rather than absolute efficacy magnitude among the analyzed interventions. Ranging from 0 to 100%, a SUCRA value represents the probability of an intervention being the “optimal regimen”. However, it should be noted that in terms of clinically meaningful differences, interventions with SUCRA differences < 10% generally lack clinically meaningful differences in ranking; and these rankings are strictly based on the currently included studies and cannot be extrapolated to other non-included interventions.

Discussion

Using network meta-analysis, this study systematically compared the effects of 12 exercise interventions on micro-inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α) in MHD patients. Exercise interventions are an effective strategy for ameliorating the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients, and different exercise intervention regimens show varying effects. IAE + NAE consistently ranked among the top two most effective interventions across all biomarkers: 2nd for CRP reduction, 1 st for IL-6 reduction, and 2nd for TNF-α reduction. This positions IAE + NAE as the most promising exercise regimen for ameliorating the micro-inflammatory state. Notably, non-dialytic exercises generally demonstrated superior effects to intradialytic exercises. The underlying mechanisms may involve the following pathways. First, intradialytic aerobic exercise (IAE) capitalizes on the hemodialysis time window by implementing low- to moderate-intensity aerobic exercises, which improve arterial stiffness and vascular endothelial function [32, 33], while simultaneously modulating the immune system and upregulating the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10) [34]. Second, the increased blood flow during intradialytic exercise enhances dialysis adequacy and promotes uremic toxin clearance, thereby further suppressing inflammatory responses [35]. On the other hand, non-dialytic aerobic exercise (NAE) exerts its effects through optimizing metabolism and immune function, thereby attenuating insulin resistance and disordered lipid metabolism, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion from adipocytes [36], enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity to mitigate oxidative stress, and consolidating the anti-inflammatory effects [37]. The combination of these two modalities not only enhances the cumulative effects of exercise and achieves dynamic modulation of inflammatory markers, but also establishes a more potent comprehensive anti-inflammatory effect through targeted interventions at different time periods. Furthermore, non-dialytic exercises can be flexibly tailored to individual patient characteristics (e.g., physical capacity, recovery status), thereby enhancing exercise adherence and long-term compliance [38].

However, several practical constraints must be addressed when implementing these exercise regimens in clinical practice. Intradialytic exercise faces key challenges regarding exercise device accessibility and spatial limitations in dialysis units. Not all dialysis centers are equipped with specialized, bedside-compatible exercise devices (e.g., stationary bicycles, resistance band racks), and the management, sterilization, and maintenance of such devices may impose additional operational burdens [39]. Non-dialytic exercise is primarily limited by patients’ time constraints and post-dialysis energy expenditure. MHD patients typically undergo dialysis three times weekly (approximately four hours per session). Coupled with disease-related burdens and complications (e.g., fatigue, anemia), they often face multiple barriers to regular exercise on non-dialysis days, including physical exhaustion, high transportation time costs, and competing work/family responsibilities, all of which reduce the feasibility and adherence to exercise regimens [40]. Consequently, when formulating individualized exercise prescriptions, clinicians must holistically balance three dimensions of interventions: theoretical efficacy, patient-specific conditions (environmental, resource, and motivational factors), and safety.

CRP is an acute-phase reactant and predictor of cardiovascular events, and its elevated levels positively correlate with both the incidence of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in MHD patients [41]. Aerobic exercise significantly reduces CRP levels through enhancing antioxidant capacity, improving endothelial function, and regulating lipid metabolism [42]. This study demonstrated that all included exercise modalities effectively reduced CRP levels, with IRE + NAE showing the most promising results. However, Ferrari et al. [43] reported that resistance exercise alone was the most effective modality for CRP reduction in MHD patients. This discrepancy may arise from the following factors: (1) differences in exercise timing classification methods: our study strictly differentiated between intradialytic and non-dialytic interventions, whereas Ferrari et al. did not categorize exercise timing; (2) we analyzed 29 CRP-reporting RCTs compared to 15 trials in Ferrari et al.’s study, providing more stable effect estimates; (3) Ferrari et al. utilized a Bayesian analytical approach in which the specified prior distributions could attenuate small-study effects, whereas our study applied frequentist random-effects models that implemented more conservative adjustments for between-study heterogeneity.

IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine that plays pivotal roles in immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and cell differentiation [44]. Aerobic exercise reduces metabolically dysregulated IL-6 levels through stimulating anti-inflammatory cytokine release, suppressing the pro-inflammatory effects of IL-6, and decreasing IL-6 secretion from both adipose tissue and skeletal muscle [45, 46]. As demonstrated in this study, the exercise modalities that could significantly lower IL-6 levels in MHD patients were IAE + NAE, NRE, IIME + IAE, NAE, IAE, ICE, IVR, and IRE. Among them, IAE + NAE emerged as the most promising regimen. Notably, while IVR showed inferior IL-6-reducing effects to combined exercise regimens, its effect remained statistically superior to UC. This may involve neuro-immune regulation: IVR suppresses IL-6 synthesis by enhancing vagal tone and inhibiting sympathetic overactivation [47]; furthermore, it attenuates pain and anxiety through cognitive distraction, thereby reducing psychological stress [48]. This makes IVR a space-efficient and adherence-friendly alternative for patients with mobility limitations or poor adherence to conventional exercises. However, Ren et al. [49] reported no significant IL-6 reduction with exercise, which may be attributed to the following factors: (1) in this study, IL-6 was analyzed as a secondary outcome (vs. primary endpoints of exercise capacity and quality of life), potentially limiting the analytical depth; (2) only four IL-6-reporting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, resulting in a limited sample size and compromising the statistical power and reliability of results; (3) this study also failed to differentiate between intradialytic and non-dialytic exercise interventions, which may have obscured time-dependent intervention effects.

TNF-α is a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a core role in inflammation, apoptosis, and immune system development [50, 51]. Aerobic exercise reduces TNF-α levels by enhancing the body’s antioxidant capacity to decrease oxidative stress-induced immune cell activation, while improving endothelial function to reduce TNF-α release from endothelial cells [52]; it also suppresses TNF-α secretion in adipose tissue by regulating lipid metabolism [53]. As demonstrated in this study, the exercise modalities that could significantly reduce TNF-α levels in MHD patients were NAE, IAE + NAE, NRE, IRE + NAE, IAE, and IRE. Among them, NAE emerged as the most promising regimen.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths: This study provides the first network meta-analysis to differentiate the effects of intradialytic versus non-dialytic exercises on the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients, yielding findings with greater credibility and clinical guidance value. Its comprehensive search strategy encompassed eight major databases, maximizing the inclusion of relevant studies on exercise interventions for the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients. The findings offer direct evidence to guide the development of individualized exercise prescriptions for MHD patients and optimize clinical practice pathways.

Limitations: (1) The inclusion of only Chinese and English literature may have resulted in incomplete coverage of relevant studies; (2) subgroup analyses for potential effect factors such as age, dialysis vintage, or geographic region were not performed; (3) patient characteristics (age, comorbidities, dialysis duration), timing of outcome measurements, and laboratory assay methodologies for inflammatory markers may cause confounding effects, and heterogeneity may arise due to different patient adherence levels; (4) the special nature of exercise interventions precluded participant blinding, potentially introducing placebo effects and bias in findings.

Conclusions

This network meta-analysis systematically evaluated the effects of different exercise interventions on the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients. The findings demonstrated significant variations in the efficacy of exercise modalities for ameliorating the levels of inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α). SUCRA-based rankings indicated IRE + NAE, IAE + NAE, and NAE as the most promising interventions for reducing CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α levels, respectively. When comprehensively evaluating all three biomarkers, IAE + NAE was identified as the most promising combined exercise regimen for ameliorating the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients. Furthermore, non-dialytic exercises generally demonstrated superior anti-inflammatory effects to intradialytic exercises. Based on these findings, and incorporating the evidence on IVR (immersive virtual reality) exercise’s effects of ameliorating inflammatory markers and its unique neuromodulatory mechanisms, this study proposes the following strategies: (1) priority should be given to promoting the IAE + NAE regimen, which requires dialysis centers to provide specialized devices and intradialytic guidance while encouraging regular aerobic exercise on non-dialysis days; (2) in settings where combined regimens are unfeasible, NAE should be prioritized or IAE implemented in dialysis centers with adequate clinical resources; (3) all exercise regimens must be strictly individualized through comprehensive patient assessment to ensure safety and feasibility; (4) for patients with mobility limitations or poor adherence to conventional exercises, IVR is recommended as a valuable alternative due to its space-efficient nature and potential compliance advantages—future studies should delve into the optimized application of VR exercises in MHD populations; (5) structured exercise interventions should be formally incorporated into clinical guidelines and treatment pathways at dialysis centers for micro-inflammation management in MHD patients. These evidence-based exercise strategies are expected to effectively alleviate the micro-inflammatory state in MHD patients, reduce the risk of related complications, and ultimately improve prognosis.
