Intestinal parasitic co-infections associated with Helicobacter pylori among paediatric patients with gastrointestinal illness attending a general hospital in southern Ethiopia
Authors: Addis Aklilu, Melat Woldemariam, Edilu Wanke, Mohammed Seid, Aseer Manilal, Javed Masood Khan, Idhayadhulla Akbar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConcurrent infections or co-infections caused by intestinal parasites and Helicobacter pylori are quite rampant in paediatrics living in endemic areas of sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, and if left untreated, can result in severe complications and hence must be addressed to ensure their health and well-being.ObjectivesTo determine the prevalence of intestinal parasitic and H. pylori co-infections and associated factors among paediatric patients with gastrointestinal symptoms who attended the Arba Minch General Hospital (AMGH), Arba Minch, southern Ethiopia, from September to November 2020.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among a study population of 299 paediatric patients with gastrointestinal symptoms who visited AMGH. Stool samples were collected and analysed to detect H. pylori and intestinal parasites. A rapid lateral flow chromatographic immunoassay was employed to identify the H. pylori copra antigen, whereas the latter was detected using wet mount saline preparation and formol-ether concentration method. Socio-demographic, clinical, behavioural and other factors were obtained by means of a pre-tested structured questionnaire. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analysis were done by Statistical Package for Social Service (SPSS) version 25; P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsThe prevalence of Helicobacter pylori and intestinal parasites was 14% (n = 42) and 37.1% (n = 111), respectively, whereas that of the co-infections with these pathogens was 6.4% (n = 19). Giardia lamblia was the most prevailing parasite, 21.4% (n = 64). Informal maternal education [AOR = 5.14; 95% CI: 1.98–15.70] and lack of hand washing practice were significantly associated with the extent of co-infections [AOR = 4.18; 95% CI: 1.36–12.80].ConclusionNearly one in twenty pediatric patients with gastrointestinal symptoms had intestinal parasitic infections and H. pylori co-infections, representing a silent health problem that is to be addressed through effective control strategies. Health administrators should consider the importance of co-infections in clinical diagnosis and planning aimed at its prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05026-0.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori is a causative agent in gastric and extra-gastric diseases, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. It is one of the most common bacterial pathogens in humans, with an estimated 4.4 billion cases worldwide, including paediatric patients, and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths [2]. The global prevalence of H. pylori infection over the past three decades was 48.9% in adults and 32.3% in children/adolescents, showing substantial heterogeneity [3, 4]. The prevalence of H. pylori remains significant among children and adolescents in most of the World Health Organization regions [3].

Children can acquire this organism from parents or peers, leading to the pathogenesis of peptic ulcers, notably duodenal ulcers, and almost all of them with this disease have tested positive for the bacterium. Growing quanta of evidence suggests that acquiring an H. pylori infection in childhood is a significant risk factor connected to the onset of gastric cancer, such as adenocarcinoma [5]. The incidence and prevalence rates of childhood infection with H. pylori vary significantly in Africa, reflecting the role of various factors like compromised immune system, low socio-economic, educational status, consumption of contaminated water, as well as the presence of H. pylori infection existing in the family [6]. Dyspepsia is the illness prevailing among Ethiopians, including children visiting outpatient departments of health facilities, and it has been reliably correlated with H. pylori infection [7]. The prevalence of H pylori infection varied considerably across countries and the highest is in the African continent (41.3% in children) [3]. A systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the overall pooled prevalence of H. pylori infection in Ethiopia as 52.2% (95% CI: 45.8–58.6) [8]. Gastric cancer is the 13th most common disease and the 10th most common cause of death in the country [9]. In Ethiopia, the latest reported prevalence of H. pylori infection in children is 49.3% [3].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has enlisted parasitic intestinal infections as neglected tropical diseases, and an estimated 3.5 billion (24% of the world population) globally are afflicted with 450 million cases of symptomatic illness and an annual mortality rate of over 0.2 million [10]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, it is estimated that up to 250 million are infected with at least any one species of intestinal parasite [10]. Intestinal parasitic infections are widespread globally and continue to be the leading cause of human morbidity and mortality, especially in children, due to poor sanitation and hygiene. As per the report of WHO, soil-transmitted helminthic infections are the second leading cause of mortality in children below six years in the African continent [11].

It is reported that over 267 million preschool-aged children and approximately 568 million school-aged children live in areas where these parasites are transmitted intensively [12]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that the prevalence of intestinal protozoan parasites among African school children was 25.8% [13]. In Ethiopia, 25.3 million schoolchildren live in situations where they can contract intestinal parasites easily, and hence, a high probability of H. pylori co-infections exists. Intestinal parasitic infections are highly prevalent among children, ranging from 66.7 to 83.8% in the country and have been correlated with adverse outcomes, including anaemia, malnutrition, stunting, thinness and other mental and physical health problems [14].

The importance of poly-microbial infections has increased enormously in recent years, and synergistic infections have also been identified; co-infections involving several species of gastrointestinal parasites are commonly found in endemic areas of developing countries and are particularly common in young people [15]. The presence of intestinal parasites in children is detrimental, and the consequences include tissue damage, decreased nutrient absorption, malnutrition, or even immune deficiency. Colonisation by H. pylori and intestinal parasites is widespread, and it is a fact that their co-existence mainly occurs in low-income developing countries, which could be linked pathologically [15]; H. pylori shares some associated characteristics of intestinal parasites and has the same transmission route [9]. Both groups share similar predisposing factors, including poor sanitation/hygiene, low socio-economic status, and overcrowding [10]. A previous comprehensive review showed that co-infections by intestinal parasites and H. pylori can be correlated to several factors of immunological severe consequences [16].

In several cross-sectional studies, the pooled prevalence of co-infections with H. pylori and intestinal parasites in different populations was around 11% (95% Confidence interval: 6.7–17.6%) [17]. A literature scan indicated that H. pylori and intestinal parasitic co-infections range from 10.6 to 51.4% among the African population [6, 15, 18–20]. The overall burden was twice as high in sub-Saharan Africa due to the low economic status of the region and inferior hygienic practices [6].

In addition, the co-infections with H. pylori in children were between 23 and 30% in Ethiopia [15, 19]. A closer look at the literature reveals that co-infections involving H. pylori and intestinal parasites are also endemic in many regions of the country, but this fact is ignored. Most of the studies done nationwide in Ethiopia so far only focused on infections caused by either H. pylori or intestinal parasites. If the co-infection is not adequately diagnosed or treated promptly, it can become severe. One of the primary obstacles in effectively implementing public health measures to address the problems of co-infections is the inadequacy of comprehensive and current data. Therefore, studies on prevalence and risk factors associated with co-infections involving intestinal parasites and H. pylori are relevant in the context of combating the menace in a specific study area. According to AMGH clinical reports, intestinal infections are currently cited as one of the top reasons for consultation. The present study was designed to determine the prevalence of co-infections by intestinal parasites and H. pylori, as well as the associated factors existing among paediatric patients with gastrointestinal illness who were subjected to stool examination at AMGH.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted at AMGH (Fig. 1) from September 01 to November 30, 2020, which is located in the Gamo Zone [21]. It currently serves more than 1.5 million annually, providing preventive, rehabilitative, and curative care through multiple departments [22]. Approximately 25,000 children receive different healthcare services from this hospital yearly [22]. In addition, there exist a primary hospital and two health centres in the study area. In total, there are eight government and eleven private primary schools in Arba Minch. According to a report on the health and demographic surveillance system, the total population of the town is 74,157, of whom 20,062 (27.1%) are aged between 6 and 14 years [23].

Study population

The study population comprised paediatric patients (less than 15 years) with gastrointestinal symptoms and clinically suspected of intestinal parasitic infections, who attended the outpatient departments of AMGH during the study period as per the inclusion criteria mentioned below.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants selected for the study are aged below 15 years, who attended the outpatient departments during the above study period, were clinically diagnosed by the physician based on gastrointestinal complaint and have requested a stool examination.

Exclusion criteria

The paediatric patients who had received antibiotics and anti-parasitic drugs, proton pump inhibitors, and drugs containing bismuth compounds four weeks before the commencement of the study were exempted.

Sampling technique

A systematic random sampling technique was used to include study participants who met the inclusion criteria until the required sample size was reached. The sampling interval was derived by dividing the total number of target patients by the sample size. The 𝐾th value was inferred by the N/n formula (N is the number of patients who visited the hospital in the same period, and n is the sample size) from which the daily number of patients who availed the stool examination facilities in the hospital during the study period and was found to be 2. The first study participant was selected by the lottery method, and subsequently, all 2nd paediatric patients were recruited into the study.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome of this study is the prevalence of co-infections with Helicobacter pylori and intestinal parasites. The secondary outcome includes the identification of independent factors that influence the co-infections via analysis.

Independent variables

Independent variables considered in this study include socio-demographic status, clinical history, and hygiene related factors mentioned below.

Data collection

A pre-tested structured questionnaire was developed [4, 8, 13, 15] and used to accumulate the data. Face-to-face interviews with the parents/guardians of each participant were conducted with the help of well-trained health professionals. Relevant details concerning the socio-demographic status (age, sex, maternal education, occupation, residence and monthly income), hygiene-related issues (latrine utilisation and hand-washing practices) and clinical factors (intestinal parasitic infections encountered during the previous four-month period, history of epigastric pain and treatment measures taken for intestinal parasitic infections) were solicited (Supplementary Table 1).

Specimen collections, transportation and processing

The paediatric patients and or guardians were instructed to place the stool samples directly into a plastic cup with a tight-fitting lid, following standard collection and transportation protocols [24]. Participants were also instructed to wash their hands with soap and water after collecting the sample. Approximately 20–40 g of stool samples were collected into a clean, dry, wide-mouth sterile plastic container labelled with an identification code and collection date. The stool samples were then transported to the Medical Microbiology and Parasitology Laboratory of the Department of Medical Laboratory Science.

Detection and identification of parasites

Stool specimens obtained from all participants were processed without delay. They were examined for the presence of all developmental stages of parasites such as cysts, oocysts, eggs, trophozoites, larvae and adults by direct wet mount method using physiological saline and a formol–ether concentration technique as per the WHO guidelines [24]. Specimens were considered positive even if one or more helminth eggs, larvae, cysts, or protozoan trophozoites were detected [25]. All examinations were done twice by two experienced microbiologists, independently.

Helicobacter pylori Ag determination

The stool samples were tested for the presence of Helicobacter pylori copra antigen by means of a rapid lateral flow chromatographic immunoassay (Guangzhou Wondfo® Biotech Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China). This qualitative test has a sensitivity of 99.1% and a specificity of 99.2%. The Wondfo One Step H. pylori faeces test (Cassette) uses a combination of H. pylori antibodies coated particles and anti-mouse IgG to detect H. pylori antigens in just a few minutes. Using a sampling stick, stool samples were transferred to a sample collection tube prefilled with assay diluent solution and mixed well by gently shaking for a few minutes. Afterwards, 80µL of sample solution was added to the loading window of the cassette. After an incubation period of 15 min, the result was read off in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The presence of rose-pink bands, both in control and test regions, was defined as a positive result, whereas the presence of rose-pink band only in the control region indicates a negative result (i.e., the concentration of H. pylori stool antigens in the sample is below the detectable level).

Data quality assurance

A pre-test of the structured questionnaire was carried out on 5% of the sample size at the Seikala Health Centre, Arba Minch. Before collecting the actual data and samples, adequate training was provided to the data collectors and laboratory technicians (who assisted in the sample collection). Data were checked daily for accuracy, clarity, and completeness, and any incompleteness and errors found were immediately rectified. The quality of the H. pylori copra antigen testing kit was assessed through a quality control test using a known positive sample, to maintain the quality, standard operating procedures (in-house SOP manual) were followed during collection (aseptic technique), transportation, and processing.

Operational definition

Gastrointestinal complaints: Paediatric patients clinically suspected of any of the following manifestations (upper abdominal pain, abdominal bloating, nausea, vomiting, early satiety, heartburn, regurgitation, epigastric pain/burning, and belching occurring at least thrice a week) by paediatricians.

Hand washing practices: Washing hands with soap, always after toileting and before meals.

Co-infections of intestinal parasite and H. pylori: A positive result for both H. pylori and any intestinal parasitic infection.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were coded, cleaned, and entered using Epi-info version 7.2 and then exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for further analysis. The IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) was used for data analysis, and descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean, and standard deviations, were used to describe the study participants. The dependent variable in the analysis was co-infections of H. pylori and intestinal parasites. In order to assess associated factors, a bivariable logistic regression analysis was done, and to control the probable confounding effect, only variables with P ≤ 0.25 values in the former analysis were included in the final model, i.e., multivariable logistic regression analysis. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness fit test was employed to assess the fitness of the model. The strength of association was determined using adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), and statistical significance was assigned only when P values ≤ 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic, clinical and hygiene-related factors

A total of 299 paediatric patients with gastrointestinal symptoms who met the inclusion criteria took part in the study with a 100% response rate. The mean age was 8.1 ± 2.5 years; 62.2% (n = 186) were aged between 5 and 9 years; there was female preponderance, 52.5% (n = 157) and 87.3% (n = 261) were urban dwellers. Most of the maternal parents, 80.9% (n = 242), had a formal education, and more than half of them, 51.2% (n = 153), were homemakers. Details of the socio-demographic data of participants are shown in Table 1. Less than half of the paediatric patients with gastrointestinal symptoms had the habit of hand washing (44.8%, n = 134), and 91.3% (n = 273) had the habit of using a latrine. In regard to the history of illness, 12% (n = 36) and 28.4% (n = 85) of clients had experienced parasitic intestinal infections and epigastric pain, respectively (Table 1).

Distribution of intestinal parasites, Helicobacter pylori and co-infections

A total of 37.1% (n = 112) of the paediatric patients were found to be infected with at least any one intestinal parasite. Giardia lamblia was the most commonly isolated parasite (21.7%, n = 65), followed by E. histolytica / dispar (27%, n = 9). Helicobacter pylori was detected in 14% (n = 42) of the patients. Of the 299 paediatric patients tested for H. pylori and intestinal parasites, 6.4% (n = 19) were co-infected (Table 2). The most prevailing co-infections observed were due to the pair H. pylori and G. lamblia (Fig. 2).

Co-infections and associated factors

Bivariable analysis was utilised to examine the association between intestinal parasites and Helicobacter pylori co-infections and each of the determinant factors. The results of bivariable logistic regression analysis indicate that the co-infection was significantly associated with the sex [P value 0.13, Crude Odds Ratio = 2.54; 95% Confidence Interval: 0.94–6.86], informal maternal education [P value 0.02, COR = 4.35; 95% CI: 1.68–11.28], lack of hand-washing practices [P value 0.06, COR = 2.22; 95% CI: 1.04–7.60], history of infection with intestinal parasites [P value 0.21, COR = 2.07; 95% CI: 0.04–5.26], and epigastric pain [P value 0.004, COR = 1.92; 95% CI: 0.20–1.35] (Table 3). However, in the multivariable logistic analysis, only informal maternal education and lack of hand-washing practices were significantly associated (P value ≤ 0.05) with a higher prevalence of co-infections. It was observed that paediatric patients having parents without formal education were found to be five times more likely to be co-infected than their peers [P value 0.001, Adjusted Odds Ratio = 5.14; 95% CI: 1.98–15.70], also those without hand-washing practices were 4.1 times more prone to co-infections than their counterparts [P value 0.011, AOR = 4.18; 95% CI: 1.36–12.80].

Discussions

Gastrointestinal infectious diseases are prevalent in developing countries, and Ethiopia is of no exception. Despite the control measures in place, the magnitude and impact of Helicobacter pylori and intestinal parasitic infections and co-infection are on the rise. This study provides some basic information on the individual prevalence of H. pylori and intestinal parasitic infections and also the co-infections prevailing among paediatric patients attending AMGH. Our results revealed that intestinal parasites and co-infections involving them and H. pylori are growing threats to paediatric patients attending the study setting.

The prevalence of these parasites varies across regions, even within a country. It is more commonly found in zones and countries characterised by insufficient access to clean drinking water and inadequate sewage disposal systems. We found that the rate of H. pylori infection was 14% (n = 42) and was higher than the results (10.4%) of a study done in Butajira among a group of 6.5-year-old Ethiopian children [26] and also Lebanon (5.4%) [27]. In contrast to these, another earlier study conducted in the capital city of Ethiopia reported a much higher regional prevalence of 36.8% [28], whereas reports from Nigeria (33.77%) [20], Egypt (42%) [29], and Turkey (29%) [30] showed some variations in the prevalence. Likewise, a couple of studies done in Butajira, Ethiopia, found that 41 and 44% of 3 and 5-year-old paediatric patients were infected with H. pylori, respectively [31]. A comparatively lower prevalence of H. pylori observed in our study could be explained by the fact that only symptomatic patients were included.

These observed discrepancies could stem from fluctuations in socio-economic status, environmental exposure, sample size, hygiene practised by participants and other risk factors existing across geographic locations. In a nutshell, the prevalence observed in our study is lower than the global average in children (32.6%) [4]. This may be due to improvements in sanitation, access to quality water sources, behavioural changes, improved living conditions, increased awareness on the transmission of H. pylori infection, prompt diagnosis, eradication therapy, as well as effective prevention and control schemes [8].

Parasitic infection is a more prevalent problem due to poor sanitary conditions, and it affects society depending on the living conditions of individuals in general, which include several components such as shoe-wearing habits, availability of proper restrooms, and other geographic factors irrespective of the zones or regions. Besides, socio-environmental factors at the family or community levels have also been shown to be critical due to the correlation between intestinal parasites and WASH (water, sanitation, and hygiene) inequalities, parents’ education level, sanitation, and pet health [32].

It is vital to prioritise the routine screening and treatment of parasitic infections in children to prevent complications and to uphold economic productivity and quality of life. The prevalence of intestinal parasites was found to be moderate, i.e., 37.4%, highlighting the necessity of immediate intervention and severe planning for prevention. The results of several studies carried out in a similar group of patients in other parts of the country, such as Ziway (35.7%) [15], Bahir Dar (31.0%) [33], and Gondar (34.2%) [34] point towards the urgency of eradication of intestinal parasites. On the other hand, the condition is considerably better compared to Chencha, a site closer to our study area (81%) [35], Sekota (44.3%), Ethiopia [36] and also Egypt (58.6%) [29] and Nigeria (75.7%) [37]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis done in Ethiopia itself showed that the prevalence of intestinal parasitosis in children of school and preschool age was 48.0% and the situation is region-specific [38] and is comparatively worse than the scenario existing in Hawasa (26.6%) [39], Dessie city (16%) [40], and Rama town (24.4%), Ethiopia [41] as well as Nigeria (21.19%) [20] and Lebanon (6.4%) [27]. Fluctuations observed in our results and those found in previous reports could be due to differences in the perception of symptoms, levels of exposure to risk factors selection criteria, method of diagnosis, study design, and the socio-economic status of the study population.

In the present study, a total of five species of intestinal parasites were detected in varying percentages. Out of them, two were protozoans (G. lamblia and E. histolytica), and the remaining were helminths (A. lumbricoides, H. nana, and T. trichuris). As for the protozoan intestinal flagellate, G. lamblia was the most commonly isolated, i.e., 21.7% (n = 65). The prevalence and the aetiological agents found by us are identical to those reported in recent literature. For instance, a similar type of predominance was observed in previous studies conducted in Ziway [15] and Amhara [19] in various proportions. A study done in Accra, Ghana, also reported the same pattern of predominance [42].

The extent of G. lamblia infection found in our results, i.e., 21.7%, was similar to that published in a number of research done in different parts of Ethiopia, such as Sidama (27.1%) [43], Pawi (26.6%) [44] and Dilla (28%) [45]. However, a systematic and meta-analysis study revealed that E. histolytica / dispar, followed by Giardia spp., were the most predominant parasites among schoolchildren in Africa [13]. The second most predominant protozoan found in the current study was the intestinal amoebae, E. histolytica / dispar, i.e., 9% (n = 27), and this trend resembles the results of a couple of studies performed in Ethiopia [15, 19], even though there are slight variations in percentages, i.e., between 11.3 (n = 49) − 12.9 (n = 47).

Helminthic infections contribute significantly to gastrointestinal diseases. A meta-analysis study performed on Ethiopian children reported an overall prevalence of 33% (95% CI: 28–38%) [38]. In this study, the tiny intestinal nematode, A. lumbricoides, showed its presence, i.e., 3% (n = 9), and the same pattern of the predominance of this nematode in various percentages was reported in a study done in another part of the country; Amhara, 1.1% (n = 4) [19]. In the case of A. lumbricoides, a pooled prevalence of 17% (95% CI: 15–19%) was observed in Ethiopian schoolchildren earlier, hinting that one in every six children in the country lives with Ascariasis [38]. The second most predominant helminth was the intestinal cestode, H. nana, i.e., 2.3% (n = 7), consistent with the percentage reported in a study conducted in Ziway (n = 10; 2.3%) [15]. A recent meta-analysis done in Southern Nations Nationalities and Peoples Regions proved that this region of Ethiopia carries the highest burden of soil helminthiasis and hence can be categorised as a Moderate Risk Zone [38]. The prevalence of intestinal parasitic infections can fluctuate widely due to varying diagnostic criteria, alterations in the characteristics of clinical populations enrolled, and some mismatching factors existing among different study populations. The presence of parasites indicates potential environmental faecal contamination, likely due to poor hygiene and the absence of quality water sources, proper sewage systems, inadequate healthcare, and low levels of public awareness and education [46].

We found that the prevalence of co-infection was 6.4%, which was significantly lower than that reported by studies that were carried out among a similar group of participants in several locations in Ethiopia: Ziway (23%) [15] and Amhara (30.8%) [19] and also from Lebanon (11.8%) [27] and a couple of studies from Nigeria [(10.4%) [20] and (14.1%) [47]]. A study done among Egyptian children also reported a higher co-infection rate (i.e., 43.9%) [18].

The probable reasons for these fluctuations could be linked to the fact that most of the previous studies used a serum antibody test to detect H. pylori, whereas we used the copra antigen test. The latter testing method may underestimate the extent of consolidation of the prevalence of H. pylori and the rate of co-infections. Above all, the observed variations in the co-infection rate could also be attributed to differences in the degree of personal hygiene practised, socio-economic status, and lifestyle of those at risk of infection.

With respect to the combination of organisms associated with co-infections, the majority of H. pylori-positive patients are infected with the protozoan G. lamblia, too. A series of studies have reported a higher frequency of co-infection involving H. pylori and G. lamblia among children [6, 19, 29, 45]. One review article held the view that H. pylori could aid the colonisation of intestinal parasites such as Giardia in the human gastrointestinal tract by producing urease enzymes, helping to overcome gastric acidity [18]. Conversely, a parasitic gastrointestinal infection can affect the inflammatory response to H. pylori [18]. This significant co-existence may indicate that H. pylori could be a risk factor for parasitic intestinal infection or vice versa. The co-existence of intestinal parasites and H. pylori could interact synergistically, leading to serious health outcomes that could be influenced by hosts and environmental factors [18]. These results suggest that children spend most of their time in highly contaminated environments, either connected to their residences or schools.

In regard to the factors analysed, the educational status of the maternal parent was significantly associated with the prevalence of co-infection. Paediatric patients having mothers with informal education were about five times more likely to be co-infected. This finding is consistent with the results of studies conducted in other parts of the country [15] and also Lebanon [27]. However, there exists a sharp contrast in this aspect with respect to previous studies reported from Ethiopia itself [41] and Ghana [42]. This may be due to the fact that enlightened households may have an enhanced awareness of the transmission of infectious diseases and their prevention. Education brings in changes in healthy behaviours at the household level. Likewise, participants with no hand-washing practices were significantly associated with co-infections, which agrees with the findings of a meta-analysis carried out earlier in the country [13]. Our results indicate that transmission of co-infection could stem from the fecal-oral route, possibly due to inadequate hand hygiene practices.

The limitations of this work include a cross-sectional study design with a limited number of participants/sample size, shorter tenure, and the inclusion of only a few variables. The inclusion and exclusion criteria selection may also influence the final result to a certain extent. Serodiagnosis (IgG test), urea breath test or molecular diagnosis in the case of H. pylori were not done, and due to a lack of antigen testing facilities, E. histolytica and E. dispar were not separately identified. Finally, because of the time constraints experienced during stool collection, no attempt was made to examine the influence of seasonality with respect to the infection rate.

Conclusion

This study is the first of its kind describing the prevalence of intestinal parasite infections and Helicobacter pylori, as well as co-infection rates among paediatric patients attending AMGH. The results revealed a comparatively lower prevalence of H. pylori and its co-infections with intestinal parasites in paediatric patients. It was identified that G. lamblia and H. pylori were the most common infections in our study area. Furthermore, informal maternal education and lack of hand-washing practices were identified as independent predictors of co-infections. Therefore, large-scale control programs such as screening, diagnosis, treatment and surveillance, as well as improved hygiene practices, along with health education, linking the potential sources of infection and transmission of H. pylori and intestinal parasites, are essential in the study area. Large-scale community-based cohort studies are imperative to accurately characterise and establish a cause-and-effect relationship between the risk factors and co-infection.
