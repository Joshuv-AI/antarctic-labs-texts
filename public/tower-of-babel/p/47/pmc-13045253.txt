Ultracentrifugation and Ultrafiltration Differentially Alter the Composition and Functionality of the Biomolecular Corona of Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Frederic St‐Denis‐Bissonnette, Hala Halabi, Marybeth Creskey, Melanie Kirkby, Xu Zhang, Dylan Burger, Lisheng Wang, Jessie R Lavoie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile extracellular vesicles (EVs) are increasingly recognized for their diagnostic and therapeutic potential, many unknowns remain. An emerging and important concept in EV biology is the EV biomolecular corona, a dynamic layer of absorbed biomolecules to the EV membrane that influences EV behaviour, cellular uptake, biodistribution and function. In de‐coronisation studies, a key challenge involves identifying effective purification methods to selectively and reliably remove enzymatically digested surface proteins. This study assesses the effectiveness of ultracentrifugation (UC) and ultrafiltration (UF) purification techniques following proteinase K (PK) treatment of pre‐isolated natural killer cell‐derived EVs (NK‐EVs). The efficiency and specificity/selectivity of these purification methods in removing cleaved proteins while preserving EV physical properties (size), biomolecular profile (surface immunophenotyping, cytokine and proteomic profiling) and cytotoxicity against cancer cells were evaluated. While both methods achieve comparable NK‐EV particle recovery, only UF purification selectively removed PK‐cleaved proteins, as depicted in the protein‐to‐particle ratio, biomolecular profiling and NK‐EV cytotoxicity after PK treatment. In contrast, UC purification caused non‐specific corona disruption, dramatically reducing the cytokine payloads and abolishing NK‐EV cytotoxicity. Interestingly, NTA and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (positive staining) analyses showed that de‐coronised EVs were smaller than their untreated counterpart. This finding suggests that PK treatment impacts surface‐associated proteins with different sensitivity to proteolysis, an effect that remained consistent independent of the subsequent purification methods. Collectively, these results highlight UF as the purification method of choice for controlled de‐coronisation studies, potentially supporting the advancements of EV research across multi‐omics, investigatory, and preclinical applications.Keywords: biomolecular corona, extracellular vesicles (EVs), natural killer (NK) cells, proteinase k (PK), purification, ultracentrifugation (UC), ultrafiltration (UF)

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), released by various cell types, are essential mediators of intercellular communication, facilitating the transfer of bioactive molecules like proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (Welsh et al., 2024). Their roles in diagnostics, drug delivery, and therapeutic applications have garnered substantial interest in their biophysical properties and molecular interactions. Among these, the EV biomolecular corona, a dynamic layer of adsorbed biomolecules, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, metabolites, etc., has emerged as a key factor influencing EV uptake, biodistribution, and functionality (Toth et al., 2021, Wolf et al., 2022, Ahsan et al., 2018, Buzas, 2022). This study primarily focuses on the protein composition of the biomolecular corona.

While EVs have membrane proteins embedded in their lipid bilayer (i.e., tetraspanins, integrins), the biomolecular corona focusing primarily on the proteins, refers to those that adsorb onto the surface of the EVs from the surrounding biological environment. The formation of the EV biomolecular corona is driven by a combination of electrostatic forces and affinity‐based interactions (Toth et al., 2021, Cvjetkovic et al., 2016, Palviainen et al., 2020) and is further modulated by the surrounding pH and temperature, which can alter protein folding states (Ahsan et al., 2018, Heidarzadeh et al., 2023). Although early studies investigated this phenomenon without recognizing its significance (Cvjetkovic et al., 2016), the concept was formally introduced in 2020 (Palviainen et al., 2020). The EV biomolecular corona consists of two layers: a stable “hard corona” and a more transient “soft corona” (Ahsan et al., 2018, Palviainen et al., 2020, Milani et al., 2012, Walkey et al., 2012, Blundell et al., 2016). Hard corona layer (e.g., apolipoproteins, complement factors, and immunoglobulins) remain stably associated with the EV surface, whereas soft corona layer (e.g., albumin and fibrinogen) are loosely attached to the EV surface and rapidly exchange with the surrounding milieu. The EV biomolecular corona is inherently complex and highly dynamic, continuously reshaping in response to its microenvironment (Heidarzadeh et al., 2023). A more detailed discussion on the EV biomolecular corona composition is available in a recent review (Heidarzadeh et al., 2023). Much of our understanding of the EV biomolecular corona has been derived from research on the lipid nanoparticle corona, which has established fundamental principles of protein adsorption, corona dynamics, and biological interactions (Palviainen et al., 2020). Understanding the EV biomolecular corona is essential for unravelling the mechanisms underlying EV intercellular communication and characterizing key biophysical and morphological features, such as EV stability (Singh et al., 2023). This knowledge will further aid in the development of EV therapeutics or other EV‐based applications.

The EV biomolecular corona plays a critical role in modulating EV behaviour, including cellular uptake and biodistribution processes (Toth et al., 2021, Wolf et al., 2022, Heidarzadeh et al., 2023, Liam‐Or et al., 2024). While the EV biomolecular corona is primarily influenced by its surrounding environment, EV isolation and purification methods can also contribute to its composition (Toth et al., 2021, Wolf et al., 2022). For instance, using FBS during EV production introduces xenogeneic material that very likely integrates into the biomolecular corona, raising safety and regulatory concerns for therapeutic/commercial applications. Despite its importance, research on the EV biomolecular corona is still in its early stages, as it has not been a major focus in most studies to date. Although some early EV biomolecular corona‐focused studies have shed some light on its broader implications on EV biology, methods for studying and manipulating its compositions (e.g., removing or altering the corona) are still under development. For example, the 2021 study by Toth et al. showed that EVs in blood plasma acquire a biomolecular corona that alters their density and bioactivity, challenging the notion of plasma proteins as mere contaminants in EV research (Toth et al., 2021). Similarly, a 2022 study showed that EVs from placental‐expanded stromal cells form a functional biomolecular corona enriched with immunomodulatory and proangiogenic proteins. This corona was found to enhance angiogenesis and skin regeneration, while its removal disrupts these effects, demonstrating the biomolecular corona's critical role in EV bioactivity (Wolf et al., 2022). More recently, the 2024 study by Liam‐Or et al. revealed that the biomolecular corona influences EV uptake and in vivo distribution. Specifically, albumin‐enriched coronas enhance liver cell targeting while reducing phagocyte clearance, offering a potential strategy to improve EV‐based liver therapies (Liam‐Or et al., 2024). Another study demonstrated how the combination of complementary and orthogonal particle‐sizing techniques yields structural insights into the EV biomolecular corona, going beyond what each method can capture individually (Varga et al., 2020).

A key challenge in biomolecular corona studies is identifying effective purification methods that selectively and reliably remove enzymatically cleaved proteins during the de‐coronisation process while preserving the structural integrity (closed physical membrane architecture and spherical morphology) and functionality of untreated EVs. Although the EV biomolecular corona is often examined after enzymatic treatment, the optimal purification methods for removing cleaved proteins without compromising the EV characteristics remain uncertain. While ultracentrifugation (UC) has historically been the standard purification method in corona studies, particularly with synthetic nanoparticles, alternative purification methods remain underexplored for studying the EV biomolecular corona (Toth et al., 2021, Wolf et al., 2022, Palviainen et al., 2020, Liam‐Or et al., 2024, Dietz et al., 2023). More specifically, UC isolates EVs based on size and density differences by applying high centrifugal force, separating them from smaller contaminants due to differences in sedimentation rates. A review of published studies suggests that the most commonly used post‐treatment purification methods following enzymatic treatment after UC are tangential flow filtration (TFF) and ultrafiltration (UF), sometimes referred to as “column‐based separation” (Wolf et al., 2022, Cvjetkovic et al., 2016). While TFF has merit for de‐coronisation studies (i.e., gentle filtration process), this method often requires large sample volumes, making it less practical when sample quantities are limited (i.e. in this current study, 0.5 mL was processed; a volume not suitable for TFF). In contrast, UF and UC are better suited for limited sample volumes. UF uses membrane filters to separate particles by size, retaining EVs while allowing smaller cleaved proteins to pass through (for reference, TFF functions on a similar principle). Despite these options, the optimal method for removing enzymatically cleaved proteins from de‐coronised EVs while preserving their structural integrity and characteristics has yet to be determined. Importantly, control EVs (untreated with enzymes) should maintain their functionality following purification to ensure the removal process is specific to enzymatic cleavage.

Among enzymatic treatments, proteinase K (PK), a broad‐spectrum serine protease, is commonly employed for disrupting the EV biomolecular corona due to its ability to cleave a wide range of surface‐accessible proteins (Cvjetkovic et al., 2016, Ebeling et al., 1974, Skliar et al., 2018). PK cleaves peptide bonds adjacent to the carboxyl groups of aliphatic and aromatic amino acids, particularly targeting hydrophobic amino acids like phenylalanine, methionine, leucine, and tyrosine. Importantly, PK effectively degrades these proteins while preserving the structural integrity of the EV lipid bilayer, as it cannot penetrate the EV membrane (Cvjetkovic et al., 2016, Ebeling et al., 1974, Skliar et al., 2018). In this context, preservation of EV structural integrity specifically denotes maintaining a closed lipid bilayer and the three‐dimensional spherical morphology, without signs of rupture or collapse.

Natural killer cell‐derived EVs (NK‐EVs) were used as an EV model in this study. They were manufactured from the NK92‐MI cells using a clinically relevant large‐scale biomanufacturing workflow with extensive vesicle characterization provided in the original publications (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). Briefly, the EV isolation workflow includes a purification step using size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) with a 700 kDa cutoff bimodal resin, powered by Fast Protein Liquid Chromatography (FPLC). This SEC set up removes small molecules (e.g., salts, nucleic acids, etc.) and proteins smaller than ∼ 700 kDa, effectively eliminating free‐floating, and low‐molecular‐weight proteins that are not associated with EVs. Finally, the NK‐EVs are concentrated and PBS‐buffer exchanged by UF with a 10 kDa cutoff.

Given that most soluble proteins present in the cell conditioned media, including cytokines (∼15‐25 kDa), chemokines (∼8‐12 kDa), granzymes (∼25‐32 kDa), and albumin (∼66 kDa), fall well below the 700 kDa threshold of the SEC resin, they elute separately from EVs during chromatography. As a result, upstream SEC substantially reduces freely soluble protein carryover prior to downstream comparative purification experiments. Accordingly, proteins detected in the final EV preparation are enriched for vesicle‐associated molecules, including intraluminal, membrane‐bound, and externally associated components. Cytokines detected after SEC processing are therefore unlikely to represent simple freely soluble contamination, although low levels of loosely associated or co‐isolated proteins cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, NTA comparison analysis demonstrated very high particle purity relative to non‐EV particles (≥99% EV‐sized particles) in the NK‐EV preparation as noted in our previous work (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023). While SEC is considered a relatively gentle isolation method, it may, like any purification method, alter loosely associated biomolecular corona components (Welsh et al., 2024). Therefore, the SEC‐UF isolated NK‐EVs used in this study represent a standardized post‐isolation corona state rather than the fully native, pre‐isolation EV corona as it exists in conditioned media or under physiological conditions. All subsequent comparisons are made relative to this defined starting material. To minimize the upstream variability, this study utilized pre‐isolated vesicles and focused on comparing UC and UF purification approaches for their efficacy in selectively removing PK‐digested surface proteins in NK‐EVs.

Both methods' effectiveness in removing PK‐cleaved proteins and their impact on the EVs' physical properties and cytotoxicity were assessed. The EV proteome was also characterized by combining total protein staining on blots, mass spectrometry proteomics, and multiplex‐bead‐based assays for surface immunophenotyping and cytokine profiling.

Materials and Methods

NK‐EV Biomanufacturing from NK92‐MI Cells Using a Closed‐Loop Hollow Fibre Bioreactor System

NK‐EVs were manufactured from NK92‐MI cells using a clinically relevant large‐scale biomanufacturing workflow that adheres to Good Manufacturing Practices and the MISEV guidelines (Welsh et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, Thery et al., 2018). Our previous publications provide comprehensive details about the ex vivo biomanufacturing process, the NK‐EV product's identity, safety profile and cytotoxicity (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). For context, EV isolation was performed using SEC coupled with UF.

NK‐EVs’ Corona Enzymatic Digestion Using PK Treatment

An aliquot of purified NK‐EV sample (100 ± 25 mg of proteins; measured by Qubit Protein BR Assay Kit described below), resuspended in 1 mL PBS was treated with 100 µg/mL of PK (PK; ThermoFisher Scientific, cat#EO0491), with added 2 mM CaCl2 (Sigma, cat#21115), for 2.5h using a temperature control orbital shaker set at 37°C and 450 RPM. These conditions maximize the effectiveness of PK treatment by ensuring abundant presence and extended treatment duration compared to other studies (Cvjetkovic et al., 2016, Skliar et al., 2018). The control NK‐EV sample was processed identically but without PK treatment. PK activity was then inhibited using 10 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Thermo Fisher, cat#36978) for 10 min. All steps were performed in a 1.5 mL Protein LoBind tube (Eppendorf, cat#0030.108.116), minimizing the risk of protein loss.

Purification Methods Following Enzymatic Digestion

Samples were processed either by UC in a 5 mL Open‐Top Thinwall Ultra‐Clear Tube (Beckman Coulter, cat#344057) at 100 000 x g for 1 h at 4°C and washed once with filtered‐PBS or UF using the 10 kDa MWCO Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Sigma, cat#UFC501096) to separate de‐coronised EVs from peptides and cleaved proteins in solution; the columns were washed twice with water before loading samples. Spun samples were washed twice with filtered‐PBS. NK‐EVs were resuspended in 90 – 260 µL of filtered‐PBS. All steps were performed in a 1.5 mL Protein LoBind tube, minimizing the risk of protein loss.

NTA

NK‐EV's NTA was performed as previously described (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). Briefly, after vortexing NK‐EV samples, NTA analysis was conducted using a NanoSight NS300 with a 488 nm laser (V3.4 software; Malvern, UK). Camera settings were adjusted to capture 30 – 120 particles per frame: the camera level was set to 14, and the detection threshold to 15 for analysis. Samples were injected into the chamber and analyzed in flow mode with five 1‐min captures. The EV concentration was corrected for dilution before averaging.

TEM Analysis of Individual NK‐EV

TEM imaging of NK‐EVs (positive staining) was performed identically as previously described (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2026). Briefly, an equal amount of vesicles was used for imaging across the different conditions. ImageJ software (NIH; V1.54 g.) was used to measure all visible vesicles in the TEM micrographs, and the data were analyzed to determine the average particle size.

Total Protein Quantification

Protein concentration of NK‐EVs was performed as previously described using the Qubit Protein BR Assay Kit (Invitrogen, cat#A50669) (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024).

Total Protein Staining Blot Detection of NK‐EV Protein Markers

Total protein staining blot detection of NK‐EV proteins was performed identically to our previous publication (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023). Briefly, purified NK‐EVs were lysed using RIPA detergent buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat#78441) under cold conditions with sonication. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (14,000 x g), and 30 µg of EV protein lysates were denatured under reducing conditions and prepared for total protein staining blot detection using the Revert 700 Total Protein Stain and Wash Kit (LI‐COR, cat#926‐11015).

Cytokine Profiling Using Multiplex Bead‐Based Flow Cytometry of NK‐EVs

Cytokine profiling using the human MACSPlex Cytokine Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, cat#130‐125‐800) multiplex bead‐based flow cytometry analysis was performed identically to our previous publication, with the exception that NK‐EVs were not lysed in this study to assess the cytokine profile of the EV biomolecular corona specifically (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024).

Immunophenotyping of Surface Antigens Using Multiplex Bead‐Based Flow Cytometry of NK‐EVs

Immunophenotyping of EV surface antigens was performed using the human MACSPlex EV Kit IO (Miltenyi Biotec, cat#130‐108‐813), a multiplex bead‐based flow cytometry assay, following the same procedure as described in our previous publication (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024).

Mass Spectrometry Analysis of NK‐EV

Sample Preparation: To increase the depth of measurement and to better reveal membrane protein profiles in EVs, a protein‐level fractionation was conducted by lysing NK‐EVs with a commercial Mem‐PER Plus Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher, cat# 89842). Both the cytosolic and membrane fractions were processed separately and protein quantification was done using the Qubit Protein BR Assay Kit, as previously mentioned. Protein lysates were then processed for proteomic analysis using a Single‐Pot Solid‐Phase‐enhanced Sample Preparation (SP3) workflow as described previously with modifications (Hughes et al., 2019). Note: the SP3 workflow includes multiple ethanol‐based bead washes and aqueous organic rinses to remove detergents and small molecules prior to digestion and LC‐MS injection. Briefly, for each sample, 20 µg of protein lysate was reduced with 10 mM DL‐dithiothreitol (DTT; Sigma, cat#43815) at 37°C for 1 h, alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAA; Sigma, cat#I1149) at 23°C for 30 min protected from light, then the reaction was quenched with a further 10 mM DTT. A 1:1 mixture of 100 µg hydrophobic: 100 µg hydrophilic Sera‐Mag magnetic Carboxylate modified particles (Cytiva, cat# 44152105050250, 24152105050250,) were combined in 10 µL MS‐grade water (Sigma, cat#18963‐500ML‐KC), and protein binding was performed at 23°C for 20 min in the presence of 75% acetonitrile (ACN; Fisher, cat#A955‐4). Magnetic beads were immobilized to the side of the tubes with a magnetic rack (Fisher, cat#AM10027) and washed three times with 200 µL 80% ACN and once with 100% ACNh. A two‐step trypsin digestion protocol was used, the first step an addition of 0.4 µg of sequencing‐grade trypsin (Promega, cat#V511A) in 40 µL Ammonium Bicarbonate (ABC; Sigma, cat#09830) at 37°C for 4 h (enzyme‐to‐protein ratio of 1:50), followed by adding additional 0.4 µg of trypsin in 10 µL ABC and incubate for overnight (≥ 16 h) at 37°C. Beads were immobilized and the digested peptides in the supernatant were transferred to a new tube and acidified to pH 3 with formic acid (Fluka, cat#56302). Residual beads were removed by centrifugation at 5000 x g for 5 min prior to LC‐MS analysis. No additional desalting was performed.

DIA‐PASEF LC‐MS: LC‐MS measurements were carried out on a timsTOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonik, Bremen, Germany) coupled to a nanoElute 2 UPLC system (Bruker Daltonik). The instrument was calibrated before sample run using the Chip Cube High Mass Reference Standard (Agilent, G1982–85001) and the instrument performance was monitored by running in‐house quality control cellular protein digest in a regular basis. Peptide separations were achieved using a two‐column configuration consisting of a C18 trap column in line with a PepSep 25 analytical column (25 cm × 75 µm, 1.9 µm C18 particles (Bruker, cat#1893477). Chromatographic separation was conducted at a flow rate of 0.5 µL/min using a 48 min gradient. Mobile phase A contained 0.1% formic acid in water, and mobile phase B contained 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient commenced at 2% B, increased linearly to 35% B over 40 min, followed by a rapid ramp to 95% B at 40.5 min, which was held until 48 min. Eluting peptides were analyzed on the timsTOF Pro 2 operated in data‐independent acquisition PASEF (DIA‐PASEF) mode. Scans covered an m/z range of 100–1700 and an ion mobility range of 0.6–1.60 Vs/cm2. The TIMS analyzer was operated at a 100% duty cycle with 100 ms accumulation and ramp times; yielding a total cycle time of approximately 1.8 sec. Precursors within the m/z range of 400–1200 were divided into 16 DIA windows, each containing 32 ion mobility steps. Isolation windows were set to 26 Da with a 1 Da overlap between adjacent windows. Collision energy was applied linearly according to ion mobility, ranging from 59 eV at 1/K0 = 1.3 Vs/cm2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 = 0.85 Vs/cm2.

Database Search and analysis: Raw MS data were searched with DiaNN 2.3.0, using the Predicted from FASTA mode with SwissProt reviewed human protein database (downloaded 20241120, 20428 entries). N‐terminal methionine excision enabled, peptide length set to 7–30, precursor m/z set to 300–1800, precursor charge set to 1–4, fragment m/z set to 200–1800, Maximum 1 missed cleavages, cysteine carbamidomethylation enabled as a fixed modification, Match between Runs enabled. Protein group intensity table was processed in R. Proteins were identified using the Protein.Names field. For each condition, cytosolic and membrane fractions were combined to generate a single EV dataset measurement. Common contaminants were excluded prior to downstream comparisons, including keratin proteins and abundant blood/plasma‐associated proteins (e.g., ALBU, HBA, HBB, CAH1, and RET4). Protein presence within each condition was defined using a replicate‐consistency criterion. A protein was considered present if detected (intensity > 0 in the combined EV dataset) in at least 2 of 3 independent biological replicates. Presence lists were generated for each condition and used to compute shared and condition‐enriched protein sets. Venn diagrams were generated in R using these presence lists and designed on Affinity Designer (V3.0.3.4027). Protein‐protein interaction network was generated using STRING (https://string‐db.org/; V.12.0). Using the STRING database of known and predicted protein‐protein interactions, the NK‐EV proteome, focusing on biologically relevant proteins, was analyzed with the Markov Cluster Algorithm (MCL; unsupervised algorithm) to cluster proteins based on the likelihood that they frequently co‐occur within the network), with an inflation value of 3.

In Vitro Potency Testing of NK‐EV Against the MDA‐MB‐231 TNBC Model

Functional cytotoxicity testing of NK‐EV was performed using our published potency assay (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). Adherent MDA‐MB‐231 triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells (1,500 cells per well) were seeded in 96‐well plates and incubated for 18 – 24 h. Effector EVs were added at varying concentrations (1 × 109, 1 × 1010, and 1 × 1011 EV/mL), with untreated cells as controls. After 4 h, PrestoBlue HS Cell Viability Reagent Assay (Invitrogen, cat#P50200) and Cell Event Caspase 3/7 Green Detection Reagent (Invitrogen, cat#C10723) were added, and fluorescence was measured after 90 min using a plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader; cat#SH1M2G‐SN; Gen5 software V3.14). Results were averaged and corrected for background.

Data Availability (EV‐TRACK)

All relevant data have been submitted from our experiments to the EV‐TRACK knowledgebase (EV‐TRACK ID: EV240190) (Consortium et al., 2017). The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD074153.

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as the means ± the standard deviation (SD) or the mean (SEM) standard error and normalized to the control group for relative comparison, as indicated in each figure legend. The number of experimental and technical replicates analyzed is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., LaJolla, CA, USA), where a p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significant differences were marked with a single (p < 0.05), double (p < 0.01), triple (p < 0.001), or quadruple (p < 0.0001) asterisk. Ratio analysis was performed using the Bland‐Altman method for comparison. One‐way or Two‐way ANOVA, followed by post hoc tests (Tukey's or Sidak's multiple comparisons), were used and identified in the figure legends.

Results

Figure 1A illustrates the assessment of the two most commonly used purification methods after PK‐mediated digestion of the NK‐EV biomolecular corona. Details on NK‐EV production and isolation methods are available in our previous publications (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). In this current study, PK‐treated NK‐EVs were purified from PK‐cleaved peptides using UC and UF purification methods. A non‐PK‐treated NK‐EV control sample was included to verify that the purification process did not alter the inherent characteristics or cytotoxicity of NK‐EVs. This critical control ensures the specificity and selectivity of PK treatment in comparative analyses. Using a MISEV‐centric approach, validated techniques (NTA, protein quantification by Qubit Protein Assay, surface immunophenotyping of EV surface‐bound proteins using multiplex bead‐based assay, cytokine profiling using multiplex bead‐based assay, TEM (positive staining) imaging, proteomic assessment, and functional assays (resazurin‐based cell viability assay and caspase 3/7 activation assay), both purification methods were evaluated on their effectiveness in removing PK‐cleaved peptides from de‐coronised EVs and their impact on EV's physical characteristics and cytotoxicity.

UC and UF Purification Methods Cause Differential De‐Coronisation of NK‐EV Protein Following Enzymatic Digestion with PK

NTA revealed that both purification methods resulted in comparable size distributions Figure 1B‐i. However, UF‐PK NK‐EVs appear slightly smaller than control (CTL) NK‐EVs, according to NTA, with statistical significance Figure 1B‐ii.

Importantly, sample recovery volume post‐purification ranged from 90 – 260 µL, which needs to be considered when normalizing particle and protein quantifications Figure 1C. NTA analysis revealed that both purification methods resulted in comparable particle recovery, indicating that neither method caused substantial loss of EV material (Figure 1D–E). Conversely, UF‐PK NK‐EVs exhibited a significant reduction (p < 0.0004) in protein‐to‐particle ratio compared to UF‐CTL NK‐EVs (Figure 1F–G), suggesting efficient removal of proteins likely associated with the corona. Quantitative analysis revealed that protein concentration was reduced by approximately 52% in UF‐PK NK‐EV samples, whereas UF‐CTL NK‐EVs retained high protein content. By comparison, no significant difference in protein concentration was observed between UC‐PK NK‐EVs and UC‐CTL NK‐EVs, suggesting that protein loss during UC purification was largely non‐PK‐specific (Figure 1F–G). Despite expectations that the control NK‐EV sample should retain abundant proteins, nearly all proteins were lost during UC purification, implying that this method markedly disrupts the EV biomolecular corona, including both loosely and tightly bound (hard corona) proteins. Furthermore, total protein staining of NK‐EV lysates confirmed that UF‐CTL NK‐EVs displayed a distinct protein profile compared to UF‐PK NK‐EVs, indicating that the PK treatment effectively altered NK‐EV proteome, likely through removal of the biomolecular corona Figure 1H.

Representative TEM images (positive staining of the EV membrane, (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2026)) depicted multiple single EV vesicles in all conditions for analysis Figure 1I. Detailed TEM analyses confirm that UF‐CTL NK‐EVs exhibited statistically larger sizes and surface areas compared to UF‐PK NK‐EVs Figure 1J and (Tables S1–S2). However, UC‐purified samples, PK and CTL were of comparable size. Cumulatively, these results indicate that both PK treatment and UC purification alter the physical characteristics of NK‐EVs, particularly their size, likely due to the loss or modification of EV biomolecular corona. This interpretation aligns with previous findings (Heidarzadeh et al., 2023, Skliar et al., 2018).

PK Digestion and Subsequent Purification Differentially Impact Corona‐Associated Cytokines and Membrane‐Bound Receptor Proteins on NK‐EVs

To better understand the protein‐specific impact of PK treatment and subsequent purification methods, biomolecular profiling of several membrane‐bound proteins and cytokines associated with NK cells were probed using multiplex flow cytometry assays on intact NK‐EVs (non‐lysed) from all conditions Figure 2 (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023).

Immunophenotyping of NK‐EV surface‐bound proteins revealed differential effects of PK treatment on membrane‐bound proteins (including HLA‐ABC, HLA‐DR/DP/DQ, CD2, CD24, CD29, CD44, CD45, CD56, CD63 and CD81; Figure 2A). First, assessing the impact of purification method on the immunophenotyping profiling, no statistical differences were observed between UF‐ and UC‐purified controls NK‐EV across all measured membrane‐bound proteins (α < 0.05). This suggests that the choice of purification method alone does not substantially alter the prevalence or abundance of these membrane‐bound proteins. Second, evaluating the effect of PK treatment on the immunophenotyping profiling revealed a substantial reduction in several transmembrane proteins: CD2 (UF‐purified EVs only: p < 0.05), CD44 (UF‐purified EVs: p < 0.05 and UC‐purified EVs: p < 0.001), CD56 (UC‐purified EVs only: p < 0.05), and CD81 (both UF‐ and UC‐purified EVs: p < 0.001). Other membrane‐bound proteins, such as HLA‐ABC, HLA‐DR/DP/DQ, CD24, CD29, CD45, and CD63, were not significantly affected by PK treatment. Interestingly, CD63 remained largely resistant to PK digestion, whereas CD81 was markedly reduced.

Cytokine profiling of NK‐relevant proteins using non‐lysed NK‐EV samples revealed that PK treatment affects cytokine levels differently, including interferon‐gamma (IFNγ), perforin (PFN), and interleukin (IL)‐2, IL‐6, and IL‐10 Figure 2B. UC‐purified NK‐EV showed diminished or undetectable levels of all cytokines except IL‐2, with no statistical difference between PK‐treated and control NK‐EVs across any cytokine, including IL‐2. This suggests that the UC purification method results in substantial protein loss independently of PK treatment. Conversely, UF‐CTL NK‐EVs displayed high levels of IFNγ, PFN and IL‐10, whereas these cytokines were significantly reduced in UF‐PK NK‐EVs (p < 0.0001). Although IL‐2 and IL‐6 levels appeared lower following PK treatment, these reductions did not reach statistical differences, suggesting that PK selectively degrades specific NK‐EV‐associated cytokines under the conditions tested.

Together, biomolecular profiling of NK‐EVs suggests that PK treatment selectively impacts surface‐bound proteins, exhibiting protein‐specific sensitivity, and that specific cytokines are associated with the NK‐EV biomolecular corona.

Biomolecular Corona Disruption by Purification Methods and PK Treatment Is Confirmed by Proteomics

To directly assess how purification methods and PK treatment impact the NK‐EV biomolecular corona, mass spectrometry‐based proteomic analyses were performed. To increase the depth of measurement and to better reveal membrane protein profiles in EVs, a protein‐level fractionation was conducted by lysing NK‐EVs with a commercial membrane protein extraction kit, and both fractions were used for protein digestion using the SP3 protocol prior to LC‐MS analysis Figure 3A. In total, 639 proteins were quantified for the entire dataset, 34 were shared exclusively with ExoCarta, 27 with Vesiclepedia alone, 543 overlapped with both databases, and 35 proteins were unique to the NK‐EV dataset Figure 3B. Overall, around 95% of NK‐EV detected proteins overlapped with one or both EV reference databases, indicating strong enrichment for EV‐associated proteins. The small subset of unique proteins consisted predominantly of broadly expressed cellular proteins related to cytoskeletal organization, membrane trafficking, core cellular regulation, nuclear or chromatin‐associated processes, and immune‐related functions. Notably, canonical NK‐EV markers such as CD63, CD81, annexins, Rab family proteins, CD107a/b (LAMP1/2), KIRs (KIR3DL3/KIR2DS4), CD56 (NCAM), and PFN were detected, consistent with NK cell and NK‐EV biology (refers to the upcoming Tables for protein ID). While canonical ESCRT‐associated proteins such as ALIX or TSG101 were not detected, the presence of multiple tetraspanins (CD63, CD81), Rab GTPases, lysosomal markers (LAMP1/2), cytosolic EV‐enriched proteins, and NK lineage–specific cytotoxic proteins collectively supports the exosomal identity and NK origin of the vesicles, consistent with prior NK‐EV studies. Furthermore, our original publication, in which the NK‐EVs were thoroughly characterized, demonstrated the presence of FLOT1 and the absence of known EV contaminants (i.e., GM‐130 and calnexin) (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023).

Next, the influence of purification method on the NK‐EV biomolecular corona was assessed in the absence of PK treatment Figure 3C. across UF‐CTL and UC‐CTL NK‐EV samples, a total of 536 proteins were detected (Table S3–S5). Of these, 255 proteins (47.6%) were shared between both purification methods, representing a common core EV proteome (intraluminal and transmembrane proteins). In contrast, only 32 proteins (6.0%) were detected exclusively in UC‐purified samples, whereas 249 proteins (46.5%) were detected exclusively in UF‐purified samples. The UC‐only dataset was relatively limited in size and largely composed of intracellular and membrane‐associated proteins. Conversely, the UF‐only dataset was substantially larger and encompassed a broader spectrum of intracellular proteins, membrane or surface‐associated proteins, and soluble or extracellular‐associated proteins, suggesting improved preservation of EV‐associated and corona‐associated components following UF purification.

Next, the impact of PK treatment following UF purification was assessed Figure 3D. across UF‐CTL and UF‐PK NK‐EV samples, a total of 737 proteins were detected (Table S6–S8). Among these, 247 proteins (33.5%) were shared between both conditions, defining a PK‐resistant core proteome. Based on protein identity, functional clustering (STRING MCL), and known cellular localization, approximately 70–80% of proteins detected in the PK‐resistant NK‐EV proteome can be confidently assigned to the intraluminal compartment. These proteins are dominated by cytoskeletal, metabolic, chaperone, ribosomal, and RNA‐binding proteins, consistent with protected luminal EV cargo. The remaining proteins comprise a smaller fraction of transmembrane proteins. In contrast, 407 proteins (55.2%) were detected exclusively in UF‐CTL NK‐EVs, whereas only 83 proteins (11.3%) were unique to UF‐PK NK‐EVs. These findings demonstrate a substantial reduction in detectable proteins following PK treatment, in agreement with results obtained using orthogonal analytical approaches (i.e., MACSplex antigenic surface and cytokines profiling). Several membrane‐associated proteins, including CD63, CD81, and HLA class I proteins, CD45 (PTPRC), CD54 (ICAM1), CD166 (ALCAM), and integrins (ITB1/ITB2), were no longer detected following PK treatment, indicating sensitivity of their surface‐accessible domains to proteolytic digestion. Importantly, PK selectively cleaves extracellularly accessible protein regions, while intraluminal and transmembrane domains remain protected by the intact EV lipid bilayer. Conversely, multiple non‐membrane‐associated proteins were absent from the UF‐PK NK‐EV dataset. These included apolipoproteins (APOA1/2, APOC1/2/3, APOD/H), plasma protease inhibitors and carrier proteins (i.e., A1AT, AACT, AMBP, ITIH4, SHBG, VTDB, CERU), complement and inflammatory proteins (i.e., CO4B, KNG1, CLUS), and extracellular matrix or adhesion‐associated proteins (i.e., TIMP1, CH3L1, PI16, LUM). Of note, some cytotoxic effector (e.g., GzmB, IFN‐y, etc) were not detected by mass spectrometry, however, they were identified using orthogonal methods, such as the multiplexed cytokines assay. The selective loss of these proteins following PK treatment supports their classification as externally accessible, likely corona‐associated components rather than intraluminal EV cargo. Protein‐protein interaction analysis using STRING revealed biologically relevant proteins clustered according to intraluminal proteins (8 clusters), transmembrane proteins (2 clusters), and likely corona‐associated proteins (2 clusters) Figure 3E.

UC and UF‐Purifieda NK‐EVs Demonstrate Differential Cytotoxic Functionality Against Cancer Cells

The impact of corona alteration following PK treatment was evaluated by measuring the cytotoxicity of NK‐EVs against MDA‐MB‐231 triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells. As expected, UF‐CTL NK‐EVs retained robust cytotoxic activity, showing a dose‐dependent reduction in cell viability Figure 5A‐i and caspase‐3/7 activation Figure 5B‐i. This observation is consistent with our previous findings, where NK‐EVs were isolated from conditioned media using SEC, followed by UF for concentration and buffer exchange, and the final product (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). Interestingly, UF‐PK NK‐EVs exhibited a marked decrease in cytotoxicity, with a 74% (p < 0.01) reduction in cell viability, as measured by the resazurin‐based assay, and a 43% (p < 0.0003) reduction in caspase 3/7 activity, compared to control NK‐EVs.

Conversely, UC‐CTL NK‐EVs exhibited no cytotoxicity, as measured by the resazurin‐based cell viability assay (Figure 5A‐ii) and caspase 3/7 activation assay (Figure 5B‐ii). These findings further support that UC purification disrupts NK‐EV functionality, likely by altering the EV biomolecular corona, which is pivotal to NK‐EV functionality and cytotoxicity against TNBC cells.

Discussion and Conclusion

This study underscores the important role of the EV biomolecular corona in preserving both the protein composition and cytotoxic functional activity of NK‐EVs. PK treatment markedly altered the EV biomolecular corona, as evidenced by a reduction in total protein content, selective changes in biomolecular profile, and diminished NK‐EV cytotoxicity, effects that were selective with UF purification but largely non‐specific with UC purification. This aligns with the growing recognition that the EV biomolecular corona is crucial for mediating intracellular communication, biodistribution, bioavailability, and EV stability (Toth et al., 2021, Wolf et al., 2022, Heidarzadeh et al., 2023, Liam‐Or et al., 2024). The findings provide key insights into the role of the EV biomolecular corona in modulating NK‐EV functionality, specifically cytotoxic functions against cancer cells.

Notably, NK‐EVs initially produced using a GMP‐compliant biomanufacturing workflow using chemically‐defined (xeno‐free and serum‐free) culture conditions and isolated using a scalable workflow (SEC coupled with UF; SEC‐UF), were found to contain a high amount of proteins in the final NK‐EV product (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023, St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). Again, the SEC‐UF isolated NK‐EVs used here do not necessarily represent the fully native EV corona present in conditioned media prior to isolation or present physiologically. Rather, they represent a controlled and standardized post‐isolation baseline for this study. As such, bulk protein quantification primarily reflects vesicle‐associated material and is interpreted in conjunction with orthogonal profiling rather than as a standalone metric. Accordingly, the conclusions of this study pertain specifically to how UC and UF differentially impact the biomolecular corona that remains after SEC‐UF isolation, in combination with or without enzymatic digestion of surface‐accessible proteins. A small proportion of proteins may reflect co‐isolated soluble rather than true EV‐associated components, representing an inherent limitation of filtration‐based workflows. Accordingly, rigorous upstream purification and high particle purity are critical to ensure that bulk protein measurements predominantly capture vesicle‐associated material, as previously demonstrated (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023).

In the context of this study, UC was identified as a non‐selective purification method, as it removed NK‐EV corona‐associated proteins, independently of PK treatment. Beyond changes in protein‐to‐particle ratio and biomolecular changes, the most striking effect was the substantial loss of NK‐EVs’ cytotoxicity against the MDA‐MB‐231 TNBC cells. Notably, UC‐CTL NK‐EVs exhibited virtually no cytotoxic activity, suggesting that UC treatment might be too harsh to preserve the integrity of EV biomolecular corona. This finding has broad implications, as UC remains the most commonly used method for EV isolation across diverse sources due to its accessibility (Toth et al., 2021, Wolf et al., 2022, Palviainen et al., 2020, Liam‐Or et al., 2024, Dietz et al., 2023). It underscores the critical importance of selecting purification strategies that maintain EV biological activities, as method choice can directly impact functional outcomes. A limitation of this study is that the assessment is confined to a single type of vesicle, NK‐EVs. It remains unclear whether other types of EV vesicles, such as MSC‐EVs, which have significant therapeutic potential and a growing body of supporting evidence, would respond similarly to corona disruption during purification.

Conversely, this study identified UF as a selective purification method that effectively removes PK‐cleaved peptides specifically while preserving EV integrity. UF‐CTL NK‐EVs' biomolecular profile and cytotoxic activity are comparable to the starting materials, including potent cytotoxicity against the MDA‐MB‐231 TNBC cells. Similarly, these NK‐EVs had previously shown cytotoxic function against leukemia cancer model, demonstrating reproducible function against various cancers (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2024). Only following PK treatment were the reduction of protein‐to‐particle ratio and alterations in biomolecular composition measured, corresponding to a marked loss of cytotoxicity. The decrease in protein contents following PK treatment suggests that the EV biomolecular corona serves as a significant reservoir of bioactive proteins associated with NK‐EVs’ cytotoxic function. These findings align with previous reports that PK treatment efficiently removes surface‐accessible proteins without compromising the structural integrity of EVs (Cvjetkovic et al., 2016, Park, 2020). These results suggest that corona‐associated proteins play a critical role in mediating NK‐EV cytotoxicity, emphasizing that the therapeutic potential of NK‐EVs extends beyond their core structure, intraluminal content, and membrane‐bound receptors. Instead, the compositions of the biomolecular corona itself appear integral to NK‐EVs’ bioactivity, at least with regards to their cytotoxic function. The influence of the biomolecular corona on NK‐EV immunomodulatory function remains to be determined in future studies.

Based on the collective findings, both UC and UF, in combination with or without enzymatic digestion, have merit in EV de‐coronisation studies. Each method provides unique insights into the corona composition and functionality. UC, independent of enzymatic digestion, removes the majority of the EV biomolecular corona, whereas UF allows for the selective removal of enzymatically cleaved proteins, preserving the EV biomolecular corona in control EV samples (no PK treatment). For example, the lack of significant difference in membrane‐bound proteins between UC‐ and UF‐purified control NK‐EVs suggests that vesicular membrane structure remains stable, while the concurrent loss of corona‐associated cytokine during UC implies extensive stripping of NK‐EV biomolecular corona. Thus, comparatively to UC alone, only UF enables exploration of the selective impact of PK treatment (might also be applicable for other enzymes) on the EV biomolecular corona with a focus on its proteomic composition.

Under the enzymatic digestion conditions used in this study, PK treatment differentially impacts both likely corona‐associated cytokines and membrane‐bound surface proteins on NK‐EVs. Overall, soluble proteins and cytokine levels were reduced across all analyses tested, with the most abundant cytokines exhibiting the most significant susceptibility to PK treatment. Whilst we can speculate which proteins contribute to the soft corona (i.e., IL‐2, IL‐6, IL‐10, IFN‐γ, and PFN) vs hard corona (i.e., APOA1/2, APOC1/2/3, APOD/H), further research is required to confirm these assignments with high confidence. For example, multiple experimental or biological replicates would be required to determine whether proteins assigned to the soft corona become undetectable after PK treatment, indicating they are easily removed, while proteins assigned to the hard corona remain detectable, albeit at reduced abundance, reflecting their relative resistance to removal. Differential treatment with other proteolytic or glycolytic enzymes may also provide supporting evidence in future studies.

Additionally, PK treatment selectively decreased the abundance of specific surface‐bound proteins, suggesting different levels of PK sensitivity among membrane‐associated molecules. For example, although both CD63 and CD81 are canonical tetraspanins enriched in EV membranes, they exhibited strikingly different responses to PK treatment by MACSPLEX assessment. Mass spectrometry confirms the expression levels of proteins in the sample. The molecular basis underlying these differences remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Potential contribution factors may include protein orientation within the vesicle membrane, post‐translational modifications (e.g., glycosylation), variations in membrane microdomain composition and lipid packing, intrinsic protein stability and steric accessibility, as well as experimental variables, such as temperature, incubation time, enzyme concentration, and detergent use. These results were confirmed by proteomics, whereby some membrane‐bound surface proteins on NK‐EVs responded differently to PK‐treatment compared to others. Collectively, these findings underscore the need for systematic investigations to elucidate how surface protein topology and biochemical context influence EV susceptibility to enzymatic digestion. In the present study, the UF‐purification workflow appear to selectively remove PK‐cleaved proteins present in the extracellular environment of the vesicles, without significantly affecting integral membrane components, offering a robust approach to dissect the structural and functional roles of the EV biomolecular corona.

In conclusion, this study provides the first evidence that alternation of the NK‐EV biomolecular corona leads to a substantial loss of cytotoxic activity, highlighting the pivotal role of corona‐associated proteins in mediating NK‐EV therapeutic effects. These results underscore the functional importance of corona composition in NK‐EV‐based therapeutics and emphasize the need for a clinically relevant biomanufacturing workflow that minimizes exposure to xenogeneic components (e.g., FBS) and employs non‐disruptive EV isolation methods, as previously demonstrated (St‐Denis‐Bissonnette et al., 2023). Importantly, these conclusions are limited to the comparative effects of UC versus UF on the removal of PK cleaved, protease accessible external proteins under the conditions tested, rather than broader biology of the biomolecular corona (i.e., stability, reversibility, reformation, etc.). Future studies should also focus on distinguishing the composition and role of the hard and soft corona proteins in mediating NK‐EV function (i.e., cytotoxic functions vs immunomodulation functions). For example, removing enzymatically cleaved peptides from de‐coronised EVs is essential for accurately mapping the corona's composition and determining spatial topology. And this will require the combination of complementary approaches. Importantly, the schematic in Figure 4, derived from multiple orthogonal methods/results/data obtained by our group, does not depict every NK‐EV particles in solution, but rather represents an “average” NK‐EV particle, capturing the overall biomolecular heterogeneity. Understanding biomolecular compartmentalization within vesicles can offer deeper biological insights for future diagnostic/therapeutic developments. Alternatively, radiolabeling the EV biomolecular corona can be useful for studying its composition and dynamics, offering valuable insights to accelerate the clinical translation of EV‐based therapeutics (Yerneni et al., 2022). Further research could explore how corona composition varies among different EV sources and how targeting specific corona components would improve the delivery and efficacy of EV‐based treatments. Additionally, manipulating the EV corona to alter its composition may offer novel strategies to enhance NK‐EVs’ biodistribution and therapeutic potential. Advancements in understanding the complexity of the EV biomolecular corona, including its dynamic interactions, stability under physiological conditions, and broader implications for EV‐based therapeutics, are ongoing. These studies will have important implications for developing EV‐based therapies.
