Bonding performance of polymer waterproof coating and expansive soil
Authors: Min Ma, Benfu He, Haijun Huang, Fangjun Wu, Shaokun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the bonding performance between polymer waterproof coating (PWC) and expansive soil, 40 groups of expansive soil pull-out tests under different working conditions were carried out in this study. The variables considered included soil water content (12–27%), soil density (1.6–2.0 g/cm3), and PWC coating thickness (0.8–1.5 mm). The effects of these factors on bonding performance were systematically analyzed and discussed. The results show that soil density has the greatest effect on bond strength (relative importance = 0.7010), followed by water content (0.2778), while the contribution of coating thickness is minimal (0.0212). Bond strength increases approximately linearly with increasing soil density and reaches a maximum of 0.244 MPa at a soil density of 2.0 g/cm3. With increasing water content, bond strength first rises and then declines, following a quadratic relationship, with a peak value occurring near the optimum water content (15.1%). As the coating thickness increases, bond strength shows a decrease followed by an increase, and the highest value is obtained at a thickness of 1.5 mm. To enable a convenient and rapid evaluation of the bonding performance between PWC and expansive soil under engineering conditions similar to those considered in this study, predictive equations for bond strength were developed from the test results for different cases. The pulled out soil body (failure-body) exhibited a “soil–nail” effect. The longer the length of the failure-body, the stronger the “soil–nail” effect, and the more conducive to the bonding of PWC with expansive soil. Based on these analyses, recommended parameter ranges for practical application are proposed: a soil density not lower than 1.8 g/cm3 (degree of compaction not lower than 90%), a water content close to the optimum water content, and a coating thickness of 1.5 mm. The findings provide a scientific basis for the engineering application of PWC in expansive soil projects.Keywords: Polymer waterproof coating, Expansive soil, Pull-out test, Bond strength, “Soil–nail” effectSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Expansive soil exhibits significant unfavorable engineering properties such as hydrophilicity, cracking, and swell-shrinking characteristics1. Owing to these characteristics, expansive soils can cause severe damage to foundations, buildings, highways, railways, embankments, and channels; consequently, they are often referred to as the “cancer” of civil engineering2–4. Expansive soils are widely distributed across the globe, covering an area exceeding 100,000 km2 in China alone5,6. The annual economic losses caused by expansive soil hazards in the United States and China are up to 10 billion dollars and 100 million RMB, respectively7. Among the approximately 21,000 km of planned highways in western China, nearly 3300 km are projected to traverse expansive soil regions8. These statistics clearly demonstrate that the treatment of expansive soils has become one of the most critical challenges in civil infrastructure development, particularly in the mitigation and stabilization of expansive soil slopes. Appropriate and effective treatment of expansive soil-related issues is therefore essential to ensuring the safety and performance of engineering projects.

Expansive soil slope failures represent one of the most common forms of expansive soil hazards. The occurrence of such slope failures is predominantly governed by variations in soil moisture content9,10. The dry–wet cycles induce fluctuations in water content, which in turn cause swelling or shrinkage of the soil mass. These volumetric deformations reduce the shear strength of the soil, making expansive soil slopes highly susceptible to landslides under heavy rainfall conditions11–13. Therefore, from the perspective of engineering control strategies, the core of slope protection for expansive soils lies not only in enhancing strength and restraining deformation but, more critically, in long-term suppression of water infiltration and stabilizing the moisture field within the slope.

In view of the pronounced water sensitivity and swelling-shrinkage characteristics of expansive soils, numerous researchers have undertaken extensive studies on slope protection measures based on the principles of waterproofing, anti-cracking, and anti-weathering. Common protection methods include the replacement method14, soil modification techniques15–17, reinforcement using geosynthetic materials18–21, and ecological protection measures22,23. These methods differ substantially in both their technical mechanisms and their applicable conditions. Soil replacement and modification primarily aim to reduce swell–shrink potential and improve strength by altering material properties; however, field implementation often requires extensive excavation and large quantities of materials, leading to relatively high costs, and the large-scale use of modified soils may also pose adverse environmental impacts14–17. Geosynthetic reinforcement and vegetation systems can provide a reinforcing effect that enhances resistance to deformation and erosion; however, reinforcing materials and plant roots may increase the permeability of the soil layer. As a result, under sustained rainfall infiltration, it can still be difficult to maintain a stable slope water content over the long term18–23. Overall, although existing methods can improve stability locally, the long-term performance of expansive soil slopes remains challenging if rainfall infiltration is not effectively suppressed and fluctuations in slope water content are not reduced.

Building on the practical challenge that rainfall infiltration is difficult to block effectively over the long term, Ma et al.24–26 proposed protecting expansive soil slopes using a polymer waterproof coating (PWC). Their model tests showed that PWC can effectively reduce fluctuations in slope water content and restrain swell–shrink deformation of the soil. In addition, PWC offers excellent waterproofing capacity, water resistance, and tensile properties, allowing a continuous and moderately ductile waterproof barrier to be formed on the slope surface. By doing so, rainfall infiltration can be weakened at the source, which gives this approach considerable potential for expansive soil slope protection. Such a flexible, infiltration control oriented strategy stands in clear technical contrast to conventional approaches that focus primarily on reinforcement or modification. The former prioritizes stabilizing the slope moisture regime, whereas the latter typically seeks to counteract the consequences of moisture variation by increasing strength or restraining deformation. In practice, however, PWC is expected to withstand repeated swell–shrink deformation as well as rainfall scouring over extended periods. If the bonding performance at the interface between PWC and the soil is inadequate, debonding may occur, preferential flow pathways can develop, and the protective function may be compromised, ultimately reducing the stability and service life of the protection system. For these reasons, the bonding performance between PWC and expansive soil warrants further investigation and is of clear engineering relevance.

Waterproof coatings are mainly used in the field of waterproofing of buildings, roads and bridges at present. In practical engineering, several performance attributes of waterproof coatings warrant particular attention, including durability, tensile properties, water resistance, waterproofing capacity, and low‑temperature flexibility27–30. Equally important is the bonding performance between the coating and the protected substrate, because it governs whether the coating can remain well adhered to the base and maintain a continuous waterproof barrier over the long term. When a waterproof layer cracks, adequate bonding performance helps prevent water from channeling between the coating and the substrate. In engineering practice, tensile performance is commonly used to describe the deformation capacity of the coating film, impermeability tests are used to evaluate water-blocking capability, and interfacial pull-out tests together with interlayer shear tests are used to assess the resistance of the coating to peeling and sliding. Among the factors involved, coating dosage, thickness, temperature, and loading rate are often regarded as primary variables governing interface strength. For example, Wang et al.31 compared different waterproof coating systems using interlayer shear tests and pull-out tests, and demonstrated the sensitivity of interface strength to coating dosage, temperature, and shear rate. They also reported a fitted correlation between shear strength and bond strength, and noted that interface performance should be evaluated using multiple indicators rather than a single metric. Further studies on polyurethane systems, epoxy-modified coatings, and self‑stratification coatings have improved heat resistance, low-temperature flexibility, water resistance, and bonding performance through formulation design32–35. Therefore, although different material systems place emphasis on different aspects, their common engineering objective is to achieve a practical balance among seepage resistance, extensibility, durability, and interfacial bonding performance. Collectively, these findings indicate that the engineering effectiveness of waterproof coatings depends not only on the intrinsic properties of the material, but also on whether the coating to substrate interface can remain continuous, stable, and resistant to debonding under specific service conditions.

It should be noted, however, that most of the above findings have been established for relatively stable substrates such as concrete. In practice, the coating to concrete interface and the coating to soil interface differ fundamentally. Concrete substrates are comparatively rigid, with a stable pore structure, and interfacial behavior is governed mainly by chemical and physical adhesion as well as mechanical interlocking. In contrast, expansive soil is a porous, deformable, and water-sensitive medium. Changes in water content alter matric suction and drive swelling and shrinkage, while moisture may also migrate along the interface and increase the risk of debonding. Therefore, it is not sufficient to directly apply bonding principles derived from concrete systems to soil protection. Although PWC has been widely used in concrete structures, studies on the use of waterproof coatings for soil protection remain limited, particularly systematic work that quantifies the bonding performance between the coating and soil and clarifies the controlling factors. This gap is especially evident for expansive soil slopes, where adequate bonding performance between PWC and expansive soil is a prerequisite for reliable protection. Accordingly, the potential of PWC as a new protective material for expansive soil slope protection warrants further exploration.

To explore the bonding performance between PWC and expansive soil in detail, this study conducted 40 groups of pull-out tests on expansive soil specimens under varying conditions. The influences of expansive soil parameters—namely soil water content and soil density—as well as PWC coating thickness on the bond strength and the length of the pulled out soil body (failure-body) were analyzed and discussed. The research also elaborated on how failure-body length influences bonding performance and proposed recommended values for soil water content, soil density, and PWC coating thickness for practical engineering applications. The findings provide a theoretical foundation and scientific basis for the application of PWC in embankment and slope protection within expansive soil regions.

Materials

A waterborne rubber-based polymer waterproof coating (PWC) with high solid content was used in this study. Prior to curing, the PWC exists as a gray-black paste with moderate viscosity and flowability, as shown in Fig. 1a. Specimen making device

Custom-designed and fabricated specimen preparation mold was employed to prepare the expansive soil specimens. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, specimen preparation mold consists of four components: a lid, a frame, a square cutting ring, and a base. The square cutting ring has internal dimensions of 70 × 70 × 20 mm (length × width × height), which are identical to those of the expansive soil specimens and comply with the specimen size requirements specified in the standard “Test Methods for Building Waterproofing Coatings” (GB/T 16777-2008). The assembly procedure was as follows: the base was first positioned, followed by the installation of the square cutting ring and the frame. The pre‑weighed expansive soil was then placed inside the mold. Subsequently, the lid was secured, and pressure was applied to the mold using a jack to compact the expansive soil specimens (Fig. 3b). Methods

The use of PWC for protecting expansive soil slopes represents a novel engineering application. Quantifying the bonding performance between expansive soil and PWC through experimental testing is therefore essential and holds significant engineering value.

The water content and soil density of expansive soil, along with PWC coating thickness, are the three primary factors influencing bonding performance. In this study, their effects were investigated via pull‑out tests. The detailed experimental program, presented in Table 3, comprised 8 groups of tests, each containing 5 subgroups corresponding to different levels of water content, soil density and coating thickness. In total, 40 distinct test conditions were prepared for the pull‑out tests, with 5 specimens fabricated for each condition. Consequently, a total of 200 expansive soil specimens were produced for the entire testing campaign.

The soil water content ranged from 12 to 27%, and the soil density ranged from 1.6 to 2.0 g/cm3 (corresponding to a soil compaction degree of 80–100%, respectively). These parameters cover conditions of relatively high water content and high density. They were selected to represent post-rainfall or high-humidity conditions commonly encountered in expansive soil slopes, and to examine the bonding potential at high density so as to provide guidance for engineering practice. The coating thicknesses were set to 0.8, 1.2, and 1.5 mm, respectively. The prepared expansive soil specimens were quickly and tightly wrapped in plastic wrap to prevent water content deviations caused by moisture evaporation. The expansive soil specimens, PWC, and related tools were then placed in a laboratory with controllable temperature and humidity and conditioned under standard test conditions for more than 24 h, namely a temperature of (23 ± 2) °C and a relative humidity of (50 ± 10)%. In the laboratory, temperature was regulated using an air-conditioning system, and humidity was adjusted using a humidifier.

After completing the preceding procedures, the plastic wrap covering the surface of the specimen was carefully cut and removed, exposing an expansive soil substrate measuring approximately 55 × 55 mm. The PWC was then applied, fully covering the exposed surface and overlapping the remaining plastic wrap to form a continuous film that retained moisture. The specimens were cured according to the standard “Test Methods for Building Waterproofing Coatings” (GB/T 16777-2008). After curing, the surface of the coating was pasted together with the upper clamp of the tensile testing machine. A knife was used to gently cut the coating along the edge of the upper clamp so that the actual bond size between the coating and the expansive soil was 40 × 40 mm. The specimen was installed as illustrated in Fig. 4a, after which a tensile testing machine was used to conduct the pull-out test (Fig. 4b). The tensile speed was controlled at 5 mm/min. Results and discussion

Effect of expansive soil parameters on bond strength

Water content

Figure 5 presents the variation curves of bond strength with soil water content (ω) at different soil densities (ρ). As seen in the figure, bond strength follows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing as water content rises. In the lower soil density range (ρ < 1.8 g/cm3), the overall magnitude of change in bond strength with increasing water content was relatively small. However, as soil density increased, the magnitude of variation in bond strength rose markedly.

Variation curves of bond strength with water content at different soil densities.

The bond strength reached its peak at a water content of 15% or 18%. This suggests that, for a given soil density, bond strength attains its maximum value in the vicinity of the optimum water content. Near the optimum water content, the bond strength differences between different soil densities are more pronounced, with a maximum difference of 0.175 MPa. When the water content increased gradually to 27%, the difference in bond strength between different soil densities was minimal, with a maximum value of only 0.038 MPa—representing a reduction of approximately 78% compared with that at the optimum water content. This phenomenon is likely related to changes in soil matrix suction. Within a certain range, lower water contents correspond to higher matrix suction and consequently stronger interaction force between soil particles36,37. Once the water content exceeds a specific threshold, matrix suction decreases rapidly, and the interaction force between soil particles diminish accordingly. This facilitates the separation of soil particles, thereby indirectly reducing bond strength. Therefore, in practical engineering applications of PWC in expansive soils, it is advisable to maintain the soil water content close to the optimum water content to ensure a more favorable bonding condition.

The relationship between water content and bond strength was fitted using the test results, with outcomes shown in Fig. 5 and Table 4. The analysis indicates that a quadratic function provides a reasonable representation of the variation of bond strength with water content. To quantify the scatter of the experimental data in Fig. 5 and to evaluate the accuracy of the fitted equations, Table 4 summarizes the statistical metrics of the quadratic fits, including R2, the sum of squared errors (SSE), mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), the F-statistic, and the p-value. As shown in Fig. 5, the measured bond strength values exhibit some scatter at the same soil density. The maximum absolute residual, |emax|, is 0.0213 MPa (ρ = 2.0 g/cm3, ω = 15%), indicating that, under the most unfavorable condition, the deviation between the fitted curve and the experimental point is on the order of 10−2 MPa. In terms of residual statistics, the SSE values for the fits at different density levels range from 4.01 × 10−5 to 1.95 × 10−3, and the MSE values range from 1.34 × 10−5 to 6.51 × 10−4, corresponding to RMSE values of approximately 0.0037–0.0255 MPa. These metrics provide a quantitative measure of the degree of scatter around the fitted curves. Moreover, in the low-density range (ρ = 1.6–1.7 g/cm3), the RMSE is approximately 0.0037 to 0.0043, whereas in the medium to high density range (ρ = 1.8–2.0 g/cm3), the RMSE increases to about 0.0109 to 0.0255. This indicates an overall tendency for the absolute deviation of data points from the fitted curve to increase as soil density increases. Overall, although the test results show inherent scatter, its magnitude can be quantified and it does not alter the general trend that bond strength first increases and then decreases with increasing water content.

In terms of error analysis, the R2 values range from 0.823 to 0.904, indicating that the quadratic function captures the overall trend of how bond strength varies with water content. The SSE, MSE, and RMSE values in Table 4 further show that the fitting errors fall within a clear and quantifiable range. In addition, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to test the overall significance of the regression relationships. The significance level was set to α = 0.05 (p < 0.05 considered statistically significant). Results with 0.05 < p < 0.10 were described as marginally significant to reflect weaker, yet non-negligible, statistical evidence. The results show that the regression models under high-density conditions (ρ = 1.9–2.0 g/cm3) are statistically significant (F = 13.771–14.185, p = 0.0296–0.0308), whereas those under low-density conditions (ρ = 1.6–1.8 g/cm3) are marginally significant (p = 0.0710–0.0745). This suggests that the effect of water content on bond strength in the low-density range is supported by some statistical evidence, although the evidence is comparatively weaker. This difference may be related to the smaller response amplitude of bond strength at lower densities and the limited number of data points available for each fitted curve. Taken together, the SSE, MSE, RMSE, |emax|, and ANOVA results indicate that the quadratic fits are statistically supported within the parameter ranges investigated in this study and that the prediction errors are quantifiable. The fitted relationships can therefore be used to quantitatively characterize the relationship of bond strength and water content, while additional data are still needed to verify their applicability beyond the ranges considered here.

Soil density

Figure 6 illustrates the variation pattern of bond strength with soil density under different water contents. It is evident from the figure that, at a given water content, bond strength increases with increasing soil density. In the lower density range (ρ < 1.8 g/cm3), the difference in bond strength between samples with different water contents was minor; however, once soil density reached or exceeded 1.8 g/cm3, this difference became pronounced. Furthermore, the bond strengths at all water contents reached their maximum values at the soil density of 2.0 g/cm3.This finding suggests that bond strength is influenced, to some extent, by the strength of the specimen itself; higher soil density corresponds to greater soil strength38, which in turn indirectly enhances the bond strength between soil and the coating. As shown in Table 5, the slope of the fitted equation initially increases and then decreases with rising water content, reaching its maximum at a water content of 15%. A larger slope indicates a faster rate of increase in bond strength as soil density rises, and thus a more pronounced enhancement in bond strength. Notably, the 15% water content corresponds to the fastest rate of bond strength growth. When the water content exceeds 18%, the rate at which bond strength increases with soil density declines markedly. Once the water content is greater than 21%, the increase in bond strength becomes comparatively insignificant. This suggests that, with continuous increases in both water content and soil density, their contribution to bond strength diminishes substantially. There exists an optimal range of soil water content and soil density that maximizes bond strength. From the above analysis, it follows that the soil should be compacted prior to applying PWC to enhance the bonding performance between the soil and PWC. From the perspective of pull-out test results and engineering safety, it is recommended that the soil should be compacted to a density of not less than 1.8 g/cm3, i.e., a compaction degree of not less than 90%.

Effect of coating thickness on bond strength

The variation in bond strength with soil density under various coating thicknesses (h) is presented in Fig. 8. For all coating thicknesses, bond strength increased as soil density increased, consistent with the trends identified in the preceding analysis. Meanwhile, it can be observed that the bond strength showed a first decrease and then increase in different soil density with the increase of coating thickness. There was no significant difference in the bond strength values corresponding to 1.5 mm and 0.8 mm coating thicknesses. These results indicate that the influence of coating thickness on bond strength is less pronounced than that of the expansive soil parameters.

Variation curves of bond strength with soil density at different coating thickness.

The relationship between bond strength and soil density under different coating thicknesses was fitted, with results presented in Fig. 8 and Table 6. For all coating thicknesses, the relationship between bond strength and soil density exhibited a linear trend, in agreement with the findings of the preceding analysis. As shown in Fig. 8, the test points for each thickness are generally distributed around the fitted straight line, and the scatter can be quantitatively described using the residual statistics reported in Table 6. Specifically, the SSE values of the three linear fits range from 4.45 × 10−4 to 9.64 × 10−4, with corresponding MSE values of 1.48 × 10−4 to 3.21 × 10−4 and RMSE values of approximately 0.0122 to 0.0179. Relative importance analysis of the influencing factors of bond strength

Based on the above analysis, it is evident that the three influencing factors—water content, soil density, and coating thickness—jointly affect the bond strength, and exhibit a certain degree of interaction. Since the effects of these factors cannot be quantitatively evaluated by the preceding analysis alone, a random forest integrated learning method was employed for further investigation39. A higher relative importance reflects a greater influence of that factor. As shown in Fig. 10, the overall trend in relative importance is similar to that obtained in the preceding analysis. Soil density exhibits the highest relative importance (0.7010), significantly exceeding the other two factors, demonstrating that soil density plays a decisive role in determining bond strength. The relative importance of water content (0.2778) ranks second, while that of coating thickness (0.0212) is markedly lower than the other two factors, indicating a very limited impact on bond strength.

Therefore, from an engineering perspective, reliable bonding performance between PWC and expansive soil can be achieved provided that key construction parameters are properly controlled. In field applications, compaction quality of the expansive soil should be treated as a primary control index. The soil can be compacted in layers using a roller to meet the design requirement (degree of compaction not lower than 90%), and adequate compaction plays a dominant role in achieving favorable bonding performance. Water content should be adjusted to near the optimum value through watering or air-drying, because both excessive and insufficient moisture can adversely affect bond strength. In addition, coating thickness can be selected according to site conditions to ensure effective bonding and to satisfy abrasion resistance and durability requirements, while avoiding unnecessary material consumption.

Water content

After the pull‑out test, observations and measurements were conducted on the destructed specimens. It was found that the cross‑sectional shapes of the pulled out soil body, referred to as the failure-body, connected to the tensile upper clamp and the PWC coating were predominantly inverted cone and inverted trapezoid shapes, as shown in Fig. 11.

The cross-sectional shape of the failure-body.

The length of the failure-body (l) corresponding to different water contents was measured and analyzed, with the results presented in Fig. 12. The failure‑body length exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease as the water content increased, reaching a peak near the optimum water content—a trend similar to that observed for bond strength. The maximum length of 15.4 mm occurred at a water content of 15% and a soil density of 1.8 g/cm3. The difference in failure‑body length under different soil densities was gradually smaller with the increment of water content. This implies that the influence of the water content on failure‑body length will gradually decrease when the water content exceeds a certain range.

Figure 13 illustrates the variation pattern of the failure‑body length with respect to soil density. As shown in the figure, the failure‑body length generally exhibits a trend of initial increase followed by stabilization as soil density rises. When the soil density increases from 1.6 to 1.7 g/cm3, the failure‑body length shows a substantial growth, with a maximum increase of 4 mm (at ω = 12%). At higher soil densities (ρ > 1.8 g/cm3), the failure‑body length displays slight fluctuations or minor decreases, although the magnitude of variation remains small. It indicates that the effect on the failure‑body length gradually decreases with the gradual increase of the soil density.

Under a constant soil density, the failure‑body length exhibits a trend of initially decreasing and then increasing as coating thickness increases. Conversely, at a given coating thickness, the failure‑body length shows a trend of increasing and then stabilizing with rising soil density, with a pattern similar to the previous results. “Soil–nail” effect of failure-body

The cross-section of the failure-body had an inverted conical or inverted trapezoid shape, which corresponded to a small “soil–nail”. This configuration provides an additional assurance of strong bonding between the PWC coating and the expansive soil, while also offering a potential reinforcing effect. When the “soil–nail” has a tendency to be pulled out by external forces, the soil along the potential failure surface develops frictional resistance and particle‑to‑particle interactions, which inhibit the displacement of the “soil–nail”. There exist interaction forces between soil particles and their aggregates40,41, making it more difficult to separate the soil masses on either side of the potential failure surface. The longer the “soil-nails”, the larger the potential failure surfaces and the greater the corresponding frictional and interactive forces. Consequently, this increases the difficulty of detaching the “soil-nails” from the soil strata and strengthens the “soil–nail” effect (Fig. 15). This effect contributes to enhance the bond strength between the PWC and the expansive soil, reducing the likelihood of coating debonding from the expansive soil surface.

The “soil–nail” effect of the failure-body.

From the above analysis, it can be seen that an appropriate increase in soil water content and soil density increases the failure-body length and enhances the “soil–nail” effect. However, increasing the coating thickness results in no significant change in the failure-body length, suggesting that its contribution to the “soil–nail” effect appears to be limited. From the perspective of improving both the soil nail effect and overall safety, it is recommended that, in practical engineering applications, the soil water content be maintained near the optimum water content, the soil density be 1.8 g/cm3 or higher, and the coating thickness be set to 1.5 mm.

Conclusion

In this study, the bonding performance between PWC and expansive soils were investigated through pull‑out tests, and the effects of soil water content, soil density, and coating thickness on bond strength and failure-body length were analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows:

The bond strength increased initially and then decreased with rising water content, with the relationship between the two well approximated by a quadratic function. The bond strength reached its maximum value near the optimum water content. The bond strength increased with the increase of soil density, and there was a linear relationship between the two. With increasing coating thickness, the bond strength decreased at first and subsequently increased. Based on the pull-out test results, nonlinear surface fitting was performed to derive bond strength prediction equations for different scenarios. It should be noted that these prediction equations are applicable only within the parameter ranges investigated in this study. Extrapolation beyond these ranges should be treated with caution, and further experiments are needed to verify their accuracy.

From an engineering perspective, the pull-out tests indicate that adequate soil compaction is the dominant factor controlling the bonding performance between PWC and expansive soil. Therefore, compaction quality should be treated as a key control index in practice. It is recommended that water content be adjusted to a range close to the optimum water content first, and the soil then be compacted to achieve a soil density not lower than 1.8 g/cm3 (degree of compaction not lower than 90%), while avoiding excessively high water content that can weaken the bonding effect. In addition, an appropriate PWC coating thickness can be selected according to site conditions (about 1.5 mm in this study) to balance bonding performance, construction efficiency, and material cost. Finally, subsequent procedures should be carried out only after the PWC protective layer has met the specified curing conditions, so as to maximize bond strength and make full use of the beneficial soil–nail effect.

The findings of this study are more applicable to moderately expansive soils with soil properties and parameter ranges similar to those investigated here. For weakly expansive or strongly expansive soils, the applicability of the conclusions still needs to be verified through further tests. Future work will extend the present study by examining the bonding performance between expansive soil and PWC under dry–wet cycles, while monitoring the volume changes of expansive soil induced by dry–wet cycles and evaluating their influence on bonding performance. In addition, future studies will further investigate the bonding performance between PWC and different types of expansive soils.
