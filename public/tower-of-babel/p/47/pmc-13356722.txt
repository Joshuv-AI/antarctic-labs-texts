Effects of dietary supplementation with bile acids on production performance, egg quality, nutrient digestibility, and morphology of reproductive tract in late-laying hens
Authors: Zhenhui Liu, Yuye Jin, Yunlong Zhang, Shuzhen Jiang, Qingsong Fan, Weiren Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study investigated the effects of dietary supplementation with bile acids (BAs) on production performance, egg quality, nutrient digestibility, reproductive organ morphology, intestinal morphology, and blood parameters in late-laying hens. A total of 1080 Hy-Line Brown laying hens, each 76 weeks old, were randomly assigned to four groups receiving a basal diet supplemented with 0 (CON), 200 (BA200), 300 (BA300), or 500 (BA500) mg/kg BAs for 35 days, with 6 replicates per treatment and 45 hens per replicate. Results showed that dietary BAs supplementation increased laying rate, Haugh unit, albumen height, and yolk color in a linear and quadratic manner (linear and quadratic, P < 0.05), while decreasing the feed-to-egg ratio quadratically (quadratic, P < 0.05). The most pronounced improvements were observed in the BA300 group (P < 0.05). Dietary BAs supplementation significantly increased oviductal mucosal fold height, width, perimeter, and epithelial height, as well as uterine fold height and mucosal epithelial height (linear and quadratic, P < 0.05), with the most pronounced effects observed in the BA300 group (P < 0.05). Apparent nutrient digestibility increased significantly with increasing dietary BAs levels (linear and quadratic, P < 0.05). In particular, the BA300 group exhibited significantly higher apparent digestibility of dry matter, organic matter, crude protein, ether extract, gross energy, and arginine, lysine, methionine, threonine, valine, isoleucine, glutamic acid, and aspartic acid (P < 0.05). These improvements were accompanied by enhanced intestinal morphology and favorable changes in serum biochemical parameters, especially in the BA300 group. Overall, under the conditions of the present study, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs produced the most pronounced beneficial effects across all measured parameters. These findings suggest that 300 mg/kg BAs may represent the optimal supplementation level and support the use of BAs as an effective nutritional strategy to alleviate performance decline in late-laying hens.Keywords: Bile acid, Production performance, Egg quality, Laying hen, Reproductive organs

Introduction

In the poultry industry, egg production and egg quality typically decline during the late laying period (Liu et al., 2018; Ye et al., 2020). During the early and peak laying phases, hepatic fat synthesis and peripheral fat utilization remain a delicate dynamic balance (Kersten, 2001). However, in the late laying period, decreased metabolic efficiency and impaired fat mobilization increase the risk of hepatic steatosis in hens. Hepatic steatosis not only compromises hen health but also reduces egg production and egg quality (Kartsoli et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022b). These metabolic disturbances highlight the importance of regulating lipid metabolism and improving nutrient utilization in late-laying hens. Therefore, effective strategies are required to mitigate the losses in production performance and egg quality.

Bile acids (BAs) were hydroxylated steroid compounds synthesized from cholesterol in the liver and were widely used in animal nutrition as dietary fat emulsifiers, which facilitated lipid hydrolysis and improved nutrient utilization (Monte et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2023). Sun et al. (2023a) found that dietary supplementation with BAs reduced hepatic lipid accumulation and decreased mortality in 50-week-old hens. Yang et al. (2022b) reported that supplementing porcine BAs at 60 mg/kg improved egg production in late-laying hens, while supplementation at 60 and 90 mg/kg reduced serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), decreased abdominal fat deposition, and beneficially altered the cecal microbiota composition. Li et al. (2024) found that incorporating 0.01% and 0.02% of chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) and hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA) into high-fat diets boosted egg production, improved egg quality, and positively affected gut microbial composition in 28-week-old hens. Studies have shown that egg quality is closely associated with the morphology and health of reproductive tract in laying hens (Carillon et al., 2016; Sah and Mishra, 2018). Despite the growing body of research on BAs in poultry, limited information is available regarding their effects on the histomorphology of reproductive tract, particularly in late-laying hens. Moreover, the reported responses to dietary BAs supplementation in this stage remain inconsistent across studies, possibly due to differences in BAs sources, hen age, and experimental duration.

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of graded levels of dietary BAs supplementation on production performance, egg quality, nutrient digestibility, serum biochemical parameters, reproductive organ histomorphology, and intestinal morphology in late-laying hens.

Materials and methods

BAs products

The BAs product was of porcine origin and provided by Shandong Zhongjing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Taian, China), with 21.53% active components, including 3.05% CDCA, 6.59% phocaecholic acid, 0.24% hyocholic acid, 0.49% HDCA, and 11.16% cholic acid.

Animals, diets, and experimental design

This study was conducted on a commercial farm in Ningyang County, Shandong Province, China. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Agricultural University (approval no. SDAUA-2023-817). A total of 1080 Hy-Line Brown laying hens (76 weeks of age) with similar body weights (2.05 ± 0.05 kg) were randomly assigned to 4 dietary treatments: a basal diet supplemented with 0 (CON), 200 (BA200), 300 (BA300), or 500 (BA500) mg/kg BAs. Each treatment consisted of 6 replicates, with 45 hens per replicate. The experiment consisted of a 7d adaptation period followed by a 35d formal experimental period. The basal diet (Table 1) was formulated in accordance with the Nutrition Requirement of Chicken in China (NY/T 33-2004) to satisfy or exceed the recommended nutrient requirements basal diet. All laying hens were housed in three-tier wire-floored cages (220 × 45 × 45 cm). The housing environment was maintained at 20–25 °C with approximately 65% relative humidity under a 16L:8D photoperiod. Feed and water were available ad libitum.

Sample collection

On days 14, 28, and 35 of the trial, six eggs from each replicate were randomly selected for quality assessment. On day 28, two hens per replicate were selected, and fresh excreta samples were collected over three consecutive days. Then excreta samples from each replicate were pooled, mixed with 10% sulfuric acid for nitrogen fixation, and stored at −20 °C until analysis. On day 35, two hens per replicate were selected, fasted for 12 h, and then blood samples were collected from the wing vein. Serum was obtained by centrifuging the blood at 1,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. After euthanasia, tissues including the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, oviductal ampulla and uterus, and ovary were dissected and preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent analysis.

Laying performance

Throughout the entire experimental period, egg production, the numbers of cracked or deformed eggs, and the amounts of feed intake were recorded daily for each replicate. Based on the methods described by Li et al. (2024), average daily feed intake (ADFI), average egg weight (AEW), feed conversion ratio (FCR), laying rate (LR), and abnormal rate (AR)were calculated.

Detection of egg quality

Egg weight, albumen height, Haugh unit, and yolk color were measured using an EMT-5200 multifunctional egg quality analyzer (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). Eggshell color parameters, including brightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*), were determined using a CR-10 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Japan). Eggshell strength was measured with an EFG-0503 eggshell strength tester (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan), and eggshell thickness was determined using an ETG-1061 eggshell thickness gauge (Robotmation, Tokyo, Japan). The longitudinal and transverse lengths of each egg were measured using a digital caliper (Merck, Germany), and the egg shape index was calculated as the ratio of longitudinal length to transverse length. Yolk ratio and eggshell ratio were calculated by dividing yolk weights and eggshell weights by total egg weights, respectively, following the method described by Li et al. (2023).

Apparent nutrient digestibility

Apparent nutrient digestibility was determined using acid-insoluble ash (AIA) as an indigestible marker (Wetherbee and Gruber, 1993). The AIA content in feed and excreta was determined using the 4 M hydrochloric acid method. Dry matter (DM) was determined by oven drying at 105 ± 2 °C (AOAC 934.01). Crude protein (CP) was analyzed by the Kjeldahl method (AOAC 954.01). Ether extract (EE) was determined by diethyl ether Soxhlet extraction (AOAC 2003.05). Crude ash (CA) was measured by ashing at 550 °C (AOAC 942.05). Organic matter (OM) was calculated as OM (%) = DM (%) – CA (%). Carbohydrates (CHO) were calculated as CHO (%) = OM (%) – CP (%) – EE (%).

Gross energy (GE) was calculated using the following formula: GE = 23.64 × CP (g/kg) + 39.54 × EE (g/kg) + 17.5 × CHO (g/kg).

The apparent digestibility (AD) of nutrients was calculated according to De Marco et al. (2015) using the following formula: AD (%) = [1 - (a / b) × (c / d)] × 100. a = AIA content in the feed (mg/kg); b = AIA content in feces (mg/kg); c = Nutrient content in feces (g/kg); d = Nutrient content in the feed (g/kg).

Apparent amino acid digestibility

The amino acid (AA) content in feed and fecal samples (excluding tryptophan) was determined according to AOAC Official Method 994.12. Samples were first hydrolyzed with 6 mol/L hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 h to release AA. For sulfur-containing AA (methionine and cystine), oxidative pretreatment with peracetic acid was performed prior to acid hydrolysis. The hydrolyzed amino acids were separated by ion-exchange chromatography or high-performance liquid chromatography, followed by qualitative and quantitative analysis using amino acid standards. The apparent AA digestibility was calculated using a modified method described by Knabe et al. (1989), according to the following formula: Apparent AA digestibility (%) = [1 - (a / b) × (c / d)] × 100. a = AIA content in feed (mg/kg); b = AIA content in feces (mg/kg); c = AA content in feces (g/kg); d = AA content in feed (g/kg).

Serum biochemical parameters

Preserved serum samples were analyzed using a COBAS MIRA Plus automated biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The following serum metabolites were measured: albumin (ALB), total protein (TP), glucose (GLU), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), creatinine (CRE), TG, and TC. Serum enzyme activities, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), were also determined.

Histological and morphological analysis

Fixed tissue samples from intestinal segments and reproductive organs were dehydrated through a graded ethanol and xylene series according to standard histological procedures, followed by paraffin embedding as described by Zhou et al. (2025). The embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm using a semi-automatic microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Morphological evaluation and image acquisition were performed using an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) coupled with a JD-801 image analysis system (Jeda Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The intestinal morphological parameters included villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and the villus height to crypt depth ratio (V/C). The reproductive organ parameters included mucosal fold height (MFH), mucosal fold perimeter (MFP), mucosal fold width (MFW), and epithelial height (EH) in the ampulla, as well as uterine fold height (UFH) and uterine epithelial height (UEH).

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was assessed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and variables with a P-value greater than 0.05 in the Shapiro-Wilk test were considered to meet the assumption of normality. Subsequently, data were analyzed using the GLM procedure in SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) for one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and polynomial regression analysis was performed with the REG procedure to evaluate the linear and quadratic effects of increasing dietary BAs levels. Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to compare treatment means. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Effects of dietary BAs supplementation on production performance during the late laying period

Effects of dietary BAs supplementation on the production performance of laying hens during the late laying period are presented in Table 2. Compared with the CON group, dietary BAs supplementation significantly increased laying rate (P < 0.05). In addition, laying rate increased linearly and quadratically with increasing dietary BAs levels (linear and quadratic, P < 0.05). Furthermore, dietary BAs supplementation significantly decreased the FCR of laying hens compared with the CON group (P < 0.05). Compared with the BA500 group, BA200 and BA300 significantly decreased FCR (P < 0.05). A significant quadratic relationship was observed between dietary BAs levels and FCR (quadratic, P < 0.05).

Effects of dietary BAs supplementation on apparent nutrient digestibility during the late laying period

Effects of BAs supplementation on apparent nutrient digestibility are shown in Table 6. Significant quadratic increases were observed for DM, CP, EE, and GE digestibility with increasing dietary BAs levels (quadratic, P < 0.05), whereas OM digestibility showed significant linear and quadratic increases (linear and quadratic, P < 0.05). In addition, compared with the CON group, the apparent digestibility of DM, OM, CP, EE, and GE was significantly increased in the BA300 group (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, the BA300 group showed significantly higher apparent digestibility of OM and CP than the BA500 group (P < 0.05), and significantly higher GE digestibility than the BA200 and BA500 groups (P < 0.05).

Effects of dietary BAs supplementation on apparent AA digestibility during the late laying period

Effects of dietary BAs supplementation on apparent amino acid digestibility are shown in Table 7. With increasing dietary BAs levels, the digestibility of arginine, isoleucine, leucine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid increased linearly and quadratically (linear and quadratic, P < 0.05). Moreover, the digestibility of serine and alanine linearly increased with increasing dietary BAs levels (linear, P < 0.05), whereas lysine, methionine, threonine, and valine digestibility quadratically increased (quadratic, P < 0.05). Compared with the CON group, the digestibility of lysine, methionine, threonine, valine, arginine, isoleucine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid was significantly increased in the BA300 group (P < 0.05). In addition, valine digestibility was significantly higher in the BA200 and BA300 groups than in the CON group (P < 0.05), whereas arginine digestibility in the BA300 group was significantly higher than that in the BA200 group (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Both FCR and LR are key indicators of laying performance in commercial production (Wan et al., 2021a; Yang et al., 2022c). Hens experience a gradual decline in their laying rate in the late laying period, which constitutes roughly half of the overall laying period (Liu et al., 2023). In the present study, dietary BAs supplementation increased LR and decreased FCR with the higheat LR and lowest FCR at 300 mg/kg. Yang et al. (2022a) demonstrated that 60 mg/kg BAs improved LR and FCR in 45-week-old Hy-Line Grey laying hens. Xing et al. (2025) also found that dietary supplementation with 120 mg/kg BAs significantly increased LR in Hy-Line Brown laying hens during 62 to 69 weeks of age, and LR showed a continuous increase when the supplementation level was increased to 200 mg/kg during 70 to 75 weeks of age. However, Sun et al. (2023a) indicated that supplementation of 100 and 200 mg/kg porcine BAs in 50-week-old Hy-Line Brown laying hens had no significant effect on LR or FCR. Such discrepancies may stem from differences in hen age, BAs supplementation levels, and experimental duration across studies (Sun et al., 2023a). In summary, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs was identified as the optimal level for improving laying performance in late-laying hens in the present study.

Egg quality is a key factor influencing consumer egg purchases and is often assessed based on external parameters and internal indicators; the internal indicators include Haugh unit, albumen height, and yolk color (Lilburn and McIntyre, 2024). Albumen height and Haugh unit tend to decline with advancing hen age, leading to a deterioration in albumen quality, which consequently results in economic losses to the egg production industry (Nasri et al., 2020; Krawczyk et al., 2021). In this study, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs increased albumen height and Haugh unit in late-laying hens, indicating improved albumen quality during the late laying period. Similarly, Li et al. (2024) also indicated that supplementation with 0.02% BAs increased albumen height and Haugh unit in 28-week-old laying hens. In addition, yolk color is a major determinant of consumer acceptance, and its improvement can enhance consumers’ purchasing preference (Saleh et al., 2021). Wang et al. (2024) reported that dietary supplementation with both low-dose (95.01 mg/kg) and high-dose (189.99 mg/kg) BAs in 75-week-old Hy-line Brown laying hens significantly increased yolk color. Sun et al. (2023b) also found that dietary supplementation with 100 and 200 mg/kg BAs increased yolk color in 58-week-old laying hens. In our study, supplementation with BAs increased yolk color on days 14 and 35, with a 300 mg/kg dosage yielding the optimal effect. Overall, in the present study, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs effectively improved egg quality in late-laying hens.

Serum biochemical parameters are considered important indicators of physiological and metabolic status in poultry (Wan et al., 2021; 2023). Our results showed that supplemented with 300 mg/kg BAs exhibited significantly reduced serum ALT, AST, and ALP activities, along with increased ALB concentration. Serum ALT and AST activities are sensitive indicators of hepatocellular damage, whereas increased ALP activity is associated with cholestasis and impaired bile excretion (Kajal et al., 2023; Lala et al., 2023). As ALB is synthesized exclusively in the liver and reflects hepatic protein synthesis capacity, its elevation also suggests improved liver function (Spinella et al., 2016; Moman et al., 2025). Therefore, these changes in the present study indicated that dietary BAs supplementation improved hepatic function and bile flow in late-laying hens. In addition, we also found that serum GLU concentration was reduced in hens fed the diet supplemented with 300 mg/kg BAs. Schokker et al. (2021) found that serum GLU levels were lower in high-producing laying hens than in low-producing hens at 95 weeks of age. Similarly, Liu et al. (2024) observed that decreased serum GLU occurred concurrently with increased egg production rate. These findings suggested that a moderate reduction in serum GLU was positively associated with the maintenance of laying performance. In parallel, serum TG and TC concentrations were significantly lower in the 300 mg/kg BAs group than in the CON group, which was consistent with the results of previous study (Yang et al., 2022a). BAs are known to promote cholesterol conversion and excretion in the liver, thereby enhancing overall lipid metabolism (Li and Chiang, 2009; Chiang, 2013). Improved lipid metabolism efficiency could facilitate the transport of lipids from the circulation to the ovary to support follicular development, thereby improving production performance in late-laying hens (Ji et al., 2023). Collectively, in this study, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs improved serum biochemical parameters in aged laying hens, contributing to better production performance in hens.

In laying hens, the oviduct receives the ovum released from the ovary and provides the biological environment required for the sequential formation of each egg component (Sah and Mishra, 2018). The magnum of the oviduct, as the primary site of albumen formation, may influence albumen quality through alterations in its morphology (Sah and Mishra, 2018; Sah et al., 2021). In the present study, dietary supplementation with BAs increased MFH, MFP, MFW, and EH, with the most pronounced effects observed in the 300 mg/kg BAs group. Toussant et al. (1995) reported that hens laying eggs with higher Haugh unit values exhibited significantly greater MFH and EH in the magnum than hens laying eggs with lower Haugh unit values. The results of the present study suggested that dietary BAs supplementation markedly improved the histomorphological characteristics of the magnum in late-laying hens, as evidenced by increased MFH, MFP, MFW, and EH. In addition, Chang et al. (2024), based on a longitudinal study of 20,000 Hy-Line Brown laying hens from 20 to 80 weeks of age, demonstrated that albumen height and Haugh unit were closely synchronized with changes in magnum histomorphology. Therefore, the improvements in magnum morphology observed in the 300 mg/kg BAs supplementation group in the present study provided a structural basis for the enhancement of albumen quality, particularly the increases in albumen height and Haugh unit. In addition to the oviduct magnum, dietary BAs supplementation also increased UFH and UEH in laying hens. However, no improvement in eggshell quality was observed despite the enhanced uterine morphology. This may be attributed to the fact that eggshell quality is a complex trait regulated by multiple factors, rather than relying solely on the structural integrity of the uterus (Jonchère et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2019). Although dietary supplementation with different levels of BAs did not significantly affect ovarian histomorphology in late-laying hens, a higher proportion of atretic follicles was observed in the control group. The reduced number of atretic follicles in the BAs-treated group suggested that BAs supplementation might delay follicular atresia, thereby contributing to the maintenance of ovarian function during the late laying period (Xing et al., 2026). Collectively, these results suggested that BAs supplementation might improve egg quality in late-laying hens by enhancing the structural integrity of the oviduct and alleviating age-related degenerative changes in the ovary.

Given the observed improvements in reproductive tract morphology and egg quality, it is reasonable to hypothesize that BAs supplementation may enhance nutrient utilization efficiency to support these physiological processes (Arshad et al., 2021; Iqbal et al., 2025). In the present study, supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs significantly increased the apparent digestibility of DM, OM, CP, EE, and GE in late-laying hens. Studies have shown that improved EE digestibility facilitated hepatic lipogenesis and the synthesis and secretion of yolk precursors, thereby promoting follicular development and sustaining egg production (Wu et al., 2024), while also enhancing the intestinal absorption of fat-soluble carotenoids, which are the principal determinants of yolk color (Yang et al., 2022b). Li et al. (2025) reported that improving CP availability could enhance egg white quality of laying hens. These findings might explain the aforementioned finding that supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs improved LR and egg quality in late-laying hens. Notably, the improvement in macronutrient digestibility was accompanied by increased digestibility of key amino acids, including methionine, lysine, threonine, valine, arginine, isoleucine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid. Methionine and lysine, the first and second limiting AAs in poultry diets, play essential roles in protein synthesis and egg production (Bunchasak, 2009; Chang et al., 2018); their increased availability has directly supported albumen protein deposition, thereby contributing to the improvements in albumen height and Haugh unit observed in this study (Macelline et al., 2021). In addition, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and threonine are associated with intestinal development and the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and can enhance intestinal absorptive function, thereby contributing to enhanced laying performance in hens (He et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2017). In addition, branched-chain AAs, including valine and isoleucine, are involved in facilitating the transport of yolk precursors to the ovary, thereby influencing yolk formation (Kim et al., 2022). Arginine, as a key substrate for protein synthesis, directly supports the synthesis of albumen proteins when its availability is increased (Dao et al., 2021). In summary, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs improved both apparent nutrient and AA digestibility in late-laying hens, providing a nutritional basis for the observed improvements in laying performance and egg quality.

Intestinal morphology, including VH, CD, and V/C, is an important indicator of intestinal health. The increase in VH directly expands the effective absorptive surface area of the intestinal mucosa, while a higher V/C reflects a greater proportion of functionally mature epithelial cells (Belote et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). Increased VH, decreased CD, and an elevated V/C suggest enhanced digestive and absorptive capacity of the intestine (Pirarat et al., 2011). In the present study, dietary supplementation with BAs significantly increased VH in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, as well as the V/C in the jejunum and ileum of late-laying hens, with the most pronounced effects observed at 300 mg/kg. Similarly, Liu et al. (2025) reported that BAs supplementation increased jejunal VH in broilers. Similar results were also found in studies on pigeons (Ma et al., 2023) and geese (Li et al., 2023). Therefore, dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs improved intestinal morphology, thereby providing structural support for the enhanced nutrient utilization in late-laying hens.

Conclusions

Dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg BAs could improve production performance, egg quality, and apparent digestibility of nutrients and amino acids in late-laying hens. These benefits were accompanied by favorable shifts in serum biochemical parameters and improvements in reproductive organ morphology. Our findings provided new insights into the role of bile acids in regulating production performance and improving egg quality during the late laying period.
