Ultrafast switching and high-endurance nonvolatile memory enabled by intrinsic switchable polarization in semiconducting Janus monolayers
Authors: Ngoc Thanh Duong, Swathi Krishna, Minh Chien Nguyen, Hang Sik Kim, Vu Tien Lam, Hamza Zad Gul, Soo Ho Choi, Minjeong Kim, Daekwon Lee, Thi Uyen Tran, Vu Khac Dat, Van Dam Do, Van Huy Hoang, Heesuk Rho, Young-Min Kim, Woo Jong Yu, Ki Kang Kim, Seong Chu Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The discovery of two-dimensional (2D) semiconducting ferroelectric (SFe) materials marks a pivotal step toward the realization of ferroelectric random-access memory. Despite their potential as ideal platforms for ultrafast switching and ultralow power consumption, SFe monolayers remain largely unexplored. In this work, we demonstrate field-effect transistor employing monolayer Janus MoSeS, which exhibits switchable polarization when integrated on an amorphous ZrO2 layer with a high dielectric constant. The non-centrosymmetric structure of MoSeS enables gate-switchable spontaneous out-of-plane polarization, attributed to atomic displacements. The device demonstrates counterclockwise (CCW) memory windows of ~5 V, an on/off ratio exceeding 106 at zero gate bias, and a retention time much longer than 10,000 s. Furthermore, it achieves fatigue-free endurance over 107 cycles and ultrafast responses to 16.5 ns voltage spikes, resulting in ultralow power consumption of 113.9 fJ for programming and 89.8 fJ for erasing in each cycle. These performances establish Janus monolayers as promising candidates for next-generation nonvolatile memory and neuromorphic computing applications. Further work will be necessary to establish the switching mechanisms of the devices.

Introduction

Compute-in-memory architecture has emerged as a promising solution to the challenges of vast unstructured and redundant data in the era of big data and the Internet of Things, often outperforming conventional von Neumann architectures1–3. Modern computing systems remain fundamentally limited by the mismatch between processor speed and memory access latency. While central processing units (CPUs), graphics processing units (GPUs), and neural processing units (NPUs) operate at sub-nanosecond timescales, commercial nonvolatile memory technologies, such as solid-state drives (SSDs), mainly use floating-gate transistors, which are limited by slow write/erase speeds (~0.1 ms), complex fabrication, and restricted bandwidth4. This large latency gap creates a fundamental bottleneck in data transfer between processing units and memory, commonly referred to as the von Neumann bottleneck. Ferroelectric field-effect transistors (Fe-FETs) have garnered attention for their low power consumption and high-speed write/read operations, making them appealing candidates for nonvolatile memory applications5. However, ferroelectric random-access memory (FeRAM) fabrication remains costly because of material complexities and high-temperature processing requirements. Furthermore, scalability to sub-100 nm process nodes, a critical factor for increasing memory density and reducing costs, has proven challenging. Many ferroelectric materials also suffer from fatigue effects and native depolarization fields, limiting endurance and retention capabilities5,6.

Recent advancements in semiconducting ferroelectric (SFe) materials have introduced a novel paradigm for next-generation electronics, utilizing ferroelectric materials as channels rather than gate dielectrics. Two-dimensional (2D) ferroelectric compound semiconductors from III-VI groups, described by the formula M2X3 (M = Al, In, Ga; X = S, Se, Te), exhibit non-centrosymmetric unit cells. In quintuple-layer α-In2Se3, the ionic displacement of the central Se atom drives polarization7,8. Similarly, 3R-stacked bilayer MoS29 and artificially twisted bilayer hBN10,11 have shown promise as polarization-based information storage materials for non-volatile memory applications. These materials generate out-of-plane electric dipoles via charge redistribution in non-centrosymmetric interlayers, driven by hybridization between occupied and unoccupied states of adjacent layers. Polarization in these systems can also be induced and controlled by integrating incommensurate 2D layers. Challenges in the scalable synthesis of phase-controlled monolayers of In2Se3 or precise stacking order in bilayer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) remain significant for large-scale electronics12. Although monolayer semiconductors with switchable polarization present an ideal platform for low-power and high-speed memory, they have remained unexplored.

In this work, we developed a switchable-polarization field-effect transistor (SP-FET) nonvolatile memory using monolayer semiconducting Janus MoSeS. The non-centrosymmetric unit cell was achieved by selectively replacing the top sulfur atoms with selenium in monolayer MoS2 via hydrogen plasma treatment at room temperature. The Janus structure inheres a strong vertical dipole, which is switchable via atomic displacement by an external field, as confirmed by first-principles calculations, and cross-sectional STEM imaging. By integrating high-κ (κ denotes dielectric constant) amorphous ZrO2 as the gate dielectric, offering fewer interface traps compared to SiO213,14, the cyclic gate sweep of MoSeS SP-FET devices exhibits robust counterclockwise (CCW) hysteresis with a 5 V memory window and an on/off ratio of 107, reproducible across over 100 devices. A fatigue-free endurance exceeds 107 program/erase cycles. Furthermore, the memory responds to ultrafast voltage spikes as short as 16.5 nanoseconds, with an ultralow power consumption of 113.9/89.8 fJ per write/erase spike.

Results

Janus monolayer synthesis and switchable-polarization transistors

Janus TMDs with the formula MX1X2 (M = transition metals; X1, X2 = chalcogens (S, Se, Te); X1 ≠ X2), represent a unique class of two-dimensional materials, featuring a metal atom sandwiched between two different chalcogen atoms on either side of the unit cell (Fig. 1a)15–18. To synthesize the monolayer Janus structure, a monolayer MoS2 film was first grown on a SiO2/Si substrate using chemical vapor deposition (CVD) (see Method). The film was then transferred to a custom-designed remote plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (RP-ECVD) system, where it was exposed to hydrogen plasma and selenium at room temperature (Fig. 1a; Supplementary Fig. 1)16,19. In addition to lattice vibrations, the bandgap evolution also reflects the structural transformation from MoS2 to MoSeS. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra recorded at various deposition times show a redshift in emission peaks from 1.82 eV (MoS2) to 1.72 eV (MoSeS), eventually approaching the characteristic bandgap of monolayer MoSe2 (Fig. 1c). These spectral changes further validate the gradual selenization process and the structural evolution of the Janus monolayer.

Furthermore, structural and elemental analyses were conducted to confirm the atomic replacement using annular dark field -scanning transmission electron microscopy (ADF-STEM). A representative false-colored ADF-STEM image, acquired after 1.5 h of conversion and denoised using a deep neural network20, was used to quantify the atomic site occupancies, including S vacancies (Svac), S2 (S atoms at both the top and bottom layers), SeS (the Janus configuration), and Se2 (Se atoms occupying both layers) (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Figs. 3–8). The analysis revealed that approximately 87% of MoS2 was converted to MoSeS after 1.5 h of selenization (Fig. 1e).

To further investigate the chalcogen transformation kinetics, a statistical analysis of atomic quantification results from all growth conditions was performed (Fig. 1f). The analysis revealed a linear increase of SeS site occupancy (66.3 % for 0.5 h – 77.4 % for 1 h–87.0 % for 1.5 h) with a corresponding decrease of S2 site occupancy (29.1–19.5–6.9%). In contrast, the concentration of Svac exhibited non-linear fluctuation, suggesting an inherent presence of chalcogen vacancies across all samples21. Additionally, the negligible increase in Se2 species implies that the conversion predominantly involves top-side stripping of S and subsequent replacement with Se. Chemical composition analysis using STEM-energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDX) further confirmed the successful conversion of MoS2 to MoSeS (Supplementary Fig. 9). This time-dependent analysis underscores the dynamic changes in species concentrations, highlighting the gradual replacement of sulfur with selenium in the monolayer and the progression toward a stable Janus MoSeS structure.

For electrical characterization, hBN/MoSeS/hBN sandwich devices were fabricated to apply a vertical electric field to the MoSeS layer through nonpolar hBN sheets (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 10). Under a back-gate voltage Vbg = 60 V, the cyclic sweep of top-gate voltage (Vtg, forward −6 V → 8 V, backward 8 V → −6 V) exhibited bistability in conductance between 300 and 100 K. The hysteresis width (ΔV) increased from 2 V at 300 K to 12 V at 100 K (Fig. 1h), signifying the polarization switching instead of charge trapping. This conductance loop at room temperature was further reproduced under back-gate voltage sweeps. Notably, devices with a high equivalent oxide thickness (EOT) of 285 nm SiO2 displayed weak clockwise hysteresis above 260 K, which was attributed to the high oxide-trap density in SiO2. At higher temperatures, more ionic impurities in the SiO2 substrate become ionized, and subsequently, they will capture and release free carriers, contributing to hysteretic transfer curves22. Conversely, below 260 K, the SiO2 trap sites will be deionized and neutral, not responding to the gate electric field. Instead, the inherent dipoles of the MoSeS crystal strongly interact with the external gate electric field at low temperature. Consequently, the hysteresis loop reverted to counterclockwise behavior during the down sweep (Supplementary Figs. 11–15 and Supplementary Note 1–3).

To verify the switchable polarization of MoSeS and examine the effect of gate dielectric, we fabricated multiple MoSeS FETs incorporating: (1) low resistivity p++-Si 285-nm SiO2 substrate, (2) 16-nm high-κ amorphous HfO2 (a-HfO2), and (3) 15-nm high-κ amorphous ZrO2 (a-ZrO2) gate dielectric deposited via atomic layer deposition (ALD) at a temperature of 250 °C. This deposition process results in an amorphous structure of both gate dielectrics, which do not exhibit ferroelectric properties (Supplementary Figs. 12, 13 and Supplementary Note 2). The temperature is compatible with the back-end-of-line process. The transfer curves of Janus MoSeS monolayer transistors fabricated on SiO2 (285 nm, κ = ~3.9), a-HfO2 (16 nm, κ = ~25), and a-ZrO2 (15 nm, κ = ~32.57) gate dielectrics are compared with each other at 300 K under Vd of 10 mV. Cyclic transfer curves showed CCW hysteresis loops at room temperature from all devices (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 14). These bistable conductance states persist despite the variations of the drain–source voltage (Vds) (Supplementary Fig. 15). In particular, a-HfO2 and a-ZrO2-gated devices exhibit much larger polarization-induced hysteresis window, compared to that of SiO2 dielectric, suggesting the emergence of field-driven polarization switching by the high-κ dielectric layer. Together with consistent CCW hysteresis in Fig. 1i, the conductance turn-on occurs approximately 0.1 Vnm−1 regardless of oxide substrates, further confirming the modulation of the channel conductance dominated by switchable polarization. In contrast, multiple MoS2 FETs exhibit clockwise hysteresis under the same device structure, shown in Supplementary Fig. 15d.

Figure 1j explains that a positive gate bias aligns polarization upward, shifting the Fermi level of MoSeS, causing mobile charge accumulation, and contributing the FET to a low resistance state (LRS). Conversely, a negative gate bias suppresses polarization, concurrently depleting mobile charges from the channel and transitioning the device to a high resistance state (HRS). Although the responsible mechanism can be further detailed after theoretical calculation, including the screening and redistribution of mobile charges, it is expected that the external gate bias modulates the atomic polarization of MoSeS6.

Structural origin and polarization switching in Janus monolayers

We conducted theoretical calculations using density functional theory (DFT) to investigate the origin of polarization switching behavior in the Janus MoSeS monolayer under applied external electric fields (Methods). The calculations examined the relative positional shift of Mo and Se atoms with respect to S atoms within an electric field range of ±2 V/Å, comparable with estimates obtained from COMSOL simulations (Supplementary Fig. 16 and Note 4). Furthermore, the polarization states varying upon the position of Mo and Se atomic planes relative to the S atomic plane were taken into account (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Figs. 17a, b, and Note 4). Under a positive (negative) field, the distance between the Mo-S atomic planes (dMo-S) decreases (increases) significantly from the equilibrium position, while the Mo-Se distance (dMo-Se) increases (decreases). Both dMo-Se and dMo-S exhibit stronger response under positive bias, which is aligned with the inherent dipole direction, compared to negative bias. These variations induce a redistribution of charge density through charge transfer in the Janus MoSeS monolayer, leading to a reversal of polarization (P) direction (insets of Fig. 2a, b). Interestingly, dMo-Se exhibits a pronounced CCW hysteresis relative to the equilibrium position under an external electric field. Additionally, structural analysis using the cross-sectional STEM images was conducted to support the polarizability caused by atomic displacement. Our investigation revealed the presence of two distinct atomic configurations in dMo-Se and dMo-S at different locations across the Janus MoSeS monolayer (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 18), leading to the existence of different polarization states. Furthermore, we found that the Janus MoSeS monolayer has a switching energy barrier (Eb) of approximately 90 meV, as determined by normalizing the total energy of each switching pathway (Supplementary Fig. 17c). This asymmetric switching barrier is probably attributed to the broken symmetry of binding energies inherent in Janus structures. Such asymmetry-driven polarization dynamics are not typically observed in conventional sliding or bulk ferroelectric systems23–25.

As the driving voltage increases, a pronounced color contrast differentiates the Janus MoSeS region from the substrate (Fig. 2d), confirming a significant OOP piezoelectric response by MoSeS. The PFM deformation amplitude (ΔZ, in picometers) increases linearly with the applied AC voltage (VAC) (Fig. 2e). We employed two distinct PFM modes. The 180° phase switching in Fig. 2h was obtained using pulse-mode (off-field) spectroscopy, which probes the remanent electromechanical response and shows clear reversible switching with a butterfly-shaped amplitude loop, confirming bistable out-of-plane polarization. The reduced phase contrast observed in imaging-mode PFM arises from the different measurement geometry and electrostatic conditions. Janus MoSeS is an n-type semiconducting monolayer in which polarization is coupled to the local band modulation and generation of mobile carriers; thus, the displacement field includes a free-carrier contribution (Supplementary Note 5)28–30. Under quasi-static raster scanning, mobile carriers can redistribute and partially screen bound polarization charges, reducing the apparent electromechanical response detected in imaging mode. This effect is enhanced in the ultrathin limit (~0.7 nm), where the entire layer lies within the electronic screening length. The reduced phase contrast has also been widely reported in other ultrathin 2D ferroelectrics. Nevertheless, the intrinsic polarization switching with full phase reversal is strongly supported by independent pulse-mode spectroscopies31,32.

The retention time of the nonvolatile memory was measured using standard pulse-IV measurements at Vg = 0 V and Vd = 100 mV after programming and erasing with pulse gate voltages of Vg = +5 and −5 V, respectively, for 1 s (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 30). Scaling the effective programming/erasing speed to the nanosecond regime can be achieved with ferroelectric-based memory, a capability unattainable with conventional trapping/de-trapping devices. The custom-designed hardware for standard pulse and transient IV measurements is shown in Supplementary Fig. 31, and Fig. 4d, respectively, and was used to effectively switch the nonvolatile memory with a 50 ns full-width at half maximum (FWHM) pulse (Fig. 4e). A high-bandwidth oscilloscope captured the reference or amplified voltage bursts. We benchmarked the performance of various device parameters among 2D SP-FETs in terms of energy per spike, endurance, and write speed (Fig. 4i). Monolayer Janus MoSeS outperforms all parameters, positioning it as a highly promising candidate for next-generation nonvolatile memory and neuromorphic devices. The Janus MoSeS device exhibits superior endurance and ultrafast operation, distinguishing it from previous 2D ferroelectric memory systems. While conventional α-In2Se3 and SnS ferroelectrics typically suffer from slow polarization dynamics and limited cycle stability (<104 cycles) and sliding ferroelectrics like 3R-MoS2 are constrained by mechanical interlayer motion, the Janus nonvolatile memory achieves robust electrical switching through intrinsic lattice asymmetry and strong out-of-plane dipole coupling (Fig. 4i). This unique polarization mechanism enables rapid and reversible domain reorientation without structural degradation, leading to endurance exceeding 107 cycles and stable retention. Additionally, unlike floating-gate or trap-based memories that rely on charge storage in defect states, the Janus SP device operates via true polarization reversal in a semiconducting lattice, ensuring both speed and long-term reliability.

Discussion

We demonstrate a nonvolatile memory platform based on monolayer Janus MoSeS, exhibiting large memory windows, fatigue-free endurance, and long-term retention. Compared with conventional charge-trapping memories and previously reported ferroelectric layered semiconductors, the present device, operating through polarization-modulated conductance, demonstrates not only superior performance in terms of ultrafast switching and low-power operation but also offers distinct advantages in scalability and compatibility with modern field-effect transistor architectures. These results highlight the potential of semiconducting Janus monolayers for next-generation memory technologies.

While the primary focus of this work remains the switching polarization of the Janus monolayer, the high degree of control over the internal dipole allows for the emulation of multi-level states. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 38, the MoSeS SP-FET effectively emulates artificial synaptic functions, including long-term potentiation (LTP), long-term depression (LTD), and spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), while maintaining a high conductance (G) ratio, Gmin /Gmax. These findings underscore the potential of monolayer Janus materials as foundational elements for neuromorphic hardware, paving the way for more energy-efficient in-memory processing. Further work will be necessary to establish the switching mechanisms of the devices for advanced synaptic operation in addition to the memory function.

Methods

Growth of MoS2 using CVD

A two-zone atmospheric pressure CVD system was employed to grow monolayer MoS2 films on SiO2/Si. A uniform layer of the Mo precursor (0.01 M sodium molybdate dihydrate, Na2MoO4⋅2H2 O, ≥ 99%, Sigma Aldrich) was spin-coated on SiO2/Si at 3000 rpm for 60 s. The precursor-coated substrate was placed in the downstream zone of a quartz tube within the CVD system, while sulfur powder, serving as the S precursor, was positioned in the upstream zone. The downstream and upstream zones were heated to 750 °C and 200 °C, respectively, over 7 min and maintained for 9 min to facilitate the growth of the MoS2 film. Ar gas was supplied at 200 sccm throughout the process. After the growth, the furnace was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature.

Conversion of monolayer MoS2 to monolayer MoSeS using RPECVD

A custom-built RPECVD system was employed to convert MoS2 to MoSeS. The MoS2 sample was placed downstream of the plasma tail generated by a 50 W RF power source connected to a three-turn copper inductor within a 1.6-inch-diameter quartz tube. Before starting the conversion process, the system was evacuated to a pressure of 250 mTorr while introducing 30 sccm of hydrogen gas to create the plasma environment. Selenium pellets were positioned near the hydrogen plasma zone to enable the delivery of selenium vapor to the MoS2 sample (Supplementary Fig. 1). Hydrogen plasma selectively removed the top-layer sulfur atoms, facilitating their replacement with selenium. The conversion process was optimized by adjusting the duration of exposure to plasma.

First-principles calculations

First-principles calculations were performed using the CASTEP package36, based on the plane-wave pseudopotential approach37. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV was employed, and the total energy was converged to within 10-6 eV. The generalized gradient approximation formulated by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof38 was utilized to treat electron exchange and correlation effects. Brillouin zone sampling was constructed using Monkhorst-Pack grids of 6 × 6 × 1 and 7 × 7 × 1 for the geometry optimization and the electronic structure calculations, respectively. To prevent interlayer interactions in the periodic space, a vacuum slab of 15 Å was introduced. Structural relaxations were carried out via the conjugate gradient algorithm until the residual forces on each atom are smaller than 0.01 eV/Å.

Material characterization

Optical properties and thickness measurements

The optical properties of the sample were analyzed with a confocal Raman spectrometer (Nanobase, Korea), using a 532 nm excitation wavelength. The thickness of the sample was measured using an atomic force microscope (Hitachi High-Tech Science Corporation, Japan) in dynamic force microscope mode.

Quantitative analysis of atomic species (Se2, SeS, S2, and Svac) in Janus MoSeS

Samples were transferred onto copper grids (PELCO, 200 mesh, copper, with 1.2 µm holes) using a standard wet-transfer method with poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA). Initially, the samples coated with PMMA were allowed to air dry. They were then immersed in a 3 M potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution to detach the MoSeS layer from the Si/SiO2 substrate. After separation, they were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water multiple times to remove residual KOH. The PMMA-coated samples were transferred onto transmission electron microscopy (TEM) grids and treated with acetone to dissolve and remove the PMMA layer. The resulting samples were analyzed using an aberration-corrected TEM (ARM200CF, JEOL Ltd., Japan) at 80 kV. ADF-STEM images were acquired under the following conditions: a semi-convergence angle of 24 mrad, an acceptance angle range of 34–136 mrad, and a probe current of 25 pA. The multislice calculation method in the qSTEM software was used for image simulation, maintaining the same parameters as the experiment for accurate comparison. To reduce sample damage and scan distortions during ADF-STEM imaging, a scan rate of 4 µs per pixel was applied. A deep-learning-based denoising model was utilized to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio in the STEM-ADF images, effectively removing statistical background noise with an accuracy of over 98%. This denoising approach significantly improved image clarity and reliability for subsequent quantitative analysis.

Device fabrication

Fabrication of hBN/MoSeS/hBN sandwich heterostructure

The hBN flakes (~30 nm thick) were exfoliated from the bulk crystal on Si/SiO2 (285 nm), and MoSeS flakes (grown by CVD) were transferred onto hBN using poly(propylene) carbonate films39–42. Ti/Au (10/50 nm) drain/source electrodes were patterned by electron-beam lithography (EBL, JSM-IT200) and deposited using a high-vacuum thermal evaporator. Another hBN flake exfoliated on a polydimethylsiloxane film is accurately aligned and staked on top of a monolayer MoSeS/hBN structure, creating a sandwiched hBN/MoSeS/hBN, followed by top gate electrode deposition.

Fabrication of monolayer Janus-based MoSeS memory on 15-nm high-κ a-ZrO2

A 30 nm tungsten layer was deposited onto a p++ Si/SiO2 (285 nm) substrate using a metal and dielectric sputtering system. Back-gate electrodes were defined with the Heidelberg µPG 101 micropattern generator; subsequently, the wafer was immersed in a tungsten etchant for 25 s to remove exposed tungsten. The electrodes were then rinsed with organic solvents. A 15-nm ZrO2 dielectric layer was deposited using 160 cycles of ALD at 250 °C. A full-film monolayer of MoSeS was transferred onto the a-ZrO2 surface and subsequently patterned into bar-shaped regions with a width of 5 µm (Fig. 2b) using EBL and reactive ion etching (RIE) with SF6 gas for 10 seconds. Finally, Ti/Au (10/50 nm) drain and source electrodes were patterned and deposited using EBL and a thermal evaporator, respectively.

Device characterization

Standard DC electrical measurements were performed using high-resolution and medium-power source measurement units integrated into the Keysight B5100A semiconductor device analyzer mainframe. Pulsed and transient I-V measurements were executed using a Keysight 33600 A True Waveform Generator (120 MHz), while an MDO 34 Tektronix oscilloscope captured the nanosecond pulses. The current is pre-amplified by FEMTO DHPCA 100 at a trans-impedance of 107 V/A. All the equipment was controlled using NI LabVIEW 2023 Community (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31 illustrate the homemade measurement setup).
