Sporotrichoid lymphocutaneous infection caused by Candida parapsilosis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Oranat Tanprasertkul, Phatcharawat Chirasuthat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sporotrichoid distribution is classically associated with Sporothrix schenckii, yet Candida parapsilosis remains an exceedingly rare cause. We report a primary cutaneous C. parapsilosis infection presenting with lymphocutaneous spread. The patient achieved significant clinical improvement following oral fluconazole. This case expands the recognized clinical spectrum of C. parapsilosis and underscores the necessity of fungal culture in the differential diagnosis of sporotrichoid spreading lesions to ensure prompt, targeted antifungal therapy.

Introduction

The clinical presentation of linear nodular lymphangitis, classically termed sporotrichoid spreading, remains a significant diagnostic challenge due to the wide array of potential infectious etiologies. Historically, Sporothrix schenckii, Mycobacterium marinum, Nocardia species, and Leishmania braziliensis [1]were identified as the primary pathogens associated with this pattern; however, recent clinical data suggest a notable shift in regional epidemiology. A retrospective analysis from Songklanagarind Hospital indicates that dematiaceous fungi have emerged as the predominant causative agents in Southern Thailand, surpassing S. schenckii [2]. Furthermore, non tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) represent a critical subset of infections that can mimic fungal presentations both clinically and histologically, and have been reported as important causative pathogens of cutaneous infection in Thailand [3].

Amidst these common etiologies, cutaneous infection caused by Candida species, particularly C. parapsilosis, remains exceedingly rare. While C. parapsilosis is a known commensal of human skin, its transition to an invasive primary pathogen is seldom documented in the literature. A review of the PubMed database between 2007 and 2023 identified only 12 published cases of primary cutaneous C. parapsilosis infection [4]. Of these cases, the majority manifested as onychomycosis rather than non-ungual cutaneous lesions, typically occurring in immunocompetent hosts without a definitive history of trauma [4].

The pathogenesis of C. parapsilosis is largely dictated by its route of acquisition. As an organism widely isolated from environmental sources such as soil, it can initiate primary cutaneous infection through exogenous inoculation via a breach in the skin barrier [5]. Conversely, secondary cutaneous involvement typically arises from endogenous hematogenous seeding in critically ill patients, often driven by the organism's ability to form biofilms on intravascular devices and its morphological transition between yeast and pseudohyphal forms [5].

While therapeutic management is generally supported by the organism's broad susceptibility to systemic azoles, including fluconazole and itraconazole, the optimal regimen for atypical cutaneous presentations remains to be fully elucidated [6]. To our knowledge, no previous cases of C. parapsilosis presenting with a sporotrichoid spreading pattern have been reported. By presenting this unique case, we aim to expand the existing literature on the rare clinical spectrum of C. parapsilosis and underscore the necessity of considering this pathogen in the differential diagnosis of nodular lymphangitis, even in immunocompetent individuals.

Case report

A 44-year-old Thai male with well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidemia presented to our department with a two-month history of multiple non-healing erythematous nodules on his right arm. The lesions had a soft consistency in texture and exhibited a distinct sporotrichoid distribution (Fig. 1), notably in the absence of regional lymphadenopathy, history of local trauma, or animal contact. Subsequent molecular and microbiological investigations were initiated. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing for the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and nontuberculous mycobacteria was negative, and initial bacterial and mycobacterial cultures yielded no growth. Empiric therapy with a potassium iodide solution (20 mg/mL) was initially started for suspected lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis.

On day 14, the tissue fungal culture grew numerous colonies of Candida parapsilosis. This isolate was subsequently re-confirmed by the microbiology laboratory, supporting its clinical significance rather than incidental contamination. Following this mycological confirmation, potassium iodide was discontinued, and oral fluconazole 400 mg once daily was initiated as targeted antifungal therapy.

Because the lesions had almost completely resolved after two weeks of daily treatment, the fluconazole regimen was stepped down to 400 mg once weekly on day 28 to complete a total two-month course. Discussion

This case is noteworthy because Candida parapsilosis presented with a lymphocutaneous sporotrichoid pattern, a manifestation not previously described in the available literature to our knowledge. Most reported cases of primary cutaneous C. parapsilosis infection have involved onychomycosis or localized cutaneous granuloma rather than nodular lymphangitis. Clinically, this pattern may closely mimic sporotrichosis, nontuberculous mycobacterial infection, nocardiosis, or other deep fungal infections, making tissue culture essential for diagnosis.

Interestingly, fungal elements may not be identified on histopathological examination despite granulomatous inflammation, particularly when organisms are sparse or unevenly distributed within tissue [7]. The limited sensitivity of H&E, PAS, and GMS stains, together with reported false-negative biopsy results in up to 30% of confirmed cases, underscores that negative histopathological findings do not exclude a fungal etiology [8]. In such cases, repeated isolation of the same organism in culture and subsequent clinical response to targeted antifungal therapy provide important supportive evidence for diagnosis [9].

The pathogenesis of cutaneous C. parapsilosis is largely dictated by its route of acquisition. As an organism widely isolated from environmental sources such as soil [5]. It can cause primary infection through exogenous inoculation via a breach in the skin barrier. While onychomycosis remains the most common presentation, our case represents a highly unusual clinical spectrum. To our knowledge, there have been no previously reported cases of C. parapsilosis specifically presenting with a sporotrichoid spreading pattern. This highlights the potential of C. parapsilosis to transition from a common commensal to act as a primary cutaneous pathogen, even in hosts with relatively preserved immune function.

Candida parapsilosis infection is not standardized because of its rarity. The 2016 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) candidiasis guidelines primarily address candidemia and invasive candidiasis rather than localized cutaneous infection; however, they note that C. parapsilosis has intrinsically higher echinocandin minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) than most other Candida species. Fluconazole remains a reasonable therapeutic option for selected clinically stable patients when resistance is unlikely or susceptibility is confirmed. Nevertheless, emerging azole resistance in C. parapsilosis has been increasingly recognized, highlighting the importance of antifungal susceptibility testing in clinically significant or refractory cases [6,10,11].

Due to this intrinsic resistance profile, the IDSA guidelines recommend fluconazole as the definitive first-line agent for confirmed, susceptible C. parapsilosis infections [10]. In selected patient, an intravenous or oral fluconazole (800mg loading dose, followed by 400 mg daily) serves as an acceptable alternative to an echinocandin [6]. Clinicians must, however, remain vigilant regarding emerging epidemiological shifts, as recent data from the Asia-Pacific region reveal a rising fluconazole-resistance rate of approximately 11.1% in C. parapsilosis [8], primarily driven by point mutations within the ERG11 gene [12].

Prognostically, the literature indicates a high success rate. In the reviewed cases, clinical resolution was achieved following appropriate antifungal therapy. A detailed review of all published cases is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The duration of therapy appears to be contingent upon the clinical manifestation; the median treatment duration for onychomycosis is 90 days, whereas candidal granuloma typically requires a shorter course, with a median duration of 47.5 days [4]. Itraconazole has been the most frequently utilized agent in the literature, Wu and colleagues reported that fluconazole was used successfully in 25% of the reviewed cutaneous C. parapsilosis cases [13].

In the present case, our patient which is an immunocompetent male with no prior history of trauma, demonstrated remarkable clinical resolution following a targeted regimen of fluconazole. A notable limitation of this report is that antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed. Given the global emergence of fluconazole-nonsusceptible C. parapsilosis, the favorable clinical response observed in this patient should be interpreted as a case-specific outcome rather than definitive evidence of in vitro susceptibility. Antifungal susceptibility testing should be considered for clinically significant or refractory cutaneous C. parapsilosis infections to optimize targeted therapy. In the present case, the fluconazole dose was reduced to 400 mg once weekly after near-complete clinical resolution following an initial two-week daily regimen. Because antifungal susceptibility testing was not performed and standardized treatment regimens for sporotrichoid cutaneous C. parapsilosis infection are lacking, this dosing strategy should be interpreted as case-specific rather than a standard recommendation.

In summary, we report a rare case of C. parapsilosis presenting in a sporotrichoid pattern in an immunocompetent host. This report serves to expand the existing literature by documenting a novel clinical manifestation of this uncommon cutaneous pathogen. It underscores the necessity of maintaining a high index of suspicion for Candida species in the differential diagnosis of nodular lymphangitis. Ultimately, performing skin biopsy for both histopathological examination and tissue fungal culture is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and ensure timely, targeted treatment.
