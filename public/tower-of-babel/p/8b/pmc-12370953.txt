rRNA loss induced by rifampicin addition in Escherichia coli reflects extraction artifacts rather than in vivo degradation
Authors: Mathilde Burck, Sébastien Nouaille, Agamemnon J. Carpousis, Muriel Cocaign-Bousquet, Laurence Girbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
When measuring RNA stability in Escherichia coli, RNA synthesis is usually stopped using rifampicin. Although rRNAs are considered stable in vivo, Hamouche et al. Ribosomal RNA degradation induced by the bacterial RNA polymerase inhibitor rifampicin. RNA 27, 946–958 (2021) recently presented experimental evidence that rifampicin treatment leads to a 50% decrease in total RNA levels due to degradation of 23S and 16S rRNA. Since most mRNA decay in E. coli is initiated by RNase E, which also contributes to the degradation of 23S and 16S rRNA, this suggests that mRNA and rRNA may compete for RNase E in vivo, potentially affecting cell physiology. Here, we show that in exponential growing cells, the decrease in 23S and 16S rRNA levels is an artefact of the RNA extraction protocol. We measured rRNA levels after rifampicin addition using four different protocols. Whereas the gentle lysis employed by Hamouche et al. did indeed lead to loss of rRNA, rRNA levels were stable under stronger lysis. We also measured mRNA levels after rifampicin addition with the different protocols. Our results indicate that 23S, 16S and 5S rRNAs are not degraded during the mRNA degradation period in rifampicin-treated cells. In exponential growing cells therefore, rRNAs are stable in vivo and do not compete with other RNAs for degradation by RNase E. Whether rRNA is degraded and competes with RNA for RNase E under other physiological conditions, particularly stress, remains an open question.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-14966-1.

Introduction

mRNA decay directly contributes to bacterial adaptation through its role in controlling gene expression and metabolic pathways2. The method routinely used to quantify mRNA decay in E. coli is to stop RNA synthesis using an antibiotic, rifampicin, and then follow the disappearance of RNA using transcriptomics for genome-wide analysis and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) or Northern blot at the smaller scale3–8. Rifampicin inhibits the initiation of RNA synthesis by blocking the exit channel of RNA polymerase but elongation by RNA polymerase is not sensitive to rifampicin9. In addition, rifampicin inhibits the initiation of chromosome replication but allows DNA synthesis to continue until all cells have completed replication of their chromosome10.

Messenger RNAs (mRNAs) are unstable in E. coli cells, with mean half-lives ranging from 3 to 10 min depending on growth conditions5,8that are always far shorter than the cell doubling time. mRNA decay is a complex enzymatic process with thousands of mRNA-substrates binding to the same degradation machinery. Most mRNAs in E. coli are initially cleaved by RNase E11, which is present at a few hundred copies per cell12. Previous work has suggested that competition for RNase E binding between all copies of the ~ 4500 mRNAs that make up the transcriptome contributes to the regulation of mRNA half-lives and may affect cell physiology. There is experimental evidence of a negative correlation between mRNA concentration and half-life13. Increasing the proportion of a reporter mRNA relative to total mRNA consistently decreases its stability13. A mechanistic model of mRNA decay based on in vivo competition between mRNAs explains this biological observation and predicts that competition destabilizes mRNAs that have a higher binding affinity for RNase E and stabilizes mRNAs that have a lower binding affinity14.

Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is highly abundant (accounting for more than 80% of total RNA molecules15) and is generally believed to be more stable than mRNA. However, Hamouche et al.1 recently found that concentrations of 16S and 23S rRNA decreased by 50% 10 to 20 min after addition of rifampicin under different growth conditions in different E. coli strains, suggesting that rRNA decays at a similar rate as mRNA. Since RNase E is known to be involved in rRNA decay1,16, rRNA may compete with mRNA for RNase E binding, thereby slowing the rate of mRNA decay. The question that arises is whether rRNA is unstable after inhibition of transcription by rifampicin and, if so, whether rRNA competes with mRNA for RNase E binding.

In this work, we compared the decay of rRNAs and mRNAs after rifampicin addition in exponentially growing E. coli cells. Our results show that the decrease in 23S and 16S rRNA levels described by Hamouche et al.1is an artefact of the RNA extraction protocol used. We measured rRNA levels after addition of rifampicin using four different protocols. Whereas the gentle lysis procedure employed by Hamouche et al.1. resulted in loss of rRNA after rifampicin addition, strong lysis procedures led to stable rRNA levels. Furthermore, we found that stability measurements for seven mRNAs and one sRNA were reproducible when performed using a strong lysis protocol, but not when using gentle lysis. Finally, we conclude that 23S and 16S rRNA are not degraded during the period of mRNA degradation in rifampicin treated cells, indicating that rRNA is stable and does not compete with mRNA for the initiation of degradation by RNase E in exponentially growing cells.

Results and discussion

Effects of RNA extraction protocols on yields and proportions of RNA

Different RNA extraction protocols were tested on exponentially growing E. coli cultures treated with rifampicin to determine whether the extraction protocol can bias the measurement of RNA levels and RNA stability (Fig. 1). The four tested protocols are commonly used for RNA analysis in bacteria. In Protocol A, cells were lysed in Trizol under gentle conditions using a MultiTherm shaker and RNA was purified on a column1; in Protocol B, cells were lysed with Trizol and RNA was isolated with Phase Lock tubes17; in Protocol C, RNA was extracted in a single step using thermal cell lysis and chemical RNA extraction (RNA snap)18; and in Protocol D, cells were disrupted using FastPrep homogenization with glass beads and RNA was purified on a column13. The protocols therefore differed in terms of cell lysis method (chemical, thermal and mechanical) and RNA purification method (column-based, phase separation and chemical).

Schematic representation of the studied RNA extraction protocols.

Total RNA yields after extraction and distributions of the different RNA species were measured in each case. Total RNA yields were low for Protocols A and B, with just 6 and 5 µg/mL/UOD of RNA, respectively (Table 1). Protocols C and D had higher yields (67 and 24 µg/ml/UOD, respectively) (Table 1), the RNA snap method (Protocol C) being known to give the highest yields, albeit with the lowest purity18. The proportions of the most abundant RNA species (rRNA and tRNA) were then calculated. Protocols C and D efficiently extracted large rRNAs (23S and of 16S rRNA represented about 60% and 40% of total RNA, respectively), but extraction of small RNAs such as 5S rRNA and tRNA was inefficient. In contrast, Protocols A and B extracted large proportions of tRNA and 5S rRNA instead of 23S and 16S rRNA. In theory, large rRNAs predominate (82% overall: 23S rRNA, 53% and 16S rRNA, 29%) with small RNAs only making up 18% of total RNA (5S rRNA, 2% and tRNA, 16%). Protocols A and B, which respectively yielded 70 and 53% of small RNAs, therefore greatly favoured the extraction of small RNAs. These results show that these extraction protocols differ significantly in terms of total RNA yields and rRNA extraction efficiencies.

We next studied total RNA yields over a 60 min period after rifampicin addition (Fig. 2). RNA yields decreased for Protocols A and B (by 40 and 35%, respectively between t = 0 and 60 min), whereas no decrease was observed with Protocols C or D. These results show that total RNA yields decrease substantially after rifampicin addition when RNA is extracted using Trizol, in agreement with previous findings1, but that no such decrease occurs when cells are lysed thermally or mechanically. Note also that the decrease observed with Protocols A and B is associated with low RNA yields and inefficient 16S and 23S rRNA extraction. Since the rifampicin-treated cells were prepared in the same way for all four protocols, these results strongly suggest that the decrease in RNA yields with Protocols A and B is due to less efficient extraction.

Effects of RNA extraction protocols on rRNA concentrations over time

To determine whether the decrease in total RNA yields after transcription arrest was related to rRNA loss, we measured rRNA levels over time for the different protocols. With Protocol A, 23S and 16S rRNAs levels had respectively decreased by 87% and 90% 1 h after rifampicin addition (Fig. 3a), in agreement with previous findings1. 5S rRNA levels remained stable over time. 23S and 16S rRNAs also decreased with Protocol B, by 74% and 85% respectively, while 5S levels likewise remained constant (Fig. 3b). These results confirm the bias of Protocols A and B towards the extraction of 5S rRNA over 23S and 16S rRNA, and show that the decrease in RNA yields over time with Protocols A and B is related to a decrease in 23S and 16S rRNA levels. When RNA was extracted with Protocols C and D in contrast, 23S and 16S rRNA levels did not decrease over the 60 min period (Fig. 3c and d). These results show that the extraction protocol used has a major impact on the extraction efficiency of 16S and 23S rRNA after rifampicin treatment. Furthermore, the decrease in 16S and 23S rRNA levels with Protocols A and B correlates with the decrease in total RNA extraction.

The cell lysis method: a decisive factor in the loss of 23S and 16S rRNAs

To test the hypothesis that the method used to disrupt cells is an important factor in the decrease in 23S and 16S rRNA levels after rifampicin addition, we compared the effects of gentle and harsh cell disruption. We modified Protocol A by treating cells suspended in Trizol with a FastPrep homogenizer instead of a MultiTherm shaker (Fig. 1, Protocol A.v1), and also modified Protocol C by eliminating the heat denaturing step and treating cells suspended in RNA snap solution with a MultiTherm shaker (Fig. 1, Protocol C.v1) or a FastPrep homogenizer (Fig. 1, Protocol C.v2). The corresponding results are presented in Fig. 4.

Comparing the results in Figs. 3a and 4a shows that when cells suspended in Trizol were treated with a FastPrep homogenizer, no loss of 23S, 16S or 5S rRNA occurred after addition of rifampicin treatment, with similar levels of total RNA extracted over the 1 h experiment in both cases (Supplementary Fig. S1). Since the levels of total RNA extracted at t = 0 were approximately the same with MultiTherm shaking and FastPrep homogenization (6 vs. 8 µg/ml/UOD, respectively), these results exclude the possibility that the significant increase in total RNA extraction is due to homogenization and therefore indicate that MultiTherm shaking (Protocol A) favours the extraction of small RNAs (5S rRNA and tRNA), whereas FastPrep homogenization (Protocol A.v1) results in more efficient extraction of large RNAs (23S and 16S rRNA) relative to small RNAs.

Comparing the results in Fig. 4b, c to those in Fig. 3c shows that replacing the heat denaturation step in the RNA snap protocol by FastPrep homogenization (Fig. 4c) did not affect 23S and 16S rRNA levels and only led to a slightly lower total RNA yield (67 vs. 51 µg/ml/UOD, respectively). Replacing the heat denaturation step with MultiTherm shaking (Fig. 4b) resulted in a similar loss of 23S and 16S rRNA after rifampicin treatment as observed for cells suspended in Trizol (Fig. 3a). This result is consistent with the conclusion that FastPrep homogenization is necessary for the efficient extraction of large RNAs.

Possible causes of 16S and 23S rRNA loss after rifampicin treatment under gentle cell disruption

Are some 23S and 16S rRNAs lost in the flow through of the spin column?

To test whether the loss of 23S and 16S rRNAs is due to incomplete retention by the spin column, we recovered and analysed the column flow-through from Protocol A. The flow-through was treated with chloroform to separate the phases, the aqueous phase was precipitated with isopropanol and the pellet was suspended and then loaded on a gel (Fig. 5, Test 1). No bands corresponding to 23S and 16S rRNAs were detected (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Are 23S and 16S rRNAs trapped by cell debris?

Since MultiTherm shaking is a gentle method of cell disruption, a possible explanation for the loss of rRNA after treatment with rifampicin (Protocol A) is that 23S and 16S rRNAs are trapped by cellular debris, preventing their binding to the spin column. To test this hypothesis, cells were centrifuged before loading onto the column and RNAs were quantified in the cellular debris and in the supernatant (Fig. 5, Test 2). Total RNA was extracted from the supernatant and the cell pellet as in Protocol A and 7.5 µL of total RNA from the supernatant and the cell pellet were loaded on the gel. The presence of 23S and 16S rRNAs as well as DNA (smears in Fig. 6) in the cell pellet shows that 23S and 16S rRNAs are indeed trapped by cell debris following MultiTherm shaking. The fact that no 5S rRNA was detected in the pellet strongly suggests that the presence of 23S and 16S rRNA is not due to partial disruption of the cells. However, more importantly for this study, it seems unlikely that trapping in the pellet can explain the observed loss of 23S and 16S rRNA after rifampicin treatment since the levels of 23S and 16S rRNA in the pellet did not increase significantly after rifampicin treatment.

Are 23S and 16S rRNA trapped in the extraction column by a complex of large molecules?

In Protocol A, after breaking the cells with MultiTherm shaking, the entire sample is loaded onto the extraction column. To investigate whether some of the 23S and 16S rRNAs are trapped in a complex of large DNA/protein molecules in this process, preventing rRNA elution, a second elution of the extraction column was carried out using RNA snap solution heated to 55 °C (Fig. 5, Test 3). This extraction solution contains SDS, which facilitates the removal of protein complexes. When the extraction column was eluted a second time with RNA snap extraction solution, the concentrations of 23S and 16S rRNA still decreased between 0 and 60 min (Fig. 7a, b and c); however, a band whose intensity increased between 0 and 60 min was observed near the top of the gel (Fig. 7a). This unidentified band could correspond to DNA, RNA or both, and its increase could account for the loss of rRNA observed between 0 and 60 min. This second elution therefore showed that some DNA/RNA, which is not well eluted in a single step with water, is indeed retained by the column in Protocol A.

Next, to release any potentially trapped nucleic acids in the extraction column, the bed of resin was removed after extraction with Protocol A and then broken in Trizol with glass beads in a FastPrep homogenizer (Fig. 5, Test 4). After gel electrophoresis, a large diffuse band and a broad smear were observed on the gel at each time point (Fig. 7d). This smear was much weaker when the the samples were first treated with DNase (Fig. 7e), indicating that these bands correspond in part to DNA trapped on the extraction column. Furthermore, no bands or smears were visible when the samples were also treated with RNase (Fig. 7e), indicating that some RNA molecules are also trapped on the extraction column in addition to the DNA molecules. However, since the smears did not increase in intensity between 0 and 60 min, this trapped material cannot account for the decrease in 16S and 23S rRNA after addition of rifampicin.

Do DNA and protein complexes interfere with 23S and 16S rRNA extraction?

We considered the possibility that rRNA may be sequestered in large DNA molecules if genomic DNA is not properly fragmented during cell lysis by MultiTherm shaking. Genomic DNA levels measured 15, 30 and 60 min after addition of rifampicin increased by 71% between 0 and 60 min (Supplementary Fig. S3). This result is consistent with previous findings that DNA elongation continues after DNA synthesis initiation is inhibited by rifampicin10. To test whether the increasing amounts of genomic DNA sequester increasing amounts of rRNA, DNase treatment was carried out directly on the spin column used in Protocol A (Fig. 5, Test 5). Even after DNase treatment, 23S and 16S rRNA levels decreased by 78% and 86%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3), indicating that 23S and 16S rRNA molecules are not sequestered by large DNA molecules in the spin column.

Proteinase K treatment was then added to Protocol A (Fig. 5, Test 6) to test whether 23S and 16S rRNAs are instead sequestered in protein complexes (e.g. ribosomes). 23S and 16S rRNA levels still decreased over time after rifampicin addition and 5S rRNA levels remained stable (Supplementary Fig. S4). The high molecular weight band that appeared at 30 and 60 min disappeared when the samples were treated with DNase, indicating that this band corresponded to DNA (Supplementary Fig. S4). In summary, these results suggest that 23S and 16S rRNAs are not sequestered in protein complexes.

Effect of RNA extraction protocols on the levels of other RNAs after rifampicin treatment

Since our results showed that the cell lysis method used affects 16S and 23S rRNA levels after rifampicin addition, we investigated whether this is also the case for transfer RNAs (tRNAs), messenger RNAs (mRNAs) and small RNAs (sRNAs)?

Effect of RNA extraction protocols on tRNA levels

tRNA levels 0, 15, 30 and 60 min after rifampicin addition were compared between Protocols A, B and C (Fig. 1) (Protocol D was omitted from this analysis because no tRNA was visible on agarose gels (Fig. 2)). These measurements were also performed with Protocol A.v1, in which cells suspended in Trizol were treated by FastPrep homogenization (Fig. 4a). Figure 8 shows that, in contrast with 23S and 16S rRNA levels, tRNA levels did not decrease over time, confirming that tRNAs are indeed stable. The small but significant increase in tRNA levels with Protocol B could be due to the processing of tRNA precursors after rifampicin addition.

Effects of the cell lysis method on measurements of mRNA and sRNA stability

mRNA and sRNA stabilities were compared between Protocol A, with gentle MultiTherm shaking for cell lysis, and Protocol A.v1, with strong FastPrep homogenization. Decay after rifampicin addition was measured by RT-qPCR for 7 mRNAs and 1 sRNA (Supplementary Table S1) with different CDS lengths, abundances, operon contexts and functions.

For GlmZ sRNA and purL, argT, aceE and mhpC mRNA, half-lives were calculated from the slope of Cq (quantification cycle) versus time since the regression coefficient R2 was greater than 0.5 for all replicates with both extraction methods. These half-lives were similar and reproducible for both methods (Fig. 9a). The concentration of atpC mRNA remained constant with both cell lysis methods, indicating that atpC mRNA was stable in both experiments (Fig. 9b). For maeA and ihfB, reliable (R2 > 0.5) half-lives of approximately 4.0 min and 3.5 min, respectively, were measured with FastPrep homogenization, but MultiTherm shaking led to highly variable degradation rates with low R2 values. For maeA, the calculated half-lives varied between 3.8 and 25 min with R2 values of approximately 0.2. For ihfB, the half-lives varied between 4 and 21 min with R2 values of approximately 0.3, except for the half-life of 4 min, for which the R2 was 0.86. Since maeA and ihfB are monocistronic mRNAs, respectively 1698 and 285 nt in length, the low reliability of these stability measurements does not seem to be due to mRNA length. In summary, reliable half-lives were obtained for all eight of the studied RNAs with strong cell lysis, but for only 6/8 RNAs with gentle cell lysis. None of the studied mRNAs or sRNAs for which reliable half-lives were obtained had cell lysis method-dependent stabilities.

Conclusion

This study comparing the effects of RNA extraction protocols on RNA stability in E. coli cells after rifampicin addition identifies cell lysis as the determining factor for the efficacy and biases of extraction results. Strong cell lysis methods (thermal or mechanical) had the best RNA extraction yields, led to stable levels of tRNA and rRNA after transcription arrest and allowed reliable measurements of mRNA and sRNA half-lives. On the contrary, gentle cell lysis led to lower RNA recovery, loss of 23S and 16S rRNAs after rifampicin addition and unreliable half-life measurements for some mRNAs. A loss of 23S and 16S rRNA after rifampicin addition was also previously described using hot phenol for cell lysis and RNA extraction19. We conclude that different RNA extraction protocols led to a variable fate of 23S and 16S rRNA after transcription arrest.

The rRNA degradation effect reported by Hamouche et al.1. is therefore not biological but rather an artefact of the gentle cell lysis in their RNA extraction protocol. Our results suggest the low total RNA yields and low levels of 23S and 16S rRNA observed after transcription arrest are due to the presence of large complexes that are not composed exclusively of DNA or protein. These complexes could correspond to cell debris composed of lipid membranes and cell wall. The smaller, polyploid cells1 produced by DNA replication and terminal cell division during rifampicin treatment may be incompletely disintegrated by gentle lysis methods. Regardless of these considerations, the main conclusion of our work is that 23S and 16S rRNAs appear to be stable in vivo after transcription arrest with rifampicin.

Our results showed that 23S and 16S rRNA are not subject to degradation during the phase of mRNA decay in rifampicin treated cells. The stability of rRNA is important from a physiological point of view since it implies that the most abundant type of RNA in E. coli cells does not compete with mRNA for degradation by RNase E. It has been proposed16,20 that conditions decreasing protein synthesis and increasing the number of inactive ribosomes lead to the dissociation of the ribosome into its subunits, which are then subject to RNase E-dependent degradation. Since we believe that 23S and 16S rRNA are not degraded, ribosome subunits are not accessible to degradation by RNase E after rifampicin addition in these conditions. Our results were obtained under optimal growth conditions with E. coli cells cultivated exponentially. Whether rRNA is degraded and competes with mRNA for RNase E under other physiological conditions, particularly stress, remains an open question. Ribosomal RNA degradation has been observed in several works during nutrient limitation21–23. Since competition for RNase E binding is predicted to shape RNA half-lives14, rRNA degradation could therefore have an impact on E. coli metabolism and its response to stress.

Methods

Sampling of rifampicin treated cultures

E. coli NCM3416 (K12, F−, λ−, zib-207::Tn10) was grown in LB at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. Overnight cultures in LB medium were diluted 1/100 in 200 mL LB and grown to OD600 = 0.5. RNA synthesis was inhibited by rifampicin (500 µg/mL). As described by Hamouche et al.1. samples (5 mL) were quenched with 1 mL of 5% phenol (in ethanol) before chilling on ice. A zero time measurement was taken just before adding rifampicin. For the study of ribosomal RNA stability on agarose gels, RNA was sampled 15, 30 and 60 min after adding rifampicin. For the study of mRNA stability using RT-qPCR, shorter sampling times were used (0, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 4 and 6 min). After centrifugation (3000 g, 20 min, 4 °C), supernatants were discarded and cell pellets were stored at − 80 °C. RNA sampling was performed in parallel on biological triplicates.

Second elution of the Direct-zol RNA miniprep plus column with RNA snap buffer

After the first elution with 50 µL of H2O in Protocol A, a second elution was performed with 50 µl of 55 °C pre-heated RNA snap extraction solution added to the column and recovered by centrifugation.

Extracting the membrane of the Direct-zol RNA miniprep plus column

After the first elution with 50 µL of H2O in Protocol A, the column was cut to recover the membrane, which was then placed in a tube containing 500 µL of TriReagent and 0.1 g of glass beads. The membrane was disrupted with a FastPrep-24 homogenizer as described in Protocol D. After centrifugation at 4 °C, 14 000 rpm for 15 min, the aqueous phase (250 µL) was added to an equal volume of 100% ethanol and total nucleic acids were prepared as described in Protocol A. The samples were then treated with DNase or RNase to identify the nature of the bands and smears observed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Turbo DNase (Invitrogen) treatment was performed by combining 5 µL of 10x DNase buffer with 25 µL of H2O and 20 µl of sample. Turbo DNase (2U/µL) was added and the reaction was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C before 5 µL of DNase inactivation reagent was added. After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, the sample was centrifuged (13 000 rpm, 2 min) and the supernatant was recovered. For the RNase treatment, 2 µL of RNase A (7 000U/mL, Qiagen) was added to 20 µL of each sample. After incubation for 2 min at room temperature, 2 µL of Proteinase K was added and the sample was incubated for a further 2 min. Proteinase K was inactivated by heating at 70 °C for 10 min.

DNase treatment of RNA extracts

DNase treatment in Protocol A was performed using the Direct-zol RNA miniprep plus kit (Zymo research) according to manufacturer instructions. After binding nucleic acids to the column and washing, a mix containing 5 µL of DNase I and 75 µL of DNase digestion buffer was added on the column. The column with DNase was incubated for at least 15 min at room temperature, washed and then eluted according to manufacturer instructions.

Proteinase K treatment of RNA extracts

Proteinase K treatments were performed using the Quick-RNA miniprep plus kit (Zymo research). Cell pellets were resuspended in 300 µL DNA/RNA shield buffer. The proteinase K supplied with the kit was added and the sample was incubated for at least 30 min at room temperature. After centrifugation (14 000 rpm, 2 min), cell debris were pelleted and 300 µL of the cleared supernatant was collected. One volume of RNA lysis buffer was added and the tube was shaken with the MultiTherm instrument (550 rpm, 10 min, 37 °C). The samples were transferred to a spin-away filter to remove the majority of genomic DNA. After centrifugation, one volume of 100% ethanol was added to the flow-through. Total RNA was then purified as described in Protocol A.

Nucleic acid content of Direct-zol RNA miniprep plus column flow-through

The flow-through (50% Trizol-50% ethanol) from the Direct-zol RNA miniprep plus spin column in Protocol A was recovered in an RNase-free Eppendorf tube and diluted with water (200 µL). After adding 400 µL of chloroform, vortexing and centrifugation (14 000 rpm, 4 °C for 30 s), the aqueous phase was recovered and nucleic acids were precipitated with an equal volume of isopropanol as described in Protocol B.

Agarose gel separation of RNA species and quantification

An equal volume of each RNA extract (7 µL) was mixed with an equal volume of 2X formamide loading buffer (95% formamide, 0.025% SDS, 0.025% bromophenol blue, 0.025% xylene cyanol FF, 0.025% ethidium bromide, 0.5mM EDTA) and then mixed in a MultiTherm shaker (550 rpm, 10 min, 37 °C) before migration on 2.4% agarose gels in 1X-TBE. Size markers (1 kB DNA or 100 bp DNA, NEB) were added in a parallel lane. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide and imaged with a ChemiDoc Imaging Systems (Biorad). RNA levels were quantified relative to the RNA levels in the t = 0 sample using Image Lab software.

Genomic DNA extraction

DNA from E. coli NCM3416 was extracted using the GenElute bacterial genomic DNA kit (Sigma) according to manufacturer instructions. Briefly, cell pellets prepared as described in Protocol A were resuspended in 200 µL of lysis solution T. RNase A (20 µL) and proteinase K (20 µL) were added to cell suspensions, which were then incubated at 55 °C for 30 min. Lysis solution C (200 µL) was added and the lysate was incubated for 10 min at 55 °C and then loaded onto the column. After washing the column, genomic DNA was eluted with 200 µL of 10 mM Tris HCl, pH 8.5. The extracted genomic DNA was loaded onto 0.8% agarose gel in 0.5X-TAE.

RT-qPCR determination of RNA half-life

RNAs were extracted using Protocol A and Protocol A.v1 and treated with Turbo DNase (Invitrogen). cDNAs were synthesized as previously described13 on 5 µg total RNA. Primers (Supplementary Table S2) were designed to have a GC content of more than 50% and a melting temperature of about 60 °C. The length of the PCR products ranged between 90 and 150 bp.

Quantitative PCR measurements were performed with a IQTM SYBR Green Supermix device (BioRad) in 96-well plates as previously described24. Two dilutions (10−1 and 10−2) of cDNA were performed to determine qPCR efficiencies. Minimum information for publication of quantitative RT-PCR experiments (MIQE) not reported in the main text are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Raw Cq (quantification cycle) values not shown in Fig. 9 are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Linear regression coefficients, k’, of Cq versus time (6 points) and associated coefficients of determination, R2, were calculated for each RNA species. RNA half-lives were obtained from k’ using the relationship t1/2= 1/k’. RNAs with a half-life greater than 14 min, or so stable that their half-life could not be reliably quantified, were classified as very stable. Half-lives were calculated from the slope of the regression curve if R2 was greater than 0.5; otherwise, no half-life was calculated.
