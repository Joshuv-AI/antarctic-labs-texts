Clinical and epidemiological profile of patients with colorectal cancer in Bhutan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Prabhat Pradhan, Ugyen Chophel, Sangay Wangmo, Thinley Dorji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Globally, the burden of colorectal cancer cases has more than doubled, from 0.84 million to 2.17 million, and deaths increased from 0.51 million to 1.09 million between 1990 and 2019 [2]. Colorectal cancers accounted for 10% of global cancer incidence and 9.4% of cancer deaths in 2020 and is predicted to increase to 3.2 million cases in 2040 [3]. In Asia, colorectal cancer is on a rising trend as the disease burden has shifted to middle and low-income countries mainly due to changing lifestyles, an increasing aging population, and other factors. Asia has reported the highest proportions of both incident cases (51.8%) and mortality (52.4%) among cases of colorectal cancer in 2018 [4].

Bhutan is a small, landlocked and lower-middle-income country located in the eastern Himalayas with a population of about 0.7 million. Bhutan has reported an increasing incidence of cancers; 2744 new cancer cases were detected between 2014 and 2018, and 3172 cases were detected between 2019 and 2022 [5, 6]. It is reported that the age-adjusted incidence rate for cancer per 100,000 population was 101.7 for males and 126.4 for females for the years 2019–2022 [6]. The cumulative risk of getting cancer is 1 in 7 among females and 1 in 8 among males [6]. In Bhutan, stomach cancer was the most common cancer among males and cervical cancer among females, while colorectal cancer contributed to 4.9% of the incident cancer cases among males for the period 2019–2022 [6]. In the Population-based Cancer Registry for 2019–2022, colon cancer is reported as the fifth most common cancer among males with an age-adjusted incidence of 5.2 per 100,000 population [6].

Like other cancers in Bhutan, almost all the cases of colorectal cancer are managed at the Surgery and Oncology Wards at the Jigme Dorji Wangchuck (JDW) National Referral Hospital, Thimphu, while some cases are referred to India for further management. The JDW National Referral Hospital is the only hospital in the country with onco-surgery services. This study describes the baseline epidemiological and clinical profile of patients with colorectal cancers diagnosed at the JDW National Referral Hospital, Thimphu, Bhutan.

Method

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study with a retrospective review of hospital records of patients with colorectal cancer managed at the JDW National Referral Hospital, Thimphu, Bhutan, between 2018 and 2024.

Setting

Healthcare services in Bhutan are delivered free of cost through a three-tiered structure involving Primary Healthcare Centres at the grassroots level, district hospitals at the secondary level, and regional and national referral hospitals at the tertiary level [7]. Patients with symptoms and signs of colorectal cancer may present at any healthcare centre and are invariably referred to the JDW National Referral Hospital, through ambulances free of charge, for specialist diagnosis and management. This is a standard protocol for referral of almost all patients with suspected cases of malignancy and for confirmed cases of malignancy for follow-up reviews.

In 2024, there were 568,005 patients seen in the outpatient department and 17,855 inpatient admissions at the JDW National Referral Hospital [8]. The Department of Surgery had 24,513 patients in the outpatient section and 2217 inpatient admissions [8]. Basic pathology and CT imaging are available at the Regional Referral Hospitals in Gelephu and Mongar [9]. As of 2025, basic oncopathology tests, lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, and MRI imaging are available at the JDW National Referral Hospital. At the national hospital, patients requiring oncosurgery are managed at the Surgery Ward. Those requiring neoadjuvant and adjuvant chemotherapy are managed at the Oncology Ward under the supervision of oncosurgeons. As of 2025, there were no oncophysicians, radiation oncologists, or radiation services available in Bhutan [10].

Study population and sample size

We reviewed the hospital records of all patients who were admitted to the Surgery and Oncology Wards and those who presented at Surgery Outpatient at the JDW National Referral Hospital, Thimphu, for treatment and care of colorectal cancer between 01 January 2018 and 31 December 2024.

Data variables, sources, and data collection

Data were collected using a pro forma that was designed for this study. All variables are extracted from the electronic patient registers maintained at the Department of Surgery and the Medical Record Section. Search words such as colon cancer, rectal cancer, and caecal mass were used to identify the patient records from the electronic database. Duplication of patients from readmission for repeat surgeries or treatment was avoided through careful evaluation of the patient’s identity card and hospital registration numbers (patient identifiers were deleted from the final dataset).

Data analysis and statistics

Data collected in the pro forma were entered into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Office) and analyzed using STATA (version 18, StataCorp LP USA). A descriptive analysis of the demographic and clinical profile, including clinical presentation, tumour location, histopathology subtypes, cancer stage, and treatment modalities, is summarized using frequency and proportions. Serum carcinoembryonic antigen was considered elevated if > 5.2 ng/mL (laboratory reference at the JDW National Referral Hospital). The map showing the proportion of cases reported was generated using QGIS Desktop 3.40.7.

Ethics considerations

This study was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, Khesar Gyalpo University of Medical Sciences of Bhutan (IRB/Approval/PN/2024-046/1479, approval date 13 Mar 2025). Administrative approval was obtained from the Ministry of Health, Royal Government of Bhutan and the hospital administration. Informed consent was waived off by the Institutional Review Board, Khesar Gyalpo University of Medical Sciences of Bhutan as this was a retrospective review of patient data, and patient identifiers were removed from the final data set.

Results

A total of 182 patients were diagnosed with colorectal cancer at the JDW National Referral Hospital, Bhutan, between 2018 and 2024. Discussion

This study describes the baseline information on the clinical profile and the clinical management of colorectal cancers diagnosed in Bhutan between 2018 and 2024. There is an increase in the age-adjusted incidence rate of colorectal cancer from 3.4 per 100,000 population for the period 2014–2018 to 5.2 for the period 2019–2022 in Bhutan [5, 6]. Higher proportion of cases are reported from Trashigang, Pema Gatshel, Mongar, Samtse and Sarpang districts. While there is a lack of robust data on the overall survival to determine true prevalence, a higher number of cases were reported from two eastern districts despite longer travel distance to the JDW National Referral Hospital. Some of the possible reasons may include differences in genetic predisposition, prevalence of risk factors such as alcohol consumption [11], access to health facilities, or knowledge/awareness of warning signs.

This study reports a higher number of cases among older adults. This trend is similar to that of other cancers, such as stomach, oesophagus, liver and lung cancers reported among males in the population-based cancer registry. For colorectal cancer, this is consistent with the global trend where the incidence and mortality of colorectal cancers increase after the age of 50 years [3]. In Bhutan, where the National Cancer Control Strategy envisions improving early diagnosis and timely initiation of treatment of cancers, the findings indicate a strong need for the initiation of systematic and targeted screening for colorectal cancer for older individuals [12]. While a policy decision on the initiation of routine screening needs to be taken based on the available resources, infrastructure and healthcare expenditure, we recommend a discussion involving all stakeholders on the practical aspects of the implementation of screening. It is also recommended to consider the adoption of a screening method among stool-based, blood-based direct-visualization and imaging tests, that is best suited for the health system in Bhutan [13].

The clinical presentation of colorectal cancers depends on the location of the primary tumour. The common presentations of colorectal cancers are changes in bowel habits and rectal bleeding [14]. In our study, abdominal pain, altered bowel habits, and per rectal bleeding were common symptoms, followed by abdominal distension. There are therapeutic and prognostic implications based on the location of the tumour, where right-sided colon cancer and middle or lower one-third of rectal cancer carry a relatively adverse prognosis than left-sided colon cancer and upper one-third of rectal cancer. In high-income countries, left-sided colon cancer and the upper one-third of rectal cancer are more common [14]. In our study, middle/lower rectal cancer and right-sided colon cancer were higher than left colon cancer and upper rectal cancer.

Adenocarcinoma from mucus-producing cells is the most common type of colorectal cancer [3]. Our study findings are consistent with findings from global and regional reviews where adenocarcinoma is reported as a common histopathological type [15, 16]. As of 2025, histopathological diagnostic facilities and MRI imaging are available only at the JDW National Referral Hospital in Bhutan. Histopathological specimens sampled at the other hospitals are transported to the JDW National Referral Hospital for reading and reporting. However, there are often long delays in obtaining the reports, affecting the timely delivery of healthcare services. Often, patients are referred to India for confirmation of diagnosis and management of cancers.

The treatment and prognosis of colorectal cancer depend on the stage of the disease, the patient’s performance status, and the availability of therapeutic options. Similar to reports from neighbouring countries, most of the patients presented to the hospital with advanced-stage disease (Stage III and above), which reflects missed opportunities for early detection and management [14]. In addition, there is a delay in seeking health due to inadequate knowledge among the patients and patients opting for local healing practices before hospital visits. It is recommended that patients presenting with symptoms of colorectal cancers at the primary healthcare centres or district hospitals should be identified and directed through referral pathways to the JDW National Referral Hospital. In addition, the establishment of oncology services at the two Regional Referral Hospitals will address health inequities in terms of access.

The common modes of treatment offered were surgery alone or surgery in combination with chemotherapy. Radiation therapy was briefly available at the JDW National Referral Hospital, but the service was withdrawn due to several technical concerns [17]. Currently, there are no radiation oncologists or facilities for radiation therapy available in Bhutan. Patients requiring radiotherapy are required to avail such therapies in India, adding to the increasing costs of treatment for the government. While patients were offered chemotherapy, there are major problems with the lack of a medical oncologist to deliver and monitor for response and side effects. Therefore, given the lack of a dedicated oncology team, the treatment of patients with colorectal cancer, or any other cancer, is a major challenge in Bhutan.

As a result of presentation in advanced stages, palliative care plays an essential part in the care of the patient. Palliative care is considered an active and holistic care of patients of all ages and their families facing serious health-related suffering. It helps in improving the quality of life, well-being, comfort, and human dignity for individuals, being an effective person-centered health service that values patients’ need to receive adequate, personally and culturally sensitive information on their health status, and their central role in making decisions about the treatment received [18]. One of the objectives of the National Cancer Control Strategy is to establish palliative care services across all major health centres and in communities [12]. The training for basic palliative care has been rolled out among the health staff of various disciplines to enhance the clinical skills of basic palliative care at the institution and community outreach health services. Healthcare providers, in collaboration with stakeholders from the civil society and community, are increasingly involved in expanding palliative care services, including service delivery through home visits.

Study limitations

The primary limitation of this study was the missing information on sociodemographic parameters in the electronic registers and the retrospective review of data. Given the transition to electronic medical records and the physical relocation of the medical records, we were unable to trace information for all patients. In this article, we present only the proportion as we do not have the data on the survival or mortality to present the true prevalence rates. It is recommended to develop and maintain a comprehensive database on all cancers to generate a better understanding of epidemiological and clinical parameters associated with cancers among the population in Bhutan. There may be a selection bias given that only cases from the JDW National Referral Hospital were counted. However, given the lack of diagnostic facilities at other centres, many patients with symptoms may not get a confirmatory diagnosis unless they are evaluated at the JDW National Referral Hospital.

Conclusions

The study provides baseline information about the common symptoms, tumour site, clinical stage of the disease at presentation, and the treatment modalities offered. While there are disparities in the proportion of cases across districts, there is a need for further studies to describe the epidemiological risk factors and prioritize targeted efforts in the prevention or early detection of colorectal cancers.
