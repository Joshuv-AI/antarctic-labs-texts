Family functioning and mobile phone dependency among adolescents: the mediating role of positive youth development and the moderating effect of physical activity
Authors: Feng Luo, Yannan Du, Qilong Sun, Naiyang Peng, Yansong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated the protective effect of family functioning on adolescent mobile phone dependence, tested whether positive youth development mediated this association, and evaluated whether physical activity moderated the direct and indirect pathways.MethodsA cross-sectional survey of 588 Chinese adolescents was conducted using the Family APGAR Index, Positive Youth Development Very Short Form, Mobile Phone Involvement Questionnaire, International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form, and a moderated mediation model was tested.ResultsFamily functioning positively predicted positive youth development, with physical activity significantly moderating this relationship. Additionally, moderated mediation analysis revealed that a significant direct effect of family functioning on reduced mobile phone dependence emerged only for adolescents with high physical activity. Meanwhile, indirect effects through adolescent positive youth development were significant at moderate and high physical activity levels but absent at low levels.ConclusionThese results highlight that physical activity serves as a crucial moderator in the relationship between family functioning, positive youth development, and mobile phone dependence. Enhancing adolescents’ physical activity may amplify the protective benefits of strong family functioning, promote positive development and ultimately reduce mobile phone dependence. Targeted interventions incorporating physical activity could thus optimize family-oriented strategies to manage adolescent phone dependence.

Introduction

Adolescence is universally recognized as a formative developmental stage marked by profound changes across biological, psychological, and social domains. During this period, individuals begin to consolidate their identity, develop autonomy, and establish behavior patterns that frequently persist into adulthood [1]. Among these behaviors, digital media use has emerged as a dominant feature of adolescent life. Mobile phone use has become nearly ubiquitous among adolescents, raising both opportunities and serious developmental concerns. While mobile technologies have undoubtedly facilitated educational access, social communication, and entertainment, mobile phone dependency—a maladaptive pattern characterized by compulsive checking, withdrawal symptoms, and functional impairment—has drawn increasing attention in clinical, developmental, and public health literature [2, 3]. Globally, prevalence estimates of mobile phone dependency among adolescents vary widely, often ranging from 10% to 30%, depending on cultural context and measurement tools [4]. In East Asia, where academic pressure and mobile technology penetration are high, mobile phone dependency rates are especially concerning [5]. Within these exam-oriented settings, adolescents often rely on smartphones for study related tasks while also using them for leisure and stress regulation during limited breaks from intensive schoolwork [6, 7]. This context heightens the need to examine how family and individual factors can protect against such dependency in East Asian cultures. Additionally, Mobile phone dependency has been associated with adverse outcomes including depression, anxiety, poor sleep quality, diminished meaning in life, decreased academic performance, and compromised social functioning [8–11]. These findings highlight the necessity of identifying protective factors and mechanisms that mitigate the risk of developing mobile phone dependency during adolescence.

Family functioning is increasingly conceptualized as a proximal ecological resource for adolescents’ adjustment and well-being. According to Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory [12], the family represents a proximal and influential microsystem shaping adolescent development. Furthermore, researchers have increasingly emphasized the importance of examining the role and underlying mechanisms of positive environmental factors, such as creating supportive family or peer interaction environments, in reducing mobile phone addiction [13, 14]. It provides a new perspective on intervention research, namely, guiding individuals, families, or schools to build a positive developmental environment that can promote rational mobile phone use and reduce mobile phone dependency. Building on this perspective, the present study attempted to explore the association between positive family environmental factors and mobile phone dependency, as well as the underlying mechanisms that could effectively reduce the risk of mobile phone dependency.

Nonetheless, the relationship between family factors and mobile phone dependency is not entirely straightforward, and some inconsistencies remain. Not all studies have found a simple inverse relationship between healthy family environments and reduced mobile phone use. For example, a recent study of Thai adolescents documented the counterintuitive finding that higher-quality family relationships correlate with elevated mobile phone dependency risk, despite other family functioning dimensions exerting protective effects; this pattern reflects shifting adolescent social dynamics, where peer influence and digital engagement overshadow family interactions, weaken familial discipline, blur boundaries and expose adolescents to online risks [15]. Additionally, the role of family in preventing online risk behaviors among Colombian adolescents is limited, particularly for older adolescents who spend more time with peers and may be encouraged by their friends to engage in general risk-taking behaviors [16]. These mixed findings suggest that unmeasured intermediaries or contextual moderators could influence how family environments translate into youth behavioral outcomes. In other words, family functioning may shape mobile phone dependency through mediated processes rather than a direct association, with its influence varying based on contextual conditions. To advance theory and intervention, it is important to investigate the mediating mechanisms linking family functioning to mobile phone dependency, as well as factors that modify the strength of this linkage. It is increasingly evident that this relationship is mediated by individual-level psychological resources that may either enhance or diminish the influence of family environment.

One promising mediator is adolescents’ positive youth development. The positive youth development framework provides a robust theoretical lens through which to examine these individual-level resources. Positive youth development is predicated on a strength-based model that views adolescents not as problems to be solved but as resources to be developed. Positive Youth Development theory, as articulated by Lerner and colleagues, conceptualizes optimal youth development in terms of the “5 Cs”: Competence, Confidence, Connection, Character, and Caring [17]. There have been studies both domestically and internationally exploring the relationship between the 5 Cs model and adolescent mental health and behavioral problems [18, 19]. In samples such as Chile and Norway, it has also been confirmed that 5 Cs have predictive power for positive development and problem behavior in adolescents [20, 21]. This strength-based PYD perspective is grounded in relational developmental systems theory. This meta-theory posits that positive development emerges from mutually influential, dynamic interactions between an individual and their multiple, nested contexts (e.g., family, school) [22, 23]. The individual is viewed as an active co-creator of their own development within these relationships. From this viewpoint, the family constitutes a primary proximal process, where supportive and responsive interactions are essential for fostering the growth of internal assets such as competence, confidence, and connection [24, 25]. Thus, relational developmental systems theory provides a coherent framework for understanding how family functioning, as an important contextual system, may promote PYD attributes.

In the context of mobile phone dependency, positive youth development may operate as a psychological buffer that enhances adolescents’ capacity for self-regulation and reduces their dependence on digital devices for gratification or emotional relief. A study conducted in Hong Kong demonstrated that positive youth development attributes, such as emotional competence and self-control, were negatively associated with social networking addiction among adolescents [26]. This indicates that adolescents with strong positive youth development attributes are less likely to develop addictive behaviors. Additionally, a study on the relationship between family environment and mobile phone dependency in South Korea found that positive parenting and family cohesion were associated with lower levels of mobile phone dependency, mediated by enhanced self-control and friendship quality [27]. This underscores the importance of family functioning in promoting positive youth development attributes that protect against mobile phone dependency. Furthermore, research on the association between family activities and youth development across different cultures found that positive family relationships and activities were linked to higher life satisfaction and self-control in adolescents, which are key components of positive youth development [28]. Recent studies indicate that family functioning can indirectly influence mobile phone dependency by fostering specific positive youth development attributes; however, the explicit mediating role of positive youth development in the relationship between family functioning and mobile phone dependency remains underexplored. Another critical factor is the role of physical activity. Extensive evidence supports the physical and psychological benefits of regular physical activity during adolescence. A study by Pereira et al. found that adolescents with low levels of physical activity are more prone to experiencing problems with mobile phone usage compared to their more active peers [29]. A cross-sectional study indicated that physical activity can directly reduce mobile phone dependency and indirectly alleviate its associated risks by improving emotion regulation and reducing emotional problems [30]. One meta-analysis also proposed that physical activity serves as an effective strategy for preventing or ameliorating mobile phone dependency in adolescents [31]. From the displacement hypothesis, physical activity competes with sedentary screen time and may thus directly reduce opportunities for mobile phone dependency [32]. Moreover, physical activity may interact with other developmental processes. According to the resource substitution model [33], when one protective factor (e.g., family functioning) is low, another (e.g., physical activity) may compensate for its absence. Conversely, physical activity may moderate the effectiveness of psychosocial resources: adolescents who are physically active may be more capable of utilizing internal assets (e.g., positive youth development) to regulate their behavior, whereas sedentary adolescents may be less able to convert such assets into protective behaviors [34].

Based on the above literature, this study proposes a moderated mediation model (see Fig. 1), in which positive youth development mediates the relationship between family functioning and mobile phone dependency, with physical activity level moderating both the path from family functioning to positive youth development and the path from positive youth development to mobile phone dependency.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design using convenience sampling to recruit adolescent participants from one school located in Shandong Province, China. The sample comprised 588 participants (Mage = 16.386 years, SD = 0.712, range = 15–18 years), with 180 male (30.6%) and 408 female (69.4%) respondents. Regarding family structure, 39 (6.6%) were only children and 549 (93.4%) had siblings; perceived family income was categorized as low perceived family income (n = 50, 8.5%) or non-low perceived family income (n = 538, 91.5%). Detailed demographic characteristics by gender and for the total sample are presented in Table 1. Written informed consent was obtained from the students and their parents/guardians prior to data collection, in accordance with institutional ethics approval. Survey administration took place in classrooms during a common free period, supervised by research staff and teachers. Students completed an online questionnaire battery assessing family functioning, positive youth development, mobile phone dependency, physical activity, and demographic information. To encourage honest reporting, students were assured that there were no right or wrong answers and that their responses would remain confidential and be used only for research purposes.

Measures

Family functioning

Family functioning was assessed using the Family APGAR Index [35], which is applicable to Chinese adolescents [36], and validated in our previous study [37]. This scale evaluates five components: Adaptability, Partnership, Growth, Affection, and Resolve. Example item: “I am satisfied with the support I receive from my family when something concerns me”. Each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = “hardly ever”, 2 = “almost always”), yielding a total score from 0 to 10. Higher scores reflect better family functioning. The scale has demonstrated robust psychometric properties across cultural contexts. Cronbach’s alpha in the present study was 0.904.

Positive youth development

Positive youth development was measured using the Positive Youth Development Very Short Form [38]. This 17-item instrument assesses five developmental assets: Competence (3 items), Confidence (4 items), Character (3 items), Caring (4 items), and Connection (3 items). Example item: “I think it is important to accept responsibility for my actions when I make a mistake or get in trouble”. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”). A composite PYD score was calculated by averaging all items, with higher scores indicating greater positive development. Cronbach’s alpha in the present study was 0.916.

Mobile phone dependency

Mobile phone dependency was evaluated using the Mobile Phone Involvement Questionnaire [39], which has been translated and validated in the Chinese population [40]. It contains 8 items related to the perception and behavior towards mobile phone. Example item: “I lose track of how much I am using my mobile phone”. Each item was scored based on a 7-Likert scale and the total score ranges from 8 to 56 points. A higher total score indicates the greater mobile phone dependency. Cronbach’s alpha in the present study was 0.900.

Physical activity

Physical activity level was measured using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) [41]. The IPAQ-SF comprises seven items capturing walking, moderate physical activity (e.g., cycling, brisk walking), and vigorous physical activity (e.g., sports, running) over the past week. Total physical activity was computed in MET-minutes/week according to established scoring guidelines, and participants were categorized into three physical activity levels: Low, Moderate, and High. For analysis, physical activity level was dummy-coded (1 = Low, 2 = Moderate, 3 = High).

Covariates

Age, gender, only-child status, perceived family income, and depression (Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale) were included as covariates in all regression analyses due to their documented associations with the main variables [42–44].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 and Hayes’ PROCESS macro. First, descriptive statistics and independent samples t-tests were performed to examine gender differences among main variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to assess relationships among main variables. Next, moderated mediation was tested using PROCESS Model 59, the independent variable was family functioning; the mediator was positive youth development; the dependent variable was mobile phone dependency; and the moderator was physical activity level. To probe moderation, interaction terms were included in the model. All continuous variables were mean-centered prior to interaction. Conditional effects at low, moderate, and high levels of physical activity were estimated. Bootstrapping (n = 5,000 samples) was used to obtain 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs) for conditional direct and indirect effects. Indirect effects were considered statistically significant if the CI did not contain zero.

Results

Common method bias test

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias. The unrotated factor analysis revealed 5 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor accounting for 32.11% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40.00%. This suggests that common method bias was not a significant concern in this study.

Descriptive statistics and gender differences

Descriptive statistics and independent-samples t tests were conducted to examine gender differences in the main study variables (Table 2). The results indicated that females reported significantly higher levels of mobile phone dependency than males. However, no significant gender differences were observed for family functioning or positive youth development.

Moderated mediation model

A moderated mediation model was constructed using the PROCESS macro (Model 59) to examine the moderating role of physical activity level in the relationship between family functioning and mobile phone dependency via positive youth development. The overall model was statistically significant. The model explained 35.5% of the variance in positive youth development (R² = 0.355, F(10, 577) = 31.804, p < 0.001) and 7.7% of the variance in mobile phone dependency (R² = 0.077, F(13, 574) = 3.702, p < 0.001).

Moderating effect of physical activity on the pathway between family functioning and positive youth development

Family functioning was positively associated with positive youth development, and this association was significantly moderated by physical activity level (interaction ΔR2 = 0.011, F (2, 577) = 4.762, p = 0.009). Simple slope analysis showed that for adolescents with low physical activity, higher family functioning predicted higher positive youth development (β = 0.553, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.416, 0.691]). This positive relationship remained significant but was weaker at moderate (β = 0.316, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.211, 0.421]) and high physical activity levels (β = 0.298, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.177, 0.419]) (Fig. 2). Thus, as adolescents’ physical activity increased, the strength of the relationship between family functioning and positive youth development decreased, although it remained significantly positive in all groups.

Moderating effects of physical activity on direct and indirect pathways to mobile phone dependency

Neither family functioning nor positive youth development showed a significant main effect on adolescent mobile phone dependence when not accounting for interactions (p = 0.900 and 0.181, respectively). However, both effects were qualified by significant interactions with physical activity level. The interaction between family functioning and physical activity was significant (F (2, 574) = 4.06, p = 0.018). Follow-up simple effects indicated that family functioning was associated with significantly lower mobile phone dependence only among adolescents with high physical activity (β = − 0.190, p = 0.022, 95% CI [–0.352, − 0.028]) (Fig. 3A). In contrast, this relationship was non-significant for those with low or moderate physical activity levels. Similarly, the interaction between positive development and physical activity was significant (F (2, 574) = 4.89, p = 0.008). Among adolescents with moderate or high physical activity, higher positive development predicted significantly lower mobile phone dependence (for moderate: β = − 0.207, p = 0.005, 95% CI [–0.352, − 0.061]; for high: β = − 0.213, p = 0.024, 95% CI [–0.397, − 0.028]). In the low physical activity group, positive youth development was not related to mobile phone dependence (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that both the protective effect of family functioning and the benefit of positive youth development on reducing mobile phone dependence manifested primarily for adolescents who were moderately or highly physically active.

Conditional direct and indirect effects

Conditional direct and indirect effects of family functioning on mobile phone dependence are presented in Table 4. The direct effect of family functioning on mobile phone dependence (i.e., not via the mediator) was significant only for adolescents with high physical activity (β = − 0.190, p = 0.022) and was non-significant at low and moderate physical activity levels (p = 0.900 and 0.099, respectively). In contrast, the indirect effect of family functioning on mobile phone dependence through positive youth development was significant for the moderate and high physical activity groups, but not for the low activity group. Specifically, for adolescents with moderate physical activity, family functioning had a significant indirect effect on reducing mobile phone dependence via positive development (indirect effect = − 0.065, 95% CI [–0.144, − 0.008]). Similarly, in the high physical activity group, the indirect effect was significant (–0.063, 95% CI [–0.128, − 0.008]). For low physical activity adolescents, the indirect effect was not statistically significant (0.065, 95% CI [–0.037, 0.183]). The negative sign of these indirect effects indicates that higher family functioning is associated with lower mobile phone dependence by fostering greater positive youth development, but only under conditions of moderate or high physical activity. Consistent with this pattern, the index of moderated mediation was significant for both the moderate vs. low comparison (index = − 0.130, 95% CI [–0.269, − 0.016]) and the high vs. low comparison (index = − 0.128, 95% CI [–0.258, − 0.015]). This confirms that the mediated effect of family functioning on mobile phone dependence through positive youth development varies by physical activity level.

Discussion

This study investigated how family functioning relates to adolescent mobile phone dependence, and through what mechanisms and conditions this influence is manifested. Overall, our findings support a moderated mediation model: family functioning was linked to adolescent mobile phone dependence indirectly via positive youth development, and this indirect pathway was contingent on physical activity levels. In simpler terms, adolescents from well-functioning families showed lower mobile phone dependence by having higher positive youth development – primarily among those who were physically active. For adolescents with moderate to high physical activity, better family functioning fostered greater positive youth development, which in turn significantly reduced their likelihood of mobile phone dependence. This mediated pathway was not evident in adolescents with low activity levels. Notably, without considering the mediator, family functioning’s direct association with mobile phone dependence was weak overall, suggesting that the effect of family environment on reducing phone overuse is largely channeled through improving youth developmental outcomes. These results provide a comprehensive summary of how family, individual, and behavioral factors interplay: a supportive family cultivates positive qualities in adolescents, which helps them avoid excessive mobile phone use, especially when combined with an active lifestyle.

Mediating role of positive youth development

Our findings highlight positive youth development as a key mechanism through which family functioning reduces adolescent mobile phone dependence. This mediating effect suggests that the pathway from a supportive family environment to lower digital overuse is not simply direct but is largely explained by the adolescents’ internal developmental assets. This mediating effect can be understood through multiple theoretical lenses. First, from Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory, which suggests developmental outcomes arise from the interplay between microsystemic (e.g., family) and internal assets (e.g., positive youth development characteristics) [45, 46]. Second, from a resilience-based view, positive youth development serves as a buffer against stress, peer pressure, and social alienation—factors that commonly drive mobile phone dependence. The significant role of positive youth development found herein corroborates prior longitudinal research by Dou and Shek (2021), who observed that enhanced positive youth development attributes significantly predicted reductions in adolescent internet addiction behaviors over time [47]. Similarly, Shek and Yu (2016) demonstrated that adolescents with robust positive youth development competencies exhibited markedly lower susceptibility to mobile phone dependence [44]. Yu and Shek demonstrated that both paternal and maternal responsiveness were negatively associated with social networking addiction through the mediation of positive youth development attributes [26]. When parents are more involved and responsive, their children are more likely to develop various positive attributes such as emotional competence, behavioral competence, and a positive belief in the future; attributes that reduce young people’s problematic social networking behavior. This finding is consistent with relational developmental systems theory, suggesting that positive youth development is cultivated in supportive relationships and contexts, with family functioning serving as a primary relational context that fosters these assets and thereby confers indirect protection [23, 26]. Our results extend these findings explicitly to mobile phone addiction, thereby reinforcing positive youth development as a pivotal protective factor against diverse forms of adolescent digital addiction. Additionally, recent intervention studies underscore this mediation: structured programs explicitly designed to bolster positive youth development attributes, such as self-regulatory skills and emotional competence training, consistently demonstrate efficacy in reducing adolescent mobile phone and internet addiction behaviors [48]. Collectively, these converging lines of evidence robustly highlight positive youth development as a critical mechanism through which positive family environments exert sustained protective effects on adolescent mobile phone dependency, affirming the theoretical importance and practical relevance of fostering adolescents’ developmental strengths within preventive strategies.

Moderating role of physical activity

A key contribution of this study is uncovering physical activity level as a significant moderator of the above mediation process. We found that the protective effect of positive youth development against mobile phone dependence was evident only among adolescents who were sufficiently physically active. These nuanced moderating effects underscore physical activity as a significant behavioral factor that modifies adolescents’ vulnerability to mobile phone dependence, aligning closely with existing literature suggesting that regular physical activity serves as a protective behavioral resource for adolescents. These moderation effects align conceptually with existing research that suggests physical activity is associated with enhanced self-regulatory capacities, emotional control, and psychological resilience [49]. Previous studies have consistently shown that higher physical activity is related to improved mental health outcomes [50], conditions that might reduce adolescents’ reliance on mobile phones as emotional coping mechanism [30]. Although these studies did not specifically examine moderated mediation effects involving family functioning and positive youth development, they provide indirect theoretical support for our findings regarding the role of physical activity as an enhancer of psychosocial resources and behavioral regulation.

The protective role of physical activity can be explained through the lens of self-regulation theory, which posits that physical exercise improves executive functions and impulse control, critical factors for resisting addictive behaviors [51]. Active adolescents are more likely to have structured daily routines, purposeful social interactions, and enhanced psychological resources, which collectively reduce the likelihood of excessive mobile phone use as a compensatory coping strategy. Conversely, adolescents with sedentary lifestyles may lack these protective behavioral and psychological resources, thus becoming more reliant on external sources of support, such as family functioning and positive youth development attributes, to regulate their mobile phone usage. In this regard, our findings suggest a compensatory protective effect, whereby physical activity can partially substitute or complement other psychosocial protective mechanisms. This interpretation aligns well with existing frameworks, which advocate holistic, multilevel prevention strategies that integrate behavioral, psychological, and ecological components to effectively address adolescent digital addictions [44]. Beyond individual-level mechanisms, an alternative interpretation is that adolescents’ physical activity may partly broaden family health lifestyles. For example, parental phubbing has been associated with adolescents’ mobile phone dependency, with evidence suggesting that a weakened parent–child relationship partially explains this link [52]. Thus, families that support higher physical activity may also model healthier digital habits or implement clearer screen-related rules, which could amplify the apparent protective role of physical activity [53]. In addition, lower physical activity often co-occurs with a more sedentary lifestyle; sedentary behavior has been positively associated with mobile phone dependency among Chinese adolescents [54].

Our findings regarding the Chinese sample must be interpreted within the cultural framework of academic pressure where mobile phone dependency likely stems from a mix of educational necessity and recreational compensation [55, 56]. While digital platforms are integral to modern Chinese education for homework and communication the significant moderating role of physical activity observed in this study suggests that a substantial portion of dependency may be driven by sedentary recreational use as a strategy to cope with academic burnout [56]. This implies that physical activity reduces dependency effectively by providing a healthier alternative to screen-based escapism rather than merely displacing the time spent on study related mobile usage. Together with the significant moderated mediation effects found for the indirect path through positive youth development, these results highlight that physical activity plays a more prominent role in modulating the impact of positive youth development on mobile phone dependence rather than directly moderating the relationship between family functioning and mobile phone dependence. Thus, interventions aimed at reducing mobile phone dependence may benefit from promoting physical activity to strengthen both family support effects and positive youth development.

Theoretical and practical contributions

Theoretically, this study contributes to the growing literature that integrates developmental and behavioral perspectives on adolescent health. By combining the positive youth development framework, ecological systems theory, and self-determination theory, we demonstrate that adolescent behavior is shaped by the interplay among family relationships, personal strengths, and health behaviors. Our use of a moderated mediation model advances methodological sophistication in this area, highlighting the conditional processes that underlie adolescent digital behavior. Furthermore, this research expands the theoretical understanding of physical activity beyond its physical health benefits by positioning physical activity as a developmental facilitator that enhances the effectiveness of psychosocial inputs. This integration of behavioral and psychological dimensions may inform future models of digital addiction and adolescent risk behavior.

Practically, our findings offer actionable insights into interventions. While this study utilized a global measure of family functioning, the construction is inherently multidimensional. In particular, affective involvement and family communication (e.g., Affection and Partnership) are likely to be especially protective, as emotionally supportive and communicative family environments enhance adolescents’ sense of security and reduce reliance on smartphones for emotional regulation, as shown in prior research [57]. In addition, family adaptability and shared commitment (e.g., Adaptability and Resolve) may buffer against problematic mobile phone use by promoting flexible coping and balanced family cohesion [58, 59]. Future research could deepen our understanding by investigating which specific dimensions are most influential in the observed relationships. Furthermore, programs designed to mitigate adolescent mobile phone dependence should adopt a holistic approach that combines family-based strategies with positive youth development promotion and physical activity enhancement. Schools and communities can implement comprehensive health education curricula that simultaneously target parenting practices, character strengths, and physical fitness. Moreover, digital wellness campaigns may be more effective when they incorporate physical activity challenges or sports-based family engagement initiatives.

Limitations and future directions

This study has limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes strong causal conclusions. Although the model is theoretically plausible, longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to confirm directionality. Future research should track adolescents over time to test whether increases in family functioning or positive youth development preceded reductions in mobile phone use. Second, the sample was drawn from a single school in one region of China, which may limit generalizability. While psychological mechanisms are likely universal, cultural factors (e.g., regional academic pressure, family norms) could influence perceptions of the environment and lifestyle behaviors. Replication in diverse cultural contexts (e.g., Western or other Asian samples) and multi-site sampling within China (e.g., urban vs. rural schools) is necessary. Third, all measures were self-reported, raising concerns about response biases. Objective measures of phone use (e.g., usage logs) and activity (e.g., wearable trackers) would strengthen validity in future studies. Fourth, although key covariates such as gender and depression were controlled for, the sample characteristics and the scope of psychological variables included in the model impose certain constraints. The higher proportion of female participants may limit representativeness, and the omission of other mental health indicators (e.g., anxiety) suggests that residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. Future studies should employ more balanced sampling strategies and incorporate a broader range of psychological constructs to further validate and extend the present model.

Conclusion

This study contributes meaningful insights into the complex relationships among adolescents’ family functioning, positive youth development, physical activity, and mobile phone dependence. Results underscore the significant moderating role of physical activity in the direct association between family functioning and mobile phone dependence, as well as in the mediated path through positive youth development. Specifically, the protective effects of strong family functioning against mobile phone dependence were more pronounced among physically active adolescents. Furthermore, positive youth development emerged as a critical mediator that effectively translated supportive family environments into reduced reliance on mobile phones, contingent upon adolescents maintaining adequate physical activity levels. These findings highlight the imperative of promoting active lifestyles as a key component in intervention programs aimed at improving family environments and youth development outcomes, thus effectively addressing mobile phone dependence among adolescents.
