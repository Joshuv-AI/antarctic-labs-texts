Impact of Paleoclimatic Variability on Shale Oil Enrichment in Deep to Semi-Deep Lacustrine Facies: A Case Study of the Second Member of the Funing Formation, Qintong Depression, Subei Basin, Eastern China
Authors: Shaoyu Tang, Zejin Shi, Suhua Zang, Caixia Hua, Wenjie Li, Qian Tan, Hao Fu, Hailong Xiao, Keke Ning, Hao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lacustrine depositional systems are more sensitive to climatic fluctuations than marine systems, producing significant stratigraphic and sedimentological heterogeneity that affects shale oil distribution. However, the mechanisms linking paleoclimatic variability to shale oil enrichment remain insufficiently constrained. This study investigates the coupling between hydrological variations, organic matter enrichment, and pore structure development under different climatic conditions in the Second Member of the Funing Formation from Well A1 in the Qintong Depression. Using a combination of total organic carbon (TOC) analysis, total sulfur (TS) analysis, X-ray diffraction (XRD), high-resolution geochemical profiling, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and helium porosimetry, we reveal how paleoclimate influences shale oil accumulation. The Second Member of the Funing Formation is subdivided into four stratigraphic units based on TOC variations, with units 1 and 3 corresponding to extremely arid conditions, unit 2 to arid conditions, and unit 4 to a humid climate. Both arid and humid conditions promoted elevated lacustrine primary productivity and moderate sedimentation rates, thereby favoring efficient organic matter accumulation. Increased water depth and optimal salinity also facilitated bottom-water anoxia, enhancing organic matter preservation. The study demonstrates that climatic variations in terrigenous clastic input and lake salinity affect the mineral composition of the lacustrine shale, influencing pore development. Under arid conditions, favorable mineralogical proportions enhances the formation of intercrystalline pores within the clay matrix, improving hydrocarbon storage potential. This research establishes that paleoclimate plays a critical role in shale oil enrichment by influencing both organic matter accumulation and pore structure. Among the analyzed units, unit 2, deposited under arid conditions, shows the most favorable source–reservoir characteristics and is identified as the most promising interval for future shale oil exploration.

Introduction

Shale oil resources, renowned for their vast potential, are broadly distributed across the globe. Currently, most shale oil plays under active exploration and development are located within marine sedimentary successions, including the Late Cretaceous Eagle Ford Formation and Devonian–Mississippian Bakken Formation in the United States, the Vaca Muerta Formation in Argentina, and the Ediacaran (Infracambrian) Athel Formation in Oman. − In recent years, driven by ongoing advancements in unconventional oil and gas technologies, lacustrine shale oil systems have emerged as a critical frontier for resource exploration. Significant breakthroughs have been achieved in multiple Chinese basins, such as the Triassic of the Ordos Basin, the Paleogene of the Bohai Bay Basin, the Jurassic of the Sichuan Basin, the Cretaceous of the Songliao Basin, and the Paleogene of the Subei Basin. −

In contrast to marine shale systems, which typically form under extensive and tectonically stable depositional settings conducive to sustained organic-matter burial, lacustrine basins are generally smaller in spatial extent and highly susceptible to regional climatic variability. − Consequently, they exhibit pronounced stratigraphic and sedimentological heterogeneity, , which in turn leads to spatial and temporal variability in organic matter enrichment, mineral assemblages, and hydrocarbon distribution within lacustrine shale systems. Total organic carbon (TOC) content and reservoir quality are commonly recognized as critical parameters for assessing shale oil prospectivity. Current research indicates that the accumulation of organic matter in lacustrine shales is closely linked to prevailing climatic and hydrological regimes. The TOC reflects the hydrocarbon generation potential of the shale and directly influences the degree of shale oil enrichment. , Some scholars have proposed that under arid paleoclimatic conditions, elevated salinity leads to the accumulation of biogenic nutrients (N and P), thereby stimulating phytoplankton blooms and enhancing primary productivity. This process promotes the formation of high-quality source rocks favorable for shale oil enrichment. , Conversely, other researchers argue that humid climatic conditions, characterized by increased precipitation and surface runoff, facilitate the influx of terrigenous organic matter and nutrients into lacustrine systems, leading to the development of organic-rich shales and the formation of shale oil sweet spots. ,

While high total organic carbon (TOC) is widely considered a key factor in shale oil enrichment, it is not the sole determinant. Recent research on the Upper Cretaceous Qingshankou Formation in the Songliao Basin has demonstrated that high TOC alone is not sufficient to ensure shale oil enrichment. Instead, the distribution and preservation of shale oil are strongly controlled by pore development and nanoscale pore structure, particularly the abundance of small pores (1–5 nm), while higher clay content and larger average pore diameters tend to reduce the residual shale oil capacity. Wang et al. demonstrated, through their study on the Paleogene Shahejie Formation in the Bohai Bay Basin, that the enrichment of free oil is primarily controlled by both TOC and the development of an effective pore network. Similarly, Liu et al. reported that shales characterized by high TOC, a high abundance of siliceous minerals, and elevated brittleness indices tend to possess well-developed pore systems, which provide the necessary storage space and migration pathways for hydrocarbons. More recently, Hong et al. developed a quick method to evaluate pore connectivity and ultimate imbibed porosity, showing that these parameters, as proxies for reservoir porosity and permeability, directly control shale oil storage and migration, thereby exerting a significant influence on reservoir quality and the degree of shale oil enrichment. However, in terrestrial shale systems, paleoclimate may exert a simultaneous influence on both organic matter accumulation and reservoir quality. Despite this potential dual control, the specific coupling mechanisms linking hydrological variability, organic matter enrichment, and pore development under different climatic regimes remain poorly constrained. This is particularly evident in lacustrine basins, where the synergistic effects of multiple climate–sensitive parameterssuch as lake salinity, water-column stratification, redox conditions, and terrigenous inputon shale oil accumulation have not been comprehensively investigated.

The Second Member of the Funing Formation (E1f2) in the Qintong Depression, Subei Basin, eastern China, represents a typical lacustrine organic-rich shale system. It has attracted considerable attention due to its relatively low total organic carbon (TOC) (averaging:1.32%), a distinct mixed sedimentary mineral assemblage, and the widespread occurrence of shale oil resources. , Previous studies have suggested that the depositional period of this Member records a brief Paleocene climatic episode, marked by significant alternations between arid and humid conditions. Against this backdrop, we analyze a continuous core from the E1f2 in Well A1, Qintong Depression, Subei Basin. Through mineralogical characterization and high-resolution inorganic elemental geochemistry of lacustrine sediments, we reconstruct the stage-wise paleoclimatic evolution of the E1f2. In combination with total sulfur (TS) and carbonate carbon isotopes (δ13Ccarb), we identify hydrological regime shifts associated with regional climate variability; the integrated salinity and redox proxy data sets provide detailed constraints on water-mass chemistry. Total organic carbon (TOC), Rock-Eval pyrolysis, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and helium porosimetry were used to elucidate how paleoclimate modulated organic-matter enrichment and pore development. On this basis, we propose a climate-driven shale oil enrichment model. This work has important implications for effective exploration and efficient development of shale oil resources in analogous lacustrine basins worldwide.

Geological Background

The Subei Basin is a Mesozoic–Cenozoic sedimentary basin developed atop the Paleozoic folded basement of the Yangtze Platform, representing the onshore segment of the broader Subei–South Yellow Sea Basin system. It is bounded by the Lusu Uplift and the Tan–Lu Fault to the west, the Binhai Uplift to the north, the Zhangbaling and Sunan Uplifts to the south, and the South Yellow Sea Basin to the east. The formation and evolution of the basin have been primarily governed by tectonic activity along the Tan–Lu Fault. Since the Late Cretaceous, the basin has undergone two major rift episodes, each followed by a phase of postrift thermal subsidence.

The Qintong Depression is located in the southeastern sector of the Subei Basin, situated between the Wubao Low Uplift and the Taizhou Uplift within the Dongtai Depression. Formed under a Cenozoic extensional tectonic regime, the depression exhibits a half-graben geometry, characterized by a major bounding fault on the southern margin and an onlapping sequence to the north. It features an open central depocenter with east–west–trending convergence. The depression trends northeast–southwest and covers an area of approximately 1200 km2. The Wubao Low Uplift marks its northwestern boundary, while the Qintong Fault defines its southeastern edge, separating it from the Taizhou Uplift. To the northeast, the Liangduo Low Uplift serves as a structural divide between the Qintong and Hai’an depressions (Figure A).

Structural and stratigraphic framework of the Qintong Depression in the Subei Basin; (A) regional tectonic setting, structural features, and well locations; (B) lithostratigraphic succession of the Subei Basin.

During the Paleocene–Eocene, the Subei Basin accumulated several kilometers of fluvial–lacustrine deposits, with the Funing, Dainan, and Sanduo Formations arranged in a stratigraphic sequence from base to top. The Funing Formation began with fluvial–deltaic sedimentation in a low-relief alluvial plain and recorded two major transgressive–regressive cycles, culminating in lacustrine deposition during the peak transgression stage. This evolutionary sequence resulted in the development of four distinct lithological members within the formation (Figure B). The First Member of the Funing Formation (E1f1) comprises interbedded brownish-red mudstone, silty mudstone, and brown fine-grained sandstone of variable thickness. From west to east, it transitions across a spectrum of depositional subfacies, including fluvial, lakeshore alluvial fan, and shallow lacustrine environments. During the depositional phase of the E1f2), the Qintong Depression acted as the primary sedimentary and subsidence center of the Subei Basin, characterized by relatively deep-water conditions. These settings favored the development of semideep to deep lacustrine facies, resulting in the accumulation of a dark mudstone–shale succession with a thickness ranging from 150 to 450 m. The Third Member (E1f3) consists of interbedded to heterolithic grayish-black mudstone and light gray to grayish-white siltstone and fine-grained sandstone, indicative of deposition in alternating lacustrine and deltaic environments. The Fourth Member (E1f4) reflects the reestablishment of a deep lacustrine depositional setting, marked by the accumulation of grayish-black mudstone.

Methods

Organic Geochemical Analysis

Total Organic Carbon (TOC) and Total Sulfur (TS) Analysis

The concentrations of total organic carbon (TOC) and total sulfur (TS) were measured using a LECO CS-230 carbon–sulfur analyzer. Approximately 10 g of each sample were ground into a fine powder with a particle size of less than 0.2 mm. To remove inorganic carbon components, the powdered samples were treated with 10% HCl. After acid treatment, the samples were dried in a constant-temperature oven at 60–80 °C for 12 h to ensure complete dehydration. The dried powders were then subjected to high-temperature combustion (>800 °C) in an oxygen-rich environment to oxidize organic carbon into carbon dioxide, which was subsequently detected and quantified.

Rock-Eval Pyrolysis Analysis

Rock-Eval pyrolysis was conducted using a Rock-Eval 6 Plus instrument to obtain pyrolysis parameters. For this analysis, approximately 10 g of each sample was ground to a particle size below 0.15 mm. The powdered samples were heated in a helium atmosphere to a final temperature of 600 °C. Volatile hydrocarbon content (S 1) was measured at 300 °C, while residual hydrocarbon generation potential (S 2) was recorded between 300 and 600 °C. The maximum pyrolysis temperature (T max) was defined as the temperature at which S 2 reached its peak value. The hydrogen index (HI) was calculated using the equation: HI = 100 × S 2/TOC following ref .

Vitrinite Reflectance (R o) Analysis

The determination of vitrinite reflectance (R o) follows the method outlined in the “Method for Determining Vitrinite Reflectance in Sedimentary Rocks” (SY/T 5124-2012). Rock samples were cut into blocks and mounted in epoxy and prepared as polished blocks, which were then stored in desiccators until measurement. The reflectance measurements were conducted using a Craic 308PV microscope photometer at a temperature of 23 °C ± 3 °C and relative humidity less than 70%. For each sample, a minimum of 30 measurement points were taken.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) Analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to quantify the mineral composition of the shale samples. Prior to analysis, the samples were oven-dried and finely ground using an agate mortar and pestle to obtain a particle size finer than 200 mesh. Bulk rock mineralogy was analyzed using a D/MAX-Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation. Mineral phases and their semiquantitative relative abundances (normalized to a total of 100%) were determined based on peak area calculations derived from ethylene glycol-treated diffraction patterns.

Inorganic Geochemical Analysis

Elemental Geochemical Testing

Major elements were quantified using a Philips PW2404 X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer. Fused glass discs were prepared by mixing powdered samples with lithium tetraborate flux (Li2B4O7) at a sample/flux = 1:8 (w/w) ratio and heating to 1000 °C to yield 5.0 g discs. Trace element concentrations were determined with a PerkinElmer ELAN DRC-e inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP–MS). For digestion, accurately weighed aliquots (50 ± 1 mg) were placed in sealed vessels with 1.5 mL high-purity HNO3 and 1.5 mL high-purity HF and heated at 190 °C for 48 h; after cooling, solutions were evaporated to dryness, redissolved in 1 mL HNO3, and evaporated again to dryness. Finally, 3 mL of 30% HNO3 was added, and the sealed vessels were reheated at 190 °C for a further 48 h prior to analysis. Analytical precision for trace elements was better than ±5%.

Carbonate δ13C Analyses

For δ13C analysis of carbonates, approximately 50–300 μg of dried sample powder was reacted with 100% phosphoric acid (H3PO4) at 70 °C. The resulting CO2 gas was introduced into a MAT 253 isotope ratio mass spectrometer for isotopic measurement. The δ13C values were calibrated against the international reference standard NBS-19 (δ13C = +1.95‰) and the Chinese national standard GBW04416 (δ13C = +1.61‰).

Field-Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM)

A Gatan-type argon ion polisher was used to prepare polished surfaces of small sample sections following cutting and grinding, utilizing an ion beam energy of 1 keV. After polishing, the samples were coated for 150 s using a Leica SCD500 sputter coater. Subsequent observations of mineral morphology and pore types were conducted using a Quanta 250 FEG field-emission environmental scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a working distance of 10 mm.

Helium Pycnometry

Helium porosity and bulk density measurements of the shale samples were conducted in accordance with the SY/T 5336-2006 industry standard, using a KXD-II porosity analyzer. Samples were prepared as cylindrical core plugs with a diameter of 2.5 cm and a height of 3 cm. Porosity was calculated based on the difference between bulk density and matrix density, both determined via the helium expansion method.

Results

Organic Geochemical Characteristics

TOC is a key parameter for evaluating the abundance of organic matter in shales. In addition, shale reservoirs have low permeability and primarily are dominated by low- to medium-molecular-weight liquid hydrocarbons. Therefore, the organic geochemical parameter S 1 can be used to characterize the oil content in shales. The TOC in the Second Member of the Funing Formation (E1f2) ranges from 0.38–3.49%, with an average of 1.38%. The S 1 values vary between 0.08–4.49 mg/g, averaging 1.56 mg/g. As shown in Figure , the studied stratigraphic interval is subdivided into four units based on TOC distribution: two low-TOC units (units 1 and 3), one medium-TOC unit (unit 2), and one high-TOC unit (unit 4).

Integrated organic-geochemical stratigraphic column and sampling locations for well A1, E1f2.

Unit 1 exhibits relatively low TOC, ranging from 0.45% to 1.34% (average: 0.77%), with S 1 values between 0.08 and 3.96 mg/g (average: 1.01 mg/g). The dominant kerogen types in this unit are type II2 and type III (Figure A). Unit 2, characterized by moderate TOC (0.75%–2.73%, average: 1.74%), shows the highest S 1 distribution, ranging from 0.24 to 4.49 mg/g (average: 2.85 mg/g), with type II1 kerogen as the prevailing type. Unit 3 displays similar organic matter richness to unit 1, with TOC values ranging from 0.38% to 1.29% (average: 0.73%) and S 1 values between 0.66 and 1.83 mg/g (average: 1.22 mg/g), predominantly containing type II2 kerogen. Unit 4 contains the highest TOC across the interval (1.78%–3.49%, average: 2.42%), although its S 1 values are comparatively lower, ranging from 0.84 to 2.24 mg/g (average: 1.40 mg/g), with type I kerogen being the dominant type.

Organic matter types (A) and thermal maturity characteristics (B) of the E1f2 shale.

Furthermore, vitrinite reflectance (R o) values of lacustrine shale samples range from 0.86% to 1.38%, with an average of 1.03% (Figure B), indicating that the organic matter is predominantly within the oil-generative window and currently undergoing thermally driven hydrocarbon generation.

Mineralogical and Petrological Characteristics

As shown by the XRD results (Figure ), the shale in the E1f2 is primarily composed of clay minerals and quartz, followed by carbonate minerals (Figure A), with minor amounts of pyrite and analcime. On average, the clay mineral content is 34.79%, quartz accounts for 28.59%, feldspar for 14.67%, and carbonate minerals for 18.07%, including 8.20% calcite and 9.85% dolomite. Pyrite is present at an average content of 2.72%.

(A) Mineralogical composition of E1f2 shales; (B) ternary diagram of mineral constituents for the four stratigraphic units.

The mineral ternary diagram (Figure B) shows that the E1f2 shale is primarily composed of a ternary mixture of felsic, clay-rich, and carbonate components. These shales range from traditional argillaceous rocks to fine-grained mixed sedimentary rocks, exhibiting typical characteristics of hybrid facies. The lacustrine deposits are strongly influenced by terrigenous input of felsic clastic material and intrabasinal chemical precipitation of carbonate minerals. This highlights the significant roles played by both felsic and carbonate minerals in the genesis and diagenetic evolution of these shales.

In the E1f2, five major lithofacies are identified: mudstone, calcareous mudstone, dolomitic mudstone, shale, and dolomitic/calcareous shale, with lithofacies assemblages varying significantly among stratigraphic units (Figure ). Mudstone occurs mainly in unit 4 and is characterized by abundant fine-grained feldspar, quartz, and clay minerals, with feldspar and quartz dispersed within the clay matrix; laminations are poorly developed (Figure a,b), and fish fossils are occasionally present (Figure c). Calcareous mudstone occurs primarily in mid-unit 4 and at the base of unit 1; it is dominated by clay minerals and exhibits a massive fabric (Figure d); dispersed microcrystalline calcite and well-preserved ostracod fossils are also observed (Figure e,f). Dolomitic mudstone and dolomitic/calcareous shale are interbedded in units 3 and 1. The dolomitic mudstone is largely nonlaminated, composed mainly of micritic dolomite and clay minerals (Figure m), and contains dispersed organic debris along with fossilized roots and stems of higher plants (Figure n,o). By contrast, the dolomitic/calcareous shale is distinctly laminated (Figure g,p), consisting of alternating dolomite-rich clay laminae (micrite) and feldspar–quartz laminae (Figure h,q), with calcite cement commonly filling intergranular spaces between feldspar and quartz (Figure i,r). Shale occurs in unit 2 and the lower to middle intervals of unit 1, showing well-developed horizontal lamination (Figure j), characterized by alternating feldspar–quartz laminae and organic-rich clay laminae (Figure k). Plant fossils are clearly visible in cores (Figure s), and algal fossils are also relatively common (Figure l).

Inorganic Geochemical Characteristics

The major oxide constituents of the E1f2 are SiO2, Al2O3, and CaO, with average concentrations of 52.11, 12.39, and 9.48%, respectively. Here, the enrichment levels of major and trace elements are compared with Post-Archean Australian Shale (PAAS), which has been widely applied as a geochemical reference representing the average upper continental crust composition. Such normalization provides a standard baseline to assess relative enrichment or depletion, minimizes the influence of lithological variability, and facilitates paleoenvironmental interpretations relevant to shale oil enrichment. Compared with PAAS (Figure A), the enrichment levels of four major oxidesMgO, Na2O, P2O5, and MnOexhibit significant variation across the four stratigraphic units. Units 1–3 display markedly higher enrichment of MgO and Na2O relative to unit 4. Trace elements Li and Sr also show notable enrichment (Figure B), with Li enrichment closely associated with typical saline lacustrine sedimentation and evaporation processes driven by arid climatic conditions. , Sr enrichment is strongly correlated with detrital carbonate input and the chemical precipitation of carbonate minerals in lacustrine environments. , Both Li and Sr exhibit a higher degree of enrichment in units 1 to 3 compared with unit 4. The enrichment of Cd is closely linked to total organic carbon (TOC) and sulfidic depositional conditions. Cd concentrations are significantly elevated in units 2 to 4, whereas unit 1 shows values similar to those of the upper continental crust, indicating a minor degree of depletion.

Geochemical characteristics of E1f2; (A) major oxide concentrations normalized to PAAS; (B) trace element concentrations normalized to PAAS; (C) chondrite-normalized REE patterns; (D) NASC-normalized REE patterns.

In addition to PAAS normalization, chondrite- and North American Shale Composite­(NASC)-normalized rare earth element (REE) patterns provide further insights into the geochemical characteristics. Chondrite normalization highlights the crustal REE distribution, while NASC normalization represents typical shale REE patterns, helping to interpret depositional conditions. The chondrite-normalized and NASC-normalized REE distribution patterns of the shale in the E1f2 are shown in Figure C,D. All samples exhibit consistent distribution trends, characterized by a distinct rightward inclination in their REE curves, indicating enrichment in light rare earth elements (LREE). This is attributed to the adsorption of REE from detrital mineral suspended particles onto clay surfaces and their complexation with organic matter during slow sedimentation. LREE are preferentially adsorbed onto organic matter and clay minerals, subsequently incorporated into the sediments, while heavy rare earth elements (HREE) form stable aqueous complexes and remain in the water column, resulting in significant REE fractionation. Notably, samples from units 1 and 3 exhibit steeper slopes compared to those from units 2 and 4, reflecting stronger LREE enrichment and a greater degree of REE fractionation. When normalized to the North American Shale Composite (NASC), the REE distribution patterns of the oil shale samples closely resemble typical shale patterns, with slight LREE enrichment. The total REE (∑REE) content ranges from 97.72 to 205.98 μg/g, with an average of 150.95 μg/g, slightly lower than the average value reported for North American shale (173.21 μg/g). The LREE content ranges from 87.74 to 189.35 μg/g, with an average of 137.74 μg/g, while the HREE content ranges from 9.98 to 17.24 μg/g, with a mean value of 13.21 μg/g. The ∑LREE/∑HREE ratio ranges from 8.15 to 12.31, with an average of 10.39, significantly higher than the North American shale benchmark of 7.44, indicating a pronounced LREE dominance. The (La/Yb)N ratio characterizes the slope of the REE distribution curve after chondrite normalization, reflecting the degree of fractionation between LREEs and HREEs. It is commonly used to assess relative sedimentation rates. In low sedimentation rate environments, prolonged water–particle interaction enhances LREE adsorption, leading to higher (La/Yb)N values. Conversely, higher sedimentation rates tend to preserve lower (La/Yb)N detrital signatures. ,, Among the four units, unit 2 has the highest average (La/Yb)N value (1.95), followed by unit 1 (1.83) and unit 4 (1.80), while unit 3 has the lowest average value (1.79).

The δ13Ccarb values of samples from the E1f2 range from −4.795‰ to 4.195‰, with an average of −0.158‰. Unit 1 exhibits δ13Ccarb values ranging between −4.795‰ and 1.759‰, with a mean value of −1.833‰. In unit 2, δ13Ccarb values range from −3.200‰ to 2.428‰, averaging 0.717‰. A noticeable negative excursion is observed in unit 3, where δ13Ccarb values fall between −2.617‰ and −0.553‰, with an average of −1.365‰. In contrast, unit 4 maintains consistently elevated δ13Ccarb values, ranging from 0.498‰ to 4.195‰, with an average of 2.047‰ (Figure ).

Physical Properties Characteristics

The vertical variation in helium porosity within the shale of the E1f2 is illustrated in Figure A. Higher porosity values are predominantly concentrated in the lower part of unit 2 and the basal portion of unit 1, whereas the upper section of unit 1 and the entirety of unit 4 exhibit relatively low porosity. Unit 3 maintains moderately consistent porosity throughout the interval. Figure B presents helium porosity data classified by lithofacies. Among all facies, shale exhibits the highest porosity, ranging from 3.57% to 6.90%, with an average value of 5.37%. The dolomitic/calcareous shale follows, with porosity values ranging from 2.89% to 4.68% (average: 3.64%). In contrast, the calcareous mudstone and dolomitic mudstone display poorly developed porosity, ranging from 0.73% to 2.64% (average: 1.79%) and 1.30% to 2.55% (average: 1.99%), respectively. The mudstone exhibits the least developed porosity, with values ranging from 0.73% to 2.64% and an average of only 1.17%.

(A) Vertical variation in helium porosity by stratigraphic unit; (B) helium porosity by lithofacies.

Four types of reservoir spaces have been identified in the E1f2: organic matter–related pores, clay mineral–related pores, rigid particle–related pores, and microfractures. Among them, intercrystalline pores within clay mineralsrepresenting the primary form of clay mineral–related porosityare the most widespread throughout the pore system of E1f2. These pores occur as narrow, slit-like voids dispersed within clay mineral aggregates (Figure A,B), with pore sizes ranging from 2 to 200 nm, and constitute the dominant pore type in unit 4. Intergranular pores between rigid particles, particularly within clay minerals, exhibit a broader pore size distribution (Figure C,D), typically ranging from 50 to 800 nm. These pores are primarily developed in unit 2. Nearly spherical organic pores are the most common type of organic matter–related porosity, with pore sizes ranging from 2 to 100 nm. These pores frequently occur in clusters, often forming honeycomb-like structures (Figure E). Another type of organic matter–related pore is contraction fissures that form along the edges of organic matter fragments (Figure F). Organic matter–related pores are present across all four stratigraphic units. Rigid particle–related pores mainly consist of intergranular voids between feldspar, quartz, and carbonate grains. Most of these pores are partially or entirely filled by clay minerals or cemented by calcite (Figure G). Dissolution pores within carbonate mineral grains also represent a category of rigid particle–related porosity. These pores are typically smaller (2–60 nm), distributed in isolation, and serve as the dominant pore type in units 1 and 3 (Figure H). In addition, several elongated microfractures have been observed between feldspar–quartz laminae and clay mineral laminae (Figure I).

Discussion

Paleoclimatic Reconstruction

The Chemical Index of Alteration (CIA) has been widely employed as a robust geochemical proxy for paleoclimatic reconstructions based on sedimentary deposits. , The CIA is calculated using the following formula 1 CIA=Al2O3/(Al2O3+CaO*+Na2O+K2O)×100 where CaO* represents the calcium oxide content derived exclusively from silicate fractions. To isolate the silicate-bound CaO, a correction is applied using P2O5 data following the approach of McLennan. If the corrected CaO* content is lower than Na2O (in molar proportions), the remaining CaO* is used directly. Otherwise, CaO* is assumed to be equal to the molar concentration of Na2O. Fedo et al. proposed a more refined geochemical proxy for evaluating chemical weathering intensity, namely the Plagioclase Index of Alteration (PIA). The PIA is calculated as 2 PIA=(Al2O3−K2O)/(Al2O3−K2O+CaO*+Na2O)×100

This index effectively minimizes the influence of K-metasomatism (i.e., postdepositional potassium enrichment leading to the formation of K-feldspar), thus providing a more accurate estimation of the degree of chemical weathering in sedimentary materials. A significant positive correlation between PIA and CIA is observed in the analyzed samples (Figure A), suggesting that the influence of K-metasomatism is relatively minor. Therefore, the CIA can be considered a reliable proxy for paleoclimatic reconstruction in the E1f2. In general, CIA values ranging from 50 to 65 are indicative of arid climatic conditions with low chemical weathering intensity. Values between 65 and 85 suggest temperate climates associated with moderate rates of chemical weathering, whereas CIA values exceeding 85 typically reflect warm and humid climates characterized by intense chemical weathering. , Elevated concentrations of strontium (Sr) in sediments are generally associated with water evaporation and solute enrichment under arid and hot climatic conditions. Accordingly, the Sr/Cu ratio is widely employed as a paleoenvironmental proxy for paleoclimate reconstruction: higher Sr/Cu values typically indicate arid climates, whereas lower values are commonly interpreted as reflecting humid or temperate conditions. ,,

The CIA values exhibit a stratigraphically staged variation. Samples from unit 1 and 3 fall within the CIA <50 range, suggesting that the sediments were either minimally weathered and rapidly transported to the lacustrine basina scenario consistent with the CIA values of fresh granite (≈45–55)or influenced by the input of authigenic minerals, such as fresh Barite, which typically exhibits CIA values of 30–45. , These interpretations imply that the deposition of units 1 and 3 likely occurred under extremely arid and cold climatic conditions. A transient increase in CIA values is observed in unit 2, with most values falling within the 50–65 range. This suggests a brief climatic fluctuation toward more temperate conditions, although the overall climate during this interval remained relatively arid. In contrast, unit 4 exhibits a marked increase in CIA values, with the majority of samples exceeding 65. This indicates a climatic transition toward a temperate regime, characterized by enhanced chemical weathering under comparatively more humid conditions.

The Sr/Cu ratio further supports the inference of stage-wise climatic shifts during the deposition of the E1f2, as evidenced by CIA variations. During the deposition of units 1 and 3, Sr/Cu values display relatively high-amplitude fluctuations at elevated levels, reflecting arid climatic conditions. Conversely, unit 2 shows a temporary decrease in Sr/Cu values, consistent with the inferred climatic shift toward more humid conditions based on the CIA data. In unit 4, Sr/Cu exhibits a further decline, indicating a transition to a warmer and more humid paleoclimate.

Based on these geochemical paleoclimate proxies, the E1f2 can be subdivided into three paleoclimatic intervals. Units 1 and 3 correspond to extremely arid depositional phases, while unit 2 represents a moderately arid interval. In contrast, unit 4 is associated with a humid depositional regime, indicating a progressive shift toward more temperate and wetter climatic conditions (Figure B).

Organic Matter Enrichment under the Influence of Paleoclimate

Terrestrial Input and Sedimentation Rate

Aluminum (Al) and titanium (Ti) are widely recognized as reliable indicators of terrigenous detrital input. Al is primarily sourced from fine-grained aluminosilicate clay minerals, whereas Ti is commonly associated with both clay minerals and heavy mineral assemblages. During the lake evolution process, the concentrations of aluminum (Al) and titanium (Ti) in units 1 to 3 exhibit similar variation patterns that correspond closely with climatic fluctuations (Figure ). This suggests that under a broadly arid climatic background, short-term increases in humidity enhanced precipitation in the lake catchment area. As a result, inflowing rivers delivered larger amounts of chemically weathered terrigenous detritus into the lacustrine system. During this period, the lake basin remained relatively small in spatial extent, and the increased influx of detrital material led to an overall increase in sedimentation rates within the basin (Figure B). In contrast, during the deposition of unit 4, the concentrations of Al and Ti no longer show a positive correlation with CIA values. As illustrated in Figure A, when the CIA exceeds 65, the terrigenous detrital indicators display a declining trend. This phenomenon reflects a shift in sediment delivery dynamics under more humid climatic conditions. Increased regional precipitation, combined with reduced evaporation from the lake surface, led to a rapid expansion of the lacustrine area. Consequently, fine-grained terrigenous particles became less likely to be transported across the expanded water body into the central basin, thereby limiting the accumulation of detrital sediments in distal settings.

Analysis of terrigenous input and sedimentation rates; (A) relationship between CIA and Ti content; (B) association between Ti content and (La/Yb)N; (C) correlation between Ti content and TOC; (D) relationship between (La/Yb)N and TOC.

The quantity of fine-grained terrigenous detritus and the sedimentation rate in the lacustrine system exert a dual influence on organic matter (OM) concentrationeither enhancing preservation by accelerating OM transit through the oxidative degradation zone or reducing OM content via dilution. − However, no clear correlation is observed between terrigenous input and total organic carbon (TOC) in our data set (Figure C), indicating that OM accumulation is not solely controlled by detrital influx. This decoupling can be attributed to changes in organic matter sources across stratigraphic units. In units 1 to 3, deposited under arid to extremely arid climates, OM is primarily composed of type II kerogen, derived from a mixture of terrestrial input and lacustrine autochthonous production. In contrast, unit 4formed under more humid conditionsis dominated by type I kerogen, mainly sourced from algal biomass and other lower aquatic organisms (Figure A). The enhanced detrital supply during humid intervals increased the sedimentation rate (Figure D), resulting in a nonlinear relationship between TOC and sedimentation. As the (La/Yb)N ratio increases, TOC initially rises and then declines, displaying a parabolic trend. This suggests that moderate sedimentation rates facilitate OM preservation by delivering nutrients and plant debris, whereas excessively high rates intensify dilution, ultimately hindering OM enrichment.

Paleowater Depth and Water-Column Chemical Conditions

Numerous studies have demonstrated that no marine transgression occurred during the Paleogene deposition in the Subei Basin. This conclusion is supported by detailed investigations of lacustrine paleobiota, as well as the global sea-level fall associated with the late Paleocene cooling event. − Su further compared the sulfur isotopic composition of middle Paleogene rock salt from nonmarine faulted depressions in eastern China with that of contemporaneous marine sulfate. The results reveal that sulfate in the rock salt samples exhibits δ34S values ranging from 22.0–29.5‰, significantly higher than those of Paleogene seawater sulfate (12–15‰). These isotopic signatures of evaporites provide compelling evidence for the absence of marine incursion during deposition. Therefore, the lacustrine water column during the deposition of the E1f2 can be interpreted as a closed, independently evolving system, predominantly controlled by paleoclimatic conditions.

To reconstruct paleowater depth during the deposition of the E1f2, we employed Rb/Zr ratios. Rb accumulates in quiet, deeper waters, while Zr preferentially deposits in higher-energy, shallower settings, allowing the Rb/Zr ratio to also reflect paleowater depth. ,, Paleosalinity was assessed using Sr/Ba and S/TOC ratios. Ba precipitates at low salinity as BaSO4, while SrSO4 forms at higher salinities, making Sr/Ba a robust salinity indicator: values <0.5 indicate freshwater, 0.5–1.0 brackish, and >1.0 saline conditions. The S/TOC ratio provides additional constraint, with values <0.1 suggesting freshwater and >0.1 indicating brackish to marine settings. , Paleoredox conditions were inferred using redox-sensitive trace elements such as V, Ni, Cr, Co, and U. Vanadium enriches under reducing conditions, and Ni tends to form authigenic sulfides in anoxic settings. , The V/(V + Ni) ratio is commonly used to classify redox states: >0.77 indicates strongly reducing, 0.60–0.77 moderately reducing, and <0.60 weakly stratified or dysoxic conditions. The V/Sc ratio further supports redox interpretations, as Sc is redox-insensitive and V enrichment increases with anoxia. ,

The paleoclimatic proxies in the sedimentary profiles of the study area exhibit trends that correspond well with paleowater depth, salinity, and redox indicators (Figure A,B), demonstrating that regional paleoclimate played a major role in controlling the evolution of the lake water-column. During the extremely arid depositional stages of units 1 and 3, lake levels remained relatively low, while paleosalinity proxies showed an upward trend (Figure ), accompanied by reduced anoxia. This suggests that limited precipitation and freshwater runoff not only reduced lake volume but also likely caused net evaporation, thereby increasing water salinity. The weakened stratification of the water-column further contributed to reduced anoxic conditions. In unit 2, Rb/Zr ratios display a transient increase (Figure ), likely reflecting a short-term enhancement in runoff under persistently high evaporation. Correspondingly, lake salinity decreased, and redox proxies indicate intensified anoxia. Under more humid climatic conditions during the deposition of unit 4, the lake reached its greatest water depth (Figure ), marking a further transition toward lower salinity. During this period, the water body remained stable and strongly anoxic.

Analysis of water-column geochemical conditions; (A) relationship between CIA and Rb/Zr; (B) relationship between CIA and Sr/Ba; (C) relationship between Rb/Zr and V/(V + Ni); (D) relationship between Sr/Ba and V/Sc; (E) relationship between V/(V + Ni) and TOC; (F) relationship between Sr/Ba and TOC.

A more pronounced positive correlation is observed between paleowater depth and paleoredox proxies (Figure C). This indicates that increasing lake depth leads to a transition toward more hypoxic conditions. The underlying mechanism is that, as water deepens, the surface layerheated by solar radiationexperiences a reduction in density. This creates a sharp density contrast with the colder, deeper waters, resulting in the formation of a stable thermocline that inhibits vertical mixing. At the same time, since oxygen is primarily delivered to deeper water layers through surface diffusion and wind-driven mixing, increased water depth disrupts this oxygen supply pathway, leading to oxygen depletion in the bottom waters. , Moreover, deeper water bodies establish low-energy depositional conditions that reduce the resuspension and oxidation of organic matter. This enhances the burial efficiency of organic carbon in the sediments. The increased burial efficiency, in turn, intensifies oxygen consumption, thereby reinforcing hypoxia through a positive feedback loop.

An increase in lake salinity can lead to the formation of a halocline, which further enhances water-column stratification and suppresses vertical oxygen diffusion, resulting in the accumulation of hydrogen sulfide in bottom waters. However, the observed negative correlation between paleosalinity and redox proxies in the E1f2 (Figure D) suggests that higher salinity did not lead to a halocline strong enough to inhibit vertical mixing effectively. We hypothesize that in a closed continental lake basin, excessively high salinity may lead to the formation of superdense brines, which could trigger “inverse stratification” or enhance convective mixing, thereby promoting oxygen diffusion into the hypolimnion. For example, in the modern Dead Sea, when salinity exceeds 300‰, seasonal cooling induces vertical mixing of the water-column, increasing dissolved-oxygen concentrations in bottom waters. Moreover, elevated salinity reduces aquatic biodiversity and metabolic activity of algae and plankton, thereby decreasing microbial respiration and oxygen consumption, which ultimately mitigates hypoxia. On the other hand, the proliferation of halophilic organisms may accelerate the degradation of sedimentary organic matter and hinder its preservation.

Figure E shows that TOC increases with the intensity of water-column hypoxia, indicating that anoxic to dysoxic conditions are a key factor controlling organic carbon enrichment in the sediments of the E1f2. The formation and maintenance of a reducing environment in these lacustrine settings are regulated by both paleowater depth and paleosalinity, which are themselves governed by prevailing paleoclimatic conditions. As lake depth increases, thermal stratification develops due to density differences between surface waters heated by solar radiation and the cooler bottom waters. This stable stratification hinders vertical oxygen mixing, leading to oxygen depletion in deeper layers. The lack of dissolved oxygen inhibits microbial oxidation of organic matter, thereby enhancing its preservation and burial efficiency in sediments. Simultaneously, elevated water salinity reduces aquatic biodiversity and limits the metabolic activity of algae and plankton, resulting in lower oxygen consumption. This further contributes to reduced hypoxia intensity. The negative correlation between paleosalinity proxies and TOC (Figure F) supports this interpretation, indicating that higher salinity leads to weaker lake stratification, which not only constrains the survival of aquatic organisms but also hinders organic matter preservation.

Paleoproductivity

The positive shift in carbonate carbon isotopes is positively correlated with organic carbon burial, indicating that increasing δ13C values can serve as a reliable indicator of enhanced primary productivity. During periods of high surface water productivity, photosynthetic organisms preferentially fix 12C, enriching 13C in the surface dissolved inorganic carbon pool. As a result, lacustrine authigenic carbonates exhibit relatively elevated δ13Ccarb values. , Copper (Cu), a key trace nutrient element, is also widely used as a proxy for paleoproductivity. Cu tends to form complexes with organic matter or adsorb onto iron and manganese hydroxides, which facilitates its removal from the water-column and incorporation into sediments. , Following the degradation of organic matter or the reductive dissolution of Fe/Mn hydroxides, Cu is released into pore waters, where it is either fixed by pyrite or precipitates as copper sulfide minerals, both of which are retained in sediments. Therefore, variations in the Cu/Ti ratio in sedimentary records can be effectively used to infer changes in paleoproductivity.

During the deposition of unit 1, δ13Ccarb values are relatively light, and Cu/Ti ratios remain low, indicating a reduced level of paleoproductivity under cold and arid climatic conditions. In unit 2, δ13Ccarb exhibits a marked positive excursion accompanied by an increase in Cu/Ti ratios, suggesting a phase of enhanced organic carbon burial as the climate shifted toward arid conditions. As the climate transitions from arid to humid, δ13Ccarb values in shale samples from units 3 and 4 show an initial negative shift followed by a rise toward higher values. The Cu/Ti ratios mirror this trend (Figure ), implying an initial decline and subsequent recovery in paleoproductivity. These variations in productivity proxies, consistent with climatic fluctuations, highlight the strong climatic influence on biological productivity levels (Figure A). Increased humidity promoted the delivery of nutrients and terrigenous organic matter, thereby stimulating the proliferation of larger aquatic plants and algae in surface waters, ultimately enhancing productivity in the lake system.

Paleoproductivity analysis; (A) relationship between the chemical index of alteration (CIA) and δ13Ccarb; (B) correlation between δ13Ccarb and total organic carbon (TOC).

Based on the fitted relationship between productivity indicators δ13Ccarb and TOC values (Figure B), it is inferred that paleoproductivity exerted a direct influence on organic matter enrichment in the shales of the E1f2. The positive correlation between δ13Ccarb and CIA values further indicates that productivity was regulated by climatic fluctuations. Under extremely arid conditions, the scarcity of terrestrial vegetation and limited surface runoff restricted the supply of both terrigenous organic matter and nutrients to the lake. Additionally, elevated salinity resulting from intense evaporation was unfavorable for the proliferation of aquatic organisms, thereby leading to generally low productivity levels. As arid conditions transitioned to slightly more humid phases, increased precipitation stimulated vegetation growth and reproduction, augmenting the flux of terrigenous organic matter to the lake and delivering nutrients that fueled the proliferation of autochthonous biota. The co-occurrence of abundant higher-plant debris and algal fossils in unit 2 samples provides direct evidence for this interpretation and supports enhanced productivity during this interval (Figure l,s). With the onset of warmer and more humid climatic conditions, enhanced nutrient availability stimulated widespread phytoplankton blooms. In the surface layer of the water-column, primary producers generated large quantities of organic matter through photosynthesis or chemosynthesis, resulting in a high organic matter flux and promoting further organic carbon accumulation in sediments.

Reservoir Quality under Climatic Influence

The depositional environment influences reservoir development by controlling the original mineral composition, organic matter type and abundance, as well as the stacking architecture of lithofacies. These factors collectively govern the evolution of organic and inorganic pore systems during hydrocarbon generation and diagenesis, thereby determining the reservoir space. Terrestrial shales generally exhibit low thermal maturity of organic matter, with matrix porosity contributing the dominant portion of the reservoir space. In the E1f2, mineral matrix pores are primarily intercrystalline pores within clay minerals. These pores commonly develop along the interfaces between brittle minerals such as quartz, carbonates, and pyrite, and are also found between clay mineral particles, including illite, kaolinite, and chlorite. In general, shales with higher brittle mineral content exhibit greater resistance to compaction compared to clay-rich shales, making them more likely to preserve primary interparticle pores. In this study, we discuss the impact of climate-driven changes in the depositional environment on the mineralogical composition of the E1f2 shale, as well as the subsequent influence of mineral composition on shale porosity and pore structure. This analysis elucidates the controlling role of “storage” conditions in shale oil enrichment, highlighting the importance of mineralogical and diagenetic controls on pore system development and hydrocarbon accommodation capacity.

Paleoclimate and Mineralogical Composition

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals that the shales of the E1f2 are primarily composed of quartz, feldspar, carbonate minerals, and clay minerals. This mineral assemblage reflects inputs from both terrigenous detrital sources and authigenic processes. Zirconium (Zr), a stable trace element in silicate sediments and positively correlated with SiO2, serves as a key indicator of terrigenous siliceous input. , The positive correlation between Zr and SiO2 in the shale samples from the study area (Figure A) indicates that siliceous material is dominantly detrital (quartz and feldspar), rather than authigenic silica. Correlation between Al contenta proxy for terrigenous influxand feldspar and quartz abundances supports this interpretation (Figure B). Under extremely arid to arid climatic conditions, increased terrigenous influx coincides with a marked rise in feldspar and quartz content, a relationship especially pronounced during extreme aridity. By contrast, an opposite trend is observed under humid conditions. This suggests that drier climates favored higher proportions of feldspar and quartz in terrigenous detritus, likely due to sparse vegetation in the catchment area, which was insufficient to inhibit the transport of coarser silicate particles into the lake. Consequently, feldspar and quartz contributions to the sedimentary system increased under such conditions.

As shown in Figure C, under extremely arid paleoclimatic conditions, increased terrigenous fluvial influx does not scale proportionally with clay-mineral abundance. This observation confirms that under such climates, the terrigenous influx is dominated by feldspar and quartz rather than clay minerals. As the climate transitions from arid to more humid conditions, the correlation between terrigenous-input proxies (e.g., Zr, Al) and clay-mineral content strengthens. This suggests that although feldspar and quartz are predominant in the detrital material under arid conditions, clay minerals are also concurrently transported into the lake. With the shift toward a warmer and more humid climate, the proportion of clay minerals in the terrigenous influx gradually increases and eventually becomes dominant.

The formation of lacustrine carbonates requires supersaturation with respect to carbonate minerals in the water- column. This process is primarily regulated by both biological and physicochemical factors. From a biological perspective, microbial activity can produce extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), which serve as effective binding agents for cations. These polymers are capable of adsorbing Ca2+, Mg2+, and other divalent cations from the water- column, thereby creating favorable nucleation sites for carbonate precipitation. The influence of physicochemical conditions on carbonate formation is mainly governed by variations in lake water temperature and salinity under different climatic regimes. − The weak correlation between terrigenous input proxies and carbonate mineral content (Figure D)and the clear negative correlation observed under extremely arid to arid conditionssuggests that carbonate minerals in the E1f2 are predominantly of authigenic origin. Freshwater influx and detrital input from fluvial systems likely diluted ion concentrations (e.g., Ca2+ and CO3 2–) in the water-column, thereby suppressing carbonate supersaturation and inhibiting precipitation. These findings further support the interpretation that carbonate accumulation in the study area was primarily driven by in-lake physicochemical and microbial processes rather than by direct allochthonous input. Under extremely arid to arid climatic conditions, elevated salinity significantly promoted carbonate mineral enrichment (Figure E), likely through enhanced precipitation resulting from supersaturation. In contrast, during humid phases, the correlation between salinity proxies (e.g., S/TOC) and carbonate content is relatively weak, suggesting that salinity was not the sole controlling factor. Instead, elevated lake water temperatures may have reduced the solubility of carbonate species, while enhanced aquatic productivity likely promoted the formation of biogenic carbonates. Together, these mechanisms contributed to carbonate enrichment through more complex, biologically mediated processes.

Paleoclimate exerted a strong control on the mineralogical composition of the E1f2 (Figure ). Under extremely arid climatic conditions, elevated salinity and reduced water depth promoted the precipitation and accumulation of carbonate minerals. Simultaneously, intermittent runoff events transported increased quantities of terrigenous detritusparticularly quartz and feldsparinto the lacustrine system. The resulting mineral assemblage exhibits a distinctive proportion, with the relative ratio of clay minerals to quartz and feldspar to carbonates approximately 3:5:2, reflecting a dominance of coarser siliciclastic input and authigenic carbonates over finer clay deposition. As the climate transitioned to arid conditions, enhanced terrigenous input led to a concurrent increase in feldspar, quartz, and clay mineral contents. Meanwhile, deepening of the paleolake significantly reduced water salinity, thereby suppressing carbonate precipitation. Under these conditions, the mineral ratio shifted to approximately clay: quartz and feldspar: carbonate = 4:5:1, indicating increased detrital influx accompanied by diminished carbonate accumulation. In humid climatic conditions, terrigenous input was dominated by fine-grained clay minerals. Although carbonate precipitation may still have occurred due to reduced solubility at higher water temperatures, the overall low salinity of the lake inhibited substantial carbonate accumulation. As a result, the typical mineral proportion under humid conditions is approximately clay: quartz and feldspar: carbonate = 5:4:1, reflecting the predominance of clay minerals and consistently low carbonate content in a low-salinity depositional environment.

Mineral Framework and Pore Development

Continental lacustrine systems exhibit heightened sensitivity to paleoenvironmental fluctuations, leading to pronounced variability in mineral assemblages, structural attributes, and textural configurations under different depositional settings. , These differences significantly influence the formation and preservation of both primary and secondary pore systems within shale reservoirs. Variations in mineralogical fabricsuch as grain packing, cementation style, and clay platelet alignmentnot only dictate the mechanical behavior of rocks during compaction but also govern the development of microscale to nanoscale porosity, which is critical for hydrocarbon storage and migration.

Figure A indicates that organic matter–hosted pores contribute minimally to the overall porosity in the E1f2. Instead, the pore system is predominantly composed of matrix-related pores, such as interparticle and intraparticle pores. This interpretation is supported by SEM observations, which reveal limited nanopore development within organic matter and a stronger presence of pores associated with the mineral matrix. Among these, intercrystalline pores within clay minerals represent the predominant matrix porosity type, the development and preservation of which are primarily governed by the rigid mineral framework composed of feldspar, quartz, calcite, and dolomite. Differences in mineralogical compositionresulting from paleoclimatic variabilitylargely determined the formation of this framework. Correlation analysis between the rigid mineral to clay mineral ratio and porosity reveals that when this ratio is less than 1.75, porosity increases with rising rigid mineral content (Figure B). Furthermore, samples deposited under extremely arid and arid climatic conditions generally exhibit higher porosity than those formed in humid environments. Integrated SEM analyses (Figure ) show that in humid climatic settings, the relatively low content of rigid minerals hindered effective grain-to-grain contact, causing these minerals to appear dispersed or ″floating″ within the clay matrix. This dispersed framework limited the formation of a supportive rigid skeleton, resulting in extensive compaction and collapse of intercrystalline clay pores during burial diagenesis. In contrast, SEM images from arid-climate samples display point-to-line contacts among rigid grains, with clay minerals infilling intergranular spaces. This arrangement facilitated the development of a rigid framework capable of resisting compaction, thereby preserving the intercrystalline pores within clay minerals. When the rigid mineral to clay mineral ratio exceeds 1.75, further increases in rigid mineral content do not enhance porosity; instead, porosity shows a declining trend (Figure B). Petrographic observations reveal that under these conditions, the rigid frameworks are often occluded by calcite cement (Figure ). This suggests that rigid frameworks formed under extremely arid conditions lack sufficient clay mineral infilling during deposition, making primary pores more vulnerable to occlusion by carbonate cements derived from evaporative concentration in saline lake waters or from postdepositional dissolution and reprecipitation.

Intercrystalline clay-pore development under different climatic conditions.

Correlation analysis between carbonate mineral content and porosity supports this interpretation (Figure C). When carbonate content is below 20%, porosity increases with rising carbonate concentration, as these minerals primarily contribute to the formation of a rigid mineral framework. Under such conditions, hypersaline pore waters are unable to precipitate cements within primary pores already occupied by clay minerals. Conversely, when carbonate content exceeds 20%, porosity exhibits a decreasing trend with increasing carbonate abundance. Samples with high carbonate contentformed under extremely arid climatic conditionslack sufficient clay mineral infill between rigid grains, allowing hypersaline pore waters to precipitate calcite cement. This cementation progressively occludes primary pores, reducing overall reservoir quality.

In summary, intercrystalline pores within clay minerals represent the most dominant type of reservoir space in the E1f2. The development and pore structure of these pores are significantly influenced by the abundance of rigid minerals. Shales formed under humid climatic conditions generally contain a low proportion of rigid mineral components, which hampers the establishment of a rigid grain framework and consequently restricts the development of intercrystalline porosity within clay minerals. Under arid climatic conditions, an optimal ratio of rigid to clay minerals facilitates the development of intercrystalline pores, resulting in a pore structure more conducive to shale oil enrichment. However, in extremely arid climates, the further increase in carbonate mineral content enhances the overall rigidity of the rock matrix. At the same time, carbonate minerals begin to act predominantly as cementing agents between rigid grains, occluding primary pores and thereby reducing the overall reservoir quality of the shale.

Shale Oil Enrichment Patterns Controlled by Paleoclimatic Conditions

Source rock quality and reservoir properties are the primary factors controlling the resource potential of shale oil. As shown in Figure , TOC, porosity, and S 1 exhibit strong positive correlations. This indicates that higher levels of organic matter enrichment are more favorable for shale oil reservoir development, while well-developed pore networks enhance hydrocarbon accumulation and migration.

Climate change influences the intensity of rock weathering and surface runoff, thereby regulating the influx of terrigenous nutrients and levels of biological productivity. These factors collectively control lake productivity, which in turn directly affects organic-matter enrichment. Stratification of the water-column at varying depths provides favorable conditions for both the accumulation and preservation of organic matter. Additionally, moderate sedimentation rates optimize preservation, whereas very low rates prolong exposure and oxidation and very high rates dilute organic matter. Under climatic control, fluctuations in terrigenous input and water salinity alter mineralogical composition, which governs the development and preservation of pore structures. Based on these insights, a shale oil enrichment model under climatic influence has been established (Figure ).

Paleoclimate-controlled model of shale oil enrichment; (A) extremely arid climate; (B) arid climate; (C) humid climate.

During the deposition of the E1f2, three distinct climatic stages were identified: extremely arid, arid, and humid. In the extremely arid stage (Figure A), reduced precipitation and intensified evaporation led to significant contraction of the lake basin and elevated water salinity. The scarcity of terrigenous nutrient input, combined with hypersaline conditions, suppressed aquatic biological productivity within the lacustrine system. As a result, paleoproductivity declined markedly, limiting the delivery of organic matter to the sediments and inhibiting the formation of organic-rich shales. Episodic increases in surface runoff introduced pulses of terrigenous siliciclastic material, resulting in elevated sedimentation rates. This rapid sediment accumulation exerted a strong dilution effect on organic matter, further decreasing the degree of enrichment. In parallel, progressive shallowing of the paleolake weakened water-column stratification, which in turn diminished bottom-water anoxia and further hindered organic matter preservation. The lack of vegetation in the catchment area allowed coarser-grained particles to be transported directly into the lake, leading to a terrigenous siliciclastic assemblage dominated by quartz and feldspar, with a relative deficiency in clay minerals. Elevated salinity in lake waters promoted extensive carbonate precipitation, yielding a higher proportion of rigid minerals under extremely arid conditions. While the accumulation of rigid grains favored the development of a rigid mineral framework, the lack of clay mineral infill within intergranular pores rendered these spaces more susceptible to cementation from saline pore waters, thereby inhibiting the formation of effective storage space for shale oil. Due to the relatively low organic matter enrichment, stratigraphic units 1 and 3deposited under extremely arid climatic conditionsexhibit limited hydrocarbon generation potential. Moreover, a substantial proportion of primary pores was destroyed during the depositional stage, ultimately leading to reduced reservoir quality and unfavorable conditions for shale oil accumulation.

During the arid climatic stage (Figure B), atmospheric precipitation increased while lake evaporation diminished, resulting in progressively more stable surface runoff that delivered substantial amounts of terrigenous organic matter and nutrients. This influx stimulated aquatic productivity and enhanced overall lake bioproductivity. Moderate sedimentation rates allowed for the steady accumulation and preservation of organic matter. Concurrently, rising lake levels promoted stratification of the water-column and the development of low-energy depositional conditions in deeper waters, which inhibited organic matter resuspension and oxidation, thereby improving burial efficiency. The expansion of terrestrial vegetation facilitated a balanced input of quartz, feldspar, and clay minerals into the lacustrine system, promoting the formation of a mineral framework in which clay minerals effectively filled intergranular spaces between rigid grains. Under these conditions, the relatively low salinity of lake water inhibited carbonate cementation in pore spaces already occupied by clay minerals, thereby preserving intercrystalline clay pores developed during diagenesis. The combination of high organic matter enrichment and a well-developed pore network endowed the shale in Stratigraphic unit 2with its arid climate–controlled depositional regimewith strong hydrocarbon generation potential and favorable reservoir quality, resulting in the most prolific shale oil accumulation observed in the Funing Formation.

Under humid climatic conditions (Figure C), the favorable warm temperatures further accelerated reproduction of aquatic organisms, significantly enhancing primary productivity in the paleolake, providing a strong basis for accumulating abundant organic matter. Increased precipitation expanded the areal extent of the lake and deepened the water-column, promoting stable water-column stratification. The development of a stagnant, anoxic bottom-water layer created highly favorable conditions for organic matter preservation and continued enrichment. Extensive terrestrial vegetation cover during this stage impeded the transport of coarse-grained quartz and feldspar particles into the lacustrine system, resulting in a mudstone composition with a higher proportion of clay minerals. However, the reduced presence of rigid grains limited the formation of a supportive mineral framework, rendering intercrystalline pores within the clay matrix more susceptible to compaction-induced collapse, thereby significantly constraining pore development. Although Stratigraphic unit 4formed under humid climatic conditionsexhibits the highest level of organic matter enrichment, the lack of sufficient reservoir space limits its shale oil accumulation potential.

Conclusions

Based on organic geochemistry, sedimentary petrology, and elemental geochemistry, this study reconstructed the paleoclimatic evolution of the E1f2 during the Paleogene and identified hydrological conditions linked to regional climatic variability. The controlling mechanisms of paleoclimatic evolution on organic matter enrichment and pore structure development were clarified. Ultimately, we propose a climate-driven shale oil enrichment model.(1)During the deposition of the E1f2, three distinct paleoclimatic stages were identified: extremely arid (corresponding to Stratigraphic units 1 and 3), arid (unit 2), and humid (unit 4). Paleoclimate exerted a first-order control on organic matter enrichment by influencing lake sedimentation rates, paleoproductivity levels, and the degree of bottom-water anoxia. During the humid and arid stages, moderate sedimentation rates, elevated biological productivity, and greater paleowater depth enhanced water-column stratification, resulting in higher organic matter accumulation and greater hydrocarbon-generation potential.(2)Under climatic influence, variations in the intensity of terrigenous siliciclastic input and lake water salinity controlled the mineralogical composition of the shales of the E1f2, thereby regulating the development and preservation of pore structures. During the arid climatic stage, enhanced terrigenous input and moderate salinity conditions favored the formation of an optimal ratio between rigid minerals and clay minerals. This mineralogical balance promoted the development of intercrystalline pores within the clay matrix, providing favorable conditions for the formation of effective shale oil storage space.(3)Paleoclimate exerts a significant control on shale oil enrichment by influencing both organic matter accumulation and pore system development. During arid climatic periods, unit 2 strata contain abundant organic matter and well-developed intercrystalline pores within clay minerals, resulting in relatively high overall shale porosity. This configuration favors a more effective source–reservoir coupling system, thereby promoting shale oil enrichment. For future exploration in the deep to semideep lacustrine facies of the Qintong Depression, priority should be given to shale intervals deposited under arid climatic conditions.

(1)During the deposition of the E1f2, three distinct paleoclimatic stages were identified: extremely arid (corresponding to Stratigraphic units 1 and 3), arid (unit 2), and humid (unit 4). Paleoclimate exerted a first-order control on organic matter enrichment by influencing lake sedimentation rates, paleoproductivity levels, and the degree of bottom-water anoxia. During the humid and arid stages, moderate sedimentation rates, elevated biological productivity, and greater paleowater depth enhanced water-column stratification, resulting in higher organic matter accumulation and greater hydrocarbon-generation potential.

(2)Under climatic influence, variations in the intensity of terrigenous siliciclastic input and lake water salinity controlled the mineralogical composition of the shales of the E1f2, thereby regulating the development and preservation of pore structures. During the arid climatic stage, enhanced terrigenous input and moderate salinity conditions favored the formation of an optimal ratio between rigid minerals and clay minerals. This mineralogical balance promoted the development of intercrystalline pores within the clay matrix, providing favorable conditions for the formation of effective shale oil storage space.
