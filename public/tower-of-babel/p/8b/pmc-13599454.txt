Presynaptic Congenital Myasthenic Syndromes
Authors: Ricardo A. Maselli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPresynaptic congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) encompass a large number of rare neurologic disorders caused by impaired release of acetylcholine (ACh) from motor nerve terminals. There are two main groups of presynaptic CMS: one in which the amount of ACh in synaptic vesicles (SV) is diminished and another in which the mechanism of synaptic vesicle release is impaired. The latter is often referred to as a congenital Lambert‐Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS). Presynaptic CMS, like other forms of CMS, is primarily characterized by muscle weakness and fatigue, but is often associated with additional manifestations of central and autonomic nervous system involvement, including episodic apneas, cognitive delay, seizures, and gastrointestinal complications. The most common causes of presynaptic CMS are recessive and dominant mutations in genes encoding proteins participating in the mechanisms of ACh synthesis and SV release. However, at times gene mutations can result in more complex pathogenic mechanisms. The most effective treatment of these conditions is prevention of potentially fatal episodes of apnea and other respiratory complications resulting from bulbar weakness. Pharmacologic treatments are also useful; however, in many cases the response is incomplete, and in other mild cases there is improvement with age that obviates the need for pharmacologic interventions. Preclinical studies in animal models of presynaptic CMS using targeted gene therapies are promising but these new therapies will not be available in the near future.

Introduction

Although presynaptic congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) account for only about 5%–10% of all CMS types [1], one of the earliest clinical variants identified in the 20th century was a presynaptic CMS. In 1975, Conomy et al. described two siblings who exhibited episodic muscle weakness with relatively mild symptoms between episodes and no significant ocular involvement [2]. They termed the condition “Familial Infantile Myasthenia” and noted its frequent association with sudden and potentially fatal episodes of apnea. Subsequent in vitro studies performed in muscle biopsies of patients with this condition revealed normal amplitudes of synaptic potentials at rest but reduced amplitudes after sustained nerve stimulation at 10 Hz, which pointed to a defect of the resynthesis of ACh [3]. This provided a solid clue about the possible genetic cause of the syndrome, which led to the discovery of the gene encoding the enzyme choline acetyl transferase (ChAT) as the causative genetic defect of this CMS type [4]. The condition was renamed CMS with episodic apnea (CMS‐EA); however, since it was later recognized that more than one gene can be associated with this phenotype, it is currently preferable to include the name of the suspected or confirmed gene linkage.

The use of whole exome sequencing in the 2010s led to the discovery of additional ultrarare presynaptic CMS in which the defect not only involved the synthesis or repacking of synaptic vesicles (SVs) with ACh but also the mechanism of release of SVs from the nerve terminal. Most of the genetic defects in this group involve genes encoding proteins that participate in the docking and fusion of SVs to the presynaptic membrane and are often collectively referred to as congenital Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) [5, 6]. In several other variants of CMS with suspected presynaptic failure, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms are complex and not completely elucidated. Animal models have played a pivotal role in the understanding of presynaptic CMS and the development of new therapies for these rare disorders [7].

Recently, Lin et al. described the outcome of an adeno‐associated virus (AAV) gene therapy in a model of CMS‐EA [8]. This provides hope for the treatment of patients severely affected by presynaptic variants of CMS.

Classification of Presynaptic Congenital Myasthenic Syndromes

The quantal theory of synaptic transmission developed by Sir Bernard Katz [9] and solidified by ultrastructural studies of synapses performed by Eduardo De Robertis and others [10] provided the basis of a simple classification of presynaptic CMS. The theory explains that the neurotransmitter ACh is released in discrete packets called quanta (q), each corresponding to the content of ACh in a single SV, which in mammals is around 10,000 molecules [11, 12]. The quantal content of the endplate potential (EPP) referred to by the letter m corresponds to the number of discrete packets of ACh contributing to the EPP, which in humans is around 20–25 [13, 14]. Thus, assuming normal postsynaptic function, the amplitude of the EPP depends on m times q as shown below: (1) EPP=mq The most frequent types of presynaptic CMS are those resulting from a decrease of q due to impaired synthesis and packing of SVs with ACh. The second and less frequent variant of presynaptic CMS is characterized by a decreased m due to impaired mobilization, docking, and fusion of SVs to the presynaptic membrane. In these second types, the probability (p) of quantal release is diminished while the quanta available for immediate release (n) is normal or also decreased: (2) m=np By contrast, in several other variants of CMS with a component of presynaptic failure, m is reduced exclusively due to a reduction of n and that is usually associated with small size of nerve terminals, as in CMS due to mutations in COLQ, LAMB2, MUSK, and DOK7 [15, 16, 17, 18]. Since the primary pathogenic mechanism in these conditions is not presynaptic, they will not be discussed here.

Impaired Synthesis of ACh and Packing of SVs (↓q)

Impaired SV Release (↓m)

This is a group of ultrarare CMS that share a common denominator a reduction of the probability of SV release from the nerve terminal, reminiscent of the synaptic defect encountered in LEMS. Therefore, these conditions are often referred to as congenital LEMS. Most of the causative genetic defects involve genes encoding soluble N‐ethylmaleimide‐sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) proteins or proteins tightly associated with SNAREs. However, in some cases, the defective proteins do not have an apparent association with SNARE proteins. It should be noted that in most cases, the neuromuscular transmission defect is only one small component within a broader multisystem disorder.

A useful model of synaptotagmin 2 deficiency is based on the replacement of a DNA segment spanning from part of exon 2 to exon 7 by the LacZ Neo cassette [51]. Mice homozygous for this engineered mutation showed marked impairment of evoked release of SVs and reduced life span. They developed severe weakness associated with tremors and prominent ataxia.

Munc13‐CMS

In rodents, Unc13a is a presynaptic protein with an essential role in SV priming. Only one case of CMS linked to this gene has been reported in the literature [52]. The reported patient, who had a homozygous N‐terminal stop‐codon mutation in Munc13‐1, presented with severe weakness, hypotonia, microcephalia, and electroencephalographic abnormalities [52]. The electrodiagnostic assessment showed clear presynaptic CMS, including very low CMAP amplitude at rest that increased 400% with stimulation at 50 Hz. Microelectrode recordings showed a striking reduction of miniature endplate potential (MEPP) frequency and EPP quantal content (m), confirming severe presynaptic failure. The patient showed negligible clinical improvement with treatment with pyridostigmine and 3,4‐diaminopyridine and eventually developed respiratory failure.

LAMA5‐CMS

Laminins are the most abundant non‐collagenous proteins of the basal lamina. They are large cross‐shaped heterotrimeric glycoproteins composed of one of five α subunits, one of three β subunits, and one of three γ subunits [53]. In skeletal muscle, the subunits α5 and β2 are the only two subunits that are expressed exclusively at the primary and secondary folds of the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) [54]. Mutations in the genes encoding these proteins can lead to CMS, though this is rare and typically occurs alongside the involvement of other organs.

One patient whose exome sequencing was only relevant for a homozygous missense mutation in the coil‐coiled domain of laminin α5 presented with hypotonia and generalized weakness at birth [55, 56]. Repetitive nerve stimulation at 2 Hz showed a 55% decrement of CMAP amplitudes, that facilitated a 250% increase with RNS at 50 Hz. Microelectrode studies of a muscle biopsy of the patient showed that the average quantal content (m) was less than 5% of the quantal content of age‐matched controls, thus confirming severe presynaptic failure. The patient partially responded to dual therapy with pyridostigmine and 3,4‐diaminopyridine. The underlying pathogenic mechanism of failure of neuromuscular transmission in this patient is unclear, but it may involve a distorted interaction of synaptic laminins with the presynaptic voltage‐gated calcium channel and/or vesicular proteins such as SV2 that participate in the modulation of synaptic transmission.

AGRN Nerve Terminal ( NtA )‐ CMS

Neural‐derived agrin encoded by AGRN is a large protein that is essential for the differentiation and maintenance of the NMJ [57]. The interaction between the C‐terminal LG3 domain of agrin and the low‐density lipoprotein receptor‐related protein 4 (LRP4) is crucial for the clustering of acetylcholine receptors at the motor endplate [58]. On the other hand, the binding of the N‐terminal agrin (NtA) to the coiled‐coil domain of laminin is required for its localization to synaptic basal lamina [59].

While failure of neuromuscular transmission in AGRN‐CMS results from a combined pre and postsynaptic deficit, some patients with this condition appear to have predominant presynaptic failure. Nicole and co‐workers reported that in patients from two independent AGRN‐CMS pedigrees presenting with primary distal involvement, there was up to a 285% post‐exercise increment of CMAP amplitudes in distal limb muscles consistent with predominant presynaptic failure [60]. These patients were compound heterozygotes with a truncating mutation in one allele and an NtA mutation in the other allele that determined the phenotype. The mechanism of failure of neuromuscular transmission in AGRN (NtA)‐CMS is uncertain, but given the close association of the NtA with laminins, the mechanism may be similar to that described above for LAMA5‐CMS.

Combined Mechanisms

In this group reported as presynaptic CMS, the mechanism of failure of neuromuscular transmission is not as well defined as in the previous groups, and it may involve more than one mechanism.

CMS Associated With Genetic Defects of Mitochondrial Metabolism ( SLC25A1 , TEFM, PREPL)

Considering the critical role of mitochondria in supporting neurite outgrowth, maintaining Ca2+ homeostasis, and generating acetyl‐CoA for acetylcholine synthesis, it is somewhat surprising that mitochondrial disorders do not more often show impairment of neuromuscular transmission. Nevertheless, in recent years abnormal neuromuscular transmission has been reported in patients with pathogenic mutations in various genes associated with mitochondrial metabolism [61]. Although most of these patients had multisystem disorders, they shared many clinical similarities with patients with CMS, including hypotonia, weakness, ptosis, and involvement of external ocular muscles. The number of endplate studies conducted in muscle biopsies from these patients is very limited, making it difficult to draw any firm conclusions about the underlying pathogenic mechanisms. To date congenital LEMS linked to genes related to mitochondrial function have never been reported; thus, altered Ca2+ homeostasis in the nerve terminal does not seem to play a major role in the mechanism of failure of neuromuscular transmission in mitochondrial disorders. An alternative and a more plausible mechanism is failure of the mitochondria to maintain a steady supply of the substrate acetyl‐CoA for the synthesis of ACh by the ChAT enzyme. The normal MEPP amplitudes and normal postsynaptic AChR density found in a patient with SLC25A1 are similar to findings in muscle biopsies of patients with CHAT mutations in which failure of neuromuscular transmission is due to a reduction of the quantal size q [3, 19, 62]. Similar conclusions can be drawn from the muscle biopsy of another patient with biallelic null mutations in the PREPL gene, which encodes a protein that is intimately related to mitochondrial metabolism. In the biopsy of this patient the reduction of MEPP amplitudes with normal postsynaptic AChR density also pointed to a reduction of the quantal size q suggesting an underlying deficiency of ACh synthesis [63]. Recognition of this subgroup of patients with mitochondrial diseases and failure of neuromuscular transmission is important as many patients in this subgroup may benefit from treatments with medications that improve neuromuscular transmission such as pyridostigmine and albuterol.

CMS Associated With Organelle Homeostasis and the G‐Protein Signaling Pathway ( PREPL , MYO9A , RPH3A )

This is a complex and heterogeneous group of disorders in which failure of neuromuscular transmission occurs in the context of multisystemic disease and results from mutations affecting proteins that often have more than one function. For instance, the thioester catalytic activity of PREPL is critical for mitochondrial function and homeostasis; however, recent observations suggest that PREPL has also important noncatalytic functions. Catalytic‐independent functions are primarily mediated through protein–protein interactions, particularly between PREPL and the μ‐1A subunit of AP1 that regulates AP1‐mediated transport in the trans‐Golgi network (TGN). Dysfunction of the TGN may impact the homeostasis of organelles such as endosomes and SVs that participate in synaptic transmission and may be relevant to the pathogenesis of PREPL missense mutations that do not affect the catalytic function of the enzyme [64].

Another protein linked to CMS that has multiple functions is MYO9A [65]. This protein operates as an unconventional myosin supporting the actin cytoskeleton and various functions of eukaryotic cells, including neurite outgrowth and displacement of organelles inside the nerve terminal [66]. The other important function of MYO9A is Rho‐ATPase activity, which is mediated through its C‐terminal domain and linked to signal transduction processes involving small G‐proteins [67].

Interaction with the small G‐protein Rab3A is one of the primary functions of rabphilin‐3A, a protein that has also been reported in association with presynaptic CMS [68]. Rab proteins are a family of small GTPases crucial for the process of synaptic membrane homeostasis and SV exocytosis at nerve terminals [69]. This G protein‐mediated process also involves the trafficking of membranes between the TGN, early endosomes, SVs, lysosomes, and lamellar bodies (Figure 3) [70]. Moreover, the involvement of G‐proteins in the pathogenesis of CMS establishes a linkage of CMS with another group of common genetic disorders of the G‐protein superfamily, the RASopathies. Many overlapping features have been observed in the phenotypes of CMS and some RASopathies [72, 73]. Finally, it should be noted that rabphilin‐3A shares many similarities with synaptotagmin‐2, including two tandem Ca2+ binding C2 domains, phospholipid binding, and direct interaction with the SNARE protein SNAP25 [74]. However, rabphilin‐3A is dispensable for regulated neurotransmitter release [75], thus failure of neuromuscular transmission associated with RPH3A is unlikely to involve a loss‐of‐function mechanism.

Concluding Remarks

Presynaptic CMS is a new and rapidly growing medical field that dramatically expanded after the advent of next generation sequencing (NGS) about 15 years ago. As an example, 13 of 14 genes reported in association with presynaptic CMS have been discovered using NGS. The experience accumulated thus far indicates that presynaptic CMS are rare, usually multisystemic diseases, which show a great deal of variability in terms of clinical presentation and severity. In some cases, presynaptic CMS are very severe and are associated with symptoms of central and autonomic nervous system involvement. Yet, in other cases, the disease is mild and tends to improve spontaneously with age. Presynaptic‐CMS can be classified into two major groups: those caused by impaired synthesis and repacking of ACh in SVs, which lead to a reduction of the quantal size q; and those resulting from diminished nerve‐evoked release of SVs, which result in reduction of the EPP quantal content m. This last group is often referred to as congenital LEMS. Disorders of the first group are more common than those of the second group and can be potentially complicated by episodes of apnea. Pharmacologic treatments with pyridostigmine and/or albuterol are only partially effective in ameliorating symptoms in most patients. In congenital LEMS‐like syndromes, the use of potassium channel blockers such as amifampridine can be very effective in improving muscle weakness and fatigability. Preclinical studies in animal models of presynaptic CMS using gene therapies are promising but these new treatments will not be available in the immediate future.
