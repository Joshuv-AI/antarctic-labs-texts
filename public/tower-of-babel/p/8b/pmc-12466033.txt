Perioperative symptoms in patients with thyroid cancer: a cross-sectional and longitudinal network analysis
Authors: Yuyan Yin, Zilin Fang, Xin Sheng, Jiaqi Li, Juan Xu, Ting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with thyroid cancer experience a range of symptoms during surgical treatment. Identifying their core symptoms and understanding their dynamic changes can help improve the efficiency of clinical symptom management.MethodsA longitudinal observational study design was used, and convenience sampling method was applied to select thyroid cancer patients who attended the Department of Thyroid and Breast Surgery in a tertiary hospital in Suzhou City from October 2024 to April 2025. A general information questionnaire and the Chinese version of the Memory Symptom Assessment Scale were used to assess the patients 1 day before surgery (T1), 1-2 days after surgery (T2) and 2 weeks after surgery (T3). The symptom severity bias correlation network was constructed through R language, and the core symptoms of T1, T2 and T3 were identified.ResultsThis study finally included 248 patients with thyroid cancer. The results showed that the three symptoms with the highest strength centrality in the T1 network were lack of appetite (rs=1.536), worrying (rs=1.442), and feeling nervous (rs=1.439). The three symptoms with the highest strength centrality in the T2 network were numbness/tingling in hands/feet (rs=1.613), lack of appetite (rs=1.433), and nausea (rs=1.294). The three symptoms with the highest strength centrality in the T3 network were difficulty swallowing (rs=1.778), feeling nervous (rs=1.681), and neck discomfort (rs=1.520). Neck discomfort (rs2=1.281, rs3=1.520) was the core symptom that was more stable postoperatively. In the test of network structural invariance, there were significant differences between T1 and T2 (M=0.78, P<0.001), T1 and T3 (M=0.45, P=0.009), and T2 and T3 (M=0.66, P<0.001). In terms of overall network connectivity strength, there were significant differences between T1 (GS=6.86) and T3 (GS=4.12), T2 (GS=7.66) and T3 (P<0.05), while there was no significant difference between T1 and T2 (P=0.65).ConclusionThe study identified the core symptoms present at each stage of the perioperative period in patients with thyroid cancer, suggesting that clinical practitioners can develop efficient, targeted and precise management strategies based on the core symptoms at different times, with a view to improving the perioperative symptom experience of patients and increasing the efficiency of symptom management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-02032-w.Keywords: Thyroid cancer, Perioperative, Symptom management, Network analysis

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is a malignant tumor originating from the follicular epithelial or parafollicular epithelial cells of the thyroid gland. According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2020, there were about 856,000 new cases of thyroid cancer, and it has become one of the most common malignant tumors of the endocrine system [1, 2]. Compared with global data, China has 221,000 new cases of thyroid cancer each year, with an incidence growth rate (7.3% per annum) significantly higher than the global average, and there is also a trend toward younger patients, posing a significant threat to human health [2–4]. Currently, the treatments for thyroid cancer include surgery, molecular targeted therapy, radiation therapy, biological therapy, etc., of which surgery is the main treatment for thyroid cancer patients [5, 6].

With the development of medical technology, surgical treatment has become more mature, and most thyroid cancer patients have better treatment results and higher survival rates after treatment [7]. The perioperative period is the period of time that begins when a patient decides to undergo surgical treatment and ends with substantial postoperative recovery. During this period, patients experience a series of psychological and physiological changes and discomfort due to the disease itself and the trauma of surgery [8, 9]. Thyroid cancer patients not only have high levels of anxiety while waiting for surgery, but also experience more severe anxiety, depression, fatigue, and insomnia than patients with other cancers [10, 11]. Among Chinese thyroid cancer patients, more than 60% experience moderate to severe anxiety symptoms prior to surgery, and nearly 50% continue to report anxiety after surgery [12, 13]. This may be closely related to the decision-making pressure faced by only-child families and the cultural stigma associated with cancer. At the same time, surgical treatment may cause complications such as hypoparathyroidism, paralysis of the recurrent laryngeal nerve, scarring and other symptoms such as numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, hoarseness and skin changes [14], and up to 44.6% of patients with thyroid cancer after surgery suffer from neck discomfort [15]. Although the proportion of early-stage thyroid cancer in China is high, with stage I patients accounting for more than 80%, the rate of preventive lymph node dissection in the central region is as high as 89%, which is higher than in Europe and the United States (30–50%), leading to a significant increase in the incidence of postoperative neck discomfort and difficulty swallowing [16, 17]. Studies have shown [18] that perioperative cancer patients experience symptoms such as anxiety, nervousness, depression, and difficulty in sleeping that affect the quality of their postoperative recovery, and these symptoms lead to prolonged hospitalization and delayed social reintegration, which can have an impact on the patient’s life.

Previous medical research on thyroid cancer has mostly focused on cross-sectional studies of individual symptoms or symptom clusters. For example, Cai et al. [19] explored the symptoms and three symptom clusters present in patients with advanced thyroid cancer, Alhashemi, A et al. [20] explored the quality of life and symptomatic effects of thyroid cancer, and Hughes et al. [21] explored the relationship between treatment and fatigue in patients with thyroid cancer. At different stages of the perioperative period, the patient’s symptoms will vary [22]. And at the same stage in the perioperative period, these symptoms can interact with each other [23]. In the face of such complex symptomatic changes, the single symptom management approach that is often used in current health care is not sufficiently effective [24]. Identifying targets for symptom intervention in thyroid cancer patients at different stages of the perioperative period can help assist healthcare professionals in accurate and efficient symptom management and reduce the symptom burden of patients [25].

Perioperative symptoms have three main characteristics: simultaneity, dynamism, and mutual amplification. Symptom Network Analysis, as an emerging methodology, provides a new perspective for understanding symptoms and their relationships with each other [26]. It treats the various symptoms of patients as a complex network of interconnections, quantifies the strength of the association and feedback mechanisms between symptoms through the network structure, and analyzes the interrelationships between symptoms and their impact on the overall health status [27]. The core of symptom network analysis lies in the construction of a symptom network model, in which each symptom is regarded as a node, and the degree of correlation between the interactions of the symptoms is represented as an edge, with the thicker edge representing the stronger correlation between the symptoms, and the key node being the one with the largest and strongest edges (the core symptom) [28, 29]. By analyzing the structure and characteristics of networks, such as node centrality and network connectivity density, it is possible to deeply explore the degree of association, interaction, and possible causal relationships between symptoms [30, 31]. The centrality indicators identified in this exploration can then serve as targets for intervention studies, becoming key to achieving precision nursing.

Looking back at previous studies on symptom networks, Zhou et al. [32] mentioned that by intervening on the core symptom, not only that symptom was alleviated, but also the other symptoms to varying degrees. In addition, symptom network analysis is also dynamic, tracking changes in symptoms over time and dynamic interactions between symptoms [33]. In Wang et al.’s [34] network evolution of core symptoms in postoperative lung cancer patients, it was found that fatigue was the core symptom of the network for patients in the 1–2 day postoperative network, whereas the core symptoms of the symptom network became shortness of breath, fatigue, and coughing in the 5–6 day postoperative period. This suggests that the centrality of certain symptoms changes over time in network analyses, providing important insights into the creation of staged interventions. Symptom network analysis treats all symptoms as equal nodes, enabling visualization of the correlations between symptoms and identification of core symptoms at each stage. These core symptoms serve as precise intervention targets, assisting medical staff in symptom management and alleviating the negative impact of symptoms on patients.

Currently, the core symptoms of thyroid cancer patients during the perioperative period are not clearly defined. Previous studies have primarily focused on the impact of symptoms on patients’ quality of life, individual symptoms, and cross-sectional studies of symptom clusters, with limited exploration of the dynamic interactions between symptoms. Network analysis can provide theoretical intervention targets for symptom management. Therefore, this study adopts a longitudinal observational research design, utilizing network analysis methods to map the perioperative symptom severity bias correlation network of thyroid cancer patients, to explore the core symptoms at each stage of the perioperative period, and to compare the changes in the network at different stages, in order to provide a basis for the implementation of precise symptom management in the clinic, and the construction of efficient stage-by-stage symptom management measures.

Methods

Study design and objects

This study was a longitudinal observational study design with convenience sampling method, and patients who were seen at a tertiary-level hospital in Suzhou, China, in the Department of A and Breast Surgery and underwent thyroid surgery for the first time from October 2024 to April 2025 were selected. Inclusion criteria:① age ≥ 18 years; ② postoperative pathological diagnosis of thyroid malignant tumor; ③ first thyroid surgical treatment; ④ informed consent and communication ability. Exclusion criteria: ① patients with combination of other malignant tumors; ② patients with combination of other serious diseases; ③ patients with serious mental diseases or cognitive dysfunction. Exclusion criteria: ① those who were unable to implement or refused to complete the relevant measurements and scales after inclusion; ② those who had serious adverse events during the study period; ③ those who requested to withdraw on their own in the course of the study; and ④ those who had incomplete or invalid questionnaire information. This study is a longitudinal observational study that does not involve any intervention, and therefore does not require clinical trial registration. Ethical approval was granted by the hospital ethics committee (internal approval number: 20250327052421081). All procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

This study uses a two-step method to ensure that the sample size meets the requirements of both network analysis and other statistical methods. First, the sample size for the network analysis was calculated as follows [35], sample size = number of entries × (number of entries-1)/2 × 3. The number of entries on the Symptom Assessment Scale in this study was 33, and taking into account a 10% loss-to-follow-up rate, the sample size that should have been included was approximately 195 cases. The second step is to calculate the sample size for Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) [36]. Set the effect size β = 0.20, and under the conditions of 80% statistical power and α = 0.05, use PASS 2023 software to calculate the GEE sample size, considering the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient ICC = 0.30. The results show that ≥ 180 cases can meet the requirements. The study ultimately included 248 study participants, which met the sample size requirements.

Perioperative symptom management studies typically use a three-stage model consisting of the preoperative baseline period, the acute postoperative trauma period, and the early recovery period [25, 34]. The timing of this study was based on the clinical pathway of thyroid cancer surgery symptom progression:①T1 (day before surgery): At this point, the patient is fasting prior to surgery and experiencing peak anxiety. The baseline psychological and physiological state of the patient prior to surgical stress can be captured [37]. ②T2 (1–2 days after the surgery): At this stage, the patient is in the acute phase of surgical trauma, which is the period when complications such as pain, nausea, and hypocalcemia are most prevalent [38]. ③T3 (2 weeks after the surgery): At this stage, the patient is in the early recovery period. The focus is on assessing early recovery issues such as difficulty swallowing and neck discomfort [39]. This follows the national guidelines, which stipulate that functional symptoms (difficulty swallowing, neck discomfort) require a follow-up assessment at the outpatient clinic after 2 weeks [40]. This study chose the current time point in order to prioritize capturing the dynamic critical period of the symptom network.

Patient data were collected by three graduate nursing students who received uniform training and were familiar with the content of the questionnaire. The collection time points were 1 day before the patient’s surgery (T1), 1–2 days after the surgery (T2) and two weeks after the surgery (T3). Data were collected using a combination of online and offline methods, with face-to-face data collection in the T1 and T2 periods and telephone follow-up in the T3 period. Patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study were selected for the survey and the purpose and methodology of the study were explained to the patients and their consent was obtained. After the patients signed the informed consent form using a standardized procedure, one-on-one surveys were conducted and patients’ queries were explained and clarified. Questionnaires were completed and collected on the spot, and the quality of completed questionnaires was checked to verify unclear responses with patients in a timely manner. The information collected was entered in two-person using the software EpiData to create a database. During the T1 collection period, a total of 264 patients with thyroid cancer who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of this study were included. In the longitudinal data collection phase, 16 patients’ data were excluded (3 transferred, 5 postoperative pathological diagnosis of non-thyroidal malignancy, 8 lost to follow-up), and a total of 248 patients completed data collection, with a valid questionnaire recovery rate of 93.9%.

Research tools

General information questionnaire

The general patient information questionnaire was prepared through extensive review of relevant literature and after discussion by a panel of experts. The form was divided into two parts: basic patient information and clinical data content. The basic information of patients included age, sex, place of residence, marital status, occupation, education level, per capita monthly income of the family, and history of chronic diseases, etc.; and the content of clinical data included clinical stage, whether the tumor had metastasized, and surgical methods.

The Chinese version of memorial symptom assessment scale(MSAS-Ch)

The Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS) [41] was developed by Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in the U.S. In this study, we used the Chinese version of the Memory Symptom Assessment Scale (MSAS-Ch), which was Chineseized by Cheng et al. [42]. The MSAS-Ch is a multidimensional scale for assessing patients’ symptoms, which contains 32 physical and psychological symptoms, and is mainly used to assess the symptoms of cancer patients or patients with other chronic diseases, with Cronbach alpha coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 0.87, and with good reliability and validity. In this study, the presence and severity of symptoms were chosen to assess the patients’ symptoms, and the severity of symptoms was assessed from “mild”, “moderate”, “severe” and “extremely severe” were assessed with a score of 1 to 4 (0 if no symptoms), and the higher the score, the more severe the symptoms. Through the literature review and pre-survey, other symptom entries of the survey scale were added, namely “hoarseness”, “neck discomfort”, and because most of the participants refused to answer the symptom entry “loss of interest in sex/difficulty in sex life”, this entry was deleted, and a total of 33 symptoms were finally included. The results of the reliability analysis using the scales showed good internal consistency with Cronbach alpha coefficients of 0.865 for T1, 0.884 for T2, and 0.890 for T3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using the software SPSS 27.0, count data were expressed as frequencies and percentages, measures were expressed as Mean ± SD, and internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach alpha. Univariate analysis of demographic variables was performed to identify factors affecting symptom severity at three time points in the perioperative period in patients with thyroid cancer.

Network analysis

Network analysis was performed using the software R 4.4.3. The network model was visualized using the q graph package within the R software [43] and combined with the EBIC glasso function to construct the symptom severity networks for T1, T2, and T3, respectively. The tuning parameter γ = 0.5 (default value) has been proven in simulation and empirical studies to achieve a good balance between sparsity and model fit [44]. Common centrality indicators include strength centrality, closeness centrality, and betweenness centrality [45]. Among the centrality indicators, the strength centrality of a node is calculated as the sum of the absolute values of the weights of all edges connected to a node, and the higher the strength centrality, it indicates that the symptom is more likely to be a core symptom [46], and the strength centrality is a more stable centrality indicator [47], so strength centrality was selected as the main explanatory indicator in this study. Closeness centrality is the inverse of the sum of the shortest path distances from all other nodes in the network to the node, higher closeness centrality indicates that the symptom is more likely to be in the center of the network, so the closeness centrality metrics are used as a reference. The centrality index of these nodes was expressed as a standardized score, with higher standardized scores representing greater centrality of the node. Meanwhile, in order to reduce the error rate of network analysis [35], therefore, symptoms with symptom prevalence > 20% were included in the analysis, and the factors that significantly affected symptom severity in univariate analyses were included as covariates within the symptom network. EBIC glasso itself does not support direct inclusion of covariates. This study indirectly achieved adjustment through “regression residualization,” which is in line with current best practices in network analysis [48].

Network accuracy and stability are detected by the Bootnet package in R based on the Bootstrap algorithm to estimate the 95% confidence interval (CI) and evaluate the accuracy of the edge weights and centrality metrics, with a narrower CI indicating a more accurate network model [48]. Stability was assessed using a shelled subset of bootstrap with 1000 bootstrap samples, and the centrality stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) was calculated [49]. The CS-coefficient is only used to assess the stability of the centrality estimate and is not an adjustment parameter. CS-coefficient > 0.25 indicates that the stability of the network model is acceptable, and CS-coefficient > 0.50 indicates that the stability of the network model is good, and studies have shown that the correlation stability coefficient should preferably be higher than 0.50 and not lower than 0.25 [35].

Comparison tests between networks are performed using the R-packet Network Comparison Test (NCT), which includes the significant difference in edge strength between networks, the network structure, and the global strength invariance of the network [50]. Significant difference in network edge strength is a comparison of differences in node edges of two networks. Network structure invariance refers to the difference test of the overall connection strength of different networks, the higher the network connection strength, the stronger the connection tightness between nodes. Network global strength invariance refers to the difference test of the node connectivity of different networks, when there is a significant difference in the node connectivity of different networks, it means that there is a difference in the network structure (the difference is considered statistically significant at P < 0.05).

In the network structure invariance test, the NCT results showed significant differences between T1 and T2 (M = 0.78, P < 0.001), T1 and T3 (M = 0.45, P = 0.009), and T2 and T3 (M = 0.66, P < 0.001). In terms of the overall connection strength of the network, the NCT results showed significant differences between T1 (GS = 6.86) and T3 (GS = 4.12), and between T2 (GS = 7.66) and T3 (P < 0.05), whereas there was no significant difference between T1 and T2 (P = 0.65).

Discussion

This study used network analysis to longitudinally describe the dynamic evolution characteristics of the perioperative symptom network of thyroid cancer patients and identified the core symptoms at different stages. According to the results of the study, the core symptom of T1 was lack of appetite, the core symptom of T2 was numbness/tingling in the hands/feet, and the core symptom of T3 was difficulty swallowing. It is worth noting that neck discomfort showed high strength centrality at both postoperative time points and was a relatively stable core symptom after surgery.

Lack of appetite is the core symptom of T1. Preoperative lack of appetite may stem from cancer-induced dysregulation (e.g., ACBP) and psychological stress, which alters gastrointestinal hormones (e.g., Ghrelin) [51, 52]. These factors collectively suppress appetite. And gastrointestinal hormone secretion is regulated by these factors, which will further affect the patient’s gastrointestinal function and aggravate the patient’s loss of appetite [53]. Targeted preoperative education can be used to explain the surgical process, expected results, and postoperative recovery path, and relaxation training (such as deep breathing and progressive muscle relaxation) can be introduced to alleviate patients’ preoperative tension and anxiety [54]. Poor sleep is often accompanied by anxiety and loss of appetite, so it is important to assess and intervene in sleep disorders, optimize the ward environment, establish a regular routine, and provide non-pharmacological or pharmacological sleep interventions when necessary to alleviate the patient’s loss of appetite [55, 56]. Activities to improve gut health, such as walking, can also be chosen, as well as nutritional interventions and smaller, more frequent meals [57].

Prior to surgery, patients primarily experienced a loss of appetite due to anxiety and metabolic disorders. However, in the early postoperative period, they faced new challenges, with Numbness/tingling in the hands/feet becoming the core symptoms of stage T2. During this period, most of the patient’s discomfort is directly caused by the surgical stimulus. Postoperative numbness/tingling in extremities may arise from surgical impact on parathyroid function, causing transient hypoparathyroidism. This often induces hypocalcemia, which directly triggers these symptoms [58, 59]. It is recommended that clinical preventive monitoring and early identification be conducted: routinely monitor blood calcium levels postoperatively and closely observe patients for early signs of hypocalcemia, such as numbness around the mouth and tingling in the fingertips. Develop a calcium supplementation plan based on blood calcium levels and symptom severity: Mild/asymptomatic hypocalcemia can be treated with oral calcium supplements and active vitamin D. Moderate to severe hypocalcemia or the presence of tetany requires intravenous calcium supplementation [60]. And the surgical position of the thyroid is the neck hyperextension position, which may pull or compress the brachial plexus nerve, causing transient brachial plexus palsy, resulting in numbness of the upper extremities after surgery. Therefore, it is necessary to assess whether numbness is accompanied by motor impairment in order to identify the possibility of brachial plexus traction injury. Patients should engage in safe neck and upper limb movements under the guidance of professionals in the early postoperative period, avoiding excessive stretching. Physical therapy or acupuncture may be used to relieve numbness or tingling in the hands and feet [61].

As patients recover, difficulty swallowing becomes the most core symptom in stage T3. Difficulty swallowing in this period may be due to surgical trauma, nerve damage and postoperative scar formation [62]. Patients may develop functional dysphagia as a result of psychological trepidation due to painful surgical incisions, leading to reduced swallowing motions or fear of swallowing. Nerve injuries are mainly referred to as recurrent laryngeal nerve injuries and superior laryngeal nerve injuries [63]. Injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve can lead to paralysis of the vocal cords, affecting swallowing coordination, while injury to the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve leads to cricothyroid muscle dysfunction, triggering swallowing weakness. Postoperative scar formation may compress the esophagus, leading to chronic dysphagia. Studies have shown [64] that the dysphagia experienced by patients postoperatively is mostly temporary, and for most patients, the symptom returns to preoperative levels within 2–3 months. Clinical staff can routinely conduct swallowing function screening during T3 follow-up (or before discharge) using a swallowing function screening scale or a simple drinking test. For those who test positive in the screening, refer them to a specialist for a professional assessment to determine the nature (structural, neurological, functional) and severity of their dysphagia. Intervention with a stepped diet, targeted swallowing rehabilitation (such as Shaker exercises), and multidisciplinary nutritional assessment resulted in significant improvement in [65, 66].

Among the symptoms at different stages of the perioperative period, neck discomfort was particularly prominent due to its high centrality in both the T2 and T3 stages, becoming a relatively stable core symptom in the postoperative network. At T2, neck discomfort arose mainly due to incision pain, intraoperative position that overstretched the patient’s neck and postoperative sore throat caused by the application of a transoral intubation tube during surgery [67, 68]. It may also be due to surgical trauma that causes edema in the patient’s neck muscles and the tissues surrounding the trachea, creating a feeling of compression. During this period, it is recommended that clinical medical staff assess the nature of neck pain (incision pain, traction pain, muscle pain), use analgesic drugs appropriately, and combine physical methods such as hot and cold compresses to relieve pain. At T3, neck discomfort may be due to postoperative chronic pain, scar formation, and tissue adhesions at the incision site causing difficulty in neck movement or limited range of motion [69]. Stretching exercises in sports workouts can reduce pain, relieve neck discomfort and improve neck range of motion [70]. Under the guidance of a physician, neck rehabilitation can be done with exercises and techniques that improve neck function and reduce patient discomfort, such as joint mobility exercises, neck stretches and postural education in daily activities [71].

The study found that the core symptoms of the symptom network were not the same at different stages of the perioperative period in patients with thyroid cancer, a result that is consistent with previous research findings [72]. Changes in core symptoms are a dynamic, multistage process that may be closely related to the course of surgical treatment. The preoperative patient’s primary disease causes discomfort and affects metabolic and immune function, and negative psychological feelings for a variety of reasons further influence the patient’s symptom experience. Subsequent surgical treatment brings direct stimulation to the patient, which directly affects the patient’s neurological arousal, influences hormone secretion and causes metabolic disorders. As the patient recovers after surgery, recovery dysfunction occurs and triggers corresponding psycho-emotional reactions.

The NCT results showed significant differences between T1 and T2, between T1 and T3, and between T2 and T3 in the network structure invariance test. This may be due to the fact that changes in the core symptoms affect the structure of the symptom network [29]. In terms of the overall connection strength of the network, the NCT results showed significant differences between T1 and T3 and between T2 and T3, while there was no significant difference between T1 and T2. The network connection strength of T1 and T2 is significantly higher than that of T3, indicating that the overall connection strength of the network from T1 to T3 and T2 to T3 is significantly lower. This suggests that the recovery of the T3 patients reduced the overall activity of the network and the patients’ symptoms subsided. Although the strength of network connections was slightly higher in T2 than in T1, the difference did not reach significance, which may indicate that the strength of network connections remained relatively stable from T1 to T2.

This study provides some value to the study of perioperative symptoms in thyroid cancer patients by exploring the core symptoms of patients at three time points in the perioperative period, and identifying the core perioperative symptoms in thyroid cancer patients may require more attention from healthcare professionals and researchers. The study may guide healthcare professionals to focus on the appropriate core symptoms at different stages of the perioperative period for patients with thyroid cancer, suggesting that they should focus on lack of appetite before surgery, numbness or tingling in the patient’s hands and feet in the early postoperative period, and difficulty swallowing and neck discomfort two weeks after surgery. In addition, the longitudinal exploration of this study enriched our understanding of perioperative symptom dynamics in thyroid cancer patients by identifying their core symptoms at different stages, which can be used as an intervention target to facilitate the construction of symptom management protocols, conserve healthcare resources, and reduce the symptom burden on patients.

Limitations

There are still some limitations to this study. First, convenience sampling was adopted to include study subjects from only one hospital, and the data obtained may not be representative of the wider population. Second, although the sample size taken for the study meets the sample size requirements for constructing a symptom network, the base number of study subjects is still insufficient, and the number of study subjects targeting some of the issues, such as clinical staging and surgical modalities, may be insufficient. In addition, this study design prioritized capturing the “critical period of symptom dynamics” and did not include time points more than one month after surgery, which may indeed have omitted delayed symptoms (such as hypothyroidism-related fatigue appearing 1–3 months after surgery). Meanwhile, this study was based on patients’ self-reports and lacked objective indicators, such as patients’ exaggerated descriptions of certain symptoms or withholding reports, which may affect the construction of for the network. Finally, this study used a contemporaneous network to analyze the perioperative symptoms of thyroid cancer patients, which could only reflect the correlation of symptoms in the same period and could not reveal the causal relationship between symptoms. In the future, the selection of samples could be expanded, the sample size increased, and additional survey stages such as T4 (e.g., 3 months post-surgery) added to further explore the causal relationships between perioperative symptoms in thyroid cancer patients, thereby more comprehensively revealing the dynamic characteristics of the symptom network in thyroid cancer patients.

Conclusion

In this study, a network analysis method was used to construct a symptom severity network at three time points in the perioperative period of thyroid cancer patients to explore their symptom severity, core symptoms and changes. The core symptom in preoperative patients is lack of appetite, in patients 1–2 days postoperatively is numbness/tingling in the hands/feet, and in patients two weeks postoperatively is difficulty swallowing. Healthcare professionals can develop efficient, targeted and precise symptom management strategies based on core symptoms at different times to improve the negative impact of perioperative symptoms and enhance symptom management outcomes.
