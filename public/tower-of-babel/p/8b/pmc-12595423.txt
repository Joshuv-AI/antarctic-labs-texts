Life-threatening airway stenosis due to Scedosporium infection during nontuberculous mycobacteria treatment: A case report
Authors: Ayako Kurosaki, Naoko Mato, Akinaga Iijima, Masayuki Nakayama, Shinichi Yamamoto, Masashi Bando, Makoto Maemondo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fungal pathogens generally grow in the pulmonary parenchyma, but occasionally involve the bronchial wall. A 73-year-old female was admitted to our hospital with dyspnea and a critical endobronchial fungal infection. Because the central airway stenosis was life-threatening, a silicone stent was inserted into the trachea on an urgent basis under extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. We identified the pathogen as Scedosporium apiospermum. Treatment with voriconazole gradually improved the endobronchial lesion, and the stent was extubated 6 months later. Although pulmonary infection of Scedosporium spp. is rare, the comorbidity of rheumatoid arthritis with immunosuppressive treatment and pre-existing cavitary lesion induced by Mycobacterium avium-infection were considered to be risk factors for more extensive lesion development. Urgent endobronchial intervention and appropriate fungal treatment can provide successful outcomes in patients with fulminant endobronchial infection.

Introduction

Endobronchial fungal infections are relatively uncommon, and thus far Aspergillus spp., Candida spp., Zygomycosis spp., and Cryptococcus spp. have been reported as causative microorganisms [1]. Among these fungi, the Aspergillus spp. have been the most frequently isolated from the airways of affected patients. Endobronchial fungal infection generally occurs in patients who are immunocompromised due to administration of an immunosuppressant, or who have a history of solid organ transplantation or chemotherapy for malignancies [1]. We report here a case of life-threatening tracheal stenosis caused by an endobronchial Scedosporium apiospermum (S. apiospermum) infection. The patient had a medical history of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) infection and was under medication for these comorbidities. We carried out urgent airway management and administered appropriate medication for a favorable outcome.

Case report

A 73-year-old female with a 30-year history of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) was admitted to our hospital due to progressive dyspnea, cough and sputum production. Five years before admission, she had begun taking 8 mg/week of methotrexate (MTX) due to worsening of systemic RA, and clarithromycin (CAM), ethambutol (EB), and rifampicin (RFP) due to chronic pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) infection. Despite these treatments, her sputum cultures continued to show MAC infection and her radiological findings gradually deteriorated, intramuscular kanamycin was additionally administrated. Following intramuscular kanamycin initiation, the sputum cultures became negative for MAC. However, symptoms of cough, sputum production and dyspnea worsened again, and she presented to our hospital.

Physical examination on admission revealed a body temperature of 37.2 °C, blood pressure of 137/73 mmHg, pulse rate of 100/min, SpO2 97 % (room air), and respiratory rate of 22 breaths/min. Chest auscultation revealed stridor in the upper airway and coarse crackles in the bilateral lower lungs. Laboratory findings were as follows: white blood cell count (WBC) 5100/μL; C-reactive protein (CRP) 11.45 mg/dL; normal liver and kidney function; βD glucan 8.4 pg/mL; negative for aspergillus antigen; and positive for anti-MAC antibody. A sputum culture was positive for acid-fast bacterial staining and MAC-polymerase chain reaction (PCR); no other significant bacteria were detected. A chest x-ray examination showed the pre-existing infiltration of non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) in the right upper lesion. After admission, the patient's dyspnea steadily worsened, and we performed intubation due to the appearance of labored breathing. To avoid any potential ventilatory difficulty due to the severe airway stenosis, we introduced veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (v-v ECMO) before intubating. Bronchoscopy performed after the intubation revealed that the tracheal lumen was filled with yellow necrotic tissue and was seriously stenosed (Fig. 2A). Mucosal edema and ulceration with adhesion of necrotic tissue were also observed from the right main bronchus to the upper bronchus (Fig. 2B and C). The necrotic tissue was debulked with guidance from a rigid bronchoscope (Efer-Dumon bronchoscope; EFER, La Ciotat, France), and a silicone stent (Tracheobronxane™ Dumon® Y-stent; Novatech, La Ciotat, France) was inserted to improve the airway stenosis (Fig. 1, Fig. 3). Pathological examination of the tracheal necrotic material revealed branching septate hyphae, and the microorganism was initially suspected to be Aspergillus spp. However, in the culture examination the colony gradually turned black, and Grocott's staining (Fig. 4A) and lactophenol cotton blue staining (Fig. 4B) visualized unique rounded mycelial segments at the tips of the mycelium, leading us to suspect Scedosporiosis spp. Finally, matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF, Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA) and a genetic analysis identified the pathogen as S. apiospermum.Fig. 4Grocott's staining (A) and lactophenol cotton blue staining (B) showing rounded mycelial segments at the tips of the mycelium.Fig. 4

We had initially introduced intravenous amphotericin B based on the suspicion of an Aspergillus infection. However, after the diagnosis of a Scedosporium spp. infection, we switched the antifungal drug to intravenous voriconazole (VRCZ). Rifampicin, which the patient had been taking for pulmonary MAC disease, was discontinued because of its interaction with VRCZ. The patient's CRP level gradually decreased as the concentration of plasma VRCZ increased (trough value 1–4 μg/L) (Fig. 5). After 16 days of treatment, bronchoscopy revealed a gradual diminishment of tracheal necrotic tissue and gradual improvement of the edema in the right upper lobe (Fig. 2D). A CT examination also showed an improvement of the thickening of the tracheal and cavity walls and the infiltrating shadow of the left lower lobe (Fig. 1C and D). There was concern that the lumen of the silicone stent might be clogged with sputum, but regular bronchial toileting, administration of expectorant (including nebulizer inhalation) and intensive pulmonary rehabilitation helped to drain the sputum and prevent obstruction of the stent.Fig. 5Time course of the CRP level and the concentration of plasma VRCZ. The patient was discharged on day 45 after admission. The VRCZ treatment was continued, and the bronchoscopy findings consistently improved. The tracheal silicone stent was removed 6 months later. The fungal infection was completely resolved, and no post-inflammatory airway stenosis developed. The patient died 3 years later due to renal cell carcinoma, with no recurrence of Scedosporium infection during her lifetime.

Discussion

We report a case of life-threatening tracheal stenosis caused by an endobronchial S. apiospermum infection. Scedosporium spp. comprise a genus of saprophytic fungi that are isolated from soil and polluted water and occasionally infect the human lungs via the respiratory tract [2]. Scedosporium spp. cause a variety of invasive pulmonary infections (nodular pneumonia, necrotizing pneumonia, lung abscess, pleurisy, empyema), especially among immunocompromised patients with corticosteroid treatment, solid organ transplantation, or a hematologic malignancy [3]. In addition, individuals with lung structural changes due to tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, or pulmonary non-tuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) are also considered to be at risk of developing a noninvasive infection [3]. There are several reports of pulmonary scedosporiosis associated with NTM, and the clinical presentations included a fungus ball [4,5], bronchiectasis [6], and polyp lesions in the airways [7]. In the present case, we speculate that the immunosuppressed state induced by RA treatment, the impairment of bronchial clearance, and the cavity formation due to NTM infection all contributed to the endobronchial fungal infection.

Aspergillus spp. are the fungi most commonly associated with airway disease. In the current case, the biopsied bronchial samples demonstrated acute angle branching hyphae resembling those of Aspergillus spp. under a microscope, so the patient was initially misdiagnosed as having Aspergillosis infection. However, the cultured colonies gradually turned black, which is not characteristic of Aspergillus spp. In addition, the formation of characteristic lateral conidiation on hyphae was pathologically confirmed, leading us to suspect Scedosporium spp. The mass spectrometry and genetic analyses eventually identified the fungus as S. apiospermum. Of note, amphotericin B is a widely approved antifungal agent for the treatment of Aspergillus spp., but its sensitivity for Scedosporium spp. is insufficient [2]. The accurate identification of pathogens is thus critical to the selection of an appropriate antifungal agent, especially in life-threatening cases. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is essential for patients treated with VRCZ, and we administered this drug with a target trough value of 1–4 μg/L as recommended [8]. Furthermore, VRCZ requires caution due to the large number of medications contraindicated for concomitant use. RFP and rifabutin (RFB), which are commonly used in the treatment of NTM infections, induce cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4), the enzyme responsible for metabolizing VRCZ, thereby significantly reducing its serum concentration. In our patient, rifampicin (RFP) was discontinued in order to prioritize the treatment of Scedosporium infection.

The appropriate duration of antifungal treatment for Scedosporium spp. has not been established. In prior case reports, two immunocompetent patients with Scedosporium spp. infection were cured after 6 months of VRCZ treatment [5,8], and another case involving a fungus ball in a patient with lung cancer was successfully treated by 3 months of VRCZ treatment [9]. We therefore continued our present VRCZ regimen for 6 months after the silicone stent was removed.

Endobronchial fungal infections generally present with a variety of luminal findings, including obstruction by a mass lesion, ulcer, pseudomembrane, and perforations. A review of the literature on endobronchial fungal disease found that 65 (29 %) of the 228 patients with fungal airway infection required bronchoscopic intervention [1]. Several cases have been reported in which airway stenosis progressed despite the use of optimal treatment such as the administration of antifungal agents [10,11], debulking, or balloon dilation to narrow the lesion [12,13]. In addition, it should be noted that scar-tissue formation after therapeutic endobronchial interventions can exacerbate stenosis [12,13], since patients with various types of fungal infection (e.g., aspergillosis, cryptococcosis, and mucormycosis) involving an endobronchial lesion have often required bronchial stent placement. Thus, endobronchial stent insertion has become a meaningful strategy to prevent re-stenosis in infectious diseases, and stent insertion was also applied to our case following endobronchial debulking. In addition to stent insertion, we introduced ECMO for the present patient in order to avoid complications during stenting, and the subsequent procedure could then be performed safely. We consider that these crucial interventions resolved a life-threatening condition and resulted in a favorable outcome.

Conclusion

We have presented the case of a patient with life-threatening airway stenosis due to pulmonary S. apiospermum infection. The patient was successfully managed by the insertion of a silicone stent and the administration of an appropriate antifungal agent. In patients with an immunosuppressed status or deformative pulmonary disease, scedosporiosis should be considered when new necrotic lower-airway disease appears.
