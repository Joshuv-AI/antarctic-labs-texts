Years of Life Lost due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal
Authors: Margo Tonnelier, Damien Delforge, Manisha Panta Bhandari, Brecht Devleesschauwer, Juanita A. Haagsma, Grant MA Wyper, Emilie Castin, Niko Speybroeck, Periklis Charalampous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInformation on Years of Life Lost (YLL) is instrumental in determining the mortality impact of disasters at the population level. YLL due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal, along with associated demographic disparities, has not yet been systematically studied. We therefore estimated YLL due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal and examined disparities by age, sex, and district.MethodsEarthquake-specific deaths were sourced from the 2017 Nepal Police Report and disaggregated from broad age groups into 5-year intervals using a smooth, shape-preserving cubic interpolation approach that combines district-level population structures with age-specific mortality patterns. YLL were calculated by multiplying deaths in each age group by the remaining life expectancy from the 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) reference life table. A sensitivity analysis was performed using the 2019 national life table for Nepal. Reporting follows the Standardised Reporting Of Burden Of Disease (STROBOD) statement.ResultsThe earthquake caused an estimated 8,951 deaths across 41 districts, resulting in 473,280 YLL and 2,685 per 100,000. YLL rates were higher in females (2,998 per 100,000) than in males (2,365 per 100,000). Individuals under 20 years accounted for 55% of total YLL. Male-to-female YLL ratios ranged from 0.15 (Parsa) to 6.14 (Solukhumbu). YLL rates were 27% lower for males and 21% lower for females when based on the Nepal rather than the GBD life table.ConclusionsYLL due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake varied considerably by age and sex, and estimates were influenced by the choice of life table. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake ranked among the top five leading causes of YLL in Nepal in 2015. Future research should integrate morbidity data, refine age-specific mortality estimates, and strengthen disaster risk reduction strategies targeting vulnerable groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27054-4.

Introduction

Nepal has a history of frequent natural hazards, with earthquakes and floods identified as major risks. Nepal is the seventh most affected country from climate-related disasters, primarily due to its geographical location [1]. On April 25, 2015, a 7.8 magnitude earthquake struck near the village of Barpak in the Gorkha district of Nepal (GLIDE identifier: EQ-2015-000048-NPL, www.glidenumber.net). This event was followed by a series of aftershocks, the strongest of which was a 7.3 magnitude earthquake in the Dolakha district on May 12, 2015 [2–4]. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake had a devastating impact on population health, infrastructure, care facilities, and the economy. It resulted in approximately 9,000 fatalities, 22,000 injuries, and the displacement of around 2 million people [3–5].

During the emergency period following the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, vulnerable populations—such as injured individuals, those with functional limitations, infants, and older adults—faced greater challenges in self-evacuating. Among older adults, health-related quality of life was affected by advanced age, functional status, injury, distance to health-care facilities, social support, access to earthquake-related safety information, and barriers to healthcare access [6–8]. Other studies have identified elevated hazard-related mortality among older adults compared to the general population, as well as among younger females, particularly those residing in rural areas [5, 8–15]. However, the population-level mortality loss from the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal, measured explicitly in Years of Life Lost (YLL), along with associated demographic disparities, has not yet been systematically studied. In contrast to other mortality-based indicators, YLL reflect estimates of population health loss from mortality, accounting for both the occurrence of death and the age at which death occurs. The YLL indicator aligns with the principle that the health impact of deaths at younger ages is higher than that of deaths occurring at older ages [16, 17].

The burden of disease framework, introduced in the first Global Burden of Disease (GBD), is widely used to summarise the debilitating impact of morbidity and mortality on population health in a comparable way [16]. This framework integrates the morbidity effects, quantified as Years Lost due to Disability (YLD), and the mortality effects, quantified as YLL, into a composite measure called Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) [16–19]. The DALY and its components quantify the healthy life-time loss in years and serve as a powerful resource for determining the progress of diseases and injuries, facilitating comparisons across health conditions and risk factors, geographies, and time spans, and guiding efforts to mitigate and reduce health-related disparities [16–20].

However, the burden of disease associated with specific natural hazards (e.g. flood, volcanic eruption, earthquake, cataclysmic storm, avalanche, and other earth movements or other forms of forces of nature) has not been reported separately in the recent iteration of the 2021 GBD study [20]. Instead, such natural hazards are grouped under the broader “Exposure to forces of nature” category, which limits the comparability of their impact on populations and hinders understanding of what the effect of one natural hazard relative to another is. Such information is crucial for guiding decision-making, shaping effective planning, and improving disaster preparedness.

Therefore, this study aimed to quantify mortality loss from the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal, measured in YLL, stratified by age, sex, and district, and to examine disparities in YLL across these determinants. A secondary objective was to examine variations in earthquake-specific YLL using different mortality valuation standards.

Methods

This study complies with the Standardised Reporting Of Burden Of Disease (STROBOD) statement [21], a checklist designed to ensure consistent and transparent reporting of burden of disease research (Supplementary material 6).

Case definition

The 2015 Gorkha earthquake was an inland seismic event, unrelated to tsunamis, that caused intense ground shaking in a mountainous context. In accordance with the Hazard Information Profiles (HIP) framework, developed jointly by the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction and International Science Council (UNDRR-ISC), the Gorkha earthquake was defined as a “ground shaking event associated with seismic waves resulting from a sudden slip on a fault” [22]. It triggered a range of hazards, including landslides, debris flows, avalanches, rockfalls, surface ruptures, and liquefaction, all of which impacted housing and critical infrastructure [23].

Data sources and assumption parameters

At the time of the Gorkha earthquake, Nepal was divided into five development regions, 14 administrative zones, and 75 districts, with an estimated population of approximately 28.5 million. However, in September 2015, a new constitution was adopted, and Nepal was reorganised into seven provinces. In this study, districts are referred to according to the administrative divisions in place before the adoption of the new constitution [24].

This study utilises stratified population data to process mortality data—taking population structure into account—and calculating YLL. Population and demographic data were obtained from the 2021 Nepal Population and Housing Census [25]. To estimate the population at the time of the earthquake, we calculated a national-level scaling factor based on the relative change between the 2015 and 2021 GBD population estimates for Nepal, and applied this factor to the 2021 district-level census data [25, 26]. Earthquake-specific deaths (i.e. those recorded between April 25, 2015, and June 5, 2015) were sourced from the 2017 Nepal Police Report, which detailed information on deaths by sex, district in Nepal, and broad age groups [27]. We disaggregated these death counts to fit the 5-year age structure. To perform the interpolation from 5-year to 1-year structured data, we employed a Piecewise Cubic Hermite Interpolating Polynomial (PCHIP) method [28], which preserves monotonicity between age groups. The calculation process ensures consistency in population counts and population-weighted average death rates. It should be noted that one death recorded in Kathmandu remained unclassified by sex; this death was included in the total number of deaths but excluded from both death and YLL sex-specific analyses.

Quantification of years of life Lost

Earthquake-specific YLL were estimated by multiplying the number of deaths in each age group from under 5 to 95-plus years by the residual life expectancy at the age of death. We applied the age-conditional life expectancy defined by the 2021 GBD reference life Table [26]. YLL were estimated as absolute numbers and crude rates per 100,000 of population. We did not apply any social weighting functions to the resulting YLL estimates, such as age-weighting (i.e. implying that the value of life depends on age; a greater weight can be assigned to deaths at younger ages and a lower weight to deaths at older ones) and time-discounting (i.e. implying a greater amount of disease burden estimates when interventions apply in the present than in the future) [29].

Male-to-female years of life lost rate ratios

We calculated male-to-female YLL rate ratios for each district by dividing the age-specific YLL rates for males by those for females within the same district. A value greater than 1 indicates a higher YLL rate in males compared to females, while a value less than 1 indicates a higher YLL rate for females compared to males. Rate ratios were log-transformed; standard errors were calculated on the log-scale, and the 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) were back-transformed. Statistical significance was determined based on whether the 95%CI included the 1 value. Districts without reported or estimated earthquake-specific deaths for either sex were excluded from this analysis.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate differences in YLL resulting from the use of different life expectancy values. We calculated YLL and male-to-female YLL rate ratios using the most recent national life table for Nepal (2019) [30] and compared them with estimates based on the 2021 GBD reference life table (i.e. primary analysis) [26]. We calculated percentage differences (%Δ) by subtracting the GBD-based YLL estimates from the Nepal-based YLL estimates, dividing the result by the GBD-based YLL estimate, and multiplying by 100. We also calculated absolute differences and ratios, using the 2021 GBD life table as the reference category.

All statistical analyses were performed in Microsoft Excel (version 2021). Graphs were generated in R (version 4.3.1), and the disaggregation model and spatial visualisations were developed in Python (version 3.10.4).

Results

Deaths due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, Nepal

Figure 1a illustrates the number of deaths and the distribution of shaking intensity caused by the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, including the main event (April 25, 2015) and the aftershock (May 12, 2015). A total of 8,951 deaths (females: n = 5,024 out of 8,951; 56%) were recorded across 41 districts of Nepal. Most deaths occurred in the Sindhupalchok district (n = 3,573 deaths; 40%), followed by Kathmandu (n = 1,236 deaths; 14%), Nuwakot (n = 1,112 deaths; 12%), and Rasuwa (n = 681 deaths; 8%) districts (Fig. 1a, Supplementary materials 1 and 2).

Years of life lost due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, Nepal

The 2015 Gorkha earthquake resulted in a total of 473,280 YLL, of which 265,452 (56%) YLL were attributed to females and 207,827 (44%) YLL to males. This represents an average loss of approximately 53 YLL per death for both sexes, equivalent to a rate of 2,685 YLL per 100,000 of population. YLL rates per 100,000 were higher in females (2,998 per 100,000) than in males (2,365 per 100,000; Supplementary material 4). The highest district-level YLL rate was observed in Rasuwa (82,704 per 100,000; Table 1), with higher rates among females (93,446 per 100,000) compared to males (72,488 per 100,000; Table 1 & Supplementary material 1). A similar pattern was evident in Sindhupalchok district, where the overall YLL rate was 77,997 per 100,000, with rates substantially higher among females (89,726 per 100,000) than males (66,043 per 100,000; Table 1 & Supplementary material 1). Figure 2 shows the YLL absolute numbers and rates per 100,000 due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake by age and sex. The absolute number of earthquake-specific YLL decreased with age in both Nepalese males and females, while YLL per 100,000 increased steadily from the 45–49 to the 80–84 age group (Fig. 2). In all age groups, both the absolute number and the rate of YLL per 100,000 were higher among females than males. Individuals under 20 years accounted for approximately 55% of the total YLL in both sexes. The highest YLL rates were observed in the under-five age group (females: 8,904 versus males: 6,057 per 100,000; Fig. 2 & Supplementary material 4), reflecting their high residual life expectancy at the time of death.

Male-to-female years of life lost rate ratios by districts

Male-to-female disparities in YLL rates were evaluated across districts of Nepal (n = 24), excluding those with no reported or estimated earthquake-specific deaths for either sex. Regional variations in male-to-female YLL rate ratios were observed, with nearly equal proportions of districts showing higher rates in either Nepalese males or females. Male-to-female YLL rate ratios ranged from the lowest in Parsa (0.15; 0.08–0.28 95%CI) to the highest in Solukhumbu (6.14; 5.42–6.95 95%CI), Supplementary materials 3 and 5. Eight out of the 24 districts (n = 8; 33%) had male-to-female YLL rate ratios close to or greater than 1, indicating higher YLL rates in males; three out of these districts (i.e. Dolakha, Gorkha, and Lamjung) are located in proximity to the epicentre and aftershock regions of the 2015 Gorkha earthquake. Among these eight districts, three exhibited non-significant male-to-female YLL rate ratios, as indicated by 95% CI (i.e. Gorkha, Mahottarri, and Morang, Supplementary materials 3 and 5). In addition, 15 out of the 24 districts (n = 15; 63%) had ratios less than 1, indicating higher YLL rates in females; only one out of these districts (i.e. Sindhupalchok) is located close to the epicentre and aftershock regions of the 2015 Gorkha earthquake. Four of these 15 districts exhibited non-significant male-to-female YLL rate ratios (i.e. Kaski, Kathmandu, Makwanpur, and Sarlahi), Supplementary materials 3 and 5.

Sensitivity analysis

Using the 2019 Nepalese life table instead of the 2021 GBD reference life table, earthquake-specific YLL rates were 27% lower for males (2021 GBD life table: 2,365 versus 2019 Nepal life table: 1,735 per 100,000; ratio: 0.73) and 21% lower for females (2021 GBD life: 2,998 versus 2019 Nepal life table: 2,356 YLL per 100,000; ratio: 0.79, Table 2). Percentage differences in YLL rates between the GBD and Nepal life tables varied by age and sex groups. Districts with lower death counts showed more pronounced differences in male-to-female rate ratios, despite minor differences in YLL rates between the GBD and Nepal life tables. For example, in Solukhumbu (n = 22 deaths), the absolute difference in YLL rates was 285 (GBD-based YLL: 1,053 versus Nepal-based YLL: 768 per 100,000), with the male-to-female rate ratio shifting from 6.14 (GBD-based YLL valuation) to 5.65 (Nepal-based YLL valuation). In contrast, in districts with higher death counts, such as Sindhupalchok (n = 3,573 deaths), where the absolute difference in YLL rates was 18,552 (GBD-based YLL: 77,997 versus Nepal-based YLL: 59,445 per 100,000), the male-to-female ratio varied less, from 0.74 (GBD-based YLL valuation) to 0.69 (Nepal-based YLL valuation; Supplementary materials 4 and 5).

Discussion

Main findings

This study estimated YLL due to the 2015 Gorkha earthquake in Nepal, analysing disparities across age, sex, and district. The 2015 Gorkha earthquake accounted for 473,280 YLL—approximately 53 years lost per death and 2,685 YLL per 100,000. Individuals under 20 years accounted for approximately 55% of total YLL, with the highest number of deaths and YLL observed in the under-five age group. Females had higher YLL rates (8,904 per 100,000) than males (6,057 per 100,000). Comparisons between the 2021 GBD and the 2019 Nepal life tables revealed greater YLL estimates, with more pronounced disparities among males and in sparsely populated districts.

Interpretation of main findings: mortality loss and disparities

The observed higher earthquake-specific mortality among females is consistent with previous studies highlighting sex-specific vulnerabilities during earthquakes [5, 8, 11–14]. This likely reflects cultural, societal, and economic factors that increase females’ exposure to risks and limit their capacity to adapt to disasters [31, 32]. In Nepal, gender norms that position women as primary caregivers with restricted autonomy in health-related decision-making, along with cultural practices such as home births attended by untrained individuals and the stigmatisation of complications during pregnancy and childbirth, likely exacerbated maternal mortality disparities [33–35]. Women spending more time at home—often caring for young children—and the absence of young men due to labour migration likely contributed to higher absolute numbers of earthquake-specific deaths among women. As a result, young women may have been at greater risk of injury or death during the Gorkha earthquake and its aftershocks. Notably, the first earthquake struck on a public holiday (Saturday, April 25, 2015), when many men were likely at home, thereby reducing differences in exposure, while the major aftershock on a working day (Tuesday, May 12, 2015) occurred when traditional caregiving roles and women’s greater presence at home increased their risk of injury or death [36, 37].

Furthermore, we found that the highest YLL rates were observed in the under-five age group (females: 8,904 versus males: 6,057 per 100,000), followed by the 80–84 age group (females: 7,097 versus males: 5,423 per 100,000). This finding aligns with the YLL approach, which allows researchers to respect that deaths occurring at younger ages have a higher health impact than those occurring at older ages. These age groups are particularly vulnerable during seismic events due to physical fragility, increased dependency, and reduced capacity for recovery [32, 38, 39]. Infants and young children are more susceptible to trauma due to their developing bodies and limited mobility, while older adults face increased risks associated with multi-morbid health conditions and reduced resilience, which impair their ability to respond and recover from seismic events. These vulnerabilities can be further exacerbated by Nepal’s mountainous terrain and remote areas, which hinder timely access to healthcare services [40, 41].

We also found that districts closer to the epicentre of the Gorkha earthquake, such as Rasuwa and Sindhupalchok, had the highest YLL rates (82,704 and 77,997 per 100,000, respectively). In contrast, districts farther from the epicentre, such as Siraha, had the lowest YLL rates (3 per 100,000). These regional disparities in YLL are primarily driven by spatial proximity to the epicentre but also reflect variations in disaster risk reduction measures, local governance, infrastructure resilience, unplanned urbanisation, and widespread poverty [42–44]. For instance, in districts closer to the epicentre, the widespread collapse of non-engineered housing, which lacked structural resilience to withstand seismic forces, coupled with inadequate enforcement of building codes, contributed to the high number of casualties during the Gorkha earthquake [43, 45]. Implementing strategies such as resilient construction practices, community-based disaster preparedness, and equitable resource allocation is essential for mitigating future disaster impacts in Nepal.

Children and females were identified as priority groups requiring targeted disaster risk reduction interventions, particularly in regions showing significant disparities in YLL. Despite progress in empowering females through community-based disaster risk reduction initiatives in Nepal, barriers such as patriarchal norms and limited resources hinder effective implementation [46–48]. Initiatives like the UN Women’s Disaster Risk Reduction Program and Women for Resilience focus on enhancing women’s participation in disaster risk reduction and planning [49]. Additionally, the Maternal and Neonatal Technologies in Rural Areas (MANTRA) health intervention in Nepal enhances knowledge and healthcare-seeking behaviours related to maternal and neonatal health and geohazard management [50]. Women and mothers play a crucial role in protecting both vulnerable infants and children, making them a key target group for disaster risk reduction interventions aimed at enhancing resilience across generations.

In addition, our findings are broadly consistent with other studies adopting the GBD methodology. Compared with other major causes of death in Nepal in 2015, earthquake-specific YLL were substantial, ranking as the leading cause of YLL among Nepalese females, and the fourth among Nepalese males. Specifically, we estimated 265,452 earthquake-related YLL among females, exceeding those attributed to lower respiratory infections (247,587 YLL), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (192,531 YLL), neonatal encephalopathy (152,251 YLL), and neonatal preterm birth (144,854 YLL) [51]. Among males, the estimated total of 207,827 YLL was lower than that for ischemic heart disease (286,720 YLL), neonatal encephalopathy (229,202 YLL), and lower respiratory infections (225,838 YLL), but higher than that for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (189,598 YLL) [51].

Interpretation of main findings: sensitivity analysis

Applying the 2019 Nepal life table resulted in YLL rates that were 27% lower for males and 21% lower for females than those based on the 2021 GBD reference life table. This difference arises because national life tables reflect country-specific mortality risks at a given time, whereas the GBD reference life tables reflect aspirational mortality risks representing aspirational longevity. In districts with smaller populations, such as Solukhumbu, where the number of earthquake-specific deaths was lower, we found that differences between the GBD and Nepal life tables led to greater variability in YLL and male-to-female YLL rate ratios, as each death had a larger impact on the overall YLL. In contrast, in districts with larger populations and more deaths, such as Sindhupalchok, the difference between the GBD and Nepal life tables had less influence on the resulting YLL estimates. The differences arise because the GBD reference life tables are based on relational models informed by numerous empirical life tables, yielding life expectancy estimates irrespective of sex and socio-economic status; it is constructed using the lowest age-specific mortality rates observed over a long period across all GBD locations [20, 26, 52]. The Nepal life table, on the other hand, relies on national data and reflects sex-specific mortality patterns, accounting for disparities in life expectancy [30]. As a result, the use of GBD- versus country-specific life tables may shift YLL rankings, with country-specific survival rates during a disaster influencing both YLL and the overall disease burden on the population. Using the GBD reference life table in YLL estimations should remain a valid methodological choice, as it allows for international comparisons and reflects an aspirational mortality standard [26, 53, 54]. In this study, we adopted the same methodological approach as the GBD study, given its comparative and ethical advantages.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the absence of YLD, which precludes the estimation of the 2015 Gorkha earthquake-specific DALY. In terms of morbidity, earthquakes are natural hazards that generate various types of direct injuries or secondary complications that may result from injury or difficulties accessing care services to prevent future problems due to the earthquake. The application of a data-based disaggregation model may introduce inaccuracies in the estimated deaths, potentially affecting the accuracy of the estimated YLL. This highlights the need for more granular and high-quality data in future studies to enhance the precision and applicability of burden of disease assessments. In addition, the use of a scaling factor to estimate the 2015 Nepal population may not fully capture Nepal-specific demographic changes following the Gorkha earthquake. We included all affected districts for a comprehensive view of the Gorkha earthquake’s mortality impact, but results should be interpreted cautiously as in districts with very few deaths, the YLL can be disproportionately influenced by the age of individual decedents. Despite these limitations, this study offers insights into demographic and geographic disparities and provides comparable YLL caused by the 2015 Gorkha earthquake. The inclusion of sensitivity analysis on YLL valuations highlights the impact of life table choice on mortality estimates.

Conclusions

Our findings highlight the value of the YLL indicator in capturing the health impact of natural hazards. We estimated that the 2015 Gorkha earthquake accounted for 473,280 YLL—approximately 53 life years lost per death and 2,685 YLL per 100,000—with higher mortality among females than males. Our study indicates that the 2015 Gorkha earthquake ranked among the top five causes of YLL in Nepal in 2015. We also found that the choice of life table substantially impacts the estimated earthquake-specific YLL. We recommend using the GBD reference life table in future disease burden calculations. This would help limit the potential for inconsistent interpretations of YLL valuations in future studies. Future studies require more granular and high-quality data to improve the precision and applicability of burden of disease assessments and should address methodological choices regarding reference populations and integrate morbidity data to strengthen disaster impact assessments and preparedness strategies. Targeted interventions for vulnerable groups, combined with improved structural resilience and emergency preparedness, remain essential to mitigate future disaster impacts.
