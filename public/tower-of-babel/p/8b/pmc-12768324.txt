Study of Clinical Profile and Early Response to Dopamine Agonist Therapy in Patients of Hyperprolactinemia: Experience at a Tertiary Care Centre of Nepal
Authors: Richa Nepal, Manil R. Bajracharya, Budda B. Karki, Bikash B. S. Adhikari, Kushal P. Wasti, Anjal Bisht
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Pathological hyperprolactinemia is medically treatable condition with dopamine agonist therapy. This research was aimed to study clinical profiles of patients of hyperprolactinemia started on cabergoline and assess clinical, biochemical, and radiological response to treatment at the end of 6 months.Methods:This was prospective observational study conducted over 1 year duration. All patients of pathological hyperprolactinemia who were started on cabergoline were included in this study. Cabergoline was started at 0.5 mg/week for idiopathic hyperprolactinemia and pituitary microadenomas, and 1 mg/week for macroadenomas. Clinical and biochemical responses in the form of resolution of clinical symptoms and reduction of prolactin levels were assessed at regular intervals till 6 months. With respect to tumoural hyperprolactinemia, repeat imaging was done after 6 months to assess for a tumour volume reduction of ≥50% from baseline.Results:Out of 33 patients, 42.42% had pituitary microadenomas, 33.34% had macroadenomas, and 24.24% had idiopathic hyperprolactinemia. At presentation, the median prolactin levels were higher for pituitary macroadenomas (202 ng/ml) as compared to microadenomas (80.7 ng/ml) and idiopathic hyperprolactinemia (75.7 ng/ml) (P value – 0.015). Clinical response was seen in 81.8% (27/33) patients by the end of 6 months. 97% (32/33) patients had their prolactin normalised by 6 months, out of which 75.8% (25/33) had normal prolactin by the end of the first month itself. A tumour volume reduction of ≥50% was found in 72.7% (8/11) macroadenomas as compared to 50% (7/14) microadenomas in our study.Conclusion:Cabergoline was highly efficacious for treatment of both tumoural and non-tumoural hyperprolactinemia with significant early clinical, biochemical, and radiological response to treatment.

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of hyperprolactinemia has been estimated to be 5% to 17% in patients presenting to endocrine and reproductive outpatient departments (OPDs).[1] Most of the cases of hyperprolactinemia come to medical attention for problems related to menstruation, fertility, and galactorrhea or may have symptoms due to mass effects exerted by prolactin (PRL) secreting pituitary adenomas.[23]

Prolactinomas account for 25% to 30% of functioning pituitary tumors and are the most common cause of chronic hyperprolactinemia (50%), whereas idiopathic hyperprolactinemia accounts for another 40% cases.[3] Hyperprolactinemia is an entity with excellent response to medical therapy with dopamine agonist. Dopamine agonists are the first-line therapeutic option in patients with micro- and macro-prolactinoma as well as in those with the so-called “non-tumoural” or “idiopathic” hyperprolactinemia.[3] Dopamine agonists, particularly bromocriptine and cabergoline, are highly effective in normalising serum PRL levels and restoring gonadal function in over 90% of cases.[4] Despite the cost being higher, cabergoline is preferred over bromocriptine due to benefits of greater clinical, biochemical, and radiological recovery and tolerable side effect profiles.[56] Thus, consensus endocrine guidelines have recommended cabergoline as the first-line drug for treatment of hyperprolactinemia including prolactinomas.[678]

The rationale of this study lies on the fact that hyperprolactinemia is a common endocrine condition that causes significant morbidity among patients and can be managed well with medical therapy. Data from Nepal are lacking due to a handful of endocrine departments functioning across the country who are systematically following up Nepalese patients of pathological hyperprolactinemia. Thus, we aim to evaluate patients of pathological hyperprolactinemia presenting to endocrine units in a tertiary care hospital of Nepal and assess early response of cabergoline for treatment of hyperprolactinemia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a prospective observational study carried out in the diabetes and endocrine unit of a tertiary hospital of Nepal. All patients of ≥18 years who were diagnosed of hyperprolactinemia and started on cabergoline were included in this study. All patients with physiological causes of hyperprolactinemia were excluded, which included pregnancy, lactation, recent exercise, nipple stimulation, stress, and sleep-related causes. All patients of hyperprolactinemia who were not started on dopamine agonist therapy were excluded. These cases included hyperprolactinemia due to systemic diseases like chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, hyperprolactinemia due to drugs, hypothyroidism, isolated polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), stalk hyperprolactinemia, and pituitary macroadenomas, who were directly referred for surgical intervention as per standard guidelines.

Our general objective was to study clinical profiles of patients of hyperprolactinemia who were started on dopamine agonist therapy. Our specific objective was to study about the clinical, biochemical, and radiological response at 1 to 6 months of starting therapy. Clinical response was defined as resolution of presenting clinical symptoms due to hyperprolactinemia, which might include one or more of the following: menstrual irregularities, galactorrhea, breast pain, headache, loss of libido, and visual field defects. Likewise, biochemical response referred to normalisation of PRL levels with treatment and radiological response in patients with prolactinomas (pituitary microadenomas and macroadenomas) was defined at the end of 6 months as a tumour volume reduction of ≥50% from baseline.

This study was carried out from April, 2023 till March, 2024 over the duration of 1 year. Consecutive sampling technique was used. Sample size was calculated with the formula:

Sample size (n) = N/(1+N(e2), e = margin of error, N = population size

As per medical records of diabetes and endocrine units of this institute, the total number of cases of hyperprolactinemia who were treated with dopamine agonist therapy over 1 month (August, 2022) was 3. Hence, we assumed that there would be 36 cases in a year. Hence, N = 36 and the margin of error (e) was taken as 5%. Then n = 36/(1+36(0.052) = 33. The sample size of our study was calculated as 33.

Procedure details

After obtaining informed consent, detailed history was taken regarding symptomatology due to hyperprolactinemia. In our study, hyperprolactinemia was defined as a fasting PRL level >20 ng/ml for males and >25 ng/ml for females.[9] Borderline elevated values of PRL were repeated for diagnosis of hyperprolactinemia. Hyperprolactinemia was categorised as mild: 21 to 49 ng/ml (males) and 26 to 49 ng/ml (females), moderate: 50 to 99 ng/ml, and severe: >100 ng/ml.[10] Relevant baseline investigations were done, which included renal function test, liver function test, and thyroid function test. Urine pregnancy test was done in all females of the child bearing age group to rule out pregnancy. Detailed drug history was taken to exclude drug-induced hyperprolactinemia. For all patients in our study, with clinical features of hyperprolactinemia, who reported normal serum PRL, serum samples were subjected to repeat PRL estimation after 1:100 dilution. Likewise, all patients with mild to moderate hyperprolactinemia who had pituitary macroadenomas were also subjected to repeat PRL estimation after 1:100 dilution to rule out hook effect. Ultrasound abdomen/pelvis and necessary hormonal profile were done in females suspected to have PCOS. In patients with PCOS and moderate to severe hyperprolactinemia, pituitary imaging was done to search for pituitary adenomas, whereas mild hyperprolactinemia with PCOS was sequentially followed and imaging was only ordered as per clinical judgement of treating doctors. All patients with PCOS who presented with raised PRL were screened for macroprolactin by precipitation with polyethylene glycol. Only those cases with PCOS who were negative for macroprolactin were started on cabergoline and included in this study. Patients were referred to ophthalmology for visual acuity and perimetry testing if they presented with visual complaints or were diagnosed as cases of pituitary macroadenomas leading to hyperprolactinemia. In patients with pituitary pathology, relevant hormonal evaluation was done to rule out other concomitant endocrine disorders.

PRL measurements: Serum PRL level was measured using a quality-controlled fully automated apparatus VITROS ECiQ/VITROS ECi from Ortho-Clinical Diagnostics (a Johnson-Johnson company) using enhanced chemiluminescence (eCLIA) assay through closed commercial kits at the department of biochemistry.

Imaging: At least 1.5 Tesla magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) pituitary was done for cases of hyperprolactinemia to ascertain aetiology of raised PRL levels. Dynamic contrast MRI pituitary was ordered. Follow-up MRI was done at the end of 6 months for both pituitary micro- and macro-adenomas as per standard protocols. The volume of pituitary adenoma was calculated using Di Chiro’s formula.[11] The length, width, and height of pituitary adenoma were used to calculate the volume.

Volume of pituitary adenoma = ½ × height × length × width of pituitary adenoma.[11]

Treatment protocol: Cabergoline was administered orally in patients with microprolactinoma and idiopathic hyperprolactinemia at a starting dose of 0.25 mg once weekly, then twice weekly the following week.[12] In macroprolactinoma patients, the starting dose of cabergoline was 0.5 mg once weekly, then twice weekly the following week.[12] Patients were followed up at least four times after inclusion in our study. The first follow-up was done at the end of 1 month. Subsequent follow-up was done at the end of the second and fourth months. The last follow-up was done at the end of the sixth month. However, the number of follow-up visits was increased for patients whose PRL levels failed to normalise with usual doses of cabergoline. Dose adjustment was carried out on the basis of PRL levels during each follow-up visit. In patients not normalising serum PRL levels, the dose was progressively increased to a maximum of 3.5 mg/week.

Data collection and analysis

Data were entered in SPSS software version 20 for analysis. Data were expressed as frequency and percentages for qualitative variables and median for quantitative variables. Fisher exact test was used to compare qualitative parametric variables. Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare medians of quantitative parametric variables. Values were considered significant when P values ≤ 0.05.

Ethical aspects

Before commencing data collection, proposal of this study was cleared by Institutional Review Board (IRB) of National academy of medical sciences, Bir hospital (Reference number: 843/2079/80) on 20th April, 2023. Written informed consent was taken at the beginning of this study for participation and use of patient data for research and educational purposes. All the procedures done in this study followed the guidelines laid down in Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and as revised later.

RESULTS

A total of 56 patients of hyperprolactinemia were screened for eligibility during a study duration of 1 year. To account for possible attrition, 41 patients of pathological hyperprolactinemia were included. Thirty-three patients who had complete follow-up data of 6 months duration were included for final analysis [Figure 1]. There were predominantly females in our study with a female: male ratio of 15:1 [Table 1]. Menstrual irregularity (74.2%-23/31) [amenorrhea (45.2%), oligomenorrhea (29%)] and galactorrhea (51.6% – 16/31) were the most common complaints among females with hyperprolactinemia in our study. Out of two males in this study, headache was complained by both of them and loss of libido was the presenting complaint in one of them. The most common cause of hyperprolactinemia in our study was pituitary microadenoma (42.4%-14/33) followed by pituitary macroadenoma (33.4%–11/33) [Table 1]. Out of two males in this study, pituitary microadenoma and macroadenoma was found in each one of them.

Around 70% (23/33) patients in our study had experienced some form of adverse effects to cabergoline. Dizziness (27.3%–9/33)) and gastrointestinal side effects in the form of nausea (24.2%–8/33), abdominal pain (24.2%–8/33), and constipation (24.2%–8/33) were the most common adverse effects in our study. All adverse effects were conservatively managed without the need to discontinue medication during the study period.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first research work from Nepal that has studied clinical profiles and assessed early clinical, biochemical, and radiological response to cabergoline among Nepalese patients of pathological hyperprolactinemia over the duration of 6 months. In our study, the most common cause of hyperprolactinemia was prolactinomas harboured by 75% (25/33) patients [Table 1]. Literature states that prolactinomas are the most frequent cause of chronic hyperprolactinemia and accounts for 35–40% of functioning pituitary tumours.[13] In a study by Verhelst et al.,[14] out of 455 patients with hyperprolactinemia, 83% patients had prolactinomas (41% had microadenoma, 42% had macroadenoma), 16% had idiopathic hyperprolactinemia, and 1% patients had empty sella. Another study by Colao et al.[12] evaluated 219 patients with hyperprolactinemia retrospectively and found that 49% cases had macroprolactinomas, 44% had microprolactinomas, and 6% had idiopathic hyperprolactinemia.

The majority of females in our study presented with oligo/amenorrhea (74.2%–23/31) and galactorrhea (51.6%–16/31). This finding is similar to other studies and results due to suppression of hypothalamo-gonadal axis by raised PRL.[15] Raised PRL in the background of adequate oestrogen and progesterone priming results in galactorrhea. Thus, galactorrhea is less commonly found in males. Headache was another predominant symptom among patients of hyperprolactinemia (42.4%–14/33) in our study [Table 1]. The symptomatology of headache has been classically corelated to tumour size and dural stretch; however, the neuroendocrine basis of headache has also been described related to severity of PRL elevation even in patients of microadenomas or idiopathic hyperprolactinemia.[1617] Loss of libido was the presenting complaint in one male and three females in our study [Table 1]. Hyperprolactinemia results in secondary hypogonadism, leading to loss of libido in both the sexes.[18] However, this figure could have been underrepresented in our study due to prevalent sociocultural barriers. Out of 56 patients screened for eligibility, four were diagnosed of PCOS with hyperprolactinemia. Two of them came positive for macroprolactin and were thus excluded [Figure 1]. The remaining two cases of PCOS were started on cabergoline and included in this study. One of them had normal imaging at baseline with moderate hyperprolactinemia; the other one had pituitary microadenoma with severe hyperprolactinemia [Table 1].

In our study, the median PRL level was higher (202 ng/ml) among patients of macroadenoma as compared to microadenoma and idiopathic hyperprolactinemia (80.7 ng/ml and 75.5 ng/ml, respectively) (P value 0.015) [Table 2]. 80% microadenomas are found to have PRL levels < 200 ng/ml, whereas 80% cases of macroadenomas have PRL levels >200 ng/ml.[19] Serum PRL levels have been found to correlate with tumour mass provided there is no cystic or necrotic component in the tumour.[19]

With respect to clinical resolution of symptoms due to hyperprolactinemia, most of the symptoms had resolved by the end of 6 months of treatment with cabergoline [Table 3]. This finding was similar to a study by Etoga et al.,[20] where treatment with cabergoline leads to therapeutic benefits within the first 2 to 3 months of use in terms of improvement of menstrual irregularity, galactorrhea, vaginal dryness, loss of libido, erectile dysfunction, headache, and visual field defects. Among patients who did not have clinical improvement by 6 months in our study, there were two females with pituitary macroadenomas who had persistent oligo-amenorrhea; both had received 3.5 mg/week of cabergoline and had failed early radiological recovery. This was suggestive of the possibility of dopamine agonist-resistant tumours. Another patient with persistent oligomenorrhea had concomitant PCOS with microprolactinoma. This patient had failed clinical recovery despite normalisation of prolactin levels and normal imaging at the end of 6 months.

Treatment with cabergoline was started in usual dose of 0.5 mg/week for idiopathic hyperprolactinemia and microadenomas and 1 mg/week for macroadenomas. At the end of first month itself, around 75% (25/33) patients had achieved normal PRL levels with the starting dose of cabergoline in our study [Table 4a]. All patients in our study had obtained normal PRL levels by the end of treatment, except one patient with pituitary macroadenoma, who had persistently elevated PRL levels despite titrating dose of cabergoline to 3.5 mg/week [Table 4a]. In a study by Sabuncu et al.,[21] 17 patients (eight with microadenomas, four with macroadenomas, and five with idiopathic hyperprolactinemia) were treated with cabergoline at a dose of 1 mg per week for 12 weeks. Cabergoline resulted in normalisation of PRL levels in 82% of these patients with significant reduction during the first 4 weeks of treatment. Another study by Lee et al.[22] had demonstrated that treatment with cabergoline in 40 patients with macroadenomas had resulted in normalisation of PRL in 81.8% patients by 16 months. Among these patients, 93.1% patients had their PRL normalised by the first 3 months of treatment. Efficacy of cabergoline has also been supported by various other studies;[21223] thus, endocrine society guidelines recommend cabergoline in preference to other dopamine agonist therapies for treatment of hyperprolactinemia.[78]

Prolactinomas are the only intracranial benign tumours that can be treated medically.[24] Higher dopamine receptor affinity and delayed elimination from pituitary have led to better efficacy of cabergoline than other dopamine agonists for treatment of prolactinomas.[25] In our study, cabergoline resulted in a tumour volume reduction of ≥50% in 72.7% (8/11) macroadenomas and 50% (7/14) microadenomas at the end of 6 months [Table 4b]. A higher percentage of patients with macroadenomas had radiological response as compared to microadenomas. Colao et al.[12] also reported that a higher number of patients with macroadenomas (71% of 107) had more than 30% reduction of maximal tumour diameter than microadenomas (53.6% of 97) after 6 months of treatment with cabergoline. This contrasted from a study by Sheikh et al.[25] where 83 cases of prolactinomas were serially evaluated and almost a similar number of patients with microadenomas (82.7%) and macroadenomas (78.6%) had a tumour volume reduction of more than 75% at the end of 2 years. In our study, complete disappearance of tumour was seen in 42.8% (6/14) microprolactinomas and 9% (1/11) macroprolactinomas [Table 4b]. There were no giant macroprolactinomas in our study. In a study by Shah et al.,[26] complete disappearance of prolactinomas was seen in 49.12% microadenomas, 32.43% macroadenomas, and 33.33% giant prolactinomas after 2 years of treatment with cabergoline. The dose of cabergoline was titrated to maximum 3.5 mg/week as per reduction of PRL levels; however, the duration of follow-up in this study was much longer as compared to the short duration of 6 months in our study. A higher rate of complete tumour shrinkage for both micro- and macro-adenomas could have been possible in our study, provided we had included long-term follow-up data of these patients.

A major limitation of our study was that it was a single-centred, small study with poor representation of male gender. Thus, gender-wise analysis of results could not be done. Likewise, the duration of follow-up was only 6 months. Had the study been longer, long-term response of cabergoline could have been assessed, which might have influenced radiological outcome for relatively dopamine agonist-resistant pituitary adenomas.

CONCLUSION

Prolactinomas were the most common cause of hyperprolactinemia in our study. Cabergoline was found to be efficacious in terms of clinical, biochemical, and radiological recovery for treatment of pathological hyperprolactinemia in a vast majority of our patients.
