Gallibacterium anatis as an emerging pathogen in pet birds: biofilm formation contributes to treatment challenges and persistence
Authors: Katayoon Nofouzi, Setareh Shakeri, Shadi Nikkhah, Reza H Hosseini, Monireh Khordadmehr, MohammadSadegh Madadi, Hossein Jabbari Khamnei, Naghmeh Moori Bakhtiari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGallibacterium anatis (G. anatis), a microorganism of the Pasteurellaceae family, is an emerging avian pathogen associated with reproductive and respiratory diseases in poultry. However, its role in ornamental birds is still poorly understood. The aim of the present study was to conduct the first comprehensive survey of the prevalence of G. anatis in pet birds, to investigate its antimicrobial resistance (AMR) profile and to assess its ability to form biofilms using cultural, biochemical, molecular and histopathological methods.MethodsIn this study, 191 fecal and tissue samples were collected from various companion birds. Clinical samples were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar and MacConkey agar plates to isolate bacterial pathogens. After incubation, colonies were evaluated based on their macroscopic characteristics such as size, color, and hemolytic properties on blood agar-and a Gram stain was performed as an essential preliminary step for bacterial identification. The 16–23 S rRNA gene region of G. anatis was amplified by PCR method. The disc diffusion method was used to assess microbial susceptibility and resistance. Biofilm formation was analyzed using a microtiter plate assay. Tissue samples were routinely processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned and stained with common haematoxylin-eosin (H&E).ResultsIn this study, 20 G. anatis strains were isolated from 191 clinical samples of pet birds, representing a prevalence of 10.5%. Isolates were identified by colony morphology, Gram staining, biochemical testing and PCR for the –intergenic spacer region of 16–23 S rRNA, which yielded diagnostic amplicons of 790 bp and 1080 bp. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed complete susceptibility to ciprofloxacin (100%) and remarkable resistance to erythromycin (80%). β-lactam resistance was prevalent, with 70% and 75% of isolates resistant to ampicillin and amoxicillin, respectively. Biofilm formation tests showed that 80% of isolates had moderate biofilm formation. Gross and histopathological examinations of infected birds revealed severe respiratory and systemic lesions, including tracheitis, bronchopneumonia with focal necrosis, multifocal hepatic necrosis, and vascular congestion in multiple organs.ConclusionsThese results support the idea that G. anatis is a potentially important pathogen, with a biofilm-forming ability that contributes to treatment failure and environmental persistence. The 45% prevalence of multidrug resistance (MDR) highlights the pressing need for antimicrobial stewardship in avian veterinary medicine. Given the zoonotic potential of G. anatis, our study underscores the importance of One Health surveillance efforts in mitigating risk to both poultry and humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04263-2.Keywords: Gallibacterium anatis, Pet birds, Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), PCR detection, Biofilm formation, Histopathology, One health

Background

Gallibacterium anatis (G. anatis), an enteric Gram-negative bacterium from the family of Pasteurellaceae, underwent adaptation from a commensal organism of the poultry reproductive and respiratory tract to a recently described, an emerging pathogen affecting poultry health, especially in pet birds [1, 2]. It has long been linked with commercial poultry, where it incurs an economic burden due to respiratory infections, salpingitis, and peritonitis [1, 3], but its presence in Iranian pet birds-a population that is expanding both numerically and through contact with humans-underscores the need for clarification of molecular prevalence, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), and biofilm-mediated survival [4]. This distribution underscores the necessity of understanding its epidemiology in non-poultry avian hosts, wherein human-animal interactions are common and often intimate, facilitating zoonotic transmission and AMR dissemination [1, 5]. The overall prevalence of G. anatis infection varies considerably between avian species and geographically [2, 4]. Conventional diagnostic techniques have likely led to an underestimation of G. anatis prevalence in Iranian pet birds; however, Interpretable data on established gtxA and gyrB TaqMan fluorescent quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) molecular assays indicate a detection sensitivity of up to 54.4% for tested avian clinical samples, far surpassing conventional methods [6, 7]. These differences in susceptibility highlight the importance of regionalized surveillance, especially when there are locations with a high density of pet birds in urban environments where environmental and husbandry conditions can lead to a higher risk of transmission [8].

A second issue is the rise of the bacterium to a state of expanding AMR, returning as a public health threat [9–11]. Various modern surveys demonstrated the emergence of extensively drug-resistance (XDR) and multidrug-resistant (MDR) or pandrug-resistant (PDR) bacterial pathogens from distinct sources such as poultry, fish, animals, food products, and humans [9, 12–14]. Despite that infections induced by G. anatis could be treated with antibiotics, certain unresponsive cases were reported. Multidrug resistance patterns of G. anatis to various antimicrobial classes are frequently reported by several previous studies [2, 15–18].

It is critical to understand the pathogenesis and virulence determinants of G. anatis in order to avoid the adverse effects of this pathogen. The ability of G. anatis to adhere to and invade host epithelial cells is thought to play a notable role in the pathogenesis of G. anatis infection in poultry. The predominant virulence determinants accompanying G. anatis are metalloproteases, capsules, fimbriae, hemagglutinin, and innovative elements such as rtx-like toxin (gtxA) [1, 16, 19]. Cell-free filtrates of hemolytic G. anatis strains are highly toxic to avian-derived macrophage-like cells (HD11). The leukotoxic and hemolytic activities of G. anatis are mainly attributed to the gtxA toxin, which is encoded by the gtxA gene [19]. In addition, the gtxA toxin has a cytotoxic effect on avian macrophages in vivo. Similarly, fimbriae are considered to be one of the most important virulence factors of G. anatis. The fimbriae of G. anatis are classified in the F17-like family. Mutated strains of G. anatis that lack the flfA fimbriae were noticed to be mild pathogenic to the experimentally infected birds. Other virulence determinants of G. anatis include the proteolytic proteases and the antiphagocytic capsules that initiate biofilm production. Biofilms contribute to bacterial adherence, immune system evasion, and resistance to antimicrobial treatment, all of which together lead to treatment-refractory chronic infections [12, 19–21].

We now explicitly emphasize that although G. anatis has been well characterized in poultry worldwide, there is a notable lack of data on its occurrence and characteristics in pet bird populations in Iran. Our study makes a unique contribution by providing molecular prevalence data specifically for Iranian pet birds - a host group and geographic region that has not previously been studied for this pathogen.

Methods

Sampling

Sampling was conducted between May and July 2021 to account for seasonal variations in prevalence. This time frame was chosen to assess the temporal dynamics of the bacterium under different climatic conditions. For this study, 191 fecal and tissue samples were collected from various companion birds (pigeons, Dutch doves, canaries, parrots, etc.) in the provinces of East Azerbaijan, Yazd and Khuzestan. Detailed records were kept on the approximate age, sex, clinical signs and possible post-mortem findings of the birds. The handling of the birds was authorized by the animal experimentation committee of the University of Tabriz (authorization number IR. TABRIZU. REC.1400.053). The pigeons were used for necropsy. After decapitation of the pigeons with a sharp knife, a systematic necropsy was performed in which various organs and tissues were carefully examined for possible gross lesions such as vascular congestion and hemorrhage. The study population comprised a range of different pet bird species with a balanced gender distribution (47.1% male and 52.9% female) and an average age of six months. Clinically, symptoms were observed in 58.1% of the birds, while the others appeared healthy (Table 1).

Bacterial isolation and identification

Samples were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar and MacConkey agar for bacterial isolation. The cultured plates were incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h to allow adequate colony development. After incubation, colonies were scored on blood agar based on their macroscopic characteristics such as size, color and hemolytic properties. In addition, Gram staining was performed as an essential first step for bacterial identification. In addition, several biochemical tests were undertaken to identify isolates as G. anatis by confirming motility test, and the biochemical characteristics (oxidase, nitrate reduction, catalase, indole production, methyl red, Voges-Proskauer, citrate utilization (HiMedia, India), urease test (HiMedia, India), gelatinase (HiMedia, India), sugar fermentation (HiMedia, India) tests, and growth at 37 and 42 degrees Celsius [19]. This set of actions ranging from primary culture to microscopic and biochemical identification assured a specific and accurate identification of G. anatis [22, 23].

DNA extraction

DNA extraction from fecal and tissue samples was carried out using the FavorPtep™ (Taiwan) kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was subsequently stored at − 20 °C until polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis was conducted. DNA quality (A260/A280 ratio) and concentration were analyzed via a NanoPhotometer® (Implen GmbH, Germany) to ensure integrity before downstream applications.

Molecular detection and characterization

The 16–23 S rRNA gene region of G. anatis was amplified using the following specific primers [24]:

Forward primer: 1133 F (5’-TATTCTTTGTTACCARCGG-3’).

Forward primer: 1133 F (5’-TATTCTTTGTTACCARCGG-3’).

Reverse primer: 114R (5’-GGTTTCCCCATTCGG-3’).

Reverse primer: 114R (5’-GGTTTCCCCATTCGG-3’).

The PCR reaction mixture prepared, in a total of 25µL final volume (included 2µL of extracted DNA, 0.5µL of tested primers, 12.5µL of Simply™ 2X Master Mix (GeneDireX, Taiwan), and 9.5µL of Deionized water). PCR was conducted on a BIORAD T100 thermal cycler (USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 54 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were analyzed on 1% agarose gel including a Gel Stain-GREEN™ (Sambio, Shanghai, China), and then visualized using UV light.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The disc diffusion method was used to determine antibiotic susceptibilities of the isolates. Recognizing that the culture medium significantly affects both the inhibition zone diameter and antibiotic diffusion, Mueller-Hinton agar (HiMedia, India) was selected as the standard medium [25]. In this study, the susceptibility of the isolates to ampicillin, amoxicillin, erythromycin, norfloxacin, gentamicin, cefotaxime, doxycycline, and ciprofloxacin was evaluated. Antibiotic disks (Patanteb, Iran) were stored at 4 °C and allowed to equilibrate to room temperature by removing them from the refrigerator one hour before testing; they were promptly returned to the refrigerator after use. For culture, a sterile cotton swab was thoroughly moistened in a microbial suspension adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (which was achieved by mixing 0.5 mL of a 0.175% barium chloride dihydrate (BaCl₂·2 H₂O) solution with 99.5 mL of a 1% sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) solution; The optical density (OD) of the suspension was verified at 625 nm (acceptable range: 0.08–0.13), corresponding to approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) [26]. Excess moisture was removed by gently pressing the swab against the inner wall of the test tube, and the swab was uniformly streaked over the surface of the Mueller-Hinton agar plate using the spread method. Within 15 min post-culture, antibiotic disks were carefully placed on the agar surface using sterile forceps at least 15–20 mm from the edge of the plate and between other disks. Plates were subsequently incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h and measurement of the zones of inhibition around the respective disk was done in millimeters using a graduated ruler and isolates were classified as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) interpretive criteria [27, 28]. Isolates were classified as MDR, XDR, or PDR [29]. MDR was defined as resistance to at least one antibiotic in three or more antimicrobial classes, XDR as resistance to all but one or two classes, and PDR as resistance to all classes. The Multiple Antibiotic Resistance (MAR) index was calculated for each isolate as the number of antibiotics to which the isolate was resistant divided by the total number of antibiotics tested [30]. Chisquare analyses were performed on five antimicrobial classes (aminoglycosides, βlactams, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and macrolides) according to CLSI [24].

Biofilm formation assay

Biofilm formation was evaluated using a microtiter plate assay [31]. A 24-hour bacterial culture grown in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (HiMedia, India) was diluted 1:100, and 200µL of the diluted suspension was dispensed into each well of a microtiter plate. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h. After 72 h of incubation, the wells were washed with sterile double-distilled water to remove non-adherent cells. Plates were air-dried at room temperature, and biofilms were fixed using 96% ethanol for 15 min. Biofilms were then stained with 200 µL of 1% crystal violet solution per well and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Wells were washed three times with sterile double-distilled water to remove excess stain, and the plates were allowed to dry completely at room temperature. Finally, the bound dye was solubilized in 30% acetic acid and after 15 min of incubation, the OD of each well was measured at 590 nm using Hiperion Microplate reader MPR4+ (Hiperion GmbH, Germany). The experiment was done thrice for each isolate and mean OD values were derived for analysis. The isolates were differentiated into non-biofilm producers (OD ≤ ODc), weak (ODc < OD ≤ 2×ODc), moderate (2×ODc < OD ≤ 4×ODc), or strong biofilm producers (OD > 4×ODc), where ODc (cut-off value) was determined as the mean OD of the negative control (sterile BHI broth) + 3× standard deviation (SD) of the negative control. This classification is consistent with existing biofilm quantification criteria [32].

Histopathological examinations

Tissue samples (heart, lung, trachea, and liver) were taken and fixed in formalin for at least 48 h. After that, they were processed routinely, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained using common hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). The tissue sections were studied by light microscopy (Olympus CH-30, Japan), and the probable histopathological lesions such as hyperemia, necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration were noted [33].

Data analysis

The data obtained were explained with the help of descriptive statistics. Given the qualitative, nominal nature of our data, non-parametric tests (Chi-square of independence) were used to determine the relationship between the variables in birds. Percentages with a confidence level of 95 were used to indicate the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance.

Since the histopathologic lesions were natural infections, each analysis was performed for them.

Results

Isolation and identification

Of 191 samples collected from pet birds, 20 (approximately 10.5%) showed bacterial growth. On 5% sheep blood agar, the isolates showed round, smooth colonies with well-defined edges. These colonies were transparent or semi-transparent, non-pigmented and measured up to 1 mm after 24 h or 1.5–2.0 mm after 48 h of aerobic incubation at 37 °C. On MacConkey agar, the isolates formed small, pinpoint, opaque colonies that appeared pink or red after 24–48 h of incubation.

Gram staining revealed that the bacteria were Gram-negative rods, consistent with the expected morphology of G. anatis [22]. The recovered G. anatis colonies were bright translucent, low convex, fine, circular, smooth-edged with greyish color, and mostly showed β-hemolysis on blood agar. Moreover, the colonies were small (pin point-like) and pink (lactose fermenter) on MacConkey agar. Gram’s staining displayed Gram-negative coccoid to small pleomorphic bacilli. The retrieved G. anatis isolates were positive for oxidase, catalase, nitrate reduction, sucrose, and mannitol fermentation tests. Conversely, the recovered isolates were negative for citrate utilization, indole, urease, gelatinase, motility, methyl red, and Voges-Proskauer tests.

Molecular diagnosis

PCR amplification targeting the 16–23 S rRNA gene successfully detected G. anatis in 20 isolates. Gel electrophoresis confirmed the presence of a 790 bp band, but a 1080 bp amplicon could not be observed (Fig. 1).

Antibiotic profile

Antibiotic susceptibility testing showed that the highest sensitivity to ciprofloxacin (100%) and the highest resistance to erythromycin (80%) were observed. The results are summarized in Table 2. Among the five antibiotic classes, sensitivity to fluoroquinolones was the highest (29% sensitive; Fig. 2-a), while the highest resistance was found with macrolides at 31% (Fig. 2-b). Of the 20 isolates, 9 (45%) were classified as MDR, 1 (5%) as XDR and none as PDR. The remaining 10 isolates (50%) showed resistance to less than three classes and were categorized as non-MDR. The mean MAR index was 0.27 ± 0.15, indicating a significant level of multi-antibiotic resistance among the isolates.

Pathological results

At necropsy, the affected birds were found to have peritonitis and non-specific respiratory signs such as congested trachea with foamy fluid, air sacculitis with foamy or purulent fluid, and purulent bronchopneumonia with focal consolidation and necrosis. In the livers, there were discrete, randomly distributed yellow to grey foci in the congested liver. Histopathological examinations revealed non-specific pathological lesions in the tissue sections. In the trachea, focal sloughing, necrosis and inflammatory cell infiltrations (tracheitis) in the subepithelial lamina of the tracheal epithelium. There was diffused inflammatory cell infiltration, hyperplasia of the bronchial lymphoid nodules, vascular congestion, hemorrhage, and thickening of the alveolar septa in the affected lungs. In the liver sections, numerous multifocal random hepatocyte necrosis associated with vascular congestion, mixed inflammatory cell infiltration, and bile duct hyperplasia were observed. Mild vascular congestion was found in the hearts (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our study makes a unique contribution by providing molecular prevalence data specifically for Iranian ornamental birds. These results point to potential challenges in the treatment of infections caused by this bacterium and emphasize the importance of routine diagnostic testing to enable effective therapy. In addition, the ability of G. anatis to form biofilms may contribute to persistent infections and complicate eradication efforts [34]. In the present study, we aimed to determine the molecular prevalence, AMR profile and biofilm-mediated survival rate of G. anatis in Iranian pet birds compared to other isolates from poultry.

Ecological factors and husbandry practices contribute to the spread of G. anatis, as shown by reports of 30% prevalence in Muscovy ducks in Nigeria [35]. As the provinces from which the samples were collected are located in a hot and dry climate, the survivability of G. anatis in the environment may have decreased, which could reduce the detection rates in these regions, such environmental studies are essential. This inclusion is important for understanding the transmission dynamics at the human-animal interface and for reducing the risk of zoonosis. Although G. anatis is traditionally considered an avian pathogen, it has caused disease in humans in rare cases. In 2013, an extremely immunocompromised woman from France who had undergone a double lung transplant tested positive for G. anatis. Although the source of the bacteraemia is still unclear, it is likely that the infection was due to food contamination [36]. The other case was a 71-year-old man with a lung abscess in whom the bacterium was recovered from a bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) specimen. This patient also had an exposure, presumably through chicken or egg products as a possible source of infection [37]. In addition, the first human case of diarrhea caused by this bacterium was diagnosed in a patient with hypertension and type 2 diabetes, which was probably a gastrointestinal infection [35]. 70% of the G. anatis isolates in our study were resistant to doxycycline, an antibiotic of the tetracycline class. These results are consistent with previous findings where a tetracycline resistance rate of 88–92% was found in G. anatis populations, emphasizing the wide distribution of resistance to this class of antibiotics [11, 14, 23, 36]. However, in 2020, Yapicier et al. reported 50% tetracycline resistance in G. anatis isolates [37].

The resistance of G. anatis isolates to erythromycin cited in our study was also confirmed based on such reports.

β-lactam resistance is also common, with high rates of resistance observed for penicillin and ampicillin [38]. G. anatis utilizes biofilm formation as an important virulence mechanism to be resistant to antibiotics and evade the host immune system [2, 39]. Biofilms create a protective microenvironment that promotes bacterial survival by preventing antibiotic penetration and evading host immune responses [40]. As Costerton et al. have shown, bacteria embedded in biofilms can exhibit greater resistance to antimicrobial agents than their planktonic counterparts [41]. Biofilms have been found to contain proteins that interact with host proteins, including fibronectin, fibrinogen, laminin and plasminogen, leading to changes in host homeostasis [42, 43]. Of the G. anatis isolates tested in this study, 80% exhibited moderate levels of biofilm formation. This is in contrast to a report by Karwańska et al. in which 50.8% of isolates formed strong biofilms [44]. On the other hand, Sánchez-Alonso et al. reported that the biovar ESV200 forms a moderate amount of biofilm (20% under certain conditions), while biovar 12656-12 is significantly more efficient, which is associated with increased virulence [45, 46]. Stress hormones, epinephrine and norepinephrine can upregulate adhesion and virulence factors, leading to biofilm formation [45]. The availability of nutrients as well as the pH and temperature variations, affect the production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) which reinforce biofilm matrices and protect against environmental stress [47]. Metalloproteases and outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) further degrade host defenses and deliver virulence factors, respectively [48]. These mechanisms, synergizing with biofilms, drive severe pathology and the 45% MDR phenotype observed, underscoring the need for targeted interventions like anti-biofilm therapies or vaccines against GtxA or FlfA [49].

In the present study, pathological studies exhibited pneumonia, tracheitis, and hepatitis associated with mixed inflammatory cell infiltration, vascular congestion, and focal necrosis in the tissue sections. Similar histopathological lesions were reported by other researchers in chickens experimentally infected by G. anatis [50–52]. Besides, they reported inflammatory response, edema, and epithelial hyperplasia in the air sacs [50, 52]. Indeed, these experimental studies indicated that the most tropism of G. anatis is to the respiratory tract, which is in agreement with the present findings.

While this study offers valuable insights into the molecular prevalence, AMR, and biofilm formation of G. anatis in Iranian pet birds, several limitations must be acknowledged. The research was restricted to three provinces (East Azerbaijan, Yazd, and Khuzestan) and a sample size of 191 birds, which may not fully represent the broader Iranian pet bird population or capture regional variations in G. anatis prevalence and AMR profiles. This geographic and sample size constraint could lead to an underestimation of species-specific trends, particularly among less common psittacines, and may fail to identify regions where AMR is particularly prevalent. Furthermore, the exclusion of wild bird populations limits the assessment of environmental reservoirs and spillover risks, a critical gap given their established role as reservoirs for resistant pathogens. The small sample size (n = 20 per antibiotic), zero observed resistance for Ciprofloxacin and the pooling of intermediate and resistant categories may have obscured the details of some susceptibility patterns and limit the precision of the Chi-square test. Future studies with larger samples are recommended. The absence of whole-genome sequencing also restricts our understanding of AMR transmission dynamics and the evolutionary pathways of G. anatis, which are essential for informing One Health interventions. Future studies should expand the geographic scope, incorporate wild bird sampling, and employ genomic characterization of resistance determinants to enhance insights into G. anatis ecology, resistance evolution, and zoonotic potential.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that G. anatis is an emerging pathogen in pet birds in Iran, with a prevalence of 10.5%, a high rate of antibiotic resistance (70–80%), especially to erythromycin, and moderate biofilm formation. The clinical and necropsy findings - such as peritonitis, respiratory symptoms and bronchopneumonia - confirm its pathogenic potential. The main objective of this study was to highlight the new threat of G. anatis in pet birds and to emphasize the need for effective diagnostics, treatment strategies and antimicrobial stewardship to contain its impact on avian health.
