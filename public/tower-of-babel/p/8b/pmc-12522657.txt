Association of cardiometabolic risk burden with gastrointestinal bleeding in aspirin users
Authors: Yajing Wei, Jiamin Yan, Ziting Gao, Hongye Wei, Ziyi Qiu, Jun Xiao, Wuqing Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAspirin is widely used in patients with ischemic cardiovascular diseases, most of whom have cardiometabolic disorders. This study aimed to investigate the impact of cardiometabolic risk burden on first gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding in aspirin users.MethodsThis is a cohort study, including patients with ischemic cardiovascular disease and aspirin prescription from UK biobank. Exposure was cardiometabolic risk burden, calculated as the sum of the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors, including obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia and hypertension. GI bleeding obtained from hospital inpatient admissions was the outcome. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).ResultsAfter a mean of 13.4 years of follow-up, we found that among 12,781 aspirin users, the incidence rate of GI bleeding was increasing with the growing cardiometabolic risk burden (number of cardiometabolic risk factors from 0 to 4), ranging from 4.53, 4.98, 5.47, 8.08 to 9.64 per 1000 person-years. As compared to participants without cardiometabolic risk factors and after adjustment for potential confounders, the elevated GI risk was greater with the increased burden with HR ranging from 1.09, 1.16, 1.59 to 1.95 (P-trend < 0.001), in which diabetes (HR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.02–1.51) and hypertension (HR = 1.24, 95% CI = 1.08–1.42) may play a more pronounced role. Stratified analysis found that the association was stronger in those with history of gastrointestinal disorders, younger adults or males.ConclusionsAspirin users with higher burden of cardiometabolic risk experienced significantly higher risk of GI bleeding, calling for concern of tailored strategy for GI bleeding prevention in aspirin users related to cardiometabolic risk burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04347-3.Keywords: Aspirin, Gastrointestinal bleeding, Cardiometabolic risk factors, Cohort, UK biobank

Introduction

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is one of the most widely used medications, which is the mainstream for the prevention and treatment of ischemic cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. Many studies have demonstrated that aspirin is effective in reducing the risk of acute myocardial infarction, transient cerebral ischemia, secondary stroke, as well as some cancers [3–6], such as colorectal cancer (RR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.47–0.76) [7]. However, the use of aspirin for the prevention of cardiovascular events is accompanied by non-negligible adverse effects. Among these, gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding is a well-established adverse reaction of aspirin, while the risk of GI bleeding in aspirin users varies between individuals and has been linked to multiple factors, such as age, comorbidities, co-prescribed drugs, et al. Research has found that aspirin increases the risk of GI bleeding by 60% in elderly populations, although the absolute five-year risk remains relatively low, at approximately 0.4%. However, in the presence of risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, obesity, and other metabolic factors, the absolute five-year risk can exceed 5% [8]. Appropriate intervention, such as co-prescription of proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs), are established to mitigate aspirin-related aspirin-related GI bleeding. However, the net benefit of routine PPI use depends largely on the risk of GI bleeding in a long-term time course, so a personalized estimate of GI bleeding risk is necessary [9–11].

A cluster of risk factors has been referred to as cardiometabolic risk factors, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidemia [12–16]. These factors often occur concurrently and are recognized to play a significant role in the development of cardiovascular diseases. Over the last few decades, these cardiometabolic diseases have become a global health problem. Given that aspirin is the mainstream of prevention and treatment for cardiovascular disease, cardiometabolic risk factors are commonly presented in aspirin users [17]. Some previous epidemiological studies have found that people with cardiometabolic risk factors (such as diabetes and hypertension) have an increased risk of bleeding events when using aspirin [18–20]. The mechanism underlying this synergistic injury may involve the combined effects of cardiometabolic risk factors-including proinflammatory states, vascular dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances-interacting with aspirin to produce amplified detrimental effects [21–23]. However, current research predominantly focuses on individual cardiometabolic risk factors, while studies investigating the association between the cumulative burden of cardiometabolic risk factors and aspirin-related GI bleeding is quite limited. Therefore, in this study, we identified individuals initiating aspirin after the diagnosis of ischemic cardiovascular disease from UK Biobank to clarify the association of cardiometabolic risk burden and specific cardiometabolic risk factor with the incidence of aspirin-related GI bleeding.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a prospective cohort study based on UK biobank population. The UK biobank is a large-scale, prospective cohort study that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years between 2006 and 2010 [24], designed to investigate the effects of genetic and environmental factors on diseases. All participants were interviewed using a touch-screen questionnaire, computer-assisted interview, physical and biological measurements [25]. About 45% participants in UK Biobank have been linked to primary care registry, which contain records of outpatient prescriptions [26]. In the present study, participants diagnosed with ischemic heart disease or ischemic cerebrovascular disease were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes from the “First occurrence” category, details of related codes are presented in Supplementary Table 1. A total of 74,369 participants with ischemic cardiovascular disease were identified. Then, we excluded participants without prescription of aspirin or participants with aspirin prescription before the disease diagnosis to ensure a new-user design. Finally, there were 12,781 participants included in the main analysis. The UK Biobank project was approval fby the North West Multicenter Research Ethical Committee and all participants from this study provided written informed consent.

Assessment of exposures

The primary exposure was cardiometabolic risk burden, calculated by the sum of the presence of cardiometabolic risk factors, including obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia and hypertension. Specific cardiometabolic risk factor was evaluated as secondary exposure. All exposures and covariates were defined and assessed prior to aspirin prescription. Information on cardiometabolic risk factor assessment was obtained from the “First occurrence” in UK Biobank project, which were collected from self-reported medical conditions, primary health care, hospital admission data and death registry data. Details of ICD codes are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Assessment of outcome

The outcome was gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, obtained from “summary diagnosis” fields in UK Biobank project, which were collected from hospital inpatient admissions (Details of ICD codes are presented in Supplementary Table 1). The follow-up began from the date of first prescription of aspirin after ischemic cardiovascular disease diagnosis until the date of the first occurrence of GI bleeding, date of death, loss to follow-up or the end of the study (May 20, 2022), whichever came first.

Assessments of covariates

We obtained socio-demographic information via the touch screen questionnaire. Age at aspirin use was calculated by subtracting age at the date of first prescription of aspirin after ischemic cardiovascular disease diagnosis from birth year. Given that previous studies found that advanced age (≥ 60 years) was a risk factor for GI bleeding among aspirin users, we thus categorized the age as ≥ 60 or < 60 years [27]. Ethnicity was dichotomized as White or not. Education background was classified as college or university degree, A levels/AS levels or equivalent, O levels/GCSEs or equivalent, CSEs or equivalent, NVQ or equivalent, other professional qualifications and unknown. Alcohol intake frequency was divided as daily or almost daily, once or four times a week, occasional, never and unknown. Smoking status was stratified into prefer not to answer, never, previous and current. Townsend deprivation index (TDI) and physical activity were categorized into quartiles. Participants with missing data were categorized as an ‘Unknown’ group. Information of previous gastrointestinal disorder and alcohol use disorder was obtained from the category of “First occurrence” using ICD codes, and data of cancer was retrieved from cancer registers. Medications were retrieved from primary care records, defined as ever prescription within 12 months prior to baseline, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), corticosteroids, other antiplatelet drugs, anticoagulant drugs, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, beta blocker and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). Details of ICD codes are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics across groups with varying cardiometabolic risk burdens were compared using Chi-square tests for categorical variables. We first plotted cumulative incidence curves using Kaplan-Meier method and examined the difference between groups via log-rank test. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests, and the results showed that the p-values for all exposure variables were greater than 0.05 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Based on this, COX proportional hazards regression model was employed to evaluate the association between the cardiometabolic risk burden and GI bleeding by using the group without cardiometabolic risk factors as reference, and hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Model 1 was adjusted for age at aspirin use and sex. Model 2 was further adjusted for age at aspirin use, sex, TDI, ethnic background, education background, alcohol intake frequency, smoking, physical activity, alcohol use disorder, previous gastrointestinal disorder, SSRIs, anticoagulants drugs, ACE inhibitors, beta blocker and PPIs. Test for trend was performed by including cardiometabolic risk burden as a continuous variable. We further evaluated the association between specific cardiometabolic risk factor and the risk of aspirin-related GI bleeding, in which Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, and model 2 adjusted for age at aspirin use, sex, TDI, ethnic background, education background, alcohol intake frequency, smoking, physical activity, alcohol use disorder, previous gastrointestinal disorder, SSRIs, anticoagulants drugs, ACE inhibitors, beta blocker, PPIs and other cardiometabolic risk factors (Namely, when analyzing any individual cardiometabolic risk factor, adjustments were made for the three coexisting cardiometabolic risk factors). Then, we performed stratified analyses to assess if the observed associations differed by age, sex or history of gastrointestinal disorders, as well as assessed the interactive effects. In addition, subgroup analysis was conducted to study the relationship among participants with ischemic heart disease or ischemic cerebrovascular disease. Some sensitivity analyses were further performed to assess the robustness of the results. First, competing risk model was performed to control the potential impact of competing risk from death. Second, accounting for adherence, we re-evaluated the observed association after excluding participants with less than three prescriptions of aspirin. Third, due to the relatively small sample size in the highest cardiometabolic risk burden group, we re-assessed the association by merging groups 3 and 4. Finally, both single imputation and complete case analysis were used to handle missing data, in order to evaluate the impact of missing values on the results. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS v.9.4 and R 4.3.1. All tests were performed two-sided and P value ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Cumulative incidence curve shows that the cumulative incidence of GI bleeding significantly increased with an increasing number of cardiometabolic risk factors (Fig. 1) (P<0.0001). Table 2 shows the relationship between cardiometabolic risk burden and GI bleeding risk. The incidence rates across the five groups ranged from 4.53, 4.98, 5.47, 8.08 to 9.64 per 1000 person-years. Multivariable COX regression observed a trend of increasing HR with higher cardiometabolic risk burden (P-trend < 0.001). Compared to participants without cardiometabolic risk factors, those with three or four cardiometabolic risk factors had significantly higher risk of GI bleeding, with adjusted HRs of 1.58 (95% CI = 1.22–2.06) and 1.90 (95% CI = 1.08–3.34), respectively. Competing risk model with death as a competing event showed similar results, in which the association was stronger with the growing burden of cardiometabolic risk factors. Compared to participants without cardiometabolic risk factorsand, the adjusted HR in Model 2 ranged from 1.10, 1.15, 1.50 to 1.63 (Supplementary Table 2). When excluding participants with less than three prescriptions of aspirin, the observed associations also kept stable with adjusted HR in model 2 ranging from 1.09, 1.15, 1.60, to 1.93 (Supplementary Table 3). Considering the relatively small sample size in the highest cardiometabolic risk burden group, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by combining groups 3 and 4 of the risk burden. Consistent with the aforementioned results, participants with three or more cardiometabolic risk factors had an increased risk of GI bleeding with HR of 1.62 (95% CI = 1.26–2.08) (Supplementary Table 4). Additionally, beyond the original classification approach for handling missing data, we performed sensitivity analyses using both single imputation (employing means or medians for continuous variables and modes for categorical variables) and complete case analysis, the results kept similar (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

As shown in Fig. 2, stratified analyses found that the association between cardiometabolic risk burden and GI bleeding risk was more pronounced in younger adults or males, although no significant interaction was observed. For example, younger adults with three cardiometabolic risk factors was related to 1.77(95% CI = 1.19–2.63) times higher risk of GI bleeding while no significant association was found in older adults; men with three or four cardiometabolic risk factors experienced 1.63(95% CI = 1.20–2.22) or 2.06(95% CI = 1.05–4.07) times higher risk than the reference group, while no significant increased risk was found in women with cardiometabolic risk factors. There was significantly interactive effect between the burden of cardiometabolic risk factors and the history of gastrointestinal disorder on GI bleeding (P = 0.002). Among the participants with the history of gastrointestinal disorders, the group with four cardiometabolic risk factors had the highest risk of GI bleeding (HRmodel1=3.95, 95% CI = 1.36–11.44; HRmodel2=3.92, 95% CI = 1.30–11.81.30.81), while there was no significant relation among participants with no history of gastrointestinal disorders. Table 3 shows the association between specific cardiometabolic risk factor and GI bleeding risk. We found that diabetes (HRmodel1=1.47, 95% CI = 1.22–1.77; HRmodel2=1.24, 95% CI = 1.02–1.51) and hypertension (HRmodel1=1.30, 95% CI = 1.14–1.49; HRmodel2=1.24, 95% CI = 1.08–1.42) were independently associated with an elevated risk of GI bleeding. Discussion

This prospective cohort study found a positive relationship between a growing cardiometabolic risk burden and the increased risk of GI bleeding in aspirin users with a significant upward trend. Stratified analyses indicated that the higher risk of GI bleeding linked to cardiometabolic risk burden was more pronounced in younger adults, males, as well as those with the history of gastrointestinal disorders. Further analyses regarding specific cardiometabolic risk factor observed that diabetes or hypertension was independently associated with an elevated risk of GI bleeding.

Multiple meta-analyses and randomized trials have shown that aspirin used for primary prevention significantly lowers the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE); however, the accompanying bleeding hazard cannot be ignored [28, 29]. Among various bleeding events, GI bleeding is the most common adverse effect of aspirin therapy [30]. Notably, previous studies have found an elevated incidence of GI bleeding related to aspirin use in patients with specific cardiometabolic diseases, such as diabetes or hypertension [8, 31–33]. For example, a systematic review integrating data from several trials and cohort studies found that diabetic patients had a higher risk of major GI or extracranial bleeding after taking aspirin compared to non-diabetic patients (OR = 1.55) [32]. Another meta-analysis also reported that the risk of GI bleeding was higher in diabetic patients using aspirin compared to non-diabetic patients (OR = 3.10) [31]. In addition, a prospective cohort study of 991 patients with coronary artery disease on low-dose aspirin found that patients with hypertension had a 4.6-fold higher probability of upper GI bleeding as compared to aspirin users without hypertension [33]. The ASPREE trial further confirmed that, compared with non-hypertensive participants, aspirin users with hypertension had a significantly increased risk of GI bleeding (HR = 1.48) [8]. However, how cardiometabolic risk factors affected the risk of aspirin-related GI bleeding is largely unknown. Consistent with findings from our study that diabetes was an independent risk factor of GI bleeding in aspirin users, diabetes was previously reported to be associated with a higher risk of GI bleeding in aspirin users, the mechanism behind which was linked to the depletion of mucosal prostaglandins [31]. Therefore, the guidelines recommend that aspirin therapy can be considered for primary prevention in participants with type 1 or type 2 diabetes who are at elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, but don’t recommend its use in those at low cardiovascular risk [34–36]. Apart from diabetes, we also observed an independent association between hypertension and incidence of aspirin-related GI bleeding, while the mechanisms underlying this association was rarely investigated before. But some studies have found that gastrointestinal bleeding is one of the common complications of hypertensive cerebral hemorrhage, the mechanism behind which was related to the damage of thalamic [37].

Cardiometabolic risk factors are interrelated and always clustering, resulting in elevated cardiometabolic risk burden, while there is a paucity of studies investigating if cumulative burden of cardiometabolic risk factors is linked with an increased risk of aspirin-related GI bleeding. In this study, we found that the increase of cardiometabolic risk burden, measured by the number of cardiometabolic risk factors, was associated with an increase in the GI bleeding risk, in which patients with three or four cardiometabolic risk factors was significantly associated with an increased risk of GI bleeding. Further stratified analyses showed that the strength of association was greater in younger adults than older adults, which may be related to higher susceptibility or unhealthy lifestyle in younger patients. Stratified analyses by sex found the increased risk of GI bleeding was higher in males as compared to females despite without significant interaction [38]. An interactive effect was found between cardiometabolic risk burden and previous GI disorder. Among patients with a prior GI disorder, those with four cardiometabolic risk factors suffered as high as 4.79 times risk of GI bleeding, calling for special concern of major GI bleeding risk in aspirin users with previous GI disorder and high cardiometabolic risk burden [39–42]. However, the exact mechanisms linking cumulative cardiometabolic risk burden to GI bleeding risk remain unclear. We speculate that this association may be driven by the combined effects of risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, which can lead to endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular fragility. This synergistic effect may increase the risk of bleeding when taking aspirin, especially in patients with a high cumulative risk burden [21–23].

Our study results indicate that the cumulative risk score can serve as a valuable tool for clinicians to assess the bleeding risk associated with aspirin therapy. We recommend using the risk score to guide decision-making and to establish specific thresholds for considering aspirin treatment. For example, patients with a higher cumulative risk score, especially those with a history of GI disorders, may require more cautious consideration of aspirin therapy. Moreover, compared to existing models, our study offers a straightforward tool for evaluating aspirin-related bleeding risk. Although more complex models may achieve higher predictive precision, our scoring system strikes a balance between simplicity and accuracy, making it more accessible for implementation in routine clinical practice.

There are several advantages in our study. First, this study extended the knowledge regarding the impact of cardiometabolic risk factors and their cumulative burden on the risk of aspirin-related GI bleeding. Second, a lot of information collected in UK Biobank project allowed us to take important confounders into consideration. Third, we performed some sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the results, such as competing risk model. While some limitations of this study should be noted. First, misclassification bias of asymptomatic cardiometabolic diseases cannot be excluded. Although UK Biobank collected information of waist circumference, blood pressure, blood glucose, and lipids level, the baseline in this cohort was the date of first prescription of aspirin after diagnosis of ischemic cardiovascular disease, which was not corresponding to the date of interview in UK Biobank project. We thus obtained information of cardiometabolic risk factors by combining self-reported medical conditions, primary health care, hospital admission data and death registry data. Second, only GI bleeding diagnosed in hospital was identified, and less severe bleeding episodes managed in outpatient settings or emergency departments without admission were not considered, thus this study mainly focused on major GI bleeding. Third, in this study, we employed an equal-weighting approach to evaluate the cumulative impact of four cardiometabolic risk factors (obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension) on GI bleeding risk. This method streamlined the risk assessment process, rendering the study design and data analysis more straightforward. However, the equal-weighting assumption may not fully reflect the differential contributions of these factors to bleeding risk. In clinical practice, the effect sizes of these risk factors can vary significantly. Thus, future studies are calling to explore weighted scoring approaches to enhance the accuracy and clinical utility of risk prediction. Fourth, despite adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders, residual confounding such as the use of other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and Helicobacter pylori infection could not be excluded. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to increase the reliability of the findings. Fifth, participants in the UK Biobank are generally healthier and more educated than the general population. These individuals typically have lower baseline bleeding risks, which may cause our study results to underestimate the bleeding risks observed in a broader population. Therefore, future studies may consider employing more diverse population samples to mitigate the impact of healthy volunteer bias. Finally, most participants of this study are Europeans, therefore, generalizability should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found that the growing burden of cardiometabolic risk was significantly associated with an increased risk of aspirin-related GI bleeding, in which diabetes and hypertension might play a more pronounced role. These findings call for concern of GI bleeding prevention in aspirin users with higher cardiometabolic risk burden.
