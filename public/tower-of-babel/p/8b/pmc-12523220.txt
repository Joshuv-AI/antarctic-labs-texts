Printed simulation models versus organ models in laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass training: a prospective validation study of efficacy, learning curve, and cost-effectiveness
Authors: Jianfu Xia, Minjun Xia, ZhiHao Zhu, Junjie Wu, Jinglei Mao, Xiaoping Peng, Shijie Yu, Menghui Zhou, Sidney Moses Amadi, Feng Chen, Fei Zhao, Maimaitiaili Maimaitiming, Aikebaier Aili, Wah Yang, Kelimu Abudureyimu, Zhifei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (LRYGB) is a complex bariatric surgical procedure that demands advanced technical skills. However, traditional training models (virtual simulators, cadavers, and porcine organs) are Limited by issues concerning realism, cost, and accessibility. Although 3D-printed models offer promising potential solutions, their application in LRYGB training remains underexplored.Methods3D-printed silicone models of stomach and small intestine were fabricated using patient-derived anatomical data. The physical properties of these models (tensile strength, conductivity, and rheology) were compared with those of human and porcine tissues. Face and content validity , as well as technical realism, were evaluated through expert questionnaires. Construct validity was assessed using the Modified Bariatric Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (MBOSATS). Trainees participated in repeated simulation sessions to evaluate their learning curves.ResultsThe silicone models demonstrated tensile strengths comparable to those of human tissues. Additionally, they exhibited superior aesthetics compared to porcine models (P < 0.05). Experts highly rated the face and content validity of the models, with median scores ranging from 4 to 5 out of 5. MBOSATS scores effectively differentiated between expertise levels (experts: 188.2 ± 1.48 vs. Intermediates: 145.10 ± 11.45 vs. residents: 64.1 ± 4.19, P < 0.001). In terms of training effectiveness, trainees achieved proficiency after 4–5 sessions, and post-training performance of residents on porcine models matched that of intermediates (P > 0.05). The models cost $2,000, significantly less than cadaver or animal alternatives.ConclusionsThe 3D-printed silicone models exhibit realistic mechanical properties, process high validity, and have proven effective in expediting skill acquisition during LRYGB training. They serve as a cost-effective and ethical alternative to traditional training tools, offering particular advantages in resource-constrained settings.Trial registrationThis study was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) under registration number ChiCTR2300078726 on 15 December 2023. The trial protocol was approved prior to the enrollment of the first participant, ensuring adherence to ethical standards and transparency in reporting health care intervention outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03141-y.Keywords: 3D printing, Model, Gastric bypass surgery, Learning curve, Training

Introduction

Laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery (LRYGB), a widely performed weight-loss procedure, is carried out on approximately40,000 cases conducted annually in the United States [1–3].LRYGB not only demands advanced laparoscopic surgical skills but also poses additional challenges due to the abundant intra-abdominal adipose tissue in obese patients, making the procedure more complicated [3–6].

Ideally, LRYGB training should occur in a supervised realistic clinical environment, which is not beneficial and safe [7, 8]. Adequate supervised training is crucial for achieving competence in high-risk surgeries [9]. Current, training models for LRYGB include virtual simulators, box trainers, and cadaver models. Virtual simulated LRYGB surgical training differs from the real surgical environment in terms of visual and tactile feedback, and is also cost-ineffective [10]. The EndoSuture Trainer Box Simulator (ESTBS) has recently been used to simulate laparoscopic weight-loss surgery, but it only replicates the Nissen fundoplication procedure [11, 12]. Danion et al. [13] developed a dynamic cadaver model, SimLife, which received high overall satisfaction scores from surgeons. Nevertheless, its practivality, effectiveness, and learning curve have not been investigated. Moreover, cadavers are limited by issues such as repeatability, availability, cost, and ethical concerns.

3D printing is an innovative digital shaping technology known for its personalization, precision, cost-effectiveness, rapid production cycles, and efficient material utilization [14, 15]. It enables the transition from the virtual to the real world, providing surgeons with anatomical models that offer realistic tactile feedback for pre-operative observation and manipulation of specific anatomical structures [16]. A 3D-printed silicone bowel tube model developed by Oxford et al. [17]received excellent ratings for face and content validity from nine surgical experts. However, this model focused solely on laparoscopic suturing and did not cover advanced instrument training. Despite its wide application in various surgical fields, 3D printing is rarely used in the LRYGB.

This study aims to evaluate the physical performance of 3D-printed models and their effectiveness in LRYGB training, with the objective of improving surgical skills and shortening the learning curve.

Materials and methods

Participants

After receiving approval from the Institutional Review Committee, 16 surgeons were recruited for the study. 5 experts (>500 laparoscopic surgeries, independently completing LRYGB), 6 intermediates (100–500 laparoscopic surgeries, primary assistants in LRYGB), and 5 residents (< 100 laparoscopic surgeries, no laparoscopic gastric/colon surgeries or LRYGB). All participants completed a pre-simulation questionnaire regarding their age, gender, experience level, and experience with simulation models. (Supplementary File 1).

3D model production

Anatomical models of abdominal organs and tissues derived from an obese patient were reconstructed using the MIMIC 23.0 system. Standard Tessellation Language (STL) files were extracted from the reconstructed models and imported into Magic 24 for repair, design of support structures, and positioning, for more detailes on this process, refer to our published literature [18]. Stereolithography (SLA) 3D printers were used to print molds, which were then used to create abdominal organ and tissue models. Silicone models were fabricated using AB two-component silicone materials, while hydrogel models were manufactured from Polyvinyl Alcohol (PVA) and Polyacrylamide (PAM) materials. The molds were printed using photosensitive resin materials.

3D model performance evaluation

Mechanical performance testing

The models were subjected to tensile stress and strain analysis using a high-and-low temperature dual-column universal testing machine equipped with a 10 kN load sensor.

Conductive performance testing

The conductivity of the hydrogel was measured using a digital four-probe tester. During the measurement a current of 10 µA was applied, and the Linear probe spacing was set to 1.0 mm.

Thermal conductivity testing

The thermal conductivity of hydrogel was determined at room temperature using a thermal constants analyzer that employed the transient plane heat source method.

Rheological performance testing

The rheological properties of the hydrogel were evaluated using an advanced rheometer. The test samples had a diameter of 25 and a thickness of 2 mm. The testing was carried out at 25 °C, with strain ε = 0.1% and a frequency range ω spanning from 0.1 to100 rad/s, enabling comprehensively assess the hydrogel’s viscoelastic behavior.

Microscopic morphological feature testing

Several slices were extracted from the models and examined for morphological features using a desktop scanning electron microscope VEGA3.

Model surgical procedures

The surgical procedures on the models were divided into 5 key steps [18]: establishing a small gastric pouch, creating the bilio-pancreatic Limb, forming the Roux Limb, performing side-side intestinal anastomosis, and conducting gastrojejunostomy. To facilitate procedural training, 6 different models were designed using modular approach. The stomach and small intestine were separated using fluorescent labeling techniques, eliminating the need of a stapler cartridge [18].

Face and content validity of the models

5 experts viewed pre-recorded LRYGB procedure videos, in which a bariatric surgeon performed the surgery on both 3D-printed and porcine organ models. Subsequently, these experts independently performed an LRYGB procedure on these models. After the procedures, they completed a questionnaire to assess the face and content validity of the two models. This questionnaire (Supplementary File 2) was developed based on our previously published intestinal anastomosis model questionnaire [19].

Technical authenticity of the models

Following the operations, 5 experts completed an additional questionnaire (Supplementary File 2) to evaluate the core surgical steps, the technical authenticity of each step was rated using a 5-point Likert scale.

Construct validity of the 3D models

5 intermediates and 5 residents viewed pre-recorded 3D model simulation surgery videos that included detailed procedure explanations. Subsequently, with expert guidance, they performed the surgery while being recorded. 2 experts used the Modified Bariatric Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (MBOSATS, Supplementary File 3) to evaluate the recorded videos and documented the operation times. MBOSATS, adapted from the BOSATS by Zevin et al. [20] and De La Garza et al. [11], consists of 4 Major steps, 37 sub-steps, and multiple scoring criteria.

Sample size

We used time data from the first operation of 5 intermediates and 5 residents as the effect size, assuming 80% power and a 5% significance level for a two-sided test. The sample size calculation indicated that significant differences could be detected with at least 2 individuals in each group. Finally, 3 intermediates and 3 residents were recruited for repeated training sessions with 3D models.

Repeated training of the 3D printed models

3 intermediates and 3 residents were randomly selected for participate in models’ operation training. They were informed of the training purpose, and the training was conducted at a fixed interval. A total of 8 training was conducted using 3D-printed models. The changes in scores and operation times of them were analyzed to indentify the plateau training period.

Training with ex vivo porcine organ model

Surgeons who had undergone repeated training and 3 intermediates without repeated training each performed LRYGB on an ex vivo porcine organ model. The entire surgical procedure and operation time were recorded, and 2 experienced surgeons utilized the MBOSATS to evaluate the participants’ performance.

Statistical methods

We used SPSS version 21.0 and GraphPad Prism 9 for statistical analysis. Continuous data were presented as mean ± SD or medians with interquartile range, while categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages. For comparisons between two groups, we used the Mann-Whitney U test and for comparisons among three groups, we used either one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Tukey’s test or Games-Howell test for post-hoc multiple comparisons as appropriate. The Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) method was employed to evaluated the learning curve. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Results

Models physical performance testing and material surface analysis

Tensile performance

Figures 1A-F present the maximum tensile strengths: 0.48 MPa (stomach) and 0.75 MPa (intestine) in silicone models, 0.82 MPa (stomach) and 0.52 MPa (intestine) in human models, and 0.14 MPa (stomach) and 0.25 MPa (intestine) in porcine models. Reported tensile strengths of human gastric and small intestine tissues range of 0.5–0.8 MPa and 0.9 MPa, respectively [21–23]. Based on these data, it can be suggested that the tensile strengths of silicone models are relatively close to those of human tissues. Silicone models exhibit greater strain elongation, which reduces the tearing risk.

Conductive performance analysis

Various concentrations of PVA formulations were tested in this study, and 10% PVA/PAM hydrogel was found to have optimal electrical conductivity. Figure 1G illustrates that the conductivity of non-soaked hydrogel is below 0.01 s/m, and gradually increases as the soaking time in saturated NaCl solution gradually prolonged. It exceeds 1 s/m after 6 h of soaking, which meets the conductivity requirements of most human tissues [24, 25].

Thermal conductivity analysis

Figure 1H shows that the thermal conductivity coefficient of 10% PVA/PAM hydrogel decreases from 0.58 W·m-1·K-1 to 0.54 W·m-1·K-1 as the soaking time in saturated NaCl solution increases from 0 to 6 h. Throughout this period, all thermal conductivity values are quite close to those of human or animal tissues [26], suggesting that realistic surgical experiences can be achieved when using energy surgical instruments for training on these models.

Rheological performance analysis

Figure 1I indicates that the viscoelasticity of the 10% PVA/PAM hydrogel ranges from approximately 0.1 to 0.2. Literature reports indicate that the viscoelasticity of human gastrointestinal organs spans from 0.1 to 0.45, indicating that the hydrogel exhibits rheological characteristics similar to those of living gastrointestinal tissues, with strong stability and deformation resistance [27, 28].

Microscopic morphological feature analysis

Figure 2 illustrates the microscopic features of different models observed through electron microscopy. The microscopic flat structure of stomach and small intestine was relatively dense, with a distinct double-layerstructureonthe side insiliconemodels. In contrast, the 3D printed and porcine models lacked the loose, porous architecture characteristic of real human tissues. Silicone models are particularly advantageous for simulating the cyclic loading and suture strength requirements encountered in surgical training.

Evaluation of the learning curve for the 3D models

Figures 3C-D demonstrated that operation scores increased and operation times decreased as 6 surgeons performed 8 surgeries with additional training. Intermediates performed at expert level, and residents approached intermediate level, with two residents surpassing intermediate scores by the 8th operation. Figure 3E indicated that the learning curve plateaued by the 5th training for 3 residents and by the 4th, 3rd, and 4th training for 3 intermediates.

Evaluation of porcine organ simulation operation

Figures 3F-G illustrated a pre- and post-training comparison of surgical performance among 12 surgeons (three experts, three untrained intermediates, and six trained surgeons). No significant differences were observed in operation score or time between residents who underwent repeated training and intermediates who did not (P>0.05). Repeated-training intermediates showed no significant differences with experts in operation score (P=0.557), but prolonged operation time (P=0.002).

Discussion

Early 3D-printed organ models were fabricated using commercial rigid plastics and resin materials. Although they possessed anatomic structures similar to those of human tissues, their color, tactile feedback, and mechanical properties significant differ from those of biological tissues [29]. These models lack precise stimulation of real physical and mechanical properties, thereby restricting their application in medical education and training [30, 31]. In our experiment, the stomach and small intestine models printed with soft silicone materials exhibited similar color and maximum tensile strength to those of normal human tissues.

The acquisition of surgical skills is crucial in surgical training models, with suturing and knotting regarded as focal points [32]. Cutting operations are equally important, especially using surgical instruments such as electro-knife, ultrasonic knife, and Ligasure. We utilized hydrogel materials to simulate the lesser omental sac and gastrophrenic ligament. Hydrogel models possess not only electrical and thermal conductivities as well as rheological properties similar to those of biological tissues, but also feature replaceable parts that allow repeated use.

Compared with porcine organ models, silicone models received higher scores for aesthetics, size, and stomach appearance. This was attributed to accurate reproduction of the color, size, and shape of human organs achieved through 3D printing technology. Compared with porcine models, the silicone stomach had a lower stapler response score of 3 points, which was not statistically significance. The silicone and porcine small intestine models showed stapler response scores of 4 and 5 points, respectively. Since silicone stomach has a larger contact area with the stapler than the silicone small intestine, it generates greater friction during operation, thereby increasing the difficulty. Currently, we are attempting to replace silicone with hydrogel materials to modify the model’s surface friction and texture. The lower ultrasonic knife response score of the hydrogel compared with porcine models was attributed to its inability to fully replicate ultrasonic knife procedure, although it can simulate ultrasonic knife’s effect in separating perigastric tissue, thus failing to completely reproduce real surgical situations.

Compared with porcine models, 3D models demonstrated similar content validity, which is a crucial indicator for evaluating the model’s training effectiveness. A recent systematic review reported that 3D models offer training effectiveness comparable to cadaver simulations and may replace them as 3D printing and biomaterial technologies advance [33]. And we believe that 3D models have the potential to entirely replace animal organs for LRYGB training.

According to experts, both models could accurately simulate the core steps of LRYGB with similar procedures. However, both models exhibited a median score of 3 points in dissecting the angle of His and lesser omental sac using an ultrasonic knife. This is due to the deficient perigastric tissue hierarchy of 3D models, as well as the less realistic and more time-consuming experience caused by thick and clustered perigastric tissues of porcine model. In the task of confirming the Treitz Ligament, 3D models showed a higher median score than the porcine model. This is because experts found it challenging to identify the porcine Treitz Ligament, as the jejunum started on the right side of abdominal cavity in the small intestine disc area. Despite these challenges, 3D models are the first time to represent this step accurately. 3D models had a median score lower than porcine model in side-to-side intestinal anastomosis. The relatively firm texture of 3D models makes it more difficult to align the intestinal ends properly during suturing. This reflects the suboptimal suture tactile feedback of the silicone small intestine model compared with the porcine model.

Model’s construct validity was scored by three groups. Experts conducted more LSG and LGCS procedures than LRYGB, whereas residents did not perform any LRYGB, which reflects the current situation of weight-loss surgery in China. LRYGB surgery started relatively late, and it is characterized by high difficulty and complexity, and a greater likelihood of complications. Our initial intention was to allow more surgeons to gain exposure to and become familiar with LRYGB using these models. Experts achieved higher operation score and completed the procedures in a shorter time than intermediates and residents. This outcome underscores both the inherent difficulty of LRYGB and the significant experience required to perform it proficiently. Moreover, it demonstrates the model’s ability to distinguish between surgeons of different skill levels.

Trainees have the potential to enhance their surgical abilities through the use of simulation models. Residents’ performance was close to the average level of untrained intermediates, and 2 residents showed surprisingly higher operation scores than the average level of intermediates in the 8th operation. Intermediates demonstrated performance levels close to the average level of experts, with a slow and stable learning curve. This is because residents are in a “blank” state regarding LRYGB, with higher plasticity and receptivity of new skills, and willingness for error correction, thus improving their surgical skills. While Intermediates reach pre-existing levels in the learning curve and remain relatively stable. Trained intermediates performed well than those untrained, and repeated-trained residents performed better than non-repeated-trained intermediates in porcine organ model. This finding is consistent with results from other studies. In studies with guided training programs, the learning curves of residents were not significant different from senior surgeons [34]. Learning curves based on surgical time are used to explore the training degree required to master surgical skills [35, 36]. Operation time tended to stabilize after certain training numbers by analyzing learning curves of 6 surgeons. Specifically, residents and intermediates showed stable operation time after 5 trainings, and 3–4 trainings, respectively. This is due to intermediates obtained laparoscopic skills and experience in bariatric surgery before training, allowing them to undergo the initial stage of the learning curve, resulting in an early onset of the surgical inflection point. Previous studies have confirmed that surgeons with more clinical experience were confirmed to affect the learning curve of LRYGB, with fewer operations and shorter operation time [37–39].

Surgical simulation and training require significant financial investment. The cost of laparoscopic intestinal-intestinal anastomosis was estimated at $1,500 based on the time difference between residents and attending surgeons in completing the procedure [40]. Two intestinal-intestinal anastomosis training of 5 residents showed an annual cost of $15,000. Our models are highly cost-effective, priced at only about $2,000, with a reusable laparoscopic surgery platform and organ models as their core components. The training process doesn’t require expensive anastomotic staplers but only suturing materials and hydrogel components. While other models require higher cost. For example, animal models purchased for the skills lab can only be used once, with each model costs approximately $600–800. Additional expenses such as veterinary anesthesia and animal labs also need to be considered.

Our study has several Limitations. Firstly, the relatively small sample size of 16 surgeons and should be expanded for comprehensive effectiveness evaluation in future research. Secondly, the silicone stomach and small intestine in our models have a deficient multi-layered and porous structure, and silicone stomach exhibits high friction resistance. Thirdly, the surgical skills acquired through training need to be further validated in realistic surgical environments. These limitations are expected to be addressed in the future with continuously improved technologies, such as the ongoing research on hydrogel models.

Conclusions

Our model is the first 3D-printed surgical training platform designed to replicate the key steps of LRYGB, offering realistic mechanical properties and exceptional usability. These models can reduce the learning curve, making them ideal for surgical training and skill assessment. As a result, they serve as a viable alternative to porcine models, addressing the limitations inherent in traditional training models. Notably, it is economical and not constrained by ethics or cost-related factors. Crucially, these models meet the demand for repeated use by numerous trainees.
