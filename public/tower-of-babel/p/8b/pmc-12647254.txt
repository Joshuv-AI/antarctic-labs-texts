Heavy metal contamination in urban agriculture: evidence from Nairobi
Authors: Mike Murphy, Githenya Wachira, Cecilia Onyango, Vivian Hoffmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgricultural production in urban areas plays an important role in food systems in low- and middle-income countries but also may also be subject to significant environmental hazards. We analyze samples of leafy greens grown on farms in Nairobi County selected via random geographical sampling for three heavy metals harmful to human health (lead, cadmium, and mercury). The mean levels of contamination are 0.68 ppm for lead, 0.09 ppm for cadmium, and 0.11 ppm for mercury. Spatial analysis shows that crops grown closer to roadways have higher levels of lead contamination and those grown near industrial sites have higher levels of mercury. We disaggregate our sample and test native greens and kale sourced from outside Nairobi as potential substitutes for urban-grown kale but find similar contamination levels. We estimate that 71% of adults and 69% of children in our sample are exposed to lead in excess of daily reference levels, with 12% of adults exceeding levels for cadmium and 52% exceeding levels for mercury via leafy greens alone. Using representative data for Nairobi and results from sampling leafy greens from local wholesale markets, we estimate similar dietary exposure levels for the population of the city as a whole. Our findings demonstrate the importance of systematic surveillance of foods in LMICs for heavy metals and the need to identify and mitigate sources of contamination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11356-025-37030-x.Keywords: Heavy metals, Cadmium, Lead, Mercury, Kenya, Urban agriculture

Introduction

Foodborne exposure to heavy metals has been identified as a major contributor to the global burden of disease, with the greatest impact occurring in low-income regions (Gibb et al. 2019). The term “heavy metals” within the environmental health literature refers to high-density metallic elements which are toxic to humans and animals even in low quantities. These hazardous materials can enter into food webs via transfer from soils or irrigation water to crops, leading to human exposure when the food is consumed (Gall et al. 2015; Xiong et al. 2014). Consumption in turn can disturb metabolic function, resulting in negative health impacts including neurological and cardiovascular damage and even death (Lamas et al. 2023; Rai et al. 2019). Exposure to lead in particular has been the subject of significant study and has remained high in low- and middle-income countries despite the widespread removal of lead from gasoline, imposing a significant global economic burden in the form of direct health costs and long-run pernicious effects on children’s intellectual development (Attina and Trasande 2013; Larsen and Sánchez-Triana 2023). However, the relative importance of different sources of exposure to lead and other heavy metals remains poorly understood, due in part to the diversity of these pathways and a lack of representative data on contamination levels in foods, consumer products, and the environment.

In this paper, we contribute to filling this gap by describing contamination of dark leafy greens grown by urban agricultural producers in Nairobi, Kenya, with three heavy metals (lead, cadmium, and mercury). We focus on this food group due to its widespread consumption and potential for heavy metal contamination. Dark leafy greens are the most widely consumed type of vegetable in Kenya, with 74% of households in a nationally representative survey reporting consumption over the past 7 days (Ryckman et al. 2024). Further, due to the high nutritional value and relative affordability of this food group, increasing consumption of dark leafy greens among the population in general, and infants and young children in particular, has been identified as a cost-effective strategy to address micronutrient deficiencies and is the target of ongoing public health initiatives (Ryckman et al. 2024; GAIN 2024). Alongside their nutritional benefits, however, many dark leafy green foods have the attribute common to plants of the Brassica genus of being significant accumulators of heavy metals, to the extent that they have been proposed for use in bioremediation of highly contaminated soils (Bortoloti and Baron 2022).

Our study is part of a growing literature documenting the level of heavy metal contamination in informal agricultural production in low and middle-income countries, including several studies conducted in Nairobi County, which we summarize here. Gallaher and coauthors (2013) tested greens from households engaged in sack gardening, a micro-scale urban farming technique practiced in Nairobi’s informal settlements. They report concentrations of lead and arsenic above or near Codex maximum levels and lower concentrations of cadmium. Karanja and co-authors (2012) sampled crops from two sites with a long history of wastewater use. They find concentrations of lead two orders of magnitude above the Codex limit and cadmium one order of magnitude above the limit. Mutune et al. (2014) studied toxin levels in ten indigenous crops across 25 sites in urban and peri-urban Nairobi. They report some evidence of soils and vegetables exceeding reference levels in areas close to industrial sites, though in most cases they find lower levels of contamination. Kinuthia et al. (2020) tested wastewater and soil sampled from Nairobi’s industrial area. They report that lead levels exceeded regulatory limits at five of the eight sites, and that soil taken from drainage channels exceeded limits for a range of heavy metals, which could contribute to contamination of crops grown by urban agricultural producers who use water sourced from these open channels for irrigation.

Our work makes four primary contributions to this literature. First, rather than targeting specific sites or populations, we develop and implement a geographic sampling strategy to generate a more representative sample of producers of leafy greens within urban Nairobi than previous methods which relied on convenience sampling or targeted sites around known pollution sources. Second, we compare contamination levels between urban-grown kale, urban-grown indigenous leafy greens, and kale sourced from outside of Nairobi County, to assess whether any of these could be promoted as lower risk option. Third, we combine contamination results with primary and secondary consumption data to generate estimates of dietary exposure to heavy metals through these foods. Finally, we investigate potential sources of this contamination through spatial analysis.

Methods

The primary source of data for this analysis comes from a sample of farmers growing dark leafy greens within Nairobi County. We carried out short in-person interviews with these individuals to understand growing practices and household consumption patterns and purchased samples of their produce. Samples were processed and analyzed to assess the level of heavy metal contamination at the Kenya Plant Health Inspectorate Service (KEPHIS). We additionally purchased kale sourced from outside of Nairobi from vendors at agricultural wholesale markets and tested these samples to generate a comparison with our urban sample. In this section, we describe how these datasets were generated and the procedures used for laboratory analysis.

Producer selection

Urban agriculture in Nairobi is typically informal, with producers growing on small plots or in container gardens (Gallaher et al. 2013). Since, to our knowledge, no systematic data on informal farms in Nairobi County exists, we used grid-based geographical sampling to select a representative group of urban producers. To do so, we first consulted locally based experts to understand which areas of the city were being used for agricultural production. Field staff were then sent to these locations to confirm that agricultural plots were present and currently under cultivation. Through this exercise, we determined that urban plots were cultivated primarily near the Nairobi River and its tributaries (which provide a source of irrigation water) on the western and eastern sides of the dense city center. Using this information, we defined two rectangular sample areas on either side of the city center (Fig. 1).

Spatial sampling area

The yellow areas on either side of Fig. 1 show the maximum extent of the sampling area, with the Nairobi River overlaid in red. We divided each of these rectangles into squares of length 1/10,000 of a degree (approximately 100 m) and then excluded those outside of Nairobi County or more than 1 km from the river. We then randomly sampled these grid squares with equal probability.

A list of GPS locations representing the midpoint of the selected grid squares was divided among the data collection team, who visited each location to carry out interviews. Enumerators traveled to each cell in turn, and if a plot was present at the location, they proceeded to locate the individual farming that plot. Sites were eligible for inclusion in the study if the farmer grew either Brassica oleracea (kale)1 or an indigenous leafy green crop.2 If eligible, the enumerator sought informed consent and coordinated with the farmer to arrange time for a short interview. If there was no plot present at the location, but a plot was present in an adjacent area (i.e., could be directly observed by the enumerator), then that plot was included, and the same procedures were followed to confirm eligibility, identify and consent the farmer, and conduct an interview. If no plot was present at the site—or if the site was not publicly accessible—the enumerator noted the status of the point and then proceeded to the next randomly ordered point on the list. This was repeated by each enumerator until the team successfully identified the target sample size of 100 growing areas.

Producer interview

Once a plot had been selected, the enumerator identified the person in charge of cultivation and invited them to participate in a short interview. The respondent was asked to provide some basic personal information (their age, marital status, primary occupation, sources of income, and education level) as well as details on the composition of their household. The main survey module consisted of questions about production on the sampled plot. Farmers were also asked to provide information on whether they consumed the vegetables produced on their plot and, if so, how much household members of different ages consumed in a typical week, and about their perceptions of various health risks associated with their crop. Following the interview, the enumerator then requested permission to take samples of the respondent’s crop, soil, and irrigation water (if used).

Soil, water, and crop sampling

Ten 100-g soil samples from a depth of 0–25 cm were collected from 10 different locations on the plot and combined to form one composite sample per for each farmer. One kilogram of each of the target crops (kale and indigenous leafy greens) grown by the farmer was collected from a minimum of five sampling locations each. Farmers were compensated for the value of this sample. A total of 500 ml of irrigation water was collected from up to two sites: the water source (river, well, etc.) and the irrigation outlet, if applicable.

Sampling of greens from wholesale markets

Enumerators visited Wakulima and Muthurwa markets and asked kale vendors from which county they had sourced this produce. One kilogram of kale was purchased from each vendor who indicated this had been sourced from outside of Nairobi.

Laboratory analysis

Soil, produce, and water samples were transported to KEPHIS Headquarters in Nairobi for laboratory analysis in airtight containers immediately following collection. Upon receipt, crop samples were oven-dried at 60 °C for 3 days and then manually crushed into small fragments before grinding using a laboratory grinding mill to homogenize. Soil samples were likewise dried for 24 h and then ground using a soil grinder.

Samples were processed using microwave-assisted digestion (Environmental Protection Agency, Method 3052, SW-846) prior to analysis by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Representative dried samples of soil (0.5 g), plant tissue (0.5 g), and water (45 ml) were transferred to ultra-clean and dry polymeric microwave vessels, to which concentrated nitric acid (9 ml) and hydrogen peroxide (4 ml) were added. Samples were left to react for approximately 5 min prior to sealing the vessels to allow any gases to escape. Vessels were heated gradually in the microwave oven to 150 °C over a period of 20 min, held at that temperature for 20 min, and then cooled. After cooling, samples were transferred to 250-ml volumetric flasks filled to the mark using de-ionized water and mixed well by shaking. Aliquots were transferred to labeled vials and placed in the ICP-MS for measurement.

Samples were then analyzed by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Calibration was conducted using spiked standard solutions of 0, 10, 20, 30, 50, and 100 ppb. Standards were included with each batch of samples processed to ensure quality control. Recovery rates (%) of standard reference materials for each element were ([min–max], mean): [0.92–1.34], 1.01 for Cd; [0.81–1.16], 1.03 for Hg; and [0.94–1.10], 1.007 for Pb. Average pollution levels of blank samples run mid-analysis were 0.11 ppb (Cd), 2.4 ppb (Hg), and 0.39 (Pb). Relative standard deviations of ICP-MS replicates across 8 averaged 0.038 (Cd), 0.159 (Hg), and 0.016 (Pb). These values include one outlier for Hg; omitting that observation, the average RSD for Hg was 0.026.

Secondary data

To assess dietary exposure, we complement the laboratory results and primary survey data with secondary, regionally representative survey data from the Kenya Integrated Household Budget Survey. We use spatial data from OpenStreetMap (OSM) to explore potential environmental sources of heavy metal contamination (OpenStreetMap, 2025) .

Statistical analysis

Ordinary least squares regression is used to characterize the relationship between heavy metal contamination in greens and the soil and irrigation water used to grow them and the influence of distance from potential pollution sources.

For lead and cadmium, we find no statistically significant association between either soil or water levels of the respective metals and the levels found in crop samples, and our point estimates are approximately zero. For mercury, we estimate a positive but not statistically significant association between soil levels and levels in produce and a positive and statistically significant (p < 0.10) association between levels in the water sample and in the leafy greens that were tested, providing suggestive evidence that irrigation water could be a potential source of mercury contamination. The lack of a relationship between contamination in soil and water versus crops is not surprising, as the bioavailability of heavy metals is mediated by a number of factors, including pH, organic matter, and the phosphorus content of the soil (Tangahu et al. 2009).

Potential sources of heavy metal contamination

Given the health harms implied by these levels of exposure, there is a need to understand the sources of this heavy metal contamination. While the study was not designed to undertake attribution analysis, we are able to exploit available secondary data on features of urban Nairobi to explore correlations between observed contamination levels in our produce samples and potential point sources of heavy metals.

We match geographic coordinates for each of the farms in our sample to land use data taken from OpenStreetMap (OSM), an open-source dataset with coverage of many spatial features of urban areas. For each point, we determine the shortest distance to two potential pollution sources: traffic and industrial sites. For traffic, we subset roads and pathways in the OSM data to those large enough to be used for cars and other large vehicles. For industrial sites, we use the minimum distance to any polygon in the OSM data where the use of the land is classified as “industrial.” To avoid confounding due to variation in the absorption rates of different leafy greens, we restrict this analysis to kale samples.

Using ordinary least squares regression, we observe a marginally statistically significant (p = 0.09) correlation between lead levels in kale and proximity to the nearest road (Table 5). For every km further from a road, contamination in kale is lower by 1 ppm. Graphing this relationship in Fig. 3 shows it to be non-linear, and when a quadratic term is introduced to the regression, the relationship is more precisely estimated (see Table 8 in the Appendix).

Relative contamination levels of potential substitutes

We next explore whether heavy metal contamination differs by crop species or by place of cultivation. Identification of lower-contamination alternatives within this food group could enable exposure reduction strategies. Farmers could be encouraged to produce crops, and consumers encouraged to consume foods, which accumulate heavy metals at lower rates but have similar nutritional and agronomic properties. Urban production, if associated with higher contamination, could be discouraged or banned.

We compare the distributions of contamination for each of the heavy metals in samples of urban-grown kale (N = 88), native greens (N = 37), and kale produced outside the city and purchased from vendors at Wakulima (43%) and Muthurwa (57%) wholesale produce markets (N = 60). Each graph includes a dashed line indicating the reference level for a maximum daily reference level for the age group plotted.8 For lead, we use the interim reference values published by the US Food & Drug Administration (FDA) of 8.8 µg/day for women of child-bearing age and 2.2 µg/day for children under 5 (Flannery and Middleton 2022). For cadmium, we use the lower bound of the FDA reference level (FDA 2025) and assume an average adult weight of 70 kg. For mercury, we convert the FDA’s recommended maximum weekly dosage of mercury if consuming a single portion of fish (0.46 µg/g) to a daily maximum exposure.9

Based on these estimates, the median adult in our sample receives a dietary exposure of 26.6 µg of lead per day, while the median child under 5 would receive 7.9 µg per day solely from consumption of leafy greens. As a result, 71% of adults in our sample and 69% of children under 5 are estimated to exceed the FDA-recommended limits of 8.8 µg/day and 2.2 µg/day, respectively. For cadmium, the median adult receives a dietary exposure from leafy greens of 4.6 µg/day with 12% of the sample exceeding the FDA standard, while for mercury, the median adult consumes 5.1 µg/day, with 52% exceeding the FDA maximum.

Lastly, we generate estimates of total population exposure to these heavy metals among the population of Nairobi from the consumption of leafy greens. To do so, we use consumption values generated in the previous section and apply the mean value of each heavy metal from our vendor sample to generate a daily exposure quantity for the population as a whole (Table 10 in the Appendix). Under this methodology, we find similar results, with a majority exceeding the FDA threshold for lead (65% of adults, 69% of children under 5), 21% exceeding the threshold for cadmium, and 59% exceeding the threshold for mercury. While these results are necessarily approximate, they demonstrate that if the levels of contamination present in our vendor samples reflect overall contamination levels in Nairobi, dietary exposure to heavy metals from consumption of leafy greens may pose a significant health hazard for the population.

Conclusion

Evidence presented in this paper shows that the heavy metal contamination in leafy greens grown by urban farmers in Nairobi constitutes a significant health hazard. This finding concords with previous studies documenting heavy metals in vegetables grown in the city, which were based on less representative samples. Mean crop lead levels were similar to those in the three (of nine) kale-growing sites with the highest contamination as reported by Gallaher et al. (2013) at 0.32 ppm to 0.59 ppm. They fall within the range of kale contamination (0.29 ppm to 3.06 ppm) reported by Karanja and coauthors (2012) and for vegetables (0.09 to 2.5 ppm) reported by Mutune et al. (2014).

Our additional analysis comparing indigenous greens versus kale and urban-grown kale versus that sourced from outside of the city suggests that the problem is specific neither to a specific leafy green crop nor to urban farming within Nairobi. Consumption patterns of sampled households are similar to those reported in nationally representative data for Kenya, indicating that exposure to heavy metals through leafy greens could be widespread.

A limitation of this paper is the lack of specific information on where produce sourced from wholesale markets was grown. We relied on vendor reports that the produce was grown outside of Nairobi and did not attempt to trace it back to the production site. Future research could remedy this shortcoming by analyzing a representative sample of leafy greens (and other foods susceptible to heavy metal contamination) from markets across a larger geographical area. This approach has been used to assess lead contamination of a number of consumer products across 25 LMICs (Sargsyan et al. 2024) and to describe patterns of aflatoxin contamination in Kenyan maize products across product categories, regions, and time (Barasa et al. 2023).

A second limitation is that while we detect a weakly significant relationship between mercury in irrigation water and kale, we are unable to identify the source of heavy metal contamination in the sampled crops. The lack of an observed correlation between contamination in soil and crops grown in that soil does not exclude soil as the source of contamination, as a number of soil characteristics on which data were not collected mediate plants’ uptake of heavy metals (Tangahu et al. 2009). A previous analysis relating heavy metals in Nairobi-grown kale to that in the soil in which it was grown similarly found no significant relationship (Gallaher et al., 2013). Another possibility is that the sampled crops absorbed heavy metals from atmospheric pollution. This mechanism has been reported for crops grown near an industrial pollution source (Uzu et al. 2010; Schreck et al. 2012; Xiong et al. 2014). Informal used lead-acid battery recycling operations are known to be a significant source of airborne lead pollution in Nairobi (Ondayo et al. 2016). Future work could use isotopic composition analysis to investigate the source of contamination (Forsyth et al. 2019).

High levels of lead contamination in leafy greens grown in and around Nairobi reported in this paper, combined with widespread and frequent consumption of this food, imply an urgent need to understand the sources of this pollution and develop policies to mitigate population health risks due to dietary exposure.
