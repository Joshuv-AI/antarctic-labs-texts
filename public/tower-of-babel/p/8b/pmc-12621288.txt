Bioactive Compounds Extraction From Moringa oleifera Leaves: A Comparative Study of Vacuum‐Assisted and Bed‐Stirred Extractions
Authors: Olívia J S Gomes, Anabela Leitão, Hermínio C de Sousa, Licínio M Gando‐Ferreira, Mara E M Braga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Moringa oleifera leaves are a rich source of bioactive compounds with antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties. However, the efficient extraction of these compounds depends on optimized process conditions and suitable extraction techniques that ensure high yield with the compound's stability. Conventional extraction methods often expose compounds to heat and an oxidative environment, which can degrade sensitive molecules. To address this, this study evaluated the extraction efficiency of these bioactive compounds using a hydroethanolic solvent through vacuum‐assisted extraction (VAE) method. For comparison purposes, the bed‐stirred extraction (BSE) was also performed. A design of experiments was applied to optimize the process, identifying the solid‐solvent ratio (1:150, g/mL) as a key factor for both extraction methods, with similar optimal ethanol concentrations of around 80% for BSE and VAE methods. VAE significantly increased the recovery of total phenolic and flavonoid compounds by 37% and 48%, respectively, and four times the vitamin content compared to BSE. This is due to the enhanced penetration of solvents and the protection of bioactive compounds from oxidation under vacuum conditions. VAE extracts exhibited stronger free radical scavenging in DPPH and ABTS assays (23 ± 1 and 28 ± 1 µg/mL, respectively), with IC50 values up to 75% lower than BSE extracts, indicating higher oxidative stress reduction potential near the BHT reference. The VAE extract presented anti‐inflammatory activity (measured by lipoxygenase inhibition assay), with an IC50 of 52 ± 1 µg/mL near the acetylsalicylic acid (43.3 ± 2.4 µg/mL). The enhanced bioactivity of VAE extracts is attributed to their phytochemical profile, which has higher concentrations of phenolic compounds and vitamins.Keywords: anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant activity, Moringa oleifera, stirred water bath extraction, vacuum‐assisted extraction

Introduction

Efficient extraction of bioactive compounds from natural sources throughout process optimization is crucial to use their full potential and maximize their bioavailability and efficacy regarding nutritional and nutraceutical properties. Compounds including phenolics, flavonoids, vitamins, and other antioxidants, have been widely studied for their numerous health benefits, such as anti‐inflammatory, antimicrobial, and anticancer properties (Samtiya et al. 2021). Additionally, bioactive compounds significantly reduce oxidative stress, promote cardiovascular health, and boost the immune system. Their incorporation into functional foods, nutraceuticals, and pharmaceuticals can enhance nutritional value and contribute to the prevention of various chronic diseases (Pawase et al. 2024).

In this way, Moringa oleifera, a tree native to the southern Himalayas in north‐eastern India, offers a variety of edible and highly nutritious components, including flowers, fruits, seeds, leaves, and roots (Dhakad et al. 2019). Traditionally, moringa leaves have been consumed in Angola and included in the local diet as fresh or cooked food. In some studies, M. oleifera leaves have demonstrated inhibitory effects on the growth of diverse pathogens, including Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Arora and Arora 2021; Kou et al. 2018; Xiao et al. 2020). The leaves are particularly rich in essential nutrients, such as polysaccharides, polyphenols, proteins, and elevated levels of vitamins A and C, alongside different flavonoids (Elghandour et al. 2023). Extracts derived from the moringa leaves, particularly those obtained using ethanol, methanol, and ethyl acetate, have demonstrated significant antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and hypoglycemic activities (Arora and Arora 2021; Kaur et al. 2020).

Furthermore, the leaves contain other compounds with antioxidant potential, such as chlorophyll, ascorbic acid, and vitamin E, along with essential minerals like iron, potassium, and calcium, which contribute to countering degenerative processes associated with stress (Dhakad et al. 2019; Sarkar et al. 2023). The interaction of these compounds with free radicals serves to mitigate their damaging impact on cells (Kaur et al. 2020). These characteristics make M. oleifera leaves ideal for studying the extraction of bioactive compounds, addressing global health challenges such as oxidative stress and chronic diseases, and improving nutritional conditions.

Several extraction methods have been applied to M. oleifera leaves to obtain bioactive compounds, with the choice of technique and solvent significantly influencing yield and composition. Conventional methods, such as maceration and soxhlet extraction, have been widely used in the laboratory, employing ethanol, methanol, or water as solvents due to their efficiency in extracting polyphenols, flavonoids, and vitamins (Gil‐Martín et al. 2022). Ultrasound‐assisted extraction (Lin et al. 2021) and microwave‐assisted extraction (Nebolisa et al. 2023) have also been investigated, demonstrating improved extraction efficiency and reduced processing time compared to traditional methods. Additionally, supercritical fluid extraction (Rodríguez‐Pérez et al. 2016) using CO2 has been explored, particularly for extracting lipophilic compounds such as carotenoids from moringa. While alternative techniques offer enhanced efficiency, their high operational costs and complex equipment make industrial‐scale applications challenging. Traditional extraction methods, such as stirred batch extraction, have been widely used due to their simplicity and cost‐effectiveness (Yerena‐Prieto et al. 2022). Stirred bed extraction promotes mass transfer through agitation and thermal treatment, making it a popular choice for laboratory and large‐scale extractions (Braga et al. 2013; Ponphaiboon et al. 2023). The traditional extraction methods have drawbacks such as extended extraction times, excessive solvent volume use, and the risk of degrading heat‐sensitive compounds at high temperatures. High temperatures, significantly above 75°C, can cause oxidation or degradation of these compounds when used with conventional extraction techniques (Usman et al. 2023).

In contrast, the association of vacuum with traditional extraction methods has emerged as a promising alternative, enhancing extraction efficiency and bioactive compound preservation. These advantages of vacuum‐assisted extraction (VAE) are based on the enhanced release of solutes from plant matrices by accelerating the solid‐liquid mass transfer with a vacuum ranging from 0 to 1000 mbar (Wang et al. 2014). Extraction can occur at lower temperatures by operating below the solvent's boiling point since this process changes the solvent's boiling temperature according to its saturated vapor pressure. Additionally, the absence of oxygen prevents oxidative degradation, further preserving extract quality (Gil‐Martín et al. 2022). Despite these advantages, equipment for a large scale is also a challenge due to vacuum control, which may limit its use only for small scale, justifying a lack of information about scale and costs in the scientific literature. No studies have explored VAE methods for M. oleifera leaves. To address this gap, this study aims to evaluate the effects of applying a vacuum on the quality and recovery of bioactive compounds from M. oleifera leaves using a VAE by comparing it with the stirred batch extraction method.

The optimization of extraction processes intends to maximize extraction yield, phenolic, flavonoid, vitamin contents, and antioxidant activity (IC50, DPPH method) of the extracts through the extraction process parameters such as ethanol concentration, temperature, and solid‐solvent ratio, using response surface analysis. Furthermore, the phytochemical composition, mineral and vitamin content, antioxidant (DPPH and ABTS methods), and anti‐inflammatory activity (lipoxygenase inhibition) (IC50) of the extracts obtained under optimal conditions were measured and compared. This work contributes to understanding the potential of M. oleifera extracts as natural sources of health‐promoting compounds for functional foods and nutraceuticals.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

The extraction process used solvents, bi‐distilled water, and anhydrous ethanol (Fisher Chemical, USA). Chromatographic and spectrophotometric assays used the following organic solvents: methanol, glacial acetic acid (both Fisher Chemical, USA), acetonitrile (Honeywell, USA), and trifluoroacetic acid (Carlo Erba, Italy). For extract characterization, Folin and Ciocalteu's phenol reagent (Fluka, USA), sodium carbonate, aluminium chloride anhydrous, sodium nitrite, and sodium hydroxide (all Merck, Germany) were used.

Standards of phenolic acids were used for identification and quantification: chlorogenic acid, catechin, caffeic acid, p‐coumaric acid (98%–99%, Sigma, USA), gallic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid, rutin (96%–99%, Fluka, USA), epicatechin (Biochemika, Finland), and trans‐ferulic acid (Aldrich, USA). Vitamins A, E, C, and B‐complex (B1, B2, and B9) are from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA), and other compounds for reference are acetylsalicylic acid (>99%, Fisher Chemical, USA), dexamethasone phosphate (Edol, Portugal), BHT (99%, Acros Organics, Belgium), and Trolox (98%), β‐carotene (93%), and BHA (≥98.5%) from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA).

For antioxidant assays, DPPH (2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl, 95%) and ABTS (2,2′‐azinobis‐(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid), 98%), both from Sigma (USA), were employed. The anti‐inflammatory assays used lipoxygenase enzyme from soybean (99%, Fluka, USA), linoleic acid (99%, Aldrich, USA), and potassium phosphate buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA).

Plant Material

M. oleifera leaves were used since this part of the plant is already used by the local population of Angola in the local diet. Fresh leaves were collected from 5‐year‐old M. oleifera trees grown in Luanda, Angola (8°57′24.9″S, 13°13′02.9″E). The botanical identification was made at the Botanical Center of Agostinho Neto University, with an exsiccatae number of 3128.

They were washed and air‐dried in natural environmental conditions at approximately 30°C for two weeks until reaching equilibrium weight, as the traditional method used in Angola. The dried leaves were ground into a fine powder using a coffee grinder. The resulting powder was stored in vacuum‐sealed bags at 4°C, under dark conditions, to maintain quality and protect it from exposure to light and moisture. The powder was used within a year of preparation to ensure its stability and preserve its bioactive properties.

Methods of Bioactive Compounds Extraction From M. oleifera Leaves

Vacuum‐assisted extraction (VAE)—An extraction system was used for VAE, including a heat bath, a glass vessel coupled to a solvent trap to prevent solvent loss, and a condenser tube connected to a vacuum pump (Büchi Rotavapor R‐210, USA). The pressure was controlled at a fixed vacuum of 500 mbar to eliminate the oxygen from the atmosphere of the extraction volume without removing the solvent, with a total volume of 30 mL. Ethanol concentration, solid/solvent ratio, and temperature ranges were determined based on the variables defined in Section 2.3.2. Figure S2 illustrates both extraction systems.

Bed‐stirred extraction (BSE)—A thermostatic stirred water bath (model 18L, VWR, Portugal) was used for BSE at 120 rpm. The percentage of ethanol in water, solid/solvent ratio, and temperature ranges were based on the combination of variables and selected in Section 2.3.2, and 30 mL of solvent was used for each extraction.

For both extraction methods, the liquid extract was collected and separated from the solid residue by centrifuging (Medibas+, model 2741R, UK) at 4000 rpm for 15 min. Then, the solvent was removed from the extract using a rotary evaporator (Büchi Rotavapor R‐210, USA) to obtain a concentrated extract of approximately an average of 4 mg/ml. For the extract characterization, this concentration was considered for further analysis. To calculate the yield of extracts, the residual solvent was removed by a vacuum oven, and it was quantified based on the dried extract over the initial raw material mass, on a dry basis.

Screening of Solvent

The first set of experiments was based on solvent mixture screening. Six different ethanol‐water concentrations were used: 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%. The initial solvent screening extractions were performed with the BSE method at 60°C for 2 h and were conducted in triplicate. Samples of M. oleifera powdered leaf were precisely weighed into filter paper bags and then placed in vials in a solid‐solvent ratio of 1:50 (g/mL) with 20 mL of solvent. After the screening, the concentration of solvent that leads to higher extraction yield, phenolic and flavonoid contents, and higher antioxidant activity was used as the center point of the design of experiments (second set of experiments) to optimize the extraction conditions for BSE and VAE methods.

Design of Experiments (DOE)

A three‐variable‐two‐level full factorial design (FFD) was implemented to determine the optimal conditions for extracting bioactive compounds from M. oleifera leaves. The independent variables considered were ethanol concentration (X1), solid‐to‐solvent ratio (X2), and temperature (X3). The optimal combination of variables was determined to maximize yield, total phenolic compounds, flavonoid content, and vitamin concentration and minimize IC50 concentration in antioxidant activity.

Based on the solvent screening experiments, the center point of solvent concentration was defined, and for DOE planning, a concentration below and one above were selected. The experimental design included 24 experiments Table (S1), with the variables encoded at two levels: −1 and +1. The percentage of ethanol in water ranged from 70% to 90%, the solid‐to‐solvent ratio varied from 1:50 to 1:150 g/mL, and the temperature ranged from 40 to 60°C. Variable value ranges were selected based on literature data and preliminary experiments.

Extraction Kinetics

From the design of experiments, the extraction kinetics were performed for both extraction methods, BSE, and VAE. Total extraction yields were calculated on a dry basis, as the ratio between the total extract and the raw material mass. For the extraction kinetics, the kinetic parameters were calculated based on the literature (Rodríguez‐Pérez et al. 2016), where the kinetic curve was divided into three phases: (1) the constant extraction rate (CER) period, governed by convection and characterized by the removal of surface solutes; (2) the falling extraction rate (FER) period, where mass transfer shifts to diffusion control; and (3) the diffusion‐controlled rate (DCR) period, where extraction is solely diffusion‐driven. The parameters corresponding to each phase were estimated by linear regression analysis of the kinetic experimental data obtained.

Characterisation of M. oleifera Leaves Extracts

All extracts obtained were characterized according to the following methodology, using at least a triplicate for each measurement.

Determination of yield (%)—The yield is quantified as a relative proportion in percentage, by dividing the weight of the dried extract by a known mass of the leaf sample.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)—Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin‐Ciocalteu method (Vieitez et al. 2018), with absorbance measured at 765 nm on a JASCO V‐530 spectrophotometer. Initially, 20 µL of extract was mixed with 1580 µL of ultrapure water, 100 µL of diluted Folin reagent, and after 5 to 8 min, 300 µL of sodium carbonate solution (33%). The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 40°C and in the dark. The results were expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) in mg/g dry basis (d.b.).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)—Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminium chloride colorimetric method, as described by León‐Félix et al. (2017). Briefly, 20 µL of the extract was combined with 800 µL of ultrapure water and 60 µL of 5% NaNO2. After 5 min, 60 µL of 10% AlCl3 solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 6 min. Subsequently, 400 µL of 1 M NaOH and 660 µL of ultrapure water were added. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a JASCO V‐530 spectrophotometer. Results were expressed as catechin equivalents (CE) in mg/g dry basis (d.b.).

Antioxidant capacity (DPPH and ABTS)—The antioxidant activity of the samples was assessed using the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay (Almeida et al. 2021). The 2,2′‐azinobis‐(3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid) (ABTS•+) radical scavenging activity was determined according to (Re et al. 1999). The results are presented as the concentration of the sample required to scavenge 50% of the DPPH or ABTS radical cation (IC50). The IC50 value was expressed in a concentration of the extract (µg/mL).

Anti‐inflammatory activity—The study evaluated the anti‐inflammatory properties of M. oleifera leaf extracts by measuring their lipoxygenase inhibition activity according to an experimental method adapted from the literature with slight modifications (Seabra et al. 2012). The experiment tested three extract concentrations using linoleic acid as the substrate (100 µM). The enzyme and substrate solutions were dissolved in potassium phosphate buffer 0.1 M (pH 9), and the inhibitory effects were measured by comparing the samples with a control (pure ethanol). The IC50 values were then calculated based on the inhibition percentages. The percentage inhibition of the enzyme activity was calculated based on the comparison among the samples, 3 standards (quercetin, acetylsalicylic acid, and dexamethasone phosphate, 100 µg/mL), and the control. Each inhibitor concentration that gave 50% inhibition (IC50) was interpolated from the outline of the inhibition percentage reported as a function of the inhibitor concentration (n = 4).

Determination of water‐soluble and fat‐soluble vitamins—For the analysis of fat‐soluble vitamins (vitamins A and E), a chromatographic method was used, as reported by Garai (2017). A Luna C18 column (150 mm × 4 mm, 5 µm) was used with a mobile phase of methanol and bi‐distilled water in a ratio of 95:5 (v/v), at an isocratic flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The injection volume for all samples was 10 µL. Vitamin A was detected at a wavelength of 325 nm, while vitamin E was detected at 293 nm. Vitamin A and E content were quantified using a calibration curve (R2 = 0.992 and R2 = 0.998, respectively).

As for the water‐soluble vitamins (vitamin C and complex B—B1, B2, and B9), Seal et al.'s method was followed (Seal et al. 2019). The analysis used an Ultra‐fast Liquid Chromatograph (UFLC) system coupled with a DAD (SPDM20A, Shimadzu, Japan) detector. Vitamins C and B1 were detected at a wavelength of 245 nm, while vitamins B2 and B9 were detected at 275 nm. A reversed‐phase C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) was used, with the mobile phase consisting of aqueous trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 0.01%) (Solvent A) and acetonitrile (Solvent B). A gradient elution program was applied as follows: 1% B and 99% A for 5 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min; a linear increase to 25% B over 16 min; 45% B maintained for 8 min; followed by a return from 45% to 1% B within 5 min. The mobile phase was then re‐equilibrated to the initial condition (1% B, 99% A) and held for 1 min before the next injection. Calibration curves for vitamin standards were prepared with concentrations from 0.100 to 0.500 mg/mL and linearity with R2 values exceeding 98%.

Determination of polyphenolic compounds—The analysis was performed using a UFLC chromatograph system coupled with a DAD detector (SPDM20A, Shimadzu, Japan). Separation was performed on a Luna C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm) maintained at 33°C. The mobile phase consisted of two solutions: A (1% acetic acid in water) and B (acetonitrile). A gradient elution method was used as follows, 5% B at the beginning, increasing to 25% B at 20 min, followed by an increase of 40% B at 30 min and 60% B at 35 min. At 40 min, the B solvent was reduced to 30%, and at 45 min to 5% B, held until 50 min. The injection volume was 10 µL, the flow rate was 0.5 mL/min, and the UV detector wavelength was 270 nm. Calibration curves were prepared with ten phenolic HPLC grade standards (chlorogenic acid, gallic acid, catechin, epicatechin, vanillic acid, syringic acid, caffeic acid, rutin, p‐coumaric acid, and trans‐ferulic acid) dissolved in water at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 mg/mL and R2 values exceeding 98%. The analysis criteria were based on each compound's retention time and spectrum (Gomes et al. 2024; Khalid et al. 2023).

Statistical Analysis

Experimental data obtained from Section 2.3 underwent analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Analysis Tool Pak to perform Excel data analysis (Microsoft 365, Excel 2311, USA). Mean comparisons were conducted using the least significant difference at a 5% significance level. The results obtained are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation of at least three measurements for all analytical determinations in this study.

The design of experiments (refer to Section 2.3.2) was performed using JMP software (Version Pro 7.0, SAS) to find the desirability function, response models, and surfaces. ANOVA evaluated the statistical significance of the main effects, interactions, quadratic terms, regression coefficients, and model fitting. The coefficient of determination R2 and adjusted coefficient of determination R2 adj checked model adequacy.

Results and Discussion

Extraction of Bioactive Compounds From M. oleifera Leaf Powder

Screening of the Solvent

The influence of the solvent concentration on the extraction of bioactive compounds was studied by conducting the process at a controlled temperature (60°C), fixed solid‐to‐solvent ratio (1:50 g/mL), and contact time of 2 h, while varying the water‐ethanol proportion. Six different solvent mixtures of ethanol and water were used from 0% to 100%. Figure 1a represents images of the extracts obtained. The color variation in the liquid extract ranges from light orange with pure water as a solvent to deep green with pure ethanol. The increase in green indicates the presence of chlorophylls, as also supported by the literature (Abebe 2023).

Images of M. oleifera leaf extracts obtained using different solvent mixtures of (A) ethanol and water and (B) their yield, total phenolic compounds, total flavonoid content on the (left axis), and antioxidant activity (IC50, dashed line) on the right axis.

Figure 1b shows the extraction yield (%), total phenolic compounds as TPC (GAE mg/g, d.b.), total flavonoid content as TFC (CE mg/g, d.b.) and antioxidant activity measured with DPPH (IC50, µg/mL). The highest extraction yield of M. oleifera leaves was observed with 80% ethanol (29.6% ± 1.2%), followed by 60% (22.7% ± 1.3%), and 40% (21.3% ± 0.3%), as shown in Figure 1b. Comparatively, 80% ethanol has a higher yield than 100% ethanol since the mixture with water in the solvent possibly extracted more water‐soluble sugars and/or fibers. From 0% to 40% ethanol, the yield, TPC, and TFC were similar, indicating that those ethanol concentrations did not affect the extracted mass and extract phenolic/flavonoid composition.

Above 60% ethanol in the solvent mixture, there is co‐extraction of some undesirable compounds such as chlorophyll, easily identified in Figure 1a, among others. This co‐extraction close to the 80% ethanol concentration can still be beneficial, contributing to the antioxidant activity and definitely to the increase in yield, but above this concentration, the compounds contribute negatively to the activity of the extract, especially in the case of BSE. Below 60%, TPC‐type compounds are not extracted due to their hydrophobic nature and their low affinity with the solvent. The ideal proportion, around 80% ethanol, reveals that the affinity of the extracted compounds, such as phenolics, flavonoids, and some vitamins is greater at this concentration, converging to IC50 values of interest.

The highest phenolic and flavonoid contents of 32.7 ± 0.4 GAE mg/g and 7.6 ± 0.2 CE mg/g were achieved with 80% ethanol, respectively, resulting in the lowest IC50 value of 120.5 ± 3.7 µg/mL. These extracts contain glycosylated phenolics, such as catechin, epicatechin, and rutin present in moringa extracts (as identified later in the optimized extracts), probably promoting this best IC50 value.

In contrast, TPC from pure water was lower at 22 ± 2 GAE mg/g (Figure 1b), highlighting the significant impact of solvent mixture choice on the efficiency of bioactive compound extraction. Stronger antioxidant activity is also related to other bioactive compounds, such as vitamins. The analysis of this content revealed that the concentration varied along the different ethanol mixtures. The data presented in Table 1 reveals variations in vitamin levels across different ethanol mixtures. Water‐soluble vitamins (B‐complex and C) generally showed higher concentrations in more aqueous mixtures, while fat‐soluble vitamins (A and E) showed significant differences in content in lower aqueous mixtures. Pure water and ethanol extracts had lower values than mixtures because both have strong hydrogen bonds. The polarity of ethanol facilitates the extraction of organic and inorganic substances, while its binary mixture with water promotes the dissolution by interaction with various functional groups present in M. oleifera leaf extracts (Gil‐Martín et al. 2022). Notably, the highest total vitamin content was found in an 80% ethanol mixture, mainly vitamins A and B9, indicating a stronger antioxidant activity as seen in IC50 concentrations, as shown in Table 1.

The trend in fat‐soluble vitamin concentrations indicates a solubility dependence on ethanol proportion. While vitamin A levels increased up to 80% ethanol, a decrease at 100% suggests that some plant components require minimal water for efficient extraction. Similarly, vitamin E extraction was enhanced with increasing ethanol concentration, reaching its peak at 100%. This highlights the role of ethanol in breaking hydrogen bonds and facilitating the release of lipophilic compounds. However, pure ethanol may lack sufficient polarity to extract all bioactive components effectively, explaining variations in extraction efficiency at different concentrations.

The combination of ethanol and water demonstrated a complementary effect in the extraction process. Ethanol disrupts the interactions between bioactive compounds and the plant matrix, enhancing the release of solutes, while water promotes matrix swelling, improving the solubility of hydrophilic compounds. As a result, ethanol‐water mixtures provide an optimal balance for extracting a wide range of bioactive compounds. Furthermore, the high dielectric constant of water increases the polarity index of ethanol in solution, further enhancing its extraction efficiency (Gil‐Martín et al. 2022).

The optimal ethanol‐water ratio for extracting bioactive compounds varies among plant species due to differences in phytochemical composition. Previous studies report similar findings, supporting the use of ethanol‐water mixtures for enhanced bioactive compound recovery. Abebe (2023) demonstrated that ethanol‐rich mixtures (60‐65%) maximized polyphenol and vitamin extraction in M. oleifera leaves, reinforcing the efficiency of this solvent system. These results suggest that ethanol‐water mixtures provide a balanced medium for extracting a broad spectrum of bioactive compounds, making them a preferred choice for optimizing extraction in M. oleifera leaves.

For the first set of tests aimed to screen the solvent, the ethanol concentration of 80% obtained a higher extract yield, phenolics and flavonoids, and it is a compromise between the quantities of the different vitamins.

Extraction Kinetics

Kinetic for BSE and VAE extraction curves are illustrated in Figure 2, with distinct phases marked. The extraction curves exhibit significant differences in both quantity and shape. Figure 2 shows the different trends of the results according to the extraction conditions, such as solid‐solvent ratio, temperature, and solvent concentration. These factors significantly influence the efficiency and yield of the extraction process. During the process's initial stage, the solvent phase's total concentration increases quickly, and the CER phase for VAE is faster than that of BSE under the same conditions. This is because vacuum‐induced bubble formation enhances liquid‐liquid mass transfer by agitating the solvent and accelerating the transfer of solid‐liquid mass (Wang et al. 2014). After the first stage, the extraction rate starts to decrease, and the diffusion phenomena of the solutes become dominant. In the third stage, the extraction yield versus time curves assume an asymptotic trend, indicating no significant variation in extraction yield (Rodríguez‐Pérez et al. 2016).

Kinetic yields (%) extraction of M. oleifera leaves for bed‐stirred extraction (BSE) and vacuum‐assisted extraction (VAE) at different ethanol concentrations (70% and 90%), solid‐to‐solvent ratios (1:50 and 1:150 g/mL), and temperatures (40°C and 60°C).

In Figure 2 for BSE results, the CER phase occurs in the first 10 min (Figure 2a, d) with an average extraction rate of 1.8% ± 0.4% (d.b./min). This is followed by the FER phase until about 30 min (0.6% ± 0.2%, d.b./min) and then the DCR phase (0.1% ± 0.1%, d.b./min). Higher temperatures (60°C vs. 40°C) lead to more efficient extraction, as seen in lower yields at 40°C, except for Figure 2c, also considering the errors. Maximum yield is reached in about 30 to 40 min for both temperatures, but increasing yield also raises energy consumption. Figure 2b shows distinct phases, with higher yields at 60°C compared to 40°C, highlighting the temperature's positive effect on extraction. Figure 2c indicates a steeper initial slope, greater CER phase, extended FER phase, and gradual DCR transition. The solid‐solvent ratio is higher, as reflected in the initial yield burst. Temperature also plays a significant role, with 60°C outperforming 40°C, particularly Figure 2d.

In Figure 2 for VAE results, the extraction profile shows higher efficiency compared to previous results (Figures 2e, h). The CER phase occurs in the first 10 min with a medium extraction rate of 2.0% ± 0.5%, followed by the FER phase until 20 min at 0.5% ± 0.2% and, finally, the DCR phase at 0.02% ± 0.01%. The yield increases quickly during the CER phase, peaking around 20 to 30 min. For lower solvent proportions (solid:solvent ratio of 1:50), the effect of temperature overrides the amount of solvent, but when there is more solvent in the system (solid:solvent ratio of 1:150), the effect of solvent is more pronounced than temperature, showing that the solubility of compounds in the solvent depends more on their affinity with the medium than on the physical parameter of temperature. It also happens in other studied systems in the literature (Kashaninejad et al. 2021; Zhou et al. 2023). The influence of the solid‐to‐liquid ratio is evident for 1:150, with higher ratios resulting in increased yields; however, at 90% ethanol (Figure 2h), there is a combined effect between temperature and the solid‐solvent ratio. In general, the effect of temperature is consistent, with 60°C leading to higher yields. This could be due to the direct effect of the increased temperature or the increased molecular diffusion coefficient, decreased fluid viscosity, and increased mass transfer coefficient. However, if the temperature gets too high, the bioactive compounds can be degraded, reducing yield.

Although Figure 2 shows that the yield increases with temperature, in Figure 2g for VAE, the lower temperature of 40°C slightly increased the yield. For this curve shape, which has the highest CER phase in a short time, it is possible to predict that almost all raw material was already extracted, leading to the fast extraction process in these conditions. Table (S2) presents the kinetic parameters comparatively for all kinetic extraction conditions. These results clearly show that at 70% ethanol, the effect of temperatures is similar, but at 90% ethanol, the temperature of 60°C stands out regardless of the solid‐solvent ratio, and this happens for both extraction methods, BSE and VAE.

For both methods, using 70% ethanol at 40°C with a solid‐solvent ratio of 1:150 yielded the highest results. This suggests it is the optimal condition for achieving high yields with lower energy costs, allowing for solvent recovery.

Extraction Optimization

The linear regression coefficient of each experimental value vs predicted (R2 > 0.97 and R2 > 0.96) indicates that 3% and 4% of total variations are not explained by the model for BSE and VAE, respectively. The adjusted determination coefficient for a good statistical model should be close to R2. Table 2 shows that both regression coefficients were close to 1 (R2 > 0.97 and R2 adj > 0.96). The experimental and predicted values are very close, as the coefficient of variation (CV < 8.38%) and root mean square error (RMSE < 8.53) were low.

Hence, the proposed model is adequate and could work well to predict bioactive compound extraction from M. oleifera leaves in the range of experimental variables. Statistical significance of each coefficient was measured using p‐ and F‐values, where a smaller p‐value and greater F‐value indicate that the studied variables are significant in the considered response (Setyani et al. 2023). The proposed model has a p‐value < 0.001, meaning it is significant and can be used to optimize different extraction variables.

The Pareto chart (Figure S5) highlights the significant impacts of various variables, including linear and quadratic terms and their interactions, ranked by importance. The bar lengths represent the absolute values of estimated effect coefficients, with the vertical line indicating the statistical significance threshold at a 95% confidence level. The study identifies the solid‐solvent ratio as the most significant factor for BSE, while temperature is the key factor for VAE, with respective values of 17.28 and 17.86. Other important variables include the percentage of ethanol and the interaction between ethanol and the solid‐solvent ratio.

Analysis of Desirability Function and Response Surface

A desirability function and response surfaces (see Figure S6) were used to identify optimal conditions for extracting bioactive compounds from M. oleifera leaves. The optimization was based on maximizing extraction yield, total phenolic content, total flavonoid content, antioxidant activity (IC50), and total vitamin content, which are critical indicators of bioactive compound recovery. By maximizing process desirability, 3D response surfaces (Figure 3) illustrate the impact of independent variables and their interactions. This approach provides insights into the complex relationships between factors that influence bioactive compound extraction.

Response surface of M. oleifera leaf extract as a function of BSE (A) and VAE (B).

In Figure 3, a strong interaction between the extraction percentage of ethanol and the solid‐solvent ratio was found in both methods. Increasing the temperature and the solid‐to‐solvent ratio obtained the maximum content of bioactive compounds. Consequently, the optimal condition values for BSE and VAE were determined as follows: ethanol concentration of 78.68% and 80.07%, solid‐solvent ratio of 1:84.3 and 1:88.6 g/mL, and temperature of 40.3°C and 55.2°C, respectively.

These values highlight a difference in the optimal extraction conditions influenced by vacuum application. When a vacuum is employed, there is a reduction in the solid‐solvent ratio. This can be explained by the fact that the driving force governing mass transfer relies on the concentration gradient between the solid and the liquid bulk, and this gradient intensifies when a lower solid‐solvent ratio is employed. The lower values of solid‐to‐solvent contribute to more significant contact between the plant material and the solvent, leading to increased extraction yields through the increasing concentration of the solvent phase without saturation.

Table 3 presents that total phenolic and flavonoid contents were around 37% and 48% higher in VAE extracts than in BSE extracts. Total vitamin content is four times higher in VAE extracts than in extracts obtained at conditions of atmospheric pressure (BSE).

The expressive increased concentration of lipophilic vitamins (in particular, vitamin E is almost ten times higher) shows the difference. Comparing the composition between BSE and VAE extracts, almost all presented significant differences (p < 0.05), except for the mineral composition of calcium and potassium, which showed similar contents.

Furthermore, Table 3 also shows that using a vacuum positively affects extract composition, demonstrating that vacuum levels play a crucial role in enhancing mass transfer during extraction. This effect is attributed to the vacuum‐assisted breakdown of plant cell components, improving solvent penetration into pores and matrix particles. Under sub‐atmospheric pressures, occluded gases within the plant matrix expand, causing the porous structure to open (Görgüç et al. 2020). This mechanism is particularly effective for compounds located deep within the matrix (Erihemu et al. 2014). Furthermore, factors such as material morphology, porosity, and pore size distribution directly affect gas removal and the penetration of liquids, as noted by Panarese et al. (2013) and Betoret et al. (2015).

Additionally, temperature is a significant factor in VAE. By increasing temperature, yields can increase without losing bioactive compounds and bioactivity. The solvent viscosity also decreases, enhancing its ability to diffuse within the solid material. By operating below the boiling point of the solvent, attained through vacuum adjustment, the extraction can occur at lower temperatures (Taticchi et al. 2021), which avoids thermal degradation. Additionally, the absence of oxygen prevents oxidative degradation, further preserving extract quality (Gil‐Martín et al. 2022).

Similarly, Taticchi et al. (2021) studied the impact of high vacuum‐assisted phenolic and volatile compound extraction from olive oil. The study found that using a vacuum system improves the extraction of olive oil by enhancing mass transfer and changing cell structure. This leads to increased porosity and capillarity, promoting the merging of oil droplets and causing the collapse of cell membranes. As a result, this process helps release more oil, resulting in higher oil extractability. These principles are mirrored in VAE, making it a superior method for extracting bioactive compounds from moringa leaves compared to traditional techniques.

Table 4 indicates that compared to standards, VAE extracts demonstrate substantial antioxidant capabilities near the BHT values. Lower IC50 values indicate higher antioxidant capacity, with VAE extracts demonstrating a 75% reduction in the IC50 (DPPH method) concentrations and 38% lower ABTS + radical scavenging activity compared to BSE. This may be because DPPH primarily measures hydrophilic antioxidants, whereas ABTS evaluates both hydrophilic and lipophilic compounds (Oldoni et al. 2022).

The higher antioxidant activity of VAE extracts is likely due to enhanced preservation of thermosensitive bioactive compounds, including phenolics and flavonoids such as rutin, gallic acid, syringic acid, and epicatechin. These compounds neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) via hydrogen atom donation or electron transfer, effectively interrupting oxidative chain reactions (Muvhulawa et al. 2022). Additionally, flavonoids stabilize ROS, while vitamins E and C synergistically regenerate oxidized phenolics, prolonging antioxidant activity (Ribeiro et al. 2015).

In terms of anti‐inflammatory activity, lipoxygenases (LOXs) play a crucial role in inflammation by generating lipid hydroperoxides. Antioxidants can inhibit LOX by scavenging lipid peroxy‐radicals formed during enzyme peroxidation, reducing the availability of its key substrate (Yong‐Bing et al. 2019). VAE extracts demonstrated stronger LOX inhibition (IC50 52.7 ± 0.6 µg/mL) compared to BSE (75.5 ± 2.6 µg/mL), approaching the activity of standard anti‐inflammatory agents such as acetylsalicylic acid (43.3 ± 2.4 µg/mL) and dexamethasone phosphate (66.2 ± 3.1 µg/mL). The enhanced inhibition is attributed to the higher retention of polyphenols, flavonoids, and vitamins, which are known for their LOX‐inhibitory effects (Chiș et al. 2023; Yong‐Bing et al. 2019). The used drug references are nonsteroidal (NSAID) and steroidal anti‐inflammatories, respectively. This probably shows the extract's ability to act on different mechanisms, like those drugs, and it should be explored in the future.

Several studies have identified phenolic acids, flavonoids, and tannins as potent LOX inhibitors. El‐Sherbiny et al. (2024) detected key antioxidants in M. oleifera leaves, including quercetin, chlorogenic, gallic, vanillic, syringic, ferulic, and coumaric acids, all of which help combat oxidative stress and inflammation. These compounds may inhibit enzymes directly, while flavonoids act synergistically with vitamins A and E, enhancing inhibition. Chiș et al. (2023) outlined three primary anti‐inflammatory mechanisms of M. oleifera: (a) inhibition of pro‐inflammatory enzymes (COX and LOX), (b) regulation of cytokine production via NF‐κB modulation, and (c) antioxidant activity that reduces oxidative stress and cytokine production.

The enhanced bioactivity of VAE extracts can be linked to their higher rutin concentrations (Table 3), for example, which have been shown to reduce oxidative stress and inflammation markers (Muvhulawa et al. 2022). The combination of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and vitamins (A, E, B9, and C) contributes to the observed antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory effects (Chiș et al. 2023; Yong‐Bing et al. 2019). Despite promising results, further studies are needed to elucidate the physicochemical interactions involved in VAE and validate these findings.

The challenge of this work is related to the lack of information in the literature on the effects and benefits of using a vacuum coupled with the solid‐liquid extraction of natural compounds. Although there are some related scattered studies, the physicochemical interactions are not simple and require further studies to confirm the observed effects.

Nevertheless, the VAE comparative study clearly showed that it is possible to obtain compounds with nutritional properties to be applied in the food, pharmaceutical, and nutraceutical industries. To our knowledge, no work with vacuum extraction has been carried out for moringa, which makes this work innovative. The nutritional capacity of moringa can only be used industrially if the extraction and separation methods do not degrade or eliminate the nutritional compounds of interest.

Conclusions

This study is related to the VAE of bioactive compounds from M. oleifera leaves using an aqueous ethanolic solvent, and compares it to the efficiency of the BSE.

Process optimization allowed us to identify that the best solvent composition was around 80% ethanol for both methods, while the solid‐solvent ratio (1:150) was a key factor in improving the recovery of the bioactive contents in higher yield. The vacuum applied to the solid‐liquid extraction significantly improved the recovery of total phenolic and flavonoid compounds by 37% and 48%, respectively, comparatively to the BSE method, in addition to the vitamin contents four times higher. The vacuum improved the biocompound extraction by enhancing solvent penetration on raw material, increasing the extract yield, and protecting the compounds from oxidation, offering a high‐quality extract verified by its antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties when compared to BSE extracts.

The stronger free radical scavenging of VAE extracts presented similar values of the BHT reference (18.4 ± 0.2 µg/mL for the ABTS method), while its anti‐inflammatory activity reached values near the acetylsalicylic acid (IC50 of 52 ± 1 µg/mL). These findings suggest that the enriched bioactive profile of VAE extracts, including higher concentrations of total phenolic (51 ± 1 GAE mg/g), total flavonoid (19.8 ± 0.1 CE mg/g), and vitamins (A, B9, and E), underscores their potential application in developing nutraceuticals and functional foods.
