Longitudinal Intracardiac Right Ventricular Flow Analysis in Infants With Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome in Interstage I Using Novel Doppler Velocity Reconstruction
Authors: Maren Brinken, Brett A Meyers, R Mark Payne, Roland Axt‐Fliedner, Can Yerebakan, Pavlos Vlachos, Yue‐Hin Loke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEchocardiography provides noninvasive frontline assessment of right ventricular (RV) function in hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS). However, conventional metrics do not quantify RV intracardiac hemodynamics. A novel Doppler velocity reconstruction measures kinetic energy, vortex strength, and flow energy loss through conventional color Doppler acquisitions. We investigated longitudinal changes in RV intracardiac flow in infants with HLHS during the critical interstage period, hypothesizing that these trends may inform disease progression.MethodsThis was a single‐center, retrospective analysis of patients with HLHS who underwent stage 1 palliation (bilateral pulmonary artery bands/hybrid palliation or Norwood operation). Echocardiographic studies at 5 time points from before to 6 months after surgery were selected for postprocessing. Apical views of the RV with adequate color Doppler imaging were analyzed. Significant tricuspid regurgitation was defined as vena contracta jet width ≥2.5 mm. Adverse events included death, heart transplant, use of ventricular assist device, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.ResultsThirty‐six patients with HLHS (21 hybrid, 15 Norwood) were included. Flow energy loss, vortex strength, and kinetic energy increased over time for all interstage patients with HLHS (P<0.0001) and correlated with RV size, body surface area, and E wave. Significant tricuspid regurgitation was associated with elevated flow energy loss and vortex strength (P=0.0007 and P<0.0001). Adverse events showed early changes in flow parameters and altered trends over the interstage period (vortex strength, P<0.0001; kinetic energy, P=0.0009).ConclusionsDoppler velocity reconstruction can assess RV intracardiac flow in infants in HLHS interstage I. While the results should not be used to interpret the surgical strategies, the longitudinal analysis of intracardiac flow may elucidate diastolic filling dynamics, with deviations associated with adverse events throughout the interstage period.Keywords: DoVeR, Echo, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, intracardiac flow, single ventricleSubject Categories: Echocardiography

Hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) is characterized by incomplete development of the left‐sided heart structures, resulting in ductal‐dependent systemic circulation. Neonatal surgical intervention is often performed, as patients with HLHS deteriorate quickly from pulmonary overcirculation/systemic undercirculation within the first few days of life. Contemporary surgical management consists of a series of interventions resulting in total cavopulmonary connection supported by the systemic right ventricle (RV). The Norwood procedure (stage 1 palliation) is the most critical intervention, 1 and despite extensive surgical experience there continues to be high mortality/morbidity rates as demonstrated in large multi‐institutional trials. 2 , 3 , 4 An alternative palliative strategy consisting of bilateral pulmonary artery banding and ductal stenting has been proposed to avoid early major surgery necessitating cardiopulmonary bypass, particularly in neonates with preoperative high‐risk factors. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Regardless of surgical intervention, noninvasive cardiac imaging is frequently required for structural and hemodynamic assessments. However, there are challenges in providing adequate surveillance of overall RV function.

In general, patients with HLHS have altered volume and pressure loads in the systemic RV, leading to higher risk of dysfunction. 10 , 11 , 12 Conventional echocardiography can provide detailed assessment of RV wall motion/deformation and the presence of tricuspid regurgitation (TR). 13 However, there are few quantitative flow metrics (eg, myocardial strain) to track these patients due to the unique and heterogeneous RV geometry in HLHS. A potential approach involves analyzing the complex intracardiac flow to measure vortex strength (VS), kinetic energy (KE), and flow energy loss (FEL) within the RV. As the total sum of clockwise and counterclockwise circulation, VS represents the overall organization of the flow field. FEL converts KE into thermal energy by viscosity‐driven friction, which contributes to attenuating flow organization. 14 Altered ventricular morphology is known to have unnatural vortex ring formations and higher FEL, thus requiring more KE to preserve the cardiac output and reducing cardiac efficiency. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Thus, intracardiac flow analysis of the RV could characterize ventricular efficiency and (dys)function. 15 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 However, this strategy is not routinely implemented in infants with HLHS, and most echocardiography‐based measures rely on vendor‐specific software and hardware.

To that end, we have developed a novel, vendor‐independent Doppler velocity reconstruction (DoVeR) to identify intracardiac flow changes that may reflect the clinical course of patients with HLHS. The DoVeR reconstruction algorithm resolves the underlying color flow imaging velocity field to derive intracardiac flow metrics from conventional retrospective acquisitions. 24 , 25 , 26 In this study, we applied DoVeR analysis to a cohort of patients with HLHS with longitudinal echocardiographic imaging in the critical “interstage period,” from birth to the bidirectional Glenn. We hypothesized that DoVeR could characterize longitudinal changes of intracardiac flow in the systemic RV that would inform on disease progression in infants with HLHS.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Children’s National Hospital’s Institutional Review Board on December 21, 2022.

A waiver of informed consent was granted for this study.

This was a retrospective, longitudinal, single‐center cohort study at Children’s National Hospital. All patients with HLHS born from 2018 to 2023 were identified for chart review and independent review of all clinically indicated echocardiographic studies.

Data Availability

The data underlying this article will be shared upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All included patients had underlying HLHS subtypes (aortic stenosis/mitral stenosis, aortic atresia/mitral atresia, or aortic atresia/mitral stenosis, all with intact ventricular septum). The anatomic subtypes of HLHS were classified by the clinical echocardiography team interpreting day‐of‐life 1 imaging on the basis of established clinical guidelines and the appearance of the aortic and mitral valves, as well as presence/absence of flow across the respective valves. Patients underwent either Norwood palliation, hybrid palliation, or delayed Norwood palliation. Per standardized institutional criteria, hybrid patients consisted of those with high‐risk preoperative features (ie, prematurity, low birth weight [<2.5 kg], low cardiac output syndrome, greater than moderate TR, restrictive/intact atrial septum) or with baseline cardiogenic shock refractory to medical management. 5 Delayed Norwood palliation consisted of a subset of patients who received hybrid/bilateral pulmonary artery banding and underwent Norwood operation after stabilization (≈4–6 weeks after initial palliation). These patients were still analyzed as part of the hybrid cohort. HLHS patients who died prior to intervention or were treated with noninterventional palliation therapies were not included. Echo studies with insufficient quality in B‐mode and color flow imaging in the apical plane were excluded. Echo studies performed after stage II palliation were not included in this analysis. Cardiac catheterization data and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) data before stage II palliation were also collated and reported.

Data Acquisition

Standard‐of‐care echocardiography was performed using a Vivid E‐95 (General Electric) or Epiq 5C/7C (Phillips) following standard methodology according to the American Society of Echocardiography. 27 All echocardiograms were clinically indicated as part of routine surveillance at this center (transthoracic echocardiography every 1–2 weeks). For each patient, 1 preoperative and 4 postoperative studies were extracted for analysis. The postoperative studies were grouped into 4 time points: 2 to 4 weeks, 1 to 2 months, 3 to 4 months, and 5 to 6 months after initial palliation (Norwood or hybrid). B‐mode and color flow imaging of the apical plane were reviewed to ensure good visualization of the atrium, tricuspid inflow, RV body, RV free wall, and intraventricular septum. Additionally, the studies were reviewed for adequacy of frame rates, at least >3 cardiac cycles in the acquisition period, adequate visualization of the RV free wall, and appropriate Nyquist limit with minimal aliasing. This limit was configured to two‐thirds of the peak tricuspid inflow velocity, determined during the scan session while recording pulse‐wave Doppler.

Stratification of Subjects With HLHS

The subjects were categorized based on their initial palliation (hybrid versus Norwood, delayed Norwood kept within the hybrid cohort), presence or absence of significant TR, and presence or absence of composite adverse events before stage 2 palliation. Significant TR was defined on the basis of a vena contracta jet width of ≥2.5 mm, which is associated with adverse outcomes based on echocardiographic studies from the SVR‐1 (Single Ventricle Reconstruction) trial. 2 Composite adverse events were categorized by mortality rate, postoperative use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and permanent use of a ventricular assist device or heart transplant at any point between 2 weeks and 6 months after initial palliation.

DoVeR Intracardiac Flow Analysis and Conventional Strain Analysis

An in‐house suite of semiautomated algorithms implemented via MATLAB (MathWorks) was used to reconstruct the underlying velocity field in the RV. DoVeR was specifically developed to enhance the role of echocardiography in intracardiac flow analysis. 24 , 28 It uses the velocities of color Doppler flow to quantify intracardiac flows and does not need intravascular contrast. DoVeR resolves the underlying 2‐dimensional velocity field for blood flow by using the stream function–vorticity equation while conserving mass. 25 In brief, boundary conditions for the stream function are defined using the delineated endocardial edges, while the initial condition for vorticity is derived from the Doppler velocity field (Figure 1). The numerical solver for DoVeR employs an iterative process, allowing vorticity to be updated based on the solution of the stream function–vorticity equation in a 2‐dimensional–Laplacian context through matrix inversion. Insonation deviations of ±25° did not significantly impact the reconstruction accuracy due to the small‐angle approximation; as long as the tricuspid inflow is properly aligned and resolved in the plane of interest, DoVeR reliably captures the velocity field. This method has previously been validated against 4‐dimensional flow CMR imaging, 28 , 29 and intra‐/interobserver variation analysis has not exceeded 3% to 5% for any parameter with strong/very strong intraclass correlation values ranging from strong to very strong (0.880–0.993). 24

The algorithms were initialized using 3 user‐selected feature points (RV apex, annulus free‐wall edge, annulus septal edge) in 3 acquisition frames (first, middle, and last recording frames). A machine vision algorithm identified the endocardium edges of the RV for segmentation. 26 The edges and color Doppler velocity were used to impose boundary and initial conditions for the DoVeR algorithm, based on the relationship between volume flow rate and fluid rotation. 25 The reconstructed velocity vector fields were then processed to quantify intracardiac flow parameters (see Data S1 for full calculations), including KE (characterizing the “work” of blood flow), FEL (attributed to energy dissipation due to friction and viscosity causing flow redirection), and VS (the net sum of absolute vorticity/“spin” over the RV area, a surrogate for total flow circulation based on the vortex features produced during diastolic filling). Each parameter’s total, systolic, and diastolic average were extracted. FEL was normalized against KE to account for potential physiologic variations. VS and KE were normalized against RV area in longitudinal analysis.

To analyze RV myocardial strain, feature tracking software of conventional B‐mode imaging was performed on IntelliSpace Cardiovascular Ultrasound Workspace (Phillips) using the RV strain module. The RV endocardium in end‐systolic and end‐diastolic frames were manually adjusted to initiate feature tracking and derive global longitudinal strain. 30

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic, echocardiographic, and clinical measures are presented as mean±SD or as frequency and percentage, depending on the data type. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ 2 or Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were analyzed using t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate. This study compared intracardiac flow parameters in patients with adverse events versus no adverse events and studies with no TR versus significant TR. As secondary assessment, we also compared preoperative flow parameters in patients undergoing hybrid versus Norwood palliation, although we did not investigate the postoperative differences given multiple confounders related to the variability in surgical practices (eg, the inclusion of a delayed Norwood palliation strategy). To examine correlations between intracardiac flow and physiologic parameters (eg, RV size, E‐wave velocity, body surface area), Pearson correlation analysis was performed. The Mann–Kendall trend test was used to assess monotonic trends in the mean parameters over time. To evaluate the effect of adverse events on intracardiac flow parameters, while adjusting for time points as a covariate, an ANCOVA was performed. The ANCOVA model included interaction terms between time point and group status (adverse event versus no adverse event) to identify differential trajectories of intracardiac flow parameters, with each patient treated as the unit of analysis. The interaction effect was evaluated using the F value, which quantifies the ratio of variance explained by the interaction term (eg, time point×adverse event) to the residual unexplained variance. A significant F value indicates that the time‐dependent changes in flow parameters differ significantly between the 2 groups. The significance level for all analyses was set to α=0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

RESULTS

Demographics

This study included 36 infants with HLHS in interstage I who underwent initial hybrid (n=21) or Norwood (n=15) palliation. As a result of standardized institutional protocols based on operative risk assumptions, 5 the patients who underwent hybrid palliation had a higher incidence of prematurity compared with the patients who underwent Norwood palliation (Table) and higher incidence of syndromic diagnosis (n=3 [14%]). Of the 21 patients undergoing hybrid palliation, 11 transitioned to delayed Norwood‐type palliation. Seventeen standard‐risk neonates underwent traditional neonatal Norwood palliation. Five patients (4 hybrid, 1 Norwood) required reintervention due to recoarctation/stent narrowing of the ductus arteriosus. Stage II palliation (bidirectional Glenn) was performed electively at age 4 to 6 months in 27 (75%) patients. Over the interstage period, the average BSA increased from 0.19±0.03 m2 to 0.31±0.03 m2, while RV area increased from 6.6±1.5 cm2 to 12.8±2.7 cm2. A subgroup of patients with HLHS also underwent pre–stage II catheterization or CMR (n=26 and n=9, respectively) or both with mean peak RV systolic pressure of 81.9±11.7 mm Hg, mean RV end‐diastolic pressure of 10.2±2.8 mm Hg, and RV indexed end‐diastolic volume of 104 ± 21 mL/m2.

General Physiologic Correlations and Longitudinal Trends in Intracardiac Flow Measurements

Overall, 116 of 121 echocardiographic studies (≈95%) were successfully processed by DoVeR. The remaining 5 studies failed because the quality of the extracted studies was not sufficient for reconstruction. Before palliation, patients undergoing Norwood and hybrid palliation had no statistically significant differences in intracardiac flow/strain differences (Table S1). The overall HLHS cohort demonstrated a longitudinal increase in diastolic FEL, KE, VS, and E‐wave velocity during the interstage period (FEL, P<0.0001; KE, P<0.0001; VS, P<0.0001; E‐wave, P=0.0208; Figure 2). These observed trends are associated with several clinical parameters (Figure 3, Table S2). There was also modest correlation between KE and VS flow parameters with tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (r=0.34 [95% CI, 0.14–0.51], P=0.0012; and r= 0.26 [95% CI, 0.06–0.45], P=0.0134, respectively). We noted a correlation with BSA and RV size, whereas heart rate was not a notable factor; for example, diastolic VS correlated with BSA (r=0.42 [95% CI, 0.25–0.56], P<0.0001) and RV area (r=0.62 [95% CI, 0.49–0.73], P<0.0001) but not with heart rate (r=0.045 [95% CI, −0.15 to 0.23, P=0.65]).

Adverse Events and Associations With Intracardiac Flow

Over the course of the first 6 months after initial palliation, 14 patients experienced adverse events defined by death (n=9), use of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (n=7), heart transplant (n=1), or implantation of a ventricular assist device (n=1). The interaction between time point and experiencing adverse events was statistically significant for VS diastolic average (F[8, 108]=19.20 P<0.0001) and KE diastolic average (F [8, 108]=7.45, P=0.0009). Patients with adverse events demonstrated blunted changes and flatter trajectories for both flow parameters over time (Figure 4), whereas patients without adverse events demonstrated consistent increases. Normalized trends against the presurgical (time point 1) show similarly altered patterns in patients with adverse events (Figure S1).

The most notable differences in longitudinal changes occurred during the first 2 to 4 weeks after surgery (Figure 4, Figure S2); for patients with HLHS without adverse events, normalized diastolic average KE and normalized VS increased (37.2±21.5 versus 92.7±51.9 Ws/m2; P=0.0066; 0.0125±0.0044 s−1 versus 0.0236±0.00873 s−1; P=0.00084) while normalized diastolic average FEL/KE decreased (0.793±0.228 ms−1 versus 0.564±0.141 ms−1; P=0.0073), reflecting the rapid improvement in ventricular filling efficiency during the perinatal period (Figure 5). There was no significant change in E‐wave velocity (58±25 cm/s versus 69±22 cm/s, P=0.23) or septal/lateral E/E′ (11.2±6.7 versus 13.0±5.5, P=0.49; 12.7±9.9 versus 12.6±7.3, P=0.96, respectively). In patients with HLHS with adverse events, this progression was not consistently observed (KE diastolic average: 59.7±39.9 versus 67.5±36.9 Ws/m2; P=0.5775; VS diastolic average: 0.0198±0.0098 versus 0.0219±0.0058 s−1; P=0.4039; FEL/KE diastolic average: 0.0.7297±0.2314 ms−1 versus 0.6984±0.3028 ms−1; P=0.7851; Figure 6). There were no statistically significant differences in global longitudinal strain, although patients with adverse events had a trend toward decreased strain magnitude in the first 2‐ to 4‐week postsurgical period (RV global longitudinal strain, 17.54±3.7 versus 15.31±2.97%; P=0.0751). Furthermore, in patients with HLHS with adverse events, those who underwent Norwood palliation tended to have lower KE and VS in the immediate postoperative period. There was also no significant change in E‐wave velocity (53±30 cm/s versus 60±22 cm/s, P=0.59) or septal/lateral E/E′ (11.2±9.4 versus 13.7±6.3, P=0.49; 9.92±6.7 versus 13.3±8.3, P=0.19, respectively).

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study investigated longitudinal changes to intracardiac flow of infants with HLHS in interstage I by postprocessing conventional echocardiographic color flow imaging imaging with novel DoVeR. We characterized the general longitudinal increase of KE and VS with patient age/growth. The primary finding was that adverse events during the interstage period were associated with inconsistent progression in intracardiac flow (especially 2–4 weeks after surgical palliation) and that significant TR is associated with higher FEL.

The interstage period in HLHS is a critical period characterized by drastic changes to intracardiac physiology, making it essential to determine changes in ventricular filling and wall motion and understand the underlying flow mechanics governed by disease progression and ventricular wall motion. In our study, we observed and identified general temporal trends in FEL and KE across the interstage period. By identifying early deviations to these trends, intracardiac flow analysis could determine those patients at risk for adverse outcomes.

Beyond echocardiographic assessment for systolic function and other modifiers (such as arch reobstruction and TR), the diastolic function of the RV remains a crucial yet challenging element to assess in HLHS. Abnormal filling and myocardial stretching in the pressure/volume‐loaded systemic RV may result in long‐term maladaptive remodeling and reduced ventricular compliance that is detrimental to maintaining low central venous pressures after the Fontan. 31 , 32 , 33 Ventricular filling is also important in trending the effects of medications that improve pulmonary venous return and ventricular compliance (eg, udenafil in a recent large multicenter study). 34 Longitudinal imaging studies by echocardiography have focused on RV systolic function, 35 , 36 as conventional Doppler metrics have not corresponded well to ventricular compliance. 37 Metrics such as tissue Doppler imaging have shown limited validity in detecting myocardial dysfunction during the neonatal period, particularly in the context of the systemic RV. 38 Thus, the neonatal single‐ventricle physiology represents a distinct entity with unique hemodynamic challenges that necessitate specialized evaluation approaches. This was previously explored in a DoVeR‐based comparison against biventricular circulation in the fetal/neonatal transition period. The RV, which would typically experience a reduction in volume load after transitional physiology, continued to exhibit significantly elevated stroke volume, cardiac output, and E‐wave filling velocities in HLHS, reflecting its adaptation to systemic circulation demands. Disordered flow patterns persisted, characterized by elevated VS, KE, and FEL, indicating inefficient diastolic function and altered intracardiac flow dynamics. 39

Given the complexity of the systemic RV, most intracardiac flow analyses have focused on 4‐dimensional flow as derived by CMR or virtual computational simulations. 40 , 41 CMR intracardiac flow analysis remains the gold standard, as it resolves the complex flow in a 3‐dimensional space. We have also found in 4‐dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging that patients undergoing pre‐Fontan procedures with HLHS have altered KE/FEL compared with patients with other forms of single‐ventricle defects, which corresponded to short‐term adverse outcome. 42 At the same time, CMR is challenging for serial evaluation, entailing long scan times that necessitate the use of general anesthesia in high‐risk infants. 43 Thus, a conventional echocardiography‐based method such as DoVeR offers a strong alternative to assess the longitudinal intracardiac flow changes in HLHS given the high availability and safety of echocardiography. 44 , 45 The hydrodynamic parameters such as KE and VS revealed clinically significant changes, particularly in patients without adverse events. DoVeR offers a robust, noninvasive approach to identify these critical flow changes over time, providing actionable insights beyond traditional metrics. DoVeR has the further advantage of being vendor independent and has previous validation against 4‐dimensional flow and congenital heart disease. 28 , 29

Intracardiac vortical flow plays a fundamental role in linking myocardial tissue function and blood hemodynamic effects. As the RV maintains its in utero role as the systemic pump, diastolic function ideally should maintain ventricular efficiency with less FEL for the same amount of KE. Additionally, every intervention and surgery will alter the intracardiac flow dynamics. RV hemodynamics have previously been studied in the context of systolic function for biventricular patients, such as repaired tetralogy of Fallot. 24 In prior work with DoVeR, we demonstrated a negative association between energy loss/vortex strength with systolic function and exercise capacity in this population. Four‐dimensional flow CMR studies have also identified alterations in the tricuspid inflow filling pattern as a key driver of dysfunction. 46 Longitudinal trends of intracardiac flow could reflect atypical maladaptation that may be a risk factor for adverse events. This finding is also consistent with systolic function trends in that nonsurvivors have early changes to higher fractional area change and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion with a lack of improvement in these parameters over time. 35 Our findings did not identify clinical association with conventional Doppler metrics of diastolic function, suggestive that intracardiac flow‐derived diastolic measures may be more sensitive in identifying diastolic dysfunction in this unique population.

These patterns of altered flow efficiency may be driven, in part, by underlying changes in RV geometry during the interstage period. The systemic RV undergoes significant remodeling in HLHS, and prior studies have shown that abnormal geometric phenotypes are associated with impaired function and adverse outcomes. For example, an “apical bulge” configuration, previously described by Rösner et al, is associated with reduced regional myocardial strain and appears across multiple HLHS subtypes. 47 Similarly, Fricke et al identified that the presence of a “globular,” pouch‐like residual left ventricle also correlated with increased death. 48 These geometric configurations likely alter intraventricular pressure gradients and streamline formation thereby affecting both KE and FEL. Although our current data set lacks serial imaging of RV wall thickness or 3‐dimensional chamber shape, we believe these structural variations play a central role in shaping flow dynamics and may partly explain heterogeneity in functional adaptation. Future prospective work combining echocardiographic flow analysis with RV shape remodeling or myocardial strain tracking will be important for clarifying these relationships.

The interpretation of intracardiac flow parameters such as KE and FEL must also be understood within the context of mechanical loading conditions. Preload and afterload significantly influence ventricular work and, by extension, the resulting flow dynamics within the RV cavity. For instance, patients with relative hypervolemia or significant TR may demonstrate elevated KE not as a reflection of better myocardial contractility, but rather as an adaptive response to excess volume. Conversely, patients with acutely diminished preload or elevated afterload may show blunted flow patterns despite intact contractile mechanics. While our current methodology does not disentangle these load‐dependent effects nor provide isolated markers of myocardial function, we view KE/FEL as indirect, integrative measures of the myocardial work required to sustain effective flow. Conventional strain metrics are similarly influenced by loading conditions, and there is no universally accepted echocardiographic surrogate for pressure–volume loop‐derived load‐independent indices. However, if flow indices could be reliably correlated with diastolic pressures, 42 it may be possible to dynamically capture the respiratory variation in KE and FEL over time. By combining these temporal fluctuations with the inherent volume loading changes that occur during the respiratory cycle, one could potentially derive a composite metric analogous to the end‐diastolic pressure–volume relationship in a noninvasive manner. Such an approach would offer a nuanced assessment of diastolic function, providing insights into both ventricular compliance and load dynamics that are currently challenging to isolate with standard echocardiographic techniques. Future studies incorporating invasive hemodynamics or volume status monitoring will be critical to parsing out these contributions more precisely.

The results should not be used to infer any comparisons in surgical palliation strategies. It was anticipated that hybrid patients would inherently have higher‐risk features, such as prematurity, which may influence outcomes. These differences, along with the delayed Norwood palliation in 11 of 21 patients undergoing hybrid palliation, further confound any direct comparisons. At the same time, the results demonstrate the potential in intracardiac flow analysis to provide longitudinal assessments of RV hemodynamics particularly across the interstage period. Patients with HLHS are known to have potentially protracted interstage course during the first several months of life, 49 with much of the literature focused on the impact of surgical palliation on RV systolic function, for example, Blalock–Taussig Thomas shunt versus Sano conduit. In our data, we did not demonstrate any significant differences between patients undergoing Norwood palliation versus patients undergoing hybrid palliation before intervention or afterward, although the alternative strategy delayed Norwood palliation likely confounded the analysis between the 2 groups. Still, we observed that patients undergoing Norwood palliation with adverse events trended toward having lower diastolic VS and KE after initial surgical palliation. This association may reflect early myocardial depression leading to lower filling with incremental impact on diastolic function and adverse outcomes during the interstage period. Thus, systematic hemodynamic comparisons will be necessary to assess the long‐term efficacy of these strategies.

Limitations

This single‐center retrospective study was limited by a small sample size, primarily due to the availability of retrospective echocardiographic imaging of patients with HLHS with serial color Doppler data suitable for DoVeR analysis, as well as the inherent rarity of HLHS. This was particularly challenging for critically ill patients, where focused studies often lacked the prerequisite frames necessary for DoVeR. These limitations, however, can be corrected in future research through the implementation of a prospective, intracardiac flow‐specific color Doppler imaging protocol, which we intend to implement in prospective studies. DoVeR is limited by its reliance on 2‐dimensional imaging, which cannot capture through‐plane turbulence from the RV outflow tract, a likely significant contributor to overall RV energy loss and vorticity. Finally, HLHS includes a heterogeneous group of anatomic variations driving individualized therapeutic approaches.

While our longitudinal modeling accounted for repeated measures within subjects, exploratory plots such as correlation analyses and TR associations were based on individual echocardiographic studies. These analyses should be considered hypothesis generating, and future studies will incorporate intrasubject correlation structures to determine the progression of TR.

Despite these challenges, our findings provide valuable preliminary insights into the potential of intracardiac flow analysis as a hemodynamic assessment tool. While limited by sample size and lacking statistical significance in some areas, these results underscore a need to analyze intracardiac flow properties as part of any larger, multicenter prospective studies that intend to investigate the evolving outcomes of HLHS patients. Given the ability of applying DoVeR on conventional color Doppler imaging platforms, this should be a feasible methodology and would allow the systematic assessment of the relationships between novel flow parameters and contemporary functional metrics. In clinical practice, DoVeR could serve as a sensitive adjunct for monitoring hemodynamic changes over time, particularly when used to establish pre‐ and postoperative baselines in high‐risk patients. Its ability to capture subtle shifts in intracardiac flow may help identify patients with evolving ventricular dysfunction or low cardiac output physiology, especially when conventional metrics remain equivocal. While further validation is required to define therapeutic thresholds, this approach could eventually support bedside decisions regarding volume status, inotropic support, or diastolic unloading strategies.

This foundation can guide future research evaluating surgical palliation strategies, such as the hybrid procedure or delayed Norwood palliation.

CONCLUSIONS

DoVeR is a viable imaging surveillance technique to monitor intracardiac flow dynamics in patients with HLHS. Longitudinal trends of intracardiac flow are key to understanding diastolic filling in patients with HLHS, and deviations to these trends are associated with adverse events in the interstage period. While the current study results should not be used to inform a comparison between surgical strategies, a DoVeR‐derived surveillance strategy may be key to assessing HLHS hemodynamics as they relate to interstage treatment and outcome.
