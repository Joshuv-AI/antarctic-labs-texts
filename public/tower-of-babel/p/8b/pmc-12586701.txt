Antimicrobial resistance of Enterobacterales in Central Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mathilde Garé, Cédric B Chesnais, Jérémy T Campillo, Marie-Paule Ngogang, Marie-Christine Fonkoua, Vincent Le Moing, Charlotte Boullé
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Enterobacterales is a growing concern in sub-Saharan Africa, yet data remain scarce for Central Africa.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis (2008–2024) to estimate the prevalence of extended spectrum ß-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-EB) and AMR frequency across nine Central African countries. Systematic searching strategy and risk of bias assessment followed PRISMA guidelines. Prevalence or frequencies and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using a random effects meta-model with the Der Simonian and Laird method.ResultsHere we show an ESBL-EB pooled prevalence of 22% (41% for carriage and 10% in clinical samples) for the 24 selected articles assessing ESBL-EB. For the 26 selected articles assessing AMR frequency, we find it is high for cotrimoxazole (63–81%), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (43–63%), ciprofloxacin (24–33%), and remains lower for nitrofurantoin (12–18%) and amikacin (13–19%). We provide pooled estimates for alternative agents and highlight the near-total absence of data for WHO Reserve antibiotics, including carbapenems, newer β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, and fosfomycin. Significant heterogeneity and high exclusion rate due to risk of bias reflect major diagnostic limitations in the region.ConclusionsThe high prevalence of AMR in Central Africa underscores the urgent need to strengthen antimicrobial susceptibility testing capacities, restrict empirical use of key antibiotics, and ensure equitable access to effective antimicrobials. Without coordinated investments for microbiology capacities scale-up, prescription regulation, strategies for water, sanitation and hygiene, and infection prevention and control, patient outcomes and regional AMR containment will be jeopardized.Subject terms: Bacterial infection, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

The Central African Region, regrouping low-income (Central African Republic (CAR), Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), Chad) and middle-income countries (Angola, Cameroon, Gabon, Equatorial Guinea, Republic of Congo (RC), São Tomé and Príncipe), faces a triple burden of non-communicable diseases (mainly with arterial hypertension and diabetes, and to a lesser extent, cancers), communicable diseases (mainly with HIV/tuberculosis/malaria, and to a lesser extent, Neglected Tropical Diseases), and injuries. This combination leaves the population vulnerable to severe, repeated, and complicated bacterial infections. In 2019, the burden of disability adjusted life years (DALYs) associated with 85 pathogens was the highest in Sub-Saharan Africa compared to other regions, according to Naghavi et al.1 Among these pathogens, Klebsiella pneumoniae ranked among the top five in DALY burden in Gabon, RC, and DRC.

At the same time, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) of Enterobacterales (EB), particularly through enzymatic mechanisms such as extended-spectrum ß-lactamase (ESBL) and carbapenemase production, has been rising worldwide, including in Africa2,3. In 2021, AMR was estimated to be associated with 923,000 deaths and directly responsible for 209,000 deaths in Sub-Saharan Africa, with a projection reaching nearly 1.5 million associated deaths and 323,000 attributable deaths in 20504. Beyond mortality, AMR exacerbates poverty by reducing labor supplies, increasing healthcare costs, and restricting livestock production and trade5.

In regards with the AMR situation worldwide, the World Health Organization recently ranked third-generation cephalosporins (3GC) resistant EB as critical priority pathogens6. Nevertheless, Central African countries score the lowest rates in AMR preparedness among African regions, according to the WHO Joint External Evaluation7. Implementation of the Global Action Plan on AMR performance score varied from moderate (Angola) to poor (DRC, Equatorial Guinea, CAR), with no available data for Cameroon (even though the country joined the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System in 2022) and RC. Surveillance systems and antimicrobial stewardship efforts remain particularly weak, except in Angola.

Despite the growing AMR crisis, data on AMR of EB in Central Africa remains scarce. The only regional review dates back to 1955–2008, before the widespread emergence of ESBL-producing EB (ESBL-EB)8. A 2017 review on AMR in Africa included limited studies from Central Africa9, while a 2019 review by Mouiche et al. adopted a One Health approach in Cameroon, but did not focus on EB nor assessed ESBL prevalence10. Moreover, new data published since 2019 provide valuable insights into the extent of EB resistance and the spread of ESBL-EB in this region.

Hence, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to estimate the frequency of AMR in Enterobacterales (Escherichia coli, K. pneumoniae, and AmpC-producing EB) in Central Africa in the most recent period (2008–2024), with a particular focus on ESBL-EB prevalence, and to summarize available data on alternative therapeutic options. We find an ESBL-EB pooled prevalence of 22% (41% for carriage and 10% in clinical samples). We also show that AMR frequency is high for cotrimoxazole, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, and ciprofloxacin, but remains lower for nitrofurantoin and amikacin. Data for WHO Reserve antibiotics is almost absent, including for carbapenems, newer β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, and fosfomycin. Significant heterogeneity and a high exclusion rate due to risk of bias reflect major diagnostic limitations in the region.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we searched the PubMed and Web of Science databases from January 1st, 2008, to November 19th, 2024. Other sources included citations retrieval and institutional sources. The full search strategy and keywords are described in Supplementary Note 1. The study protocol was recorded prospectively on Open Science Framework (10.17605/OSF.IO/URZYN)11 and followed PRISMA guidelines for systematic researches12,13. We included all studies with AMR of Enterobacterales as the main topic (including AMR surveillance data, retrospective laboratory findings, cross-sectional studies, and prospective studies), performed in Central African countries (Angola, Cameroon, DRC, RC, Equatorial Guinea, Chad, CAR, São Tomé and Príncipe), without language restriction.

We excluded animal or environmental studies, studies without AMR phenotypic data (i.e., reporting only genome sequencing of resistant bacterial strains, or studies without antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results), studies focusing exclusively on Salmonella sp. or Shigella sp., studies focusing on antimicrobial properties of physical, chemical, or organic compounds, studies published prior to 2008, and literature reviews or protocols.

A first selection was performed after title and abstract readings, then a second selection was performed after full-text reading. The risk of bias (ROB) was assessed through a specific tool, using broad criteria from the JBI critical appraisal tool14, with specific sub-criteria for each component (checklist available in Supplementary Table 1), inspired by the checklist used in Tadesse et al.’s meta-analysis9. Articles with a high ROB were excluded from the analysis. We used the Cochrane robvis tool for the ROB assessment visualization15. Two reviewers (MG and CB) extracted all data separately and independently. Contextual data on each study were also extracted (see Supplementary Note 2). Situations of disagreement were further solved through discussions between the two parties, and interrogation of a third party in case of persistent disagreement (VLM).

Data analysis

The primary outcome was the presence of an ESBL-producing phenotype for E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and group 3 EB (EB3) with intrinsic low-level AmpC cephalosporinase (Enterobacter, Serratia, Providencia, and Morganella groups, Citrobacter freundii, Klebsiella aerogenes, Hafnia alvei). ESBL prevalence was reported as the number of subjects harboring or infected with at least one ESBL-EB over the total number of subjects screened.

The secondary outcomes were AST results for E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and EB3, for all antibiotics recommended by EUCAST/CA-SFM (EUropean Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) (amoxicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, piperacillin, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefoxitin, ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, ertapenem, imipenem, meropenem, gentamicin, amikacin, nalidixic acid, ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, cotrimoxazole, fosfomycin, nitrofurantoin) and chloramphenicol, tetracyclin and colistin which are used in Central Africa, and potential alternative drugs cefiderocol, tigecycline, or combinations of β-Lactam-β-Lactamase Inhibitor (BL/BLI) including ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, meropenem/vaborbactam, and imipenem/cilastatin/relebactam. AMR frequency was reported as the number of intermediate or resistant strains over the total number of strains tested for each bacterial species.

Statistical analysis was performed using the package meta in R software16. Prevalence or frequencies and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated using a random effects meta-model with the Der Simonian and Laird method. A Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation was used to transform raw prevalence estimates. Between-study heterogeneity was characterized with the τ² statistic and quantified by Higgins I² 17. Subgroup analysis was performed for ESBL-EB carriage and clinical samples, as well as meta-regression on country and study year. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots.

Ethical statement

No institutional research board approval was required, as this study is a literature review and meta-analysis of already published data.

Reporting summary

Results

Study selection and report characteristics

A total of 393 records were identified (367 from databases and 26 via other sources). Sixty-four duplicates were removed, 220 articles were excluded based on their title or abstract and 24 after full-text reading, leading to 85 records (Fig. 1). Among these records, 24 were classified as having a high risk of bias (Supplementary Fig. 1, with detail per study figured in Supplementary Fig. 2) and were excluded from the analysis (reasons for exclusion are summarized in Supplementary Data 1)18–41.

Six articles were included in both analyses, 13 could not be included due to the impossibility to retrieve the number of isolates for each specie, 1 was excluded due to duplicate reporting, and 3 were further excluded (2 due to major concerns with the microbiological method, and 1 for repeated data inconsistency, while scoring as with some concerns in the ROB analysis, see Supplementary Data 1)30,86–101. Most studies were conducted in Cameroon (n = 10/25; 40.0%) and Gabon (n = 7/25; 28.0%). Both hospital patients and community participants were included in the majority of studies (n = 10/25; 40.0%).

Bacteriological identification methods predominantly relied on API-20E galleries (n = 9/25; 36.0%) or MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (n = 8/25; 32.0%). AST was most frequently conducted using the disk diffusion method (n = 20/25; 80.0%). Fecal carriage of ESBL-EB was assessed in 11 studies, with a pooled prevalence of 41% (95% CI: 30–53%). Among the 14 studies analyzing ESBL-EB prevalence in clinical samples, half focused on urinary specimens, with an estimated pooled prevalence of 10% (95% CI: 7–13%) (Fig. 3). Meta-analysis of AMR-EB frequency

The characteristics of the 26 articles included in the AMR-EB frequency meta-analysis are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Data 2 and detailed in Supplementary Data 4.

ROB was rated low in 8 (30.8%) studies and with some concerns in 18 (69.2%) studies. In total, 14,261 subjects were included, mostly from studies conducted in Cameroon (n = 14/26; 53.8%). Most studies followed a cross-sectional design (n = 16/26; 61.5%), were conducted in urban settings (n = 21/26; 80.8%), and took place in hospital settings for 12 of them (46.2%). Bacteriological identification mainly relied on API-20E galleries (n = 14/26; 53.8%), and AST was most frequently performed using the disk diffusion method (n = 22/26; 84.6%). Most studies (n = 20/26; 76.9%) included data from symptomatic patients. The most frequently analyzed sample types were urine (n = 8/26; 30.8%) and stool (n = 6/26; 23.1%).

Amoxicillin/clavulanic acid resistance was high, reaching 43% (95% CI: 25–63%) in E. coli (N = 1486) and 63% (95% CI: 43–81%) K. pneumoniae (N = 780). 3GC resistance was higher in K. pneumoniae (39%, 95% CI: 23–57%, N = 836) compared to E. coli (28%, 95% CI: 17–40%, N = 1415), with similar resistance patterns observed for ceftazidime.

Imipenem resistance was low for all species: 3% (95% CI: 0–12%, N = 962) for E. coli, 5% (95% CI: 0–22%, N = 338) for K. pneumoniae, and 13% (95% CI:0–48%, N = 188) for EB3.

Ciprofloxacin was the most frequently assessed fluoroquinolone. Resistance rates were high, with pooled frequency estimates of 24% (95% CI: 15–35%, N = 1358) in E. coli, 33% (95% CI: 16–52%, N = 519) in K. pneumoniae, and 32% (95% CI: 18–47%, N = 243) in EB3. Highest resistance rates were found for cotrimoxazole with a pooled prevalence of 63% (95% CI: 43–82%, N = 1203) in E. coli, 81% (95% CI: 58–97%, N = 663) in K. pneumoniae, and 72% (95% CI: 45–94%, N = 599) in EB3. Among aminoglycosides, resistance to gentamicin was higher than to amikacin, with pooled frequency estimates of 22% (95% CI: 12–33%, N = 1386) versus 13% (95% CI: 5–24%, N = 822) in E. coli, and 31% (95% CI: 14–51%, N = 643) versus 13% (95% CI: 1–32%, N = 305) in K. pneumoniae. Nitrofurantoin resistance remained low across all species, with pooled prevalence estimates of 15% (95% CI: 2–35%, N = 550) in E. coli, 12% (95% CI: 0–37%, N = 210) in K. pneumoniae, and 18% (95% CI: 0–49%, N = 149) in EB3. Data were limited for fosfomycin (one study) and cefepime and aztreonam (four studies). Additionally, most antibiotics from the WHO AWaRe Reserve category that can serve as alternatives for EBSL or carbapenem-resistant infections had no available data like newer BL/BLI (ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, meropenem/vaborbactam, imipenem/cilastatin/relebactam), tigecycline, or cefiderocol (Fig. 4). Colistin was tested in a single study68, with 30/30 E. coli reported as resistant but was not included in the present analysis, as the AST method used was Kirby Bauer disk diffusion which is not suitable for colistin due to its high molecular weight, which impairs diffusion in solid media.

Discussion

The prevalence of ESBL-EB carriage and ESBL-EB in clinical samples from Central African studies was alarmingly high. Consistent with our findings, a 2017 systematic review on AMR in Sub-Saharan Africa by Tadesse et al.9, reported resistance rates of 31.5% (95% CI: 6.9–47.7%) and 47.3% (95% CI: 25–62.8%) to 3GC in E. coli and K. pneumoniae, respectively. However, only 15% (21/144) of the included studies were conducted in Central Africa, with most originating from West and East Africa. A more recent systematic review and meta-analysis of AMR in EB among children in Sub-Saharan Africa reported similarly high resistance rates to 3GC in clinical samples: 40.6% (95% CI: 27.7–55%) for E. coli and 84.9% (95% CI: 72.8–92.2%) for Klebsiella spp.102. The pooled prevalence of 3GC-resistant EB carriage in the same population was 32.2% (95% CI: 25.2–40.2%)103. Our meta-analysis thus shows that AMR has the same high prevalence in Central Africa as in other regions of the continent. Historical data show that AMR has recently increased in sub-Saharan Africa. A review by Vlieghe et al. covering the 1955–2008 period reported lower resistance rates, ranging from 0 to 9% across four studies8. The increase in 3GC-resistant strains since 2008 aligns with the global surge in AMR, particularly driven by the worldwide dissemination of CTX-M type ESBLs3.

Resistance to fluoroquinolones was also high, with approximately one-third of strains exhibiting resistance to ciprofloxacin. Tadesse et al.9 reported similar resistance rates of 23.2% in 7877 E. coli strains and 24.3% in 2473 K. pneumoniae strains. In Cameroon, Mouiche et al.10 found even higher resistance rates, with 39.1% of E. coli and 26.7% of K. pneumoniae strains being resistant. Furthermore, the 2022 national surveillance report documented even greater resistance rates, reaching 67.4% for E. coli in bloodstream infections and 57.8% in urinary tract infections (UTIs)104. These findings underscore the need for cautious use of fluoroquinolones in the absence of AST.

Similarly, resistance to cotrimoxazole was high, as reported in the Cameroon annual surveillance report, with rates of 86% in E. coli bloodstream infections (n = 20) and 83% in UTI isolates (n = 253)104. This may be attributed to cotrimoxazole routine use in prophylaxis for immunocompromised HIV patients or as an antiparasitic drug. Given these resistance patterns, cotrimoxazole should not be prescribed empirically for suspected or documented EB infections in the absence of AST confirmation. In line with this, Africa CDC guidelines do not recommend its use for undocumented UTIs105, and the WHO AWaRe antibiotic book ranks cotrimoxazole as a third-line option for uncomplicated UTIs, restricting its use in acute infectious diarrhea following AST106.

Resistance to gentamicin was also concerning. The meta-analysis by Kowalski et al.102 found resistance rates of 42.7% (95% CI: 30–56.5%) in E. coli and 77.6% (95% CI: 65.6–86.3%) in K. pneumoniae pediatric clinical samples. Amikacin was investigated far less frequently than gentamicin, likely due to its lower availability in Sub-Saharan Africa. Interestingly, resistance levels for amikacin remained low, ranging from 0.2% (95%CI: 0–24.5%) in E. coli to 12.6% (95% CI: 1.6–37.7%) in K. pneumoniae. Our results were consistent with the existing literature with a half lower frequency of resistance to amikacin (13%, 95%CI: 5–24% for E. coli and K. pneumoniae 13%, 95%CI: 1–32%) compared to gentamicin (22%, 95%CI: 12–33% for E. coli and K. pneumoniae 31%, 95%CI: 14–51%).

In contrast, nitrofurantoin resistance was low, reinforcing its designation as a preferred antibiotic for uncomplicated lower UTIs according to Africa CDC guidelines105. However, prolonged treatment should be avoided due to potential adverse effects. Notably, no previous meta-analysis has reported data on nitrofurantoin resistance. Similarly, fosfomycin has been scarcely studied, despite being recommended by both EUCAST/CA-SFM and CLSI for EB antibiograms. Given its efficacy as a single-dose oral regimen for uncomplicated lower UTIs in women, and recent data favoring its use in complicated UTIs, fosfomycin should be prioritized in future research107,108.

The heterogeneity among studies was high, likely due to variations in study settings and sample types. Additionally, many studies focused on specific populations, such as patients with diabetes or living with HIV, rather than the general population, making extrapolation challenging. Reporting quality was also inconsistent, with some studies presenting only resistance proportions and, in some cases, pooling resistance data from different EB species. Outliers were also identified, raising concerns about the reliability of microbiological results in some studies, despite a moderate risk of bias. Furthermore, in retrospective studies based on clinical samples, AMR prevalence may have been overestimated, as AST is often at the patient’s expense, limiting access to those who can afford it or those already experiencing treatment failure. Distinguishing between community-acquired and nosocomial infections or bacterial carriage is often difficult, as many hospital-based studies did not specify whether samples were collected from inpatients or outpatients. More than half of the included studies originated from Cameroon, limiting the representativeness of the findings across Central Africa. Even though the visual analysis of the funnel plots (Supplementary Figs. 75–157) would indicate a likely underlying publication bias, we recall that there is no reference method to assess publication bias in prevalence meta-analysis109. A substantial number of studies were excluded following the risk of bias assessment, highlighting the crucial need to strengthen AST capacities in the Central African region. Most studies were conducted in urban settings, limiting the representativeness of our findings. AST capacity remains scarce in rural areas, where microbiology laboratories are often lacking. As a result, resistance data likely reflect urban healthcare contexts, overlooking the burden in remote settings and reinforcing existing diagnostic and treatment inequalities. Finally, this review was limited to human data, excluding animal and environmental sectors, although the burden of AMR is widely recognized as requiring a One Health approach. The choice reflects both the study scope and the current lack of standardized, comparable data from other sectors. Nonetheless, the integration of animal and environmental data remains essential and should be addressed in future systematic reviews. Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first meta-analysis to assess the prevalence of ESBL-EB in both carriage and clinical strains in Central Africa. We conducted a comprehensive review with no restrictions on languages or journal selection. Our methodology adhered to PRISMA guidelines, and we utilized the JBI critical appraisal tools to ensure the data quality.

Several structural and behavioral factors contribute to AMR in Central Africa. Antibiotic consumption data in Central Africa remain scarce and heterogeneous. The MAAP studies estimated it at 5.1/1000 daily defined doses (DDD) in Cameroon and 25.8/ 1000 DDD in Gabon, highlighting the challenge of interpreting sales data in contexts where self-medication and informal supply chains prevail34,110. These conditions also pose challenges regarding antibiotic quality, including the circulation of substandard or falsified products. Of note, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid accounted for half of antibiotic use in Gabon110, while cotrimoxazole represented 20% in Cameroon34, which is consistent with our observations on associated resistance rates.

Self-medication is widespread, driven by cost, accessibility issues, and trust in vendors as highlighted by qualitative studies by Doctors Without Borders (MSF) (Sudan, Guinea Bissau, CAR, and RC)111 and the University of Douala112. In Cameroon, one-third of pharmacy customers purchased antibiotics, half without a prescription113. In Douala, 41.9% (95% CI: 36.5–47.5%) of patients self-medicated for respiratory infections before hospital admission—mainly with cotrimoxazole and amoxicillin114, and 10% of parents gave antibiotics to their children without prior medical consultation115. Misconceptions are common, with some patients using antibiotics for pain relief116, menstrual cramps, or even as a preventive measure to “clean the body” in the absence of water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) facilities117. Incomplete antibiotic courses are also frequent due to financial constraints, medication-sharing, and prescription misunderstandings exacerbated by language barriers111.

Inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions further contribute to AMR. A review of 30,000 prescriptions from primary care facilities in Cameroon found that over one-third contained antibiotics118. In tertiary hospitals, physicians had a mean AMR knowledge score of 56%, and only 42% prescribed appropriate antibiotics for ESBL-producing E. coli infections119. While 93% acknowledged AMR as a national problem, 60% still did not perceive it as an issue in their own wards, mirroring findings of 2013120. Heavy workload, poor hygiene conditions, pressure to deplete antibiotic stocks, and patient demand also influenced prescribing behaviors111. Additionally, AST capacity in the region remains critically low, with only 34/200 laboratories in Gabon and 19/360 in Cameroon equipped to perform it, and reported median AMR detection scores below 50% in Gabon and 60% in Cameroon, with major internal disparities121.

AMR transmission is further exacerbated by environmental and nosocomial factors. WASH is of utmost importance in the community transmission of AMR. Lewnard et al. modeled that improving WASH could prevent 5.7% (95% CI: 3.7–8.0%) of AMR-related deaths in LMICs, when in DRC, only 15.5% of the domestic wastewater is safely treated. Contaminated water sources have been implicated, with ESBL-EB found in 5.3% of the one-third of sachet water contaminated with EB122, and 7.4% in wastewater treatment plants in Kinshasa123.

Animal reservoirs also contribute to AMR transmission, with high rates of 3GC resistance detected in poultry (20.1%)124 and pigs (88%)91. Meat consumption further exacerbates the issue, as higher intake of pork and beef was linked to increased carriage of ciprofloxacin-resistant EB in humans37. A study on 358 rural poultry farmers in Cameroon found low knowledge scores on AMR (0.26/1 ± 0.22), and oxytetracycline, amoxicillin, and colistin were commonly used as growth promoters or for prophylaxis125. While most farmers recognized AMR as a threat to animal health, fewer than half perceived it as a public health risk. In veterinary clinics, Mouiche et al.126 reported that 64% of antibiotics prescribed for dogs belonged to the “highly important” category, and 34.9% to the “critically important.”

Infection prevention and control (IPC) was the most effective mitigation strategy modeled to reduce AMR-related deaths in LMICs, with an estimated 7.8% reduction (95% CI: 5.6–11.0%)127. However, its implementation is hindered by limited IPC knowledge among healthcare workers in Sub-Saharan African countries, including the DRC128. In Uganda, a neighboring country to Central Africa, long-term horizontal transfer of ESBL genes via plasmids, between healthcare and community settings, was evidenced129. E. coli was the most prevalent pathogen in a meta-analysis of healthcare-associated infections in Sub-Saharan Africa130.

Addressing AMR in Central Africa thus requires a multi-sectoral approach, involving human, animal, and environmental health. Strengthening surveillance and generating reliable data are essential first steps. Reducing antibiotic misuse in humans necessitates stricter prescription regulations, improved AMR training for healthcare professionals, market diversification for antibiotics, limitation of fluoroquinolones and cotrimoxazole use in the absence of AST, and reinforced AST capacity in local laboratories. However, without universal health coverage, equitable access to quality antibiotics, medical advice, and specimen cultures and AST remains unattainable. In the agricultural sector, stricter regulations are needed to curb antibiotic use as growth promoters. Finally, AMR transmission will persist unless WASH and IPC measures are strengthened.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the high prevalence of resistant Enterobacterales in Central Africa, including ESBL-EB carriage. These findings emphasize the urgent need to strengthen AST capacity to guide appropriate antibiotic use and enhance participation in the GLASS surveillance system. Additionally, improved access to antibiotics from the WHO Watch and Reserve categories, including carbapenems and new β-Lactam-β-Lactamase Inhibitor Combinations, is crucial to ensuring effective treatment options in these countries.
