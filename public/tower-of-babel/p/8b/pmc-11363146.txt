Quantum-Confined Lifshitz Transition on Weyl Semimetal Td-MoTe2
Authors: Hyunjin Jung, Kyung-Hwan Jin, Minki Sung, Jimin Kim, Jaeyoung Kim, Han Woong Yeom
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Adsorption of alkali atoms onto material surfaces is widely utilized for controlling electronic properties and is particularly effective for two-dimensional materials. While tuning the chemical potential and band gap and creating quantum-confined states are well established for alkali adsorption on semiconductors, the effects on semimetallic systems remain largely elusive. Here, utilizing angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy measurements and density functional theory calculations, we disclose the creation of two-dimensional electron gas and the quantum-confined Lifshitz transition at the surface of a Weyl semimetal Td-MoTe2 by potassium adsorption. Electrons from potassium adatoms are shown to be transferred mainly to the lowest unoccupied band within the gapped part of the Brillouin zone, which, in turn, induces strong surface band bending and quantum confinement in the topmost layer. The quantum-confined topmost layer evolves from a semimetal to a strong metal with a Lifshitz transition departing substantially from the bulk band. The present finding and its underlying mechanism can be exploited for the creation of electronic heterojunctions in van der Waals semimetals.

One very efficient route to tailor the electronic properties of van der Waals (vdW) materials is the atomic or molecular adsorption on their surfaces. Some surface adsorbates, most notably alkali metals, can dope huge amounts of electrons into surface layers while avoiding strong structural and chemical perturbations on the 2D layers themselves. Moreover, alkali adsorbates exhibit a diverse range of effects extending beyond the mere chemical potential shift, such as the band gap engineering,1−3 the Stark effect due to the enhanced surface dipole field,1,4 the metal–insulator transition,1,5 the modification of topological surface states,6−8 the formation of two-dimensional electron gas through quantum confinement,9 and the Lifshitz transition,10 to say a few. A Lifshitz transition refers to changes in the shape or connectivity of Fermi surfaces, such as the emergence/vanishing of an electron (hole) pocket and the splitting/merging of an existing pocket(s).10−14 Such changes induce abrupt changes in the carrier density or sign to result in drastic changes in transport properties. While most of these effects are well demonstrated and established for semiconducting 2D materials, similar works have been limited in semimetallic vdW systems partly due to the challenges related to the enhanced screening and the existence of multiple electronic bands near the Fermi level.15,16

Nevertheless, the study of surface charge doping by adsorbates would definitely be interesting for semimetallic 2D systems, such as topological Weyl semimetals and topological nodal-line semimetals. A type-II topological Weyl semimetal Td-MoTe2 (or Td-WTe2) is a particularly promising material in this context, which has attracted a great deal of attention because of its exotic physical properties, such as topological Fermi arcs, negative nonsaturating magnetoresistance,17−20 pressure-driven superconductivity,21,22 and fractional Chern insulator.23 The orthorhombic Td-structure (called γ-phase with a Pmn21 space group symmetry, Figure 1) is reached from monoclinic 1T′-MoTe2 (a P21/m symmetry) through a structural phase transition at Tc ∼ 250 K, which breaks inversion symmetry.24,25Td-MoTe2 is the first type-II topological Weyl semimetal, where the Fermi arc26 predicted was identified by angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)27−31 and scanning tunneling microscopy (STM).32

In this work, we investigate the unusual effect of surface adsorbate doping on Td-MoTe2 using ARPES and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Upon alkali metal K adsorption, we observe a strong electron doping effect, which is highly localized on the topmost layer. This drives the electronic bands of the surface layer to deviate largely from the bulk band through a paramount surface band bending and the quantum confinement of the topmost layer in a manner similar to that of semiconducting materials. This behavior is mainly induced by the electron transfer to the lowest unoccupied band of in-plane Mo 4d orbital within a large partial gap. The electron filling of the surface band increases drastically upon the increase of the adsorbate coverage, which induces a Lifshitz transition confined within the topmost layer. The double-well-shape unoccupied band and the large partial gap at the zone boundary are crucial to understand the quantum-confined Lifshitz transition. The present finding can be utilized to create 2D electronic states and their heterojunctions on vdW semimetals.

Results and Discussion

Figure 1a,b presents the side and top views of the crystalline structure of Td-MoTe2, respectively. Each layer consists of three sublayers of Te–Mo–Te and is stacked along the c axis by a van der Waals interaction. Our ARPES spectra of pristine Td-MoTe2 measured along the Γ̅–X̅ direction of surface–Brillouin zone (SBZ) are displayed in Figure 1c. The band dispersions observed are consistent with the previous reports27,29,30 and the present DFT calculations (Figure S1 for our own calculation). Our data taken at hv = 68 eV elucidates the electronic structure proximal to the Z point in the bulk Brillouin zone, as detailed in the previous ARPES measurement.30 Along the Γ̅–X̅ line, one can observe mainly one metallic state (denoted as SS), which produces electron pockets in the Fermi surface map. This state has been identified as a trivial surface state localized within the topmost layer. Although largely overlapped with the Fermi contour of SS, the characteristic Fermi arc, a hallmark of a Weyl semimetal, is identified (guided by the yellow box in Figure 1d).27,29,30 On the other hand, one can clearly identify the strongly dispersing hole bands of the bulk. The conduction band of the bulk is only marginally occupied, which appears as a tiny electronic pocket (e in Figure 1d,e) overlapping partly with that of the surface state.

The adsorption of potassium atoms induces drastic changes in the band dispersion measured. Figures 2 and 3 show the evolving band structure of Td-MoTe2 upon the increase of the K coverage along X̅–Γ̅ and Y̅–Γ̅, respectively. The bands shift to a higher binding energy with increasing the coverage (θK, see Figures S2 and S3 for the coverage calibration), which indicates the chemical potential change by electrons transferred from adatoms. However, one can immediately notice that the shift is not a rigid overall shift of the whole band manifold, but is strongly dependent on individual bands. Namely, while the bulk valence and conduction bands (cut1 and cut2, representing h and e in Figure 1d, respectively) shift marginally (about 50 meV) and rigidly only in the initial coverage below 0.2 ML, the surface state SS exhibits a progressive shift in the whole coverage range amounting to 200 meV, an order of magnitude larger than that of the bulk bands (see the Figure S4). Furthermore, a new linear hole-like band, indicated by the black arrow in Figure 2a, emerges, which corresponds to the h2 surface state band in our DFT calculation (see Figure S5). At the same time, another parabolic band β appears along the Γ̅–Y̅ line above 0.2 ML and its occupation increases gradually with a strong energy shift (black arrows in Figure 3a and cut4). As revealed through the DFT calculations (also see Figure S5), this band is another surface state, which is strongly localized to the topmost layer. Supporting its surface origin, its energy-shift trend is consistent with that of SS. The present result indicates that adsorbate doping has a predominant effect on the topmost layer. Moreover, the occupation of another surface band β leads to a Lifshitz transition on the topmost layer (see the Fermi maps at different K coverage in Figure 4). The emergence of the β band below the Fermi level not only alters the topology of Fermi surfaces but also enhances the localization of the electron wave function within the surface layer as discussed below. This produces rather abrupt spectral weight transfers from some bulk bands to the surface state bands between 0.18 and 0.22 ML.

To understand the origin of the new surface state and the Lifshitz transition on the topmost layer, we performed DFT calculations. For the pristine MoTe2 surface bands (Figure 5a), we used the DFT calculation with an extra on-site Coulomb energy correction, which results in a good match with the experimental band structure.13 We set up 2 × 2 and superstructures (see Figure 5f for the latter), which correspond to the coverage of 0.25 and 0.5 ML. One can find that the bulk bands are largely robust against the K adsorption. In clear contrast, the surface state along Γ̅–X̅ shifts to a lower energy with finite intermixing with the bulk bands. This is due to the semimetallic bulk band structures along Γ̅–X̅. The most prominent change, however, can be observed around Y̅ as the splitting off of a conduction band and its shift to a higher binding energy (Figure 5b,c). This corresponds unambiguously to the β parabolic band observed in the experiment. Furthermore, we found that the β parabolic band at Y̅-point is primarily composed of Mo orbital (see the details of orbital projected band calculations in Figure S6). This in-plane orbital character reduces the interlayer coupling, which makes the conduction bandwidth at Y̅ very narrow and a large partial gap opening of about 0.5 eV. At the same time, the spilt band β is well isolated from other bands in energy and its wave function is well localized within the topmost layer.

Figure 5d presents the schematic evolution of the band structure upon increasing the potassium coverage (θK) on Td-MoTe2, as derived from the above DFT results. At a lower coverage than 0.2 ML, the doped electrons fill the electron pocket along Γ̅–X̅ and partly intermixed with the bulk electronic states. However, over 0.2 ML, the occupation of the β band indicates a stronger localization of the doped electrons. This would explain the weak energy shift of the bulk band below 0.2 ML and a strong shift of the β band at a higher coverage. We further calculated the charge distribution of the β band for the pristine surface and the K-adsorbed surface, as shown in Figure 5e,f, respectively. The localization of the electron wave function is enhanced (Figure 5g) from 80 to 100% after the K adsorption. This is due to the quantum confinement effect, which separates this band from the bulk conduction band manifold and prohibits hybridization with bulk bands.

The appearance of an electron band localized on the topmost layer within the band gap can also be understood from the strong band bending in the surface region and the subsequent formation of a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) through the quantum confinement,9,33,34 as observed in a few vdW semiconducting materials with alkali adsorbates.9,33,35,36 The extra charges provided by the alkali atoms will change the Fermi level of the topmost layer, which bends the band along the surface normal and provides an electric potential for quantum well states. The band bending can be quantified by the self-consistent Poisson–Schrödinger (PS) calculation (see Supporting Information for details). Figure 6 presents the calculated results of one-electron potential V(z) and the wave function’s depth distribution from the surface for the localized state β at 0.9 ML. This state shows a quadratic dispersion with m* = 1.1 × me in ARPES measurements, which is consistent well with a conventional 2DEG. The DFT calculations are in good agreement with this result, which demonstrate that the charge density is predominantly localized to the first layer. While a small fraction of the conduction band is occupied around the Brillouin zone center, an extra parabolic band β at Y̅-point becomes occupied as K coverage increases. The latter corresponds to the onset of a Lifshitz transition. The Thomas–Fermi screening length of the present systems is estimated to be 0.6 nm in consistency with the previous works for similar 2D materials,33,37 which matches very well the observed quantum confinement effect (see Supporting Information for the details of the estimation). This observation highlights the validity of our 1D quantum well model. The occupation of additional conduction bands through alkali metal adsorption in turn enhances the localization surface electronic states as indicated by the DFT results, leading to two-dimensional Lifshitz transitions only on the topmost layer of Td-MoTe2.

Conclusion

We utilized in situ ARPES measurements, Poisson–Schrödinger calculations, and DFT calculations to investigate the evolution of the electronic band structure of Td-MoTe2 under the interaction with surface alkali adsorbates. We observed distinct behaviors of the bulk bands and the surface states localized on the topmost layer. While the bulk bands exhibit moderate rigid shifts by the alkali adsorption, the surface bands show much enhanced energy shifts, which lead to the creation of an extra electron pocket at the zone boundary, indicating a Lifshitz transition. The DFT calculations reveal that doped electrons are strongly confined within the topmost layer to create a quantum-confined 2DEG with a Lifshitz transition as a function of the adsorbate coverage. While this system is a semimetallic system with multiple metallic bands, the existence of a large partial gap and an unoccupied band with strong in-plane orbital characteristics therein leads to a well-confined Lifshitz transition. The present study demonstrates that surface alkali adsorption is a very effective way to control the electronic property of van der Waals semimetallic systems when they have partial band gaps. The mechanism can be exploited further to create 2D electronic states and their heterojunctions on vdW semimetals.

Methods

Insitu ARPES measurements were performed at beamline 4.0.3 of the Advanced Light Source (ALS) with a linearly polarized undulator photon beam of 68 eV. Td-MoTe2 crystals (HQ graphene) were cleaved in ultrahigh vacuum environment. Potassium atoms were deposited onto cleaved surfaces of Td-MoTe2 held at 10 K during the continuous ARPES measurements. The coverage of K adatoms was estimated from K 3p photoemission intensity (Figures S2 and S3).

We performed first-principles calculations within the framework of DFT using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof-type generalized gradient approximation for the exchange–correlation functional, as implemented in the Vienna Ab Initio simulation package.38,39 All calculations were carried out using the kinetic energy cutoff of 400 eV on a 12 × 10 × 6 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh for the pristine bulk. A K-adsorbed surface was modeled using a six-layer MoTe2 slab and a 2 × 2 surface supercell with a single K adatom. The on-site Coulomb energy Ueff correction40 with Ueff = 2 eV is considered to reproduce the experimental band structure of Mo 4d. The spin–orbit coupling is included in the self-consistent electronic structure calculation. The lattice constants are taken from experiments21 and the atoms within a supercell are fully relaxed with a force cutoff of 0.01 eV Å–1. We constructed Wannier representations by projecting Bloch states from first-principles calculations of the bulk onto Mo s, Mo d, and Te p orbitals41 in order to calculate the surface-layer density of states and Fermi surfaces using the surface Green’s function method. The band-bending potential and wave function of surface states are obtained by solving the self-consistent Poisson–Schrödinger equation (see the details in the Supporting Information).
