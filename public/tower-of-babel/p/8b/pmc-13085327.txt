Exercise, nutrition, physical agent therapy in older adults with sarcopenic obesity: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Juehan Wang, Lin Mao, Yang Yang, Wangning Peng, Wangxiao Bao, Haijun Li, Rouna Wu, Zhao Yao, Xueqiong Zhu, Daming Wang, Zuobing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenic obesity (SO) is characterized by the co-existence of excess adiposity and low muscle performance, with a high prevalence and poor prognosis in the geriatric population.ObjectivesThis systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) aims to assess the most effective non-pharmacological interventions for SO patients, including nutrition, exercise, and physical agent therapy.MethodsA systematic search of six electronic databases was conducted from their inception until July 5, 2025, for randomized controlled trials. The NMA utilized a random-effects model, pooled mean difference (MD) and standardized mean difference (SMD), with 95% credible intervals (CrI), accounting for correlations within multi-arm trials. Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses were also performed.ResultsTwenty-five studies were included, with 19 studies (n = 1,085 participants) eligible for the NMA. Interventions were categorized into 12 groups: no intervention, nutrition, whole-body electromyostimulation (WBEMS), WBEMS plus nutrition, resistance training (RT), aerobic training (AT), mixed exercise (RT + AT), mixed exercise plus nutrition, energy restriction plus nutrition, high-speed resistance training, RT plus nutrition, and electrical acupuncture plus nutrition. Intervention duration ranged from 8 weeks to 8 months. Mixed exercise was the most effective intervention for reducing body fat percentage (BF%) (MD: -3.8, 95% CrI: -6.8, -0.8), followed by RT (MD: -2.4, 95% CrI: -4.5, -0.46). RT also significantly improved grip strength (MD: 4.4, 95% CrI: 1.6, 6.8). In the fat outcomes combined model, electrical acupuncture plus nutrition showed efficacy (MD: -2.2, 95% CrI: -3.5, -0.78). In the muscular outcomes combined model, both RT (MD: 0.36, 95% CrI: 0.14, 0.56) and RT plus nutrition (MD: 0.83, 95% CrI: 0.092, 1.7) were effective. Sensitivity analyses indicate that the models for body weight, BF%, and the two composite outcomes lack robustness.ConclusionsRT can reduce BF% while simultaneously improving grip strength, representing an effective management strategy for SO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07207-1.

Introduction

The global spread of obesity has become one of the most significant public health issues, driven by lifestyle changes and economic development. The prevalence of severe obesity is predicted to double from 10 to 20% between 2020 and 2035, highlighting the urgency of integrating obesity management into public health strategies [1]. Apart from the globalization of obesity, the prevalence of sarcopenia has risen in modern societies, largely attributable to increasingly sedentary lifestyles and a decline in occupations requiring manual labor [2]. The frequency of sarcopenia in the older population worldwide ranges from 10 to 16% [3]. The hallmarks of obesity include pathological excess body fat accumulation [4], which, through processes such as intramuscular fat deposition [5], oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [6], and the buildup of lipotoxic chemicals that impact muscle metabolism [7], contributes to sarcopenia. In turn, the reduced mechanical activity and muscle weakness characteristic of sarcopenia can exacerbate obesity, creating a vicious cycle. The well-documented bidirectional relationship between obesity and sarcopenia prompts the formal recognition of 'sarcopenic obesity' (SO) as a distinct clinical entity. In 2022, an expert consensus from the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) and the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) defined SO as the co-existence of obesity and diminished skeletal muscle mass and strength and/or reduced physical performance [8].

Complex connections between oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, hormone changes, and lifestyle compose the pathogenesis of SO [9]. The synergistic effects of sarcopenia and obesity substantially elevate the risk of adverse clinical outcomes and all-cause mortality risk, compared to either condition alone [10]. The mortality rate for patients with SO is 24% greater than non-SO patients, which is particularly significant in men [11]. The dismal prognosis of SO is accelerated and exacerbated by age. Older adults with SO face an elevated risk of numerous adverse outcomes, including falls, fractures, metabolic disorders, cognitive impairment, and depression [12, 13]. Among those 75 years of age and above, the frequency of SO is 23% [14]. Consequently, research on the health impacts of SO in older adults is of substantial scientific and public health importance, especially in light of the growing worldwide aging population.

Currently, no targeted pharmacological therapy exists for SO, instead, management approaches mostly concentrate on symptomatic relief and risk factor modification [15]. Key risk factors for SO include advanced age, comorbidities, malnutrition, and physical inactivity [16]. Exercise and nutritional interventions are therefore seen as successful non-pharmacological treatments for SO [17]. The treatment of SO necessitates a dual objective of fat loss and muscle gain, which calls for intervention strategies that integrate nutritional supplementation, calorie restriction, and multi-modal exercise training. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have evaluated exercise or nutrition in isolation [18–20], yet a synthesis of combined approaches is needed. Establishing an optimal treatment regimen requires both the comprehensive integration of multiple modalities and comparative efficacy analyses. Network meta-analysis (NMA) is a powerful method for such comparisons, offering substantial clinical value. In addition, as a key modality in rehabilitation medicine, non-motor physical agent therapy has been increasingly utilized in SO clinical management [9]. However, to our knowledge, the comparative effectiveness of these promising physical modalities has not been systematically evaluated. Therefore, a thorough systematic review and NMA on the effectiveness of non-pharmacological therapies, including exercise, nutrition, and physical agent therapy for geriatric SO patients were lacking prior to this study. This study employed NMA to specifically evaluate which nutrition, exercise, physical agent therapy, or combination intervention measures are effective in improving body composition and muscle performance in geriatric SO patients. This includes muscle-related outcomes (appendicular fat free mass [AFFM], grip strength, and gait speed) and fat-related outcomes (body weight [BW], body fat percentage [BF%], and body fat in kilograms [BF in kg]). It is anticipated that these findings will inform evidence-based clinical guidelines, optimize SO management, and ultimately improve outcomes for this vulnerable population, thereby providing actionable evidence for healthcare policymakers and providers.

Methods

This systematic review and NMA of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/, identifier CRD42025633889). The PRISMA 2021 guidelines were applied for reporting NMA [21].

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, WOS, EMBASE, CINAHL, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) for relevant RCTs from database inception through July 5, 2025. No restrictions were applied regarding publication language or date. We also searched for gray literature using the https://clinicaltrials.gov/ and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry (ICTRP). No unpublished or ongoing trials were included. Two reviewers (JW, LM) independently screened all records. The complete search strategy, including all terms and syntax, is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study selection

We included RCTs with either parallel-group or crossover designs that addressed our predefined PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes) criteria, detailed in Table 1. SO is defined by the coexistence of obesity with reduced skeletal muscle mass and strength and/or physical function. Due to evolving and heterogeneous diagnostic criteria for SO, we included studies that explicitly reported specific diagnostic criteria for both skeletal muscle mass/function and obesity (Supplementary Table 2). RCTs with specific target groups, like HIV patients or truck drivers, as well as those focused on people with particular medical conditions, like cancer (breast cancer, prostate cancer, non-small-cell lung cancer, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer), heart failure, Crohn's disease, COPD, or chronic kidney disease, were not included. RCTs including pharmaceutical therapies and trials without a detailed explanation of the intervention were also disqualified. Two independent reviewers (JW, YY) screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles, using the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction and quality assessment

Data from the final included full-text publications were separately extracted by two reviewers, and any differences were settled by a third-party discussion. The research characteristics, patient characteristics, interventions, adherence to the interventions, and patient outcomes were all included in the standardized data gathering form that we created. Two separate reviewers evaluated the RCTs' methodological quality using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, consulting the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [22].

Data synthesis: interventions

Based on a systematic analysis of the included studies, interventions were pragmatically grouped into 12 categories: [1] no intervention, [2] nutrition supplements, [3] whole-body electromyostimulation (WBEMS), [4] WBEMS + nutrition supplements, [5] resistance training(RT), [6] aerobic training (AT), [7] mixed exercise (RT + AT), [8] mixed exercise (RT + AT) + nutrition supplements, [9] energy restriction + nutrition supplements, [10] high speed resistance training, [11] RT + nutrition supplements, [12] electrical acupuncture + nutrition supplements. Similar interventions were grouped within these predefined categories.

Statistical analysis

The mean difference (MD) between baseline and post-intervention values was calculated for each outcome measure and used as the primary metric for statistical analysis. Most studies reported means and standard deviations at both baseline and follow-up. Following Cochrane Handbook methods, we input a correlation coefficient of 0.5 to calculate MD and standard deviation when not directly reported. If the MD and standard deviations were unavailable, they were derived from P-values and credible intervals (CrI). The NMA employed a Bayesian random-effects model construction that accounts for study-to-study associations in multi-arm trials and utilizes node partitioning to detect inconsistencies. The NMA was constructed by the gemtc package [23]. We ran three Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains. Prior distributions were specified using the Bayesian inference engine implemented in Just Another Gibbs Sampler (JAGS). Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistics, estimated via the DerSimonian-Laird method. The Egger test was used to assess potential publication bias in the data, identifying asymmetry in the funnel plot. The MD was used for analyses of the same continuous outcome. In models including multiple outcomes, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was employed to ensure comparability. We developed two distinct analytical models. For the fat model, the first available outcome measure was selected in the following prior order: BW, BF%, BF in kg [24]. The first accessible outcome measure for the muscular model was chosen in the following order: AFFM, grip strength, gait speed [25]. For every outcome model, we also performed a number of subgroup analyses. First, we examined gender-stratified data from studies that included either only women, only males, or both genders reported separately. Second, we compared interventions of shorter duration (< 14 weeks) to those of longer duration (≥ 14 weeks) [24]. Finally, sensitivity analyses were performed for all models; this involved sequentially excluding each study to evaluate the robustness of the results. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses, including the NMA and subgroup analyses, were performed using R (version 4.1.3) and OpenBUGS software.

Results

We identified and screened 1355 records, from which 25 RCTs met the selection criteria (Fig. 1). One study was excluded due to non-comparable intervention components that prevented network formation. Two studies only performed a secondary analysis of the earlier research. Two studies were excluded for using outcome measures that differed from the primary focus of this study. Duplicate publications from one trial were consolidated into a single network node [26, 27]. Consequently, our final NMA included 19 nodes [26–45] evaluating non-pharmacological SO interventions among 1085 older adults. Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flow diagram documenting study selection.

Characteristics of the included RCTs

Among 19 eligible RCTs, intervention types included: exercise-only (n = 13), nutrition-only (n = 1), combined nutrition-exercise (n = 2), and nutrition-physical agent therapy (n = 3). Study populations comprised mixed-gender (n = 5), female-only (n = 11), and male-only (n = 3) cohorts. Sample sizes ranged from 21 to 137 participants. Studies originated from 9 countries, with the majority taking place in China, see Table 2 for complete distribution. We systematically classified 25 distinct intervention protocols from these studies into 12 clinically relevant categories based on their core components. Intervention durations spanned 8 weeks to 8 months, with 12-week programs being most frequent (n = 9). Most exercise interventions (n = 6) employed twice-weekly 60-min sessions. BW

Although BW was a frequently reported outcome measure, 11 studies lacked post-intervention weight data, leaving 8 studies (15 pairwise comparisons, 5 treatment groups) for analysis. While no interventions demonstrated statistically significant effects on BW, mixed exercise programs showed the largest reduction (MD: −4.4, 95% CrI: −9, 1.6).

BF%

BF% was the most common reported outcome, included in 18 studies (34 pairwise comparisons, 12 treatment groups). Mixed exercise performed the greatest significant BF% reduction (MD: −3.8, 95% CrI: −6.8, −0.8). RT also significantly reduced BF%, though less substantially (MD: −2.4, 95% CrI: −4.5, −0.46). While other interventions showed non-significant effects, electrical acupuncture plus nutrition supplements produced the largest BF% reduction (MD: −6.5, 95% CrI: −13.0, 0.22).

BF in kg

Absolute BF in kg provides a weight-independent adiposity measurement, complementing BF% assessments. Eight studies (20 pairwise comparisons, nine treatment groups) reported BF in kg. Unfortunately, no intervention significantly reduced BF in kg, though mixed exercise showed the largest non-significant reduction (MD: −5.51, 95% CrI: −13.0, 2.4).

AFFM

AFFM provides a direct measure of limb muscle mass, serving as a sensitive indicator of muscle quantity changes. AFFM was included in eight studies (15 pairwise comparisons, six treatment groups). While no significant effects were observed, RT combined with nutrition supplements showed the greatest AFFM increase (MD: 0.89, 95% CrI: −0.37, 2.1).

Grip strength

Grip strength, as a common muscle function measure, was assessed in 11 studies (27 pairwise comparisons, 10 treatment groups). The most effective intervention for improving grip strength was RT (MD: 4.4, 95% CrI: 1.6, 6.8). While other interventions showed non-significant effects, high-speed resistance training demonstrated the greatest efficacy (MD: 4.8, 95% CrI: −2.9, 0.13).

Gait speed

Gait speed, another key functional outcome, was analyzed in 11 studies (22 pairwise comparisons, 9 treatment groups). Node-splitting analysis revealed significant inconsistency, leading to exclusion of one study (final: 19 pairwise comparisons, 9 treatment groups). While no intervention significantly improved gait speed, mixed exercise showed the largest non-significant improvement (MD: 0.21, 95% CrI: −0.008, 0.39).

Fat outcomes combined model

We developed a composite model by hierarchically merging fat-related outcomes based on our predefined outcome priority. The analysis incorporated 18 studies (34 pairwise comparisons, 12 treatment groups). The most effective and only significant intervention was electrical acupuncture plus nutrition supplements (MD: −2.2, 95% CrI: −3.5, −0.78). The combined model included 8 BW and 10 BF% studies, suggesting predominant BF% influence in the composite outcome.

Muscular outcomes combined model

All results related to muscle were combined in a model, with 16 studies available (35 pairwise comparisons, 11 treatment groups), based on predefined outcome priorities. Combined RT and nutrition supplements showed the largest significant improvement in muscle quantity and quality (MD: 0.83, 95% CrI: 0.092, 1.7). RT alone was also significant (MD: 0.36, 95% CrI: 0.14, 0.56). Other treatments were not significant. It is worth noting that AT uniquely reduced muscle outcomes (MD: −0.086, 95% CrI: −0.73, 0.5). The combined model equally weighted AFFM (n = 8) and grip strength (n = 8) outcomes, providing a comprehensive assessment of muscle quantity and quality.

Inconsistency

Network inconsistency tests revealed no significant inconsistency for: BW (P = 0.0531), BF% (P > 0.2517), BF in kg (P > 0.5011), AFFM (P = 0.7734), grip strength (P > 0.5227), fat model (P > 0.257), or muscular models (P > 0.1365). However, gait speed analysis showed significant network inconsistency (P > 0.0037). Exclusion of one outlier study [26] resolved this inconsistency (post-exclusion P = 0.0776).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Duration subgroup analysis (14-week cutoff) showed non-significant between-group differences (Fig. 6), though effect sizes consistently favored longer interventions (≥ 14 weeks) across all outcomes except grip strength. Gender subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences between male-only and female-only cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, gender subgroup analyses were not performed for BW, BF in kg, AFFM, grip strength, gait speed, and the muscular model due to insufficient male-only studies (< 3 per model). We conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of all models (Supplementary Table 3). For the models of BF in kg, AFFM, grip strength, and gait speed, omission of any individual study did not substantially alter the results. In the BW and BF% models, the significant effect of mixed exercise was lost only upon exclusion of the study by Ferhi et al. [37]. Similarly, the significance of RT in the BF% model was lost after removing the study by Luis et al. [44]. In the combined model for fat outcomes, the significance of electrical acupuncture combined with nutritional supplements disappeared following the exclusion of either the study by Kemmler et al. [27] or Liao et al. [35]. Within the muscular outcomes model, RT alone demonstrated stability. Discussion

This systematic review and NMA aimed to identify the most effective non-pharmacological interventions, including nutrition, exercise, and physical agent therapy, for improving body composition and muscle quality in elderly SO patients. As the dual-disease co-occurrence pattern of SO, separate modeling was conducted for all fat-related outcomes and all muscle-related outcomes to explore the combined efficacy of interventions on fat and muscle, aiming to identify effective treatments that simultaneously reduce body fat and increase muscle function, rather than focusing on outcomes in isolation. In the single-outcome NMA models, several nutritional and exercise interventions demonstrated efficacy. Mixed exercise was most effective in reducing BF%, followed by RT. However, neither mixed exercise nor RTg significantly reduced BF in kg. RT significantly improved grip strength but had no effect on walking speed. No interventions significantly reduced BW or enhanced AFFM, but the number of studies included in these two models was also limited. Electrical acupuncture combined with nutrition supplements was effective in the fat model. RT, both alone and combined with nutrition supplements, was effective for improving muscle outcomes. However, the effect estimates for the combined intervention were not robust in sensitivity analyses.

Evidence-based guidelines continue to promote energy restriction as a fundamental therapeutic weight loss therapy [50]. However, energy restriction combined with nutrition supplements was ineffective in all fat-related models, including three single outcome models and the composite model. Firstly, our systematic search did not identify any studies that employed energy restriction as a standalone intervention for SO. This absence is likely because the potential benefits of weight loss are offset by the significant risk of exacerbating sarcopenia, which limits the use of energy restriction alone in SO [51]. Furthermore, the protocol from one included trial in NMA [30], involving 12% energy restriction with nutrition supplements, was originally designed for young, healthy, lean, or slightly overweight adults [52]. This approach may not have the same weight loss effects in older SO patients [53]. RT is recommended to counteract the muscle mass loss associated with energy restriction. For instance, Valentina et al. [49] recently examined the combined effects of energy restriction, RT, and protein supplementation on SO. However, the absence of a non-intervention control group precluded its inclusion in the NMA. Therefore, future RCTs are warranted to rigorously evaluate the efficacy of this multi-component intervention. Such a comprehensive strategy, integrating energy restriction, RT, and protein supplementation, may represent the optimal therapeutic approach for SO management.

It is noteworthy that both RT and mixed exercise were effective in reducing BF% but not absolute fat mass. These findings are consistent with previous meta-analyses examining the effect of RT on body composition in SO patients [54, 55]. The primary reason is that the two models included different literature, which must be acknowledged. Because of varying diagnoses of SO [8], the baseline body composition of the populations included in different studies may have slight differences. Secondly, this result may reflect that RT and mixed training promote muscle hypertrophy more effectively than they promote weight loss in SO patients. This disparity would result in a smaller net reduction in overall mass relative to fat loss, consequently amplifying the observed decrease in BF%. However, RT itself is not a monolithic intervention, which encompasses various modalities, intensities, volumes, and durations. Therefore, future research should investigate how these specific RT parameters independently influence body composition in the SO population.

Muscle loss constitutes another cardinal manifestation of SO, frequently accompanied by age-related myocellular apoptosis. Therapeutic strategies should therefore focus on preserving or increasing muscle mass and quality. RT can significantly increase grip strength, but its effects on AFFM and gait speed are limited, suggesting that RT may be liable in improving upper limb muscle strength. We reviewed the RT protocols included in the NMA, with most studies employing combined upper and lower limb RT, except for the study by Vasconcelos et al. [43], who used only lower limb RT. Margoni et al. investigated gender-related differences in the extent and time course of muscle soreness and neuromuscular fatigue induced by three different RT protocols targeting the upper or lower limbs. They reported that neuromuscular fatigue was greater in the lower limbs than in the upper limbs for both sexes [56]. The high neuromuscular fatigue observed in the lower limbs following RT may impede improvements in muscle mass and physical function among older adults with SO. Additionally, the typically greater loss of lower-body compared to upper-body muscle mass in older adults may increase the perceived difficulty of training and diminish its efficacy. Future RCTs in older adults with SO should directly compare the effects of upper- and lower-body RT regimens on body composition. Additionally, no significant intervention effects were observed in the AFFM and gait speed models, possibly due to the lack of standardized parameters for the interventions. Concurrently, meta-analyses and NMA ought to place specific emphasis on comparative exercise parameters, such as volume, intensity, and frequency.

SO is characterized by both obesity and sarcopenia. To comprehensively evaluate obesity, this study employed BW to track overall mass changes, BF% to assess body composition shifts, and BF in kg to obtain precise quantitative data. For muscle-related features, we use AFFM to assess muscle mass, grip strength, and gait speed to evaluate muscle quality. This study adopted a dual-model analysis strategy, constructing independent models for obesity-related and muscle-related outcomes to systematically assess the dual pathological characteristics of SO. The method simultaneously facilitates detailed evaluation of a single outcome and performs an integrated evaluation of obesity and sarcopenia, thereby elucidating the dual therapeutic efficacy of interventions and identifying those capable of concomitantly reducing adiposity while augmenting lean mass. In the fat model, we were surprised to find that the combination of electrical acupuncture and nutrition supplements performed the best therapeutic effects. Electrical acupuncture, which integrates traditional acupuncture with modern electrical stimulation, is thought to facilitate weight loss through multiple physiological mechanisms, such as modulating neuroendocrine function, regulating lipid metabolism, and suppressing central appetite pathways [57, 58]. Electroacupuncture shows promise as a viable alternative to conventional exercise for managing weight. Older adults with limited mobility, multiple comorbidities, or residing in community settings often encounter barriers to traditional exercise interventions, such as a lack of professional guidance, low adherence, and elevated safety risks. As a non-pharmacological and relatively safe integrated therapy, the combination of electroacupuncture and nutritional support may provide a more feasible and personalized management alternative for this population. However, electroacupuncture entails multiple parameter domains, such as acupoint selection, treatment duration, and stimulation frequency. Standardized treatment protocols remain to be established in future work.

In terms of muscular models, both RT alone and combined nutrition supplements are effective, but the results of combined interventions are inconsistent. As the details of RT have been thoroughly discussed in preceding sections, this section analyzes potential reasons for the inconsistent outcomes of combined interventions and comprehensively evaluates existing research evidence to provide guidance for the design of future RCTs. Both grip strength and the muscular model exhibited publication bias. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed that the overall efficacy outcome for RT alone was robust and remained unchanged. However, the combined intervention of RT and nutrition supplements became statistically insignificant upon the sequential removal of five studies. A review of five studies revealed potential methodological concerns. First, the study by Chiu et al. was assessed to have a high risk in three dimensions, including randomization, missing data, and outcome measurement [45]. Second, the four-arm design of the study by Kim et al. introduced analytical complexity, complicating its integration into the meta-analysis [40]. Third, the findings of Nabucco et al. are limited by a small sample size, reducing the statistical power and reliability [31]. Finally, the studies by Liao et al. and Kemmer et al. potentially introduced bias by drawing their cohorts from previously published datasets, raising concerns of overlap or double-counting [26, 27, 34]. Similarly, the studies by Liao et al. and Kemmer et al. influenced the robustness of the obesity-related outcome model. The exclusion of either study resulted in the effect of electrical acupuncture combined with nutrition supplements losing statistical significance. Furthermore, the robustness of BW and BF models was also affected by the study by Ferhi et al. [37]. Upon review, this trial's inclusion criteria were based solely on muscle function indicators, with no restriction on muscle mass. This approach may have selected a population with higher baseline functional activity than those in other trials. Furthermore, most studies reporting grip strength recorded only a single measurement or did not specify the measurement protocol, thereby deviating from the standard practice of obtaining three repeated measurements. Here, we must point out that the overall quality of RCTs on SO is notably low. Specifically, only two trials were classified as low risk, while nine exhibited a high risk of bias in at least one domain. Furthermore, statistical power was frequently limited by small sample sizes. Nine studies enrolled fewer than 20 participants per intervention group. In total, nearly half of the studies enrolled exclusively female participants, while only three were conducted in exclusively male cohorts. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings is limited by a significant sex imbalance, particularly for male populations. Accordingly, these findings must be interpreted with caution. In summary, SO is a field with valuable clinical significance and substantial research potential, but is also marked by considerable knowledge gaps. Rigorous, large-scale, high-quality RCTs employing unified diagnostic criteria are urgently needed to establish effective, comprehensive interventions and to identify optimal treatment strategies through subsequent NMA.

The varying diagnostic criteria for SO constitute a primary source of clinical heterogeneity in research on this condition [59]. As SO involves the confluence of two distinct conditions, obesity and sarcopenia, each lacking a single, uniform diagnostic standard, the resultant heterogeneity in SO diagnosis is particularly pronounced. Our analysis found that obesity diagnosis focused on BMI, BF in kg, BF%, and waist circumference with inconsistent thresholds. Moreover, sarcopenia diagnosis exhibited variability not only in the cut-off values for muscle mass but also in the choice of functional assessments. Consequently, few studies applied a specific, consistent SO diagnostic standard, hindering meaningful subgroup analysis. These inconsistent criteria directly lead to heterogeneity in disease severity and the pathophysiological states of included patient populations, as this study demonstrates. Therefore, future research urgently requires high-quality comparative studies based on unified, consensus-driven diagnostic criteria to generate precise and generalizable evidence. Until such evidence is available, clinicians must carefully consider the alignment between their patients' characteristics and the heterogeneous populations analyzed in this and similar studies when applying these findings.

This study employed a systematic review and NMA, strictly adhering to the guidelines of the Cochrane Collaboration Manual, and included a large number of targeted populations. The NMA methodology allows for the simultaneous direct and indirect comparison of multiple interventions within a single statistical model, thereby increasing the precision and breadth of the comparative effectiveness analysis. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the pragmatic categorization of similar but non-identical interventions into single nodes may have introduced clinical or methodological heterogeneity, potentially biasing the pooled effect estimates. Second, differences in diagnostic criteria, intervention duration, subject characteristics, and geographic settings among the included trials make the transferability hypothesis unavoidable. Third, some network analysis results showed inconsistencies. Finally, while composite models integrating obesity and muscle function indicators offer a multifaceted assessment, their application in clinical decision-making warrants caution. Although we explored possible sources of this inconsistency, we cannot definitively rule out its effect on the findings. Despite these limitations, this synthesis provides critical evidence to inform future clinical guidelines and the development of interventions aimed at improving body composition and muscle function in older adults with SO.

Conclusion

This NMA indicates that RT alone is the most effective strategy for improving physical condition in SO patients, as it simultaneously reduces BF% and increases grip strength. Mixed exercise demonstrates superior efficacy than RT in reducing BF%, though neither significantly improved muscle mass nor function. The current evidence does not support the significant efficacy of any intervention for BW, BF in kg, AFFM, or gait speed, likely due to the limited number of studies available for the corresponding analyses. Nutritional support alone is not recommended for SO patients. Regarding the two outcomes combined models, electrical acupuncture plus nutrition supplements demonstrates significant fat-reducing effects, while RT alone or combined with nutrition supplements effectively improve muscle performance. However, clinical decision-making based on combined models requires caution. Comprehensive intervention protocols integrating energy restriction, RT, and protein supplementation, as well as alternative modalities such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation, retain significant potential for clinical application.
