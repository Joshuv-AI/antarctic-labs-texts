A harmonised gridded input dataset for simulation modelling of the major crops of South Asia
Authors: Paresh B. Shirsath, Prasun Gangopadhyay, Pramod K. Aggarwal, Uttam Puri Goswami, Aniket Deo, Anasuya Barik, Sanjoy K. Bandyopadhyay, Roshan B. Ojha, Sarath Chandran MA, Vinay K. Sehgal, A.G. Chandrapala, Rameshwar Rimal, Rahul Tripathi, Md. Abdus Salam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Crop modelling at regional scales depends critically on the quality and representativeness of climate, soil, crop, and management input data. Existing global products provide detailed, bias-corrected climate data even at regional scales. For other inputs, these products provide spatial consistency, but they often underrepresent the agronomic heterogeneity that shapes crop productivity across the region. This limitation is particularly important in South Asia, where fragmented landscapes, diverse farming systems, and variable management practices create strong spatial variability.This paper presents a high-resolution gridded input dataset developed specifically for process-based crop simulation modelling across South Asia. The dataset integrates soil properties, crop distribution, the extent of rainfed and irrigated areas, crop phenology, planting dates, nitrogen application rates, and representative irrigation and fertiliser schedules. It covers 10 major crops that together account for nearly 80% of the region's agricultural area. All variables are harmonised within a unified 50 × 50 km spatial framework.The dataset is designed for integration with major crop modelling platforms, including DSSAT, APSIM, and InfoCrop. It is also compatible with ISIMIP-aligned climate inputs. The dataset was developed through a systematic synthesis of regional literature, government reports, online repositories, expert knowledge, and selected global products, and was verified with stakeholders' input. This approach ensured spatial completeness and internal consistency across variables. Soil hydraulic parameters were harmonised using pedo-transfer functions. Crop and management variables represent dominant production systems and typical farmer practices circa 2019. The modular structure allows users to apply individual layers independently or recalibrate them using local observations where available.The dataset is intended primarily for sub-national to regional-scale applications. It supports assessments of crop productivity, climate sensitivity, drought response, management interventions, and adaptation planning under current and future climate conditions. Potential applications include crop insurance, climate change impact assessment, early warning systems, and evaluation of adaptation strategies across South Asia.Some uncertainty remains unavoidable, particularly in data-sparse regions and heterogeneous smallholder systems. Management variables reflect representative regional practices and should not be interpreted as prescriptive recommendations. Similarly, the 50 × 50 km spatial resolution captures broad regional patterns but cannot fully resolve local variability. Users are therefore encouraged to conduct sensitivity and uncertainty analyses and incorporate updated or locally calibrated inputs where finer-scale applications are required. These datasets are available from Zenodo with an Open Access licence.

Background

Process-based crop simulation models (CSMs), including DSSAT, APSIM, InfoCrop, and WOFOST, are central tools for assessing yield responses, climate impacts, and adaptation strategies by representing interactions among weather, soil, genotype, and management [[1], [2], [3]]. Coordinated efforts such as AgMIP have further advanced large-scale applications of these models [4,5]. Rapid expansion of global datasets, including SPAM, SoilGrids, ERA5, and WorldCereal, has enabled regional and global CSM applications [[6], [7], [8]].

Yet critical limitations persist. Global simulation analyses use advanced bias-corrected climate data. Still, spatially consistent datasets overstate local agronomic fidelity, smoothing fine-scale variability in management practices, cultivar parameters, and soil properties, which are poorly represented in data from developing countries [5,9]. This is likely to introduce bias into the results, as uncertainty in management inputs, sowing dates, irrigation scheduling, and nitrogen application can exceed that of climate forcing [5,10,11]. This imbalance is particularly consequential in rainfed/partially irrigated or fertilised smallholder systems [12]. Recommendations on climate change impacts and their mitigation, based on such global analysis, are therefore subject to errors in resource-constrained environments. Against this backdrop, this study develops a high-resolution, gridded input dataset for South Asia, integrating locally derived information with globally consistent products to better reflect ground realities while retaining broad comparability.

Data Description

The dataset architecture captures the core components required to simulate crop growth. Each component is generated as a spatially explicit layer at a common grid resolution of 50 × 50 km, ensuring consistency across inputs and compatibility with ISIMIP climate data [13,14] and other modelling frameworks. Additionally, we have uploaded a high-resolution dataset at 0.5-degree resolution. The datasets include: (i) a crop extent defining the spatial extent and area of the target crop, (ii) crop phenology, (iii) soil datasets representing both surface and sub-surface properties relevant to water and nutrient dynamics, (iv) gridded nitrogen application data, (v) irrigation extent and typology, (vi) spatially explicit planting dates, and (vii) irrigation and nitrogen application schedules aligned with crop growth stages. All datasets are modular, interoperable, and designed for cross-model use. They can be used independently for other studies or as an integrated input package across different CSMs. The methodological approach emphasises transparency and reproducibility, with clearly documented processing steps, standardised formats, and explicit linkage between source data and final outputs. We have used rice as an example to demonstrate the developed datasets. South Asia cultivates rice on about 60 million hectares, accounting for 37.5% of the global area. The same harmonization protocols were applied to all 10 crops (cotton, chickpea, groundnut, monsoon maize, monsoon rice, millets, mustard, winter sorghum, soybean and wheat), which are now being considered. The complete datasets for all crops are available on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.20037050). The repository includes a README file (README_ACASA_datasets.docx), and each data type is in a separate subfolder. For instance, in India's Indo-Gangetic Plain (IGP), the WHC is considerably higher, ranging from 400 to 600 mm. Conversely, when calculated using global soil depth data, the WHC ranges from 200 to 300 mm, accounting for nearly 50% of the global WHC. This discrepancy is critical for crop modelling because WHC directly determines the size of the soil moisture reservoir available to crops. Overestimating WHC using global soil depth can artificially delay water stress, increase evapotranspiration, and overestimate crop yield and resilience under rainfed conditions. Regionally constrained soil depth provides a more realistic view of rooting-zone limits and soil water storage, thereby improving crop water-stress simulation. Therefore, incorporating region-specific soil depth information is essential for improving the reliability of process-based crop models in water-limited agroecosystems.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset prepared for CSM followed a systematic methodology, compiling and reanalysing it alongside published global and local data. The detailed methodology of each dataset is presented in the following section. All the spatial databases were prepared at 0.05-degree resolution for internal consistency and later resampled to 0.5 degrees with WGS4326 projection (geographic latitude/longitude) to synchronise with widely used ISIMIP [13,14] climate data. These 0.5-degree datasets are available in the repository. We have added an example flowchart for calculating nitrogen consumption in Supplementary 2. Additionally, we have added source code for the same, available at https://github.com/aniketdeo1992/ACASA_Data-in-brief.

Study region

South Asia is one of the most intensively cultivated and agriculturally diverse regions globally, supporting a wide range of cropping systems shaped by strong gradients in climate, soils, and water availability. Major crops include rice, wheat, maize, pulses, and oilseeds, with highly intensified systems such as rice–wheat rotations dominating large parts of the Indo-Gangetic Plains [24]. Agriculture remains central to livelihoods, employing around 40–50% of the workforce across countries such as India, Bangladesh, and Nepal [25]. The sector contributes approximately 15–20% to the region's gross domestic product (GDP), underscoring its continued economic importance [25,26]. Farming systems are dominated by smallholders, with >80% of farms operating on <2 hectares and contributing substantially to food production [27]. These systems are highly heterogeneous, shaped by local resource constraints, access to inputs, and institutional conditions. Agricultural production is strongly influenced by climate variability, particularly monsoon dynamics, temperature extremes, and water availability, which directly affect productivity and stability [25,26]. This combination of high crop diversity, smallholder dominance, and climate sensitivity makes South Asia a critical region for developing high-resolution datasets to support robust crop simulation and agricultural planning.

Crop mask

The crop extents and spatially explicit crop area estimates were developed using an integrated approach that combines crop statistics data and gridded land-use information. District-level crop area statistics for major crops (2001–2019) were obtained from official government sources [16,[28], [29], [30]] and served as the primary reference for the total cultivated area. These data were harmonised with a gridded dataset of cropped area at 0.5° spatial resolution, with cropped area per grid cell, derived from Sentinel 2 LULC data [31]. The LULC dataset was processed to extract the total cropped area within each grid cell, ensuring consistency with administrative boundaries through spatial alignment and masking.

To disaggregate district-level crop statistics to the grid level, a proportional allocation method was applied. First, crop-specific fractions within each district were estimated using reported crop area shares per season. These fractions were then used to allocate the total cropped area in each 0.5° grid cell among crops, assuming that the relative crop composition within a district is reflected spatially in the distribution of cropped area. Seasonal differentiation was incorporated by assigning crop areas to specific growing seasons using crop calendars and reported statistics, thereby preserving the temporal dimension of cropping patterns.

The allocation procedure constrained grid-level crop areas to match reported district totals for each crop and season. This ensured statistical consistency while preserving spatial heterogeneity in land use. The resulting dataset provides a harmonised, spatially continuous crop extent at moderate resolution, suitable for analysing crop-specific exposure, productivity, and climate risk. Where district crop statistics were inconsistent, we adjusted distributions using variability from MAPSPAM [32] alongside reported district totals. For areas lacking district records, such as in Pakistan and Afghanistan, MAPSPAM values were retained. This approach preserves the underlying spatial patterns while aligning totals with official statistics [33]where available.

Planting dates

Planting dates were developed at 0.5° resolution by integrating multiple data sources and applying crop- and region-specific rules to ensure spatial realism and consistency with field practices. Baseline sowing windows were compiled from published crop calendars, government contingency plans [34], and district-level planning datasets from agricultural insurance agencies [[33], [34], [35]]. These sources were cross-checked and refined based on stakeholder input to align with prevailing agronomic practices. For Sri Lanka, planting dates were derived from national crop calendars developed by the National Agrarian Information and Communication Centre, which account for the bimodal rainfall regime and associated cropping seasons. In Nepal and other mountainous regions, planting dates for monsoon crops were stratified by elevation using gridded topographic data [36]. Crop calendars for different elevation zones were applied to account for temperature gradients and growing-period constraints.

For rice and other major monsoon crops, planting dates were tied to the onset of the monsoon. Onset was derived from daily CHIRPS precipitation data and defined as the first occurrence of three consecutive rainy days that exceeded a threshold (2.5 mm day-1), constrained to the IMD climatological onset window for each region. This approach captures local rainfall variability while maintaining climatological consistency. In northwestern India and adjoining regions of Pakistan, where irrigation supports pre-monsoon sowing, planting dates were assigned using documented regional crop calendars and agricultural reports. These sources reflect established irrigated practices and avoid reliance on rainfall triggers alone.

For post-monsoon crops, which depend less on the onset of rainfall and more on residual moisture and irrigation, planting dates were assigned based on state- and country-level agricultural reports. These provide stable sowing windows across regions. For crops with uniform management practices, fixed planting dates were used. For example, mustard was assigned a planting date of 15 September.

All planting dates were harmonised to the 0.5° grid using spatial interpolation and rule-based allocation. The final dataset captures both climatic drivers and management controls, providing a consistent and spatially explicit representation of crop calendars for large-scale modelling.

Crop phenology

A database of dominant flowering and maturity dates was compiled for rice, wheat, maize, chickpea, mustard, soybean and sorghum across five major agro-ecological zones of India. The dataset includes key phenological parameters, especially anthesis duration and total maturity duration, which are critical inputs for calibrating crop models. The information was synthesised from existing published literature [33,[37], [38], [39], [40]]. To improve the reliability and regional relevance of the dataset, consultations were held with crop experts and researchers familiar with the respective agro-ecological zones. This dataset provides a harmonised representation of the phenological characteristics of crops across major production environments.

Soil

In regions such as South Asia, where agro-ecological conditions are highly heterogeneous, dependence only on global soil products can introduce substantial uncertainty, as local soil depth and other hydraulic properties affect crop productivity [9,41]. The dataset uses a hybrid strategy, prioritising in situ and country-specific soil profile information wherever available and filling gaps with globally consistent soil datasets. A total of 690 soil profiles were compiled across South Asia, including 540 from India, 80 from Sri Lanka, and 70 from Nepal, from the national research partner organisations.

All datasets were subjected to quality control, harmonised for units and formats, and spatially referenced before integration. Soil depth, a key determinant of rooting potential, plant-available water, and drought sensitivity, was compiled primarily from country-specific sources. National soil databases were compiled for Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and India [42,43]. Point observations were interpolated to a 5 km grid using co-kriging with elevation as a covariate. In data-scarce regions, ISRIC soil depth data at 250 m resolution were aggregated to 5 km. Soil depth was constrained between 250 and 1750 mm to maintain agronomic realism.

Soil physical and chemical properties were derived from the ISRIC SoilGrids 2.0 database, which provides globally consistent estimates at standard depth intervals. These data were aggregated to 5 km to align with the modelling framework. Key variables included bulk density, soil texture fractions, pH, soil organic carbon, field capacity, and wilting point.

Hydraulic parameters not directly available in the above datasets include saturated hydraulic conductivity, saturation moisture content, and air-dry moisture content. We estimated these parameters using regionally validated pedo-transfer functions. Saturated hydraulic conductivity was derived using [44], while moisture parameters were estimated using the pedo-transfer function suggested by Santra et al. [45].

Other inputs, Curve Number (CN) and Electrical conductivity (EC) for crop modelling, came from global datasets; CN for runoff estimation was obtained from the GCN250 Global Curve Number dataset [46], and EC was taken from the FAO Harmonised World Soil Database version 1.1 [47].

The final dataset provides soil depth and key physical, chemical, and hydraulic properties at 5 km resolution across South Asia. An example of derived data is shown in Fig. 2. Comparing water-holding capacity (WHC) from global and regional soil datasets reveals a source of uncertainty in crop modelling. The Fig. 2 shows that global soil depth data are generally higher across South Asia, resulting in higher WHC estimates and an overestimation of plant-available water. Conversely, regional soil depth data reflect local limitations, such as shallow soils, resulting in lower, more accurate WHC estimates. These differences can significantly affect crop simulation outcomes, especially under water stress. This analysis suggests that relying solely on global soil depth data without regional adjustments can introduce bias, whereas using local data offers a more accurate representation of regional soil–plant–water relationships in crop modelling [9].

Nitrogen use

Several attempts have been made to estimate gridded crop nitrogen consumption. For example, Nguyen [48] developed the NPKGRIDS global dataset, which integrated crop extent information with eight published datasets of fertiliser application rates to derive nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium applications at 0.5° resolution. However, the key challenge of such global products is a lack of spatial heterogeneity and over-generalisation, leading to misrepresentation of regional/local variability and potential biases in model outputs [49]. The high-resolution crop nitrogen consumption was estimated using a two-step framework that integrates district-level fertiliser statistics with pixel-level proxies of crop productivity [50] and soil characteristics. This approach enables spatial downscaling while preserving consistency with reported fertiliser use.

In the first step, crop-wise nitrogen use was estimated at the district level. With this, district-level cropped area [16] and recommended fertiliser doses [37] were used to compute the recommended nitrogen application for each crop. These values were then proportionally scaled to match total reported district nitrogen consumption [51], ensuring alignment with observed use. Upper and lower bounds were imposed based on cost-of-cultivation data [52] to ensure realistic application ranges. This dataset includes nitrogen consumption rates for India, while for other countries, we used global data [18]. This resulted in a harmonised dataset of district-level, crop-specific nitrogen consumption, expressed in kg/ha.

In the second step, district-level estimates were downscaled to a 5 km grid. Season-specific gross primary productivity (GPP) derived from remote sensing served as a proxy for spatial variations in crop productivity. GPP was converted to crop yield using crop-specific harvest indices from the literature [50]. Pixel-level nitrogen demand was estimated from crop yield, while soil nitrogen supply was derived from soil properties, including soil organic carbon and bulk density, along with crop duration. Fertiliser applied at each pixel was calculated as the difference between crop nitrogen demand and soil nitrogen supply, adjusted for the nitrogen use efficiency factor. Because the GPP dataset is restricted to India, this nitrogen-use downscaling approach only applies over India. For the other South Asian countries, we have used published global data [18].

Pixel-level fertiliser applications were then aggregated to the district level and compared with the adjusted district totals from the first step. A scaling factor was applied to ensure agreement between pixel-level estimates and district-level consumption, while preserving spatial variability. Crop-specific upper and lower thresholds were enforced to avoid unrealistic values. Finally, the estimates were validated against national-level benchmarks [53] for major crop-producing regions in India. Nitrogen scheduling

The estimation and standardisation of fertiliser doses and nutrient schedules for crop simulation modelling across diverse agroecological regions were carried out using a harmonised, evidence-based, and expert-informed approach (Table 4). Crop-wise recommended nutrient doses (primarily nitrogen) were compiled from the package of practices, AICRP trials, NFSM guidelines, and recommendations from state agricultural universities [[19], [20], [21]]. These inputs reflect farmer-adopted practices rather than experimental optima, representing dominant fertiliser schedules and agronomic recommendations across South Asian cropping systems despite localised variations.

Irrigation and rice typology

Irrigation shares for India were primarily based on district-level statistics from government sources. These values were uniformly assigned to all pixels within each district, ensuring consistency with official data but not capturing within-district variability. Where district-level data were missing or incomplete, irrigation shares were estimated using MAPSPAM. For specific country-crop combinations, rule-based assumptions were applied where strong empirical evidence exists. For example, cotton in Pakistan and Afghanistan was treated as predominantly irrigated. The resulting irrigation maps were evaluated through consistency checks, including comparison with national benchmarks. For instance, irrigation shares for rice in India were cross-validated against reported values of about 71%. This approach combines official data, spatial allocation, and informed assumptions to produce a consistent irrigation dataset.

The rice typology layer was developed by integrating remote sensing classification, topographic, and soil information. The initial classification was based on Gumma et al. [54], which maps rice ecosystems across South Asia into 13 clusters using satellite data. These clusters capture differences in hydrology, management, and seasonal patterns. For modelling consistency, the rice typology classes provided by Gumma et al. [54] were grouped into three main categories: irrigated systems, rainfed systems, and deepwater paddy systems. Other land-use classes were excluded because they represent non-rice or mixed areas.

The rainfed rice category was further divided into lowland and upland systems using elevation, slope, and soil properties. Elevation and slope were derived from the SRTM Digital Elevation Model [36], and soil data from SoilGrids [41]. Lowland systems are flat areas with higher moisture retention, while upland systems are steeper, better drained, and more water-limited. This integrated approach produces a spatially explicit rice typology suitable for analysing climate risks and adaptation options.

Irrigation scheduling

Irrigation regimes were classified as irrigated or rainfed. Scheduling was based on critical growth stages and soil moisture thresholds from the literature (Table 4). In irrigated systems, irrigation timing and depth were aligned with evapotranspiration demand and crop coefficients. In rainfed systems, contingency measures such as life-saving irrigation at the flowering and grain-filling stages were incorporated where feasible [22,23].

Usage note and limitations

The ACASA Spatial Crop Modelling Dataset is a harmonized collection of gridded biophysical and agronomic inputs developed to support process-based crop simulation modelling across South Asia. The dataset integrates crop area, crop phenology, planting dates, irrigation extent, nitrogen application rates, soil properties, and historical district yields within a consistent spatial framework. It is designed primarily for use with crop simulation models such as DSSAT, APSIM, and InfoCrop, while its modular structure also allows individual layers to be used independently for spatial analyses, yield assessments, climate risk studies, and agricultural planning.

The dataset is optimized for sub-national to regional-scale applications and provides a consistent basis for analysing crop productivity, climate sensitivity, management interventions, and adaptation options. All gridded layers follow standardized formats, naming conventions, and documentation to facilitate integration into modelling workflows.

Users should recognize that the dataset represents dominant production systems and typical farmer management practices around 2019 rather than field-specific observations or prescriptive recommendations. Crop management variables, including planting dates, irrigation schedules, and nitrogen application rates, were synthesized from regional literature, government statistics, expert knowledge, and publicly available datasets to provide spatially consistent inputs suitable for comparative modelling.

Nitrogen application rates were developed using multiple information sources, including district-level fertiliser statistics, recommended fertiliser doses, crop productivity indicators, and soil characteristics. In India, high-resolution downscaling methods were applied to derive spatially explicit crop nitrogen consumption, whereas published global datasets were used in regions where local information was unavailable. Users should therefore consider differences in source data and associated uncertainties when undertaking cross-country comparisons.

The dataset captures broad spatial patterns across South Asia; however, some intra-grid heterogeneity characteristic of smallholder farming systems remains unresolved. Consequently, application at field scale or finer spatial resolutions requires caution unless additional downscaling or local calibration is undertaken. Where higher-quality local observations are available, users are encouraged to replace or recalibrate selected layers to improve model performance.

Crop phenology and variety parameters represent dominant production conditions and may be refined for cultivar-specific applications. Soil hydraulic properties were derived using pedo-transfer functions to ensure consistency across diverse source datasets, although uncertainty remains in data-sparse regions. The dataset is compatible with ISIMIP-aligned climate inputs; however, users should explicitly evaluate uncertainty propagation and conduct sensitivity analyses when assessing climate impacts, management interventions, or adaptation strategies.

Finally, all baseline inputs are static and represent conditions circa 2019. Because agricultural systems, management practices, and input use evolve over time, users should incorporate updated datasets when modelling objectives require temporally dynamic representation of management or production systems.
