Post-COVID-19 condition in individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2 in autumn 2023 in the Netherlands: a prospective cohort study with pre- and post-infection data
Authors: Siméon de Bruijn, Anne J. Huiberts, Stijn P. Andeweg, Christina E. Hoeve, Maarten Schipper, Diederick E. Grobbee, Hester E. de Melker, Janneke H.H.M. van de Wijgert, Susan van den Hof, Mirjam J. Knol, Cees C. van den Wijngaard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPost-COVID-19 condition (PCC) risk may have changed due to vaccination status, virus variants, prior infections, and other factors. We aimed to estimate PCC prevalence for one year in individuals infected with SARS-CoV-2 during autumn 2023, controlling for pre-infection symptoms and prevalence in recently uninfected participants.MethodsVASCO, a Dutch ongoing prospective cohort, collects three-monthly questionnaires and six-monthly SARS-CoV-2 serology. Participants indicated severity of 23 symptoms on a five-point Likert scale, and of fatigue and concentration problems on the Checklist Individual Strength. We matched participants who did with those who did not report a SARS-CoV-2 infection between September 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024, and censored follow-up time for both upon serological or antigen test evidence of infection. We estimated PCC-prevalence as the excess prevalence of at least one PCC-related symptom between matched infected and uninfected participants at 90, 180, 270, and 360 days post-infection. Additionally, participants could self-attribute long-term symptoms to SARS-CoV-2.FindingsWe 1:1 matched 5621 infected to 5621 uninfected participants. The PCC prevalence, estimated as the marginal mean excess prevalence of PCC-related symptoms between infected and uninfected participants, was 0.2% (95% confidence interval: −1.9 to 2.3, p = 0.84) at 90 days, 0.5% (−1.6 to 2.6, p = 0.63) at 180 days, 0.7% (−1.3 to 2.8, p = 0.48) at 270 days, and 0.0% (−2.1 to 2.1, p = 0.99) at 360 days. Excess prevalence of new mild and severe long-term symptoms self-attributed to SARS-CoV-2 between infected and uninfected participants were both elevated at 90 days (mild: 7.2% (5.1–9.2), severe: 0.6% (0.4–0.8)) and 180 days (mild: 3.2% (2.0–4.4), severe: 0.3% (0.2–0.4)) post-infection (all p-values <0.0001), but no longer thereafter.InterpretationThis double-controlled study, incorporating pre- versus post-infection and uninfected symptom data, found a low risk of PCC among a community-dwelling adult population infected during the autumn 2023 SARS-CoV-2 wave. The prevalence of PCC-related symptoms in infected and uninfected individuals was not significantly different at 90–360 days post-infection. The excess prevalences of self-attributed long-term symptoms were elevated at 90 and 180 days post-infection but no longer from 270 days onwards. These findings suggest that the 2023 wave inferred a lower PCC risk than during the pandemic period.FundingFunded by the 10.13039/501100002999Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched Embase from inception to June 1, 2025, using the terms (‘long covid’/exp OR ‘post covid’ OR ‘pasc’ OR ‘post covid 19 condition’/exp) AND (‘prevalence’ OR ‘cluster’ OR ‘incidence’), focusing on studies of non-hospitalized SARS-CoV-2-infected adults. Previous research evaluating symptom excess prevalence among infected versus uninfected individuals showed a significantly higher risk of symptoms in the infected (pooled OR: 2.032; 95% CI: 1.787–2.310). However, few studies used a pre- versus post-infection design, which allows for assessment of pre-existing symptoms, that could erroneously have been attributed to PCC. Notably, studies with such designs in the Netherlands found a 12.7% PCC prevalence three to six months post-Alpha or Delta and another found no elevated fatigue 120 days after Omicron infection. Results of a decomposition analysis showed a decreasing PCC risk with early Omicron subvariants, largely attributed to vaccination (72.0%) and to a lesser extent to era-related factors (28.1%). To date, no studies on the PCC prevalence for later post-pandemic virus subvariants have employed a double-controlled design—that is, one that accounts for pre-infection symptoms and adjusts for background prevalence among time-matched uninfected individuals.

Added value of this study

This is the first post-pandemic prospective cohort study to assess the risk of PCC using a double-controlled design that integrates pre- and post-infection symptom data, background symptom prevalence, and antigen testing as well as serology to verify infection status. Infected participants reported a positive SARS-CoV-2 test between September 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024, while uninfected participants had no recent positive test or serological evidence of infection in that same time period. Symptom severity for 25 PCC-related symptoms was assessed using three-monthly questionnaires, in which participants also reported any new mild or severe symptoms that they personally attributed to SARS-CoV-2 infection. PCC prevalence was determined as the excess prevalence of at least one incident PCC-related symptom in infected versus contemporaneous recently, uninfected individuals. Our analysis showed that the overall PCC prevalence in the first year post-infection was less than 1.0%. Mild and severe self-attributed symptoms were increased at 90 days (7.2% and 0.6%, respectively) and 180 days post-infection (3.2% and 0.3%, respectively), though they were no longer elevated thereafter.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings demonstrate a substantially lower post-pandemic PCC risk compared with earlier pandemic estimates, suggesting that the virus variants that circulated during the pandemic years carried a higher PCC risk than post-pandemic virus variants. While severe PCC cases following the autumn 2023 wave cannot be ruled out at the individual level, the population-level risk in community-dwelling adults was low. This is crucial information for the public-health assessment of PCC, and emphasizes the need for continued PCC monitoring through well-designed cohort studies with systematic evaluation of exposure and symptoms.

Introduction

Post-COVID-19 condition (PCC) can follow SARS-CoV-2 infection and is often assessed by comparing excess symptom prevalence in infected versus uninfected groups.1,2 This, however, only indirectly addresses the PCC diagnostic criterium of excluding alternative diagnoses.1 Comparing newly developed post-versus-pre-infection symptoms within an individual increases the likelihood that symptoms occurred as a consequence of SARS-CoV-2. A study that included both pre- and post-infection and control symptom data, found that in 12.7% of SARS-CoV-2 cases, somatic symptoms present three to six months after an Alpha (B.1.1.7) or Delta (B.1.617.2) infection were attributable to PCC.3 More recent research suggests a lower PCC risk after infections with early Omicron (B.1.1.529) BA.1/BA.2 subvariants, due to vaccination, reduced viral pathogenicity and previous infections.4, 5, 6 One study among US veterans reported that the additional risk of PCC after reinfections appeared to increase with the number of infections compared with no reinfection.7 Contrarily, other studies found no significant differences for the effect of reinfection on PCC risk or even reported a lower risk of PCC after a second SARS-CoV-2 infection than the first.8,9

The post-pandemic risk of developing PCC after infection is unknown given ongoing temporal SARS-CoV-2 subvariant dynamics and the lack of post- versus pre-infection symptom studies. We used data from the large, ongoing VAccine Study COvid-19 (VASCO) cohort to estimate PCC prevalence in the year after the autumn 2023 SARS-CoV-2 wave in the Netherlands. We matched participants who were or were not infected that autumn and defined objective PCC-related symptoms using severity scores of symptoms that are associated with PCC, and by self-attribution (i.e., participants were asked whether they attributed mild or severe symptoms to SARS-CoV-2). This study also evaluated whether the XBB-1.5-targeted booster reduced PCC risk and if post-infection fatigue rates differed after the autumn 2023 SARS-CoV-2 wave compared to previous waves.

Methods

Study design and population

The VASCO study is a large, prospective Dutch cohort study primarily aimed at estimating COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness against SARS-CoV-2 infection. The cohort, detailed previously, includes community-dwelling adults aged 18–85 years enrolled between May and December 2021, and are followed for five years.9,10 Data on demographics, vaccination, symptoms, and SARS-CoV-2 tests were collected through online questionnaires every three months, with finger prick serology tests every six months.10 Since May 2022, participants received self-administered antigen tests to encourage testing after public facilities closed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In the current analysis, participants were classified as infected if they reported a positive SARS-CoV-2 test between September 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024, corresponding to the autumn 2023 Omicron JN.1 wave in the Netherlands.11 Eligible infected participants completed at least one questionnaire ≥14 days before infection and ≥1 questionnaire 30–360 days after infection. Eligible recently uninfected participants reported no positive SARS-CoV-2 test and provided serological samples showing no evidence of seroconversion or a two-fold increase in nucleocapsid (N) antibody titers between March 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024, and completed at least one questionnaire after January 7, 2024.

Outcomes and covariates

Symptom severity was assessed for 23 symptoms (Appendix p2) using a five-point Likert scale, with fatigue and concentration difficulties additionally evaluated through the Checklist Individual Strength (CIS) questionnaire, specifically the CIS-Fatigue subscale (range: 8–56) and CIS-Concentration subscale (range: 5–35).12,13 The symptoms were either related to acute SARS-CoV-2 infections (e.g., coughing and fever) or to PCC (e.g., fatigue, PEM, and cognitive symptoms); (Appendix p2). Severe fatigue was defined as a CIS-Fatigue score of ≥35, a validated threshold in both chronically fatigued patients and the Dutch general population. Severe concentration difficulties was defined as a CIS-Concentration score of ≥18, a cut-off previously used in the PCC context.12 For each participant, changes in symptom scores between the pre-infection and post-infection period were assessed. The incidence of a symptom potentially associated with PCC was defined as a pre-infection score of <3, and an increase of ≥1 point to a score of ≥3 post-infection, analogous to Ballering et al.3 Potentially PCC-related fatigue was defined as a CIS-Fatigue increase from <35 pre-infection by ≥6 to ≥35 post-infection, and potentially PCC-related concentration difficulties as a CIS-Concentration increase from <18 by ≥3 to ≥18 post-infection.12,13 The primary outcome, estimated PCC prevalence at 90, 180, 270, and 360 days post-infection, was defined as the excess prevalence of having at least one incident symptom for the 23 five-point Likert scale symptoms, CIS-Fatigue or CIS-Concentration (referred to hereinafter as PCC-related symptoms) in infected versus uninfected participants. Additionally, all participants could self-attribute mild and severe long-term symptoms to PCC in each questionnaire independent of any infection, i.e., we asked “Are you currently experiencing long-term symptoms that you believe are partly or entirely caused by the coronavirus?”. Covariates age, sex and medical risk condition were self-reported at study entry (Appendix p2 for full list of comorbidities).9,10 The number of cumulative infections per individual was determined by reported positive SARS-CoV-2 tests. Data on COVID-19 vaccinations was collected by self-reporting and cross-referenced with the Dutch national COVID-19 vaccination Information and Monitoring System (CIMS).14 Booster vaccinations administered in the Netherlands between October 2, 2023 and December 22, 2023 were considered Omicron XBB.1.5-targeted boosters. Eligibility for the booster campaign included individuals aged ≥60 years, healthcare workers, or those with medical risk conditions.

Matching

Details of all analyses are in Appendix pp3–9. For the main analysis, with the estimated PCC prevalence as outcome, we created a principal matched set by pairing each infected participant 1:1 with an uninfected participant. Matching was based on the exact month of pre-infection questionnaire submission, cumulative number of COVID-19 vaccinations, 15-year age group, sex, and presence of a medical risk condition. Propensity scores were calculated using 25 pre-infection symptom scores and the prior cumulative number of positive SARS-CoV-2 tests. These scores were then used for nearest neighbor matching to further minimize differences between infected and uninfected pairs. For symptom specific prevalence analyses, we constructed 25 per-symptom matched sets. Here, to optimize pre-infection symptom similarity, only the respective symptom's pre-infection score was included in the propensity score. Uninfected participants were matched only once in a dataset, and their follow-up time was aligned with the infection timeline of their matched counterpart. Fourteen days prior to a (new) positive test in either individual, or on the date preceding evidence of seroconversion or a two-fold increase in N-antibody titers in the uninfected pair member, follow-up for both members of the pair was censored.

Statistical analyses

Prevalence of at least one PCC-related symptom was estimated using generalized additive models with binomial distributions, including a penalized time spline (30–360 days post-infection) stratified by infection status. Excess prevalence was calculated as the difference in estimated marginal mean probability between infected and uninfected participants at 90, 180, 270, and 360 days (Appendix p3). The estimated marginal mean represents the model-adjusted predicted averages of the outcome, accounting for and averaging over the levels of covariates included in the analysis. Additional analyses included: the excess prevalence of each PCC-related symptom separately for both the infected and uninfected participants as well as the excess prevalence; PCC prevalence in those aged 18–59 and those aged 60–85 years; the excess prevalence of newly-developed mild and severe long-term symptoms self-attributed to SARS-CoV-2 in infected compared to uninfected participants; the prevalence of at least one incident symptom in the infected XBB.1.5-booster eligible population comparing individuals who prior to infection received the booster and those who did not receive the booster; and finally the prevalence of incident CIS-Fatigue across different SARS-CoV-2 infection waves in the Netherlands (Appendix pp3–9; calendar dates in Appendix p10). In the latter two analyses, pairwise matching was unsuccessful and all covariates were adjusted for in the models with the full eligible cohorts instead. To assess the robustness of the estimated PCC prevalence, six sensitivity analyses were conducted (Appendix pp3–9). First, PCC prevalence was estimated by limiting the included symptoms to those that at any post-infection timepoint significantly exceeded the prevalence observed in infected participants compared to uninfected participants. Second, while PCC-related symptoms in the main analysis were defined as incident by applying a restriction on a maximum pre-infection score, the analysis was repeated without that restriction. Third, the analysis population was restricted to participants with serological samples available. Fourth, rather than 1:1 exact matching infected and uninfected participants, we included all eligible participants and adjusted for the covariates age, sex and medical risk condition. Fifth, to further decrease the possibility of misclassification, we applied a 1.5-fold N-antibody increase criterion to assess infection with higher sensitivity in the uninfected group.15 Sixth, educational level was added to the matching procedure. All analyses were performed in R Statistical Software (v4.4.2) with the MatchIT (v4.5.4), mgcv (v1.9-1), ordinal (v2023.12-4.1), and emmeans (v.1.10.4) packages.16, 17, 18, 19

VASCO complies with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol, was approved by the not-for-profit independent Medical Ethics Committee of the Stichting Beoordeling Ethiek Biomedisch Onderzoek (BEBO), Assen, the Netherlands (NL76815.056.21) and written consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. VASCO was registered in the online Dutch clinical trials register (trialsearch.who.int, study registration number NL9279, 17/02/2021).10

Results

Between September 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024, 6374 participants reported a positive SARS-CoV-2 test. Restricting to participants with available pre- and post-infection questionnaires resulted in 6113 infected participants eligible for the main analysis (Appendix p10). Between March 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024, 36,782 participants did not report a SARS-CoV-2 positive test. Of these, 7945 were eligible for matching because they had completed questionnaires in the study period and had submitted serological samples without N-seroconversion or two-fold N-antibody increase between March 25, 2023 and January 7, 2024. Of these, 1470 were censored due to infection after January 7, 2024. In the principal matched set, at the time of the pre-infection questionnaire, infected participants were younger, more often female, and had fewer cumulative positive SARS-CoV-2 tests than uninfected participants (Table 1). Matching procedures yielded 5621 pairs (92% of 6113 infected participants), with a median follow-up of 308 days [IQR: 287–340] for infected and 303 days [IQR: 269–335] for uninfected participants. Most (96.1%, n = 10,805) submitted at least five questionnaires (Appendix p10). Pre-infection symptom severity scores distributions were very similar between matched infected and uninfected participants (Appendix p11). Absolute standardized mean differences between groups for all covariates were <0.04, indicating well-balanced distributions, except for the previous number of positive tests at 0.298. Hence, this covariate was added to the models additionally. Post-COVID-19 condition and symptom incidence

Fig. 1 shows that the PCC prevalence, as determined by estimated marginal mean excess prevalence of having at least one incident PCC-related symptom, between infected and uninfected participants, was 0.2% (95% confidence interval (CI): −1.9 to 2.3; p = 0.84) at 90 days, 0.5% (CI: −1.6 to 2.6; p = 0.63) at 180 days, 0.7% (CI: −1.3 to 2.8; p = 0.48) at 270 days, and 0.0% (CI: −2.1 to 2.1; p = 0.99) at 360 days. Prevalence of at least one incident PCC-related symptom decreased over the first 270 days for both the infected and uninfected group, but increased thereafter. At 90 days post-infection, significant excess prevalences were observed for incident CIS-Fatigue, anosmia or ageusia, and memory problems (Fig. 2, Appendix pp12–15). Significant excess prevalences were additionally found for malaise (180 days), arthralgia, memory problems, and paresthesia (180 and 270 days), and arthralgia, memory, and concentration problems (360 days). Conversely, uninfected participants showed significant excess prevalences versus infected participants for headache, sore throat, fever/shivers, runny nose, and abdominal pain at various time points (Appendix pp9–12). The prevalence of incident mild and severe long-term symptoms self-attributed to SARS-CoV-2 was significantly higher in infected participants compared to uninfected participants at 90 and 180 days post-infection. At 90 days, the excess prevalences of newly-developed mild and severe self-attributed long-term symptoms was 7.2% (95% CI: 5.1–9.2) and 0.6% (95% CI: 0.4–0.8), respectively. At 180 days, these were 3.2% (95% CI: 2.0–4.4) and 0.3% (95% CI: 0.2–0.4), respectively. All p-values were <0.0001 (Table 2). Subsequent estimates were 0.8% (CI: −0.1 to 1.7) and 0.1% (CI: 0.0–0.2) at 270 days and −0.2% (CI: −1.1 to 0.7) and 0.0% (CI: 0.0–0.1) at 360 days. These differences were no longer significant. Additional analyses

Receiving the XBB-1.5-targetted booster prior to infection resulted in non-significantly lower excess prevalences of at least one incident PCC-related symptom compared with eligible non-XBB-1.5-target boostered counterparts, though the difference increased with 1.0%-point between 90 and 360 days (Appendix pp17–18). We compared incident CIS-Fatigue across the Omicron JN.1 and earlier infection waves in the Netherlands. Excess prevalence was highest in the first 180 days post-infection and greater for the Delta and first three Omicron waves than for later waves (Appendix pp20–22). At 270 and 360 days post-infection, the highest excess prevalence was observed for Omicron BA4/5 at 1.2% (CI: 0.4–1.9), with all other point estimates ≤1.0% (Appendix p21). Post-versus-pre-infection CIS-Fatigue scores followed similar trends with a relatively larger impact for Delta than Omicron waves, particularly at 90 and 180 days post-infection (Appendix p22). PCC estimates stratified by the groups of 18–59 and 60–85 years were comparable with all point-estimates <1.0% (Appendix p23). Sensitivity analyses produced PCC prevalence estimates that were largely similar to those from the main analysis (Appendix p24). Notably though, restricting symptoms to those elevated at any time point, applying a 1.5-fold N-antibody increase criterion for infection, and including educational level in the matching procedure all resulted in higher, non-significant estimates.

Discussion

This uniquely designed prospective study, which controlled for pre-infection and background prevalence of 25 symptoms, found a low prevalence of post-COVID-19 condition (PCC) following the Omicron JN.1 2023 autumn wave. This prevalence was not statistically significantly elevated in our study population of over 5500 infected participants compared to the same number of matched, recently uninfected participants. The PCC prevalence at 90 days was 0.2% (95% confidence interval (CI): −1.9 to 2.3), and at 180, 270, and 360 days this was 0.5% (CI: −1.6 to 2.6), 0.7% (CI: −1.3 to 2.8), and 0.0% (CI: −2.1 to 2.1), respectively. Additionally, the excess prevalences of newly-developed self-attributed severe long-term symptoms to SARS-CoV-2 were 0.6% at 90 days and 0.3% at 180 days and declined to <0.1% at 270 and 360 days. Notably, during the first 180 days post-infection, the excess prevalence of newly-developed mild self-attributed symptoms (7.2% at 90 days and 3.2% at 180 days) was significantly higher than the excess prevalence of the severe self-attributed symptoms and than the PCC prevalence estimates. This difference was no longer observed at later time points. The elevated excess prevalence of mild, self-attributed symptoms appeared to be transient, and by 90 days post-infection, it approximated the rate observed among uninfected participants who newly developed fatigue or other individual symptoms. This could reflect partial overattribution of symptoms to SARS-CoV-2 by infected participants. Alternatively, it may indicate a lack of sensitivity in our estimated PCC definition for capturing mild PCC.

Our observation of at most modest and self-limiting PCC prevalence after recent infections are in line with our prior analysis of post-infectious fatigue in the same cohort, where we found that CIS-Fatigue scores after a first infection—within the Omicron era—were elevated in the first 90 days following first infection but no longer at 180 days.9 The present analysis included an extended number of symptoms potentially associated with PCC, and SARS-CoV-2 reinfections. Other studies from the Netherlands that assessed PCC prevalence among community-dwelling adults were restricted to earlier SARS-CoV-2 variants. Our own earlier findings in another cohort, reported an excess prevalence of 10.7% at 90 days following Omicron infection but did not account for pre-infection symptomology.5 Other studies did not consider contemporaneous background prevalence and/or reported on pre-Omicron virus variants. For example, Van Dijk et al. estimated a PCC prevalence of 5–10% up to 100 days following infection.3,5,20 PCC prevalence estimates, even in community-dwelling adults from the same country, vary with PCC definition, methodology, diagnostic criteria, follow-up period and calendar time.21 Our study design was analogous to Ballering et al. who reported a prevalence of 12.7% between 90 and 150 days for the Alpha or Delta variants.3 In this and another Lifelines population cohort study it was shown that anosmia and ageusia comprised 7.2% of the 12.7% total PCC prevalence and that the primary COVID-19 vaccination series was protective against this symptom profile.22 In a separate study from the Netherlands, this protective vaccination effect was also shown.23 The present study finds that incident anosmia or ageusia was only significantly elevated at 90 days post-infection, with 0.8% prevalence. The considerably lower PCC prevalence in the present study compared with Ballering et al. across two distinctly different calendar periods, may therefore be explained in part due to a decrease in anosmia and ageusia resulting from build-up of immunity and changes over time such as viral adaptations.6,24

Some studies have shown that the first three doses of COVID-19 vaccination reduced the risk of PCC, but the effect of additional doses has been understudied.25 Previously and in this analysis, we found no significant protection of the bivalent and the XBB.1.5-targetted booster against PCC, though we do observe a non-significant higher elevation in excess symptom prevalence for the non-boostered group compared to the boostered group with time since infection.9 This may partly be explained by differing susceptibility to influenza, which was epidemic at the end of the follow-up period, as 69% of the booster vaccine recipients reported also receiving influenza vaccinations at study entry. Indeed, other pathogens may cause both acute symptoms and post-acute infectious syndromes.26 The slight excess prevalence of some acute respiratory symptoms of SARS-CoV-2 uninfected compared to infected participants possibly could be due to viral interference, where SARS-CoV-2 uninfected participants may have been more likely to have had other respiratory infections during follow-up.27 Although further research is needed, studies suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection may inhibit subsequent replication of other respiratory viruses.28,29 The increased prevalence of several respiratory symptoms in recently uninfected participants, but not of the SARS-CoV-2 hallmark symptoms anosmia and ageusia, supports the possibility that other viruses could be responsible; however, this hypothesis requires further investigation. Interestingly, a small percentage of SARS-CoV-2 uninfected participants reported newly-developed mild and severe long-term symptoms that they attributed to SARS-CoV-2. This could potentially be due to misattribution of symptoms to SARS-CoV-2 if participants experienced respiratory symptoms from another pathogen but did not test for SARS-CoV-2. While we cannot rule out the possibility of undetected infections, willingness to test was high in this group, with 83% reporting that they always or mostly tested when experiencing COVID-19-related symptoms.

This study found that a low PCC risk was likely not limited to the Omicron JN.1 wave, as Delta and earlier Omicron waves also showed low excess CIS-Fatigue prevalence at 270 and 360 days. Excess fatigue was higher in earlier compared with later waves during the first 180 days post-infection. No other symptoms, however, were available in this analysis. There is some evidence to suggest that PCC risk between Delta and early Omicron variants was comparable, e.g., when genotyped samples from Delta and Omicron infected people were taken from the same calendar period in Norway or when comparing pre- and post-infection symptomology and when corrected for vaccination status.22,30 Nevertheless, our finding that the prevalence of PCC has changed following Omicron JN.1 infection, compared to the prevalence reported earlier for Alpha and Delta variants by Ballering et al., highlights the critical importance of ongoing PCC monitoring through well-controlled cohort studies with well-defined exposure assessment. Moreover, our estimates clearly suggest that the current risk of PCC per infection is substantially lower than the 30% reported in a meta-analysis of pre-2023 infections based on single-controlled studies.31,32 A contributing factor to a lower risk may be repeat SARS-CoV-2 exposure. Studies indicate that while each subsequent infection increases the risk of PCC, the incremental risk decreases with each new infection when compared to an uninfected group, or there are lower odds of new-onset self-reported PCC in individuals with repeat infections compared to those with a first infection.7,8 In our population, the proportion of repeat infections was relatively high, and many of those with a significant predisposition for PCC upon first infection may thus have been precluded from contributing to the new-onset PCC prevalence following JN.1 infection, possibly effectively lowering population-level PCC risk.

A limited risk of post-COVID after the Omicron JN.1 wave is further supported by two cross-sectional analyses of 12,000 adults in the Netherlands in December 2023 and 2024, showing self-attributed PCC prevalence of 3.3% and 2.9%.33 These rates align with the stable 2–3% prevalence of severe self-attributed long-term symptoms measured pre- and post-autumn 2023 infections as measured in the current study, suggesting most severe PCC cases in the Dutch population likely originated from infections before the autumn 2023 wave.

Several key strengths characterize this study. First, we were able to account for pre-autumn 2023 infection symptomology as an indication of whether symptoms developed as a potential consequence of a SARS-CoV-2 (re)infection in autumn 2023. Excess risk between infected and uninfected populations without consideration of pre-existing symptom prevalence relies on the assumption that both groups are comparable in that regard, which—when breached—could for instance lead to a bias of healthy controls and possible overestimation of PCC prevalence.2,34 Second, with accessible, promoted self-testing kits and serology-based SARS-CoV-2 infection detection, we were able to identify participants who were most likely not recently infected and match them with infected participants on calendar time and important confounders, and censor on a subsequent infection. Third, as an extension of our previous study, we were able to include 25 symptoms with severity scales, including the most common PCC symptoms.9,35 Finally, our main conclusions were robust to the sensitivity analyses exercised, though small changes to point estimates with different model choices were evident.

Certain limitations are present in this study. First, the most severe PCC patients, including those with severe PCC following a 2023 infection, may have dropped out of the study due to the burden of participation. Nevertheless, only 1% of participants submitted fewer than two post-infection measurements. Second, the primary objective of the VASCO study is to assess COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness; recruitment focused on inclusion by target groups based on age (18–59 and 60–85 years) and medical risk condition. As a result, the cohort is not fully representative of the Dutch population.10 Although the impact on PCC prevalence is uncertain, we observed similar estimates among participants younger and older than 60 years (Appendix p23). Third, N-antibody production following infection may have waned or altogether not have occurred. A two-fold increase in N-antibody concentrations as exclusion and censoring criteria was used to minimize misclassification of recently, uninfected participants. Previously, we found that 82% and 77% of participants with a positive antigen test showed a 1.5-fold and 2-fold N-antibody increase, respectively, while among those without a reported infection, 19% and 15%, respectively, showed such increases.15 The main conclusions here did not change when lowering the criterion from two-fold increase to 1.5-fold. We expect, together with the high willingness to test in this uninfected group, only a small percentage of SARS-CoV-2 infections were missed. Infected individuals were solely identified based on report of a positive test and we therefore did not include (asymptomatic) non-testers, which could lead to some over- or underestimation of PCC prevalence if non-testers would have a different risk of developing PCC.36 Third, due to computational limits we were unable to correct for repeated measurements. Fourth, the criteria for a clinically relevant change in CIS-Concentration scores and the five-point Likert scale symptoms have not been validated, and a change of at least 3 and 1 points, respectively, may not have been sufficiently sensitive. Similarly, while the CIS-Fatigue has been validated for use in patients with chronic fatigue, it has not yet been validated for patients with PCC. Finally, although in none of the analyses we found an indication for substantial population-level development of severe long-term PCC after infection, this does not preclude that a certain number of individuals developed severe PCC as a result of an autumn 2023 SARS-CoV-2 infection.

In conclusion, this well-controlled study with active follow-up of SARS-CoV-2 infection status found that, in autumn 2023, the risk of new-onset PCC among community-dwelling, mostly vaccinated, adults was low. The excess prevalence of PCC-related symptoms in infected versus uninfected individuals was not significant, and from 270 days post-infection, the excess prevalence of newly-developed self-attributed long-term symptoms was no longer significantly elevated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to show that, when accounting for pre-infection data, the per-infection risk of long-term PCC in 2023 was relatively low.
