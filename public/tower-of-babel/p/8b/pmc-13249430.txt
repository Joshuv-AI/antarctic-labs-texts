A stand-alone ICln release module revealed by deconstructing the SMN complex with engineered Brr1
Authors: Yan Hu, Ji Zhang, Bin Xu, Yingzhi Wang, Li Mu, Rundong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe assembly of the Sm core, a ring-shaped protein complex essential for spliceosomal small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particle biogenesis, is chaperoned by the SMN complex in most eukaryotes. Intriguingly, the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae possesses a simplified version of this machinery—a single protein, Brr1—raising the question of whether it can functionally replace the entire multi-subunit SMN complex. Here, we engineered Brr1 (eBrr1) to bind Schizosaccharomyces pombe Sm proteins D1/D2/F/E/G (5Sm) and introduced it into fission yeast, which relies on a five-membered SMN complex. We demonstrate that eBrr1 functionally substitutes for the essential Gemin2 subunit. Surprisingly, in this context, the Gemin2-binding domain of SMN becomes dispensable, revealing that a stable SMN-Gemin2 interaction is not required for viability. Furthermore, we show that the SMN/Gemin6–8 subcomplex alone is sufficient to displace the assembly chaperone ICln from the ICln/5Sm/eBrr1 complex. Our findings provide key mechanistic insights into the stepwise action of the SMN complex and propose an evolutionary model in which the ICln-release machinery predated the stable incorporation of Gemin2.

Introduction

U-rich small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles (snRNPs) are essential components of the spliceosome, the molecular machinery responsible for pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) splicing in eukaryotic cells [1, 2]. Each snRNP consists of two main components: a noncoding small nuclear RNA (snRNA, e.g. U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6) and a set of specific proteins, including the Sm core proteins (common to U1, U2, U4, and U5 snRNPs) or Sm-like (Lsm) proteins (U6 snRNP), as well as other unique proteins [1, 2]. Sm and Lsm proteins belong to the same protein family and they share a common Sm fold characterized by an N-terminal helix followed by five highly bent β-strands [3–5]. U1, U2, U4, and U5 snRNPs are sometimes termed Sm-class snRNPs because they share a common Sm core structure. In the Sm core, seven Sm proteins form a ring-like structure arranged in the order of D1-D2-F-E-G-D3-B around a specific sequence in the snRNA (termed the snRNP code), typically consisting of the nonanucleotide PuAUUUNUGPu (Pu = purine), which is termed the Sm site, followed by a stem-loop [6, 7]. U6 snRNP also has a similar core structure, in which seven Lsm proteins (Lsm2–8) form a ring-like structure around its 3′-end U-rich sequence [8, 9].

Despite the similarity in their core protein components and structures, the biogenesis of Lsm-class and Sm-class snRNPs differs [1, 10]. The biogenesis of Lsm-class snRNP is simple, as it is entirely assembled within the nucleus and does not require the assistance of chaperones. In contrast, the biogenesis of Sm-class snRNPs is much more complex. It involves multiple steps, occurs in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, and requires the assistance of numerous interacting proteins and assembly chaperones [1, 10, 11]. Sm-class snRNA is transcribed by RNA polymerase II in the nucleus, acquiring a 5′ monomethylated cap, and then exported to the cytoplasm via the exportin-1 (CRM1) pathway. In the cytoplasm, the seven Sm proteins assemble on the snRNA, and the 5′ cap is hypermethylated to a 2, 2, 7-trimethylguanosine (TMG) cap. Subsequently, the snRNP is imported back into the nucleus via importin-β and snurportin-1 (which recognizes the TMG cap). In the nucleus, snRNPs undergo further modifications, and additional snRNP-specific proteins are assembled.

The assembly of the snRNP core (or Sm core) occurs in the cytoplasm and depends on several chaperone proteins [1, 11–13]. Early studies showed that the Sm core can self-assemble in vitro by combining snRNA with three preformed Sm subcomplexes—SmD1/D2, SmF/E/G, and SmD3/B [14]. This process follows a defined pathway: first, a stable Sm subcore intermediate (SmD1/D2/F/E/G bound to snRNA) forms, which then matures upon the incorporation of SmD3/B [14]. In most eukaryotes, Sm core assembly is facilitated by two key protein complexes—the PRMT5 (protein arginine methyltransferase 5) complex and the SMN (survival of motor neuron) complex [1, 11–13]. In vertebrates, the PRMT5 complex includes PRMT5, WD45, and pICln [15, 16], while the SMN complex consists of SMN, Gemin2–8, and Unrip [17–24]. Even in simple organisms like Schizosaccharomyces pombe, orthologues of these complexes (e.g. pICln, SMN, Gemin2, and Gemin6–8) are present [25–30]. Many of these proteins are essential for viability. In vertebrates, loss of SMN, Gemin2, or pICln leads to embryonic lethality [31–33], while in invertebrates, deletion of SMN or Gemins (Gemin2–5, Gemin8) is fatal [34–38]. In S. pombe, disrupting any SMN complex component (SMN, Gemin2 or Gemin6–8) severely impairs cell growth [25–27, 29, 30]. Notably, SMN deficiency in humans causes spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), a severe neurodegenerative disorder affecting ∼1 in 10 000 individuals [39, 40]. Even nonessential chaperones like S. pombe pICln orthologue, ICln, contribute to optimal cell growth [28], underscoring their functional importance. Thus, these assembly factors play critical and often indispensable roles in snRNP core formation across eukaryotes.

Recent biochemical and structural studies have elucidated most of the stepwise mechanism of Sm core assembly mediated by these two key chaperone complexes. Generally, the assembly process occurs through two distinct phases. In the initial phase, the PRMT5 complex prepares the Sm proteins for assembly. The pICln protein contains a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain structurally similar to the Sm fold [41, 42]. It binds and organizes SmD1/D2 and SmF/E/G into a ring-shaped 6S complex [12, 42]. This complex maintains the five Sm proteins (5Sm) in their proper configuration while preventing premature RNA binding [12, 42]. Simultaneously, the PRMT5/WD45 complex mediates arginine methylation of SmB, SmD1, and SmD3 C-termini [43, 44], which may facilitate subsequent interactions with the SMN complex, although this modification is not essential in all species [45].

The second phase involves the SMN complex, which receives the 5Sm complex and displaces pICln from the 6S complex [12, 30]. Specifically, Gemin2 serves as the primary acceptor: its N-terminal domain binds SmF/E, while its C-terminal domain (CTD) interacts with SmD1/D2 [42, 46]. The SMN protein itself engages with Gemin2 through its N-terminal domain [17, 42, 46], and its C-terminal YG box mediates oligomerization and binding to Gemin8, which in turn associates with Gemin6/7 and Unrip [23, 47, 48]. Once pICln is released, cognate snRNAs can enter the central pocket of the horseshoe-shaped 5Sm and allosterically displace Gemin2 (and the entire SMN complex) from 5Sm [49]. Recruitment of SmD3/B facilitates the release of the SMN complex and forms the mature Sm core [49]. Additional components include the Gemin3/4 subcomplex (containing a DEAD-box helicase) and Gemin5, which delivers pre-snRNAs to the assembly machinery and participates in translation regulation [50, 51]. Notably, Gemin3–5 and Unrip are absent in unicellular eukaryotes like S. pombe [29, 30], suggesting these components may represent evolutionary additions to the core assembly machinery rather than essential factors.

In understanding the general mechanism of Sm core assembly, we further focus on the differences in this process among different species, especially two common yeast species. Recently, ICln and the five-membered SMN complex in S. pombe have been demonstrated to be both essential and sufficient for Sm core assembly, representing a miniature chaperone system [30]. In contrast to the five-membered SMN complex in S. pombe, the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae possesses only one homologous protein, Brr1, which is a distantly related orthologue of Gemin2. Although it is nonessential, it is found to play a role in Sm core assembly [52]. This raises intriguing questions about the evolutionary divergence between these two yeast species in their assembly mechanisms. For example, what are the differences and similarities between these two species in their Sm core assembly? Can Brr1 alone act as the holo-SMN complex in other species?

While conducting a systematic study of Sm core assembly in S. cerevisiae to address the first question [53], we also investigated the second one using S. pombe. Although initial studies indicated that Brr1 could not bind to spSmD1/D2 and spSmF/E/G, we engineered Brr1 (eBrr1) to bind spSmD1/D2/F/E/G and introduced eBrr1 into S. pombe. We found that eBrr1 can replace spGemin2 in cells. Surprisingly, although eBrr1 cannot replace both Gemin2 and SMN, the N-terminal Gemin2-binding domain (Ge2BD) of SMN can be removed. This indicates that tight physical interaction between Gemin2 and SMN is nonessential. The molecular mechanism was further investigated using a combination of biochemical, biophysical and genetic approaches. These findings provide mechanistic insights into how the SMN complex displaces ICln during Sm core assembly and shed light on the evolutionary trajectory of the SMN complex.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

The haploid S. pombe strain 503 (h+ ade6-704 leu1-32 ura4-D18 his3-D1) from our previous study [30] was cultured in YES or EMM2 medium at 30°C. All generated strains are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The yip11/yip12 knockout strain was cultured in EMM2 medium lacking uracil but supplemented with 100 μg/ml nourseothricin (NTC), 50 μg/ml G418, and 0.25 g/l each of histidine, adenine, lysine and leucine (EMM2-ΔUra).

Construction of plasmids for protein expression in Escherichia coli

Plasmids containing genes for S. pombe (sp) Sm proteins, ICln [full-length and the C-terminal truncated version ICln (1–160)], and SMN complex proteins (SMN, Gemin6, Gemin7, Gemin8) were all from our previous study [30]. The plasmid containing the wildtype Brr1 gene was also from a previous study [53]. N-terminal truncated SMN variants, SMNΔ (1–40) and SMNΔ (1–78), were cloned into the pRSF vector with an N-terminal His6-TEV-tag [creating pRSF-spSMNΔ (1–40) and pRSF-spSMNΔ (1–78)], analogous to the full-length SMN construct. Insert and vector fragments were assembled using a homologous recombination method (seamless cloning) with Monclone Single Assembly Cloning Mix (Monad Biotech, China). All the constructs were verified by DNA sequencing.

Protein expression and purification

Recombinant proteins and complexes were expressed in BL21 (DE3) competent cells, as previously described [30]. Transformed cells were cultured in Luria–Bertani medium with 50 μg/ml appropriate antibiotics at 37°C and 200 rpm until OD600nm reached 1.0. Protein expression was induced with 0.30 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and cultures were grown for 18 h at 16°C and 200 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 250 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol, pH 8.0). The suspension was either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at −20°C or used immediately for purification.

Cells were lysed by high-pressure homogenization (800 Bar, 4°C, 5 min), and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. Proteins or complexes were typically purified by Ni-NTA-affinity chromatography, followed by ion exchange chromatography, and finally, gel filtration chromatography (GFC). When required, the His6-TEV tag was removed by TEV protease cleavage after Ni-NTA-affinity step; tag-free proteins were collected by passing the mixture over a second Ni-NTA-affinity column at a low imidazole concentration. Protein quality and quantity were monitored at each step by sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) staining.

Protein–protein interaction by pull-down assay

Interactions between Brr1/eBrr1 and the Sm subcomplexes (SmD1/D2 and SmF/E/G) were analyzed by pull-down. Approximately 100 μg of Ni-NTA agarose beads were incubated with equimolar amounts of HT-Brr1 or HT-eBrr1, tag-free SmD1/D2, and SmF/E/G in binding buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, 250 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol, 0.01% (v/v) NP-40, and protease inhibitors, pH 8.0] for 1 h at 4°C. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation (600 × g, 3 min), and the beads were washed three times with 1 ml of binding buffer. Proteins bound to the beads and the supernatant fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and CBB staining.

Protein–protein interaction by GFC

The interaction between eBrr1 and SMN or SMNΔ (1–40) was analyzed by GFC. Purified eBrr1 was mixed with an equimolar amount of SMN or SMNΔ (1–40) and incubated at 4°C for at least 1 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 13 000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris, and the supernatant was injected an analytical Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (GE healthcare) equilibrated with running buffer (25 mM HEPES, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 7.5). Fractions (0.5 ml) were collected and analyzed by SDS–PAGE and CBB staining.

ICln release pull-down assay

Tag-free reconstituted 6S complex [5Sm/ICln or 5Sm/ICln (1–160)], SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6/7/8 complex [or SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6/7/8 complex], and HT-eBrr1 were incubated with Ni-NTA beads separately or in combination at an equimolar ratio for 1 h at 10°C in binding buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, 250 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 5% glycerol, 0.01% (v/v) NP-40, and protease inhibitors, pH 8.0]. After collecting the supernatant, the beads were washed three times with 1 ml of binding buffer. A control containing only the 6S complex and HT-eBrr1 was processed in parallel.

ICln release fluorescence resonance energy transfer kinetic assay

Purified His-ICln (1–160) and His-SmD1/D2 were labeled with Cyanine 3 NHS ester (Cy3) and Cyanine 5 NHS ester (Cy5), respectively, for 16 h at 5°C. Free dye was removed using a HitrapTM Desalting column in phosphate buffered saline buffer containing 0.05%Tween-20 and 1 mM Trolox. The labeled proteins were concentrated, and Cy3-ICln (1–160), Cy5-SmD1/D2, and unlabeled SmF/E/G were mixed at an approximately equimolar ratio. The 6S complex was isolated by GFC, and its concentration was adjusted to 1 μM. An equimolar amount of the SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6/7/8 complex or SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6/7/8 complex was added to 50 μl of the dye-labeled 6S complex (n = 4 per group). Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) signal (excitation 520 nm, emission 665 nm) was recorded every 5 min for total 80 min using a BMG Clariostar Plus plate reader. FRET efficiency was calculated as: FRET% = (Ftime − Fmin)/(Fmax − Fmin) × 100%.

Construction of plasmids for gene manipulation in S. pombe

The plasmid pFAN-URA3-spGe2 was from our previous study (where it was named pFA6a-URA3-spGe2) [30]. To avoid confusion, we have renamed this plasmid and its derivatives (which contain the ARS1 origin for replication in fission yeast) with the prefix “pFAN”, where F, A, and N denote fission yeast, ARS1 origin, and nmt1 promoter, respectively. Plasmids pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 and pFAN-URA3-SMN were constructed similarly by replacing the spGemin2 gene with the eBrr1 or spSMN gene. The empty pFAN-URA3 vector was generated by removing the coding region after the nmt1 promoter. The empty pFAN-LEU2 vector was created by replacing the URA3 marker with the LEU2 marker. Plasmids pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1, pFAN-LEU2-His-eBrr1, pFAN-LEU2-SMN, and pFAN-LEU2-SMN (1–40) were constructed similarly using the pFAN-LEU2 backbone. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2. All fragments were generated by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and assembled via seamless cloning.

For pCAS-sgRNAGe2 and pCAS-sgRNAsmn construction, the pCAS vector (Addgene #60 847) fragment and single guide RNA (sgRNA) inserts were generated by PCR and assembled by seamless cloning. To maximize Cas9 cleavage efficiency, we designed three different pCAS-sgRNAGe2 plasmids targeting the YIP11/YIP12 (Ge2) genes [30] and five different pCAS-sgRNAsmn plasmids targeting the SMN gene. Primers for sgRNA fragments are listed in Supplementary Table S2. All plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing.

In vivo genetic manipulations of Gemin2- and SMN-expressing genes in S. pombe

The haploid S. pombe strain 503 was used for Cas9-assisted knockout of the Gemin2-expressing genes YIP11 (SPAC19B12.12c) and YIP12 (SPAPB17E12.02) via homologous recombination, in the presence of pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 or pFAN-URA3-spGe2. Cells from glycerol stocks were resuscitated and cultured in YES or EMM2 medium at 30°C.

The creation of the Ge2Δ/pFAN-URA3-spGe2 stain was described previously [30]. The Ge2Δ/pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 stain was created similarly. Since YIP11/YIP12 double deletion is lethal [30], cell growth is supported by the pFAN-URA3-spGe2 or pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 plasmid.

The Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 stain was generated for subsequent genetic manipulations. First, the Ge2Δ/pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 stain was transformed with pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 and selected on EMM2 lacking leucine. Transformants were then plated on medium containing 0.5 mg/ml 5-fluoroorotic acid (5-FOA) to counter-select against the pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 plasmid; surviving colonies constituted the desired strain.

All strains with truncated SMN variants were derived from the Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 stain. To prevent growth defects during SMN truncation, the plasmid pFAN-URA3-spSMN (expressing full-length SMN) was first transformed into this strain. Subsequently, the resulting strain was co-transformed with a pCAS-sgRNAsmn plasmid and a donor DNA fragment. The pCAS-sgRNAsmn plasmid expresses Cas9 and a sgRNA targeting the SMN locus to enhance recombination efficiency. Donor DNA fragments (Supplementary Table S3), generated by PCR, contained the truncated SMN gene flanked by homology arms. Strain with His-tagged SMN, His-tagged SMNΔ (1–40), SMN (41–78)P, or SMNΔ (1–40)-SMN (41–78)P were created using the same method.

Transformants were selected on EMM2 plates lacking uracil and leucine, and supplemented with 100 μg/ml NTC, 100 μg/ml G418, 0.05 g/l adenine, and 0.25 g/l each of histidine and lysine (EMM2-ΔUra-ΔLeu). Genotypes were verified by PCR with primers P3-F/P3-R (Supplementary Table S2) and Xba I digestion, followed by sequencing. To assess phenotypic effects of SMN truncations, verified transformants were cultured in EMM2-ΔUra-ΔLeu medium, serially diluted 1:10, and spotted onto EMM2-ΔLeu plates containing 0.5 mg/ml 5-FOA for essentiality testing, and onto EMM2-ΔUra-ΔLeu plates as a control. The pFAN-URA3-spSMN plasmid is lost in the presence of 5-FOA. Plates were incubated at 30°C for 3–5 days. Growth was assessed based on both colony size (reflecting individual proliferation rate) and colony number (reflecting plating efficiency/cell viability). All strains used in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from S. pombe cells (25–40 mg cell pellet) cultured to mid-log phase (OD600 = 0.8–1.0) using the Yeast Total RNA Isolation Kit (B518627, Sangon Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with OD260/280 ratios of 1.9–2.1 considered acceptable. Genomic DNA was removed, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 2.5 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript reverse transcription (RT) Reagent Kit with genomic DNA (gRNA) Eraser (RR047A, Takara Bio) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA integrity was assessed by quantifying the 5′ and 3′ fragments of the reference gene ACT1 using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B).

qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green I Master Mix (Beijing LABLEAD) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each 20 μl reaction contained 10 μl of 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.2 μM each of forward and reverse primers, and 3 μl of a 1:6 dilution of cDNA. The cycling conditions were: 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 58°C for 30 s. A dissociation curve analysis was performed to verify amplification specificity. ACT1 was used as the endogenous reference gene. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. For each sample, three independent biological replicates were analyzed, each with three technical replicates. No-template controls and no-reverse transcription (no-RT) controls were included in each run. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S4. A two-tailed t-test was used performed on the ΔCt values for statistical analysis.

Identification of proteins interacting with eBrr1 or SMN in S. pombe by pull-down and mass spectrometry

Strains Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-His-eBrr1 and Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-His-eBrr1/SMNΔ (1–40) were created to identify proteins interacting with His-eBrr1. Strains Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1/His-SMN and Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1/His-SMNΔ (1–40) were created to identify proteins interacting with His-SMN and His-SMNΔ (1–40), respectively. A strain with His-SMN replacing the wild-type SMN gene served as a control. These strains were grown in EMM2 lacking leucine and supplemented with NTC to an OD600nm of 0.8–1.0, then harvested by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min). Cell pellets were resuspended in cold lysis buffer and disrupted by high-pressure homogenization (800–1000 bar, 8–10 min). After centrifugation (15 000 rpm, 35 min), the supernatant was subjected to Ni-NTA pull-down. After washing, proteins eluted from the beads were concentrated to 300 μl and analyzed by SDS–PAGE and CBB staining.

Visible bands were excised and subjected to in-gel trypsin digestion and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis as previously described [5, 53]. Liquid chromatography coupled with Tandem MS (LC-MS/MS) was performed using a ThermoFisher Vanquish Neo LC system coupled to a ThermoFisher Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. Peptide data were searched against the S. pombe proteome database (supplemented with the eBrr1 sequence) using the MaxQuant software (v1.6.2.6). The resulting list of candidate proteins was refined based on molecular weight, number of unique peptides, sequence coverage, and MaxQuant score (Supplementary Table S5).

Results

Engineering Brr1 to bind spSmD1/D2/F/E/G

To explore whether Brr1 can substitute for the entire SMN complex in S. pombe, we first tested its ability to bind to S. pombe SmD1/D2 and SmF/E/G. Despite being a Gemin2 orthologue in S. cerevisiae (Fig. 1A and B), Brr1 was unable to bind spSmD1/D2 and spSmF/E/G (Fig. 1C, lane 7). This was not entirely surprising. While Gemin2 homologs can be functionally interchangeable between some species—for instance, fruit fly Gemin2 can bind human Sm proteins [12, 42]—Brr1 and human Gemin2 fail to rescue the Gemin2 gene disruption in Arabidopsis [54].

The three SmD1/D2-interacting regions (labeled R1–R3) are highlighted in yellow. (B) Sequence alignment of CTDs from Gemin2 orthologs and engineered Brr1 (eBrr1). The six point mutations introduced into Brr1 are highlighted in green. (C) Pull-down assay testing the binding of HT-Brr1 (wild-type) and HT-eBrr1 to the spSm proteins. The structural basis for Gemin2’s interaction with Sm proteins provides an explanation. The N-terminal F/E-binding residues are highly conserved, featuring the YLxxVxxE motif followed by a four-residue sequence containing β-branched residues (e.g. V, T, I) that preferentially form a β-strand with β2 of SmF [46]. The C-terminal D1/D2-binding domain is also conserved; however, the D1/D2-interacting residues exhibit low conservation (Fig. 1A and B). The CTD of Gemin2 contains three regions (R1–R3) that interact with D1/D2: the N-terminal portion of α5, the loop connecting α6 and α7 (along with the N-terminal portion of α7), and the C-terminal 310 helix (Fig. 1A and B). R2 is primarily responsible for interactions with D1/D2; however, its sequence varies across species.

We therefore hypothesized that this sequence divergence in R2 underlies Brr1’s inability to bind to spSmD1/D2, and predicted that engineering Brr1’s R2 to match the sequence of spGemin2 would restore binding. To test this, we created an engineered Brr1 (eBrr1) by replacing six residues in its R2 region with the corresponding sequence from spGemin2 [“RVTA (268–271)” to “LLNG”, plus T274I and K283S; Fig. 1B]. As anticipated, eBrr1 successfully bound both spSmD1/D2 and spSmF/E/G in a pull-down assay (Fig. 1C, lane 5).

Expressing eBrr1 can substitute for Gemin2 in S. pombe cells

Following the successful in vitro engineering of Brr1 to bind spSmD1/D2/F/E/G, we next investigated whether expressing eBrr1 in S. pombe would interfere with Sm core assembly and cell growth. We constructed the plasmid pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 and transformed it into the S. pombe 503 strain, using the empty plasmid pFAN-URA3 as a control. Both transformed cells could grow on EMM2 plates lacking uracil, indicating the presence of the plasmids inside cells. To compare growth rate, both strains were spotted in serial dilutions on EMM2 plates lacking uracil and incubated at 30°C for 3–5 days. Compared with the control strain, the strain expressing eBrr1 exhibited a slight growth defect based on reduced colony number (data not shown). This indicates that eBrr1 bound SmD1/D2/F/E/G inside S. pombe and was involved in Sm core assembly. As overexpression of wildtype spGemin2 in S. pombe cells has been reported to cause a growth defect [55], the growth defect observed upon eBrr1 expression likely reflects an additive effect of eBrr1 and endogenous spGemin2, analogous to spGemin2 overexpression.

We further investigated whether eBrr1 could completely substitute for spGemin2 in S. pombe cells. To this end, we needed to knock out the YIP11 and YIP12 genes, both expressing spGemin2, in the presence of eBrr1 to test cell survival. As spGemin2 is essential to cell survival, knockout of both YIP11 and YIP12 would cause cell death unless there is a protein functioning similarly to spGemin2. We used the same Cas9-assisted knockout approach (Fig. 2A) that we previously employed to determine the essentiality of spGemin2 [30]. In addition, we used a strain transformed with pFAN-URA3-spGe2 as a control. We transformed these cells with a second plasmid, pCAS-sgRNA, which would express Cas9 and a specific sgRNA targeting an identical site in both the YIP11 and YIP12 genes, and a mixture of two PCR products (1:1) which contained the NAT1 gene flanked by the upstream and downstream sequences of YIP11 and YIP12, respectively. Cas9-assisted cleavage at the YIP11 and YIP12 genes facilitated homologous recombination. Transformants were grown on EMM2 plates lacking uracil, and successful spGe2 knockout strains were verified by PCR (Fig. 2B and C). We used the wildtype strain transformed with the empty plasmid pFAN-URA3 as a second control.

All three strains were spotted in serial dilutions on EMM2 medium containing 5-FOA to test for plasmid dependence (Fig. 2D). As anticipated, the wildtype strain containing the empty pFAN-URA3 vector grew well. However, the yip11Δ/yip12Δ (Ge2Δ) mutant strains carrying either pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 or pFAN-URA3-spGe2 failed to grow on FOA. This indicates that these strains are unable to lose the plasmid, demonstrating that eBrr1 is able to complement the essential function of spGemin2. Furthermore, when cultured in liquid medium at 30°C, all three strains exhibited similar growth rates (Fig. 2E), confirming that eBrr1 compensates for the loss of spGemin2 under standard growth conditions. To test whether eBrr1 functions in snRNP core assembly, we measured the steady-state levels of U1, U2, U4, and U5-snRNAs in the Ge2Δ/pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 strain using qRT-PCR and found no significant differences compared to wildtype (Supplementary Fig. S1). This further supports that eBrr1 complements spGemin2 specifically within the snRNP assembly pathway.

Survival of the S. pombe strain with eBrr1 replacing Gemin2 and Ge2BD deletion in SMN

After eBrr1 successfully substituted for Gemin2 in S. pombe, we asked if SMN, or part of SMN, could be further dispensed with inside cells (Fig. 3A). Because SMN is essential for wildtype cell survival, truncation and deletion of the SMN gene is likely lethal [25–27]. To avoid this situation, we planned to supply the plasmid pFAN-URA3-SMN inside cells to enable the deletion and truncation of the SMN gene in the genome, and then evaluate cell survival in the presence of FOA to exclude the SMN-expressing plasmid. Therefore, we generated the strain Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 by transforming the plasmid pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 and excluding pFAN-URA3-eBrr1 on FOA-containing plates and used this strain as the starting strain for further genetic manipulation of the SMN gene (Fig. 3B). Similar to Gemin2 manipulation, the Cas9-assisted recombination method was used. To facilitate identification of the correct recombination of SMN variants in the genome, an Xba I restriction cleavage site was introduced into the donor DNA, and therefore, the correct strain would be identified by the length of the PCR product as well as its cleavage by Xba I. The SMN gene was successfully substituted for by SMNΔ (9-40), SMNΔ (1–40), SMNΔ (1–78), SMNΔ (1–119), or SMNΔ in the presence of pFAN-URA3-SMN, as verified by the length of the PCR product and its cleavage by Xba I (Fig. 3C). All these strains exhibited comparable colony size and number on EMM2 plates lacking leucine and uracil (Fig. 3D). When these strains were spotted on EMM2 plates lacking leucine and containing FOA to exclude full-length SMN expression, the strains with SMNΔ (1–78), SMNΔ (1–119), or SMNΔ could not grow (Fig. 3D). This indicated that SMN, at least the CTD of SMN, is crucial to cell survival. However, the strains SMNΔ (9-40) and SMNΔ (1–40) grew similarly to the starting strain Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 (Fig. 3D). This is surprising because SMN (10-35) is the Gemin2-binding domain (Ge2BD) and deletion of this domain causes cell death [25–27]; therefore, these two SMN truncated variants were anticipated to fail to survive.

To further verify this phenomenon, we created two controls for each SMN truncated variant: (i) the wildtype strain transformed with the empty plasmids pFAN-LEU2 and pFAN-URA3, and (ii) each SMN truncated version on the wildtype background which contains the two plasmids pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 and pFAN-URA3-SMN. These strains were spotted in serial dilutions on EMM2 plates lacking leucine and uracil, as well as on EMM2 plates lacking leucine but containing FOA (Fig. 3E). In the presence of episomal full-length SMN expression, the SMNΔ (1–40) and SMNΔ strains derived from both the Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 and wildtype background strains exhibited growth patterns similar to those of the wildtype control. However, the SMNΔ (1–78) and SMNΔ (1–119) strains from both backgrounds displayed slightly reduced plating efficiency. These indicate that the more extensively N-terminally truncated SMN molecules form oligomers with the full-length SMN and may interfere with the Sm core assembly. In the absence of episomal full-length SMN expression, regardless of whether the background featured wildtype Gemin2 or eBrr1 replacing Gemin2, the expression of SMNΔ (1–78) and SMNΔ (1–119) failed to support cell survival. However, the expression of SMNΔ (1–40) supported cell growth when eBrr1 replaced Gemin2, but not in the presence of wild-type Gemin2. These results further confirm that replacing Gemin2 with eBrr1 in S. pombe permits the deletion of the Ge2BD of SMN.

eBrr1 has no interaction with the SMN/Gemin6–8 complex or any other unknown protein factors

Although the Ge2BD of SMN is the primary Gemin2-interacting region within the same species, eBrr1—derived from S. cerevisiae—might interact with SMNΔ (1–40) and full-length SMN in S. pombe. To test if eBrr1 can bind to them directly, purified eBrr1 was incubated with equimolar amounts of purified SMNΔ (1–40) or full-length SMN, followed by GFC. In each experiment, eBrr1 eluted separately from SMNΔ (1–40) or full-length SMN (Fig. 4A and B), indicating no direct interaction between eBrr1 and SMN.

To investigate if eBrr1 has indirect interactions with SMN as well as potential unidentified binding partners inside cells, we engineered N-terminal His-tagged eBrr1 in a strain with eBrr1 replacing Gemin2, and performed Ni-NTA bead pull-down assays. Additionally, we constructed N-terminal His-tagged SMN or SMNΔ (1–40) in the Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1 strain to pull down its interacting proteins. A wildtype strain expressing His-tagged SMN served as the control. Bound proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and then stained with CBB, and the bands were excised, digested with trypsin, and analyzed by MS (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S5). The control group showed His-SMN pulling down Gemin2, Gemin6–Gemin8, and 5Sm proteins D1/D2/F/E/G (Fig. 4C), consistent with previous studies [29, 30]. In the eBrr1-replacing-Gemin2 strain, His-SMN or His-SMNΔ (1–40) pulled down only Gemin6–Gemin8 (Fig. 4D and E), indicating that the SMN/Gemin6–8 complex does not interact with eBrr1 or Sm proteins. His-eBrr1 pulled down SmD1/D2/F/E/G and ICln, but no SMN/Gemin6–8 complex members and other proteins (Fig. 4F and G). These results confirm that eBrr1 binds spSmD1/D2/F/E/G, consistent with the in vitro experimental result described above, and that eBrr1 does not interact with any SMN/Gemin6–8 complex members. Compared to the control (in which the SMN complex pulled down only the 5Sm proteins), eBrr1 additionally pulled down ICln, albeit to a lesser extent. This suggests that eBrr1 binds 5Sm together with ICln, and the SMN/Gemin6–8 complex or SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 complex may partially release ICln from the eBrr1/5Sm complex.

The SMN/Gemin6–8 complex releases ICln from eBrr1/5Sm in vitro

Based on the above results, eBrr1 is unlikely to mediate Sm core assembly by directly interacting with the SMN/Gemin6–8 complex or recruiting other proteins. However, the SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 complex appeared to be capable of releasing ICln from eBrr1/5Sm in vivo, at least partially. To test this in vitro, we reconstituted the 5Sm/ICln and SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 complexes using purified proteins and incubated them with His-tagged eBrr1 and then performed a Ni-bead pulldown assay. As a control, the 5Sm/ICln complex was incubated with His-tagged eBrr1 alone. Since purified ICln was prone to degradation, we also used its C-terminally truncated version, ICln (1–160), which retains the entire PH domain and was utilized in our previous in vitro study [30]. In addition, ICln (1–160) can replace the full-length ICln in S. pombe cells without a growth defect (unpublished data). As expected, without SMN complex incubation, His-tagged eBrr1 retained both 5Sm and ICln (1–160) or ICln (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S3A). However, after the SMN complex incubation, His-tagged eBrr1 pulled down only 5Sm, not ICln (1–160) or ICln or any SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 complex members (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S3B). This indicates that the SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 complex releases ICln from eBrr1/5Sm, consistent with the in vivo His-eBrr1 pulldown experiment results (Fig. 4F and G).

We further tested whether the SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8 complex could release ICln from eBrr1/5Sm using the same approach. Surprisingly, incubating the 6S complex [5Sm/ICln (1–160) or 5Sm/ICln] and His-eBrr1 with SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8 also released ICln from eBrr1/5Sm (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S3C). This contrasted with the growth failure of the corresponding strain where eBrr1 replaced Gemin2 and SMNΔ (1–78) replaced full-length SMN (Fig. 3D). Considering that pull-down assays reflect only final interaction states and neglect kinetic differences, we hypothesized that the two SMN complex variants differ in their ICln release rates. Thus, we used fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) to compare ICln (1–160) release rates from eBrr1/5Sm in the presence of these SMN complexes (Fig. 5D). Initially, Cy3-labeled ICln (1–160) was incubated with Cy5-labeled spSmD1/D2 and spSmF/E/G to form the 6S complex. After purification by GFC, the 6S complex was incubated with equimolar SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 and eBrr1 or SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8 and eBrr1, and FRET signals were recorded over time. As anticipated, SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 reduced the FRET signal faster than SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8 (Fig. 5E), suggesting that SMN (41–78) facilitates SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 recruitment near ICln/5Sm/eBrr1, accelerating ICln release compared to SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8.

The entire region between SMN’s Ge2BD and YG box (residues 35–120) is predicted to be a flexible loop lacking regular secondary structures [30]. The segment of SMN (41–78) contains many charged and polar residues, including 10 negatively charged (26.3%), 4 positively charged (10.5%), and 12 polar residues (31.6%) (Fig. 5F). Given its high negative charge density, we investigated whether these residues facilitate Sm core assembly. We introduced mutations in SMN (41–78), creating SMN (41–78)P (where all 12 negatively charged residues were mutated to positively charged residues) (Fig. 5F) in the genomic SMN gene of the Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1/SMNΔ (1–40) strain and the wildtype strain. To mitigate potential growth defects, we transformed these strains with the plasmid pFAN-URA3-SMN. The wildtype control received only empty vector pFAN-URA3. Strains were spotted in serial dilutions on YES plates and YES + FOA plates (Fig. 5G). As anticipated, all strains grew similarly on YES plates. However, on YES + FOA plates (excluding pFAN-URA3-SMN), their growth phenotypes differed. Unexpectedly, introducing SMN (41–78)P into the Ge2Δ/pFAN-LEU2-eBrr1/SMNΔ (1–40) strain completely halted cell survival. Introducing SMN (41–78)P into the wildtype strain also severely reduced growth rate, as evidenced by substantially reduced colony size, while colony numbers remained comparable to control (Fig. 5G). These results suggest that the negatively charged residues in SMN (41–78) not only maintain loop flexibility, but also play a critical role in Sm core assembly, likely by facilitating ICln release from the 6S complex.

Survival of the cells with SMN split into SMN (1–40) and SMNΔ (1–40)

Given that SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 releases ICln from 5Sm/eBrr1 while supporting cell growth, and that the Ge2BD of spSMN (residues 1–35) enhances spGemin2 binding to spSmD1/D2/F/E/G [30], we hypothesized that spSMN (1–40), provided in trans, would bind to spGemin2 and form a stable complex that functions similarly to eBrr1, thereby rescuing the viability of a strain expressing only the C-terminal portion of SMN [SMNΔ (1–40)]. In other words, we proposed that SMN’s function could be split between two polypeptides—SMN (1–40) and SMNΔ (1–40)—to sustain cell growth.

To test this hypothesis, we created an SMNΔ (1–40) strain with plasmid pFAN-URA3-SMN and transformed it with a second plasmid: pFAN-LEU2-SMN (1–40) (testing the hypothesis), pFAN-LEU2-SMN (positive control), or the empty pFAN-LEU2 vector (negative control). We also used the wildtype strain containing two empty vectors (pFAN-URA3 and pFAN-LEU2) as an additional positive control. These strains were spotted in serial dilutions on control plates (EMM2 lacking leucine and uracil) and counter-selection plates (EMM2 lacking leucine but containing FOA) to assess growth after loss of the URA3-containing plasmid (Fig. 6A). On control plates, all strains grew similarly due to full-length SMN expression. On FOA plates, as anticipated, the wildtype strain grew as well as it did on the control plates. However, the other strains exhibited varying growth phenotypes. Among them, the SMNΔ (1–40)/pFAN-LEU2-SMN strain exhibited the best plating efficiency due to episomal full-length SMN expression. The SMNΔ (1–40)/pFAN-LEU2 strain failed to grow, which is consistent with prior genetic studies on N-terminal SMN truncation [26, 27] and our previous in vitro finding that Gemin2 cannot bind 5Sm without SMN’s Ge2BD [30]. As expected, the SMNΔ (1–40)/pFAN-LEU2-SMN (1–40) strain grew, albeit with reduced plating efficiency compared with the wildtype and SMNΔ (1–40)/pFAN-LEU2-SMN strains. Furthermore, when strains that survived FOA selection were cultured in liquid medium at 30°C, their growth density curves were correlated with their plating efficiencies observed on the FOA plates (Fig. 6B). These results support our hypothesis that the Ge2BD of SMN, when provided in trans and presumably in complex with endogenous Gemin2, can partially substitute for eBrr1’s function and that the Ge2BD can be physically separated from the C-terminal region of SMN while retaining essential biological activity.

Discussion

In this study, to determine whether Brr1, the only component of the SMN complex in S. cerevisiae [52], could functionally replace the entire multiple-membered SMN complex in other species, we engineered Brr1 to bind to S. pombe SmD1/D2/F/E/G and substituted eBrr1 for Gemin2 and SMN inside S. pombe cells to explore the Sm core assembly mechanism. The SMN complex in S. pombe contains only five members, each of which is essential for cell survival [29, 30]. It therefore represents a miniature multiple-membered SMN complex and serves as a good model for studying the fundamental mechanism of chaperone-dependent Sm core assembly [29, 30]. Since Brr1 is the orthologue of Gemin2, only a minimal number of mutations were required to enable eBrr1 to bind to spSmD1/D2/F/E/G (Fig. 1). The replacement of spGemin2 by eBrr1 inside cells was successful (Fig. 2). However, eBrr1 could not replace both spGemin2 and spSMN, indicating that spSMN plays a crucial role in Sm core assembly (Fig. 3). Interestingly, in the strain where eBrr1 replaced spGemin2, the removal of the Ge2BD of spSMN still supported cell growth, in contrast to the control SMNΔ (1–40) strain in the wildtype Gemin2 background, which failed to grow (Fig. 3). While an in vitro interaction assay demonstrated no direct interaction between purified eBrr1 and SMN, pulldown assays using in vivo-expressed His-tagged eBrr1, SMN, or SMNΔ (1–40) provided further evidence that no stable indirect interaction exists between the eBrr1/5Sm/ICln and SMN/Gemin6–8 complexes (Fig. 4). Using reconstituted complexes, we showed that the SMN/Gemin6–8 complex can release ICln from the 5Sm/eBrr1 complex, even in the absence of the N-terminal segment (residues 1–78) of SMN (Fig. 5). This indicates that the CTD of SMN and Gemin6–Gemin8 together play an important role in releasing ICln from the 6S complex. However, the negatively charged residues in the middle loop (residues 41–78) of SMN also play an important role in Sm core assembly, most likely facilitating ICln release from the 6S complex (Fig. 5). Based on these findings, we further demonstrated that separating the SMNGe2BD from the rest of SMN, which contains the middle loop and YG box, still allows for Sm core assembly and maintains cell survival (Fig. 6). Although this study did not produce an S. pombe strain with its entire SMN complex replaced by eBrr1, it generated several interesting results that provide insights into the mechanism and evolution of chaperone-dependent Sm core assembly.

Although the basic mechanism of Sm core assembly has been largely elucidated by previous biochemical and structural studies, a key mechanistic question remains: how does the SMN complex trigger the release of pICln from the 6Sm complex? Previous studies provided only limited insights, establishing that all five members of the SMN complex are required [30] and suggesting that the flexible SmG–pICln contact may be the initiation point for release [42]. This study provides new and intriguing insights into this mechanism. First, we show that the CTD of SMN and its bound Gemin6–Gemin8 are not only essential for pICln release but can perform this function without binding to Gemin2. This is demonstrated by our finding that SMNΔ (1–40)/Gemin6–8 remains functional for Sm core assembly in vivo, as evidenced by the viability of strains containing eBrr1 (replacing Gemin2) and SMNΔ (1–40), or where SMN is split into SMN (1–40) and SMNΔ (1–40) (Figs 3 and 6). Furthermore, in vitro release assays confirmed that even SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8 retains the ability to release pICln, albeit at a reduced rate (Fig. 5C–E). Second, we identify the loop between the Ge2BD and the YG box of SMN as a critical element for pICln release. This loop not only tethers the SMN/Gemin6–8 complex near the Gemin2/5Sm/pICln complex, but its charged residues (41–78) may also play an active role. Supporting this, replacing the wild-type SMN loop with the mutant SMN (41–78)P causes a significant growth defect (Fig. 5G). Given that pICln has a highly acidic pI value (around 4) and contains a conserved, extended acidic loop (L5) [42], the negative charges in the SMN loop may facilitate the expulsion of pICln from 5Sm. The exact role of this loop in Sm core assembly, including whether it acts via specific side-chain interactions or net charge density, awaits further investigation.

In a parallel study, we systematically investigated the Sm core assembly mechanism in S. cerevisiae. We demonstrated that although only two chaperone proteins, Brr1 and Lot5, are involved in this process, they are nonessential for cell survival [53]. Furthermore, using purified Sm proteins, we showed that scSmD1/D2 and scSmF/E/G can form a stable 5Sm complex, which provides a mechanistic explanation for the nonessentiality of these chaperones at a molecular level [53]. The current work provides additional support for the connection between Sm protein interactions and chaperone-dependence by introducing a key chaperone from S. cerevisiae into a different species, S. pombe, where Sm core assembly is chaperone-dependent and its SmD1/D2 and SmF/E/G cannot form a stable 5Sm complex. Although eBrr1 can bind to spSmD1/D2/F/E/G and replace spGemin2 in cells, cells require either eBrr1 or spGemin2 to survive (Fig. 2D). Moreover, even in the presence of eBrr1, SMN cannot be completely disrupted (Fig. 3D and E). These in vivo results indicate that Brr1 is not a stand-alone protein capable of replacing the entire five-member SMN complex, further supporting a tight linkage between the inability to form a stable 5Sm complex and the chaperone dependence of Sm core assembly.

Finally, this work provides new insights into the evolution of assembly chaperones for the Sm core. Because of the small number of assembly chaperones and their nonessentiality, the Sm core assembly system in S. cerevisiae represents a very early stage in the evolutionary tree, where the direct assembly pathway is dominant (Fig. 7A). In contrast, the Sm core assembly in S. pombe is chaperone-dependent, relying on the five-membered SMN complex and ICln [30]. It therefore represents a miniature chaperone-dependent Sm core assembly system (Fig. 7D). From an evolutionary perspective, a significant gap exists between these two distinct chaperone systems. For example, why and how did two chaperones evolve into six? Considering the structure and function similarity between Lot5 and ICln, this question can be reduced to: why and how did the single protein Brr1 evolve into a five-membered SMN complex? The creation of eBrr1 and our attempts to replace components of the SMN complex in S. pombe provide clues to address this question.

Proposed model for the evolution of snRNP core assembly chaperones. (A) In species like S. cerevisiae, SmD1/D2 and SmF/E/G can form a stable 5Sm complex, favoring a direct assembly pathway. Although Gemin2 and pICln are present, they are not essential. (B) An intermediate evolutionary stage is proposed, where an early SMN/Gemin6–8 complex could catalyze pICln release from the 6S complex in the presence of Gemin2, despite lacking a direct physical interaction with Gemin2. (C) A subsequent mutation in the SMN N-terminus enabled direct binding to Gemin2, leading to the formation of the stable, five-subunit SMN complex, which enhances pICln release efficiency. Because SMNΔ (1–78)/Gemin6–8 is able to release ICln from the 5Sm/eBrr1 complex, the SMN/Gemin6–8 heterotetramer may have evolved separately, acquiring the capability to release ICln before establishing a direct interaction with Gemin2 (Fig. 7B). Later, mutations in the N-terminal segment (residues 10–35) of SMN might have enabled it to bind to Gemin2, forming the minimal five-membered SMN complex, which can then efficiently remove ICln from the 5Sm/Gemin2 complex (Fig. 7C).

Structural analysis provides additional insight into the potential evolutionary trajectory by which SMN acquired the ability to bind Gemin2. The interaction mode between Gemin2 and SMN has been revealed by crystal structures from both humans and fruit flies [42, 46]. In Gemin2, the interface that binds SMN is formed primarily by helices α2, α6, and α8, which together create a mostly hydrophobic surface (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B). In the structural model of Brr1, the corresponding interface is similarly hydrophobic (Supplementary Fig. S4C). The Ge2BD of SMN is small, consisting of only about 12 residues. It forms a typical amphipathic three-turn helix and interacts with Gemin2 through its hydrophobic face (Supplementary Fig. S4D and E). In the structural model of Brr1, the region corresponding to the SMN Ge2BD is occupied by an extended loop between α7 and α8. This loop appears less capable of covering the exposed hydrophobic interface compared to the SMN Ge2BD helix [Supplementary Fig. S4C; compare with panels (A) and (B)]. These observations suggest that only a small number of mutations in the unstructured N-terminal region of SMN could have enabled it to fold into a short α-helix, thereby acquiring a stronger binding interface with Gemin2 than the intramolecular coverage previously provided by Gemin2 itself.

Due to the efficiency of this five-membered SMN complex, mutations at the Sm protein interfaces that would otherwise be deleterious might have become better tolerated. Consequently, SmF/E/G could form a ring-like dimer of trimers, which competes with interactions with SmD1/D2 and prevents the formation of a stable SmD1/D2/F/E/G complex (Fig. 7D). ICln binds to SmD1/D2 to form ICln/SmD1/D2, which has two interacting surfaces and becomes a better binder to SmF/E/G than SmD1/D2 alone. Therefore, ICln might have become an essential chaperone to enable the formation of SmD1/D2/F/E/G in the 6S complex, and the five-membered SMN complex might have subsequently become the essential chaperone system to remove ICln (Fig. 7D). Interestingly, not only the unicellular S. cerevisiae but also the multicellular plant Arabidopsis contain nonessential Gemin2 and pICln [53, 56, 57]. This suggests there may be other, less-studied species that represent these evolutionary intermediate stages of assembly chaperone systems.

In vertebrates, the SMN complex comprises nine members, and the PRMT5 complex contains three, representing a significant increase in complexity compared to the miniature system in S. pombe. The precise roles of these additional vertebrate-specific chaperones in Sm core assembly remain poorly understood. For instance, Gemin5 binds both pre-snRNAs and mRNAs, implicating it in both snRNP biogenesis and translation regulation [50, 51]. Similarly, Unrip was initially identified as an Unr-interacting protein involved in cap-independent translation before its role in the SMN complex was discovered [24]. Furthermore, PRMT5/WD45 catalyzes arginine methylation not only on Sm proteins but also on histones and other signaling proteins [58]. A key question arises: do these multifunctional proteins have dedicated, nonredundant functions within the snRNP assembly pathway, or are their roles in this process a consequence of their broader cellular activities?

Beyond the addition of new subunits, the evolution of the core chaperone machinery itself may have introduced new layers of regulation. This is exemplified by the insertion of a Tudor domain within the linker region between the Ge2BD and YG box of the vertebrate SMN protein. This domain, which binds methylated Sm protein tails, is a site where point mutations can cause SMA [40, 59], highlighting its critical, and likely regulatory, function. Similarly, while the C-terminus of pICln is dispensable in S. pombe, it is subject to phosphorylation in higher eukaryotes, a modification that influences snRNP biogenesis [60, 61]. These examples suggest that as the assembly system became more complex, even its core components acquired new regulatory modules.

The simplified and genetically tractable S. pombe system, combined with the insights from our evolutionary model, provides a powerful foundation for dissecting these fundamental mechanisms. Future work aimed at reconstructing elements of the vertebrate chaperone system in S. pombe could help unravel the specific, nonredundant functions of the additional chaperones and regulatory modules that have emerged over the course of evolution.
