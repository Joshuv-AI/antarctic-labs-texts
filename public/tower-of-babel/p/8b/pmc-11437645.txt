Coronary artery calcium score and other risk factors in patients at moderate and high risk of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity
Authors: Anna Borowiec, Patrycja Ozdowska, Magdalena Rosinska, Agnieszka Maria Zebrowska, Agnieszka Jagiello-Gruszfeld, Sławomir Jasek, Joanna Waniewska, Beata Kotowicz, Hanna Kosela-Paterczyk, Elzbieta Lampka, Katarzyna Pogoda, Andrzej Cieszanowski, Zbigniew Nowecki, Jan Walewski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe presence and burden of coronary artery calcium (CAC) is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events. Current guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) for cardio-oncology do not recommend the use of the CAC score to determine the status of risk in cancer patients. The aim of this study is to evaluate the presence and burden of CAC on cardiac tomography and the distribution of the cardiovascular toxicity risk factors in patients with moderate and high baseline risk of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity.MethodsThe study prospectively included cancer patients, diagnosed and qualified for systemic treatment with anthracycline chemotherapy. Clinical data and blood samples were collected from all patients. Additionally, the echocardiography and coronary computed tomography (CCTA) with the calculation of the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score were performed.ResultsA total of 80 patients (mean age 60.5 years, 75 female) were included in the study. The majority of patients (62, 77.5%) had breast cancer, 11 (13.8%) were diagnosed with sarcoma, and 7 (8.8%) with lymphoma. There were 42 (52.5%) patients classified as having moderate (MR) and 38 (47.5%) as having high risk (HR) of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity according to current ESC guidelines. In comparison with moderate risk, high risk patients were older and more likely to have hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and chronic kidney disease. The mean coronary artery calcium score was significantly higher in the HR group (150.4 vs. 24.8; p = 0.000). Furthermore, cardiac biomarkers were also higher in high-risk patients (p = 0.000). In echocardiographic parameters global longitudinal strain (GLS) was lower (p = 0.012), and diastolic dysfunction was more common in the HR group. However, the left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF) was similar in the MR and HR groups.ConclusionsIn patients at high and moderate risk for cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity, cardiovascular toxicity risk factors were common and more prevalent in the high-risk group. The coronary artery calcium score was also significantly higher in the high-risk group. Assessing the presence and burden of coronary artery calcium is an attractive option to assess additional cardiovascular risk in cancer patients.

Introduction

In recent years, cancer mortality has decreased significantly for many cancers, mainly due to prevention, earlier diagnosis, and advances in oncological therapies. Improved patient survival has led to an increase in recognition of short- and late-term cardiovascular (CV) complications [1–3]. Cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity (CTR-CVT) is defined as any of the following: chemotherapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD), myocarditis, vascular toxicity, hypertension, arrhythmias, prolongation of QT, valvular heart diseases, coronary artery disease, thrombosis, thromboembolism, pericardial diseases, peripheral artery disease or pulmonary hypertension [1].

Many cardiovascular toxicity risk factors for cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity (CTR-CVT), such as older age, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and smoking, overlap with risk factors for atherosclerosis [1]. According to the Heart Failure Association - International Cardio-Oncology Society, baseline risk factors for CV toxicity in anthracycline-related CTR-CVT include: heart failure/previous CTRCD, severe valvular heart disease, coronary artery disease, left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF) < 54%, elevated cardiac biomarkers, age > 65 years, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, previous exposure to cancer therapy, smoking history and obesity [4]. Hyperlipidaemia is mentioned as a CTR-CVT risk factor for VEGF inhibitors, BCR-ABL inhibitors and multiple myeloma therapy, but not for anthracycline-based chemotherapy. However, statins (class IIa) are recommended for the primary prevention of CTRCD during anthracycline chemotherapy in adult patients at a high and very high CV toxicity risk [1]. This recommendation is based on retrospective studies and a few prospective studies [5–10].

The presence and burden of coronary artery calcium (CAC) is a strong predictor of cardiovascular events and is correlated with the presence of coronary artery stenosis [11]. In asymptomatic patients, CAC-based models have been shown to predict future cardiovascular events and mortality, independent of classic cardiovascular risk factors [12, 13].

Current guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) for cardio-oncology do not recommend the use of the CAC score to determine the status of risk in cancer patients. Although the CAC score is easily detected on routine chest computed tomography, the benefits of its clinical application in assessing cardiovascular risk in oncological patients before chemotherapy are currently controversial and unclear [14–20].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the presence and burden of CAC on cardiac tomography and the distribution of the cardiovascular toxicity risk factors in patients with moderate and high baseline risk of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity, as defined by the current ESC Cardio-Oncology Guidelines. This is the first study to assess the presence of coronary artery calcium score (CACS) in association with CV toxicity risk factors in these group of patients.

Methods

Study patients and structure

The study prospectively included 80 cancer patients, diagnosed and qualified for systemic treatment with anthracycline chemotherapy at the Maria Sklodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology in Warsaw. The patients gave their informed consent to participate in the study and met al.l the inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria, specified in the previously published protocol [21]. The trial was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. The conduct of the study was approved by an Independent Ethics Committee at the Maria Sklodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology in Warsaw (the date of approval – 28 October 2021, approval no. 80/2021). The registration identifier on clinicaltrials.gov is NCT05118178.

Cardiovascular toxicity risk factors, CAC scoring, and echocardiography

Clinical variables such as age, gender, cardiovascular toxicity risk factors, previous medical history, concomitant heart failure therapy, statins or other lipid lowering medications were collected by study investigators based on medical documentation provided by the patient and medical anamnesis. Patients were classified as having moderate (MR) or as having high risk (HR) of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity according Heart Failure Association–International Cardio-Oncology Society baseline cardiovascular toxicity risk stratification model, which is recommended in latest ESC guidelines [1]. None of the patients had previously received any type of chemotherapy or had radiotherapy. None of the patients had been diagnosed with coronary artery disease or heart failure.

All patients underwent coronary computed tomography (CCTA) with calculation of the coronary artery calcium (CAC) score according to the Agatston method. Cardiac CT scanning for CAC quantification was performed with a 64-slice CT scanner (Revolution Evo, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, USA) with prospective ECG triggering. The CAC score was quantified using the Agatston method with dedicated software by a well-trained researcher.

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed using a Philips EPIQ Elite instrument (Philips Healthcare, Andover, Massachusetts). The following echocardiographic parameters were assessed: ventricle diameters, wall thickness, left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF), and mitral inflow with the use of Doppler echocardiography. Diastolic function was evaluated by mitral inflow E/A pattern, E/A ratio, annular tissue Doppler curves and E/E’ ratio. Images were digitally stored for offline analysis on custom software (Q-Station, Philips Healthcare, Andover, Massachusetts). The two-dimensional left ventricular peak systolic global longitudinal strain (GLS) was analysed using an off-line semi-automated speckle tracking imaging technique from the three standard apical views. The analyses were performed by a board-certified physician, who was blinded to all clinical characteristics.

Blood samples were collected and analysed by the central laboratory (The Maria Sklodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology). Laboratory tests included among others: creatinine, glucose, morphology, glycated hemoglobin, lipid profile, plasma levels of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP).

Data acquisition and statistical analysis

Data acquisition and quality control

All laboratory measurements were performed according to routine diagnostic standards. Critical measurements (CCTA, ECHO) were reviewed and coded by a trained member of the study team to ensure comparability of the results (see above). Electronic case report forms (eCRF) were developed to capture study subject data in a secure internal environment, ensuring completeness and consistency of data through validity checks at data entry. Clinical data was entered manually and laboratory data was extracted from the Hospital Information System (HIS), matched by the patient’s unique identifier and the date of the first visit. The outlying values were manually validated by a study researcher.

Statistical analysis

In the descriptive analysis we summarised the distribution of patient characteristics, laboratory and imaging findings, overall and by CTRCT groups (moderate, MR and high risk, HR) assigned according to the latest ESC guidelines [1]. Differences in the distribution of variables between the MR and HR groups were evaluated with the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank sum test for numerical variables.

CAC score across risk factors was compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. To investigate the association between the CAC score and plasma levels of hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP, we used the CAC score categories: 0; 1–99; 100–399; 400 + as it was carried out in previous studies [13, 16, 18, 22–24]. We used Jonckheere–Terpstra test for trend in the levels of hs-cTnT and NT-proBNP with increasing CAC score.

Results

A total of 80 patients (mean age 60.5 years, 75 female) were included in the study. The majority of patients (62, 77.5%) had breast cancer, 11 (13.8%) were diagnosed with sarcoma, and 7 (8.8%) with lymphoma. The baseline characteristics of the patient are shown in Table 1. There were 42 (52.5%) patients classified as having moderate (MR) and 38 (47.5%) as having high risk (HR) of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity according to latest ESC guidelines [1]. Coronary artery calcium (CAC) score greater than 100 was calculated in 18 (22.5%) patients, above 200 in 14 (17.5%) and above 400 in 3 (3.8%) patients. In 30 (37.5%) patients baseline NT-proBNP was elevated and in 6 (7.5%) Troponin T was above normal laboratory limits. The coronary artery calcium score was higher in older patients, with elevated NT-proBNP, hyperlipidaemia and chronic kidney disease, Table 2.

Discussion

Oncological patients with cardiovascular risk factors and cardiovascular diseases are at increased risk for cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction and other cardiovascular complications. Cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity risk stratification using HFA-ICOS dedicated tools is recommended prior to cardiotoxic therapies [1].

By exclusion/inclusion criteria only patients with moderate and high risk for anthracycline cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity were included in our study and we note that nearly half of them were classified as high risk. According to the ESC guidelines, this group requires a cardiology evaluation before starting anticancer treatment.

In this study of patients at high and moderate risk for cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity, all CV toxicity risk factors were common and, as expected, were more prevalent in the high-risk group. These findings are consistent with the results of other studies [18, 25, 26]. In the CARDIOTOX registry, approximately 893 patients (67.4%) had at least one risk factor [26]. A recent study by Patel et al. reported hypertension in 33.1%, dyslipidaemia in 61.2% and diabetes in 7.3% of cancer patients [18]. There are limited prospective data on the prevalence of CV toxicity risk factors in high- and moderate-risk patients according to the HFA-ICOS risk score. In a retrospective cohort study of women with HER2 + early-stage breast cancer treated with trastuzumab, there were 7% of patients with diabetes, 21% with hypertension and 14% with hyperlipidaemia in the MR group. Furthermore, in the HR group diabetes was present in 28%, hypertension in 72% and hyperlipidaemia in 47% of the patients [27]. In our group of patients, the prevalence of risk factors was slightly higher, but this may be due to the retrospective nature of the Suntheralingam et al. study.

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) is a useful tool for measuring the calcium score and atherosclerosis burden in asymptomatic individuals at risk of coronary artery disease. The calcium score improves risk classification and helps guide primary prevention with statins in the general population [28]. In this study, coronary artery calcification was present in 53.7% of the patients and the coronary artery calcium score above 100 was calculated in 18 (22.5%) and more than 200 in 14 (17.5%) patients and was significantly higher in the high-risk group. These values are higher than reported in the literature, but the literature usually refers to cancer patients in general, while we specifically focus on the moderate to high risk subgroup. In the Gernaat et al. study, approximately 24% of breast cancer patients planned for radiation therapy had a CAC score above zero and one in three patients with CAC score above 100 did not have other cardiovascular risk factors and would not have been identified as high risk [16]. In a retrospective study by Hooks et al. coronary artery calcification was present in 194 (32.2%) and the extent of CAC was a significant predictor of the coronary composite outcome, but not the composite outcome of heart failure [14]. In a multicentre cohort of 66,636 asymptomatic adults without cardiovascular disease (CVD) at baseline, cancer mortality due to CV disease was reported in 306 patients in 6 years. Of these patients, CAC greater than 100 was present in 55.5% of the patients and was significantly higher in patients with CVD cause of mortality. Furthermore, high CAC predicted the risk of CVD death independently of CVD risk factors [20]. In the study by Shen et al. among 1468 patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma planned for anthracyclines, 286 (19.4%) patients had a CAC score above 100 [24]. In a recent retrospective analysis of over 700 cancer patients, it was observed that cancer patients had elevated coronary calcium scores even despite having a lower prevalence of CV risk factors compared to non-cancer patients [18]. On the contrary, in a retrospective study by Brann et al., there was no significant difference in CAC burden in the cardio-oncology clinic compared to the general clinic population [15].

Recent studies suggest a cardioprotective effect of statins on cardiotoxicity during oncological treatment [5–9, 29–31]. On this basis, the European Society of Cardiology 2022 Cardio-Oncology Guidelines recommend considering statins for the primary prevention of cardiac dysfunction associated with cancer therapy in patients at high and very high cardiovascular toxicity risk. Despite this, neither hyperlipidaemia nor CAC score is explicitly mentioned as a cardiovascular toxicity risk factor for anthracycline-based chemotherapy [1]. At the same time, we observe that hyperlipidaemia and high CAC score are quite prevalent both in moderate and high-risk groups and statins were already used for 31.2% of our study group.

The study included 25 (31.3%) patients taking statins, but after risk-adjustment (including lipids and CAC score), an additional 20 patients started statin therapy. Ultimately, 45 patients (56%) were taking statins before starting anthracycline chemotherapy.

Limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The population included a small cohort from a single cancer center, and the small sample size may have limited the power of this study. Moreover, the study population consists almost exclusively of women, as the majority of patients had breast cancer. Therefore, the results cannot be extrapolated to the general population. The strength of the study is that patients meeting previously published inclusion criteria [21] were prospectively included, making the collected data more reliable than in retrospective studies. Another strength of this study is that, all patients underwent computed tomography and echocardiography as part of the planned procedure, on the same devices, with the same acquisition protocols and described by the same sonographer and radiologist blinded to the clinical data.

This is the first study to assess the presence of coronary artery calcium score (CACS) in association with CV toxicity risk factors in patients at moderate and high baseline risk of cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity, as defined by the latest ESC Cardio-Oncology Guidelines. Future studies are needed to evaluate the prognostic impact of the CAC score, but at this stage more intensive statin prophylaxis may be recommended for patients with hyperlipidaemia and an associated high CAC score.

Conclusions

In this study of patients at high and moderate risk for cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity, CV toxicity risk factors were common and more prevalent in the high-risk group. In addition to the typical risk factors for cardiovascular complications during anthracycline treatment, the large number of patients with hyperlipidaemia should be emphasised. Furthermore, the coronary artery calcium score was also significantly higher in the high-risk group. Many cancer patients undergo non-gated chest computed tomography for cancer staging prior to treatment, therefore assessing the presence and burden of coronary artery calcium is an attractive option to assess additional cardiovascular risk. Further prospective studies are recommended to evaluate the impact of coronary artery calcification score and hyperlipidaemia, in addition to already established risk stratification, on the cancer therapy-related cardiovascular toxicity development during and after anthracycline treatment.
