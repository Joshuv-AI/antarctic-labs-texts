Development of a Vertical Flow Dot-Immunobinding Assay (Dot-Iba) for Rapid Detection of Neoscytalidium dimidiatum
Authors: Akkarapong Plengpanich, Sumanas Bunyaratavej, Anchalee Tungtrongchitr, Nawannaporn Saelim, Thapani Srisai, Charussri Leeyaphan, Piriyaporn Chongtrakool, Pichet Ruenchit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoscytalidium dimidiatum is a non-dermatophyte mold that commonly causes skin and nail infections in tropical regions and often resists conventional antifungal therapies. Because its clinical and laboratory features often resemble dermatophyte infections, diagnosis is frequently delayed and treatment is sometimes inappropriate. We therefore developed a dot-immunobinding assay (Dot-Iba) to detect N. dimidiatum antigens. We generated a highly specific monoclonal antibody, 3E6F7 (MAb 3E6F7), for antigen capture, and used goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) as the signal generator. The test pad comprised a test hole, a nitrocellulose membrane (NC), and water-absorbent pads in a vertical flow-through format to allow a rapid antigen–antibody reaction. The assembled system detected N. dimidiatum antigens in vitro with high specificity and yielded visible results within 2 h; its detection limit was 0.9 µg without cross-reactivity to dermatophyte or non-dermatophyte fungi. This rapid, specific, and easy-to-use assay shows strong potential as a diagnostic tool, particularly in settings with limited access to fungal culture or advanced molecular diagnostics, where early, accurate identification is crucial.Keywords: Dot-immunobinding assay (Dot-Iba), Fungal foot infection, Nail infection, Neoscytalidium dimidiatum, Rapid test

Introduction

Neoscytalidium dimidiatum is a non-dermatophyte mold that causes skin and nail infections, with high prevalence in tropical countries such as Thailand [1–6]. Transmission is believed to occur through contact with soil or contaminated environments [7]. This organism exhibits high virulence, with more rapid and deep nail invasion than dermatophytes [8, 9].

Fungal foot infections and onychomycosis can be caused by dermatophytes, non-dermatophyte molds, and yeasts [10]. Among non-dermatophyte molds, N. dimidiatum, Aspergillus spp., Fusarium spp., and Scopulariopsis brevicaulis are common causative agents of skin and nail infection [11, 12]. These fungi account for approximately 2%–12% of all fungal nail infections [12]. The global prevalence of non-dermatophyte skin and nail infections is gradually increasing [1]. In Thailand, 1.7% of patients attending dermatology clinics were diagnosed with onychomycosis, with 51.6% of cases caused by non-dermatophyte molds, predominantly N. dimidiatum [13]. Contributing factors may include tight footwear, poor foot hygiene, nail abnormalities or trauma, increased immunosuppression, and peripheral vascular disease [14, 15].

Although clinical presentations are often indistinguishable, treatment approaches differ significantly for dermatophyte versus non-dermatophyte infections [6, 16]. Non-dermatophyte molds tend to be more resistant to standard antifungal therapies. First-line treatments are typically based on regimens effective against dermatophytes [17]; consequently, infections caused by Neoscytalidium spp. often require prolonged therapy or combination of antifungal treatment, and many cases show poor response to conventional agents [11, 18–21]. For example, terbinafine and itraconazole are generally effective against dermatophyte-related onychomycosis but exhibit limited efficacy against non-dermatophyte infections [22]. Therefore, accurate species-level identification is essential to guide appropriate therapy, as misdiagnosis may lead to suboptimal outcomes and contribute to antifungal resistance.

Fungal culture is currently the standard method for diagnosing N. dimidiatum infections. However, species identification requires specialized expertise to interpret macro- and micromorphological features, and the prolonged incubation time (1–3 weeks) often delays appropriate treatment. Microscopic examination of skin or nail specimens using potassium hydroxide (KOH) is a rapid, inexpensive alternative but lacks the specificity to distinguish N. dimidiatum from other fungi. For instance, brown hyphae observed in KOH preparations may be misinterpreted, as Trichophyton rubrum can also produce similar features [23]. Molecular techniques such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and DNA sequencing are considered gold standards for distinguishing N. dimidiatum from dermatophytes [24]. However, high cost, limited availability in tertiary care centers, and occasionally suboptimal sensitivity and specificity hinder routine use. Therefore, we aimed to develop a rapid, specific, and cost-effective diagnostic tool for early detection of N. dimidiatum to guide treatment. A dot-immunobinding assay for the rapid detection of N. dimidiatum could offer a simple, interpretable, and widely applicable diagnostic alternative.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement and Biosafety

A BALB/cAJcl mouse was used for monoclonal antibody production. Animal husbandry and experimental procedures were approved by the Mahidol University-Institute Animal Care and Use Committee (MU-IACUC), Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University (project code: SI-ACUP 008/2565; approval number: 010/2565). Approval was also obtained from the Siriraj Safety Risk Management Taskforce, Mahidol University (approval number: SI 2022–039). Certified scientists and veterinarians performed all animal procedures according to the Ethical Principles and Guidelines for the Use of Animals issued by the National Research Council of Thailand.

Fungal Isolates and Culture Conditions

Neoscytalidium dimidiatum, isolated from a nail specimen of an onychomycosis patient and preserved in the Clinical Mycology Laboratory culture collection, Department of Dermatology, Siriraj Hospital, was used for antigen preparation. A single isolate was cultured on Sabouraud dextrose agar supplemented with chloramphenicol (50 µg/mL; Glentham Life Sciences Ltd., Corsham, UK) and gentamicin (22.5 µg/mL; T.P. Drug Laboratories (1969) Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand). Cultures were incubated at 27 °C for 14 days. The species was confirmed by sequencing the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions of fungal rDNA using ITS1 and ITS4 primers prior to further processing (accession number PV960087) [25]. Additional non-dermatophyte isolates—including Aspergillus niger; Candida, Cladosporium, Curvularia, and Penicillium spp.; and Fusarium solani—and the dermatophyte Trichophyton mentagrophytes were obtained from the same culture collection. These isolates are clinically relevant colonizers associated with N. dimidiatum. All isolates were grown on the same medium under identical conditions.

Extraction of N. dimidiatum Antigen

Antigen was extracted from an N. dimidiatum isolate using a modified protocol from Packeu et al., 2013 [26]. Briefly, 500 mg of fungal hyphae were harvested from Sabouraud dextrose agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol and gentamicin using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4) and an inoculation loop. After centrifugation at 13 000 × g for 15 min, the fungal pellet was washed with an ethanol solution (absolute ethanol: sterile deionized water, 3:1). Antigen was extracted by mechanically grinding the mold in a homogenizer chamber with 70% (v/v) formic acid (Univar, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Taren Point, Australia) at a 1:10 ratio (fungal mass: formic acid). This step was followed by absolute acetonitrile (QReC, QREC Chemical Company Ltd., Chonburi, Thailand) at a 1:10 ratio (fungal mass: acetonitrile; Fig. 1b). The homogenate was centrifuged at 13 000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C, and the crude antigen solution was decanted from the supernatant. Antigens were purified using a HiTrap Desalting column (Cytiva, Cytiva Sweden AB, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., California, USA). Crude antigens from other fungi were prepared using the same optimized procedure.

Characterization of Neoscytalidium dimidiatum and antigen preparation. a The isolate was classified by colony morphology on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA), potassium hydroxide (KOH) microscopy, and sequencing with phylogenetic analysis of internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions. b Antigen was prepared from N. dimidiatum and its protein profile analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

Production of a Monoclonal Antibody Specific to N. dimidiatum Antigen

A 6-week-old female BALB/cAJcl mouse (Mus musculus) received seven intraperitoneal injections at 2-week intervals of N. dimidiatum antigen mixed with Imject alum at a 3:1 ratio (antigen: alum). Imject alum was sourced from Thermo Scientific, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Rockford, USA (Fig. 2a). After confirming the antibody titer, the spleen was collected and splenocytes were harvested. Splenocytes were fused with P3X63Ag8.53 myeloma cells to generate hybridomas using the ClonaCell-HY Hybridoma Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). Hybridoma clone 3E6F7 was selected for high production yield and specific immunoreactivity to N. dimidiatum antigen by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The monoclonal antibody produced by clone 3E6F7 (MAb 3E6F7) was collected after hybridoma cultivation in RPMI Medium 1640 (Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, New York, USA). Cultures were supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Cytiva, Global Life Sciences Solutions Austria GmbH & Co KG, Pasching, Austria) and 10% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, New York, USA). An additional 10% Glutamax-I was included (Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, New York, USA). Cells were then cultivated in ISF-1 Hybridoma Medium (PAN-Biotech GmbH, Aidenbach, Germany) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. MAb 3E6F7 was purified by affinity chromatography using a HiTrap IgM Purification HP column (Cytiva, Cytiva Sweden AB, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoglobulin purity was validated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and the concentration of purified antibody was determined. MAb 3E6F7 immunoreactivity was confirmed by indirect ELISA and Western blotting. Cross-reactivity was evaluated against Aspergillus, Candida, Cladosporium, Curvularia, Fusarium, and Penicillium spp., and Trichophyton spp.

Production of monoclonal antibody 3E6F7. a MAb 3E6F7 was generated by fusing P3X63Ag8.53 myeloma cells with splenocytes from an immunized BALB/cAJcl mouse; a clone with high yield and specific reactivity to N. dimidiatum antigen was selected. b Antibody purity and chains were evaluated by SDS-PAGE

Grid-Based Optimization of Primary and Secondary Antibody Concentrations

We optimized MAb 3E6F7 (primary) and goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) (secondary) for Dot-Iba reactions. MAb 3E6F7 was spotted onto three nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (Pall Life Science, Pall Corporation, Florida, USA). Spots contained 3000, 1500, 750, 375, 187.5, 93.75, 46.875, or 23.4375 ng in 3 µL. After drying at room temperature for 10 min, membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS-T (0.05% Tween 20; pH 7.4) for 1 h at room temperature (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., Maharashtra, India; Affymetrix Inc., Ohio, USA). After washing, goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) in PBS-T was added at 1:3000, 1:1500, or 1:750 and incubated for 45 min at room temperature (SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, USA). Membranes were washed with PBS-T and activated with Tris-hydrochloride solution (0.1 M, pH 9.6). They were then developed with KPL BCIP/NBT phosphate substrate, and color was observed within 5 min (SeraCare Life Sciences Inc., Milford, USA). Crude mouse serum and PBS served as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Development of the Dot-Iba Test System

To develop a Dot-Iba for detecting N. dimidiatum, we used a vertical flow-through format to allow a rapid antigen–antibody reaction. A test pad (3 × 4 × 0.6 cm) with a test hole 10 mm in diameter was assembled according to Eamsobhana et al. [27] (Fig. 4a). Two pieces of Whatman CF6 filter paper (2 × 3 cm; GE Health Sciences, Global Life Sciences Solution Operation UK Ltd., Buckingham, UK) were placed on the base as absorbent pads. A 0.2-µm pore size nitrocellulose (NC) membrane (2 × 2 cm; Pall Life Science, Pall Corporation, Florida, USA) was laid on top, followed by a cover lid with the test hole. Purified N. dimidiatum antigen (0.9 µg in 6 µL PBS-T) was dotted onto the membrane at the test hole center and dried at room temperature for 30 min (Fig. 4b). Next, 100 µL of 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (Millipore Corporation, Illinois, USA) in PBS-T was added dropwise and incubated for 15 min at room temperature; this blocking step was repeated once. The membrane was washed three times with 200 µL PBS-T. Then, 375 ng of MAb 3E6F7 in 50 µL PBS-T was added dropwise, allowed to absorb, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. After washing, 50 µL of goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP), diluted 1:3000 in PBS-T, was added dropwise and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. After a final wash, 50 µL of Tris–HCl (0.15 M, pH 9.6) was added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, 50 µL of KPL BCIP/NBT phosphate substrate was added; the test pad was incubated in the dark, and the reaction was stopped by adding sterile distilled water. A magenta dot was observed after 5 min. Presence of a magenta dot indicated a positive result, and its absence indicated a negative result (Fig. 4c). For process controls, MAb 3E6F7 was blotted on the NC membrane and reacted with goat anti-mouse Ig-AP as the positive control; PBS-T served as the negative control. Each sample was tested in duplicate to confirm reproducibility.

Dot-immunobinding assay (Dot-Iba) device and workflow. a The vertical flow-through device comprises a pad base, absorbent pads, a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane, and a cover lid with a test hole. b The assembled device was dotted at the test-hole center with N. dimidiatum antigen, followed by sequential addition of MAb 3E6F7 (primary; antigen-capturing) and goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) (secondary; signal-generating). c Presence of a visible magenta dot indicated a positive result; absence indicated a negative result

Selection of a Suitable Extraction Buffer

To identify a suitable extraction buffer for fungal extraction from nail clipping samples, we evaluated PBS-T, 5% Triton X-100 in PBS-T (pH 7.4), and 5% CHAPS in PBS-T (pH 7.4). Triton X-100 was from Calbiochem (EMD Millipore, Massachusetts, USA), and CHAPS from Anatrace (Anatrace Products LLC, Ohio, USA). We spiked 900 ng of N. dimidiatum antigen into each buffer and applied it to the center of the test hole, then performed the Dot-Iba according to the protocol described above. Presence of a magenta dot indicated a positive result, and its absence indicated a negative result. Each sample was tested in duplicate to confirm reproducibility.

Evaluation of the Limit of Detection and Cross-reactivity

Dot-Iba was used to detect varying concentrations of N. dimidiatum antigen in vitro. PBS-T was spiked with 0, 0.45, 0.9, or 1.8 µg of antigen in a 6 µL final volume. Samples were applied to the center of the test hole, and Dot-Iba was performed as described above. The visually observable limit of detection (LOD) was defined as the lowest antigen concentration that produced a positive signal.

To evaluate specificity, antigens from common pathogenic fungi were tested at 0.9 µg in PBS-T. The panel included the dermatophyte Trichophyton mentagrophytes and the non-dermatophytes Aspergillus niger; Candida, Cladosporium, Curvularia, and Penicillium spp.; and Fusarium solani. Assays were performed as described above, with N. dimidiatum antigen and PBS-T serving as positive and negative controls, respectively. For a positive process control, MAb 3E6F7 was blotted on the NC membrane and reacted with goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP).

Results

Production and Characterization of a Highly Specific Monoclonal Antibody Against N. dimidiatum

To generate a highly specific monoclonal antibody against N. dimidiatum, we prepared antigen from a taxonomically confirmed N. dimidiatum isolate identified by colony morphology, microscopy, and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing (Fig. 1a). The antigen was used to immunize a BALB/cAJcl mouse, and hybridoma technology yielded MAb 3E6F7 (Figs. 1b and 2a). SDS-PAGE showed distinct light and heavy chains at approximately 25 and 70 kDa, respectively (Fig. 2b). Immunoglobulin isotyping with the Pierce Rapid ELISA Mouse Monoclonal Antibody Isotyping Kit (Invitrogen, Bender MedSystem GmbH, Vienna, Austria) identified MAb 3E6F7 as immunoglobulin M (IgM), kappa class (data not shown). Indirect ELISA confirmed specificity to N. dimidiatum antigen, with no cross-reactivity to the dermatophyte Trichophyton mentagrophytes or to other non-dermatophyte fungi—Aspergillus niger; Candida, Cladosporium, Curvularia, and Penicillium spp.; or Fusarium solani (Fig. 3a).

Antigens (1 µg/well) from common fungal pathogens were coated on 96-well plates, incubated with MAb 3E6F7 (1:5000 in 100 µL PBS-T; 37 °C, 1 h), and detected with goat anti-mouse Ig-HRP (1:5000) and a peroxidase substrate. Optical density was measured at 405 nm; the cut-off was 0.0725 (half the OD405nm for the N. dimidiatum well, OD405nm = 0.145). A no-antigen well served as the negative control. b Grid-based Dot-Iba optimization. MAb 3E6F7 (3000, 1500, 750, 375, 187.5, 93.75, 46.875, or 23.4375 ng in 3 µL) was spotted onto a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane and probed with goat anti-mouse Ig-AP at 1:3000, 1:1500, or 1:750. Color development used KPL BCIP/NBT substrate; signals were visible within 5 min

Optimization of MAb 3E6F7 and Secondary Antibody Concentrations

Monoclonal antibody 3E6F7 and goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) served as the primary (antigen-capturing) and secondary (signal-generating) antibodies, respectively, in the Dot-Iba technique. A grid-based optimization assay identified the primary-to-secondary antibody concentration combination that produced a visible signal. The optimal combination was 375 ng of primary antibody with a 1:3000 dilution of secondary antibody (Fig. 3b). This concentration combination was subsequently applied in the Dot-Iba detection system.

Performance Evaluation of the Dot-Iba Detection System

To evaluate the performance of the Dot-Iba system used in this study (Fig. 4a), N. dimidiatum antigen in PBS-T was applied to the test hole center. MAb 3E6F7 and goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) were then added sequentially as the antigen-capturing and signal-generating antibodies, respectively (Fig. 4b). The Dot-Iba system detected N. dimidiatum antigen, producing a visible magenta dot within 2 h (Fig. 4c). However, antigen spiked in 5% CHAPS produced only a faint signal, whereas 5% Triton X-100 produced no visible signal (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that PBS-T is the most suitable extraction buffer for preserving clinical specimens, such as skin scrapings, for the detection of N. dimidiatum antigen.

Detection used MAb 3E6F7 (375 ng) and goat anti-mouse Ig-AP (1:3000). After addition of KPL BCIP/NBT substrate and incubation in the dark, results were read at 5 min. Analytical Sensitivity and Specificity

The Dot-Iba system’s sensitivity was also evaluated in vitro using PBS-T containing varying concentrations of N. dimidiatum antigen. The visually observable limit of detection was 0.9 µg of antigen (or approximately 10⁶–10⁸ fungal cells) (Fig. 6a). Regarding specificity, the assay showed high specificity for N. dimidiatum antigen. No cross-reactivity was observed with other non-dermatophyte fungi—Aspergillus niger; Candida, Cladosporium, Curvularia, and Penicillium spp.; or Fusarium solani—or with the dermatophyte Trichophyton mentagrophytes (Fig. 6b).

Limit of detection and cross-reactivity of the Dot-Iba device. a The visually observable limit of detection was assessed in vitro using N. dimidiatum antigen at 0, 0.45, 0.9, and 1.8 µg in 6 µL PBS-T; the limit was defined as the lowest concentration yielding a detectable signal. b Cross-reactivity was tested with antigens from non-dermatophytes (Aspergillus, Candida, Cladosporium, Curvularia, Fusarium, Penicillium spp.) and a dermatophyte (Trichophyton spp.). N. dimidiatum antigen and PBS-T were the positive and negative controls, respectively; MAb 3E6F7 blotted on NC and reacted with goat anti-mouse Ig-AP served as a positive process control

Discussion

Dot-immunobinding assay (Dot-Iba) is a simple, rapid protein detection method analogous to Western blotting but without protein separation [28]. Because N. dimidiatum infections are often clinically indistinguishable from dermatophyte infections, early, accurate identification is essential for appropriate treatment [29]. We developed a Dot-Iba system to detect N. dimidiatum antigen using a novel monoclonal antibody, MAb 3E6F7, for capture and goat anti-mouse Ig conjugated with alkaline phosphatase (AP) for signal detection. Our results show that the Dot-Iba assay is rapid, specific, and cost-effective, addressing limitations of current N. dimidiatum detection methods.

Dot-Iba produced a visible result within 2 h. By contrast, a PCR-ELISA for five common dermatophytes from skin scrapings and nail samples yields results within 24 h [30], and standard PCR requires approximately 4–5 h for nail specimens [31]. These comparisons highlight the speed of the Dot-Iba system; however, immunochromatographic tests (ICTs) remain faster, with processing times under 30 min [32]. ICT development requires two monoclonal antibodies: one labeled with gold nanoparticles for antigen capture and another immobilized in the detection zone [33]. Accordingly, ICT is a promising next step if a second monoclonal antibody compatible with MAb 3E6F7 can be generated.

Our Dot-Iba system demonstrated high specificity, producing a visible signal only in the presence of N. dimidiatum antigen and negative results for other dermatophyte and non-dermatophyte fungi tested. The assay detected antigen concentrations as low as 0.9 µg in vitro. This finding parallels a previous study in which Dot-Iba showed 100% specificity for detecting antimycobacterial antibodies in cerebrospinal fluid samples, while sensitivity was 60% for antibodies and lower for antigens [34]. These observations are consistent with our findings, in which Dot-Iba sensitivity was limited to 0.9 µg of antigen. In comparison, Tsuboi et al. (2021) reported that a lateral flow immunochromatographic assay (LFIA) for dermatophyte detection demonstrated sensitivity and specificity of 84.8% and 83.9%, respectively, suggesting that LFIA served as a useful supplement rather than a replacement for direct KOH microscopy [35]. These findings highlight the importance of combining multiple diagnostic techniques for accurate dermatophyte detection. In addition, the antigen used in the present study was obtained from a single, non-reference N. dimidiatum isolate, which may limit the robustness and generalizability of the assay in clinical applications.

The Dot-Iba test is cost-effective because it requires no expensive manufacturing equipment, making production simpler and less costly than immunochromatographic test devices [36]. It is easy to use and can be operated by personnel without specialized training. Moreover, fungal culture is not required prior to testing with the Dot-Iba assay, thereby reducing costs related to equipment, labor, and turnaround time. Even when a culture-based method is required beforehand, the Dot-Iba assay minimizes the risk of fungal misidentification by non-skilled technicians due to its high specificity for N. dimidiatum. Although molecular approaches such as PCR effectively diagnose N. dimidiatum infections, they require costly equipment, trained personnel, and are relatively time-consuming [37]. Antigen detection helps identify active infection and estimate infectious burden [38]. Currently, no rapid diagnostic test detects N. dimidiatum antigen in clinical specimens, so Dot-Iba addresses a critical gap for early detection of skin and nail infections.

This study was limited to in vitro testing; clinical validation is essential to determine performance in real-world settings. Comparative evaluation against fungal culture and PCR will help establish diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility. In summary, Dot-Iba provides a rapid, easy-to-use, and promising alternative for N. dimidiatum antigen detection, particularly where access to fungal culture or molecular diagnostics is limited. It has the potential to improve diagnosis and management of N. dimidiatum-associated skin and nail infections.
