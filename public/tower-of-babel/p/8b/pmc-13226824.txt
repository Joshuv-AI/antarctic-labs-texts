Face-to-face or screen-to-screen: gynecologic oncology fellowship interview applicant perspectives on interview format
Authors: Christopher M. Mayer, Natasha McGlaun, Simone A. Sasse, Grace M. Pipes, Emily E. O’Brien, Pamela T. Soliman, Whitfield Growdon, Kenneth H. Kim, Margaret I. Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Applicants interviewed at nearly twice as many programs during the virtual interview era.•Applicants were more satisfied with in-person interviews, however, only 53% would prefer that format over the virtual interview format.•Financial impact and residency coverage were two main barriers impacting preference for interview format.

Introduction

In recent years, the landscape of medical education interviews for residency and fellowship has witnessed a significant transformation. Traditionally, in-person interviews were the standard for assessing applicants and programs. In-person interviews allow for face-to-face interactions that enable evaluation of skills, qualifications, and applicant or program attributes. (Downard, 2015) However, the COVID-19 pandemic led to the exclusive use of virtual interviews. (Day, 2020).

Despite an almost complete return to pre-pandemic operations and lifting of social restrictions, virtual interviewing has largely persisted in place of in-person interviewing. This is likely due to several benefits afforded by virtual interviewing. In-person fellowship interviews are financially burdensome to applicants due to cost of travel and often create significant staffing challenges for residencies, particularly when multiple residents are applying to fellowship. (Peebles, 2023, SL, W., et al., The Burden of the Fellowship Interview Process on General Surgery Residents and Programs - PubMed. J./ surgical education, 2017, Gm, 2017, Frishman, 2016) In 2017, a survey of gynecologic oncology fellowship applicants found that applicants spent, on average, $6,000 on the interview process, often relying on credit cards and personal loans to support the expense; additionally, they spent a mean of 15 days away from work, and 37% reported difficulty arranging their coverage. (Gm, 2017) The implementation of virtual interviews during the COVID-19 pandemic helped overcome geographical barriers, reduce costs, and enhance accessibility for both applicants and interviewers. (JJD, 2022, AP, 2021, Armstrong, 2022).

Despite these improvements, questions have been raised about the impact of the virtual interviewing format on evaluating both applicants and programs. Particularly among surgical specialties and subspecialities, where the training periods are long, intense, and include a smaller cohort of trainees, there are concerns of applicants ability to ascertain an accurate understanding of the program’s “culture” and “fit” of the program for an applicant. Studies across surgical fellowships have repeatedly found that applicants and faculty alike feel virtual interviewing hinders their ability to assess fit. Many report that virtual interviewing alone is not a suitable replacement for in-person interviews and some studies advocate for a hybrid interview format. (Peebles, 2023, JJD, 2022, AP, 2021, Armstrong, 2022, SCC, 01/06/2022., JMP, KM, LNA, The virtual interview format for fellowship recruitment in obstetrics and gynecology: a nationwide survey of program directors - PubMed. Medical education online, 2022, GT et al., 12/20/2023., NMC et al., 2019, KAR, 2021, MMG et al., 2021, RA et al., 2022).

Understanding the factors, such as interview format, that influence program and applicant ranking decisions serves to provides insight to programs about the elements that applicants consider most important when evaluating a program. It may shed light on whether the interview format (in-person or virtual) plays a key role in shaping these decisions.

Various interventions have been introduced to differentiate applicant interest in programs.(Tavarez, 2022 Dec) Program signaling, a formal mechanism first implemented in 2021 for medical residency programs, allowed applicants to indicate heightened interest in specific programs by allocating a limited number of signals.(Pletcher et al., 2021) In obstetrics and gynecology (OBGYN) residency, applicants are allotted 18 total signals (3 gold and 15 silver). (Gynecologists, 2023) Starting the 2026 application cycle, gynecologic oncology fellowship programs could opt into participating in program signaling with applicants given 12 total signals. This is in addition to fellowship applicants using optional geographic preferences in their applications.

Insights into applicant preferences for interview formats may guide programs when optimizing their selection processes. As some programs return to in-person interviewing while others maintain the virtual format, there is a unique opportunity to compare these interviewing formats. The primary objective of this study was to characterize the perspectives of OBGYN residents applying to gynecologic oncology fellowship on how interview format (in-person versus virtual) affects their experience during the fellowship interview process.

Material and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional survey study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Cedars-Sinai (IRB #00003046). Eligible participants were obtained by combining the list of gynecologic oncology fellowship applicants from three gynecologic oncology programs in the United States during the 2023 application cycle: Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, MD Anderson Cancer Center, and New York University. These three programs were selected to represent three different regions of the United States.

Data collection

A voluntary, anonymous survey was distributed to all applicants who applied to at least one of the three gynecologic oncology fellowship programs. The survey was available online through RedCap and was distributed in February 2024 after the conclusion of the match process for that cycle to the preferred email correspondence that was used during the application cycle. (PA, H., et al., 2009) The survey remained open for one month. Reminder emails were sent weekly for three consecutive weeks. Participants did not receive any compensation or incentive for completing the survey.

The survey consisted of 44 questions, including 8 demographic questions, 12 application process questions, 23 interview format perspective questions, and 9 interview-supporting and future interview preference questions. Of the 23 interview format perspective questions, five questions were comparative and conducted on a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). The survey is included as an Appendix.

Standard descriptive statistics summarize the data with categorical variables described by frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were comprised of application, acceptance, rejection, and interviewing practices and were described using median and interquartile range (IQR). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare Likert scores for applicants who completed both in-person and virtual interviews and are reported using median, U-value, and p-value. A p-value of < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Open-ended responses from participants were coded by two reviewers (C.M., N.G.). Themes were identified and compared for consistency, and illustrative quotes were selected.

Results

Demographics

Of the 89 applicants who participated in the 2023 gynecologic oncology match, 86 (96.6%) were contacted. Forty-seven (54.7%) individuals responded and are referred to as applicants throughout the remainder of the study. The majority of applicants were 31–35 years-old (66.0%, n = 31), white (66.0%, n = 31), female (72.3%, n = 34), affiliated with an academic institution (76.6%, n = 36), located in the Northeast (53.2%, n = 25), living in a 2-person household (53.2%, n = 25), reported a household income of greater than $100,000 (57.4%, n = 27), and did not have children (80.9%, n = 38). Application process

Of the 65 programs enrolled in the 2023 match, applicants applied to a median of 50 programs (IQR 42–60), accepted 24 interviews (IQR 20–32), declined 4 interviews after accepting (IQR 2–9), and attended 21 interviews (IQR 17–24). All applicants surveyed attended at least one virtual interview and most attended two or more interviews in a single day (76.6%, n = 36).

Of 39 applicants who were offered an in-person interview, 76.9% (N = 30) attended at least one in-person interview. 36.7% (N = 11) applicants who declined at least one in-person interview and these individuals cited the following reasons: distance (72.7%, n = 8), time conflict due to travel (72.7%, n = 8), cost (54.6%, n = 6), and other (18.2%, n = 2). Of 27 applicants who expressed interest in attending an in-person second-look opportunity, 10 (21.3%) attended at least one second-look visit. Of the 13 s-look experiences, applicants reported that most (69.2%) were organized by the fellowship program.

In-person interview travel

Of the 30 applicants who attended in-person interviews, the median furthest distance traveled was 1,100 miles (IQR 156–2,520). The primary mode of transport was by plane (70.0%, n = 21). There were 63.3% (n = 19) of applicants who canceled a separate interview to attend an in-person interview.

Interview experiences

The 30 applicants who experienced both in-person and virtual format interviews were asked to compare their experiences. The responses are summarized in graphical format in Fig. 1. Among this subset of respondents, applicants were more satisfied with the in-person interview experience (4.47 vs 3.85, p < 0.0001). Compared to the virtual format, applicants more strongly agreed with the statements that they were able to accurately interpret body language (4.53 vs 3.59; p < 0.0001), form personal connections with the interviewers (4.27 vs 3.85; p < 0.0001), and connect with other applicants (4.83 vs 2.46; p < 0.0001) during in-person interviews. Applicants more strongly agreed that the in-person format allowed for better understanding of the geography around the program (4.69 vs 2.65, U = 1258, p < 0.0001), culture (4.50 vs 3.39; U = 1148, p < 0.0001), and hospital facilities (4.63 vs 2.70; U = 1277, p < 0.0001) compared to the virtual format. There was no difference in applicants’ responses to interacting effectively with interviewers (4.26 vs 4.13; U = 819.5, p = 0.11, NS). At the conclusion of the survey, there was an opportunity for applicants to provide open-ended comments and perspectives from the interview season. Eighteen of forty-seven (38.3%) of those surveyed responded to this optional question. Several themes favoring in-person interviews included regulation of interview volume and better evaluation of program fit. Themes for applicants’ preference against in-person interviews were cost and missing resident clinical duties. Specifically with regard to cost, applicants noted that cost was a system-level barrier that could limit a cohort of applicants from interviewing fairly to gynecologic oncology fellowship. Signaling and geographic preferences

Of 47 total applicants from our survey, program signaling was viewed favorably by 36.2% (n = 17) of applicants, and 38.3% (n = 18) felt geographic preferencing would be a beneficial for future application cycles.

Discussion

Here we report fellowship applicants’ perspectives and comparisons of in-person and virtual interview format after the first year both formats were used for gynecologic oncology fellowship interviews. Excellent participation (response rate of 54.7%) highlights the importance of interview format to applicants. Those that attended both types of formats found that in-person interviews provided them with a better understanding of the program’s geography, culture, and hospital facilities. Additionally, these applicants were more satisfied with their interview experience and were better able to connect with other applicants and form personal connections with interviewers. However, 53% preferred in-person format.

Consistent with prior studies, the application volume and interview acceptance rates were high compared to pre-pandemic rates. (DJC, 2023 Dec) Applicants attended a median of 21 interviews, which is an approximately 90% increase from a mean of 11 interviews per applicant from cycles prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. (Gm, 2017) Importantly from 2006 to 2025, the number of gynecologic oncology fellowship programs increased from 36 to 73, with available positions increased from 43 to 88. In contrast, applicant numbers demonstrated temporal fluctuations with the highest peaks during COVID-19 era interviews (106 applicants in 2020); whereas the number of applicants had been relatively stable prior to the pandemic. (Supplemental Table 1) Fig. 2 shows these data compared to historical data from interview cycles between 1993–2016. Among applicants who attended in-person interviews, almost two-thirds had to decline another interview due to time conflicts. This suggests that transitioning to in-person format would reintroduce interview selection pressure for the applicant. This could decrease burnout from excess interviews and improve interview distribution more equally across the applicant cohort by limiting the number of individuals who attend a higher number of interviews. At this time, no optimal number of fellowship interviews has been established. This will continue to fluctuate depending on the volume of both the applicant pool and participating programs. A reflexive decrease in interview volume secondary to in-person format may also ultimately ease the cumulative time away from clinical responsibilities—a concern noted among the survey’s open-ended responses. The overall impact of in-person interviews is complex given that this format can lead to higher expenses and longer absences from clinical responsibilities, but with the added benefit of obtaining a more holistic view of potential fellowship programs.

Applicants who experienced both interview formats reported greater satisfaction with in-person format. This also provided a better ability to interpret body language, connect with other applicants, and appreciate hospital facilities and surrounding geography of the program. Beyond the structured interview, other studies have echoed the absence of hospital tours as a detriment to the virtual format. (KAR, SB, GSP, 2021) However, this increased satisfaction did not translate into a statistically significant preference for in-person interviews.

This paradox between interview format satisfaction and preference for future interview format may be explained by the burden of increased cost and time away from residency. Uniquely, the entire cohort from this study also experienced exclusively in-person interviews during their residency interview cycle, which allows them to more aptly compare the two formats compared to later application cycle. From the survey respondents, over half of the applicants lived in a household with 2 or more people and had cumulative annual gross salary of over $100,000. These individuals may be able to offset the financial hardship associated with traveling for in-person interviews. Our findings reflect other recent studies which cited financial barriers as a significant factor for OBGYN residency applicants. (Heisler, 2022, Mirzaei, et al., 2023).

The 2024 gynecologic oncology fellowship application cycle included an increase of in-person interviews. In line with concerns expressed by respondents in our survey, applicant-reported financial and time constraints should be addressed. The Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) has suggested that programs could offer a hybrid two-step option for applicants or financial support for applicants to attend in-person interviews. (AAMC. Interviews in GME: Where Do We Go From Here, 2024) The two-step process would include a preliminary virtual format followed by an in-person component, while ensuring that attendance to any optional in-person component visit does not impact programs’ ranking of applicants. While a virtual format relieves the financial burden, it does not eliminate time burdens as there was an increase in overall interviews attended by each applicant. Furthermore, financial support from individual programs, if not standardized by AAMC, could highlight financial capacity differences and propagate institutional inequality between programs.

To better distribute interviews, signaling and geographic preferences during the application process were recently incorporated into the OBGYN residency match process to communicate interest level to programs. These will be incorporated into the gynecologic oncology 2026 fellowship match process. Only one-third of respondents were in favor of including these tools into the gynecologic oncology fellowship process. This apprehension could be related to overall fewer gynecologic oncology fellowship positions compared to OBGYN residency programs.

There are several limitations to our study. Although the applicant list of three different programs was used for this study, this did not include all applicants who participated in the 2023 application cycle or all applicants who participated in the in-person format. The National Resident Matching Program (NRMP) released the number of applicants that participated in the match from this 2023 application cycle was 89 individuals, meaning almost all (96.6%) applicants were contacted to participate. (Program, 2023) While there was a moderately high response rate of 55%, there may be selection bias in which non-respondents either were more likely to be individuals who preferred in-person or vice versa and cannot be measured. In addition, the comparative questions were only asked to a subset of the applicants who attended both in-person and virtual interviews; however, those who attended only virtual interviews may be more likely to prefer virtual interviews overall.

In-person interviews provided a better interview experience and improved the holistic understanding of a program.(Hernandez and Tatem, 2024) It could also reduce the number of interviews that applicants attend, however, the incongruity with format preference indicates that limitations still persist. Future studies regarding the comparison of in-person and virtual interview formats could include program perspectives on the transition to in-person interviews and satisfaction with match outcomes. Additionally, as more programs transition to in-person, a future study could examine the resulting applicant-program fit based on program interview format. Importantly, applicant concerns regarding logistic and financial barriers toward in-person interviews remain an important concern to improve the equity of the interview process. Continued development and testing of strategies to optimize the number of interviews attended per applicant and to offset the financial and time costs of interviews are necessary.
