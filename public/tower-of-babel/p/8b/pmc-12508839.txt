Bioinformatics differential expression analysis of the effect of cannabidiol in chronic myeloid leukaemia cell line
Authors: Petar P Donchev, Svetla T Danova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) is the first clonal myeloproliferative disorder of pluripotent stem cells to be associated with a specific genetic abnormality, the Philadelphia chromosome, bearing the BCR-ABL1 fusion oncogene. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors are used as first-line treatment for the chronic phase of CML, although alternative treatments are necessary for resistant cases. Cannabidiol (CBD) is a major constituent of hemp oil that exerts a broad range of pharmacological effects in various malignancies. However, its molecular mechanisms in leukaemia remain unclear.In the present study, Imatinib-sensitive K-562S cells were subjected to CBD treatment (IC50: 17.69 μM) for 4 and 12 h, followed by RNA sequencing to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The subsequent transcriptomic profiling revealed 3518 DEGs at 12 h and 3433 DEGs at 4 h of treatment, including significant modulation of metallothionein-regulated oxidative stress responses (MT1, MT2, and SLC30A2) and p53-mediated apoptosis (TP53TG3, DDIT4, BBC3, CHAC1, NOXA1, and DAPK2). Additionally, the DEGs were enriched in alterations in immune signalling pathways—including type I interferon activation and PI3K-Akt-mTOR and Toll-like receptor signalling—crucial in leukaemia progression, as well as variations in lipid metabolism and mitochondrial homeostasis.The results presented in this study validate the considerable potential of CBD to induce broad transcriptional and signalling alterations, related to immune modulation, apoptosis, and metabolic processes in K-562S cells. These findings provide novel insights into the therapeutic potential of CBD and lay the groundwork for further investigation into its precision applications in haematological malignancies.Keywords: Myeloid leukaemia, Cannabidiol, Transcriptome, Metallothioneins

Introduction

Cannabis sativa (marijuana, hashish) has been used for centuries as an herbal remedy for the treatment of various ailments, as well as for its psychotropic properties [1], [2], [3]. More than 550 constituents have been identified in cannabis, of which greater than 100 are represented by the family of phytocannabinoids [4]. The most abundant amongst them are the psychoactive Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), which exhibit primary anti-cancer effects on various malignant diseases, including leukaemia [5], [6].

In general, cannabinoids can be categorised as endocannabinoids, synthetic cannabinoids, and phytocannabinoids [7]. Cannabinoids primarily interact with the endocannabinoid system through G-protein coupled cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2. The CB1 receptor is predominantly expressed in the central nervous system (CNS), wherein it regulates neurotransmission and is responsible for mediating the psychoactive effects of Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol [8]. Meanwhile, the CB2 receptor is mostly present on immune cells [9]—including T and B cells, macrophages and hematopoietic cells—where it modulates immune responses [10]. Studies have demonstrated that THC can influence various physiological processes in the immune and nervous systems by interacting with both these receptors [2], [9]. Conversely, CBD possesses low affinity for CB1 and CB2 receptors and does not exert any psychoactive properties [11], [12]. Related studies describe the diverse pharmacological actions of CBD, which include inti-inflammatory, anxiolytic, neuroprotective and attenuation of delayed-type hypersensitivity [13], [14]. Apart from the endocannabinoid system-associated receptors, CBD could also interact with non-canonical receptors, including the G protein-coupled receptors (GPR55, GPR3, GPR6, GPR12, GPR18) [15], [16] as well as the transient receptor potential vanilloid receptors (TRPVs) [15], [17], [18]. Moreover, CBD activates the nuclear receptor PPARγ (Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma) and is a partial agonist as well as allosteric modulator of the serotonin 5-HT1A [19] receptor.

CBD’s pharmacodynamics has been extensively characterised across various experimental models, demonstrating its hypnotic and sedative [20], along with its anticonvulsive [21] and anti-inflammatory properties [22]. A substantial body of evidence has also uncovered CBD’s anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic potential against various malignant neoplasms, which is mediated through mechanisms such as induction of cell death, autophagy, and cell cycle arrest [23]. Furthermore, CBD has been shown to modulate the tumour microenvironment by reducing tumour migration, neovascularisation, and cancer cell invasiveness [24].

In clinical settings, a daily oral intake of 50 mg CBD by young individuals has been linked to improved quality of sleep, along with increased cytotoxicity of natural killer (NK) cells against the leukemic cell line K-562 in situ [25]. CBD can also reportedly induce mitophagy in K-562 cells—an established model of chronic myeloid leukaemia—through TRPV2 cannel [26] activation. Furthermore, exposure of K-562 cells to CBD rich formulations is known to produce concentration-dependent pro-apoptotic effects. Interestingly, not only do CBD rich extracts inhibit cell viability and exert antiproliferative activity, but they can also increase cytoplasmic levels of cytochrome-c and decrease mitochondrial membrane potential [27]. Additionally, advanced research methods implementing targeted omics analysis of the interactome using Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS) combined with targeted-Limited Proteolysis-Mass Spectrometry have been used to explore CBD’s multiple receptor affinities. Both mass spectrometry and in silico analyses have demonstrated the binding of CBD to multiple targets, including the spliceosome-related oncogenic protein EFTUD2 [28]. Furthermore, a lipidomics-based approach uncovered altered levels of cardiolipins (CL), phosphatidylcholines (PC), sphingomyelins (SM), and triacylglycerols (TG) in K-562 cells following exposure to CBD, along with the upregulation of several triacylglycerols (TG) following the treatment [29].

Despite the accumulated research evidence in support of CBD and its ligands in the modulation of complex biological processes—including their promising therapeutic effects in cancer and leukaemia—a detailed understanding of CBD’s underlying molecular mechanisms remains elusive. Various methodological approaches have uncovered several significant cellular and metabolic changes induced by CBD. However, the gene expression alterations influenced by CBD—particularly in myeloid leukaemia models such as K562 cells—remain poorly understood. The present study aims to advance our knowledge in this area by exploring the transcriptomic landscape shaped by CBD in K-562 cells and providing insights into its potential therapeutic applications in haematological malignancies.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Cannabidiol (CBD; CAS No. 13956–29–1) was obtained from Cayman Chemical Company (Ann Arbor, MI, USA) as an analytical reference material. This phytocannabinoid (molecular weight 314.5 g/mol) was manufactured and certified under ISO/IEC 17025 and ISO 17034 standards.

Cell line, culture conditions, and viability

Imatinib-sensitive K-562S cells (an established cell line of chronic myeloid leukaemia) were purchased from Leibniz-Institut DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany. The K-562S cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (with phenol red, without HEPES, without sodium pyruvate, CAPRICORN™) supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated foetal calf serum (FCS)(non-USA origin, Sigma-Aldrich, F9665), L-glutamine (2 mM)(Sigma-Aldrich, G7513), 2 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate, and grown at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5 % CO2. The cells were maintained in their logarithmic growth phase and redistributed into fresh flasks prior to the treatment with CBD.

The cytotoxic effect of cannabidiol was evaluated by seeding K-562S cells at optimal cell density (3 ×105 cells/mL) in culture medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with FCS 10 % v/v) into a 96-well flat-bottom plate (100 μL/well). After cultivation for 24 h (37°C in humidified incubator containing 5 % CO2), the cells were treated with different concentrations of CBD (8 μM–44 μM) and incubated for 72 h. Cell viability was determined following the addition of 10 μL (3- (4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) −2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) dye (5 mg/mL in PBS) with further incubation for 4 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the formazan crystals formed were resuspended in 100 μL isopropanol with 5 % v/v formic acid and the absorption was measured at 570 nm using the INNO microplate spectrophotometer (LTEK Co., Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, South Korea).

RNA extraction and quality control

Three experimental conditions were created depending on the time of exposure of the K562-S cells to CBD: (i) treatment on the 4th hour of exposure to IC50 of CBD (n = 3); (ii) treatment on the 12th hour of exposure to IC50 of CBD (n = 3); and (iii) untreated control (n = 3). Thereafter, mRNA isolation from K562 cells was performed using the Aurum™ Total RNA Mini Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA, #7326820). Total RNA isolates were subjected to purification on silica membranes in spin-columns. Quality control of mRNA isolates was assessed by BGI Europe according to the following sample test methods: concentration determination (Agilent 4150), 28S/18S & 23S/16S tests (Agilent 4150), and overall RNA integrity was quantified as the RNA integrity number equivalent (RINᵉ)(Agilent 4150). Only samples with good integrity (RIN > 8) were used for sequencing.

RNA sequencing analysis

RNA sequencing was performed by BGI Europe using their proprietary DNBSEQ high-throughput sequencing that combines Probe-Anchor Synthesis (cPAS), linear isothermal Rolling-Circle Replication and DNA Nanoballs (DNB™) technologies, followed by high-resolution digital imaging. The workflow included sample preparation, library construction, sequencing (150 bp paired end [PE150]), and raw data output, each of which were followed by quality control steps. The reference genome implemented in this project was: NCBI_GCF_000001405.39_GRCh38.p13

Analysis pipeline and filter criteria

The sequencing data was filtered using the SOAPnuke workflow tool. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome via the HISAT2 algorithm, followed by the utilisation of Eriscript (0.5.5–5) and rMATS (v4.1.1) packages for detecting genes and differential splicing, respectively. Additionally, the Bowtie2 package was used for RNA identification. Gene expression levels were calculated using the RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximisation)(v1.2.28) package. Furthermore, differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed using DSEeq2. The DEGs heatmaps were generated with pheatmap. The most significantly DEG were selected by adjusting the fold change (FC) to 2-fold or above with p ≤ 0.05 and corrected p ≤ 0.05. Subsequently, a decreased threshold for the FC cut-off was used (1.5-fold change with p ≤ 0.05) to screen for a broader spectrum of DEGs involved in the mechanism of action of cannabidiol. To test the DEG enrichment for biological processes, a gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed via a hypergeometric test using phyper. The level of significantly enriched terms and pathways were corrected by Q (adjusted p) value with the threshold Q ≤ 0.05.

Statistical analysis

A paired Student’s t-test was used to calculate the p values, whereas the corrected p (Q) values were calculated by implementation of the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for false discovery rate (FDR). The p and corrected p (Q) values of DEGs were calculated using R software.

All output tables, diagrams, and plots have been processed in Dr. Tom, which is a web-based solution for the convenient analysis, visualisation, and interpretation of all types of RNA data, including mRNA, miRNA and lncRNA.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment (GSEA) analysis was performed on a pre-ranked list of genes using the log2 fold-change valued derived from the CBD treatment versus control as the ranking metric. Genes were ordered according to descending log2FC. Additionally, to ensure a strictly ranked gene list, identical log2 fold-change values were minimally perturbed using random noise (via jitter, set.seed = 42), which allowed each gene to occupy a unique position in the ranking. Assessment of enriched biological processes was conducted with the gseGO function in clusterProfiler (v4.6.1) using the org.Hs.eg.db annotation package (v3.17.0). The included parameters were: ont = "BP", keyType = "ENTREZID", minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 500, and pvalueCutoff = 0.05. Moreover, normalised enrichment scores (NES) were computed to identify significant up- or downregulated pathways.

Pathway statistics

PathVisio functionality was used to perform pathway over-representation analysis. The software’s algorithm measures the total number of elements (total) in a curated collection of WikiPathways human pathways, the number of genes present in the experimental data (n), and the number of genes in the same dataset that meet the cut-off criterion (r) (p < 0.05). Thereafter, a z-score for over-representation analysis was determined by computing the individual score for each pathway within the collection. A confidence interval of 95 % corresponds to z-score values above 1.96.

Results

The present study aimed to provide a comprehensive characterisation of the mechanisms of action of CBD in relation to the cellular and metabolic processes in an established model of chronic myeloid leukaemia, the K562-S cell line. The evaluation was focused on the changes in gene expression induced by CBD treatment under conditions of short-term (4 h) and prolonged (12 h) treatment.

Cell viability of imatinib-sensitive K-562S cells following CBD treatment

K-562 cells were exposed to different concentrations (8–44 μmol/L) of CBD for 72 h, followed by MTT cell growth assay for the evaluation of cell proliferation inhibition (Fig. 1).

Dose-response curve of K-562 cells treated with cannabidiol (CBD) for 72 h, as measured by the MTT assay. Note: Data represents mean values from three independent experiments; standard deviation (SD) bars were omitted for clarity; Concentrations ranged from 8–44 μmol/L, yielding an IC50 of 17.69 μmol/L.

Cannabidiol demonstrated antiproliferative effect over a wide range of concentrations in a dose-dependent manner. A maximum effect of 80 % and 90 % reduction in cell proliferation was achieved at CBD concentrations of 24 μmol/L and 27 μmol/L, respectively. Additionally, the average IC50 value of the three independent MTT assays was 17.69 μmol/L CBD.

Transcriptomics analysis of K-562S cells treated with CBD

To elucidate the transcriptomic changes that drive apoptosis in K-562S cells following CBD exposure, three groups of samples were prepared: first at the 4th hour (n = 3), second at the 12th hour (n = 3) of treatment with IC50 CBD (mean IC50 = 17.69 μmol/L), and a corresponding untreated control group (n = 3). Subsequently, three comparisons were established based on the sample groups: CBD_4h vs control, CBD_12h vs control, and CBD_4h vs CBD_12h. As a result of the treatment, mRNA-seq analysis revealed multiple DEGs. CBD significantly altered the expression of 1648 protein coding genes in the CBD_4h vs CBD_12h comparison, along with 3433 genes in the CBD_4h group (Fig. 2a) and 3518 genes in the CBD_12h group (Fig. 2b).

After applying a significance threshold of logFC ≥ 2, p ≤ 0.05, and corrected p ≤ 0.05, 15 genes (2 downregulated; 13 upregulated) were found to be significantly modulated at the 4th hour of CBD treatment. Meanwhile, at the 12th hour, 41 genes (7 downregulated; 34 upregulated) exhibited significant changes in expression post CBD exposure.

Differential expression in CBD_4h vs control (logFC ≥ 2.0)

The selected cut-off criteria applied to the 4th hour of treatment revealed nine genes from the metallothionein family, including MT1 members (MT1E, MT1G, MT1H, MT1F, MT1B, MT1X, MT1A, and MT1M) and an MT2 family representative (MT2A), all of which are essential to metal ion homeostasis and oxidative stress protection. Among the upregulated genes were also the functionally relevant to immune-modulation genes such as TP53TG3, TICAM2, and SLC30A2, along with CT47A10, which encodes a cancer/testis antigen implicated in chromatin organization and exhibiting tumor-associated immunogenicity. Conversely, the downregulated genes included SLC39A10 (ZIP10) and GAGE 13 (G Antigen 13) (Fig. 3a).

Differential expression in CBD_12h vs control (logFC ≥ 2.0)

Similar to the 4-hour time point, the most significantly upregulated DEGs at the 12th hour of treatment (Fig. 3a) were those associated with zinc and copper metabolism, including MT1 (MT1G, MT1H, MT1E, MT1F, MT1B, MT1A, and MT1X) and MT2 (MT2A) genes. Additionally, seven other genes involved in immune response (TICAM2, ULBP1, C5AR2, and CCR4) as well as inflammation (PRTN3, TMEFF1, and FCER2) exhibited significant changes. Stress response and apoptosis-related genes—including DDIT4, CHAC1, and AATK—were also overexpressed due to the CBD treatment. In contrast, the cell adhesion and migration mediator THBS1 (Thrombospondin-1) was downregulated. Notably, the significantly upregulated genes in this treatment group were related to Hedgehog signal transduction and growth factor signalling, such as SMO. Furthermore, the negative regulator of fat cell differentiation C1QL4, along with the VLDLR and CYP27A1 genes involved in lipid metabolism and transport, were significantly activated. Conversely, PLA2G4E (cytosolic phospholipase A2 ε) and FGF17 (fibroblast growth factor 17) were downregulated. The treatment with CBD also activated genes that play a central role in neuronal and developmental processes, including USH1G, KCNMB4, GPRASP2, SRRM3, and HAPLN2. Additional upregulated genes involved in neurogenesis and neuronal functions were CDHR1, REEP2, the chlorine-ion channel-related BEST1, FIGNL2, and KIRREL2. A few other less specific modulated DEGs were included the downregulated PLGLB2, RPRML, HSPE1-MOB4 readthrough, and KRBA2. In contrast, the sushi domain-containing 3 (SUSD3) and GOLGA8Q genes were upregulated.

Differential expression in CBD_4h vs DBC_12h (logFC ≥ 2.0)

Differential expression analysis comparing the 4th hour of treatment to the 12th hour of treatment revealed upregulation of the FAM47E-STBD1 readthrough gene and the zinc transporter ZIP10 (SLC39A10) gene, which is essential for cellular zinc homeostasis, at the later time point. In contrast, the CT47A10, MT1X, ACTA1, and MT1E genes—which are involved in metal ion homeostasis and cytoskeletal organization—were downregulated at the 12-hour time point. Additionally, the HSPA1A (Hsp70) gene—a molecular chaperone important for stress responses—was also downregulated post 12 h of exposure to CBD (Fig. 3b).

In order to provide broader identification of significantly expressed genes post CBD treatment, a fold-change threshold of ≥ 1.5 or ≤ -1.5, with p ≤ 0.05 and Q ≤ 0.1 was applied. As depicted in Fig. 4a, eleven DEGs displayed logFC values within ±1.5 to ±1.99 in the control vs CBD_4h comparison group, including growth and development regulators (NBL1 and HAPLN2), cell cycle and proliferation regulators (CCNG2 and MAP3K13 [part of the MAPK family pathway]), and actin cytoskeleton remodelling regulator ENAH. Additionally, the zinc transporter gene SLC30A1 (involved in stress responses), MCOLN3 (lysosomal Ca2+ release channel gene involved in autophagy), as well as the metabolic and drug processing regulator CYP26A1 were also upregulated. Notably, the drug resistance-related gene RDD1 (C1orf115) was activated alongside Phostensin-like (LOC107987457), whose function remains putative. Moreover, THBS1 (Thrombospondin 1)—an important mediator of cell adhesion—was negatively modulated in response to the treatment.

GO-P – gene ontology cellular processes

Summary of GO enrichment 4 h post CBD treatment

At the 4th-hour treatment time point (Fig. 5a), GO analysis identified significant enrichment in biological processes predominantly related to RNA metabolism and protein synthesis. These GO terms included ribosome biogenesis (GO:0042254) and rRNA processing (GO:0006364), revealing activation of pathways involved in ribosomal component assembly and maturation. Furthermore, processes such as mRNA processing (GO:0006397), mRNA splicing via the spliceosome (GO:0000398), RNA splicing (GO:0008380), and mRNA export from nucleus and mRNA transport (GO:0006406; GO:0051028) were also significantly enriched. These findings clearly demonstrate significant alterations in post-transcriptional gene regulation and mRNA metabolism in response to CBD treatment. Moreover, enrichment in processes related to cell cycle (GO:0007049) and cell division (GO:0051301) was observed, which further demonstrated the impact of CBD on cellular proliferation dynamics. Additionally, DNA maintenance mechanisms, including DNA repair (GO:0006281) and cellular response to DNA damage stimulus (GO:0006974), along with chromatin organisation (GO:0006325) were overrepresented, implying potential CBD-related effects on genome stability and epigenetic regulation processes.

Interestingly, several virus-related processes were overrepresented at the 4-hour time point, such as viral process (GO:0016032) and intracellular transport of virus (GO:0075733). This observation supports the hypothesis that CBD may modulate host antiviral responses or trigger viral mimicry mechanisms. Other significantly enriched terms included tRNA processing (GO:0008033) and export from nucleus (GO:0006409), regulation of translation (GO:0006417), and regulation of cellular response to heat (GO:1900034). Collectively, these results suggest a broad impact of CBD treatment on protein synthesis and cellular stress responses.

Summary of GO enrichment 12 h post CBD treatment

The GO analysis at the 12-hour time point (Fig. 5b) revealed continuous and significant enrichment of processes related to ribosome biogenesis (GO:0042254) and rRNA processing (GO:0006364), thereby reinforcing the sustained treatment-related impact on ribosomal assembly and function. Critical RNA-related processes—such as mRNA processing (GO:0006397), mRNA splicing via the spliceosome (GO:0000398), and mRNA export from nucleus (GO:0006406)—remained significantly influenced and indicated the ongoing modulation of RNA maturation and transport. Notably, DNA replication (GO:0006260) and DNA repair (GO:0006281) pathways demonstrated significant enrichment and reflected CBD’s influence on genomic maintenance and cell cycle progression at this later time point. The cellular response to DNA damage stimulus (GO:0006974) and regulation of cellular response to heat (GO:1900034) were also enriched, which indicated the activation of cellular stress response pathways. Additional enriched processes included tRNA processing (GO:0008033), ribosomal large subunit biogenesis (GO:0042273), and maturation of small and large ribosomal subunits (GO:0000462; GO:0000463), thereby demonstrating comprehensive treatment-induced effects on the protein synthesis machinery. The analysis also revealed enrichment in mRNA 3’-end processing (GO:0031124) and broader RNA processing (GO:0006396), which denoted the widespread modulation of RNA-related pathways. Moreover, the positive regulation of type I interferon production (GO:0032481) was significantly enriched, thereby suggesting that CBD may contribute to the regulation of antiviral immune responses or influence innate immune signalling.

Summary of GO analysis of differential gene expression at 4 h vs. 12 h post CBD exposure

GO analysis comparing gene expression between the groups post 4 h and 12 h of CBD treatment (Fig. 5c) revealed significant enrichment in several important biological processes. Genes involved in lipid metabolism and long-chain fatty acid metabolism enriched lipid metabolic process (GO:0006629), long-chain fatty acid metabolic process (GO:0001676), and regulation of lipid metabolic process GO terms (GO:0019216), thereby signifying CBD’s capacity to modulated cellular lipid handling over time. Furthermore, there was significant enrichment in genes associated with the response to unfolded proteins (GO:0006986) and protein folding (GO:0006457), which indicated the activation of cellular stress responses and protein control mechanisms. Another group of genes enriched the neutrophil degranulation GO term (GO:0043312), thereby demonstrating CBD’s effects on innate immune cell activation and release of granule contents from neutrophils. The adhesion of symbiont to host term (GO:0044406) was also significantly enriched and reflected the changes in cellular interactions associated with immune defence or microbial binding.

Next, to pinpoint enriched biological processes and stress-response signatures at pathway level a Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was carried out.

GSEA of 4-hour CBD treatment

At the 4th hour post treatment with CBD (Fig. 6a), the most significantly upregulated gene sets were associated with metal ion response and detoxification. These were associated with the cellular responses to copper (GO:0046688; NES = 2.32, p.adjust = 4.23 × 10⁻⁸), cadmium (GO:0046686; NES = 2.32, p.adjust = 4.23 × 10⁻⁸), and zinc ions (GO:0010043; NES = 2.31, p.adjust = 4.23 × 10⁻⁸). Additionally, very similar enrichment was demonstrated in cellular responses to cadmium ion (GO:0071276; NES = 2.30, p.adjust = 4.23 × 10⁻⁸) and intracellular zinc ion homeostasis (GO:0006882; NES = 2.26, p.adjust = 1.06 × 10⁻⁷). Other enriched pathways included detoxification of inorganic compounds (GO:0061687; NES = 2.17, p.adjust = 4.23 ×10−8), stress response to metal ions (GO:0097501; NES = 2.14, p.adjust = 1.69 ×10−7), and the broader biological descriptor of detoxification (GO:0098754; NES = 2.13, p.adjust = 3.21 ×10−7). Notably, the negative regulation of growth (GO:0045926; NES = 1.99, p.adjust = 1.05 × 10⁻⁶) was also activated at this early time point, although it remained positively enriched at 12 h (NES = 1.85). However, the lower score at the longer exposure period suggests a modest attenuation of this anti-proliferative signal over time.

GSEA of 12-h CBD treatment

At the 12-h of treatment with CBD (Fig. 6b), responses to metal ions and detoxification processes became intensified. Responses to cadmium ion (GO:0046686; NES = 2.51, p.adjust = 4.01 × 10⁻⁸) and intracellular zinc ion homeostasis (GO:0006882; NES = 2.47, p.adjust = 4.01 × 10⁻⁸) exhibited higher enrichment scores than the 4-hour treatment. Furthermore, the cellular responses to cadmium (GO:0071276; NES = 2.46, p.adjust = 4.23 ×10−8) and copper ions (GO:0046688; NES = 2.40, p.adjust = 4.23 ×10−8) remained strongly positive, along with the detoxification of inorganic compounds (GO:0061687; NES = 2.36, p.adjust = 4.23 ×10−8).

Conversely, negatively enriched terms extended beyond RNA modification to encompass multiple layers of RNA processing, methylation, and protein folding. In particular, enrichment in methylation (GO:0032259; NES = −1.49, adjusted p = 0.0437) and macromolecule methylation (GO:0043414; NES = −1.59, adjusted p = 0.028), protein folding (GO:0006457; NES = −1.53, adjusted p = 0.037), as well as the regulation of mRNA metabolic processes (GO:1903311; NES = −1.53, adjusted p = 0.010) emerged only after 12 h of CBD exposure. Additionally, spliceosome-related gene sets were strongly suppressed—including RNA splicing (GO:0008380; NES = 1.71, adjusted p = 4.91 ×10−7) and mRNA processing (GO:0006397; NES = −1.76, adjusted p = 5.61 ×10−8)—along with nuclear export (GO:0051168; NES = −1.79, adjusted p = 0.0017) and ribosomal small subunit biogenesis (GO:0042274; NES = −2.42, adjusted p = 4.23 ×10−8). At this later time point, additional regulatory programs—such as regulation and negative regulation of extrinsic apoptotic signalling (GO:2001240; NES = −2.02, adjusted p = 0.0155; GO:2001239; NES = −1.90, adjusted p = 0.035) and negative regulation of signal transduction under ligand-deprived conditions (GO:1901099; NES = −2.02, adjusted p = 0.0155)—were also suppressed. Overall, these patterns suggest that prolonged CBD exposure attenuates survival-associated pathways.

Differential pathway analysis

Similar to the DEG analysis, three different comparison groups were examined: control vs 4-hour treatment, control vs 12-hour treatment, and 4-hour vs 12-hour treatment. All altered pathways from each group were ranked based on their z-scores, with a cut-off criterion adjusted to a z-score of 1.96 or above, with five or more altered genes in a pathway and p < 0.05.

The differential pathway analysis uncovered significant alterations at each treatment time point (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3). For instance, in relation to metal ion homeostasis, both ‘Zinc homeostasis’ (4 h: r[11]; z-score [19.27], 12 h: r[9]; z-score [11.54]) and ‘Copper homeostasis’ (4 h: r[8]; z-score [11.29], 12 h: r[8]; z-score [8.16]) pathways demonstrated strong modulation. ‘Fatty acids lipoprotein transport in hepatocytes’ pathway was also significantly affected post 12 h of treatment (r[6]; z-score[6.73]). With regard to immune regulation and inflammatory processes, several important pathways were identified in the 12-hour of treatment—including ‘Allograft rejection’ (r[5]; z-score [3.87]), ‘Macrophage-stimulating protein (MSP) signalling’ (r[5]; z-score [2.53]), ‘Complement system’ (r[5]; z-score [3.06]), ‘Overview of proinflammatory and profibrotic mediator’ pathway (r[5]; z-sore [3.61]), and ‘Lung fibrosis’ (r[5]; z-score [4.7]). Additionally, significant activation in viral and disease-associated pathways was observed following 12 h of exposure to CBD—such as ‘Extracellular and follicular B cell activation by SARS-CoV-2’ (r[5]; z-score [4.01]), ‘Network map of SARS-CoV-2 signalling pathway’ (r[8]; z-score [2.75]), and ‘Measles virus infection’ (r[5]; z-score [2.1]). Lastly, the ‘IL-26 signalling pathways’ (r[5]; z-score [5.21]) was found to be significantly activated at the 12-hour time point. Although several pathways in this time point did not meet the stringent threshold level, they exhibited high z-scores justifying their consideration. Some of these at 12th hour of treatment reflected modulation in apoptosis and cell death related pathways, particularly ‘Apoptosis’ (r[4]; z-score [2.26]) and ‘Apoptosis modulation and signalling’ (r[4]; z-score [2.16]), in conjunction with metabolic and biotransformation pathways such as ‘Metapathway biotransformation Phase I and II’ (r[5]; z-score [1.95]) and ‘Oxysterols derived from cholesterol’ (r[4]; z-score [4.14]).

Discussion

Over the past two decades, extensive research has confirmed the potential of various cannabinoids as antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, and pro-autophagic agents in cancer cells [30]. Among them, cannabidiol demonstrates polypharmacological actions by interacting with several receptor groups, as well as by exerting non-receptor related pleiotropic effects [31], [32]. Previous studies from our research group have verified the anti-proliferative and anti-clonogenic effects of CBD in experimental models of multiple myeloma and chronic myelogenous leukaemia [33], [34]. A plethora of preclinical studies have also highlighted the potent, concentration-dependent anti-tumour effects of CBD in a wide variety of cancer models, although evidence from randomised clinical trials remains scarce [24]. Moreover, CBD dose regimens ranging from 60–400 mg per day have been associated with a low incidence of adverse effects [35]. Nevertheless, though CBD appears to be a promising therapeutic alternative for many neoplasms, the gene network and intricate regulatory processes underlying its mechanism of action remain poorly understood.

The present study demonstrated a concentration-dependent reduction in the viability of K562 cells treated with CBD. The consecutive mRNA transcriptomic analysis quantified the gene expression and revealed important pharmacogenomic responses in the treated cells, thereby providing in-depth insights into the complex physiological interactions evoked by the treatment [36]. Furthermore, differential expression analysis identified 3433 modulated genes at the 4th hour of treatment and 3518 modulated genes at the 12th hour of treatment.

Metallothioneins and metal ion homeostasis

Amongst all the significantly expressed genes, metallothioneins (MTs) exhibited the highest activation. These genes encode a family of low molecular weight (7 kDa), cysteine-rich proteins characterised by a large number of sulfhydryl groups, which grant them an exponential capacity for free radical scavenging, antioxidant potential, and oxidative stress protection [37]. Physiologically, MTs play a central role in copper and zinc ion homeostasis and provide protection against cadmium toxicity [38], [39]. Additionally, MTs are involved in tumour formation, resistance to therapy, and cancer progression [13]. The expression profile of MTs varies among different tumours, depending on the presence of external stimuli, gene mutations, as well as the type and progression of the malignant disease in general [40]. Moreover, MTs play а protective role by preventing DNA fragmentation [41] and apoptosis via regulation of cellular ion levels [42].

Studies in hematopoietic cells have demonstrated that MTs play an important role in cell proliferation [43], [44] and differentiation [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], along with the inhibition of programmed cell death [49]. Additionally, MTs have been associated with drug resistance in Acute lymphocytic leukemia [50] and Acute myeloid leukemia [51] and serve as a negative prognostic indicator in patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphomas [52]. Previous studies have highlighted CBD’s ability to modulate oxidative stress and metal ion transport via its interaction with PPARγ, a nuclear receptor involved in metabolic regulation [26]. The concurrent upregulation of metallothionein genes along with SLC30A2 (zinc antiporter) and ZIP10 (SLC39A10) in this study further supports the hypothesis that CBD treatment influences metal ion transport and homeostasis. Owing to the key role played by MTs in oxidative stress management and detoxification, their upregulation suggests a cytoprotective response induced by CBD, which may be potentially associated with its well-documented antioxidant properties [53].

Apoptotic and stress responses

Substantial changes in apoptotic and stress-related pathways were observed following CBD treatment. For instance, the significant alterations in the p53 transcriptional gene network were indicative of stress-induced apoptosis. The upregulation of TP53-inducible genes (TP53TG3, DDIT4, and BBC3 [PUMA]), along with oxidative stress markers (CHAC1, NOXA1, and DAPK2) supports oxidative stress-induced apoptosis—a mechanism that is widely reported in various cancer cell lines. Furthermore, the observed activation of the mitochondrial cell death effector HTRA3 [54] and apoptosis-associated tyrosine kinase (AATK) [55] is consistent with previous findings demonstrating CBD’s role in apoptosis modulation in cancer cells.

Viral mimicry and immune modulation

Significant enrichment of genes related to immune response—particularly those linked to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection, such as PDCD1, IFIT1, and TICAM2—along with the activation of the type I interferon pathway (GO:0032481), provides additional evidence that CBD may trigger antiviral responses resembling viral mimicry. This finding is consistent with previous research data indicating that CBD modulates NK cell function, consequently influencing immune surveillance and viral defence mechanisms [56]. The observed activation of Toll-like receptor (TLR) signalling and complement system pathways further supports CBD’s role in immune modulation, a property that has been explored in inflammatory diseases [57]. Several inflammation-related pathways—including IL-18 signalling and allograft rejection—further underline CBD’s potential role in immune modulation. Moreover, some studies suggest that CBD exerts both pro-inflammatory as well as anti-inflammatory effects, depending on dosage and context [58] which require further investigation.

Metabolic shifts and lipid regulation

Interestingly, exposure to CBD significantly altered the genes involved in lipid metabolism, particularly in pathways related to fatty acid transport and cholesterol homeostasis. The observed upregulation of VLDLR, CYP27A1, and SLC7A11 suggests that CBD may influence both lipid metabolism [59], [60] and oxidative stress response [61], [62]—findings that are consistent with previous studies on CBD’s metabolic effects. Additionally, the observed suppression of the mitochondrial uncoupling protein UCP3 may indicate a disruption in energy balance as well as decreased mitochondrial membrane potential as a consequence of CBD treatment.

Signalling pathway modulation

Another important finding was the influence of CBD on critical signalling pathways, including PI3K-Akt-mTOR, MAPK, and TLR signalling. These pathways play crucial roles in cell proliferation, adhesion, and immune interactions. Consequently, their modulation is an indication that CBD may exert anti-cancer [63], [64] and immunomodulatory effects [65], [66]. The observed activation of Wnt regulators (TPBGL and CDK14), the Wnt/Hedgehog/Notch pathway regulator MSI1, growth factor-related genes (FGF17 and SMO), as well as the tumour suppressor gene CCNG2 further supports CBD’s potential role in modulating oncogenic signalling.

Overall, GO analysis in the 4-hour and 12-hour treatment groups revealed several important biological processes that were activated by CBD exposure (Table 4). At both treatment intervals, strong enrichment in ribosome biogenesis (GO:0042254), rRNA processing (GO:0006364), and mRNA splicing via the spliceosome (GO:0000398) were observed. This demonstrated CBD’s ability to modulate transcriptional and translational machinery, thereby potentially altering protein synthesis dynamics. Notably, the latter corresponds with earlier research on targeted omics in K-562 cells, which identified EFTUD2 as one of the primary CBD targets. EFTUD2 is a key component of the spliceosome complex and has been found to be responsible for pre-mRNA splicing and RNA maturation processes. Thus, the enrichment of GO terms related to mRNA processing (GO:0006397) and RNA transport (GO:0006406) observed in this study may indicate CBD’s potential interference with spliceosomal function, which can ultimately lead to disruptions in oncogenic RNA processing.

Additionally, the enrichment of general cell cycle processes observed at the 4-hour time point, followed by the prominent activation of DNA replication and repair pathways at 12 h, could be the manifestation of cell cycle progression and enhanced genomic maintenance activity. It was also found that GO terms related to viral processes were enriched at both time points, along with a significant increase in immune signalling (type I interferon production) at 12 h, as an indication of an evolving antiviral or immune modulatory response. Notably, stress response terms—such as cellular response to heat and other stress signals—remained active, thereby reflecting an ongoing cellular adaptation to CBD exposure.

Furthermore, gene enrichment analysis comparing DEGs at the 4th hour and 12th hour of treatment revealed that CBD treatment led to a shift toward the modulation of lipid metabolism, protein folding, stress responses, and innate immune processes—particularly those involved in neutrophil function and degranulation [58], [67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73]. These findings align with recent studies showing CBD potential to alter neutrophil activity, reduce degranulation, as well as modulate oxidative and inflammatory responses in innate immune cells.

The findings from GSEA analysis support a model in which CBD initiates a two-phase response, starting with an acute activation of metal detoxification and growth inhibition, followed by the suppression of gene expression and survival networks. Such pharmacodynamics align with the CBD’s reported neuroprotective and anti-tumour effects, whereby induction of oxidative stress and interference in protein synthesis machinery can trigger cell death in malignant cells, while allowing healthy cells to recover. Notably, the observed suppression of the extrinsic apoptotic signalling regulators and signal transduction under ligand-deprived conditions at the longer exposure interval indicates that prolonged treatment with CBD may impair survival pathways. By lowering the anti-apoptotic defences, cells become more susceptible to additional stressors or co-treatments, consequently offering potential CBD-based combination strategies in oncology.

The results from differential pathway analyses further supported CBD’s multifaceted effects on cellular homeostasis. Among the most significantly altered pathways were zinc homeostasis (CDB_4h [z-score=19.27], CBD_12h[z-score=11.54]), copper homeostasis (CBD_4h [z-score=11.29], CBD_12h [z-score=8.16]), fatty acids and lipoproteins transport in hepatocytes (CBD_4h [z-score=6.03], CBD_12h [z-score=6.73]), complement system (CBD_12h [z-score=3.06]), p53 transcriptional gene network (CBD_12h [z-score=3.00]), apoptosis modulation and signalling (CBD_12h [z-score=2.16]), as well as extracellular and follicular B cell activation by SARS-CoV-2 (CBD_12h [z-score=4.01]). These activated pathways suggest CBD’s involvement in tumour suppressor networks, immune signalling, and metabolic regulation, thereby reinforcing its potential as an anticancer and immunomodulatory agent. Furthermore, the observed activation of TLR signalling (CBD_12h [z-score-2.16]) and PI3K-Akt-mTOR (CBD_4h [z-score=1.53]; CBD_12h [z-score=1.18]) pathways provides additional evidence for CBD’s modulatory effects on immune homeostasis and oncogenic signalling.

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence for the diverse effects of CBD on K-562S cells, namely by demonstrating its influence on metallothionein-regulated stress responses, apoptosis, immune pathways, lipid metabolism, and transcriptional regulation. Moreover, the observed enrichment of immune and antiviral signalling pathways emphasises broad therapeutic potential for future studies through single-cell analysis, epigenetic profiling, and functional experiments.
