Evaluation of a conversation aid for patients with thyroid nodules considering a biopsy: pilot multicenter randomized control trial

Introduction

Thyroid nodules are very common and often discovered incidentally [1–4]. Most are asymptomatic and ~ 50% appear low risk for thyroid cancer on radiological exams [1–3, 5, 6]. However, up to 10% of all thyroid nodules are found to be malignant [7]. As the detection of these nodules continues to rise, how to best care for patients with thyroid nodules remains crucial since a missed clinically relevant thyroid cancer diagnosis can significantly impact a patient’s well-being but overdiagnosis of low-risk thyroid cancer leads to patient harm and increased healthcare costs [8–12].

During clinical encounters, patients with thyroid nodules and their clinicians should collaborate to develop a personalized care plan that best addresses each patient’s unique clinical situation. This care approach is called shared decision making (SDM) [3, 13–15]. This process involves considering individual factors influencing thyroid cancer risk, evaluating compressive symptoms, interpreting laboratory and thyroid ultrasound findings, weighing the risks and benefits of diagnostic options, and aligning care decisions with patient expectations and preferences [3, 13–16]. Clinical decision-making that does not systematically consider clinical factors along with patient values and preferences may lead to suboptimal selection of patients for thyroid biopsy, negatively impacting clinical outcomes [17].

Post-visit surveys and evaluations of clinical encounters, suggest that effectively collaborating with patients with thyroid nodules to determine the best diagnostic approach can be challenging in practice, highlighting opportunities for improvement in these conversations [3, 13–15, 18]. For example, many patients remain unaware of their thyroid cancer risk after a consultation for thyroid nodule evaluation, and the risks and benefits of thyroid biopsy and ultrasound surveillance are not always thoroughly discussed [3, 13–15, 18].

To address this gap, our team developed a web-based Thyroid Nodule Conversation Aid (TNOC) to support the care of patients with thyroid nodules [13–15]. TNOC is a point-of-care tool designed to facilitate diagnostic conversations and care planning by supporting SDM activities: evaluating thyroid cancer risk, weighing the risks and benefits of diagnostic options such as biopsy, and incorporating patient values and preferences into decision-making [13–15]. Preliminary studies, including a quasi-experimental study and mixed-methods evaluations of patient and clinician experiences with TNOC, suggest its potential to enhance the quality of decision-making for patients with thyroid nodules [13–15]. In this pilot multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT), we assess the feasibility of implementing study procedures to evaluate TNOC’s effectiveness in improving the quality of care for patients with thyroid nodules.

Methods

Design, setting, and participants

This encounter-level multicenter pilot RCT compared standard care for patients with thyroid nodules to standard care enhanced by the use of TNOC during clinical visits. The primary aim was to assess the feasibility of implementing study procedures and to explore the preliminary effects of these approaches on SDM and clinical outcomes. The institutional review boards at the University of Florida and Mayo Clinic-Rochester approved the study procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The reporting adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for RCT [19].

Participant recruitment was completed between 2022 and 2024 at the University of Florida Endocrinology and Head and Neck clinics, as well as the Mayo Clinic Endocrinology clinics. All clinicians at the participating sites who routinely engaged in discussions with patients about thyroid nodules were eligible to participate. Eligible patients included English-speaking adults (aged ≥ 18 years) presenting for evaluation of a thyroid nodule. Patients were excluded if they had hyperthyroidism, were pregnant, had already undergone a biopsy of the nodule of interest, or had received counseling for thyroid nodule management within the year before their visit.

Randomization and intervention

Encounters were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either standard care or standard care enhanced by TNOC, a point-of-care tool designed to support diagnostic conversations for patients with thyroid nodules [15]. A randomization algorithm, generated within the Remote Data Capture (RedCap) software system, was used to maintain allocation concealment [20]. Study coordinators reviewed the upcoming patient calendar to identify potentially eligible participants. After explaining the study to patients and obtaining their agreement to participate, they were randomized.

In the standard care arm, clinical encounters were conducted as per the clinicians’ usual approach. No formal communication guidelines or structured protocols were in place for these encounters, and consultation duration was not standardized across centers. In the intervention arm, clinicians used TNOC during their encounters. Clinicians received a brief training session—approximately 15 min in duration—focused on familiarizing them with the TNOC web interface and how to effectively incorporate the tool into clinical visits. TNOC presents the patient’s risk of a thyroid nodule being cancerous using a risk bar, a risk pictograph with natural frequency expressions (e.g., “out of 100 people like you”), and 100-person pictographs illustrating the proportion of people with and without thyroid cancer. This risk is calculated according to the American Thyroid Association (ATA) or American College of Radiology Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (ACR-TIRADS) criteria and can be further personalized by the clinician [21, 22]. The tool presents options for managing the nodule, including monitoring or testing it, with brief explanations of each option, possible harms, and next steps. There is a space for clinicians and patients to ask questions and choose the treatment that best fits the patient’s needs. Once a decision is made, a printable summary is available, detailing the discussion from the encounter and frequently asked questions [13–15]. (Appendix)

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to assess the feasibility of implementing study procedures, which was evaluated through clinical records and participant logs. To measure feasibility, we examined the number of eligible patients, the percentage of patients who refused participation, the percentage of participants who completed surveys, and clinical visits that were successfully recorded.

Secondary outcomes included the preliminary impact of TNOC on SDM and clinical outcomes. These were evaluated through clinical record reviews, participant surveys, and clinical visit recording reviews. Clinicians completed a baseline survey at enrollment, and both patients and clinicians completed surveys immediately following the clinical encounter. Baseline characteristics—including patient sociodemographic information (such as health literacy and subjective numeracy), clinical status, and nodule features—were obtained from patient surveys and clinical records [23–26].

To provide preliminary evidence of TNOC’s effectiveness, we focused on evaluating SDM as a multidimensional outcome. This included assessing knowledge transfer, collaboration in care plan development, and satisfaction with the decision-making process [27, 28].

SDM - Participant reported outcomes

To assess knowledge transfer related to thyroid nodules, patients completed a 12-item questionnaire with Yes/No and “I don’t know” response options [15]. Decision-making was evaluated using the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS), a 16-item measure transformed into a 1-100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater decisional conflict [29]. Satisfaction with the care approach was assessed using a 1–7 Likert scale for patients, measuring their willingness to recommend the approach to others. Clinician satisfaction with the care approach was assessed using a 1–5 Likert scale, while their likelihood of recommending the approach was measured on a 1–8 Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction and likelihood of recommendation [15, 27, 28].

SDM - Observed encounter outcomes

Encounters were evaluated by two reviewers, who, due to the nature of the intervention, were not blinded to trial group assignment. A coding guide was developed and implemented. Reviewers initially met weekly to familiarize themselves with the guide and clarify the evaluation procedure [15]. They then independently assessed each clinical visit recording, entering responses using a piloted form in REDCap [20]. Final data entries were determined by consensus through weekly meetings, where reviewers discussed their independent evaluations of 3–5 clinical visit recordings per session. One reviewer reviewed all encounters across sites, and periodic team discussions were held to ensure calibration and consistency in scoring across sites.

Reviewers evaluated evidence of a discussion about choice awareness, defined as an explicit acknowledgment that multiple diagnostic options are available to address the patient’s situation [30]. They also reviewed evidence of a thyroid biopsy and surveillance as diagnostic options. Additionally, they assessed whether the main factors guiding the diagnostic decision were clearly identified (e.g., biomedical considerations or patient preferences), whether the patient delegated the final decision to the physician, and whether the patient voiced a clear preference for the desired diagnostic plan.

To evaluate the extent to which clinicians involved patients in the decision-making process, we used the OPTION score, which consists of 12 items assessing on a 4-point scale the effort clinicians make to involve patients in the decision-making process. The total score (max 48) is transformed into a 0-100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater effort to involve patients [31].

We also evaluated the extent to which components of TNOC were used during clinical visits (e.g., discussion of the clinical situation, potential diagnostic options, patient preferences). This evaluation served as a measure of fidelity in the intervention group and as an assessment of contamination—i.e., the extent to which components of TNOC were used in the control group. Additionally, we assessed how often TNOC was used in usual care encounters, providing evidence of contamination [27, 28].

We also evaluated the extent to which diagnostic reconciliation was evident, which involves clarifying the diagnostic situation and plan of care [32]. This included, when applicable (e.g., when a thyroid biopsy or ultrasound surveillance was recommended), specifying where and when the diagnostic test would be completed and how results would be communicated [32].

Clinical outcomes

To evaluate the effect of TNOC on clinical outcomes we focused on the decision to perform a thyroid biopsy as the next diagnostic step. Furthermore, we evaluate the appropriateness of thyroid biopsy according to the ACR-TIRADS recommendations using only ultrasound criteria [22].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation; SD) or median (interquartile range; IQR) as appropriate and categorical variables as % (n). Group comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All analyses were conducted following the intention-to-treat principle. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3).

Results

Participant characteristics and feasibility

During the study period, we screened 531 patients scheduled for a thyroid nodule evaluation. Of these, 213 (40.1%) were eligible, and 120 (56.3%) presented to the clinic on the day of their visit and were invited to participate. Of those, 90 (75%) agreed to participate and were randomized. We enrolled ~ 5 patients/month at the University of Florida and ~ 2 patients/month at Mayo Clinic, Rochester.

Among the 90 patients who were randomized, 4 were later deemed ineligible due to factors such as previous biopsies not documented in the medical record. Ultimately, 43 participants were included in each of the study arms. Post-visit surveys were completed by 97.7% of patient participants, and clinical visit recordings were completed for 98.8% of encounters.

We invited 18 clinicians to participate, 17 of whom accepted. All clinicians completed the baseline survey, and 85 (98.8%) completed the post-visit surveys. No patient or clinician participants withdrew from the study. (Fig. 1)

Secondary outcomes: SDM - Observed encounter outcomes

The frequency of diagnostic choice awareness was higher in the TNOC group compared to the usual care group (90% vs. 65%, p = 0.008). The OPTION score was also higher in the TNOC group (35 vs. 27, p < 0.001).There was no clinically or statistically significant difference in visit duration between groups (22 min in both TNOC and usual care, p = 0.9). Although patient expression of a diagnostic preference was more common in the TNOC group (76% vs. 56%), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.067). Similarly, delegation of the decision to the clinician was more frequent in the TNOC group (12% vs. 7%), but this difference was also not statistically significant (p = 0.483). In the usual care group, clinicians did not use TNOC in any encounters. The fidelity of TNOC use in the intervention group was 79%. (Table 2) The fidelity assessment reflected the occurrence of discussions on key communication topics during clinical encounters. Overall, most encounters included discussions about management options, next steps, and thyroid cancer risk. Notably, the TNOC arm showed a higher frequency of discussions about prognosis, diagnostic uncertainty, and provision of a printable summary (Supplemental Table 1).

Secondary outcome: clinical outcomes

At the encounter (patient) level, a decision to proceed with biopsy was made in 40% of patients in the TNOC group compared to 51% in the usual care group (p = 0.386). Among the patients who underwent biopsy, there was no statistically significant difference in adherence to ACR-TIRADS biopsy recommendations between the groups. (Table 2)

Discussion

In this multi-center pilot RCT, we demonstrated the feasibility of implementing study procedures to assess the efficacy of a conversation aid designed to support decision-making for patients with thyroid nodules. Our team successfully identified and enrolled participants and completed data collection from multiple sources, including clinical medical records, participant surveys, and clinical visit recordings. Preliminary efficacy evaluation supports the scientific premise for a larger, adequately powered study. While TNOC demonstrated a positive impact on participant-reported outcomes (e.g., higher knowledge, lower decision conflict), observed encounter outcomes (e.g., OPTION score, diagnostic choice awareness), and clinical outcomes (e.g., lower utilization of thyroid biopsy) for most measures of interest these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size of this pilot study and magnitude of expected impact.

This pilot RCT has several strengths, including participant enrollment at two geographically distinct academic sites. This design allowed for the evaluation of barriers to study implementation across sites and practice settings. Additionally, data were collected from diverse sources, enabling triangulation of research findings to strengthen inferences and understanding potential barriers to survey study instruments and recording of clinical visits. However, the study also has limitations that can inform the design of a future adequately powered trial. Most participants were White women, highlighting the need to ensure broader demographic representation. We did not assess patients’ initial management preferences prior to their clinical encounter. Future studies incorporating pre-visit preference assessments may offer a more direct evaluation of how conversation aids influence changes in patient diagnostic choices during the clinical consultation [33]. Additionally, randomization was conducted at the encounter level, which may not fully account for potential contamination, as clinicians in the control group were exposed to the intervention. Randomizing at the clinician level in future studies may help mitigate this issue, although, in none of the encounters randomized to usual care was TNOC utilized. Moreover, usual care was provided by experienced endocrinologists and head and neck surgeons at academic institutions without the use of standardized communication protocols. Although this reflects real-world practice, variability in clinician style and encounter dynamics may have contributed to differences observed between groups in this pilot study.

Inappropriate selection of patients with thyroid nodules is a significant clinical issue, given the high prevalence and frequent detection of these nodules in the population [2, 12, 17, 34]. Inaccurate selection of patients for thyroid biopsy can lead to the overdiagnosis of thyroid cancer. Due to the substantial harm associated with overdiagnosis, there is a clear clinical need to reduce its frequency. Clinical guidelines reflect this concern by supporting more stringent criteria to identify patients who truly need a biopsy based on cancer risk and nodule size [21, 22]. However, patients and clinicians find it challenging to implement less aggressive management once a thyroid cancer diagnosis is made [35–37]. These findings underscore the importance of intervening early in the diagnostic process and developing interventions that can improve the selection of patients for thyroid biopsy.

Conversation aids like TNOC can support patient-clinical conversations [38]. A systematic review and meta-analysis, which included 23 randomized controlled trials and 30 non-randomized studies, found that the use of conversation aids improved patients’ knowledge, observational assessments of SDM, and satisfaction with the decision-making process while reducing decisional conflict—all without increasing visit duration [38]. Our findings align with previous research evaluating conversations aids, reinforcing the need for well-powered studies to evaluate the impact of TNOC on clinically relevant outcomes, including SDM and thyroid biopsy utilization and appropriateness. These results are also consistent with our prior quasi-experimental study and mixed-method evaluation, in which patients and clinicians reported positive experiences with TNOC [13–15, 39]. In addition, our findings contribute to the growing interest in enhancing communication between patients and clinicians in the management of thyroid disease. For example, recent work conducted in a specialized thyroid unit in Italy described the development and pilot testing of a structured communication approach for counseling patients with indeterminate thyroid nodule cytology. This approach demonstrated potential benefits in making care more patient-centered and in addressing the psychological impact of decision-making amid clinical uncertainty and trade-offs [40].

Notably, visit duration remained unchanged between groups at approximately 20 min—a reasonable timeframe for new thyroid nodule consultations. This finding, consistent with our prior work supporting SDM, is particularly relevant for future large-scale implementation, as it addresses a common clinician concern regarding the feasibility of incorporating SDM-support tools into routine practice [38, 41].

We also evaluated the process of diagnostic reconciliation, a novel concept that aims to ensure alignment between the patient’s understanding of their diagnostic status, the clinician’s assessment, and the agreed-upon next steps in care [32]. The introduction of TNOC appeared to influence discussions about logistics and next steps, which are critical factors for patients in ensuring the completion of diagnostic testing (e.g., where and when will the thyroid biopsy or ultrasound will be completed and how will the results be communicated).

We observed lower utilization of thyroid biopsy in patients in the TNOC group compared to the usual care group, consistent with our previous quasi-experimental study [15]. However, due to small sample sizes, we were unable to adequately assess the appropriateness of thyroid biopsy utilization. Our assessment of biopsy appropriateness was based on ACR-TIRADS recommendations, which rely solely on ultrasound findings [5, 22]. Future studies should adopt a more comprehensive approach by incorporating additional patient factors, such as comorbidity burden, life expectancy, and patient and preferences, as well as longitudinal follow-up to evaluate long-term outcomes and well-being [17, 34]. This framework would allow for a more accurate evaluation of the appropriateness of thyroid biopsy [17, 34]. For example, a thyroid biopsy may be indicated based on thyroid cancer risk and nodule size. However, in a patient with limited life expectancy and a high comorbidity burden, it may not be the most appropriate diagnostic step. Conversely, in a patient where a thyroid biopsy might not typically be indicated based on cancer risk and nodule size, it may still be appropriate if the clinical context or comorbidities suggest a need—such as in cases of primary hyperparathyroidism with a surgical plan or evaluation for a solid organ transplant. This highlights the importance of individualizing the care plan. Conversation aids like TNOC can help facilitate these discussions and guide SDM for patients with thyroid nodules.

Conclusions

This pilot multicenter RCT demonstrated the feasibility of implementing study procedures to evaluate the efficacy of a conversation aid in supporting the care of patients with thyroid nodules, particularly in outcomes related to SDM and thyroid biopsy utilization. Preliminary effect estimates provide a strong scientific premise for conducting a larger multi-site RCT to assess the impact of the conversation aid on improving patient selection for thyroid biopsy.
