Quantitative phenolic profiling and protective effects of grape seed extract on mancozeb-induced cellular and genetic toxicity
Authors: Kübra Uslu, Kültiğin Çavuşoğlu, Emine Yalçın
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study the comprehensive toxic effects of the fungicide mancozeb on Allium cepa L., a widely used non-target model organism, and simultaneously explored the protective potential of grape seed extract (GSE) were studied. A broad set of biological responses was assessed, encompassing physiological parameters (germination rate, root elongation, biomass accumulation), cytogenetic markers [mitotic index (MI), Micronucleus (MN) frequency, chromosomal aberrations(CAs)], biochemical indicators [malondialdehyde (MDA), proline, superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and chlorophyll levels], as well as anatomical changes in root meristematic tissues. Additionally, Comet assay was applied to detect DNA fragmentation. The experimental design comprised six treatment groups: a negative control irrigated with tap water, two groups administered GSE at concentrations of 465 mg/L and 930 mg/L, a group exposed solely to mancozeb (150 mg/L), and two groups co-treated with mancozeb and each GSE concentration. After the exposure period, root and leaf samples were harvested for comprehensive analyses. The control group exhibited optimal physiological growth and minimal cytogenetic and biochemical stress indicators. Mancozeb treatment, in contrast, significantly elevated the frequency of MN and CAs, increased lipid peroxidation and proline accumulation, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD and CAT), alongside DNA damage and marked anatomical disruptions in meristematic cells. However, the co-application of GSE mitigated these adverse effects across all measured endpoints, with the higher GSE concentration demonstrating superior efficacy. Cell proliferation, which mancozeb application reduced by 36.3% compared to the control, improved by 17.4% in the mancozeb + 930 mg/L GSE applied group. The protective effects of GSE were attributed to its rich phenolic composition, including compounds such as quercetin, resveratrol, and gallic acid, which likely contributed to its antioxidant and cytoprotective properties. In conclusion, mancozeb induced pronounced toxicity in A. cepa at the cellular, biochemical, and anatomical levels, while GSE supplementation effectively ameliorated these toxic impacts. These findings highlight the dual necessity of regulating pesticide application to prevent non-target organism harm and considering natural phytochemical agents like GSE as potential bioprotectants against agrochemical-induced stress.Keywords: Alliumcepa, Comet assay, Meristematic cell damage, Genotoxicity, Lipid degradation, Antioxidant enzymesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Pesticides, which are employed to control a wide range of plant diseases and pests across various sectors, have also contributed significantly to public health by targeting harmful organisms such as mosquitoes, ticks, rats, and mice in homes, workplaces, shopping centers, and urban areas. Within agriculture, these chemicals are vital for reducing or entirely preventing damage caused by vectors that lead to crop losses and serious plant diseases. Farmers have come to rely heavily on pesticides to manage weeds and pests, making them an indispensable element of crop production, particularly after substantial improvements in agricultural yields were observed1. Without pesticide use, significant losses occur in the production of fruits, vegetables, and grains. As a result, nearly one-third of agricultural output worldwide depends on pesticides. Consequently, pesticides play a crucial role in boosting food availability by enhancing crop quality and yield. In addition to these direct effects, pesticide usage also supports food safety, nutritional value, and crop productivity, along with secondary benefits such as increased labor efficiency, enhanced biodiversity, and greater crop variety2. The vast majority instead contaminates non-target species and environmental media, leading to pollution that negatively impacts human health, wildlife such as birds and fish, beneficial insects, non-target plants, and essential environmental components including air, water, and soil3,4. Pesticides applied or released in agricultural settings often spread into air, water, soil, and various organisms within these environments, where they undergo processes like movement and degradation after dispersal5,6. These chemicals can be carried from their intended target areas to other parts of the environment or to non-target plants through mechanisms including adsorption, leaching, volatilization, spray drift, and runoff. Pesticides can also be harmful to plants; pesticides like maneb, paraquat, and rotenone increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to physiological damage7. Additionally, carbamate chemicals and their acid derivatives inhibit seed germination, and triazine herbicides interfere with photosynthetic electron transport8,9.

Fungicides have been employed in agriculture for over 200 years to control fungal diseases and reduce crop losses. Fungicides are a key element of disease management strategies in agricultural production. These chemical agents are used to prevent the emergence or slow the progression of fungal diseases in plants. Unlike insecticides and herbicides, which eliminate weeds, fungicides prevent the development of infection by forming a protective layer on plant tissues rather than directly killing the pathogen. In assessing the risks associated with these chemicals, it is evident that crop productivity and financial returns can be improved by using fungicides that effectively target fungal pathogens yet minimize harm to beneficial organisms. However, fungicides that act on specific binding sites may inadvertently affect non-target species. For example, fungicides containing glucopyranosyl antibiotic structures can be highly toxic to bacteria by disrupting amino acid synthesis and may also pose risks to some higher eukaryotic organisms beyond fungi10. Due to insufficient regulation concerning the sustainable use of many fungicides, their potential impacts on non-fungal microorganisms are not fully understood. Therefore, careless application of fungicides might result in unforeseen detrimental effects. Supporting this concern, People are exposed to fungicides through consumption of contaminated fruits and vegetables or through contact with water, soil, air, and other environmental media. Fungicide exposure increases the incidence of cancer, metabolic and neurological diseases, endocrine disorders and reproductive system abnormalities in humans11. Yang et al.12 reported that fungicides can cause damage to cell membranes, disrupt nucleic acid and protein synthesis, interfere with signal transduction, and negatively affect respiration, mitosis, and cell division. Mancozeb, which belongs to the class of ethylene bisdithiocarbamate fungicides and contains the metabolite ethylenethiourea, was initially approved in the United States as a broad-spectrum fungicide in 1948. Since then, it has been extensively used for over 70 years against more than 400 plant pathogens affecting various crops including potatoes, tomatoes, grapevines, and citrus fruits. Due to its widespread use, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) classifies mancozeb as a general-use pesticide13. Mancozeb is a manganese/zinc ethylene-bis-dithiocarbamate fungicide that reacts with the sulfhydryl groups of amino acids, proteins, and many enzymes in fungal cells, causing structural deterioration. It disrupts the biochemical pathways involved in these molecules, primarily lipid metabolism, protein metabolism, respiration, and ATP production. In larvae, mancozeb increases developmental time and pupal mortality, and causes abnormalities in the ultrastructure of larvae, such as swelling of the nucleus and endoplasmic reticulum and decreased cytoplasmic glycogen content14. Exposure to mancozeb can occur through ingestion of contaminated foods like tomatoes and potatoes or through polluted drinking water. After entering the body, mancozeb is rapidly metabolized15. However, exposure has been associated with neurotoxic effects such as Parkinsonian symptoms16, disturbances in thyroid hormone regulation17, and developmental abnormalities in the fetal brain18. Long-term contact with mancozeb and its metabolite ethylenethiourea has been linked to endocrine disruption, teratogenicity, mutagenicity, and carcinogenicity13. Recent studies have identified persistent genotoxic and premalignant effects in human ovarian and immune cells after exposure to mancozeb19. Animal experiments showed that mancozeb impairs reproductive health, induces DNA damage, and has a high potential to initiate tumor formation in fetal cell20. Additionally, research by Belpoggi et al.21 demonstrated that prolonged exposure to mancozeb leads to cancer development in rats. There is also evidence connecting mancozeb exposure to both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism22.

In recent years, natural extracts such as those derived from Ginkgo biloba L., green coffee, green tea, black cumin, lycopene, dandelion, nettle, and royal jelly have gained popularity for mitigating pesticide-induced toxicity. Among these, grape seed extract (GSE) has attracted significant attention. Vitis vinifera L., a grape species widely consumed around the world, belongs to the genus Vitis within the Vitaceae family23. Leading grape producers include the United States, China, Italy, and various European countries. Grape seeds, commonly considered a by-product of grape processing, are utilized to produce GSE. The extract is rich in polyphenols and flavonoids, compounds known for their antioxidant properties and ability to scavenge free radicals24. These polyphenols exhibit stronger antioxidant effects than well-known antioxidants such as vitamins C and E and beta-carotene. In addition to antioxidant activity, enzymes present in GSE play a role in modulating histamine release during inflammatory and allergic responses25. The oil content of grape seeds varies by grape variety but generally contains high amounts of unsaturated fatty acids, which have been linked to the management of cardiovascular diseases, cancer, hypertension, and autoimmune conditions26. Furthermore, grape seed oil has shown benefits in reducing arteriosclerosis27. Both grape seeds and skins provide significant dietary fiber, which is associated with lowered risks of colon cancer, heart disease, diabetes, and obesity28. GSE demonstrates a range of beneficial effects including anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, anti-necrotic, cardioprotective, and anticancer activities, making it useful in treating various health conditions including skin aging29. By protecting cells against oxidative stress, antioxidants in GSE help reduce oxidative damage and inflammation30. This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the toxic effects of mancozeb, a fungicide widely used in agricultural practices, on Allium cepa L. and to determine the potential protective role of GSE. Mancozeb, a frequently used pesticide in agriculture, can cause significant biological stress on non-target organisms in the environment. In this context, the significance of this study stems from its contribution to both a better understanding of the ecotoxicological risks of pesticides and the potential use of natural biological agents (phytochemicals) to mitigate these risks. This study provides a unique contribution to the literature by examining the damage caused by mancozeb at the physiological, cytogenetic, biochemical, anatomical, and genotoxic levels with a holistic approach and by experimentally demonstrating the mitigating role of grape seed extract in these harmful effects.

Materials and methods

Collection, identification, and extraction procedures of plant materials

A. cepa bulbs (with a chromosome number of 2n = 16) were selected as the bioindicator organism. Mancozeb (CAS Number 8018-01-7) was procured from Merck. GSE, used as a natural protective agent, was prepared from grapes of the “Delice White” cultivar, harvested in the Delice district of Kırıkkale Province, Türkiye. After being brought to the laboratory, species identification of both the A. cepa bulbs and grape samples was confirmed by Prof. Dr. Zafer TÜRKMEN, a faculty member in the Department of Botany at Giresun University. The collection and use of all plant materials in the experiments were carried out in compliance with institutional, national, and international ethical standards and regulations. All images presented in this study were produced by the authors. The grape seeds separated from the fruit were initially dried at 35 °C using a laboratory oven and subsequently ground into a fine powder. For extraction, 2 g of this powder were immersed in 100 mL of ethanol and subjected to maceration. The process was carried out in a shaking incubator at 22 °C for a duration of 24 h to facilitate compound release. Following incubation, the mixture was passed through Whatman No. 4 filter paper to eliminate solid residues. The filtered extract was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to obtain a clarified supernatant. This liquid phase was concentrated using a rotary evaporator to remove the ethanol. The resulting dry extract was collected and preserved for further experimental procedures31.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Quantitative analysis of phenolic constituents in grape seed samples was carried out using LC-MS/MS. For this purpose, 1 g of the sample was extracted using a methanol–dichloromethane mixture in a 4:1 ratio. The resulting extract was filtered through a syringe filter with a pore diameter of 0.45 µM prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved using an ODS Hypersil column on a Thermo Scientific LC-MS/MS system. The column oven was maintained at 30 °C, with a mobile phase flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. The total run time for the analysis was 34 min32,33. All LC-MS/MS measurements were performed at the Scientific and Technological Application and Research Center of Hitit University (HUBTUAM).

Dose preferences and experimental groups

In determining the mancozeb dose, the highest toxic dose level reported by Fatma et al.34 on A. cepa was taken into account. Grape seed extract (GSE) doses were selected based on the doses applied by Yalçın and Çavuşoğlu35 on A. cepa, which have been proven to limit toxicity.

A total of six experimental groups were formed in the study, each consisting of 10 bulbs. Group I was designated as the control and was treated with tap water only. Group II was treated with GSE at a concentration of 465 mg/L, and Group III with 930 mg/L GSE. Group IV was treated with 150 mg/L mancozeb alone. Co-treatment protocols were created by administering 465 mg/L GSE along with 150 mg/L mancozeb in Group V, and 930 mg/L GSE along with 150 mg/L mancozeb in Group VI. Bulb germination was conducted at 22 °C using sterile glass beakers measuring 85 × 100 mL. During this period, bulbs in Group 1 (control) were treated with tap water, while those in Groups II through VI received 150 mg/L mancozeb and GSE at concentrations of 465 and 930 mg/L. The beakers were checked daily throughout germination, and any evaporated or lost solution was replenished accordingly. At the conclusion of the germination period, root and leaf tissues were harvested. These samples were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water, then subjected to standard homogenization and crushing protocols before being prepared for subsequent analyses and observations36.

Examining growth and germination parameters

Three key metrics were carefully measured in order to analyze the phytotoxic effects of mancozeb and the possible protective effectiveness of GSE on physiological performance: root elongation (cm), total fresh biomass increment (g), and germination percentage (%). To reduce size-related variability, uniformly sized Allium cepa bulbs were carefully chosen for each experimental cohort. The initial fresh weight of each bulb was carefully measured on a high-resolution analytical balance. After a period of uniform germination, the bulbs were meticulously removed, excess moisture removed by surface drying, and reweighed to determine their final fresh weight37. The difference between final and beginning weights was used to compute the net weight gain, which served as a gauge of growth performance under treatment. To ensure accuracy in elongation evaluation, measurements of root length were made quantitatively using a calibrated ruler, starting from the radicle emergence point and ending at the apical root tip. Root lengths were randomly measured from each of the ten bulbs used in each group for the purpose of measuring root elongation38. The ratio of bulbs exhibiting root growth to the total number of bulbs was known as the germination percentage, and it was calculated as a percentage (Eq. 1).

Chromosomal abnormality and MN testing

To investigate CAs and the presence of MN, a modified acetocarmine staining method was utilized. Root tips approximately 1 cm in length were excised and fixed for 2 h using Clarke’s fixative, composed of three parts ethanol and one part glacial acetic acid. After fixation, the samples were rinsed in 96% ethanol for 15 min and preserved in 70% ethanol at 4 °C until further processing. The preserved root tips underwent hydrolysis in 1 N hydrochloric acid at 60 °C for 17 min. Following this, they were immersed in 45% glacial acetic acid for 30 min to prepare the tissue for staining. The samples were then stained with acetocarmine for 24 h to enhance chromosomal visibility. After staining, the root tips were placed onto microscope slides, gently squashed under a coverslip, and observed under an IRMECO IM-450 TI research microscope at 400 × magnification. Photographs were captured to document chromosomal structures and abnormalities39. The mitotic index (MI) was calculated as the ratio of dividing cells to the total number of observed cells, expressed as a percentage (Eq. 2).

Comet assay

DNA extraction from A. cepa root cells was performed using the protocol described by Sharma et al.40. For the comet assay, the procedure outlined by Dikilitaş and Koçyiğit41 was followed. Initially, the slides were soaked in ethanol for 24 h, then dried in an oven. 100 μL layer of agarose was applied to each slide, covered with a coverslip, and left to solidify. The slides were then chilled in a refrigerator for 5 min to harden the gel. Next, a mixture of freshly isolated cells and 1% low melting point (LMP) agarose prepared at 40 °C in distilled water was combined at a ratio of 1:8 and pipetted (100 μL) over the first agarose layer. A coverslip was placed on top and the slides were refrigerated again for 5 min. After removing the coverslips, the slides were placed into an electrophoresis chamber filled with buffer solution and incubated for 40 min to allow DNA unwinding before running electrophoresis. Following electrophoresis, the slides were rinsed three times, each for 5 min, using 0.4 M Tris-HCl at room temperature to neutralize the alkaline environment and improve image clarity. Subsequently, the slides were stained with 100 μL of ethidium bromide, covered with coverslips, and observed under a fluorescence microscope. Comet images were captured and analyzed using TriTek 2.0.0.38 Automatic Comet Assay software. For each group, 1000 cells were examined, and the percentages of DNA in the comet’s head and tail were measured. The amount of tail DNA was used as an indicator of DNA damage, following the classification scale proposed by Pereira et al.42.

A total of 1000 cells are scored on a scale from 0 to 4: class 0: undamaged cells (not included in the calculation), class 1: slightly damaged, class 2: moderately damaged, class 3: highly damaged, class 4: severely damaged. The percentage of cells observed in each class is recorded. This percentage is then multiplied by the corresponding class coefficient (1, 2, 3, or 4). DNA damage was assessed using the Comet assay scale based on the percentage of tail DNA, categorized as follows: undamaged cells (≤ 5%), slightly damaged (5–20%), moderately damaged (20–40%), highly damaged (40–75%), and severely damaged (≥ 75%). The values obtained for all classes are summed, and this total represents the Damage Index (DI) value.

Briefly damage index (DI), that is the sum of (% of class 1 × number of cell) + (% of class 2 × number of cell) + (% of class 3 × number of cell) + (% of class 4 × number of cell).

Determination of MDA content

MDA levels were quantified following the procedure described by Unyayar et al.43. 0.5 g sample of root tips was homogenized in 1 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The resulting homogenate was transferred into a sterile tube and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. An equal volume of 0.5% thiobarbituric acid was then added to the supernatant. This mixture was incubated in 20% TCA at 96 °C for 30 min and immediately cooled in an ice bath. After cooling, the samples were centrifuged again at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The absorbance of the clear supernatant was measured at 532 nm to determine the MDA concentration.

Proline measurement

Proline accumulation was assessed using the method developed by Bates et al.44. 0.5 g sample of root tips was homogenized in 10 mL of 3% aqueous sulfosalicylic acid. The resulting mixture was filtered through filter paper, and 2 mL of the filtrate was combined with 2 mL of glacial acetic acid and 2 mL of acid ninhydrin reagent in a sterile test tube. The reaction was carried out at 100 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, the tubes were cooled in an ice bath. Subsequently, 4 mL of toluene was added to extract the reaction product. The mixture was vortexed for 20 s, and the toluene phase—containing the chromophore—was carefully separated from the aqueous layer. The toluene extract was then brought to room temperature (21 °C), and its absorbance was recorded at 520 nm using toluene as the blank. Proline concentration was calculated based on a standard curve and expressed relative to the fresh weight of the samples using Eq. (3).

Antioxidant enzyme assays

All steps of the enzyme extraction were performed at 4 °C to preserve enzyme activity. 0.5 g sample of root tips was ground in a mortar with 5 mL of cold sodium phosphate buffer. The resulting homogenate was centrifuged at 10.500 × g for 20 min. The supernatant, containing the crude enzyme extract, was collected and stored at 4 °C until further analysis45. SOD activity was determined based on the method described by Beauchamp and Fridovich46. 3 mL reaction mixture was prepared containing the following components: 1.5 mL of 0.05 M sodium phosphate buffer, 0.3 mL of 130 mM methionine, 0.3 mL of 750 μM nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), 0.3 mL of 0.1 mM EDTA-Na2, 0.3 mL of 20 μM riboflavin, 0.01 mL of enzyme extract, 0.01 mL of 4% insoluble polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and 0.28 mL of deionized water. The reaction solution was transferred to sterile test tubes and exposed to illumination from 15 W fluorescent lamps for 10 min to initiate the reaction. To stop the reaction, the tubes were kept in the dark for another 10 min. The absorbance of the solution was then measured at 560 nm. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the enzyme amount necessary to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction under the assay conditions36. Catalase activity was evaluated based on the protocol outlined by Beers and Sizer47, with slight modifications. The reaction mixture had a total volume of 2.8 mL and contained 1.5 mL of 200 mM monosodium phosphate buffer, 1.0 mL of distilled water, and 0.3 mL of 0.1 M hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The assay was carried out at 25 °C using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer. The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding 0.2 mL of the enzyme extract to the reaction medium. The activity of catalase was determined by observing the decline in absorbance at 240 nm, which reflects the decomposition of H₂O₂36.

Extraction and quantification of chlorophyll

The procedure outlined by Kaydan et al.48 was used to measure the amounts of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b in order to determine the impact of mancozeb and GSE treatments on photosynthetic pigment levels. Fresh leaf samples weighing 0.2 g were stored at 4 °C before being finely ground with a mortar and pestle. The ground material was mixed thoroughly with 5 mL of 80% acetone in a dark environment to prevent pigment degradation. After extraction procedure the absorbance of the chlorophyll extract was measured at 645 nm and 663 nm using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer. Chlorophyll a and b concentrations were measured using Eqs. 4 and 5, respectively, in compliance with the procedures described by Witham et al.49.

Anatomical observations

Root tips from each group were carefully sandwiched between pieces of styrofoam foam. Thin transverse slices were cut in one clean stroke using a sterilized razor blade. After that, these slices were put on microscope slides, coated with coverslips, and stained for two minutes with 5% methylene blue. Using an Irmeco IM-450 TI research microscope, the slides were inspected. Meristematic cell damage was noted and recorded using 200 × magnification photos.50.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using the SPSS Statistics 22 software (IBM SPSS, Turkey). To determine statistical differences among groups, one-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Duncan’s test for multiple comparisons. The results are expressed as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Differences were considered statistically significant at p values below 0.05. The data showed normal distribution according to both the Shapiro–Wilk Test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the Swekness-Kurtosis values of the data were also within the normal distribution range (− 1, + 1). Therefore, parametric tests were used for statistical analysis. Since the Levene test demonstrated homogeneous distribution of variances during data analysis, the Duncan test was applied to determine statistical differences between the groups.

Results and discussion

Physiological findings

The effects of mancozeb and GSE treatments on selected physiological parameters of A. cepa are summarized in Table 1. The highest rates of germination, root elongation, and weight gain were recorded in the control group (Group I) as well as in Groups II and III, which received two different doses of GSE. No statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) were observed among these groups for the measured physiological parameters. In contrast, Group IV, treated with 150 mg/L mancozeb, exhibited significant reductions (p < 0.05) across all evaluated parameters. Relative to the control, decreases of 49% in germination, approximately 3.96-fold in root elongation, and 6.67-fold in weight gain were observed. When GSE was co-applied with mancozeb at concentrations of 465 mg/L (Group V) and 930 mg/L (Group VI), significant improvements (p < 0.05) in physiological parameters were noted, with the most pronounced effects seen in Group VI. Compared to Group IV, Group VI showed increases of 23% in germination, approximately 2.37-fold in root elongation, and about 4.33-fold in weight gain. Our results align with those reported in previous studies investigating the physiological effects of mancozeb on various plant species. Anitha and Savitha51 observed that mancozeb applied at doses between 1 and 9 mg stimulated seed germination, seedling length, and vigor index in Eleusine coracana (finger millet) over a 14-day period, whereas higher concentrations of 9 and 12 mg inhibited these parameters. Fatma et al.34 demonstrated that exposure of A. cepa bulbs to increasing mancozeb concentrations (ranging from 10 to 150 ppm) for 24 and 48 h resulted in a concentration- and time-dependent reduction in both average root number and root length. Tejashwini et al.52 reported that treating the Ragi seed variety INDAF7 with mancozeb at concentrations of 0.2–0.4% led to dose-dependent decreases in germination percentage.

The observed reductions in physiological parameters following mancozeb treatment may be attributed to impaired water and mineral nutrient uptake by roots, diminished mitotic activity, and structural damage to root tissues. Supporting this, Kesti et al.53 reported that fungicides hinder plant growth by disrupting the transport of essential water and minerals required for root development. Fatma et al.34 demonstrated that mancozeb inhibits the cell cycle in A. cepa root tip cells, leading to decreased mitotic activity, which in turn limits root growth and biomass accumulation—highlighting the critical role of mitotic division in plant development. Consistent with this, our study observed a decline in the MI of root cells after mancozeb exposure, reinforcing this mechanism. Since roots serve as the primary sites for water and mineral absorption, structural damage to root tissues can reduce germination rates, root elongation, and overall biomass by impeding nutrient uptake. In line with these findings, Macar et al.54 reported fungicide-induced anatomical alterations in A. cepa roots, including damage to epidermal and cortex cells, thickening of cortex cell walls, and decreased uptake of water and minerals.

Genotoxicity findings

The genotoxic effects of mancozeb on A. cepa root meristem cells are summarized in Table 2 and Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 1. The highest MI values were recorded in Group III (930 mg/L GSE), Group I (control), and Group II (465 mg/L GSE), with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) among these groups. These same groups also exhibited the highest percentages of head DNA, indicating intact DNA. Additionally, frequencies of MN and CAs—including vagrant chromosomes, sticky chromosomes, and unequal chromatin distribution—were low and statistically non-significant (p > 0.05). In contrast, Group IV, treated with mancozeb alone (150 mg/L), showed significant reductions in MI and head DNA percentage (p < 0.05), alongside significant increases in tail DNA percentage, MN frequency, and CAs (p < 0.05). Compared to the control, MI decreased by approximately 36.3%, and head DNA decreased by around 66.9% in Group IV, while MN and CAs increased markedly. CAs in mancozeb-exposed root meristem cells included chromosomal fragments, sticky chromosomes, bridges, unequal distribution of chromatin, irreguler mitosis, disorientation, and binucleated cells. Co-treatment with GSE at 465 mg/L (Group V) and 930 mg/L (Group VI) alongside mancozeb led to significant improvements, with increased MI and head DNA percentages and reduced tail DNA, MN frequency, and CAs (p < 0.05). These protective effects were more pronounced at the higher GSE dose (930 mg/L) in Group VI, where MI increased by approximately 17.4%, head DNA increased by 31.6%, and MN frequency decreased by about 1.53-fold compared to Group IV. Damage index values calculated from the comet data also indicate the genotoxic effect of mancozeb and the protective properties of GSE. While the calculated damage index was 39.2 ± 9.47, 34.7 ± 8.65 and 33.9 ± 8.43 in the control and GSE-only groups, respectively. The Mancozeb-treated group showed the highest damage index (336 ± 35.5), reflecting severe genotoxicity. In the Mancozeb + GSE groups (V and VI), the damage index decreased significantly, particularly to 258.4 ± 29.8 in Group VI, which received 930 mg/L GSE, demonstrating the strong protective effect of GSE.

Chromosomal damages promoted by mancozeb.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have examined mancozeb-induced genotoxicity across different species. Barakat et al.55 reported decreased MI values and CAs—including chromosome stickiness, C-metaphase, and disrupted metaphase—in A. cepa root tip cells following exposure to mancozeb for 3, 6, 24, and 48 h. Goldoni et al.56 demonstrated significant DNA damage in Wistar rats using the Comet assay, indicating the genotoxic potential of mancozeb and its detrimental effects on DNA integrity. Additionally, Many pesticides have been reported to trigger MN formation in non-target organisms57,58. The MI, which indicates the frequency of cell division in the root growth zone, is widely recognized as a reliable marker for assessing cytotoxicity59. The observed reduction in MI following mancozeb exposure may be attributed to disruption of microtubule organization, particularly affecting spindle fiber formation during mitosis. This notion is supported by studies showing that pesticide exposure significantly impairs tubulin polymerization, a critical process for microtubule assembly60. Additionally, literature suggests that mancozeb-induced decreases in mitotic activity may result from cell cycle arrest during interphase, inhibition of nuclear protein synthesis essential for proper mitotic progression, suppression of DNA replication, and alterations in the duration of mitotic phases54. The observed increase in MN frequency and CAs, along with the reduction in DNA content following mancozeb exposure, may arise from two primary molecular mechanisms. First, mancozeb may interact directly or indirectly with DNA, causing structural damage. Second, mancozeb may induce DNA damage indirectly by promoting the generation of ROS within the cell. Several studies support these hypotheses. Kaur and Kaur61 reported that oxidative stress induced by ROS produced in response to pesticide exposure disrupts DNA integrity and impairs DNA repair mechanisms. Usman et al.62 found that the highly reactive nature of ROS leads to genotoxicity and mutations through direct interaction with DNA. Jameel et al.63 demonstrated that certain pesticides can directly interact with DNA and key enzymes, significantly affecting their function. However, in the case of mancozeb, the genotoxic effects on MN formation, CAs, and DNA damage are predominantly attributed to an indirect mechanism involving ROS generation rather than a direct interaction. Lori et al.64 identified oxidative damage caused by ROS as the underlying cause of mancozeb-induced genotoxicity. Liu et al.65 showed that mancozeb elevates ROS levels, resulting in DNA damage that triggers apoptotic pathways and abnormal epigenetic modifications.

Biochemical findings

The biochemical effects of mancozeb on A. cepa are summarized in Fig. 2. The lowest levels of MDA and proline, along with the lowest activities of SOD and CAT enzymes, and the highest chlorophyll content were observed in the control group (Group I) as well as in Groups II and III, which received two different doses of GSE. No statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) were found among these groups for the measured biochemical parameters. Conversely, Group IV, treated with mancozeb alone, exhibited significant increases (p < 0.05) in MDA and proline levels, SOD and CAT activities, and significant decreases (p < 0.05) in chlorophyll content. Compared to the control, Group IV showed approximately 2.93-fold increase in MDA, 2.20-fold increase in proline, 2.17-fold increase in SOD activity, 2.76-fold increase in CAT activity, a 2.88-fold increase in chlorophyll a, and a 3.16-fold decrease in chlorophyll b levels. Groups V and VI, treated with combined mancozeb and GSE at 465 and 930 mg/L respectively, demonstrated significant (p < 0.05) reductions in MDA, proline, SOD, and CAT levels, alongside significant increases (p < 0.05) in chlorophyll content relative to Group IV. These improvements were more pronounced in Group VI exposed to the higher GSE dose. Specifically, compared to Group IV, Group VI showed approximately 2.06-fold decrease in MDA, 1.63-fold decrease in proline, 1.39-fold decrease in SOD activity, 1.55-fold decrease in CAT activity, and increases of 1.96-fold and 2.25-fold in chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b levels, respectively. Although comprehensive studies on the biochemical toxicity of mancozeb in A. cepa are lacking, our findings align with reports investigating mancozeb-induced biochemical alterations in other plant species. Dias et al. 66observed increased proline content and lipid peroxidation levels in lettuce leaves 7 days after mancozeb application, accompanied by a decline in chlorophyll content attributed to reduced photosystem II photochemical efficiency. Hatamleh et al.67 reported that exposure to mancozeb led to decreases in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll levels in Solanum lycopersicum L. (tomato) plants, along with elevated activities of CAT, SOD, ascorbate peroxidase (APX), peroxidase (POD), and guaiacol peroxidase (GPX). Along with literature studies reporting increases in antioxidant enzyme activities, there are also studies reporting decreases in activity in high dose pesticide applications68.

The observed increase in proline levels in roots following mancozeb exposure is believed to represent a plant defense response against pesticide-induced stress. This notion is supported by literature indicating a positive correlation between proline accumulation and plant tolerance to various stressors. Proline accumulation enhances resistance in plants exposed to diverse stress factors, including pesticides, heavy metals, salinity, and xenobiotics, and is recognized as an indicator of stress sensitivity in plants69–72. The elevated MDA levels in roots following mancozeb exposure are likely attributed to cell membrane damage caused by the pesticide. Mancozeb can induce lipid peroxidation within the membrane structure, resulting in increased MDA production as a byproduct of membrane degradation. This concept is supported by previous findings. Hatamleh et al.67 reported that mancozeb exposure increased ROS levels and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) production in Solanum lycopersicum roots, which was accompanied by enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities to mitigate free radical damage. Saber and El-Aziz73 demonstrated that mancozeb, containing transition metals, promotes oxidative stress through ROS generation via the Fenton reaction, leading to increased antioxidant enzyme levels. The reduction in leaf chlorophyll content following mancozeb exposure may result from multiple factors, including structural damage to leaf tissues, heightened oxidative stress, inhibition of enzymes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis, and diminished light absorption. Supporting evidence from the literature reinforces this explanation. Gülengül and Karabulut74 demonstrated that pesticides trigger oxidative stress in chloroplasts, leading to increased ROS production, which accelerates chlorophyll degradation. Hatamleh et al.67 found that pesticides negatively affect metabolic enzymes responsible for synthesizing chlorophyll and carotenoids, thereby reducing their levels. Schneider et al.75 noted that since chlorophyll pigments are essential for light absorption during photosynthesis, mancozeb exposure impairs photosynthetic efficiency by decreasing photon absorption.

Anatomical findings

Anatomical alterations induced by mancozeb in the root meristem cells of A. cepa are summarized in Table 4 and illustrated in Fig. 3. Microscopic examination revealed no cellular damage in Group I (control) as well as Groups II and III, which received two different concentrations of GSE. In contrast, treatment with mancozeb in Group IV resulted in pronounced damage, including severe disruption of epidermal and cortex cells, nuclear flattening, and thickening of conductive tissues in root meristem cells. However, in Groups V and VI, where mancozeb exposure was combined with GSE at doses of 465 and 930 mg/L respectively, the severity of these anatomical damages was notably reduced. This protective effect was more evident in Group VI, which received the higher GSE concentration, where conduction tissue thickening was fully restored to normal levels. There is a lack of comprehensive studies examining microscopic alterations induced by mancozeb in A. cepa root meristem cells, with most existing research focusing on macroscopic observations. Fatma et al.34 reported a dose-dependent reduction in the average root number of A. cepa treated with mancozeb at concentrations ranging from 10 to 150 ppm. Research on anatomical damage caused by mancozeb in other plant species is also scarce. Hatamleh et al.67 observed cell death in the roots of Solanum lycopersicum following mancozeb exposure. Güç et al.76 documented several anatomical injuries in A. cepa roots exposed to ridomil fungicide (containing 65% mancozeb), including nuclear flattening, thickening of conductive tissues, damage to epidermal and cortex cells, and accumulation of materials in the cortex cell walls.

Mancozeb-induced meristematic cell damage.

The epidermis and cortex cell damage, along with nuclear flattening observed in root meristem cells following mancozeb exposure, is believed to represent a plant defense mechanism aimed at limiting the pesticide’s entry into the cells. Microscopic analysis revealed a significant increase in the number of epidermal and cortex cells in roots exposed to mancozeb compared to controls. This cellular proliferation likely acts as a physical barrier to pesticide infiltration but also generates mechanical pressure due to increased cell-to-cell contact, which may result in deformation of these cells and their nuclei. Additionally, the thickening of conductive tissues observed after mancozeb treatment is thought to be another defense strategy, restricting the spread of the pesticide to other tissues. Supporting this, previous studies77–79 have reported that plants increase cell proliferation and conductive tissue thickness in response to pesticide exposure as protective adaptations. Furthermore, plants are known to activate chemical defenses such as elevated synthesis of alkaloids, terpenoids, phenolics, and stress-related proteins (e.g., phytochelatins and metallothioneins)80, alongside morphological changes including increased cell layers and cell wall thickening36,37 to mitigate toxic effects. Another possible explanation for epidermal cell deformation is the loss of membrane integrity due to oxidative stress; mancozeb-induced ROS can damage the lipid components of cell membranes, causing structural abnormalities. Nuclear flattening may also result from mancozeb-induced damage within the nucleus, including chromosomal loss and DNA damage, which can reduce nuclear volume or cause morphological alterations. These hypotheses align with findings reported by Dahl et al.81 and Dauer and Worman82.

Protective role of GSE

Mancozeb caused toxicity across all examined physiological, cytogenetic, biochemical, and anatomical parameters in A. cepa. It led to reductions in germination rate, root elongation, weight gain, MI, and chlorophyll content, while inducing DNA damage and increasing the frequency of MN, CAs, MDA, proline, SOD, and CAT activities. The combined treatment of mancozeb with GSE at concentrations of 465 and 930 mg/L mitigated these adverse effects to some extent, although the measured values did not fully return to control levels. The protective effect was more pronounced at the higher dose of 930 mg/L GSE. Our findings align with previous studies that demonstrate the protective effects of GSE against various types of toxicity in A. cepa. Kalefetoğlu Macar et al.83 showed that GSE at concentrations of 150 and 300 mg/L alleviated physiological, cytogenetic, biochemical, and anatomical damage caused by cobalt(II) nitrate in A. cepa root cells. Yalçın and Cavusoglu35 reported that GSE at doses of 465 and 930 mg/L provided protection against the multifaceted toxic effects of potassium bromate in A. cepa. Yilmaz et al.84 also demonstrated that GSE reduced genotoxicity induced by benzyl benzoate in A. cepa. Comparable protective effects of GSE have been observed in animal studies as well. Kwatra85 highlighted GSE’s broad pharmacological activities against oxidative stress, including antidiabetic, cholesterol-lowering, and antiplatelet effects. Freedman86 further confirmed GSE’s antioxidant properties in cardiovascular health by decreasing platelet aggregation and superoxide production.

The protective effects of GSE against the physiological, cytogenetic, biochemical, and anatomical toxicity induced by mancozeb can be attributed to its strong antioxidant properties and bioactive compounds. Table 5 and Fig. 4 present the LC-MS/MS analysis results of the phenolic compounds found in GSE. The analysis revealed that catechin (73.926 µg/g) and epicatechin (26.376 µg/g) were the most abundant components in the extract. Following these, quercetin, resveratrol, and gentisic acid were detected in concentrations between 5 and 10 µg/g, while vanillin, taxifolin, protocatechuic acid, rosmarinic acid, salicylic acid, naringenin, benzoic acid, and oleuropein were found at levels below 5 µg/g. Catechin, the predominant compound in GSE, is a polyphenol commonly present in fruits such as apples, blueberries, kiwis, strawberries, grapes, and in beverages like green and black tea. It is known for its protective effects on liver, kidney, and heart functions, as well as its antimicrobial, anticancer, antihypertensive, anticoagulant, and antiulcer properties87. Epicatechin, found mainly in cocoa products and green tea, exhibits antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, improves muscle performance, and offers protection against cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, diabetic, and neurological disorders88. Quercetin, present at moderate levels, is a bioflavonoid recognized for its potent neuroprotective effects, especially in Alzheimer’s disease, along with strong radical scavenging and antioxidant activities89. Resveratrol, abundant particularly in grapevines, is noted for its antifungal, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-aging, cardioprotective, neuroprotective, and anticancer effects, effectively neutralizing free radicals90. Gentisic acid, another phenolic compound identified, is found in various plants including citrus fruits, hibiscus, olives, and berries. It serves as an antioxidant and cardioprotective agent, with smooth muscle relaxant and antiatherogenic properties, and has been shown to protect against septic shock and cardiac hypertrophy91. Vanillin, the primary constituent of vanilla and found in essential oils, has been reported to possess antioxidant, anticancer, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and cardioprotective effects92. Lastly, oleuropein, a polyphenol abundant in olive oil, is known for its free radical scavenging, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-carcinogenic, and anti-apoptotic activities. Experimental studies have demonstrated its protective effects against intestinal ischemia, reperfusion injury, cell apoptosis, and cardiomyopathy93.

Conclusion

The study concluded that high concentrations of mancozeb, a widely used broad-spectrum fungicide for controlling fungal diseases in crops, induced multiple toxic effects in A. cepa, a non-target organism. The combination of physiological, biochemical, and anatomical assessments alongside MN, CAs, and Comet assays proved to be highly effective in detecting this toxicity. These findings reaffirm that the Allium test is a reliable and practical tool for evaluating the toxic impact of environmental pollutants, including pesticides. The combined use of GSE with mancozeb significantly alleviated the toxic effects caused by mancozeb, resulting in statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05) across all measured parameters. This protective and restorative effect of GSE is attributed to the presence of 16 distinct phenolic compounds within its composition. Consequently, GSE has potential as a dietary supplement to help mitigate or prevent the harmful effects of pesticide exposure. It is crucial to identify pesticide dose levels that are highly effective against target pests but pose minimal risk to non-target organisms before using chemicals like mancozeb in agriculture. Moreover, educating farmers about the correct dosages, timing, and duration of pesticide applications is essential to ensure safe and responsible use. Strict adherence to pesticide guidelines should be enforced, with continuous monitoring from planting through harvest by authorized regulatory bodies. Legal penalties must be imposed on those who fail to comply with these regulations. While some previous studies have examined the toxic effects of mancozeb on plant test materials, they typically focus on only one or two parameters. In contrast, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of mancozeb-induced toxicity in plant test material by evaluating multiple parameters simultaneously. Furthermore, this research is the first to explore the protective effects of GSE against mancozeb toxicity in A. cepa. For these reasons, we believe this study offers a significant contribution to the existing body of knowledge.
