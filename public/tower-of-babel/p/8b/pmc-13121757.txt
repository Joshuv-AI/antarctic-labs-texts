Optimization of Falcon concentrator for iron recovery from blast furnace sludge using Box–Behnken design
Authors: Çağrı Çerik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlast furnace sludge (BFS) is an iron and carbon rich by-product whose direct recycling to ironmaking routes is often constrained by its fine particle size and the presence of volatile/corrosive species. This study evaluates Falcon enhanced gravity separation as a physical pre-treatment for iron upgrading from fine-grained BFS and optimizes operating conditions using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with a three-factor, three-level Box-Behnken design (solid ratio 10–30%, fluidization water pressure 0.5–1.5 psi, and G-force 50–250 G). Statistical modeling confirmed that fluidization water pressure and G-force dominate separation performance, producing a clear grade–recovery trade-off: increasing water pressure improves Fe grade but reduces recovery, whereas increasing G-force tends to increase recovery while diluting grade. The BFS feed contained 34.40% Fe, 18.44% C and 2.94% Zn, under multi response optimization, the validation test yielded a concentrate grading 51.42% Fe at 57.88% Fe recovery. A two-stage scavenging configuration increased overall recovery to 77.52% at 49.35% Fe in the final concentrate. The results demonstrate that an optimized, two-stage Falcon circuit can effectively upgrade BFS and reduce mass for downstream treatment, while noting that Zn remains concentrated in the Fe-rich stream and may require subsequent removal depending on the intended metallurgical route.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The iron and steel industry generates substantial quantities of solid by-products throughout ironmaking and steelmaking operations. Among these, blast furnace (BF) dust and BF sludge are particularly attractive secondary resources because of their elevated iron and carbon contents. However, they are also complex metallurgical residues that can pose environmental risks if not adequately managed. Inappropriate storage or disposal may lead to the contamination of water, soil, and the atmosphere, while the presence of potentially toxic constituents especially heavy metals raises further concern due to their capacity to leach under natural conditions and disturb ecological balance1–6. In parallel with these environmental considerations, the progressive reduction in landfill capacity and the tightening of disposal regulations have increased the urgency to develop sustainable and economically viable pathways for waste minimization and resource recovery7,8.

In integrated steel plants, BF dust and sludge generation is typically on the order of 20–40 kg/t of hot metal, and this amount may increase as the quality of raw materials deteriorates9–11. These residues are primarily produced within the gas cleaning plant, which operates in two sequential stages: primary and secondary gas cleaning. The primary stage is commonly conducted under dry conditions to remove coarse particulates, whereas the secondary stage may operate under either wet or dry conditions to capture fine and ultrafine particles12–17. Because of their remaining iron, carbon, and flux-bearing components, BF dust and BFS have long been considered promising candidates for recycling within the ironmaking route, particularly through sintering18,19. However, direct recycling is often limited in practice. Their very fine particle size and the presence of alkalis and volatile or corrosive elements such as Zn, Pb, and Sn may negatively affect sintering performance, promote equipment corrosion, and lead to the internal accumulation of undesirable species during repeated recycling cycles20–23.

In response to the need to recycle wastes generated in iron and steel production, numerous treatment options have been proposed, spanning pyrometallurgical and hydrometallurgical methods and, increasingly, their combined application within integrated process flows24–30. Pyrometallurgical treatments can achieve high removals of deleterious metals; however, they are economically constrained by high energy demand. Hydrometallurgical methods can likewise be efficient, but the use of concentrated acids and poor selectivity toward Zn over Fe limit their practical applicability31–33.

Enhanced gravity separation techniques provide an alternative route for the recovery of fine and ultrafine iron-bearing particles34–36. Compared with conventional gravity based methods, centrifugal concentrators generate much higher effective gravitational forces, thereby improving density based separation at fine particle sizes where conventional units become inefficient. Among these devices, the Falcon concentrator has been widely used in the beneficiation of fine mineral particles because of its ability to intensify stratification under centrifugal force37–41. Nevertheless, despite the established use of Falcon systems in mineral processing, their application to blast furnace sludge remains relatively limited, especially in the context of systematic process optimization for iron upgrading from fine and compositionally complex metallurgical residues. This gap is important because BFS differs from conventional mineral feeds not only in particle size characteristics, but also in its heterogeneous composition, the coexistence of metallic and non-metallic phases, and the presence of volatile contaminants that influence downstream utilization.

In the present study, iron recovery from blast furnace sludge was investigated by Falcon centrifugal concentration. The method was evaluated as a physical pre-treatment technique to assess its capability to upgrade iron content and recover iron-bearing fractions from fine-grained blast furnace sludge. Rather than addressing zinc removal, the study focuses exclusively on iron enrichment and the potential role of the physical separation method in improving the suitability of blast furnace sludge for subsequent metallurgical utilization. The results provide insight into the strengths and limitations of enhanced gravity separation as a pre-treatment option for iron recovery from blast furnace sludge.

Material and methods

Experimental material and analysis

A total 200 kg of BF sludge was provided from an integrated steel plant in Turkey. The bulk material was dried, homogenized and reduced to a representative laboratory sub-sample using coning and quartering.

Chemical analysis was performed by flame atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS) using a PerkinElmer Pinnacle 500 instrument. The total iron content was determined in accordance with ASTM E246, Test Method B - Stannous Chloride Reduction Dichromate Titration Method42. Carbon contents were determined by using an Eltra CS-2000 carbon/sulfur analyzer. Additionally, mineralogical examinations were performed using a Rigaku Miniflex 2 X-ray diffraction (XRD) device (Cu Kα radiation, 30 kV, 15 mA).

Table 1 reports the chemical composition of the BF sludge, and Fig. 1 shows a representative XRD pattern. The material closely resembles BF sludge described in the literature1,2,4,31 and is characterized by carbon-rich matter alongside iron-bearing phases (hematite, magnetite, wustite, and α-Fe) and common gangue/flux phases (calcite and quartz), with graphite also detected. The pattern exhibits a pronounced diffuse hump at ~ 24–28° (2θ), evidencing an amorphous component typically attributed to carbonaceous material, consistent with entrained unburnt coke derived particles. This interpretation aligns with the proximate analysis data.

Method

A laboratory-scale, batch-type Falcon L-40 concentrator was used in the experiments (Fig. 3). The unit can generate centrifugal forces up to 300 G and typically produces 80–120 g of concentrate, depending on the operating conditions and mineralogical characteristics of the feed. For each run, the feed mass was fixed at 200 g, selected based on the heavy-mineral content and preliminary test results. The feed rate was maintained at 6 kg/h throughout all tests. After setting the bowl speed and water pressure, the sample was introduced from the top using a vibratory feeder. The solids concentration was adjusted by regulating the external water addition. At the end of each test, the concentrate and tailings were collected, dried, weighed, and analyzed chemically.

Experimental setup.

In addition to the single-stage tests, a two-stage Falcon separation procedure was applied to evaluate the improvement in overall Fe recovery. In this approach, the tailing obtained from the first-stage separation under the optimized operating conditions was used as the feed for a second Falcon test conducted under the same operating parameters. This second stage acted as a scavenging step to recover additional Fe-bearing particles that were not collected in the first stage. The concentrates obtained from the first and second stages were then combined, and the overall concentrate grade and total Fe recovery were calculated based on the combined concentrate mass and assay values. Accordingly, the two-stage test configuration consisted of a rougher first stage followed by a scavenger treatment of the first-stage tailing.

The relationship between the operating variables and the response functions was described using a second-order polynomial model commonly employed in Response Surface Methodology. Based on the Box–Behnken design, separate predictive regression equations were developed for Fe grade and Fe recovery in terms of the coded variables. The adequacy of these models was evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA), coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, predicted R2, and lack-of-fit statistics. Similar modeling approaches have been widely used in Box–Behnken-based process optimization studies reported in the literature46–48.

The literature indicates that Falcon performance is governed by a combination of feed characteristics and operating conditions; however, only a subset of variables typically dominates the response, whereas others contribute marginally. Consequently, factor selection and level setting were guided by prior studies identifying the most influential variables for grade and overall efficiency44,49–52.

Fifteen experiments were performed within an RSM framework employing a three-level, three-factor Box–Behnken design. The data were analyzed using the Minitab 17 software, and significance was assessed by ANOVA at a 95% confidence level (CI 95%).

Effect of process variables on grade and recovery

The effects of solid ratio (A), water pressure (B) and G force (C) on the predicted Fe grade and Fe recovery are illustrated by the three-dimensional response surfaces in Figs. 5, 6 and 7. Figures 5, 6 and 7 collectively show that Falcon performance is governed primarily by fluidization water pressure (B) and G force (C), whereas the solid ratio (A) plays a secondary role within the investigated range. This hierarchy is consistent with the quadratic response surface models and ANOVA outcomes, where B and C terms are statistically significant for both responses (grade and recovery), while A is not significant.

Increasing fluidization water pressure (B) produces a clear increase in Fe grade but a decrease in Fe recovery. Mechanistically, a higher fluidization (wash) water promotes better bed dilation and interstitial flow through the concentrating zone, which enhances selective rejection of low density particles that would otherwise report to the concentrate by entrainment. In other words, stronger fluidization improves “washing” and therefore upgrades concentrate quality. However, the same intensified fluidization can also dislodge or carry away borderline Fe-bearing particles, particularly ultrafine or weakly settling particles that are not sufficiently locked into the high-density bed. This results in a net loss of valuable mass to the tailings and hence a reduction in Fe recovery as B increases. This behavior is in good agreement with earlier findings reported in the literature53.

By contrast, increasing G force (C) tends to increase Fe recovery while reducing Fe grade. The most plausible explanation is that higher centrifugal acceleration strengthens the driving force for density-based stratification and increases the capture probability of fine and/or marginally dense Fe-bearing particles, improving overall mass pull and therefore recovery. At the same time, operating at higher G commonly increases the tendency for non-selective carryover of ultrafines into the concentrate, either through hydraulic entrainment or by being mechanically trapped within the bed structure. This additional non valuable mass dilutes the concentrate, producing the observed grade penalty at elevated G forces. Similar results were also observed in previous studies54–56.

Accordingly, Fig. 7 most clearly visualizes the intrinsic grade-recovery trade-off produced by the opposing roles of water pressure and G force. At the higher levels of water pressure and lower level of G force conditions favor higher grade but lower recovery, whereas higher levels of G force and lower level of water pressure conditions favor higher recovery but lower grade due to increased entrainment and reduced selectivity.

The second order response surface models included cross product terms to account for potential interactions among the operating variables (A × B, A × C, and B × C). However, none of these interaction terms were statistically significant for either Fe grade or Fe recovery (p > 0.05). This indicates that, within the investigated ranges, the effect of one parameter did not depend strongly on the level of the other parameter. Accordingly, the response surfaces in Figs. 4, 5 and 6 are dominated by the main effects and the observed trends can be interpreted largely as additive contributions rather than synergistic or antagonistic interactions.

Response optimization

Optimization was performed using a multi response desirability framework to simultaneously maximize Fe grade and Fe recovery, thereby yielding a more realistic and industry relevant operating optimum. In contrast to single response maximization, this approach provides a more holistic definition of optimal conditions by explicitly accounting for trade-offs between performance targets (Fig. 8).

Optimization plots.

Equal weights were assigned to both responses to reflect their comparable industrial importance. The resulting desirability surfaces indicated a ridge like region, where further increases in grade were achieved at the expense of a gradual reduction in recovery. The predictive performance of the optimum solutions was evaluated by comparing model-predicted responses with confirmation experiments carried out at the corresponding optimal settings (Table 6). The validation run produced a concentrate grading 51.42% Fe at 57.88% Fe recovery. All experimental results fell within the 95% confidence intervals (CI 95%) of the predicted responses. In addition, the relative standard errors (RSEs) between predicted and measured values were 0.906% for Fe grade and 0.995% for Fe recovery, with all relative errors remaining below the 5% criterion57–59. These results confirm the adequacy and accuracy of the optimized models.

Under the optimized operating conditions, beneficiation of BFS using the Falcon concentrator produced a concentrate grading 51.42% Fe with 57.88% Fe recovery. However, this recovery level may limit industrial applicability. Therefore, a two-stage beneficiation strategy was adopted to enhance overall recovery. The tailings from the first stage were reprocessed under the same optimal conditions. Conclusion

This study demonstrated that Falcon centrifugal concentration can serve as an effective physical pre-concentration method for upgrading blast furnace sludge (BFS) prior to downstream treatment. Using a three-factor, three-level Box–Behnken design, the effects of solid ratio, fluidization water pressure, and centrifugal acceleration on Fe grade and Fe recovery were systematically evaluated and successfully modeled. Among the investigated variables, fluidization water pressure was identified as the most influential factor governing the separation performance.

Under the optimized operating conditions, a concentrate assaying 51.42% Fe was obtained with 57.88% Fe recovery in the single-stage test. Although this result confirmed the technical applicability of Falcon separation for BFS, the recovery level indicated that a single-stage operation was not sufficient for practical utilization. Therefore, the tailing from the first stage was reprocessed under the same conditions, and the two-stage approach increased the overall Fe recovery to 77.52% while producing a concentrate containing 49.35% Fe. This result shows that two-stage Falcon treatment is a more effective strategy for improving the recovery of iron bearing particles from BFS.

Despite the improvement in Fe upgrading, the concentrate still contained Zn, indicating that further treatment is required before final reuse in ironmaking or related applications. Nevertheless, because nearly half of the initial mass was rejected from the material stream, the proposed pre-concentration step can substantially reduce the volume requiring further processing, thereby improving the economic and environmental feasibility of subsequent Zn removal or refining operations. Future work should therefore focus on integrating Falcon pre-concentration with selective Zn removal and evaluating the process at a larger scale.
