Dietary N-acetylcysteine supplementation improves antioxidant capacity and production performance of breeder hens and offspring
Authors: Shu Chen, Qiqi Han, Zirui Zhou, Mingkun Gao, Jianlou Song, Bingkun Zhang, Yuming Guo, Zengpeng Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of dietary N-acetylcysteine (NAC) supplementation on the antioxidant capacity and production performance of broiler breeder hens and their offspring. The breeder hens were randomly assigned to two groups: one received a corn-soybean meal-based control diet, while the other was supplemented with 1 g/kg NAC. Eggs from each group were randomly selected for incubation, and the offspring broilers were fed a basal diet. Dietary NAC treatment not only increased the albumen height and Haugh unit values of eggs but also improved fertilization rates (P < 0.05). Additionally, broiler chicks from the NAC-treated hens demonstrated higher eviscerated weight, full evisceration rate, and semi-eviscerated weight (P < 0.05). Dietary supplementation with NAC in breeder hens enhanced antioxidant capacity, as evidenced by increased total antioxidant capacity and catalase (CAT) activity in egg yolk, elevated CAT and glutathione peroxidase activities in breeder hen serum, and higher superoxide dismutase activity in chick serum. In particular, the NAC-treated group showed significantly higher glutathione levels and lower malondialdehyde levels throughout the transition from breeder hens to offspring (P < 0.05). Furthermore, maternal NAC treatment decreased the relative mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines interferon-γ, interleukin 18, and interleukin 6 in the ileum of the offspring broilers (P < 0.05). In conclusion, maternal dietary supplementation with NAC can continuously enhance the antioxidant capability and intestinal barrier function of chicken offspring, while also improving egg quality, growth performance and slaughter performance.Keywords: N-acetylcysteine, Maternal effect, Antioxidant, Gut health

Introduction

Intensive farming practices increase poultry susceptibility to environmental stressors, heightening the risk of oxidative stress (Surai et al., 2019a). Oxidative stress generates excessive free radicals that attack biological macromolecules, including DNA, lipids, and proteins, causing cellular and tissue damage (Surai et al., 2019b). Crucially, it disrupts intestinal barrier integrity by impairing tight junctions, inducing epithelial apoptosis and triggering inflammation, thereby elevating risks of malabsorption, secondary infections and economic loss (Hong et al., 2024). Modern fast-growing broilers are facing a series of unique physiological challenges. Accelerated progress in genetic breeding has significantly improved economic traits, but has also disrupted the physiological balance of organisms, leading to a marked increase in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Tallentire et al., 2016). Furthermore, prolonged exposure to a complex stress environment depletes endogenous antioxidant defence systems even further (Siddiqui et al., 2020). These mechanisms act collectively to significantly increase the susceptibility of gut to oxidative damage (Çapar et al., 2022). However, traditional antioxidants, such as vitamin E (VE) and selenium (Se), have inherent limitations in addressing these challenges. Firstly, the lipophilic nature of VE causes it to accumulate primarily within biological membrane structures. This makes it difficult to reach regions within the cytoplasm and mitochondria that are rich in ROS (Su et al., 2025). Secondly, the antioxidant system comprising VE and Se is unable to replenish glutathione (GSH) reserves quickly (Jiang, 2014). Furthermore, VE is unable to repair oxidised tight junction proteins directly, which play a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier (Hu et al., 2018).

N-acetylcysteine (NAC), as a precursor to cysteine, enhances glutathione synthesis and replenishes intracellular GSH reserves, thereby offering a novel strategy for antioxidant protection (Turkmen et al., 2019). As the most abundant endogenous non-enzymatic antioxidant, GSH directly neutralizes ROS, including hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). It also serves as a substrate for glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) in catalyzing peroxide reduction (Zhitkovich., 2019). The advantages of NAC can be seen in the following aspects. Firstly, NAC can elevate cytoplasmic GSH levels, promoting its transport to mitochondria via pathways such as the mitochondrial oxidoreductase channel to directly scavenge ROS (Li et al., 2022). This overcomes the limitations of VE in mitochondrial delivery (Lash et al., 2006). Secondly, NAC-derived GSH boosts GSH-Px activity, creating a synergistic "NAC-GSH-GSH-Px" network (Kalyanaraman, 2022). Finally, It can downregulate NF-κB and modulate AMPK-α1/HIF-1α pathway (Li et al., 2022). These functions are absent in VE and Se. Studies on poultry have shown that NAC mitigates oxidative stress induced by cadmium by enhancing the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and GSH-Px (Zhang et al., 2018). Furthermore, these antioxidant effects contribute to improved intestinal health by promoting enterocyte growth and stimulating protein synthesis (Yi et al., 2016).

Aging in breeder hens impairs yolk antioxidant reserves, adversely affecting embryonic and early offspring development, particularly intestinal maturation (Buss, 2021; Gao et al., 2023). Given the reliance of newly hatched chicks on maternal nutrition reserves, dietary intervention in breeder hens is crucial. Maternal nutrition profoundly impacts offspring growth, metabolism, intestinal development, and microbiota (Gao et al., 2025; Lv et al., 2018). Maternal VE supplementation has been shown to maintain the antioxidant capacity of eggs during storage (Yang et al., 2021), and in ovo amino acid injection can enhance the antioxidant status of chicks (Elnesr et al., 2019). However, the maternal effects of ditery NAC on the antioxidant capacity and growth performance of offspring remains insufficiently investigated.

Therefore, this study investigates the regulatory effects of dietary NAC supplementation on the antioxidant capacity and production performance of breeder hens and their offspring, aiming to reduce the impact of oxidative stress on the poultry production through maternal nutritional interventions.

Materials and methods

Trial design and broiler feeding

A total of 240 WOD188 breeder hens aged 43 weeks were randomly assigned to two dietary treatments, with six replicates per treatment and 20 hens per replicate (2 hens per cage). The control (CON) group received a corn-soybean meal basal diet, while the experimental (NAC) group received the basal diet supplemented with 1 g/kg N-acetylcysteine (99% purity, Shandong Changxiao Bioengineering Co., Ltd., China). All breeder hens were housed in an environmentally controlled facility at the Huadu Yukou Breeder Farm (Beijing, China). The feeding trial lasted for 4 weeks.

All breeder hens were artificially inseminated. Semen was collected from a cohort of fifty healthy breeding roosters. Insemination was performed at five-day intervals, with each hen receiving an intravaginal dose containing approximately 100–150 million viable sperm per insemination. On the final three days of the experimental period, 300 fertilized eggs per treatment group were collected for incubation. Eggs were surface-sanitized by fumigation prior to setting.

After hatching, a total of 120 male chicks per treatment group were transferred to a grow-out facility. Chicks were allocated to pens according to their maternal dietary treatment, maintaining six replicates of 20 birds per group. Each repetition is conducted in an individual cage, and all the cages are randomly distributed within the chicken coop. All chicks had ad libitum access to the same starter and grower diets, as well as water. The post-hatch trial lasted 6 weeks. All husbandry practices complied with the standard operating procedures of the facility. The composition of the breeder and broiler diets is presented in Table 1. The broiler diets were formulated to meet the nutrient specifications outlined in the National Chicken Feeding Standards of China (NY/T 33-2004). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of China Agricultural University (Approval No. AW31105202-1-3).

Laying performance and hatchability

Egg laying rate (%), average egg weight (g), and feed conversion ratio (FCR, per kg egg mass) were calculated on a per replicate basis. Total egg number and egg weight per replicate were recorded daily during the 4-week trial period. For each replicate, the following parameters were recorded: fertility rate (%), hatchability of fertile eggs (%), chick hatch weight (g). The calculated results represent the average values over a period of 1 to 4 weeks. The calculation formulas were as follows:

Egg laying rate (%) = [Total number of eggs laid / (Number of hens × Number of days)] × 100

Fertility rate (%) = [(Number of set eggs − Number of infertile eggs) / Number of set eggs] × 100

Hatchability of fertile eggs (%) = (Number of hatched chicks / Number of fertile eggs) × 100

Egg quality measurement

At the end of the fourth week, feeding was continued for three additional days and 20 breeding eggs were randomly selected from each replicate for egg quality determination (first two days) and one breeding egg from each replicate was randomly selected for yolk collection (third day). The following parameters were measured. Egg weight (g) was recorded using an electronic balance. Albumen height (mm), Haugh units, and yolk color (DSM YolkFan™ scale) were measured using a SONOVA™ Egg Quality Analyzer (Orka Food Technology Ltd., Hampshire, UK). Eggshell breaking strength (N) was defined as the maximum force required to fracture the shell, measured using an Egg Force Reader (Orka Food Technology Ltd., Hampshire, UK). Egg shape index was calculated as the ratio of maximum width to maximum length, measured using a digital caliper. Eggshell thickness (mm) was measured using a tube-type micrometer (precision 0.01 mm) at three equidistant points on the shell (blunt pole, sharp pole, and equatorial region) after removal of the inner shell membrane. The mean value of these three measurements was recorded.

Growth performance of broiler

The body weight and feed refusal of broiler were recorded for each replicate at 7, 14, 21, and 42 days of age. The following prduction performance indexes were calculated for each growth phase: body weight (BW), average daily body weight gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), feed conversion ratio (FCR).

Slaughter performance

On day 42 of the broiler trial, two birds per replicate whose body weight within ±5% of the group mean were selected. These birds were fasted for 10 hours with ad libitum water access, weighed (live weight) and then slaughtered humanely via cervical dislocation. Carcasses were processed as follows. Semi-eviscerated carcass weight is the weight after removing the head, feet, feathers, blood, and visceral organs (heart, liver, proventriculus, gizzard, lungs, spleen, intestines, and abdominal fat). Eviscerated carcass weight is the weight after further removing the trachea, esophagus, crop, pancreas, gallbladder, reproductive tract, and residual digesta from the gastrointestinal tract. The tissue components were individually weighed: abdominal fat (the adipose tissue surrounding the cloaca and abdominal muscles), breast muscle (pectoralis major and minor), wing (humerus, radius, and ulna with attached musculature) and leg muscle. Yield parameters were calculated as follows:

Carcass Rate (%) = (Carcass Weight / Live Weight) × 100

Semi-Eviscerated Rate (%) = (Semi-Eviscerated Carcass Weight / Live Weight) × 100

Eviscerated Rate (%) = (Eviscerated Carcass Weight / Live Weight) × 100

Breast Muscle Rate (%) = (Breast Muscle Weight / Eviscerated Carcass Weight) × 100

Leg Muscle Rate (%) = (Leg Muscle Weight / Eviscerated Carcass Weight) × 100

Wing Rate (%) = (Wing Weight/ Eviscerated Carcass Weight) × 100

Abdominal Fat Rate (%) = (Abdominal Fat Weight / Carcass Weight) × 100

Sample collection

Ileal tissues were collected from chicken embryos at embryonic day 17 (ED17) and broilers at 1, 7, and 14 days post-hatch, with one chick sampled per replicate (total n=6/group/timepoint). The mid-ileum was isolated, flushed with ice-cold 0.9% phosphate-buffered saline using a sterile wash bottle to remove luminal contents, and approximately 1-2 cm segments excised from the distal end. Tissues designated for molecular analysis were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C. Parallel samples for histology were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (4°C, 24 hours) for paraffin embedding. Concurrently, at the end of the 4-week breeder trial, blood was collected from the wing vein of hens selected based on egg production records (laying rate ± group mean). Broiler blood samples were obtained at 1, 7, and 14 days post-hatch. All blood samples were clotted, centrifuged (3,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), and resulting serum aliquots stored at -20°C.

Ileal villus morphology

Following fixation, intestinal tissues underwent graded ethanol dehydration, were cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin using an automated tissue processor (Leica EG1150H). Serial sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared with a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235), mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Morphometric analysis was performed using a light microscope (Leica DM750) at 10 × magnification. Ten intact, vertically oriented villi per sample were randomly selected for measurement. Villus height (VH) was defined as the distance from the crypt-villus junction to the villus tip and crypt depth (CD) was measured from the base to the crypt-villus transition (n=6 samples/group, 60 measurements/group). The villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was calculated.

Determination of antioxidant indicators in serum, egg yolk, and ileal tissue

Egg yolks and ileal tissues were homogenized using a high-throughput tissue grinder, while serum was used directly for measurements. The activity of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, A005-1-2) and superoxide dismutase (SOD, A001-3-2), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, A015-2-1), malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1-2) content, glutathione (GSH, A006-2-1) content and total protein quantitative (TP, A045-2-2) determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from ileal tissues using Trizol reagent. The purity and quantity of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer at 260 nm/280 nm. High-quality RNA samples (1 μg) were reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ Kit (TaKaRa, Kyoto, Japan). Primer sequences are shown in Table 2. RT-qPCR was performed using a 7500 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). Relative quantification of target gene expression was normalized to the reference gene β-actin using the 2⁻∆∆Ct method.

Data statistics and analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent samples T-tests. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with statistical significance defined at P < 0.05. Trends were noted when 0.05 < P < 0.10. Data visualization was generated using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

As shown in Fig. 2, the addition of NAC to the diet improved some antioxidant indices in the serum of both breeder hens and broiler chicks, with this effect gradually diminishing over time. In the NAC group, the enzyme activity levels of CAT and GSH-Px, as well as the GSH content in the serum of breeder hens, showed significant increases (P < 0.05). Additionally, the MDA content was significantly reduced (P < 0.05). Discussion

Oxidative stress represents a critical constraint that impairs immune function and growth performance in breeder poultry, with detrimental effects that can be transmitted intergenerationally to offspring (Khan et al., 2025). This study showed that dietary NAC supplementation effectively built a multi-level antioxidant defense system across maternal birds, eggs, and offspring. This mechanism offers a fundamental basis for improving reproductive performance in breeders and the vitality of offspring. At the breeder hen level, NAC significantly improved systemic antioxidant capacity, as shown by increased serum activities of CAT and GSH-Px alongside reduced MDA concentrations. These findings directly confirm the efficacy of NAC in alleviating ROS-induced oxidative damage, consistent with Wang et al.'s observations in chicken embryonic fibroblasts, thereby establishing a physiological foundation for maintaining hen health and productivity (Wang et al., 2023). Crucially, this antioxidant enhancement was effectively transferred to eggs, as evidenced by the significant increases in GSH content, T-AOC, and CAT activity in the yolk. As an endogenous non-enzymatic antioxidant and key enzyme cofactor, elevated GSH levels substantially fortified the antioxidant reservoir capacity of egg yolks (Liu et al., 2014). Enhanced T-AOC further reflected synergistic upregulation of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense systems. This optimized yolk antioxidant microenvironment constitutes the principal endogenous protection and nutritional source for embryonic development (Liu et al., 2020; Mishra et al., 2019). Most significantly, the antioxidant benefits derived from NAC persisted in offspring chicks. The characteristic profiles of serum antioxidant enzyme activity demonstrated a clear maternal effect, in line with report of the upregulation of hepatic antioxidant genes in the offspring of antioxidants-supplemented broiler breeders (Pasri et al., 2025). Our study extends this paradigm by providing direct evidence of the transgenerational effect of NAC on antioxidant status in chicken offspring. Notably, the reduced serum MDA levels in the offspring indicated attenuated embryonic oxidative stress, equipping the chicks with an enhanced capacity to confront post-hatch oxidative challenges. This early-life antioxidant potentiation likely underlies the observed improvements in offspring vitality and growth performance (Hassan Nawaz and Zhang, 2021).

As hens enter the late laying phase, diminished physiological regulation increases their susceptibility to oxidative stress, consequently reducing egg production and compromising egg quality (Du et al., 2024). Eggs from 32-week-old breeder hens exhibit significantly higher albumen height and Haugh units than those from 60-week-old hens (Alo et al., 2024). Oxidative stress induces ovalbumen denaturation and aggregation, impairing its water-retention capacity and gel structure integrity, ultimately leading to thin albumen and reduced Haugh units (Lévy et al., 2019). Although dietary antioxidants can partially ameliorate these effects, practical application requires consideration of compound characteristics, bioavailability, and transfer efficiency from feed to egg (Jing et al., 2022). In this study, NAC supplementation significantly increased albumen height and Haugh units. We speculate that the improvement may be due to the fact that NAC enhances the deposition of GSH in the yolk. Increased levels of GSH help protect ovalbumin from oxidative damage, thereby maintaining its native conformation and functionality. This ultimately contributes to maintain the albumen viscosity and proportion, thereby elevating Haugh units. This hypothesis is supported by elevated yolk GSH levels. Similarly, theabrownins, active compounds extracted from Pu-erh tea, increase the concentrations of vitamin A and E in the yolk of Lohmann Brown layers after 12 weeks of feeding, which enhances their antioxidant capacity (Wang et al., 2023). The precise mechanism by which NAC promotes yolk GSH deposition warrants further investigation.

Furthermore, aging impairs ovarian function and induces oxidative stress in laying hens. Elevated ROS levels compromise gamete functionality by damaging DNA integrity and disrupting lipid-protein components of cell membranes, ultimately limiting fertilization potential (Wang et al., 2025). Homocysteine supplementation has been shown to enhance fertilization rates in avian oocytes, improve early embryonic development, and reduce late-stage embryonic mortality (Santos et al., 2019). In the current study, NAC similarly increased egg fertilization rates. This effect may be related to the protective mechanisms of NAC, as reported that NAC attenuates bisphenol A (BPA)-induced suppression of oocyte nuclear maturation, counteracts BPA-reduced fertilization and blastocyst formation rates, and mitigates ROS accumulation alongside GSH depletion (Li et al., 2019). Furthermore, NAC supplementation during oocyte enhances mitochondrial membrane polarization and overall oocyte quality (Matilla et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest that NAC likely improves oocyte competence and fertilization success through ROS scavenging and optimization of the yolk antioxidant microenvironment.

The redox status profoundly influences embryonic development by modulating cellular behavior, signaling pathways, and energy metabolism throughout ontogenesis. The 21-day embryonic phase constitutes approximately 50% of the broiler production cycle, during which egg nutrients serve as critical metabolic fuel (De et al., 2008). Notably, the embryonic lethality during gastrulation in glutamate-cysteine ligase knockout mice, a phenomenon attributable to impaired GSH synthesis. This underscores that GSH plays an essential role beyond just antioxidation and detoxification in developmental processes (Markovic et al., 2007). This is further supported by observations that methionine injection into albumen increases chick weight, potentially through improved embryonic antioxidant status (Nazem et al., 2017). In our study, breeder NAC supplementation showed a trend toward increasing the body weight of 1 day-old chicks concomitant with elevated yolk GSH levels. This suggests that NAC or its metabolites, such as cysteine or GSH, are likely transferred and deposited into the yolk via maternal nutrition, thereby optimizing the embryonic redox environment and protecting developing tissues from oxidative damage. Analogously, synthetic antioxidants in breeder diets, such as vitamins E and C, can improve offspring uniformity and reduce embryonic mortality by scavenging free radicals in yolk and hepatic tissues (Pasri et al., 2025).

Furthermore, maternal NAC supplementation significantly reduced the FCR in offspring broilers during the first week age and improved carcass yield and slaughter performance at 42 days. As a direct cysteine precursor, NAC provides essential substrates for muscle protein synthesis while sparing methionine for other anabolic pathways. Through transsulfuration, cysteine-derived methionine enhances maternal methionine levels, thereby modulating the expression of genes involved in amino acid transport, metabolism, and one-carbon cycling, ultimately influencing muscle development in offspring (Palmer et al., 2021). This aligns with the report that breeder methionine supplementation elevates antioxidant capacity in offspring thigh muscle and suppresses lipid oxidation (Zhang et al., 2024). Mechanistically, NAC reduces ROS accumulation in murine skeletal muscle (García-Campos et al., 2020), and protects against heat stress-induced myofiber damage by accelerating proliferation and differentiation (Palmer et al., 2021). Therefore, maternal NAC supplementation likely enhances the slaughter performance in 42-day-old offspring broilers through improved systemic antioxidant capacity and promoted myofiber development.

Oxidative stress frequently coincides with cellular apoptosis and immune suppression (Shah et al., 2020). Studies indicate that NAC mitigates acrylamide-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades in PC12 cells through synergistic activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway and suppression of NF-κB activation (Pan et al., 2018). Furthermore, NAC treatment significantly upregulates heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression in fibroblasts, identifying HO-1 as a downstream molecular target in NAC-mediated regulation (Kartha et al., 2015). Consistent with the phenomena observed in this study, the embryonic ileum at 17 days of incubation exhibited significantly reduced mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and IL-18. A decreasing trend was also observed for IL-10 and IL-6 mRNA in 7-day-old chick ileum. In summary, the significant reduction in pro-inflammatory and immunoregulatory cytokine levels suggests that NAC may alleviate intestinal inflammation and regulate local immune homeostasis by activating the Nrf2/NF-κB signalling pathway. Furthermore, the maternal effect on the development and health may be regulated by epigenetic modificaton that warrant further investigation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, dietary supplementation of NAC for breeder hens enhanced the antioxidant capacity of both the hens and their offspring. It also improved the egg quality of breeder hens, as well as the intestinal health and slaughter performance of offspring. We can utilize the continuous maternal antioxidant effect of NAC to improve offspring health and development, which expands the strategies for maternal-offspring nutrition intervention.
