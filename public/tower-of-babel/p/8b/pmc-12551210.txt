High-acceleration pancreatobiliary MRI with deep learning-based super-resolution reconstruction for evaluating presumed pancreatic intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm
Authors: Sun Kyung Jeon, Jeong Min Lee, Junghoan Park, Sungjun Hwang, Rae Rim Ryu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the feasibility and diagnostic utility of a deep learning (DL)-based super-resolution (SR) reconstruction algorithm applied to pancreatobiliary MRI for assessing pancreatic intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs).MethodsThis retrospective study included 162 patients with presumed pancreatic IPMN (≥ 1 cm) who underwent pancreatobiliary MRI between May 2019 and May 2022. Two portal venous phase (PVP) images of dynamic T1-wegithed imaging were sequentially acquired: early PVP image obtained using standard compressed sensing (CS)-volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) (standard CS-VIBE) and late PVP image obtained using CS-VIBE with DL-based SR reconstruction algorithm to generate 1 mm-thickness images (DL-SR CS-VIBE). Arterial phase and 3-min delayed phase were also acquired using DL-SR CS-VIBE. The image quality of standard and DL-SR CS-VIBE PVP sequences was compared using Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The diagnostic performance of full-sequence pancreatobiliaryMRI including DL-SR CS-VIBE for predicting malignant IPMN was assessed using multi-reader multi-case analysis. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed using receiver operating characteristic analysis, while sensitivity and specificity were estimated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.ResultsAmong 162 patients, 15 had malignant IPMN, while 147 had benign IPMN. DL-SR CS-VIBE demonstrated significantly better overall image quality (3.73 ± 0.33 vs. 3.22 ± 0.43) and cystic lesion conspicuity (3.37 ± 0.50 vs. 2.71 ± 0.52) than standard CS-VIBE (all Ps < 0.001). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for predicting malignant IPMN was 0.858 (95% CI: 0.807, 0.909). Using the presence of high-risk stigmata as an indicator of test-positive, pooled sensitivity and pooled specificity of pancreatobiliary MRI including DL-SR CS-VIBE for malignant IPMN were 71.1% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 55.7, 83.6) and 82.8% (95% CI: 78.9, 86.2), respectively. Among MRI features, diagnostic accuracy was highest for mural nodules ≥ 5 mm (AUC, 0.736) and main pancreatic duct size ≥ 10 mm (AUC, 0.720).ConclusionPancreatobiliary MRI with DL-SR CS-VIBE enhances image quality and lesion conspicuity, offering promising diagnostic accuracy for malignant IPMN, though further studies with larger cohorts are needed to refine these findings and evaluate clinical impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-025-00932-7.

Background

Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) of the pancreas is defined by the intraductal proliferation of mucin-secreting cells originating from the main pancreatic duct (MPD) or its branches [1]. Histologically, pancreatic IPMNs demonstrate a spectrum of dysplastic changes, ranging from low-grade to high-grade dysplasia, and may progress to invasive carcinoma in certain cases [2]. The accurate determination of the malignancy potential in IPMNs is essential for selecting appropriate management strategy, as the course of treatment varies based on malignancy risk. To aid this, the International Association of Pancreatology published guidelines in 2006, with revisions in 2012, 2017, and 2024 [3, 4]. These guidelines emphasize the importance of identifying high-risk stigmata and worrisome features, such as enhancing mural nodules, MPD dilatation, and thickened or enhancing cyst walls, to assess the likelihood of malignancy [3, 4].

Because low-grade IPMNs have a risk of evolving into invasive carcinoma, the guidelines recommend lifelong surveillance for these lesions [3–5]. Imaging modalities, particularly CT and MRI with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), are essential for evaluating IPMN. Pancreatobiliary MRI (MRI with MRCP) is preferred over CT due to its superior soft tissue contrast and the absence of radiation exposure, making it the imaging modality of choice for surveillance and assessment [5, 6].

Advances in MRI technology have focused on improving spatial resolution and reducing acquisition times, which are critical for capturing detailed anatomical features while ensuring patient comfort. Techniques such as under-sampling of the k-space, including parallel imaging techniques such as sensitivity encoding, generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisition, controlled aliasing in parallel imaging results in higher acceleration, as well as compressed sensing (CS), have been explored to achieve these goals [7]. Previous studies that demonstrated the clinical application of the CS techniques or parallel imaging with CS techniques in abdominal MRI reported that shorter scanning times and higher spatial resolution can be acquired [8–11]. Despite conventional CS and parallel imaging combined with CS techniques, have substantially improved acquisition efficiency, these advancements often come at the cost of reduced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and image quality, particularly in high-resolution imaging, thereby diminishing lesion conspicuity and detection accuracy [7, 12]. In recent years, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a promising solution to address these challenges. DL-based super-resolution (SR) algorithms, particularly in 3D T1-weighted gradient-recalled-echo (GRE) sequences, have shown potential for improving image quality and lesion conspicuity while reducing scan times [13, 14]. While these advancements have been demonstrated in other abdominal MRI applications [15–18], limited evidence exists on their utility specifically for pancreatobiliary MRI.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and diagnostic utility of a DL-based SR reconstruction algorithm applied to pancreatobiliary MRI for assessing pancreatic IPMNs.

Methods

This retrospective single-center study received approval from the institutional review board of Seoul National University Hospital, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived (IRB No. H-2206-108-1333).

Study population

The study population was identified through a computerized search of our institution’s radiologic database. Patients who underwent pancreatobiliary MRI using a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) for the evaluation of presumed pancreatic IPMN between May 2019 and May 2022 were consecutively included. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who underwent pancreatobiliary MRI using a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra) including DL-based SR reconstruction algorithm for presumed pancreatic IPMN (≥ 1 cm); (2) Patients who had a reference standard for malignant IPMN, determined by either histopathologic results or imaging follow-up. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pancreatic IPMN < 1 cm; (2) patients who had previous treatment for pancreatic IPMN; and (3) no reference standard for pancreatic IPMN due to the absence of histologic confirmation or follow-up of at least 24 months. MRI acquisition

MRI examinations were performed using on a 3T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Pancreatobiliary MRI protocols included axial and coronal heavily T2-weighted imaging with breath-hold, two-dimensional (2D) MRCP using single-shot turbo spin echo, breath-hold and respiratory gating 3D MRCP, dual echo 3D Dixon GRE T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), diffusion-weighted imaging using two b-values (0, 1000 s/mm2), precontrast and dynamic T1-weighted imaging using a standard dose of extracellular contrast media (Gadovist, Bayer, Leverkusen, GVIermany). Dynamic imaging was performed using a 3D CS-volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination (VIBE) following contrast media injection. The protocol included dual arterial phase (AP) including early and late AP, two portal venous phases (PVP), and 3 min delayed phases (DP). The timing of AP was determined by a real-time bolus-tracking technique with MR fluoroscopic monitoring. Dual AP and 3 min DP images were acquired using CS-VIBE with DL-based SR reconstruction algorithm (DL-SR CS-VIBE). For PVP, two images were sequentially acquired: (a) a standard CS-VIBE PVP image with an acceleration factor of 4 and (b) a high resolution-PVP image obtained using DL-SR CS-VIBE, achieving 1 mm slice thickness. CS and DL-based SR reconstruction technique

The applied DL algorithms are described in detail in a previous study [15, 18]. In brief, for the first step, the CS-VIBE images were acquired within a single breath-hold, utilizing a total acceleration factor of 9.95, 8.05, and 8.05 for dual AP, PVP, and 3 min DP, respectively. Pseudorandom incoherent k-space sampling was implemented using a Poisson-disk pattern along the phase encoding directions [19] The second reconstruction stage employed a DL-based SR reconstruction algorithm, which upsampled images 2-fold in all spatial dimensions and performed partial Fourier reconstruction along the slice direction, as per the acquisition protocol [15, 17, 18]. Supervised training used high-resolution images as ground-truth references, with input data created by downscaling these images 2-fold in all dimensions [18]. The algorithm used in this study is a work-in-progress sequence developed by Siemens Healthineers and is currently limited to research applications.

Image analysis

Image analysis was independently performed by three abdominal radiologists (J.P., S.H., and R.R.R. with 5 years of experience in the interpretation of abdominal MR imaging).

Image quality assessment of the two PVP image sets

Image quality was independently assessed for the two PVP sequences (standard CS-VIBE and DL-SR CS-VIBE)—with reviewers blinded to the sequence type. Each reviewer evaluated both image sets in randomized order during two separate sessions, with a two-week washout period between sessions to minimize recall bias. For image quality assessment, liver and pancreas margin sharpness, motion or susceptibility artifacts, cystic lesion conspicuity, and overall image quality were rated on a 4-point scale. The scoring criteria for liver and pancreas margin sharpness were: 1, nondiagnostic; 2, moderate blurring; 3, mild blurring; and 4, excellent. Motion or susceptibility artifacts were graded as follows: 1, nondiagnostic; 2, severe artifacts with substantial image quality reduction; 3, mild artifacts with mild image quality reduction; and 4, minimal or no artifacts. Cystic lesion conspicuity was rated as follows: 1, poor; 2, fair delineation; 3, good delineation; and 4, excellent delineation. Overall image quality was evaluated as: 1, nondiagnostic; 2, poor (below average); 3, fair (average image quality in practice); and 4, good quality without significant artifacts. The mean scores from three independent reviewers were calculated to obtain representative values for each sequence.

Evaluation of cystic lesions

The evaluation of cystic lesions was conducted through a comprehensive review of the full pancreatobiliary MRI protocol, including T2-weighted imaging, precontrast T1-weighted imaging, MRCP, dynamic contrast-enhanced imaging acquired with DL-SR CS-VIBE (AP, PVP, and DP), and DWI. Reviewers assessed the presence or absence of high-risk stigmata and worrisome features in accordance with the recent 2024 international consensus guidelines [3]. If a patient had multiple lesions, the most suspicious lesion (a cystic lesion showing any high-risk stigmata or, in their absence, worrisome features) was prioritized for evaluation. If no high-risk stigmata or worrisome features were identified, the largest observed cyst was analyzed. High-risk stigmata included (1) the presence of enhancing mural nodules ≥ 5 mm, and (2) MPD size ≥ 10 mm. Worrisome features included: (1) cyst size ≥ 3 cm, (2) enhancing mural nodule < 5 mm, (3) MPD size of 5–9 mm, (4) thickened or enhancing cyst walls, and (5) abrupt MPD caliber change with distal pancreatic atrophy.

The malignant potential of IPMN was scored using a five-point scale: score 5, the presence of at least one high-risk stigmata; score 4, three or more worrisome features; score 3, two worrisome features; score 2, any worrisome feature; and score 1, the absence of both worrisome features and high-risk stigmata [20].

Reference standard

The reference standard for pancreatic IPMN was determined through either histopathological results or follow-up imaging. For patients who underwent surgical resection or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided biopsy, histopathological findings served as the reference standard. Based on World Health Organization guidelines, IPMNs were categorized as benign (low- or intermediate-grade dysplasia) or malignant (high-grade dysplasia or invasive carcinoma) [2]. In cases where multiple pathological types were present, the most aggressive type was designated as the primary tumor type. For patients without histopathological data, the presence or absence of pancreatic malignancy was assessed via imaging follow-up within 24–36 months following the MRI by a radiologist (S.K.J.) who did not involve the image review session. If imaging features remained stable for over two years, the lesions were considered benign.

Statistical analysis

For comparison of image quality between two PVP sequences (Standard CS-VIBE and DL-SR CS-VIBE), Wilcoxon signed-rank test was performed. The diagnostic performance of pancreatobiliary MRI including DL-SR CS-VIBE sequences for diagnosing malignant IPMN was assessed using multi-reader multi-case analysis [21]. To assess diagnostic accuracy, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to calculate the area under the ROC curve (AUC), while sensitivity and specificity were estimated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Pooled estimates of sensitivities and specificities were assessed using Obuchowski–Rockette approach, with both cases and readers treated as random effects. Interobserver agreements for image quality assessment, MRI features and five-point scale were assessed using Fleiss κ statistics [22]. The agreement was interpreted as follows: >0.80, excellent; 0.60–0.79, substantial; 0.40–0.59, moderate; 0.20–0.49, fair; and < 0.20, poor [23]. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and MedCalc (version 20.218; MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). A P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

A total of 162 patients (88 male and 74 female; mean age, 67.3 ± 9.9 years) were included (Table 2). Image quality assessment

The mean overall image quality was higher in DL-SR CS-VIBE compared to standard CS-VIBE (3.73 ± 0.33 vs. 3.22 ± 0.43, P < 0.001). DL-SR CS-VIBE demonstrated higher organ margin sharpness and less motion or susceptibility artifact compared to standard CS-VIBE (3.69 ± 0.26 vs. 3.11 ± 0.39; and 3.72 ± 0.34 vs. 3.23 ± 0.41; Ps < 0.001, respectively). Cystic lesion conspicuity was also higher in DL-SR CS-VIBE compared to standard CS-VIBE (3.37 ± 0.50 vs. 2.71 ± 0.52, P < 0.001) (Table 3; Figs. 2 and 3). Interobserver agreement for image quality assessment was moderate to substantial (κ = 0.470–0.664) (Supplementary Table 1).

Diagnostic performance for predicting malignant IPMN

Using a five-point scale, the AUC for predicting malignant IPMN was 0.858 (95% CI: 0.807, 0.909). Using the presence of high-risk stigmata as an indicator of test positive (score of 5), the pooled sensitivity was 71.1% (95% CI: 55.7, 83.6) for diagnosing malignant IPMN and the pooled specificity was 82.8% (95% CI: 78.9, 86.2) (Table 4).

Among MRI features, including high-risk stigmata and worrisome features, the diagnostic accuracies for predicting malignant IPMN were highest for enhancing mural nodules ≥ 5 mm (AUC, 0.736 [95% CI: 0.661, 0.802]), followed by MPD size ≥ 10 mm (AUC, 0.720 [95% CI: 0.644, 0.787]), with pooled sensitivities of 53.3% (95% CI: 29.3, 75.9) and 47.0% (95% CI: 24.0, 71.0) and pooled specificities of 88.4% (95% CI: 82.2, 92.7) and 97.3% (95% CI: 93.0, 99.0), respectively (Table 5 and Supplementary Table 2).

Regarding high-risk stigmata, the interobserver agreement was moderate for enhancing mural nodule ≥ 5 mm (κ = 0.523 [95% CI: 0.387, 0.658]) and substantial for MPD size ≥ 10 mm (κ = 0.675 [95% CI: 0.497, 0.853]). Discussion

This study demonstrated the clinical feasibility DL-SR CS-VIBE sequences for evaluating presumed pancreatic IPMN by providing a clinically practical high-resolution imaging dataset with a 1 mm slice thickness. Compared to standard CS-VIBE, the DL-based approach significantly improved image quality and cystic lesion conspicuity while reducing acquisition times. Achieving high-resolution imaging with shorter acquisition times may offer clinical benefits for pancreatobiliary MRI, particularly in the precise characterization of malignant IPMN through enhanced visualization of features such as mural nodules and ductal dilatation, supporting timely diagnosis and management [3]. While the reduction in acquisition time was modest, the associated improvements in spatial resolution and image quality may yield meaningful clinical benefits, especially in patients with limited breath-hold capacity. These findings underscore the potential of DL-based SR algorithms to enhance pancreatobiliary MRI by improving diagnostic confidence in assessing IPMN malignancy risk. Given their superior performance, DL-based SR techniques could be more widely adopted in routine clinical practice, offering a compelling alternative to conventional methods such as parallel imaging or CS.

Our findings align with prior studies, highlighting the effectiveness of DL-based SR reconstruction in abdominal MRI. Chaika et al. demonstrated that in pancreatic MRI, DL-based SR reconstruction combined with iterative denoising provided superior performance compared to standard VIBE imaging [16]. Also, Wessling et al. reported the technical feasibility of a DL-based SR algorithm incorporating partial Fourier techniques in abdominopelvic MRI, showing significant improvements in image quality, noise reduction, and sharpness, along with decreased acquisition time [17]. Although CS combined with parallel imaging accelerates MRI speed more effectively than parallel imaging alone, it often results in SNR loss and imaging artifacts [8]. In contrast, DL-based SR reconstruction mitigates these limitations by reducing noise and enhancing lesion visibility [12, 15, 24]. Furthermore, by adopting an in-line reconstruction method instead of the off-line process used in previous studies, we significantly improved clinical applicability and operational efficiency, although further validation in larger multicenter prospective studies is required to confirm its broader applicability.

Our study expands on these findings with two notable technical advancements. First, we utilized an acceleration factor of 8.05 for DL-SR CS-VIBE for portal venous and delayed phases, achieving high spatial resolution imaging with a 1 mm slice thickness, compared to the standard CS-VIBE with 3 mm slice thickness, which uses an acceleration factor of 4. This improvement was achieved through the advanced noise reduction capabilities of the DL-based SR algorithm, which outperforms the conventional CS imaging technique. Furthermore, the acquisition time with DL-SR CS-VIBE was reduced to 14 s compared to the conventional 17 s, offering a substantial advantage in mitigating motion-related artifacts. This improvement is particularly beneficial for patients with limited breath-holding capacity. Second, we obtained high-resolution dual arterial phase imaging in a single breath-hold, where the early arterial phase was tailored for evaluating key arterial structures surrounding the pancreas, and the late arterial phase provided optimal timing for detailed assessment of the pancreatic parenchyma. Furthermore, the DL-based SR reconstruction technique enables the acquisition of near-isotropic voxel sizes and enhances the visualization of peripancreatic vasculature and small anatomical structures, including mural nodules and pancreatic ducts. Using 1-mm-slice thickness high resolution image sets generated by the DL-based SR technique, high-quality multiplanar reformation (MPR) is also feasible, further improving diagnostic precision. Considering that the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend thin-section CT with MPR for pancreatic cancer evaluation [25], our high-resolution MR sequence, incorporating thin-slice axial images and MPR, demonstrates significant potential as a valuable tool for comprehensive pancreatic assessment. These advancements could be useful for the precise characterization of IPMNs and the accurate assessment of tumor extent in malignant IPMNs.

This study seeks to evaluate the diagnostic performance of pancreatobiliary MRI incorporating DL-based SR reconstruction, specifically for the assessment of malignancy in pancreatic IPMNs. Our analysis demonstrated an AUC of 0.858 (95% CI: 0.807–0.909) for predicting malignant IPMNs using pancreatobiliary MRI. In comparison, Choi et al. reported lower AUCs of 0.742 and 0.776 for conventional MRI, indicating moderate diagnostic performance [26]. This comparison underscores the potential benefits of incorporating DL-based SR reconstruction into the CS-VIBE sequence, leveraging higher acceleration factors exceeding 8 to improve risk stratification in IPMN evaluation. The improved performance of our high-resolution pancreatobiliary protocol for evaluating malignant IPMNs can be attributed to the integration of advanced DL algorithms into the reconstruction process. The DL-based SR-CS-VIBE technique enhances spatial resolution, reduces acquisition times, and effectively minimizes noise and artifacts. These advancements enable the precise detection of small enhancing nodules within ducts or cystic structures, which are key predictors of malignancy. However, in this study, we found that certain worrisome features—such as enhancing mural nodules < 5 mm, thickened or enhancing cyst walls, and abrupt main pancreatic duct caliber changes—showed significantly low interobserver agreement, consistent with a previous study [20]. This was observed despite the high spatial resolution of super-resolution pancreatobiliary protocol, highlighting inherent challenges in the evaluation of pancreatic cystic lesions.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Second, the sample size of malignant IPMN cases was relatively small, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, we included only lesions ≥ 1 cm, which are more clinically relevant for malignancy assessment. However, this limits evaluation of DL-SR CS-VIBE for sub-centimeter lesions, where its high spatial resolution may offer added value. Third, the study was conducted at a single institution using a specific scanner and protocol, limiting broader applicability. Future studies should validate these findings in multicenter settings and investigate the performance of DL-based super-resolution in other pancreatic pathologies. Fourth, A direct comparison between DL-SR and standard CS-VIBE for predicting malignancy was not performed; thus, superiority of DL-SR reconstruction cannot be concluded from our results. Future dedicated comparative studies are warranted. Fifth, although DL-SR CS-VIBE improved image quality, its impact on clinical decision-making or outcomes was not evaluated. Further clinical validation is needed.

Conclusions

Pancreatobiliary MRI with DL-SR CS-VIBE enhances image quality and lesion conspicuity, offering promising diagnostic accuracy for malignant IPMN, though further studies with larger cohorts are needed to refine these findings and evaluate clinical impact.
