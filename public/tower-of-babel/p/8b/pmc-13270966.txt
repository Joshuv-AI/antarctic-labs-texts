Recognizing PRKAG2 syndrome in a patient with ventricular preexcitation, atrioventricular block, and left ventricular hypertrophy: A case report

Introduction

PRKAG2 syndrome (PS) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by ventricular preexcitation (VP), hypertrophic remodeling of the ventricles, and progressive conduction system disease.1,2 This condition stems from pathogenic mutations in the PRKAG2 gene, which disrupt the function of the γ2 regulatory subunit of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK).3 Such perturbations lead to aberrant AMPK signaling and subsequent intracellular glycogen accumulation within cardiomyocytes.4

Owing to its multifaceted electrophysiological manifestations, PS confers a substantial risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD), with certain studies reporting an incidence of up to 20% in individuals under 40 years of age.5 Despite phenotypic overlap with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), its prognosis is less favorable. Consequently, prompt recognition, precise diagnosis, and active intervention are essential to improve clinical outcomes.

In this case report, we present a case of PS with a complex clinical presentation. Through comprehensive multimodal evaluation and family genetic studies, the definitive diagnosis was established, underscoring the critical role of integrated imaging and genetics in disease diagnosis and risk stratification and highlighting the imperative for tailored long-term surveillance.

Case report

A 25-year-old man presented with a sudden, transient loss of consciousness that resolved spontaneously within seconds. Over the preceding 5 years, he had experienced recurrent episodes of palpitations triggered by emotional stress or sleep deprivation. An electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained during a routine physical examination indicated left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH). One year before admission, he developed fulminant myocarditis after an influenza A virus infection and was diagnosed as having atrioventricular block (AVB), requiring intra-aortic balloon pump support. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at that time revealed features consistent with HCM. However, he did not receive any treatment or regular monitoring. His medical history was notable for hypertension. The clinical history was primarily obtained from patient recall, given that comprehensive medical records from the preceding years were unavailable.

On admission, ECG showed high-grade AVB, LVH, and a heart rate of 45 beats per minute (bpm). Pathologic Q waves were observed in leads I and aVL. Serum biomarkers were elevated, including troponin, B-type natriuretic peptide, and soluble suppression of tumorigenicity 2. Testing for other organ systems was unremarkable. Coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated a myocardial bridge in the posterior descending branch of the right coronary artery, with no evidence of coronary stenosis. 24-hour Holter monitoring documented intermittent high-grade AVB, intermittent second-degree type II AVB with partial 2:1 conduction, and intermittent VP (Figure 1). A total of 53,707 beats were recorded, with an average heart rate of 49 bpm. Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 57%; asymmetrical left ventricular wall thickening, with the interventricular septum measuring 18 mm, posterior wall 12 mm, and lateral wall 11 mm; and mildly reduced global longitudinal strain. Cardiac MRI confirmed heterogeneous septal thickening, maximal at approximately 21 mm. The left ventricular inferior wall measured 8–9 mm, and the lateral wall 7–8 mm. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved, although diastolic compliance was impaired. No left ventricular outflow tract obstruction was observed. Family history was significant: the patient’s maternal great-grandmother died of cardiac disease, 1 maternal great-uncle died suddenly, and another required pacemaker implantation (Figure 3). The patient’s mother underwent an ECG, which showed sinus bradycardia at 46 bpm (Figure 4). Her Holter recording demonstrated average sinus bradycardia with irregularity, frequent atrial premature complexes, and short runs of atrial tachycardia. The patient subsequently underwent prophylactic implantation of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) capable of pacing. During follow-up, his cardiac structure and function remained stable. He later developed frequent ventricular premature complexes and intermittent right bundle branch block, which were managed with antiarrhythmic medication. He remains clinically stable under continuous monitoring.

Discussion

PS is a rare autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the PRKAG2 gene, which lead to dysfunction of the AMPK γ2 regulatory subunit. This disrupts AMPK activity, resulting in pathologic glycogen accumulation within cardiomyocytes.3 The syndrome is characterized by LVH, VP, progressive conduction abnormalities, and potential involvement of skeletal muscle or other extracardiac tissues.1

Multiple case reports and clinical studies have demonstrated that PS often exhibits phenotypic variability, both within families and among unrelated individuals.6 The R302Q mutation is one of the most common mutations in PS. Existing reports on the R302Q mutation consistently highlight phenotypic heterogeneity, similar to what we observed in our case. The R302Q mutation is typically diagnosed at approximately the age of 30 years,2,5,7,8 later than some other mutations, possibly owing to its relatively mild impact on ATP/AMP affinity.9 Compared with other PRKAG2 mutations, the R302Q mutation is associated with higher rates of VP, syncope, and pacemaker implantation.7 However, a consistent observation across the literature is the absence of documented extracardiac involvement in individuals with this specific mutation.7 Likewise, we did not observe any signs of skeletal muscle involvement in our patient. However, the presence of hypertension raises the possibility of extracardiac involvement, given that some studies suggest that hypertension may reflect such manifestations. In patients with PS, more than 50% develop hypertension.7 Some studies propose that early-onset hypertension could serve as a potential risk marker for the disease. However, the precise mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

The patient had a history of myocarditis. Although myocarditis has not been reported in previous case reports, a study investigating myocarditis in genetic cardiomyopathies found that, among patients with cardiac involvement secondary to amyloidosis, storage diseases, and neuromuscular disorders, approximately one-third were diagnosed as having concurrent myocarditis.10 Lutokhina et al10 posited that myocarditis may generally act as a contributing factor, potentially activating an underlying abnormal genetic program in mild phenotypes or precipitating its full clinical expression in more aggressive forms. The lack of clinical documentation surrounding the onset and follow-up of the patient’s myocarditis precluded additional analysis, which in turn highlights the necessity of thorough record keeping in similar clinical contexts. The potential role of myocarditis as a disease modifier in PS warrants further investigation.

In the present case, the proband exhibited elevated troponin levels and ST-T segment changes on ECG at admission. CTA revealed a myocardial bridge—a finding also reported by Banankhah et al11 in a patient with the same R302Q mutation. Given that coronary CTA is not routinely performed in most PS cases, it remains uncertain whether this gene defect predisposes to myocardial bridging.

Despite phenotypic overlap with sarcomeric HCM, PS carries a substantially worse prognosis, with progressive heart failure and malignant arrhythmias representing primary mortality drivers.12 Genetic analysis remains the diagnostic gold standard for PS13 and should be prioritized in patients exhibiting LVH with complex arrhythmias.

Current studies have summarized the characteristic features on echocardiography and cardiac MRI, aiming to provide clues for their differential diagnosis. Echocardiography in patients with PS predominantly reveals concentric or eccentric hypertrophy. A large echocardiographic study of patients with PS found that, among those with mildly reduced longitudinal strain, the regional longitudinal strain patterns across the entire left ventricle, as shown on parametric (polar) maps, displayed a distinctive color gradient from light to dark pink, forming a stripe-like pattern.14

In patients with PS with the R302Q mutation, cardiac MRI often demonstrates eccentric LVH. In the early stages, hypertrophy is typically focal, affecting the mid-to-basal segments of the lateral wall. As the disease progresses, hypertrophy becomes more diffuse, with a greater focus on the interventricular septum. In early stages without LGE, T1 values may be reduced, likely reflecting glycogen accumulation in the myocardium. However, in later stages, T1 values may increase owing to the development of fibrosis, accompanied by the appearance of LGE.15 Based on this criterion, our patient is in an intermediate to advanced stage of the disease.

Current management of PS lacks established guidelines, with therapeutic approaches limited to heart failure treatment, arrhythmia control, and SCD prevention—without causal therapies.13 For this patient, an ICD with pacing function was implanted for further treatment and primary prevention of SCD. Decision making regarding prophylactic ICD implantation in patients with PS remains challenging owing to the absence of large-scale studies clearly defining its benefit. A retrospective study of 90 patients based the decision primarily on personal or family history of SCD,2 whereas another larger study recommended a comprehensive assessment of multiple individual risk factors. These include a family history of SCD, suspected arrhythmic syncope, the degree of LVH, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, specific patterns of LGE on cardiac MRI, and the coexistence of VP and conduction disorders.7 Our patient presented with notable clinical features: recent transient loss of consciousness (syncope could not be ruled out), a family history of SCD (in non–first-degree relatives), ECG confirmation of high-grade AVB and VP, and clear myocardial LGE on cardiac MRI. This combination encompasses several high-risk factors highlighted in the literature. The ICD provides protection against SCD from rapid arrhythmias involving the accessory pathway, while also offering reliable pacing support for the coexistent severe bradyarrhythmia. Accordingly, we engaged in detailed discussions with the patient and family. After fully informed deliberation, the patient ultimately decided to proceed with ICD implantation. Further research is needed to clarify the benefit of prophylactic ICD implantation and provide stronger evidence-based support.

Emerging investigations explored strategies including β-adrenergic blockade and CRISPR/Cas9-based gene correction.16,17 In our patient, although an ICD was placed, new arrhythmias emerged during follow-up, highlighting the need for more effective treatment strategies. This also underscores the necessity of long-term follow-up and timely management of adverse outcomes in such patients.

Conclusion

PS should be considered in patients presenting with ventricular hypertrophy accompanied by VP and other conduction system abnormalities. Comprehensive multimodal evaluation and family genetic studies are essential in disease diagnosis and risk stratification. Given the generally poor prognosis associated with PS, long-term follow-up and proactive management of complications are strongly recommended to optimize patient outcomes.
