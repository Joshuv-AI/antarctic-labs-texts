Study on variation characteristics of shear strength of deep-sea sediments in the South China sea under thermo-hydraulic coupling
Authors: Yan Feng, Qiunan Chen, Lihai Wu, Jinhu Tang, Guangping Liu, Zengliang Wang, Wei Hu, Bingchu Chen, Shunkai Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To investigate the mechanical response mechanisms of deep-sea sediments in the South China Sea under coupled field effects, this study systematically analyzed the influence of moisture content (15%-35%), temperature (4℃-60℃), and confining pressure (100–300 kPa) on their undrained shear strength through triaxial shear tests. A thermohydraulic-coupled model was developed. Results indicate that increased moisture content significantly reduces shear strength: at low confining pressures, strength decreases by 50% across 15%-35% moisture content ranges, while high confining pressures reduce the decrease to 30% by enhancing particle confinement. Temperature elevation accelerates cement softening and particle thermal motion, causing cohesion to drop from 17.6 kPa to 10.9 kPa and internal friction angle from 23.8° to 16.7°. Although confining pressure enhances strength, it cannot reverse cement failure caused by high temperatures. The improved thermohydraulic-coupled model, incorporating moisture content sensitivity coefficient (m) and temperature sensitivity coefficient (n), achieves precise stress-strain relationship predictions under coupled field effects (correlation coefficient R²> 0.97). These findings provide quantitative theoretical support for assessing engineering stability and risk management in resource development of deep-sea areas in the South China Sea.

Introduction

The South China Sea, as one of the key marginal seas in the western Pacific, is crucial for the engineering mechanics of deep-sea sediments, which is essential for the development of seabed resources and the safety of marine engineering1 Current research indicates that deep-sea sediments typically exhibit high water content, large porosity, low density, and complex mineral compositions, with their shear strength significantly influenced by physical parameters such as temperature, water content, porosity, and density2–4. The shear characteristics, a core mechanical parameter of deep-sea sediments, are directly linked to the stability of seabed mining systems, the reliability of cable laying, and the design of equipment traction forces5,6. This parameter not only serves as the foundation for assessing the bearing capacity of seabed foundations and analyzing slope stability but also determines the optimal threshold for optimizing construction parameters of deep-sea equipment. Notably, the deep-sea environment of the South China Sea features a significant temperature gradient7where changes in temperature can alter the viscosity of pore water, inter-particle forces, and mineral adsorption properties, further influencing the mechanical behavior of sediments. Additionally, engineering activities can change sediment temperatures, leading to alterations in the mechanical properties of sediments. Changes in sediment temperature can lead to strength degradation or enhancement effects by altering the viscosity of pore water, inter-particle forces, and mineral adsorption properties, thereby regulating the triggering thresholds for geological disasters such as submarine landslides and turbidity currents8. Therefore, by using an indoor controlled experimental system, the evolution of shear characteristics under the combined effects of temperature and moisture content is investigated. Furthermore, by analyzing the mechanical response mechanisms under multi-field coupling conditions, this approach can optimize engineering safety designs and assess the long-term impact of geothermal activities or climate change on the stability of seabed geology. This provides scientific support for risk prevention and ecological safety warnings in deep-sea engineering.

Due to the extreme deep-sea environment and the limitations of existing in-situ monitoring technologies, scholars currently primarily use remolded soil samples to study the physical and mechanical properties of deep-sea sediments. During sample preparation, the water content of the sediments plays a crucial role in influencing the shear characteristics of the specimens. Existing research indicates that soil strength parameters generally exhibit a declining trend with increasing water content: Xu et al.9 found through silty clay experiments that the elastic modulus significantly decreases, while the shape of the stress-strain curve remains stable; Almasoudi et al.10 revealed a peak strength phenomenon corresponding to the optimal water content (30%) in kaolin studies, pointing out that the strength reduction in the wet-side region stems from the attenuation of matric suction; Kang et al.11, based on particle contact theory, confirmed that the weakening of shear strength in silty clay is directly related to the reduction in interlocking effects caused by increased water content; Zhang et al.12 further observed that the strength attenuation induced by higher water content is more pronounced under low-stress conditions. Beyond strength characteristics, changes in water content also influence soil mechanical responses through multidimensional mechanisms. Hu et al.13 noted, based on modulus tests, that the discrepancy between laboratory and field moduli primarily arises from alterations in soil state caused by water content fluctuations, with different modulus attenuation patterns observed on either side of the optimal water content. Notably, Abdullahi et al.14 discovered contradictory phenomena in strength parameter studies: the internal friction angle increases with rising water content, while cohesion exhibits a significant negative correlation. Godoy et al.15 also argued that frozen soil strength increases with higher water content, necessitating coupled considerations of temperature and shear rate in design. These research findings systematically elucidate the complex mechanisms by which water content affects soil mechanical behavior, providing a theoretical basis for water content control in engineering practice.

When elucidating the coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical mechanisms governing soil mechanical behavior, the role of the temperature field exhibits significant nonlinear characteristics. Existing studies indicate that temperature rise weakens the shear strength of certain types of soils: Abuel-Naga et al.16 and Hamidi et al.17 confirmed through saturated cohesive soil tests that shear strength parameters systematically decrease with increasing temperature; Yu et al.18 further discovered that Boom clay exhibits simultaneous reductions in undrained strength, cohesion, and elastic modulus under thermal effects, a mechanism attributed to the interparticle lubrication effect caused by changes in the adsorbed water film thickness of clay minerals (e.g., montmorillonite). Similar studies by Bymyn et al.19 also observed a 15–22% decline in the shear strength of saturated clay. Guo et al.20 found that the dynamic shear strength parameters of silty clay in permafrost regions significantly increase with decreasing temperature.

Notably, Yang et al.21 revealed an inverse correlation in the low-temperature zone (< 0 °C)-temperature reduction leads to simultaneous degradation of elastic modulus, cohesion, and internal friction angle, directly linked to pore structure damage induced by ice crystal growth. Shibasaki et al.22 supplemented this with observations of residual strength attenuation (up to 18%) in montmorillonite-rich landslide surfaces under low-temperature conditions, uncovering a chain-reaction mechanism of thermally induced strength degradation in slip zone soils. These findings collectively establish a theoretical framework for temperature-soil interaction, providing critical scientific insights for engineering stability assessments in permafrost regions and thermo-mechanical coupling analyses in geothermal resource development.

Some scholars have simultaneously observed the inverse phenomenon of enhanced shear strength under heating conditions. This phenomenon is generally believed to be governed by the synergistic effects of soil type and thermo-hydro-mechanical boundary conditions: Li et al.23 and Sun et al.24 found through marine clay experiments that under drained conditions, a temperature increase (30–60°C) could raise the undrained strength by 15–28%, attributed to the redistribution of effective stress caused by thermal expansion. Tanaka et al.25 supplemented the thermal dissipation effect of pore pressure in normally consolidated soils, demonstrating that Heating reduced pore water pressure by up to 20 kPa per 10 °C, indirectly enhancing shear strength. Abuel-Naga et al.26 made a breakthrough discovery in Bangkok soft clay experiments, showing that regardless of the overconsolidation ratio, the undrained strength increased by 8–12% for every 10 °C rise in temperature, revealing a thermally activated cementation mechanism. Notably, Thota et al.27 observed a nonlinear increase in matric suction (up to 30 kPa) with temperature in the high-temperature range (> 25 °C), leading to an 18–25% improvement in shear strength due to enhanced effective stress. Wang et al.28 further validated the temperature-strengthening effect from an interfacial mechanics perspective, finding a significant positive correlation between the contact strength of kaolin-fine aggregate concrete and temperature (with a 4.2° increase in interface friction angle at 40 °C). Additionally, some studies indicate that the influence of temperature on soil strength is complex or insignificant. Certain scholars, through investigations of specific soil types or under particular environmental conditions, found that temperature variations did not induce significant upward or downward trends in shear strength. When elucidating the complex mechanisms of temperature’s effect on soil shear strength, research has revealed its material-dependent and boundary condition-sensitive nature. For specific soil types and experimental conditions, temperature changes may not induce significant strength responses or may exhibit contradictory patterns: Chen et al.29 demonstrated through tests on Hong Kong marine sediments that shear strength fluctuated by less than 5% within the 20–50°C range, highlighting the temperature-insensitive characteristics of marine deposits. Liu et al.30 noted from the perspective of pore pressure evolution that temperature indirectly regulates the effective stress path by altering pore water viscosity rather than directly affecting strength parameters. This non-explicit influence was corroborated in Henok et al.’s31 study on silty clay—the effective internal friction angle remained constant (φ’ = 28 ± 0.5°) within the 20–60 °C range. More revealingly, Aidy Ung et al.32 conducted a comparative study: illite-montmorillonite mixed clay exhibited a strength variation of < 3% during thermal cycling (− 20 °C to 80 °C), whereas kaolinite clay showed a general strength increase of 12–15% after the same thermal treatment, attributed to the thermally induced reconstruction of hydrogen bonds between kaolinite particles. These differential responses underscore the synergistic effects of mineral composition, structural characteristics, and thermodynamic boundary conditions, providing a theoretical basis for temperature-sensitivity grading assessments in geothermal pile design and permafrost slope protection. This complexity or lack of significant influence may stem from the counterbalancing or equilibrium of multiple factors, such as the strength changes induced by pore water viscosity variations and the temperature-dependent alterations in interparticle forces. In soils with complex mineral compositions, the differing thermal responses of various minerals make it challenging to define a straightforward overall strength trend. Some studies also point out that when temperature fluctuates within a relatively narrow range, soil shear strength remains largely stable, and only when temperature changes exceed a certain threshold does a noticeable strength variation occur. Such complex or insignificant effects further highlight the difficulties and challenges in studying temperature’s impact on soil shear strength, while also opening new research directions and perspectives for more in-depth and comprehensive exploration in this field.

When investigating the regulatory mechanisms of temperature on the mechanical behavior of soil-structure interfaces, studies have found that the response patterns are closely related to soil type and interface characteristics. For cohesive soil-structure interfaces, Chao et al.33 revealed through Mercia mudstone clay-GDL interface tests that thermal cycling (20–60 °C) led to a peak strength degradation of up to 22%, with the mechanism involving interface crack propagation induced by differential thermal expansion of clay minerals. Cui et al.34 further quantified the temperature effect in clayey sand-structure interface studies, finding a reduction in the interface friction angle by 2.2° (corresponding to a 15% strength attenuation), particularly under a net stress of 100 kPa, where thermally induced weakening of surface tension exacerbated slip failure risks. Maghsoodi et al.35 supplemented these findings with comparative experiments on strain accumulation effects—while cohesive soil interface strength degraded by 16%, the strain rate increased by 2–3 times, highlighting the accelerating effect of temperature on interface damage. In stark contrast, sandy soil-structure interfaces exhibited different behavior: Neda Yavari et al.36 demonstrated that the strength parameters of sand-concrete contact surfaces fluctuated by less than 3% within a 40 °C temperature range, while Maghsoodi et al.37 found that sandy soil interfaces maintained strength stability due to particle redistribution, whereas cohesive soil interfaces experienced an 8% increase in cohesion due to thermal shrinkage effects. Wang et al. analyzed the influence of temperature on the strength of marine sand38 and clay-structure39 interface under scouring conditions by numerical simulation. This differential response underscores the fundamental thermodynamic differences between granular and cemented materials, emphasizing the need to comprehensively consider the coupling effects of temperature fields and geotechnical properties in interface material selection.

In summary, the effects of sample moisture content and shear temperature on soil shear characteristics vary across different regions and may even lead to contradictory conclusions. Meanwhile, current research on the influence of water-thermal factors on soil shear characteristics primarily focuses on terrestrial and nearshore areas, with relatively limited studies conducted in deep-sea regions. For deep-sea sediments in the South China Sea, the combined effects of high moisture content and unique temperature gradients may result in shear behavior patterns that differ significantly from those observed in terrestrial and nearshore soils. Therefore, it is of great significance to conduct in-depth research on the thermal-hydro coupling effects and multi-field coupling models of shear strength in deep-sea sediments of the South China Sea. This study takes deep-sea sediments from the South China Sea as the research subject, aiming to systematically investigate the influence mechanisms of sample moisture content and temperature on their shear characteristics. A series of laboratory tests were designed to analyze the mechanical responses of deep-sea sediments under varying moisture contents and temperatures. Stress-strain curves were obtained, and the impacts of moisture content, temperature, and confining pressure on shear strength, deformation characteristics, and shear failure modes were thoroughly examined. By comparing test results under different moisture content and temperature combinations, the study delves into the governing patterns of these factors on the shear behavior of South China Sea deep-sea sediments. Furthermore, a thermal-hydro-mechanical coupling model was developed, and its parameters were calibrated and validated using experimental data. This model quantifies the quantitative relationships among temperature, moisture content, and shear strength, revealing how temperature variations regulate the correlation between soil physical parameters and shear strength. It is anticipated that this research will provide a solid theoretical foundation and scientific basis for resource exploitation and marine engineering construction in the deep-sea regions of the South China Sea, thereby supporting the safe and efficient implementation of related projects.

Mineral composition and physical properties of soil samples

The deep-sea sediment samples used in this study were collected from a specific area in the South China Sea. The sampling operation was completed by the “Hainiu” pressure-retaining deep-sea drilling rig, independently developed by Hunan University of Science and Technology, with the assistance of the Marine Geology No. 2 research vessel. This drilling rig retrieved samples at a water depth of 2,060 m. It employs key technologies such as large-hole-depth remote-controlled full-hole pressure-retaining “wireline coring” and the principle of direct full-length sealing of the core barrel for pressure-retaining sampling, marking the birth of China’s first drilling rig system suitable for large-hole-depth pressure-retaining coring on the seabed. The system is designed for environments with a maximum water depth of 4,500 m and can achieve full-hole pressure-retaining coring to a depth of 234 m, with a core diameter of 45 millimeters. Utilizing pressure-retaining wireline coring technology, the drilling tool has a length of 3.7 m, with a total of 77 drill pipes (each 3 m long) and 78 pressure-retaining core barrels (each 3.7 m long) used40. After collection, the samples underwent waterproof sealing and were carefully transported to the indoor laboratory, where they were subsequently stored in a low-temperature, high-humidity environment.

Test conditions and methods

Test equipment

The main equipment used in this chapter’s experiments includes the low-temperature high-pressure automatic environmental triaxial testing system manufactured by Xi’an Kangtuoli Instrument Equipment. This triaxial apparatus is a high-precision experimental device that integrates advanced temperature control technology with a triaxial mechanical loading system, specifically designed to investigate the shear behavior of soils under high-temperature and low-pressure conditions. It primarily consists of key components such as a metal outer shell, specimen confinement ring, pore water pressure sensor, linear variable differential transformer (LVDT), loading piston, and temperature regulation system, collectively creating a sealed testing environment. The soil triaxial apparatus is equipped with an automatic data acquisition system capable of real-time collection, reading, and storage of experimental data. Figure 6 displays the exterior and structure of the low-temperature high-pressure automatic environmental triaxial testing system. The main technical parameters of this equipment are as follows: maximum triaxial load reaches 256 kN, confining pressure upper Limit is 30 MPa, back pressure can reach up to 20 MPa, axial displacement range is 100 mm, and temperature control range covers − 30 °C to 100 °C.

Test methods

To enhance the comparability of the tests, a unified batch of remolded samples was prepared. The preparation process included pretreatment steps such as air-drying, grinding, sieving, uniform mixing, portioning, and storage, as well as test preparation procedures Like compaction and saturation. The deep-sea sediment samples collected from a water depth of 2060 m were placed in an oven and dried at 70 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the soil samples were crushed and passed through a 0.5 mm sieve. The sieved deep-sea sediment samples were then sprayed with predetermined amounts of water according to different moisture contents, thoroughly mixed, tightly sealed in containers, and left to moisten for 24 h before use.

The soil samples were then placed in saturation devices and positioned inside a vacuum chamber. After the saturation devices were fully submerged in water, the vacuum pumping was stopped, and the samples were left to stand for a period to allow atmospheric pressure to facilitate saturation. The samples were then removed, weighed to an accuracy of 0.1 g, and the degree of saturation was calculated accordingly. After achieving saturation, the samples were installed, and the triaxial apparatus automatically executed the back-pressure saturation procedure. The confining pressure in the pressure chamber was set to 100 kPa, and the back pressure was set to 80 kPa, with the termination condition set at a B-value of 0.95. When the B-value exceeded 0.95, the soil sample was considered saturated.

Upon completion of the back-pressure saturation phase, isotropic consolidation was conducted under the set confining pressure until the drainage volume stabilized (below 1 mm³/min), which served as the indicator of consolidation completion. After consolidation, the confining pressure was maintained constant, and different confining pressures were set for shearing. The shear strain rate was adjusted to achieve an axial strain of 0.1% per minute. The test termination condition was set at an axial strain of 20%. During the triaxial tests, the sediment samples were subjected to consolidated undrained (CU) compression tests under confining pressures (σ3) of 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa. As the tests progressed, the sediment samples gradually compressed under axial pressure, with axial stress and strain increasing until structural failure occurred, and the deviator stress stabilized. Analysis of test results

Multi-field response characteristics and evolution patterns of stress-strain curves in deep-sea sediments under thermo-hydro conditions

Moisture content

Triaxial tests on deep-sea sediments with varying water contents were conducted using the controlled variable method. To simulate the real environment of deep-sea sediments, the shear temperature was set at 4 °C, with confining pressures of 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa, and the water contents of the remolded samples were 15%, 25%, and 35%, respectively. Figure 7 presents the deviatoric stress-strain relationship curves of deep-sea sediments under different water contents. The results indicate that as the water content increases from 15 to 35%, the growth rate of deviatoric stress and the peak stress significantly decrease. Taking an axial strain of 10% as an example, the deviatoric stress for the 15% water content sample (under 300 kPa confining pressure) is approximately 265 kPa, whereas for the 35% water content sample under the same confining pressure, it is only about 224 kPa. This phenomenon primarily stems from the complex influence of water content on interparticle interaction mechanisms. At low water content (15%), the limited moisture between particles results in dominant frictional and interlocking forces, leading to tight particle contact and the formation of a stable structure with strong resistance to deformation. In contrast, at high water content (35%), water fills the interparticle voids, reducing friction due to lubrication effects, while increased pore water pressure partially offsets the effective stress, thereby diminishing the load-bearing capacity of the soil skeleton. Furthermore, the dense and stable structure of low-water-content sediments requires overcoming stronger structural resistance, whereas high-water-content sediments exhibit looser particle connections, making them prone to sliding and reconfiguration under lower stress.

Meanwhile, the increase in confining pressure enhances lateral confinement, restricting particle movement, thereby partially offsetting the negative effects of high water content. As shown in Fig. 7, at a water content of 35%, the deviatoric stress under 300 kPa confining pressure can reach 250 kPa, significantly higher than the 150 kPa observed under 100 kPa confining pressure. In low confining pressure environments, the sensitivity of strength to water content differences is more pronounced (e.g., a difference of approximately 50% between 15% and 35% water content under 100 kPa confining pressure), whereas the gap narrows to 30% under high confining pressure. This indicates that deep high confining pressure environments can improve soil strength by enhancing lateral confinement to restrict particle movement, but potential risks to engineering stability from long-term pore water pressure accumulation must be carefully monitored.

Figure 8 presents the Mohr’s stress circles of deep-sea sediments under different water contents (15%, 25%, 35%). The data reveals the consolidated undrained triaxial shear test results of Mohr’s stress circles for deep-sea sediments at varying water contents (15%, 25%, 35%). Analysis shows that as water content increases from 15 to 35%, both the size of stress circles and shear strength parameters exhibit non-monotonic variations. For instance, at 15% water content, the smaller stress circle radius corresponds to lower undrained shear strength parameters, indicating reduced interparticle cohesion. When water content rises to 25%, increased pore water pressure leads to decreased effective stress between particles, resulting in further reduction of shear strength. However, at 35% water content, the stress circle unexpectedly enlarges with significantly improved effective shear strength parameters, potentially attributable to particle rearrangement or enhanced chemical cementation under high water content conditions.

The primary reason for this variation lies in the complex influence of moisture content on interparticle forces. At low moisture content (15%), interparticle friction dominates while cohesion remains weak, resulting in lower shear strength. Under moderate moisture content (25%), the lubricating effect of water reduces friction, and pore water pressure further decreases effective stress, causing shear strength to reach its minimum. At high moisture content (35%), water fills the pores and promotes particle alignment, while potentially activating cementing materials to enhance cohesion and structural stability, thereby significantly improving effective stress parameters. This nonlinear relationship reflects the dynamic equilibrium between the microstructure and macroscopic mechanical response of deep-sea sediments under the influence of moisture.

Figure 9 illustrates the influence of different water contents on the triaxial test stress-strain curves of deep-sea sediments under the same confining pressure. The overall trend shows that the deviatoric stress increases with increasing confining pressure. Under the same water content, the deviatoric stress-strain curve shifts upward with increasing confining pressure. The Fig. shows that at a confining pressure of 100 kPa, the peak deviatoric stress at 15% water content is approximately 135 kPa, and at 35% water content, it is about 120 kPa. At a confining pressure of 300 kPa, the peak stress at 15% water content is 292 kPa, while at 35% water content, it drops to around 268 kPa. The differences between different water contents decrease as the confining pressure increases. At low confining pressures, the deviatoric stress varies significantly with different water contents; at high confining pressures, these differences become less pronounced. In terms of failure morphology, at low confining pressure and low water content (15%), strain hardening is evident, with the curve continuously rising without a significant peak. During shear, the particles reorganize slowly and the structure remains stable. At high confining pressure and high water content (35%), the curve may show a slight peak followed by a gradual flattening. The confinement provided by the high confining pressure delays the complete destruction of the particle structure, but the high water content still leads to localized softening.

An in-depth analysis of its formation mechanisms reveals two primary causes: First, the synergistic effect between interparticle forces and confining pressure. Under low confining pressure (100 kPa), the lateral constraints between particles are weak, making the influence of water content on frictional and interlocking forces more pronounced. At low water content (15%), particles are in close contact with high friction, leading to a rapid rise in deviatoric stress. Conversely, at high water content (35%), the lubricating effect of water weakens interparticle interactions, resulting in slower deviatoric stress growth. Second, the dynamic equilibrium of pore water pressure. Under low confining pressure, high water content (35%) facilitates pore water pressure accumulation, which offsets effective stress and leads to lower deviatoric stress. In contrast, under high confining pressure, the confining pressure suppresses pore water pressure development (due to a more stable particle skeleton), increasing the proportion of effective stress and enabling high-water-content sediments to still exhibit rising deviatoric stress with increasing confining pressure. It is evident that increasing confining pressure significantly enhances the shear strength of sediments across all water contents, particularly at high water content, where the strengthening effect of confining pressure compensates for the negative impact of water. Under low confining pressure, water content has a more sensitive influence on deviatoric stress (with differences reaching up to 50%), whereas under high confining pressure, this sensitivity diminishes (differences narrow to 20–30%). Therefore, deep-sea sediments under high confining pressure (e.g., in deep strata) can maintain a certain load-bearing capacity even at high water content. However, in shallow, low-confining-pressure environments, water content must be strictly controlled to prevent significant strength reduction.

Temperature

Further conduct triaxial tests on deep-sea sediments at different temperatures, with shear temperatures set at 4 °C, 20 °C, 40 °C, and 60 °C respectively, while maintaining confining pressures of 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa. The remolded soil samples were controlled to have a uniform water content of 25%. Figure 10 shows the stress-strain curves of specimens under different temperatures and confining pressures. The results indicate that under all temperature conditions, the load response type of specimens at various confining pressures exhibits strain hardening. Additionally, all specimens displayed plastic failure after loading. As temperature and confining pressure increased, the stress-strain curves shifted upward, with higher axial stress corresponding to the same axial strain, indicating enhanced deformation resistance of the specimens. The stress-strain behavior of deep-sea sediments varied at different temperatures: At 4 °C: The deviatoric stress increased significantly with axial strain, demonstrating pronounced strain hardening characteristics. Low temperatures strengthened interparticle cementation (e.g., freezing of bound water, electrostatic attraction of clay minerals), restricting particle sliding and resulting in increased brittleness and strength. At 20 °C: The rate of deviatoric stress growth slowed compared to 4 °C, and the final stabilized value decreased. Elevated temperatures weakened interparticle cementation (e.g., increased mobility of bound water), reducing resistance to particle rearrangement, which macroscopically manifested as diminished strain hardening. At 40 °C and 60 °C: The deviatoric stress further decreased, and the curves flattened. High temperatures caused cementing materials (e.g., organic matter, clay minerals) to soften or even decompose, with interparticle friction dominating deformation. The material exhibited plastic flow characteristics, and strain hardening nearly disappeared.

At the same temperature, an increase in confining pressure significantly enhances the deviatoric stress. Confining pressure compacts pores, strengthens particle contact forces and friction, suppresses lateral deformation, and improves overall strength. Deep-sea sediments at high temperatures exhibit higher sensitivity to confining pressure: at 60 °C, confining pressure can still increase deviatoric stress, but the increment is lower than under low-temperature conditions. This is because high temperatures weaken interparticle cementation, and confining pressure primarily enhances strength through physical compaction rather than chemical effects. Evidently, under low-temperature conditions (4 °C), strain hardening is dominated by particle breakage, rearrangement, and cementation strengthening. Broken particles fill pores, leading to structural densification, while cementation maintains particle alignment. In contrast, at high temperatures (60 °C), strain hardening is mainly driven by particle friction and the formation of localized shear bands. The failure of cementation results in slower strength growth, causing the curve to stabilize.

Meanwhile, at low temperatures (≤ 20 °C), cementation dominates strain hardening, and confining pressure further strengthens the material by enhancing particle contacts, making it suitable for high-stress environments (e.g., deep-sea engineering foundations). At high temperatures (≥ 40 °C), cementation failure leads to plastic flow, and confining pressure only improves strength through physical compaction, necessitating consideration of long-term creep risks (e.g., thermal stability design of subsea pipelines). The range of 20–40 °C may represent a transition zone between cementation-dominated and friction-dominated mechanisms, requiring further study on microscopic phase transition processes. These findings can provide guidance for confining pressure design in deep-sea resource development.

Analyzing the formation causes reveals that the mechanical properties of deep-sea sediments are significantly influenced by the coupling effect of temperature and confining pressure. At low temperatures (4℃), the interstitial materials (such as clay minerals and biocarbonates) remain stable, and the viscosity of pore water is high, which enhances cohesion and the internal friction angle. As the temperature rises (from 20℃ to 60℃), these interstitial materials gradually dissolve, the viscosity of pore water decreases, and the bonding strength between particles weakens, leading to a simultaneous decrease in cohesion (c) and the internal friction angle (φ). Additionally, high confining pressure compresses the pore space, promoting particle contact and enhancing frictional interaction, but it cannot fully compensate for the damage to the interstitial structure caused by high temperatures. This interaction between temperature and confining pressure reveals the complex rheological behavior of deep-sea sediments under thermodynamic conditions, where shear strength decreases nonlinearly with increasing temperature. This understanding is crucial for assessing the long-term stability of submarine engineering projects, such as pipeline laying and resource extraction.

It is evident that temperature has a significant impact on the shear properties of deep-sea sediments. A deeper analysis reveals that at low temperatures, such as 4℃, the presence of ice cementation can enhance particle connections, allowing for greater stress transfer under load, thus increasing the normal stress. Conversely, at high temperatures, such as 60℃, the destruction of some original cementing materials can weaken particle connections, reducing overall strength and decreasing the normal stress. As temperature rises, the viscosity of water decreases and its fluidity increases, making it easier for water to flow and redistribute between particles under load, thus reducing the effective stress transfer and lowering the overall strength and normal stress of the sediments. Additionally, although the thermal expansion and contraction effects of deep-sea sediments are relatively minor, they can still affect the contact state and stress transfer between particles. At high temperatures, particles may expand to some extent, altering the contact area and stress distribution between particles, which can lead to changes in overall strength.

In conclusion, temperature has a significant influence on the triaxial test stress-strain relationship of deep-sea sediments, which mainly affects their mechanical properties by changing the interconnection between particles, water properties and the state of mineral particles themselves.

The compensation capacity of confining pressure for temperature-induced deterioration is weaker than its moisture compensation capability. This stems from the chemical-thermodynamic coupling effect where high temperatures soften cementitious materials and intensify particle thermal motion. While confining pressure enhances particle contact through physical compression, it cannot reverse structural strength loss caused by cementitious failure.

The primary reason for this decline is that high temperatures cause clay minerals or organic cementing materials to soften or decompose, directly disrupting the chemical bonds between particles. Additionally, increased thermal motion of particles reduces the roughness of contact surfaces and decreases the viscosity of pore water, further weakening friction resistance. In general, both temperature and water content weaken soil strength by changing microstructure and particle interaction, but the influence of temperature is more related to the chemical-thermodynamic coupling mechanism, so it is necessary to optimize the confining pressure and drainage conditions in high temperature engineering environment to ensure stability.

Shear fracture characteristics

Figure 16 illustrates the failure modes of deep-sea sediments under various working conditions. The Fig. 16 shows that in most cases (such as low temperature and high confining pressure), the samples exhibit significant bulging (as indicated by the red dotted line), with the failure mode being relatively mild and characterized by plastic deformation. Consequently, the failure process is relatively mild, with the bulging degree being minimal, favoring shear and plastic deformation, and the overall deformation of the samples being relatively uniform. In contrast, high confining pressure restricts lateral deformation, intensifying stress concentration between particles. During shearing, the friction and interlocking forces between particles increase, making it difficult for them to release stress through sliding, leading to more pronounced bulging (shear bulging). Therefore, under high confining pressure conditions, especially at low temperatures, the bulging phenomenon is more pronounced.

Further analysis of the impact of temperature on the deformation patterns of deep-sea sediments. During shearing, particles are less likely to slide or rearrange, leading to stress concentration and a more pronounced shear bulge. During shearing, particles are more likely to slide and rearrange, resulting in more uniform deformation and less stress concentration. At high temperatures (40℃, 60℃), the bulging phenomenon is less pronounced, and the overall deformation pattern is more gradual, tending towards shearing. At lower confining pressures (e.g., 100 kPa), the sample surface remains relatively flat.

The results demonstrate that the shear strength of the tested sediments under combined water content and temperature degradation shows significant deterioration: when water content increased from 15 to 35%, the undrained shear strength at low confining pressure (100 kPa) decreased by 50%; while temperature rising from 4°℃ to 60℃ caused marked reductions in cohesion and internal friction angle. However, when high water content combines with elevated temperatures, particle bonding is disrupted, resulting in a 15% increase in porosity and structural instability. This study validates the microscopic mechanisms underlying the deterioration of macroscopic mechanical properties.

An enhanced thermal-hydrodynamic coupled model (THM-Coupled Model) has been developed. By incorporating temperature sensitivity coefficients and water content correction factors, the model effectively captures the mechanisms through which environmental parameter changes influence sediment mechanical properties, achieving precise predictions of mechanical behavior under multi-field coupling conditions (R²> 0.97, error < 5%). This model effectively reveals the synergistic degradation mechanism between temperature and water content, providing valuable references for optimizing submarine pipeline designs, developing deep-sea mining equipment, and assessing geological disaster risks. Future research could extend the model to long-term cyclic loading and chemical erosion coupling scenarios, while integrating field monitoring data to optimize model parameters, thereby enhancing prediction accuracy and engineering applicability for sediment mechanical responses in complex deep-sea environments.
