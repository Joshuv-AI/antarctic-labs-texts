Human Trafficking: Emergency Department Screening and Referral
Authors: Christa Pulvino, Robert S Braun, Kelli L Jarrell, Elysia M Smith, Carolyn R Smith, Priyanka Vemuru, Alexis Kimmel, Caroline E Freiermuth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOur objective was to initiate screening for human trafficking in the University of Cincinnati Medical Center Emergency Department (UCMC ED) and then estimate the number of patients who were at risk of human trafficking and the proportion who were referred for further services.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted at the UCMC ED, a large urban academic center emergency department, to describe the number of patients who screened positive for human trafficking risk and received relevant resources over a 12-month period between December 2022 and November 2023. Any patient who was approached by an early intervention program (EIP) counselor and completed a risk assessment was included. A person was considered to have a positive screen if they answered affirmatively to at least 1 of the 4 screening questions included in the previously validated Rapid Appraisal for Trafficking tool. Acceptance of referrals was also recorded by the EIP team and is reported here.ResultsOut of 557 patients assessed, 69 (12.4%) screened positive for risk of human trafficking. Of those, 39 (56.5%) patients accepted referrals to resources specific to trafficked persons, and 60 (87%) accepted referrals to any resources, including assistance with housing, substance use disorders, and food insecurity.ConclusionHuman trafficking is a problem affecting ED patients. The proportion of individuals who screened positive for trafficking risk and accepted resources indicates that many patients are willing to accept assistance. EDs should institute a mechanism to screen for human trafficking and offer services to address trafficking and other social determinants of health.Keywords: human trafficking, social emergency medicine, social determinants of health, violence

The Bottom Line

Human trafficking is a major human rights violation with significant impacts on health that often goes undetected and unaddressed in patients presenting to emergency departments. This retrospective study sought to estimate the number of patients presenting to the University of Cincinnati emergency department who were at risk for human trafficking and track the proportion of those patients who accepted lists of social resources. A total of 12.4% of patients assessed screened positive for risk of human trafficking, and 87% of those patients accepted lists of relevant resources.

Introduction

Background

Human trafficking (HT) is a human rights violation affecting approximately 27.6 million people worldwide.1 The US government defines HT as “the use of force, fraud, or coercion to obtain some type of labor or commercial sex act.”2 The National Human Trafficking Hotline identified 16,710 trafficked people in the United States in 2021.3 Due to underreporting, the actual number is likely much higher.

A survey of domestic sex trafficking survivors demonstrated that 63% will visit an emergency department (ED) while being trafficked.4 Other sources indicate the number of trafficked patients seeking health care during captivity may be as high as 88%.5 Unfortunately, many go unrecognized, as in the case of a labor trafficked individual presenting with heat exhaustion; his immediate medical needs were addressed, but his underlying exploitative situation was missed.6 Even when ED physicians and staff suspect trafficking, many lack the knowledge and resources to screen for this problem and provide adequate assistance.7

Importance

In addition to the violence and indignity imposed on trafficked people, trafficking survivors suffer from a proportionally greater burden of disease. HT is correlated with a higher prevalence of substance abuse, mental health disorders, and reproductive health problems.8 Additionally, trafficked individuals often have other intersecting social vulnerabilities.9 Attempts to address patients’ medical problems or other coexisting social needs will be incomplete if the fact that they are being trafficked goes unaddressed.

The ED exists in a unique position to identify and intervene with patients who are currently being trafficked, as well as those at risk of becoming trafficked. ED-based screening has the potential to identify HT and has been implemented successfully in other emergency departments.10 However, universal screening for HT is not common, and further data may be needed to support the allocation of resources toward such initiatives. It is imperative that we improve health care staff recognition of and response to this patient population to better serve their needs. Even when community resources for trafficking are limited, screening can be an important means of establishing the ED as a safe haven for trafficked patients. Similar to patients suffering from intimate partner violence, trafficked patients often do not self-identify and may require time and repeated contact before they feel able to leave their exploitative situation.9

Goals of This Investigation

The objective of this study was to initiate screening for human trafficking in the University of Cincinnati Medical Center Emergency Department (UCMC ED) and then estimate the number of patients who were at risk for HT and the proportion referred for further services. By collecting this data, we aimed to assess the need for additional HT-specific screening and intervention and to learn more about the social needs of this patient population. Additionally, the methodology used in this study may serve as a model for other emergency departments seeking to replicate similar screening and referral initiatives for HT.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A retrospective study was conducted at the UCMC ED, a large urban academic center emergency department in Cincinnati that sees >75,000 patients annually. The goal was to describe the number of patients who screened positive for HT risk and were referred to relevant resources over a 12-month period after the implementation of a screening process. The project was approved by the center’s institutional review board prior to the initiation of data collection. The previously validated, 4-question Rapid Appraisal for Trafficking (RAFT) screening tool for identifying trafficked patients in the ED was adopted for use in this study.11 The dichotomous questions gauge a person’s risk for being trafficked, where at least one affirmative response indicates a positive screen (Fig 1).

Rapid appraisal for trafficking screening questions.

A list of local HT resources was developed through collaboration with the center’s social workers, community partners, and a survivor of HT. These were used, in addition to national resources, to develop a resource database for use in referring trafficked patients to appropriate care. The database includes resources to assist with food and housing, medical care, legal assistance, addiction services, and safety planning.

A screening and referral protocol was developed through UCMC’s pre-existing Early Intervention Program (EIP). EIP is a public health promotion program operating within the ED and the surrounding community to screen and provide services for diseases of public health interest, such as HIV and substance use.12,13 The program uses various screening tools, including the WHO SRH SHAPE14 for sexual health, the AUDIT-C15, and the NIDA-Modified ASSIST16 for substance use, and the PHQ-917 and GAD718 tools for mental health screening. The RAFT tool was added to EIP’s existing screening questions. EIP Health Promotion Advocates (HPAs) are undergraduate students trained to perform public health risk assessment screening using motivational interviewing and trauma-informed care. EIP linkage coordinators (LCs) possess the same background as HPAs and are additionally trained in facilitating warm handoffs between patients and advanced health services, such as substance use treatment. During the study period, the EIP operated 7 days a week from 8 am to midnight. Typical shift coverage consists of 2 HPAs on weekday shifts and one on weekend shifts. LCs are available weekdays from 9 am to 5 pm.

EIP HPAs screen a convenience sample of ED patients using a REDCap survey, targeting those with chief complaints related to substance use or sexually transmitted infections or those referred by providers. All patients are interviewed alone due to the sensitive nature of the assessment questions. If a risky behavior or unmet need is identified, HPAs intervene with education, formal behavioral counseling techniques, and referrals to resources. When advanced assistance is desired, HPAs engage with an LC who can provide more intensive evaluation and sustained assistance for up to several months following the initial encounter. The need for this is determined through the risk assessment conversation between the HPA and the patient and is based on screening scores and patient request. Follow up is every 30-60-90 days and can be in person at the ED, in a public area like the library, or over the phone.

Due to the EIP’s unique position and integration within the ED, it was optimally positioned to expand its screening to include HT. Prior to implementation, EIP staff underwent a brief training providing an overview of HT, red flags to recognize, and guidance on implementation of the screening and referral process. Staff had the opportunity to practice screening and intervention via role-play simulation.

Selection of Participants

In December 2022, EIP staff began incorporating the RAFT questions into their risk assessment, with the goal of screening for HT during as many patient assessments as possible. Patients 18 years and older were included. EIP staff were also encouraged to monitor the ED track board for chief complaints, such as STI exposure or assault, and screen those patients as well. Patients presenting with a chief complaint of sexual assault were not approached without a specific request from a provider due to current ED policies. ED providers were encouraged to contact EIP to screen any patients whom they were concerned may be at risk for HT.

Interventions

When patients screen positive for HT risk, HPAs offer to refer patients to resources, which patients may accept or decline. Patients needing immediate safety planning are assisted in calling the National Human Trafficking Hotline, with social work available 24/7 in the ED to provide additional assistance. Others are referred to resources using the database created at the outset of the project. Patients are given the option to receive a small paper handout or QR code or to be directly assisted through phone calls to relevant community partners. To make them inconspicuous, QR codes are sized to fit in a phone case and initially link to a nondescript page. Patients can click a small link at the bottom of the page to access the list of resources, which can be filtered by need and eligibility requirements.

When advanced assistance is desired rather than simple referral, the HPA engages with an LC, who can provide more intensive evaluation and sustained assistance. Patients were the primary drivers of which interventions were undertaken, with HPAs offering options and providing resources as requested by patients. This is in line with current recommendations on addressing trafficking, which stipulate that patients should be treated as the experts on their specific situations and given agency about how those situations are addressed.9

Measurements

EIP staff recorded answers to the RAFT screening tool in REDCap. For all patients who screened positive, EIP staff recorded whether patients accepted referrals to resources, and if so, which resources.

Additional data collection was performed through the online resource database, accessed by patients through the QR code provided to them. The initial page prompts patients to select resources based on their needs. Once the patient clicks to the next page to access the selected resources, the site records which resource categories were selected. Through this filtering tool, the site is able to track the frequency with which patients search for specific resources, such as those related to substance use or housing. The site does not record Internet Protocol addresses or any other personal information about site visitors and therefore cannot distinguish whether 2 separate instances of resource selection were performed by the same or different patients.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measured was the proportion of patients who screened positive for HT risk that accepted referrals to resources. Secondary outcomes included the number of patients who were screened for HT risk, the number who screened positive, and the number who accepted referrals. As described previously, EIP staff routinely screen for other problems aside from HT and provide relevant referrals. Therefore, another secondary outcome measure was the frequency with which patients who screened positive for HT risk accepted referrals to various types of resources, whether trafficking specific or not. The aim behind this secondary outcome was to gather information about the social needs of patients who screened positive in order to better inform future, targeted efforts toward serving these patients.

Analysis

Data were pulled from REDCap and the online resource database and was analyzed by a senior data analyst. Analysis of the data was done using R (version 4.3.3). The number of patients assessed for HT risk, along with the number (percentage) screened positive and the number (percentage) referred for further services, was calculated. Characteristics of study patients were summarized using descriptive statistics by screening status and shown as the median (interquartile range) for continuous age and count (percentage) for all other categoric variables. The count (percentage) of EIP resources used was calculated. An analysis of the differences between the population who was screened for HT risk compared to all those who received an EIP assessment was also conducted, with mean differences and 95% confidence intervals presented.

Results

Characteristics of Study Subjects

From December 2022, when ED screening began, through November 2023, the UCMC ED recorded 55,332 patient encounters with 37,081 unique patients. During the same time frame, the EIP completed 1646 general risk assessments on 1504 unique patients (4.1% of ED patients). During these assessments, 557 patients (37% of patients who received an EIP assessment) were screened for HT risk using the RAFT tool. Main Results

As shown in Table, of the 557 patients assessed for HT risk, 69 screened positive (12.4%), meaning they answered “yes” to at least one of the 4 screening questions. Of the patients who screened positive, 39 patients (56.5%) accepted referrals to HT-specific resources, and 60 patients (87%) accepted referrals to any resources provided by EIP, whether HT specific or not. Resources provided that were not specific to HT included assistance with housing, food services, transportation, substance use, general health, and treatment for HIV or mental illness.

Limitations

Because this project was carried out through UCMC’s EIP, not all patients presenting to the ED were screened for HT risk. Most likely, some trafficked patients who presented during the study period were missed, meaning the total number of patients identified by screening was probably low. However, because EIP interacts with an under-resourced subpopulation of the ED, the percentage of patients assessed who screened positive is likely higher than it would have been if patients had been randomly selected from the general ED population.

Additionally, only 37% of patients assessed by EIP were asked the RAFT questions for HT. HPAs cited various reasons for this, including natural disruptions to medical care. HPAs also indicated that if another, more pressing, need was identified earlier in the risk assessment, they focused on assisting the patient with that need and did not complete the rest of the assessment, which would have included HT screening questions. The placement of the RAFT questions toward the end of EIP’s REDCap survey was likely a contributing factor. Furthermore, patients have the option to decline to answer questions. If they choose to do so, answers to those questions are simply not recorded; HPAs do not record in REDCap when patients decline screening. Finally, the possibility of evaluator bias in selecting patients for screening cannot be eliminated, as patients screened were more likely to be younger and female when compared with the general population assessed. Table S1 provides demographic information about patients who were screened for HT versus those who were not.

The data collected through the online resource database was also limited. Because it was designed to protect patient privacy, the site is not able to track unique visitors. Therefore, some resource searches may be conducted by the same patient visiting the site more than once. The study also did not have the resources to track whether referrals led to successful assistance; additional research in this area would be helpful.

Another limitation of this study is that its findings are specific to the UCMC ED. This is an urban, safety net hospital in a state with easy highway access to multiple bordering states, which may place its residents at a higher risk of trafficking. Given the difference in patient populations served by various EDs across the country, other sites may not encounter the same number of trafficked patients. Furthermore, there are likely to be differences in the proportions of labor versus sex trafficking in different areas of the country, as well as differences in patient demographics and social needs, which may necessitate different approaches to the provision of resources.

The role of EIP, which is unique to UCMC, also had a significant impact on the study methods and results. Implementation through EIP offloaded work from providers and facilitated more in-depth patient conversations than providers typically have time to engage in. Consequently, EIP staff were able to build relationships with patients, which likely made them more successful in eliciting patients’ needs and intervening effectively. Using EIP was also logistically more straightforward because the process and infrastructure for screening and intervention were already in place, and staff were already trained in sensitive interviewing techniques. Nevertheless, an ED-wide initiative would cast a broader net to screen for trafficked patients. Furthermore, the use of EIP in this study makes it more difficult for other centers to replicate it. Hopefully, the approach taken here could be adapted for other clinical environments depending on available resources. For example, a screening and referral process could be initiated by social workers, community health workers, or providers.

Discussion

The results confirm that HT is a problem affecting patients presenting to a large urban ED. Although the number of positive screens is small when compared with the overall ED population (0.2%), this is likely due to incomplete screening secondary to the limitations described above. A similar study screened 26,974 patients for HT and found a positive screen rate of 0.7%.19 At our site, universal screening would likely have identified additional cases. Furthermore, a high incidence is not the only indicator of clinical significance. Many disease presentations in emergency medicine are rare, but nevertheless deadly, and are therefore still important to study and manage appropriately. Because national research indicates trafficking is a problem in every state, the majority of EDs likely encounter some number of trafficked patients.3 The number of patients in this study who accepted referrals to resources indicates that many patients are willing to accept assistance.

The data also indicate that HT is a complex social problem, and effective interventions need to be nuanced. Given that just over half of patients who screened positive accepted HT-specific resources, it is likely that either the services themselves, the availability of those services, or the way they were provided did not fully meet the needs of many patients. Interestingly, more patients accepted resources pertaining to other social needs, particularly substance use disorders, housing, and mental health. Our data indicate a significant overlap between trafficking risk and substance use in particular; 52.2% of patients who screened positive by RAFT endorsed a history of substance use disorder, defined as screening positive on the NIDA-modified ASSIST. Because traffickers create and prey upon social risk factors, this is not surprising. It may be more effective to tailor interventions to other social drivers, such as housing or substance use, that increase individual risk for trafficking.

Ongoing research is needed to determine which resources and interventions are most helpful. This may be accomplished by surveying patients and community partners and by attempting to follow up with patients after their ED visits to determine whether their needs were met by the referrals provided. The online resource database created for this study includes a means for site visitors to submit anonymous feedback about resources, which may also lead to helpful information.

In conclusion, HT is a major human rights violation with significant impacts on health that affects patients who present to the ED. ED-based HT screening and intervention can help identify trafficked patients and refer them to resources. Understanding the complex social needs of patients, as well as the local resources available, is crucial for effective intervention.
