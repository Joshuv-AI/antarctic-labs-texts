Aerodynamics and Laryngeal Kinematics Following Anterior (Wendler) Glottoplasty: A Case Study
Authors: Victoria S McKenna, Gregory R Dion, Rita Patel, Renee L Gustin, Jules Madzia, Shanna D Stryker, Sarah Pickle, Mary E Wilkens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA transgender woman underwent anterior (Wendler) glottoplasty and was followed for 1‐year. We found desired pitch improvements, but also undesired changes to intensity, vocal efficiency, laryngeal vibratory kinematics, and vocal effort. This case highlights the importance of considering multimodal assessment in clinical follow‐up and outcome analysis for these patients.Keywords: aerodynamics, anterior glottoplasty, laryngeal kinematics, outcomes, voice

Introduction

Anterior glottoplasty, also known as Wendler glottoplasty, is a procedure that shortens the vibrating portion of the vocal folds to raise vocal pitch. Specifically, this involves removing the epithelial layer of the anterior vocal folds and suturing them together to create an anterior web. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis of 23 studies revealed significant improvements in speaking fundamental frequency (f o) and voice‐related quality of life (QOL) [1]. However, limited literature also supports a reduction in maximum phonation time (MPT) [1] and vocal loudness [2], and an increase in subglottal pressure [3, 4], indicating negative voice outcomes as well. Currently, there is a lack of information on how to make surgical decisions that maximize positive outcomes and reduce unwanted acoustic and physiological changes in this population.

Quantitative measures that include aerodynamics and vibratory kinematics are key to improving knowledge of the newly configured laryngeal mechanism and a foundational physiological basis for desired and undesired patient outcomes. Here, we present a case that includes quantitative tracking to suggest additional measures for research and clinical endeavors.

Case History

After informed consent, data were prospectively collected at the University of Cincinnati (IRB#2021‐0995).

The patient was a 50‐year‐old transgender woman. She completed 16 sessions of gender‐affirming voice and communication therapy from a speech‐language pathologist. She then sought pitch elevation surgery to assist in reducing cognitive effort while maintaining a feminine speaking pitch. The patient's history was unremarkable for asthma, smoking, or reflux.

The patient underwent anterior glottoplasty in the typical fashion, with the carbon dioxide laser used to de‐epithelialize the anterior third of the bilateral true vocal folds followed by suture closure of the area with 4‐0 vicryl sutures and tissue glue. The patient completed 5 days of vocal rest and then re‐engaged in voice therapy, where she attended six sessions focused on pitch retraining and resonance shaping. Additional saline nebulization was incorporated into home treatments to assist with vocal fatigue. During surveillance, a suture granuloma developed at the anterior commissure that was removed in‐office with a KTP laser 6 months post‐surgery. No other post‐surgical complications or significant events were noted.

Data Collection

The patient completed voice assessments at five timepoints: baseline (prior to surgery), 3‐, 6‐, 9‐, and 12‐months post‐surgery. All data collection was overseen by the same researcher (author V.M.) for continuity across research sessions. Acoustic data were collected via a headset microphone obtained at 40,000 Hz, aerodynamic assessment was completed with the Phonatory Aerodynamic System (PENTAX 6600), and high‐speed rigid laryngoscopy (Phantom VEO‐E 340 L) was completed at 4000 frames per second and 512 × 512 pixels. Baseline productions were obtained while producing a feminine voice. Quantitative parameters included: (1) acoustics: mean f o (Hz), harmonics‐to‐noise ratio (dB), and vocal intensity (dB SPL) from sustained /i:/; (2) aerodynamics: airflow (L/s) from vowel /i:/, as well as subglottal pressure (cm H2O), and vocal efficiency ratio [5] (dB SPL/cm H2O) from /pi‐pi‐pi/ productions; and (3) mean and standard deviation (SD) laryngeal vibratory kinematics over 100 phonatory cycles from /i:/ that included measures of glottal dynamics, mechanics, and symmetry. The patient also completed formal and informal questionnaires assessing voice‐related QOL and effort at each timepoint (Table 1).

Results

As expected, the patient exhibited post‐surgical vowel f o within established feminine ranges (M = 207.12 ± 22 Hz). She had improvements in voice‐related QOL, as evidenced by lower scores on the Transgender Self‐Evaluation Questionnaire, reduced voice‐gender incongruence, and higher voice satisfaction. She also self‐reported lower amounts of cognitive effort to maintain a feminine voice, though these remained moderate (Table 2).

At all timepoints after surgery, the patient reported relatively high physical vocal effort (as measured by an average of 61 on a 0–100 mm scale). This finding was potentially due to a small anterior gap during phonation and a reduction in vocal intensity by an average of 5.7 dB SPL post‐surgery. Further, she showed a reduction in vocal efficiency, which provides information about the aerodynamic power driving the acoustic output [5], with lower values related to increased vocal effort. She reported difficulties being heard in environments with high levels of background noise (e.g., restaurants), and subsequent vocal fatigue, though neither were directly measured here.

There were marked changes in vibratory kinematics across all measures when examining baseline to post‐surgical timepoints. There was a consistent increase in open phase duration, and further reductions in peak closing velocity and maximum area declination rate, all possibly driving the decrease in vocal intensity. Standard deviations were consistent and unchanged post‐surgery, indicating stabilization of each vibrational measure. There were no apparent changes in phase symmetry or periodicity, which were consistent with her harmonic‐to‐noise ratio acoustic data that indicated a maintenance of vocal quality. However, this finding is contrary to hypothesized aperiodicity modeled by Titze et al. [2] showing a need for further research on aperiodicity variations due to surgical approaches such as suture depth and symmetry.

The results of the present study generally reflect findings from the recent literature [1, 3, 4], but a single case report is not sufficient to generalize findings to the larger surgical population. One reason this case may vary from others is her pre‐ and post‐surgical voice therapy. Voice therapy likely impacted all measures reported here and may not be comparable to another patient who did not undergo any voice therapy. Therefore, documentation of voice therapy, with respect to surgical timing, is important for interpretation of any physiological change from baseline or in the 1‐year follow‐up period.

Discussion

This case highlights the benefits to collecting aerodynamic and vibratory kinematic data pre‐ and post‐surgery, as some measures hold promise as quantitative counterparts to patient‐reported experience.

Quantitative metrics could help to improve our approaches to surgery by individualizing surgical decisions. For example, Rogalska et al. [1] reported that surgical approaches suture between 33% and 50% of the true vocal folds, but to our knowledge, there is no specific pre‐surgical metric to motivate the proportion of suture. Titze et al. [2] modeled the tradeoff between suture length and acoustic outcomes, showing that increasing the proportion of length did, indeed, increase f o but also resulted in a reduction of vocal intensity. Additionally, surgical feminization approaches can be used in combination with one another (e.g., anterior glottoplasty and laser‐assisted voice adjustment), however, there is limited knowledge on how to leverage these two approaches to reach feminine pitch and reduce undesired vocal changes.

Computational models, physical models, and simulations have helped researchers and clinicians to understand various voice conditions [6, 7, 8]. Mau et al. [9] used a finite element simulation to compare different surgical approaches and determine which may be more appropriate to the targeted voice outcomes of f o–SPL range and subglottal pressure. Although the simulation was completed in a different patient population than the one described in this case report, it provides an example of how modeling can provide individualized surgical approaches for patients. Further, Alzamendi et al. [10] developed a lumped‐element model of phonation that utilized glottal airflow and vibratory kinematics as inputs for an individual patient. In this computational approach, physiological data were key to determining voice outcomes of subglottal pressure and vocal fold contact pressures.

In the future, a larger quantitative dataset is needed to develop and validate vocal fold models. These models could be manipulated to predict post‐surgical acoustic and aerodynamic outcomes and perhaps provide information relevant to pre‐surgical candidacy. We recommend researchers and clinicians consider incorporating further quantitative measures into their clinical evaluation and monitoring of patients undergoing anterior glottoplasty.

Conclusion Summary

Here we described aerodynamic and vibratory kinematic changes following anterior glottoplasty over 1 year. Noted reductions in vocal efficiency and changes to vibratory parameters hold promise for improving our understanding of the physiological changes driven by anatomical adjustments to the vocal folds. Further research focused on quantitative parameters and laryngeal system modeling is needed to improve our approaches to surgical care and bolster patient outcomes.
