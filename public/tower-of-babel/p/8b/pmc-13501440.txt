Bronchiectasis has gained global recognition, yet pathophysiological understanding has not kept pace, hampered by clinical heterogeneity and decades of low research priority [1]. Therapeutic innovation has also lagged behind the predominant airway diseases, asthma and COPD, where a precision medicine framework has matured [2, 3]. The heterogeneity of bronchiectasis – encompassing variable clinical phenotypes, comorbidities and outcomes – offers a foundation for building such a framework, although critical gaps remain.

Endotypes with differential therapeutic responses have been proposed in bronchiectasis. A landmark multicentre European study identified four subgroups via cluster analysis, each with distinct microbiome profiles and exacerbation risks, differentiated largely by extent of neutrophilic and type 2 (T2) inflammation [4]. Where eosinophils now guide inhaled corticosteroid and biologic use in asthma and COPD, no equivalent biomarker has been validated in bronchiectasis – and T2 inflammation has remained largely peripheral to the pathophysiological discussion dominated by neutrophilic disease [5]. Yet cohort and registry studies report elevated T2 inflammation in 20–30% of people with bronchiectasis, based primarily on peripheral eosinophils [6–8]. T2 and neutrophilic inflammation are not mutually exclusive [9], but the existance of a T2-driven dominant subpopulation provides a rationale for targeted therapy.

The T2 endotype in bronchiectasis

T2 inflammation is an immune cascade in which cytokines interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5 and IL-13 mediate airway inflammation predominated by eosinophil activation and degranulation, IgE class switching of B-cells, mast cell activation, and macrophage alternative activation. Downstream effects include mucus hypersecretion, airway injury and remodelling [10]. Biomarkers commonly used to identify T2 inflammation in clinical care of airways disease include IgE, exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FENO) and eosinophil concentrations. While obtaining all available biomarkers to classify disease is not required, a targeted combination of tests can provide nuance in predicting treatment response, which has been best characterised in asthma. For instance, IgE elevation in the absence of elevated blood eosinophils suggests that allergic inflammation predominates over systemic T2 activation [11]. Elevated FENO, an airway-specific biomarker, implicates airway-epithelial T2 signalling when blood eosinophils are normal or low, identifying individuals who may responds to inhaled corticosteroids or biologics targeting epithelial-directed T2 cytokines (e.g. IL-4 and IL-13). Overall, T2 inflammation is complex and heterogeneous, with room for novel therapies that can preferentially target discrete aspects of the response [12].

Historically, bronchiectasis management has centred on treatable traits – clinical phenotypes with differential therapeutic responses informed by aetiotypes and comorbidities thought to cause or perpetuate disease [13, 14]. Asthma and allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) are two examples, both primarily T2-driven. Yet, clinical experience and registry studies identify elevated T2 biomarkers in individuals with bronchiectasis even without these classic comorbidities [7, 15]. While inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) represent a logical therapeutic test for T2-driven disease [16], prolonged use is associated with chronic airways infections, including with nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) [17]. Empiric or chronic ICS prescription therefore warrants careful consideration, particularly in the USA where NTM pulmonary infection is prevalent in the bronchiectasis population [18].

Against this backdrop, the clinical utility of biologic therapy in bronchiectasis has emerged as a key question for many experts. In this issue of ERJ Open Research, Chalmers et al. [19] present MAHALE, the first randomised controlled trial evaluating the efficacy of a T2-directed biologic in reducing bronchiectasis exacerbations. As reported, the authors endeavoured a large international phase 3 double blinded placebo-controlled trial evaluating benralizumab, an antibody targeting the IL-5 receptor α-chain on eosinophils and basophils that results in rapid cell depletion [20]. Participants had confirmed bronchiectasis, at least two exacerbations in the preceding year, and were excluded if they were determined by the enrolling clinician to have comorbid COPD, asthma, cystic fibrosis or any other aetiology for elevated peripheral eosinophils. Enrolment was stratified 2:1 by baseline eosinophil count ≥300 and <300 cells per µL with the intention to evaluate response in both eosinophil-high and -low populations.

Despite robust efforts, conclusions are constrained by several limitations, the most critical being slow recruitment and early closure of the study due to challenges inflicted by the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result, only 100 of the planned 420 participants were randomised, including just 21 with blood eosinophil concentrations ≥300 cells per µL. Chalmers et al. [19] cautiously report that benralizumab failed to reduce bronchiectasis exacerbation rates but acknowledge statistical power limits any conclusion. Therefore, a cautious approach is necessary, and a negative study should not be used as evidence that T2 inflammation is not a therapeutically relevant target in bronchiectasis. Rather, this pioneering work should inform future study design of trials focusing on T2 biologics in bronchiectasis.

Trial design imperatives directed at T2 inflammation in bronchiectasis

What is the correct target?

Advancing T2-directed therapy in bronchiectasis hinges on identifying the optimal biologic target. Available T2 biologics in asthma act on key nodes in the inflammatory cascade (figure 1): IgE-mediated mast cell and basophil activation (omalizumab); circuling IL-5-driven eosinophil biology (mepolizumab and reslizumab); IL-5 receptor-mediated eosinophil depletion (benralizumab); the upstream epithelial alarmin thymic–stromal lymphopoietin that initiates and amplifies T2 inflammation (tezepelumab); and IL-4 and IL-13 signalling, which drive IgE class switching, Th2 differentiation and mucus hypersecretion (dupilumab) [21]. Collectively, these therapies underscore that T2 inflammation operates as a network rather than a single pathway, and that the optimal point of intervention may vary across diseases and subpopulations.

In contrast to asthma, COPD is typically characterised by neutrophilic inflammation with approximately 20–40% of patients exhibiting features of T2 inflammation [21]. Early phase 3 trials of IL-5 pathway biologics in COPD, including mepolizumab and benralizumab, yielded mixed or negative results, in part due to low eosinophil thresholds for enrolment. Subsequent analyses demonstrated greater treatment responses at higher baseline blood eosinophil concentrations, informing later trials that adopted stricter thresholds (≥300 cells per µL). In these studies, efficacy signals became more consistent: dupilumab reduced exacerbations and improved lung function, while mepolizumab reduced exacerbations without consistent improvements in symptoms or lung function [22, 23]. In contrast, benralizumab did not significantly reduce exacerbations despite effective eosinophil depletion in a recent large phase 3 trial [24].

These findings highlight two key considerations for bronchiectasis. First, when T2 inflammation is not the predominant driver, more stringent biomarker thresholds may be required to identify responders, in contrast to asthma where lower eosinophil cutoffs (e.g. ≥150 cells per µL) are often sufficient. Second, T2 inflammation is not biologically uniform; primarily targeting eosinophils may be insufficient when upstream or parallel pathways, including IL-4/IL-13 signalling or epithelial alarmins, are more central [25]. This is particularly relevant to MAHALE, which targeted IL-5 receptor-mediated eosinophil depletion. As in COPD, if key features of bronchiectasis are driven by airway epithelial processes such as mucus hypersecretion and remodelling, the lack of efficacy may reflect not only the limited power but also a suboptimal target selection, and more proximal T2 inhibition may be better mechanistically aligned. Defining the appropriate therapeutic target thus remains a critical next step in future trial design.

Who is the target population?

While imperfect, phenoendotyping is essential for stratifying patients by predicted response to T2-directed therapy. In MAHALE, enrolment was stratified using peripheral eosinophil thresholds based on prior cohort studies that demonstrated blood eosinophil concentrations ≥300 cells per μL are associated with significantly shorter time to first exacerbation and greater exacerbation rates [6, 7, 26]. While offering a pragmatic approach to trial design, relying on a single dichotomous cutoff to determine eligibility risks misclassification and may inadequately capture the spectrum of multidimensional T2 airway inflammation, a limitation similarly recognised in COPD [27]. Furthermore, most biomarkers, including eosinophils, fluctuate over time due to biological variations or analytic imprecision, which is a challenge when stratification relies on fixed thresholds.

Further defining the target population is complicated by comorbid airways diseases, often overlapping and themselves potentially T2-driven. MAHALE sought to isolate a bronchiectasis-pure cohort, excluding comorbid asthma or COPD, to determine whether T2-directed therapy is independently beneficial. However, imperfect classification of airway diseases and a clinical reality of overlap syndromes cannot be fully mitigated by exclusion criteria. This limitation reinforces the case for comprehensive biomarker characterisation at enrolment, including FENO, IgE, bronchodilator responses and potentially bronchial provocation testing [28, 29]. A more granular stratification incorporating complementary biomarkers may better identify biologically and therapeutically meaningful subgroups.

Is it all or nothing?

The rate and onset of exacerbations have served as the primary endpoint in most bronchiectasis therapeutic trials, as exacerbations are associated with the shape of quality of life, lung function and disease progression [30]. Symptom burden is also an important outcome for many individuals, and T2-mediated inflammation may be a greater contributor to symptoms than other pathological mediators. Thus, trials targeting T2 inflammation should continue to consider the secondary evaluation of chronic cough, mucus production and lung function. Validated instruments to capture daily symptom variability could provide important complementary data while concurrently supporting quantitative data for the primary outcome of interest [31].

Conclusion

MAHALE represents a meaningful step toward precision medicine in bronchiectasis, expanding toward a goal to interrogate T2 inflammation as a therapeutic target. Though negative, this work brings greater attention to T2 inflammation as a possible contributor to bronchiectasis outcomes. Despite significant circumstantial limitations, a negative trial alone should not foreclose this avenue. Rather, the field should consider key lessons to guide choice of therapy, precision phenoendotyping, multidimensional stratification and secondary endpoints in future trials targeting this inflammatory cascade.
