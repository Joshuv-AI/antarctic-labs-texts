Efficacy of using a mechanical insufflation-exsufflation versus manual respiratory physiotherapy on cough peak flow in hospitalised older adults in French hospitals: a protocol for a single-blind randomised clinical trial
Authors: Claire Estenne, Lech Dobija, Mathilde Pelletier-Visa, Bruno Pereira, Emmanuel Coudeyre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAgeing impairs the respiratory system, increasing hospitalisation rates for respiratory problems in older adults. In cases of bronchial congestion, a weak cough in older inpatients challenges respiratory physiotherapy. Mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI-E) is one of the promising solutions. However, its effects have not yet been evaluated in this population. Therefore, we aim to compare the effects of MI-E and manual respiratory physiotherapy on cough peak flow (CPF), spirometry measures, dyspnoea and session discomfort in hospitalised older adults.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a multicentre randomised controlled trial in four hospitals in France. A total of 120 older inpatients with bronchial congestion and a weak cough will be recruited from either intensive care units or conventional wards. All participants will receive three sessions of respiratory physiotherapy over one and a half days: MI-E for the experimental group and conventional manual respiratory therapy for the control group. Assessments will include CPF, forced vital capacity (FVC), maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressures, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), FEV1/FVC ratio and the modified Borg scale for dyspnoea, measured before and immediately after each session. Discomfort will be assessed using a numerical (0–10) scale immediately after each session. Between-group comparisons will be performed using a linear mixed model for continuous outcomes and a generalised linear model for categorical variables.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the referred ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile de France XI, Identifier: 24.04832.000282) on 27 November 2024. Study results will be disseminated in peer-reviewed publications and at scientific meetings.Trial registration numberNCT06730217.Keywords: Aged, RESPIRATORY MEDICINE (see Thoracic Medicine), Respiratory Therapy

Introduction

Despite the temporary disruption caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, global life expectancy continues to rise. The increase in the number of older adults in the population leads to more health issues that need to be managed.1 Consequently, the hospitalisation rate for individuals over 65 years is increasing, with respiratory problems being one of the most common causes.2 Ageing negatively affects the respiratory system by deconditioning respiratory muscles, increasing thoracic stiffness, reducing pulmonary vital capacity and increasing residual volume. These mechanisms can compromise ventilation, mucus clearance and cough efficacy, all essential for preventing bronchial congestion.3 The latter is often present on admission for respiratory pathology but can also be secondary to other causes of hospitalisation, such as musculoskeletal consequences of a fall.4 5 Hospitalisation often involves prolonged bed rest and immobility, which can impair respiratory function and increase the risk of respiratory complications.6 Infectious or viral pneumonia is a common complication that can have fatal consequences for older adults. In the USA, 1 million older inpatients are hospitalised for this pathology, and 30% of them die within the year.7 Older adults are more susceptible to pneumonia due to impaired gag reflex, reduced mucociliary function, weakened immunity, impaired fever response and various degrees of cardiopulmonary dysfunction. Additionally, central nervous system disorders or impaired gag reflexes increase the risk of aspiration pneumonia in older patients.8 9 The majority of older adults develop a productive cough, but their ability to cough effectively is often reduced.9 The cough peak flow (CPF) measurement can detect an ineffective cough and help identify individuals at risk of respiratory complications.10 11 The minimum CPF required to ensure clearance of the proximal airways is 160 L/min.12 The risk of respiratory complications increases when the CPF is below 270 L/min, as coughing strength can quickly deteriorate and fall below the critical threshold of 160 L/min.13

In the presence of bronchial congestion and an impaired cough, respiratory physiotherapy can help expel bronchial secretions and improve airway clearance.14 15 Usually, manual or instrumental techniques are employed, with the former including manually assisted cough using thoracic or abdominal compression and the latter involving mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MI-E) using a special device. The effect of MI-E on airway clearance has been reported to be superior to that of manual techniques alone in patients hospitalised in an intensive care unit (ICU) and in those with neuromuscular problems.14 16 Moreover, respiratory parameters have shown improvement with this technique in patients with neuromuscular and respiratory problems.17 On the other hand, a recent systematic review revealed that the evidence supporting the use of MI-E is limited by the methodological quality of the reviewed studies; therefore, further well-designed studies are needed.18

A special focus on the use of MI-E in older inpatients seems necessary, taking into account demographic patterns and the age-related changes in the respiratory system discussed above. Our pilot study showed that older inpatients tolerated MI-E sessions well, reporting low discomfort, no significant changes in vital signs and no adverse effects.19 The Borg scale of dyspnoea decreased significantly from 4±3 before to 3±2 after the first MI-E session (p=0.004) and even further to 1±1 after the fourth session (p=0.002). Additionally, cough efficacy improved, as recorded by an increase in CPF from before the first MI-E session to after the fourth MI-E session (121.9±44.4 vs 162.3±50.8; p<0.001). However, these results were preliminary and not sufficiently powered to draw a robust conclusion on the effect of MI-E in older inpatients. Therefore, we will conduct a randomised controlled trial to evaluate the effect of MI-E. The primary objective of this study is to compare the effects of MI-E with manual respiratory physiotherapy on CPF spirometry in hospitalised older adults. The secondary objectives are to compare the randomised groups based on additional spirometry outcomes, as well as to make assessments of discomfort and dyspnoea during the study.

Methods and analysis

Study design

We will conduct a multicentre, prospective, parallel-group, superiority, single-blind randomised controlled trial in France. Participants will be randomised at a 1:1 ratio into one of two groups: control (manual respiratory physiotherapy) or experimental (MI-E). The present study protocol adheres to the requirements of the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT).20 The corresponding SPIRIT checklist is presented in online supplemental material. The results will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Statement.21 22 The study schedule is presented in figure 1.

Participants

We will include 120 hospitalised older adults aged 60 years and over who suffer from bronchial congestion and an inefficient cough, characterised by a CPF of less than 180 L/min and warranting respiratory physiotherapy management. This CPF cut-off criterion was chosen based on a minimal cough efficacy limit of 160 L/min, with an additional 20 L/min added to account for measurement error.12 Thus, we will include patients with inefficient cough and those with uncertain cough efficacy. Recruitment and study setting

Participants will be recruited from among patients admitted to ICUs and conventional medicine wards, including downstream emergency units and pulmonology units. To achieve adequate participant enrolment and reach the target sample size, recruitment will take place in four French hospitals: Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital and the Hospices Civils de Lyon (Edouard Herriot, Saint Joseph-Saint Luc and Louis Pradel). The study is planned to run from 15 September 2025 to 18 September 2028, with recruitment ending on 15 September 2028.

The physician or physiotherapist involved in the study will screen older inpatients based on the inclusion criteria and will provide information about the study to those who meet them. Patients can take their time to consider whether to participate in the study, and any questions they have about the study will be addressed.

Assignment of interventions

The randomisation process will be conducted prior to the commencement of the trial by the data manager and biostatistician at the Clinical Research and Innovation Department of the Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital. The generated randomisation list will then be securely and centrally stored. This process will involve stratified randomisation based on the centre, the type of unit (ICU or conventional medicine ward) and the patient age (< or >75 years). The Clinical Research Associate (CRA) will use Ennov Clinical (V.8.2) software to execute the randomisation for each centre and will inform the physiotherapist. The randomisation procedure will be blinded to the second assessor responsible for conducting the second evaluation. Patients will be assigned randomly into one of two groups (1:1).

Interventions

Participants in both the control and experimental groups will receive three sessions of respiratory physiotherapy: the first in the morning and the second in the afternoon on the first day and the third in the morning of the following day (figure 1). If inclusion in the study begins in the afternoon, one session will be performed on the first day and two on the following day. In both groups, each session will last approximately 15 min and will not exceed 20 min. The duration will depend on clinical factors such as the patient’s fatigue, the amount of bronchial secretion to expel and the quality of participation. In the case of any adverse effects, such as a significant worsening of dyspnoea or other clinical parameters, or on participant request, the intervention will be stopped. All such situations will be noted in the case report form (CRF), and the participant will be excluded from the study. Each patient will be involved in the study for one and a half days.

Participants in the control group will receive manual respiratory physiotherapy. In each session, a physiotherapist experienced in respiratory physiotherapy will use the Expiratory Flow Augmentation (EFA) technique with abdominal and thoracic pressure. This is a standard procedure used in patients with bronchial congestion and inefficient cough in order to promote bronchial decongestion.12 23 Participants will be in a semisupine position at 45° on a bed or in a sitting position in a chair. Fast and slow EFA techniques will be performed, followed by controlled coughing. An experienced physiotherapist will verbally stimulate the patient to participate in the employed techniques and will adapt to the patient’s respiratory rhythm and the presence of secretions.

In the experimental group, participants will receive MI-E sessions using an EOVE-70 instrument, Air Liquide Medical Systems France. First, a physiotherapist experienced in respiratory physiotherapy will explain the function of the MI-E to the participant. Then, the physiotherapist will set the device parameters based on the participant’s characteristics and Chatwin’s algorithm to obtain optimal cough efficacy.24 The initial MI-E sets sought to allow a synchronisation of the patient’s effort with the device and adjustment of the device parameters. Similar to the control group, participants will be in a semisupine position at 45° on a bed or in a sitting position in a chair. The MI-E device will deliver a controlled positive pressure to the participant’s lungs to enhance inspiration volume, subsequently switching to negative pressure simulating a strong cough. These two phases will be repeated several times depending on the patient’s needs and tolerance. The participant will be encouraged to engage in the delivered phases, perform the best respiratory volume and expel the secretions.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the variation in CPF before and immediately after each of the three sessions. In total, six CPF measurements will be taken using an electronic spirometer (Datospir Touch Easy, Sibelmed, Rosellon 500 bajos, 08026 Barcelona, Spain). The results will be recorded in L/min and then expressed as the percentage change from the pre-session value and will be measured in both the control and experimental groups.

Conventionally, CPF is measured using a pneumotachograph.13 In our study, however, CPF will be assessed with a spirometer connected to a nasobuccal mask, using the spirometer mode typically employed for peak expiratory flow. Participants will be asked to perform a maximal inspiration, followed by coughing with maximal effort into the nasobuccal mask. Specifically, we will use the same mask that is employed for MI-E. An experienced evaluator will hold the mask on the participant’s face, ensuring that there is no air leakage between the mask and the face. Three attempts will be made and the maximal value will be used for analysis.

The CPF is the maximum instantaneous airflow rate achieved during a coughing effort. This parameter reflects cough efficacy and indirectly provides information about the degree of bronchial obstruction.13 Furthermore, this parameter shows high reliability, its criterion validity has been confirmed by positive correlations with other spirometric measurements, and it demonstrates predictive value for the risk of aspiration.25

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes include spirometry parameters related to respiratory function, assessed using the same electronic spirometer equipped with maximum respiratory pressures module at the same time points as the primary outcome. Each participant will make three attempts of 6 s inspiratory and expiratory effort, and the maximal value will be used for analysis.

Furthermore, session discomfort will be evaluated immediately after each session using a numerical scale (0=no discomfort; 10=very uncomfortable), with three evaluations per participant—after sessions 1, 2 and 3. This evaluation was found to be easy for participants to understand and time-efficient in our previous pilot study.19

Finally, dyspnoea will be evaluated before and after each session using the modified Borg scale (0=no shortness of breath; 10=maximum shortness of breath experienced during the session). All of the secondary outcomes will be measured in both the control and the experimental groups.

Participant timeline

Participant enrolment in the study will span one and a half days and will include the inclusion visit, six evaluations and three intervention sessions. During the evaluation sessions, CPF will be measured first, followed by the secondary outcomes. A schematic timeline of participant enrolment is presented in figure 1.

Sample size estimation

As the main objective of this study is to compare the effects of MI-E with manual respiratory physiotherapy on CPF spirometry in hospitalised older adults, the justification for the number of subjects required was based on a comparison between randomisation groups in CPF before and after sessions. Data from the literature and from a single-centre pilot study conducted by our team suggest an effect size of 0.5, that is, a difference between randomisation groups in terms of variation CPF around 10–15%, with an expected SD close to 25%.19 In order to highlight this difference, which can be considered moderate in relation to the recommendations proposed by Cohen, 42 patients per randomisation group are required, with a two-sided type I error at 5, 80% statistical power, a number of sessions per patient around 1.5–2 and an intraindividual correlation coefficient of 0.5 (in order to take into account the interindividual and intraindividual variability due to several sessions involving the same patient). Finally, it is proposed to include 60 patients per patient group in order to take into account losses to follow-up (premature discharge, death, unavailable data, etc). In addition, we envisage that after the MoCA questionnaire, 30% of patients who have signed a consent form may be excluded depending on the results obtained in the questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be performed according to a predefined statistical analysis plan, using the Stata software V.15.0 (StataCorp) before the breaking of the randomisation code, according to the International Conference on Harmonisation-Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

The primary analysis will be conducted in the intention-to-treat population, which includes all randomised patients except those who withdraw their consent for data use. We will also perform a per-protocol analysis on the primary outcome, that is, by removing from the analysis patients who will not be managed according to the randomisation group assigned.

Patients will be described and compared between randomisation groups according to the following variables: compliance with eligibility criteria, epidemiological characteristics, clinical characteristics and treatments. In accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement, group differences in baseline variables will not be compared using significance testing unless specifically indicated. A description of protocol deviations will also be provided.

The comparison between the two randomisation groups for the primary outcome (variation in CPF before and after sessions) will be performed using a linear mixed model in order to take into account multiple data for the same patient (before and after each session). Session, assessment and day effects will be taken into account and their interactions will be studied. The normality of the residuals will also be studied. Where appropriate, a logarithmic transformation of the dependent variable under study will be proposed. The results will be expressed with regression coefficients, effect sizes and 95% CIs and will be interpreted according to Cohen’s recommendations: 0.2=weak, 0.5=moderate, 0.8=strong. A sensitivity analysis will be carried out to study the attrition bias. The primary analysis will be conducted on available data (ie, with missing), imputed data (first using multiple data imputation method and then, as a sensitivity analysis, the last observation carried forward method) and complete cases.

Adjusted analyses will be conducted only for the primary outcome using multiple mixed linear regression to take into account covariates selected on the basis of the results of the univariate analysis and their clinical relevance: type of unit (ICU or not), age and sex. The centre will be considered as a random effect. The results will be expressed as regression coefficients, effect sizes and 95% CIs.

Planned subgroup analyses will assess heterogeneity of the effect on the primary outcome measure in prespecified subgroups of patients defined according to the type of unit (ICU or not), age and sex. According to the usual recommendations, the interactions between group randomisation and subgroups will be evaluated in regression models. The primary outcome will be evaluated in the subgroup analyses without adjustments for multiplicity.

The comparisons between randomisation groups will be carried out (1) as aforementioned for the primary outcome for continuous outcomes (respiratory muscle strength: FVC, MIP/MEP, FEV1/FVC) and (2) by generalised linear model for categorical variables (discomfort, dyspnoea). Similarly, the potential effect of whether the participant has previously used the MI-E will be analysed.

A two-sided p value <0.05 will be considered for statistical significance of all analyses. Because of the potential for type 1 error due to multiple comparisons, findings from analyses of secondary outcomes will be interpreted as exploratory.

Data monitoring and auditing

Monitoring will be carried out by the CRA, independent from the investigator’s department, after each centre has included five participants and again at the end of the inclusion period. The monitored data will include the informed consent forms, eligibility criteria, principal outcome and related medical records of all participants.

Harms

Serious adverse events will be recorded on a special form prepared by the study sponsor and transmitted to them as soon as the investigating centre becomes aware of them. This document includes information about the type of event, its consequences and the participant’s clinical characteristics. Other unexpected events or protocol deviations will be recorded in the participant’s CRF.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was approved by the referred ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile de France XI, ID: 24.04832.000282) on 27 November 2024. All participants will provide written informed consent to participate in the study (online supplemental file). The study will be conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants can request withdrawal from the study at any time during their participation. The confidentiality of participant data will be strictly maintained, with researchers taking measures to safeguard their identities. All participants’ information will undergo an anonymisation process. Each participant will be assigned a unique code consisting of a centre number and a participant number. Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and scientific conferences, ensuring that participants’ personal information remains undisclosed. Authorship of the study results publication will be retained by the authors of this protocol and any individuals who make a substantial contribution, in accordance with the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors recommendations. A proofreading service will be used before manuscript submission. Anonymised participant-level datasets and statistical codes could be shared with research institutions on request. Any modifications to the protocol that may affect patient well-being or safety will be presented for formal approval from the ethics committee through an amendment.

Access to data

Access to final trial data will be strictly controlled to ensure confidentiality and research integrity. The principal investigator (CE) and the designated biostatistician (BP) will have full access to all final study data. This measure is designed to ensure indepth analysis and accurate interpretation of the results. Furthermore, in the interests of transparency and scientific collaboration, data will be made available on request to researchers affiliated with recognised research units. This approach promotes reproducibility of results and encourages potentially beneficial secondary analyses.

Discussion

The present study aims to address gaps in knowledge regarding the efficacy of MI-E compared with traditional manual respiratory physiotherapy in older inpatients. Demographic changes and associated health issues among older adults have highlighted the need to focus on this population. Our previous pilot study confirmed the feasibility of MI-E in older inpatients.19 However, as pointed out by a recent systematic review,18 its efficacy needs to be evaluated in studies with strong methodological design. Therefore, we will assess its efficacy in the present multicentre randomised controlled trial, which is adequately powered and adheres to rigorous methodological standards. The results of the present study will provide valuable support to clinicians in choosing the optimal technique for respiratory physiotherapy in older inpatients. If the hypothesis of MI-E being superior to manual respiratory physiotherapy is confirmed, its use would be recommended, potentially impacting clinical practice and improving bronchial clearance in older inpatients. Then, further studies should explore the effects of MI-E on hospitalisation duration, rehospitalisation rates, mortality, quality of life and medicoeconomic aspects.

Our study will focus on the short-term effects, evaluating only three MI-E sessions over one and a half days, corresponding to a single episode of bronchial congestion. While this may be considered a limitation, long-term effects could be assessed in future studies. Our eligibility criteria focus more on ageing than on specific diseases, which could be seen as a limiting factor in implementing MI-E for older individuals with particular conditions. However, comorbidities are common in older adults, and all participants’ medical conditions will be recorded at baseline, thereby improving the generalisability of our results. Further, this study focuses only on hospitalised older adults, not those living at home or in a long-term care institution.
