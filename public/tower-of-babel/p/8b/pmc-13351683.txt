International validation of the Locally Recurrent Rectal Cancer—Quality of Life (LRRC-QoL) patient reported outcome measure
Authors: Niamh McKigney, Galina Velikova, Julia M Brown, Deena P Harji, LRRC-QoL Collaborators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundLocally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC) occurs in 4–8% of patients following previous curative treatment. However, it has a significant impact on patients’ clinical status and health-related quality of life (HrQoL). International collaboration has positively impacted clinical outcome reporting in this setting, however, has not extended to patient-reported outcomes such as HrQoL. Establishing an international measure of HrQoL for patients with LRRC is central to understanding its impact from a patient perspective. The LRRC-QoL measure of HrQoL in LRRC was developed and validated for use in the UK and Australia. The aim of this study was to externally validate the LRRC-QoL as an international measure of HrQoL in LRRC.MethodsPatients with LRRC undergoing treatment with both curative and palliative intent were recruited between 05/11/2020 and 04/12/2023 to a prospective, longitudinal cohort study of HrQoL in LRRC following cross-cultural adaptation in all participating countries. Scale structure was assessed through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Convergent validity was assessed against EORTC QLQ-CR29, and FACT-C scales using Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (r). Known groups comparison was assessed through independent t-tests and ANOVA. Responsiveness was assessed through effect size (ES) and standardised response mean (SRM). The study was registered on the ISRCTN registry (reference: ISRCTN13692671).FindingsOverall, 321 patients were included in the validation of the LRRC-QoL from 14 countries. The LRRC-QoL scale structure demonstrated excellent goodness of fit. Most scales demonstrated good reliability, with Cronbach’s Alpha and ICC values > 0.7. Convergent validity confirmed several hypothesised correlations. Known groups validity was demonstrated for gender, pattern of recurrence, pre-operative treatment, treatment intent, and presence of metastases. The LRRC-QoL demonstrated excellent responsiveness; patients undergoing surgery reported worse HrQoL at three (p < 0.0001, ES 0.57, SRM 0.50) and six months (p = 0.001, ES 0.37, SRM 1.11).InterpretationThe LRRC-QoL has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties in this international validation analysis and is now validated for use in 14 countries.FundingFunding was provided by Bowel Research UK and Pelican Cancer Foundation.Keywords: Health-related quality of life, Locally recurrent rectal cancer, International validation, Patient-reported outcomes

Research in context

Evidence before this study

The experiences garnered during the original development and validation of the Locally Recurrent Rectal Cancer—Quality of Life (LRRC-QoL) measure informed the design of this study. Recruitment in this rare and advanced form of pelvic malignancy is challenging, with several barriers which were considered throughout the delivery of the study. These anticipated barriers and the success of international initiatives such as the PelvEx Collaborative led to the decision to extend recruitment to a larger number of centres across 14 countries. Cross-cultural adaptation was undertaken to ensure the international versions of the LRRC-QoL were acceptable for use. This was undertaken through a process of translatability assessment, forward-backward translation, and pre-testing interviews with patients and clinicians.

Added value of this study

This study builds on the original validation of the LRRC-QoL, confirming its psychometric properties in an international cohort including patients from 14 countries. The LRRC-QoL is the only internationally validated disease-specific measure of HrQoL in LRRC and should be utilised in both clinical and academic settings to standardise HrQoL reporting in this disease setting.

Implications of all the available evidence

The LRRC-QoL should be considered the gold standard in reporting HrQoL in LRRC, ongoing prospective data collection utilising the LRRC-QoL will be an important addition to the existing literature regarding the impact of LRRC on HrQoL.

Introduction

Recent decades have seen important developments in the surgical and oncological treatment of locally recurrent rectal cancer (LRRC), with factors that were once considered absolute contraindications to surgical resection now representing routinely-implemented operative components of highly individualised approaches offered at specialist centres internationally.1, 2, 3 Increasingly focus is shifting towards balancing these procedures against their impact on the patient, particularly in relation to their quality of life.4 This is apparent in the increasing volume of literature regarding patient-reported outcomes (PROs) in LRRC.5 Reporting PROs in LRRC is particularly important given that it can offer a more holistic perspective of ultra-radical procedures and complex treatment pathways. Despite increasing focus on reporting PROs in LRRC, the existing literature is limited from a methodological standpoint, particularly in the paucity of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) which have been validated for use in LRRC.5 Existing measures frequently used to report HrQoL in LRRC include those developed for patients with primary colorectal cancer such as the EORTC QLQ-CR29 and FACT-C,6,7 and measures such as the SF-36, designed for use in patients with chronic conditions.8,9 These measures do not capture the full scope of issues which have been identified as relevant to patients with LRRC.5 This lack of a disease-specific measure for use in LRRC led to the development of the Locally Recurrent Rectal Cancer—Quality of Life (LRRC-QoL) questionnaire as a disease-specific measure of HrQoL for patients with LRRC.10 It underwent a robust development process and has been validated for use in the UK and Australia.10

National and international collaboration within the surgical community has been a major driving force in improving the management of LRRC. This has been aided by the establishment of collaborative initiatives including the UK Pelvic Exenteration Network (UKPEN) and the PelvEx Collaborative, which has published a number of retrospective studies,3,11,12 guidelines,13,14 and a randomised controlled trial (RCT).15 The value of international collaboration is evident in relation to reporting and improving outcomes in LRRC, as evidenced through the work of the PelvEx collaborative,3,11,12 however currently offers a unidimensional perspective in reporting clinical outcomes and is lacking in broader patient-reported outcomes including HrQoL. Undertaking cross-cultural adaptation and international validation of the LRRC-QoL will increase its generalisability and enable collection of international, disease-specific HrQoL data. External validation involves undertaking a psychometric analysis of a PROM in a different cohort of patients to those involved in the original PROM development. It is an important process in establishing the reproducibility and generalisability of a PROM and its psychometric properties. The original validation of the LRRC-QoL highlighted the need to undertake further analyses to confirm the reliability of the scales, convergent validity, known groups validity, and responsiveness of this measure.10 Undertaking external validation in a larger, separate cohort of patients is essential in confirming these psychometric properties. The aim of this study was to confirm the generalisability, reliability, validity and responsiveness of the LRRC-QoL in an external, international cohort to ensure its broader use.

Methods

Study design

A multicentre, prospective, longitudinal cohort study was undertaken recruiting participants with LRRC to assess HrQoL. Recruitment was undertaken at 29 centres in 14 countries between 05/11/2020 and 04/12/2023.

Ethics

The study was approved by West of Scotland Research Ethics Committee 3 (REC reference: 20/WS/0116) in the UK and at each participating country and site in accordance with local procedures. The study was registered on the ISRCTN registry (reference: ISRCTN13692671).

Participants

Inclusion criteria included age ≥ 18 years, able to provide informed written consent to participate, with a new radiological and/or histological diagnosis of LRRC including re-recurrences, or, having undergone treatment (surgery/chemotherapy/radiotherapy) for LRRC within the last two years. Both patients receiving treatment with curative and palliative intent were included in the study. Radical surgical resection represents the only curative treatment option for patients with LRRC. Palliative treatment options include chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and best supportive care. Exclusion criteria included cognitive impairment.

Procedures

Participants were asked to complete the LRRC-QoL at baseline diagnosis, 10–14 days (optional), then at three and six months. Participants were also asked to complete a demographics form, the FACT-C and the EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-CR29 questionnaires at baseline. In non-English-speaking countries cross-cultural adaptation was undertaken prior to validation. A range of recruitment strategies were employed including completion of questionnaires in person with the clinical team, via post, via telephone, or online using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture hosted by the University of Leeds Clinical Trials Research Unit). Follow-up in the UK and for patients participating online was co-ordinated by the central study team and international follow-up was co-ordinated locally.

The LRRC-QoL consists of nine scales and 29 items scored on a Likert scale of 1–4. The nine scales are Pain, Urinary Symptoms, Lower Limb Symptoms, Stoma, Urostomy, Sexual Interest, Sexual Function (gender-specific), Psychological Impact, and Healthcare Services. The HrQoL score comprises the total score for the LRRC-QoL excluding the Healthcare Services scale which measures healthcare experiences. A high score or an increase in score signifies worsening in symptoms for all LRRC-QoL scales, except the Healthcare Services scale, where an increase in score signifies better experiences. Similarly, an increase in score in the Sexual Interest scale signifies increased sexual interest.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA) and R Statistical Software (v4.2.3; R Core Team 2023) using the lavaan package (v0.6-7; Rosseel, 2012)16 and mice package (v3.16.0; van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011).17 Recommended guidelines advise that 5–10 patients should be recruited per item within a PROM to enable confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).18 The LRRC-QoL consists of 29 items and therefore the target for recruitment was 320 patients with a 10% attrition rate. Data analysis was undertaken sequentially, commencing with a descriptive analysis of the demographic and clinical data. Data completeness at an item and scale level was assessed to identify the distribution of responses and for floor/ceiling effects. The criteria for acceptable levels of missing data were <10% for items, <50% for computable total scale scores, meaning data completeness of >50% for the scale, and <80% for floor ceiling effects, meaning no items had response rates of >80% for single scores.19 Items and scales not meeting these criteria were excluded from the remaining steps of the psychometric analysis. Missing data below these levels were handled with single imputation.17,20, 21, 22

The scale structure of the LRRC-QoL has previously been assessed through multi-trait scaling analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA).10 Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to assess the reproducibility and predictability of the previously confirmed scale structure of the LRRC-QoL. CFA statistically assesses the fit of the hypothesised scale structure of the measure determined through multi-trait analysis and EFA. CFA was undertaken using the Diagonally Weighted Least Squares (DWLS) estimator given the ordinal nature of the data.23 Several goodness of fit indices were used to assess the goodness of fit of the overall model and of individual parameter estimates. These include Chi-squared (ch2), root-mean-square-error-of approximation (RMSEA), comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker Lewis Index (TLI).24 Model strain analysis was used to establish whether items belong to their specified domain through examining relationships between questions; a positive relationship indicates that the questions all contribute to the domain. A negative relationship indicates that scores are going in opposite directions, demonstrating misfit, and identifying questions which do not belong within the domain. Strain analysis is able to identify whether external factors are influencing the relationships between the questions within a domain.

Reliability was assessed through the internal consistency of the scales, measured using Cronbach’s Alpha, values of >0.7 are considered good.25 Reproducibility of the questionnaire was assessed using test-retest at 10–14 days from baseline, provided participants’ clinical state remained stable during this time. This was measured using ICC, values of ≥0.7 are recommended.26 Convergent validity was assessed using Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient (r) in a correlational analysis comparing the scales of the LRRC-QoL to those of the EORTC QLQ-CR29 and FACT-C. Pearson’s values of greater than 0.45 are considered highly correlated.27 The a priori hypotheses can be found in the Supplementary material. Demographic and clinical characteristics were used to identify groups of patients to assess whether the LRRC-QoL was able to distinguish between them using the independent t-test to compare mean scores between two groups and ANOVA to compare mean scores in groups greater than two. The characteristics used for known group comparison can be found in the Supplementary material.

Responsiveness is the ability of an instrument to illustrate changes over time. The standardised response mean (SRM) and effect size (ES) were used to assess the responsiveness of the LRRC-QoL at three and six months in patients undergoing surgical resection and patients receiving palliative treatment.28 The SRM is the ratio of the mean change to the standard deviation of that change and ES is the ratio of the mean change to the standard deviation of the initial measurement. The ES was used to interpret differences or changes in HrQoL following treatment. The higher the ES or SRM, the greater the level of sensitivity to change.27 A p value of <0.01 was used to indicate statistical significance to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Overall, 321 participants were included in this study across 29 centres in 14 countries. All patients recruited to the study were included in the analysis. There were 226 male patients (70.4%) and median age was 66 (IQR 13.0). Forty-nine patients (15.3%) were treated with palliative intent, of which seven patients were initially planned to undergo curative surgery however either developed disease progression or opted not to have surgery. Of the 203 participants treated with curative intent, 108 (53.2%) had a R0 resection, though the missing data rate for margin status was 30.5%. Data completeness

Data completeness at a scale and item level was high overall, only three items had missing data rates >10% (Supplementary material Table S1). These included Item 10 relating to pain and discharge from wounds (25.0% missing), Item 19 regarding pain during sexual intercourse (27.9% missing), and Item 21 regarding irritation or soreness in the vagina or vulva (11.3% missing). These items were felt to represent important concepts previously identified by patients during the LRRC-QoL development,10 other colorectal PROMs have similarly reported lower response rates for sexual items,29 therefore were retained and included in the analysis. None of the items had response rates of >80% for single scores, meeting the criteria for floor/ceiling effects. All scales demonstrated data completeness of >50% (Supplementary Table S2).

Scale structure

The scale structure of the LRRC-QoL was evaluated through CFA using the DWLS estimator in R lavaan package in the combined cohort of 321 patients. It was not possible to include the gender-specific Sexual Function scales within the model due to the small subsets of patients responding to the items within these scales. CFA was therefore undertaken using a model based on eight of the nine LRRC-QoL scales.

The values for the indices listed in Table 2 suggest excellent goodness of fit in the adapted model (excluding the gender-specific Sexual Function scales). This included a relatively low chi-squared value with 181 degrees of freedom, a very low RMSEA of 0.000, a high CFI of 1.000 and TLI of 1.511. Overall, the results of the strain analysis support the existing scale structure of the LRRC-QoL and can be found in the Supplementary material (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Most ICC values were >0.7, indicating good temporal stability. The smaller numbers of responses to the Urostomy (n = 20) and Female Sexual Function (n = 44) scales should also be noted.

Scale validity

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was demonstrated between the LRRC-QoL Stoma scale and the EORTC QLQ-CR29 Blood/Mucus in Stool scale (r = 0.463, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S4), between the LRRC-QoL Pain scale and the FACT-C Physical Well-Being scale (r = −0.586, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S5), and between the LRRC-QoL Lower Limb Symptoms scale and the FACT-C Physical Well-Being scale (r = −0.491, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S5). Strong correlations were also demonstrated between the LRRC-QoL Psychological Impact scale and the EORTC QLQ-CR29 Body Image (r = 0.627, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S4), the FACT-C Emotional Well-Being (r = −0.702, p < 0.0001), Physical Well-Being (r = −0.566, p < 0.0001) and Functional Well-Being scales (r = −0.528, p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S5). These results are further detailed in the Supplementary material.

Known groups comparison

The LRRC-QoL was able to identify significant differences between clinical and demographic groups, including gender, pattern of LRRC, pre-operative oncological treatment for LRRC, and presence of metastatic disease. The Psychological Impact scale demonstrated significantly higher scores, indicating worse psychological impact, in female patients (p = 0.003). Whereas male patients reported worse sexual function (p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Table S8). Patients with a lateral pattern of LRRC had significantly higher scores for the Lower Limb scale (p = 0.030) (Supplementary Table S6), indicating greater symptom burden. Patients who had received pre-operative oncological treatment for LRRC had significantly higher scores in the Stoma scale (p = 0.012) (Supplementary Table S7), denoting higher levels of embarrassment and difficulties caring for their stoma. The Healthcare Services scale demonstrated worse scores in patients without metastatic disease (p = 0.039) (Supplementary Table S8), indicating worse healthcare experiences. These results are further detailed in the Supplementary material.

Patients who underwent surgery demonstrated significant deterioration in their overall HrQoL, as detailed by the HrQoL total score. This was more pronounced at three months (p < 0.0001, ES 0.57, SRM 0.50) compared with six months (p = 0.01, ES 0.42, SRM 0.46). Conversely, patients receiving treatment with palliative intent reported an increase in their overall HrQoL at six months (p = 0.01, ES 0.37, SRM 1.11) compared to baseline. Patients who had undergone surgery for LRRC also reported worse pain at three months (p = 0.06, ES 0.47, SRM 0.29) and a significantly increased burden of lower limb symptoms at both three (p < 0.0001, ES 1.00, SRM 0.58) and six months (p < 0.0001, ES 1.30, SRM 0.66). Patients receiving treatment with palliative intent reported a significant improvement in lower limb symptoms at six months (p = 0.03, ES 0.42, SRM 0.82). This patient group also reported a significant improvement in stoma-related issues at six months (p = 0.02, ES 0.96, SRM 1.23).

Regarding the Sexual Interest scale, patients who had undergone surgery reported reduced scores at three months (p = 0.04, ES 0.32, SRM 0.37), denoting reduced sexual interest. This patient group also reported an improvement in psychological symptoms at three and six months (p = 0.06, ES 0.31, SRM 0.34). Finally, in relation to the Healthcare Services scale, scores were significantly reduced, indicating worse experiences, at three (p < 0.0001, ES 0.52, SRM 0.55) and six months (p = 0.01, ES 0.81, SRM 0.51) in patients undergoing surgery.

Discussion

This international validation analysis of the LRRC-QoL confirms the psychometric properties of the measure on a wider platform. The LRRC-QoL demonstrated robust scale structure, acceptable reliability, excellent validity, and high responsiveness to clinical change. The LRRC-QoL has been adapted for use on an international scale, including countries in Europe, Asia, North America, South America, and Australasia; meaning it can be used across many populations. The strong results of the validity and responsiveness demonstrate the ability of the LRRC-QoL measure to detect meaningful clinical change. This strongly supports its use as a disease-specific outcome measure and the LRRC-QoL should now be considered the gold standard for assessing HrQoL in LRRC in both clinical and academic settings.

This study demonstrates the capacity to successfully complete cross-cultural adaptation and external validation in rare disease settings. Though it should be noted that the majority of participants were recruited from English-speaking countries, the involvement of low- and middle-income countries, such as India and Pakistan, demonstrates the ability to undertake such studies across varied healthcare systems. The breadth of languages and countries included within this process confirm that LRRC-QoL is relevant, acceptable, and provides a valid assessment of HrQoL in patients with LRRC on a broad international platform. The international approach to the external validation of the LRRC-QoL is a significant strength of the study. Though this is not the classical approach for external validation studies, conducting a multinational study is essential in rare disease settings to reach a sample enabling robust psychometric analyses.30 Furthermore, the wide range of languages and countries represented in the analysis confirm the generalisability of the externally validated measure.

Though the recruitment target for the study was achieved with 321 patients included, it was not possible to conduct CFA for all nine scales of the measure. The inclusion of scales, such as the Urostomy or gender-specific Sexual Function scales, which only apply to specific subgroups of patients, means an even greater sample size for CFA is required, which is challenging to achieve in this rare disease setting. However, the combination of analyses undertaken provide convincing evidence to confirm the construct validity of the LRRC-QoL. The results of the psychometric analysis may also have been affected by lower response rates to specific scales, particularly the Urostomy and gender-specific Sexual Function scales, as they are only relevant to specific subgroups of patients. There are several reasons why the Sexual Function scales may have had lower response rates, including some patients avoiding answering this question due to its personal nature, patients either not having a sexual partner or sexual intercourse not currently being a priority for them during their treatment and therefore not finding this question relevant. However, these scales have previously demonstrated excellent reliability in the original development of the LRRC-QoL. As previously acknowledged, the majority of patients were recruited from the UK, several factors contributed to delays in site setup internationally, including the COVID-19 pandemic, the Brexit transition process, and delays related to legal negotiations. Additionally, it was required to complete cross-cultural adaptation at all international sites prior to recruiting to the longitudinal study. There were also regions which were not represented, with only one site in Asia and none from the African continent, further cross-cultural adaptation studies will be required to enable the use of the LRRC-QoL more widely globally. Despite the limitations described, recruitment of a cohort including over 300 patients with LRRC across 14 countries, is a landmark achievement: arguably representing the largest multinational study of HrQoL in LRRC to date.

The confirmation that the LRRC-QoL is highly responsive to changes in clinical status over time has significant implications for future research. In patients undergoing surgery with curative intent, overall HrQoL was demonstrated to worsen at three months, prior to improving by six months. These findings echo those of previous studies in patients undergoing pelvic exenteration for LRRC, reporting HrQoL utilising the AQOL, SF-36 and FACT-C.31,32 Patients undergoing surgery also reported worse pain at three months (p = 0.06, ES 0.47, SRM 0.29). This has previously been identified as a significant issue affecting patients undergoing exenterative surgery for LRRC.33,34 However, current reporting is limited by a lack of disease-specific measures to assess pain, this can now be assessed using the LRRC-QoL. Patients receiving surgical treatment also reported worse lower limb symptoms at both three (p < 0.0001, ES 1.00, SRM 0.58) and six months (p < 0.0001, ES 1.30, SRM 0.66), which has previously been identified in patients undergoing sacrectomy,35 or sciatic or femoral nerve resections,36 which may also contribute to chronic neuropathy. Interestingly, health care experiences were worse at both three (p < 0.0001, ES 0.52, SRM 0.55) and six months (p = 0.01, ES 0.81, SRM 0.51) in this patient group, this has not previously been identified and will be further explored in future prospective work, additionally further work is needed to establish unmet needs within existing care pathways. In relation to responsiveness of the LRRC-QoL in patients receiving treatment with palliative intent, overall HrQoL was significantly improved at six months (p = 0.01, ES 0.37, SRM 1.11). HrQoL reporting in this specific subset of patients is sparse with somewhat mixed results.33,37,38 Notably, the existing evidence, including the current study, is limited by small numbers of patients receiving treatment with palliative intent. This is likely to continue to be the case in future studies given the challenges of recruiting this specific subgroup of patients,39 in addition to attrition during longitudinal follow-up which was also a limitation in this study. In relation to the LRRC-QoL scales, at six months patients receiving palliative treatment reported improved lower limb symptoms (p = 0.03, ES 0.42, SRM 0.82) and stoma-related issues (p = 0.02, ES 0.96, SRM 1.23). These improvements could relate to the positive impact of palliative treatments such as chemotherapy and the comparative negative impact of complications following surgery with curative intent.

Important areas for future work include the establishment of minimally important differences (MIDs), which are the smallest difference in score which can be perceived as important to patients, prompting consideration of a change in management,40 enabling interpretation of LRRC-QoL data in a patient-centred, clinically meaningful way. In terms of standardising outcome reporting, including HrQoL outcomes, the lack of a core outcome set (COS) specifically for LRRC remains a limitation. However, this is due to change with a COS for LRRC having been recently registered in the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials (COMET) initiative database. Previous work in patients with advanced colorectal cancer suggests that HrQoL is likely to be identified as an outcome for inclusion,41 in which case the LRRC-QoL would be the unrivalled choice of disease-specific outcome measure.

The results of this international validation analysis demonstrate the excellent psychometric properties of the LRRC-QoL, confirming its status as the optimal PROM for reporting HrQoL in LRRC. Further prospective reporting utilising the LRRC-QoL will provide greater insight into the impact of LRRC on HrQoL.
