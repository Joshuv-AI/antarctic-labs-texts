An historical overview of zinc in poultry nutrition
Authors: MS Lilburn, DR McIntyre
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc is one of the most prevalent trace minerals in both the environment and in the body. It was used as a component for industrial coatings dating back to the 18th century and toxicity in smelter employees was a concern. Zinc was first reported in plants in the mid 1800′s and later that century was the first report of zinc in the liver and muscle in mammals. At this point in nutrition history, there had been research interest in iron and copper with their visible, bright colors. The first series of experiments and publications on zinc in animal systems used the marine mollusc, Sycotypus canaliculatus, with the final paper reporting the storage of copper and zinc in the liver. One hurdle to recognizing zinc's importance to animal growth was the lack of easily observed deficiency symptoms as was the case with rickets (Vitamin D) and perosis (manganese). While zinc can accumulate in selected tissues (i.e., liver, bone), its role as a cofactor in the 2 key enzymes carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase will be the focus of this review as these enzymes are critically important to optimizing egg production, egg shell quality, skeletal development and embryonic development. These enzymes can occur as different isoforms and their biochemical importance was discovered prior to the recognition that zinc was an important co-factor. Over the last 20 yr, 3 factors have dominated the zinc literature: phytase, organic versus inorganic zinc supplements and the application of molecular techniques toward the discovery of previously ignored zinc containing proteins/enzymes. The objective of this review will be to discuss the history of zinc as an essential enzymatic co-factor regulating animal growth and development with an emphasis on the aforementioned poultry systems egg production, shell quality, and skeletal development.Key words: zinc, enzyme, bone, egg shell, skeleton

INTRODUCTION

Trace minerals can be broadly described as those which are widely distributed in common feedstuffs but rarely exceed 20 ppm (Calvery, 1942). In this report, the author described 3 general classes of trace minerals: 1) those which have a role in normal physiological processes; 2) those which are non-essential but also non-toxic when dietary levels are less than 100 ppm; and 3) toxic trace minerals at levels less than 100 ppm. At the time of this publication (Calvery, 1942), the elements included in the first group included cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), iron (Fe), iodine (I), manganese (Mn), and zinc (Zn). After Fe, Zn was recognized as being the second most abundant trace mineral in the body (McCance and Widdowson, 1942).

The name zinc is thought to be derived from the German term for “of unknown origin” (Vallee, 1959). In published reports on the early history of zinc, it is thought to have been first observed during the course of brass production in biblical times (Loosli, 1991). In nature, it is most often found as a compound and the Chinese may have been the first to actually extract zinc metal. It is speculated this happened serendipitously during the melting and extraction of copper from zinc containing ore. Zinc had considerable commercial importance in the 18th century as a component of alloys, paints, and the process of galvanizing other metals (Moeller, 1959). It's importance as an integral component in the aforementioned commercial products resulted in several published works between 1825 and 1864 on different methods of zinc analysis (Moeller, 1959). In the 1870′s, Schlockow (as translated by Lanska and Remler, 2014) described a myelopathy observed in zink-smelter workers thought to be the result of long-term zinc exposure although the symptoms were subsequently attributed to nickel carbonyl (Armit, 1907) and lead (Rambousek, 1913). Zinc toxicity in various forms was subsequently described in drinking water (Gimlette, 1901) and in workers from different industrial environments (McCord and Kilker, 1921; McCord and Freedlander, 1926).

Zinc in Living Systems

Zinc was recognized as a component of living systems (plant, animal) for many years prior to being recognized as a necessary component in animal metabolism and subsequently an essential nutrient. Through the early 1900′s, the primary research outcomes were descriptive studies confirming zincs’ presence and potential importance to plant and animal growth. A second major outcome was the development of improved assay methods with smaller tissue samples which allowed for detailed studies on the specificity of tissue zinc accumulation and transport though the body. Assays based on light reflectance allowed for the determination of zinc in physiologic products (i.e., milk, egg yolk) and these generated new hypotheses with respect to the importance of zinc as a nutrient.

In the latter half of the 19th century, trace minerals were first recognized as being important to the growth of different biological systems and this stimulated research relative to identifying which minerals were important. Braun (1854) was reportedly the first to document that plants contained zinc and Raulin (1869), a purported former student of Pasteur, noted that zinc was essential for the growth of the fungus, Aspergillus niger. This observation was eventually confirmed some 40 years later by Bertrand and Javillier (1911) and almost a century later, this particular fungus would become a critical organism in the burgeoning field of biotechnology (Schuster et al., 2002). After Raulin's initial observations, the presence of zinc was confirmed in several cereal grains, human liver, and the muscle and liver from an ox (Lechartier and Bellamy, 1877; Raoult and Breton, 1877).

The early 20th century was a time of intense discovery relative to the identification of many trace nutrients and their role in plants as well as animal growth and development. Previously, most published work on trace minerals other than iron and copper was largely descriptive (Fredericq, 1878 as cited by Bonaventura and Bonaventura, 1980) and zinc studies were largely limited to plant and animal tissue content. Vallee (1959) speculated that brightly colored iron and copper containing proteins was one factor which prompted more extensive early research into these particular trace minerals versus the relatively colorless zinc containing proteins. Another contributing factor which delayed more mechanistic studies was the long-held hypotheses delineated by von Liebig and Gregory (1842) who conducted extensive studies on animal “physico-chemical” systems, the foundation for what we now call biochemistry. His widely accepted view was that animals/humans assimilated preformed organic compounds containing minerals (trace minerals) from plant-based foods (von Liebig and Gregory, 1842 as cited by Poskitt, 2003). These views continued to be supported in early 20th century books which stated that “plants can make organic matter out of inorganic elements, just this the animal cannot do at all” (Curtis, 1906) and Chittenden (1907) who believed that other than those minerals found in the skeleton and teeth, dietary inorganic salts were simply “aids to nutrition”.

Trace Nutrients Essential to Metabolism

Near the turn of the century, the initial paradigm shift relative to the importance of zinc in animal metabolism is most often attributed to a series of papers focused on the marine mollusc, Sycotypus canaliculatus (Bradlee, 1904; Mendel and Bradley, 1905a,b; Mendel and Bradlee, 1906). According to Bradlee (1904), his paper was the first to report the presence of zinc in the tissues of any animal, vertebrate or invertebrate. It is assumed that the author was not aware of the previous observations by Raoult and Breton (1877) and Lechartier and Bellamy (1877). In the last paper of this series of research publications with molluscs, Mendel and Bradlee (1906) focused on zinc and copper storage in the liver which was later confirmed by Bodansky (1920).

This same observation was also reported in the liver of newborn and stillborn children (van Itallie and van Eck, 1913 as cited by McHargue (1926)). Animal experiments by Salant et al. (1918) which included oral, subcutaneous, and intravenous administration of zinc confirmed the role of the liver as the primary storage organ and the “gastrointestinal canal” as the primary route for excretion. The authors also speculated that this was likely the same for most heavy metals.

It was well recognized that improved methodology was needed for more accurate determination of zinc in animal tissues and products as this was a limiting factor relative to research on its biological importance. Birckner (1919) and Bodansky (1921a) used a modified turbidometric procedure for zinc analysis originally described by Breyer as reported in Scott (1917). Using this method, Birckner (1919) reported that zinc was found in eggs and milk from both cows and humans. With respect to eggs, zinc was concentrated in the yolk while in cow's milk, it was highest during the early stages of lactation. Birckner (1919) cited Weitzel (1914) as being the first to actually report the zinc content in eggs and milk but noted that the quantity of material needed for their analysis would likely restrict future research efforts. Tupper at al. (1954), using radiolabelled 65Zn, would subsequently confirm the zinc-egg yolk observation. These authors went on to describe the tight binding affinity between zinc and lipovitellin and suggested that egg production represented a net loss of physiologically available zinc. Bodansky (1921b), using the modified zinc analysis method of Birckner (1919), determined that both copper and zinc were present in human brain samples. An excellent summary of the challenges associated with zinc tissue determination and procedures for a relatively rapid analysis was subsequently published by Todd and Elvehjem (1932). It is interesting that in this report, the authors cited many of the references mentioned above but not Birckner (1919).

The 2 decades following the Birckner (1919) and Bodansky (1921a) observations was a period during which the importance of many trace nutrients, both vitamins and trace minerals, were ultimately described. From a metabolism standpoint, it was confirmed that zinc excretion occurred primarily via the feces versus the urine/kidney (Batchelor et al., 1926; Drinker et al., 1927a; Fairhall, 1926b; Lutz, 1926). The report by Batchelor et al. (1926) was an intensive 48 h. study on 24 men with varying periods of exposure (2–35 yr) to different forms of zinc containing dust (zinc sulfide, fine metallic zinc dust, zinc oxide). The blood and tissue samples were analyzed using a recently developed colorimetric assay based on the binding of zinc salts with urobilin as reported by (Lutz, 1925). These authors concluded that while the urinary and fecal zinc levels were elevated, they were at the high range of “normal” values and any purported “zinc poisoning” was the result of impurities (i.e., lead, arsenic, cadmium) in mined zinc ores. Fairhall (1926a) commented that “the zinc present in the blood of various animals demonstrated a definite and somewhat constant quantity” and “the normal tenacious retention in the blood can hardly be looked upon as accidental”. This author observed that over an 18-d experimental period, the zinc level in human feces was fairly consistent at approximately 10 mg/d with a spike to 70 mg on d 10 after the consumption of a plate of oysters (d 9), known to be rich in zinc (Hiltner and Wichmann, 1919). This spike in fecal zinc was followed by a return to the baseline by d 13. Fairhall (1926b) also appreciated the potential for oral zinc exposure from an experimental rearing environment and they utilized cages and rivets made of copper and separately collected urine and feces in porcelain containers. The urine was acidified with HCL and the dried residue was ashed at a low temperature as described by Thompson (1925). In the experimental results it was noted that zinc was rapidly stored in young, growing rats compared with adults and this confirmed previous observations by Drinker and Collier (1926) relative to the importance of zinc for growth. Drinker et al. (1927b), using the previously described analytical methods (Lutz, 1925; Fairhall, 1926b), also conducted a time course study with human subjects and also observed a spike in fecal zinc after consumption of oysters but no concomitant increase in urinary zinc. Fairhall and Hoyt (1929) would later suggest that while urine only accounted for a small proportion of excreted zinc, it may actually represent a better index of total zinc absorption/excretion in healthy individuals and for those individuals with metabolic diseases.

Early Vitamin/Trace Mineral Research

The early 20th century was certainly a breakthrough period with respect to the understanding of zinc's potential importance in animal growth and its’ distribution and excretory mechanisms. This was also, however, an era in which “unidentified nutritional factors”, semi-purified diets for nutrition studies and controlled environments for animal experiments became more common. The latter 2 experimental approaches contributed greatly to the burgeoning interest in animal nutrition though there was still a need for more sophisticated methodology relative to the determination of micronutrients in tissues and feed. This was the period during which fat-soluble Vitamin A and a curative factor in cod liver oil for rickets were discovered while mineral research was still centered largely on the macrominerals, calcium and phosphorus.

A first critical step in the process of studying “unidentified dietary nutrients” was the approach of using synthetic diets with defined ingredients/nutrients in controlled animal experiments (Cooper, 1912; Hopkins, 1912; Osborne et al., 1912; Hopkins and Neville, 1913). A series of papers by McCollum and Davis, 1915a, McCollum and Davis, 1915b, McCollum and Davis, 1915c, McCollum and Davis, 1915d, McCollum and Davis, 1915e and Hart et al. (1917) were among the first to use “purified food substances” to report on the contribution of individual ingredients (i.e., cereals) and nutrient classes (i.e., minerals, protein) for rodents. Hart et al. (1917) observed that poultry (pullets) fed diets containing corn or wheat meal grew better if the diets were supplemented with a salt mixture, protein, and 2 percent butter fat (source of fat-soluble vitamin A). This occurred during the same time period in which other scientists were studying the feasibility of using poultry for nutrition studies with chicks reared in confinement (Drummond, 1916). Osborne et al. (1918 were the first to have any success in this regard although these authors noted that many chicks subsequently developed “leg weakness” and these observations were confirmed by Hart et al. (1920). In 1921, there were several reports on the benefits of cod liver oil in alleviating rickets in infants, rats and pigs (Howland and Kramer, 1921; Shipley et al., 1921a,b; Zilva et al., 1921). It is interesting to note that cod liver oil had previously been shown to be curative for rickets by Osborne et al. (1914) who attributed this to “the special nature of the fats in the oil”. Funk (1915) hypothesized that the oil contained a specific “vitamine like substance”. Hart et al. (1922) replaced up to 20% butterfat with a “liberal supply of cod liver oil” and noted that this would alleviate the expected increase in mortality at 4 to 6 wk in chicks fed diets without cod liver oil. They also observed low levels of blood inorganic phosphorus, similar to what was observed in infants with rickets (Howland and Kramer, 1921). Emmett and Peacock (1923) subsequently showed the importance of age relative to “leg weakness” in young chicks fed a “fat-soluble vitamin-free diet” from 10 to 14 d compared with older chicks at 24 d. Hogan et al. (1925) was among the first to use the term Vitamin D to describe the antirachitic factor and noted that it was not needed in chicks exposed to direct sunlight as previously reported by Hart et al. (1923).

While Hart et al. (1920;1922) were among the first to use synthetic/semi-purified diets for poultry nutrition research, there was not a consensus among nutritionists that synthetic diets were optimal for studying poultry growth and development (Hogan et al., 1925; Hogan and Shrewsbury, 1930). While the need for dietary zinc was being defined in rodents during the early 1930's, mineral research relative to poultry nutrition was largely directed at understanding macromineral balance (Ca:P ratios), mineral interactions within individual ingredients and ultimately the role of manganese in alleviating the prevalence of “leg weakness” in young chicks (Norris et al., 1930). The term “range paralysis” was a term used to describe a frequently observed sequence of connected skeletal anomalies that together resulted in a condition that was ultimately called Perosis (Hall and King, 1931; Titus, 1932; Wilcke et al., 1933). The incidence of perosis was subsequently determined to be at least partially due to the aforementioned macromineral imbalance (Ca, inorganic P) that could be exacerbated or alleviated by dietary inclusion of specific byproducts (Titus and Ginn, 1931; Herner and Robinson, 1932; Parkhurst and McMurray, 1933; Milby, 1933; Insko et al., 1934). A chance observation that a particular source of limestone which significantly reduced perosis was high in manganese proved to be the key to unlocking the etiology of the anomaly and confirmed the developing hypothesis that individual trace minerals were critical to optimizing growth with practical type poultry diets (Wilgus et al., 1936; Wilgus et al., 1937a). In a subsequent report, Wilgus et al. (1937b) suggested that different trace mineral combinations were completely (manganese, aluminum, iron) or somewhat (zinc, aluminum) effective at reducing the incidence of perosis but these observations could not be subsequently confirmed by other authors (Insko et al., 1938; Lyons et al., 1938).

The Importance of Dietary Zinc

There were hints of a dietary zinc requirement by a number of labs but the real contribution of these early zinc experiments was in providing dietary guidance and considerations for future studies. These experiments were primarily done with rodents and some of the future considerations included such factors as the status of dam nutrition and accounting for potential low zinc levels in exogenous “supplements” (i.e., milk).

The visible nature of clinical Vitamin D (rickets) and manganese (perosis) deficiencies, respectively, greatly facilitated the discovery of these key micronutrients but this was not the case for zinc. The first experimental attempts in the 1920′s at establishing a dietary need for zinc were not definitive (Bertrand and Benson, 1922; McHargue, 1926) but the details of the dietary approaches used in these 2 reports contributed to the successful experiments that followed. A conclusion from Hubbell and Mendel (1927) summarized the status of dietary zinc research at the time, “It is not unlikely, however, that there is some variation in growth with varying amounts of zinc and that the metal is not merely an accidental factor in the nutrition of the mouse.” Newell and McCollum (1933) subsequently concluded that zinc supplementation was probably not essential for growth but they did report that total body zinc was reduced in rat pups born to zinc deficient dams. Todd et al. (1933) was the first to show positive effects of supplementing zinc in rat diets but noted the only “pathology” associated with the deficiency was abnormal fur growth. Bertrand and Bhattacherjee (1934) also reported positive effects of supplementing dietary zinc with mice although the positive dietary response was measured simply in days alive. Newell and McCollum (1933) incorporated milk into their deficient diets and Stirn et al. (1935) hypothesized that there was enough zinc in the dietary casein to blunt a greater deficiency response. These authors (Stirn et al., 1935) were the first to show a significant decrease in feed intake associated with a zinc deficient diet and also confirmed the negative effects on fur growth previously reported by Todd et al. (1933). This was approximately 20 yr before the zinc supplementation was reported to alleviate parakeratosis in swine (Tucker and Salmon, 1955).

Hove et al. (1940) subsequently observed that zinc deficiency resulted in significantly reduced intestinal phosphatase activity and supplementation greatly increased phosphatase activity in crude intestinal preps in vitro, up to a point at which it became progressively inhibitory. The effects of either zinc or magnesium deficiency were subsequently found to decrease both serum and tissue alkaline phosphatase. A follow-up study almost 40 yr later confirmed that a dietary zinc deficiency in young rats (2.8 ppm vs. control, 98 ppm) reduced both the zinc concentration and alkaline phosphatase activity in femur, kidney, serum, and small intestine (Adeniyi and Heaton, 1980). Zinc Essentiality - Enzymes

While environmental zinc was not as ubiquitous as other elements, it soon became apparent that it was present throughout the body and was an important trace nutrient in animals. The growing field of enzymology and the recognition that within animal systems zinc levels were tightly regulated contributed to the research which ultimately led to our understanding of how important zinc was as a co-factor in enzymes and stabilizing factor in complex proteins. As key metabolic and regulatory enzymes were discovered, research on the physiologic controls and stability of these enzymes/proteins became pervasive and zinc was quickly identified as playing a key role in these processes.

Zinc is relatively low on the list of abundant elements in nature (27th). It is, however, the second most abundant trace mineral in the body after Fe (McCance and Widdowson, 1942) and is a co-factor in all 6 classes of enzymes recognized by the International Union of Biochemistry (Vallee and Falchuk, 1993). These authors suggested that the lack of overt zinc toxicity over a range of intakes was a function of the mechanisms governing the balance between intake, distribution within the body and excretion. This tightly regulated balance is a primary factor contributing to the association of zinc with a wide range of proteins/enzymes. It can have either catalytic or structural roles within a given enzyme (Bertini et al., 1985) and sometimes both (i.e., liver alcohol dehydrogenase; Sytkowski and Vallee, 1978). The stability of zinc compared with other essential trace minerals (Mn, Fe, Co, Cu) make it an ideal co-factor where redox-stable ions are needed (Williams, 1987; Butler, 1998). In its role as a catalytic facilitator, zinc and histidine largely stabilize the localized environment of the active site during the course of an enzymatic reaction (McCall et al., 2000). There are unique situations where histidine is more than a simple co-factor in selected dietary ingredients which may be high in zinc but have reduced digestibility (i.e. sesame seed meal; Lease, 1972;1975).

Glutamic acid, aspartic acid and cysteine can also serve in the capacity as zinc ligands albeit to a lesser degree (Vallee and Auld, 1990). Where zinc is a contributor to the structural stability of enzymes/proteins, it is often found as a single metal ion with cysteine being the most common ligand followed to a lesser extent by histidine (Christianson, 1991; McCall et al., 2000). Relative to its structural role, it is most often involved in the stabilization of the quaternary structure of complex proteins (i.e., aspartate transcarbamylase; Nelbach et al., 1972).

Given the characteristics described above, it is not surprising that zinc would be a cofactor in enzymes and proteins in similar biological systems across multiple species. This has increased the utilization of zinc in developmental “biomarker” studies, some of which have used molecular tools to describe potential new roles for zinc in previously unreported nutritional health related functions (Kambe et al., 2015; King et al., 2015; Bai et al., 2022). While the era of molecular biology has dramatically increased the techniques available to study the role of zinc in poultry, it is beyond the scope of this review to address this new and exciting area of research.

The physiological systems having the greatest impact on commercial poultry production are those involved in egg production, shell mineralization, embryonic development and skeletal growth. Each of these systems involve mineral deposition, turnover or both in the case of fertile hatching eggs destined for incubation. In this regard, 2 zinc containing enzymes, carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase, are critically important cogs in each of the physiological processes described above relative to the production of the commercial poultry products. For example, these 2 enzymes were critical to understanding one of the early, fundamental observations in poultry nutrition by Buckner et al. (1922) that “calcium from calcium carbonate can be used for egg shell formation and skeletal growth whereas calcium from phosphates will only be used for bone mineralization”.

Carbonic Anhydrase.Meldon and Roughton (1933) were the first to describe carbonic anhydrase for its ability to catalyze the 2-way reaction [ H2CO3 <——> CO2 + H2O] in red blood cells. Mann and Keilin (1938) reported that one fraction of extracted carbonic anhydrase was high in zinc and confirmed it was a zinc containing protein devoid of other trace minerals. Keilin and Mann (1939) subsequently proposed that dietary zinc was essential for enzyme synthesis and not just enzyme activity. The gastric mucosa of cats, rats, and rabbits was observed to have a relatively a high concentration of carbonic anhydrase (Davenport and Fisher, 1938). Davenport (1939; 1940a) subsequently demonstrated that the parietal cells in gastric mucosa contained a much higher concentration of carbonic anhydrase than surface epithelial cells lining the gastric mucosa in cats, rats and dogs and suggested that the enzyme was involved in the secretion of gastric hydrochloric acid (Davenport, 1940b). Keilin and Mann (1940) noted that carbonic anhydrase had approximately the same proportion of zinc (0.31–0.33%) as Fe in hemoglobin and enzyme activity was positively correlated with zinc content, subsequently confirmed by Hove et al. (1940). The zinc:carbonic anhydrase ratio was also found to be similar in both gastric mucosa and red blood cells. Approximately 30 yr later, carbonic anhydrase was purified from avian erythrocytes (Bernstein and Schraer, 1972). Subsequent studies utilizing autoradiography and immunohistochemistry confirmed the presence of avian carbonic anhydrase in different regions of the kidney, pancreatic acinar cells, shell gland, osteoclasts (bone) and surface epithelial but not pro-enzyme secreting cells of the proventriculus (Gay and Mueller, 1973,1974; Gay et al., 1974).

The early 1940's was a period of considerable basic and applied research on carbonic anhydrase. There were several known inhibitors but they were also effective against other enzymes with metal co-factors (Hove et al., 1940; Kiese and Hastings, 1940). Mann and Keilin (1940) wanted a specific carbonic anhydrase inhibitor to study the functionality of the enzyme and within the pharmacology literature were reports that some sulfonamides resulted in a “fall in the carbon dioxide binding power of the blood” (Southworth et al., 1937). Mann and Keilin (1940) subsequently demonstrated that sulfanilamide was a strong and specific inhibitor of carbonic anhydrase and the reaction was reversable. Hinshaw and McNeil (1943) and Scott et al. (1944) reported that in turkey hens and laying hens, respectively, inhibition of carbonic anhydrase activity with dietary sulfanilamide resulted in soft shells and a decline in egg production followed by a return to normal production and shell quality days after the withdrawal of the drug from the diet. It would subsequently be shown that respiratory enzymes were inhibited by sulfanilamide and respiration/metabolic activity in the shell gland (uterus) was greatest just before the onset of shell mineralization (Altman, 1946).

Alkaline Phosphatase. In the literature, 2 early papers (Grosser and Husler, 1912; Forrai, 1923 as cited by Kay, 1930) are often cited as among the first to suggest that enzymatic hydrolysis facilitates the utilization of phosphate esters within the intestine. Kay (1930) noted that the optimum enzyme activity occurred within a pH range of 8.8 to 9.3 in mammalian tissues versus serum phosphatase activity which was both significantly lower and only detectable in the pH range of 6.8 to 7.8. This led the author to make the following observations:

“Is the activity of the enzyme to be determined at the optimum pH for its activity or at the apparent pH of the tissue in which it is found - the 2 pH's very rarely coincide - or at some arbitrary pH?” and “In working out a standard method for the comparison of the phosphatase activity of 2 or more samples of plasma, certain theoretical desiderata have been abandoned in favor of convenience.” In preliminary studies, Kay (1930) observed that in normal plasma, there was glycerophosphatase activity (albeit weak) within a fairly broad pH range (7.0–9.0) and B-glycerophosphate was the preferred substrate. The plasma enzyme appeared to have the same optimum pH (8.8–9.2) as mammalian tissue enzymes and magnesium was a positive activator. The experimental pH (7.6), while lower than the pH optimum, was validated within a linear portion of a standard curve ranging from pH 7.4 to 7.9. The author also recommended an incubation period (48 h) that was considerably longer than mammalian tissue assays which contained increased de novo enzyme levels.

Jenner and Kay (1931) subsequently confirmed the role of magnesium as a phosphatase activator in the kidney, liver, bone, intestinal mucosa, red blood cells, and plasma. These authors also noted that magnesium activation of phosphatase activity in blood samples from dogs and rats was far greater than in rabbits or cattle. The original method described by Kay (1930) was subsequently modified by Bodansky (1933) and Davies (1934) subsequently observed that phosphatase extracts from the liver and spleen of sheep and cattle had 2 optimal pH ranges, < 5 and > 8, whereas the intestine, bone and blood (plasma) enzymes were only active at higher pH levels, hence the term “alkaline phosphatase”. In liver and kidney extracts, Belfanti et al. (1935a) subsequently reported both an acid and alkaline phosphatase and the activity of acid phosphatase was low in serum and bone samples. Bodansky (1936) reported that in the presence of glycine and magnesium, the activity of kidney or intestinal phosphatase is proportional to the concentration of enzyme though they did not necessarily support the concept of enzyme homogeneity across tissues as proposed by Kay (1932). Indeed, Bodansky (1937) showed that hydrolysis products of different phosphates is unique for each tissue and within a given species.

When assessing serum phosphatase, the tissue sources and health status of the research animal also need to be considered. This supported what had been proposed by Belfanti et al. (1935b) who observed that bone alkaline phosphatase was far more sensitive to an acidic medium than liver or kidney phosphatase and proposed that these tissue specific enzymes were different.

Cloetens (1942) as cited by Lorch (1946), later confirmed the inactivation of alkaline phosphatase at pH < 4.5 but noted that the degree of inactivation was considerably reduced with the addition of zinc to the low pH solution. Mathies (1958) purified alkaline phosphatase derived from swine kidneys and reported a concentration of approximately 0.15% tightly bound zinc with a smaller proportion of a more loosely bound enzyme-zinc moiety. It was also shown that magnesium was required for enzyme activation and the site of catalysis was a phosphate ester-magnesium complex. Kunitz (1960) confirmed that in animals, alkaline phosphatase is most active in the kidney, bone and epithelial lining of the intestine where it catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphate esters in the presence of magnesium at pH 9.0. Subsequent studies would show that dietary deficiencies of both zinc and magnesium would decrease the activity of alkaline phosphatase in blood and tissues where it was active and this would be due to a reduction in tissue enzyme concentration, not just enzyme activity (Heaton, 1965; Kfoury et al., 1968; Adeniyi and Heaton, 1980). In rats, electrophoresis resulted in 2 and sometimes 3 phosphatase bands (Nehlawi and Heaton, 1979). In tissues from control and magnesium deficient rats, phosphatase activity was variable in the electrophoretic bands from both treatments though qualitatively the bands appeared to be similar. The activity of chick kidney alkaline phosphatase was significantly increased by magnesium (Jahan and Butterworth, 1984;1986) and the authors suggested that the chick bone and kidney enzymes were potentially expressed by different genes.

The Zinc-Enzyme Contribution to Shell Quality and Embryonic Development

As discussed above, zinc containing carbonic anhydrase and phosphatase are involved in multiple physiological processes involving calcium metabolism for shell formation and embryonic skeletal development. It was deemed important for this review to emphasize the importance of how these zinc containing enzymes contribute to those unique aspects of poultry specific physiology underlying egg and chick/poult production. There are many commercial forms of zinc available for inclusion in poultry diets under the premise of optimizing poultry production but rarely does the marketing of these products delineate the actual role/importance of zinc in the physiologic process. The enzymes discussed above are critical to both the mobilization and redeposition of skeletal calcium during the course of shell formation. These key enzymatic regulators of calcium:skeletal metabolism in adult hens are similar to the initiation of shell calcium mobilization necessary for the onset of embryonic skeletal mineralization.

Egg Shell Formation

The decade of the 1930′s was a critical period of research on avian reproductive physiology, in particular the cellular processes and biochemistry underlying shell formation. This included research on the developmental regulation of the avian ovary, oviduct and skeletal system as it related to shell mineralization. The 2 zinc containing enzymes, carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase, were subsequently shown to have essential roles in each of the aforementioned systems. In hindsight, the observation by Buckner et al. (1922) on the specificity of dietary calcium sources destined for skeletal growth vs. shell mineralization was a fitting vision for the research which had not yet been conducted. Meldrum and Roughton (1933) were among the first to suggest a potential relationship between carbonic anhydrase and shell deposition in birds. Hens at the onset of lay, older hens with both good and poor production records and leghorn versus broiler type hens became the research models because of the recognized differences in egg production and shell quality in otherwise normal, healthy birds. Blood calcium levels were shown to vary within the same hen during a single egg cycle although the levels were usually maintained within a fairly narrow range, facilitated by what would be later be known as “medullary bone” (Knowles et al. 1935; Deobald et al.,1936). Knowles et al. (1935) included an excellent review of the literature at that time. Common (1941) reported that hens with good egg production had approximately twice the concentration of carbonic anhydrase in the shell gland when compared with poor producing hens. The reduction in carbonic anhydrase activity was specifically linked to the anion portion of calcium carbonate, not the cation (Benesch et al., 1944). This explained why hens produced soft-shelled eggs even when consuming adequate dietary calcium. Gutowska and Mitchell (1945) subsequently proposed that circulating protein bound calcium was dissociated in the shell gland and bound to carbonate ions originating from bicarbonate in the blood. Inhibitors of carbonic anhydrase would also be shown to significantly reduce calcium deposition in the shells of the common oyster Crassostrea virginica (Wilbur and Jodrey, 1955).

Carbonic anhydrase was later observed in the columnar and tubular cells of the shell gland (Gay and Mueller, 1973). The utilization of immunohistochemical techniques demonstrated the similarities in avian bone and shell gland carbonic anhydrase(s) with that in other tissues and it would later become a biomarker for osteoclast proliferation (Gay et al., 1974; Gay and Mueller, 1974). Grunder et al. (1990) observed that carbonic anhydrase activity in the shell gland was decreased in hens fed a Vitamin D deficient diet and this was reversed with supplementation. It was subsequently reported that both the transcription and expression of the gene for chicken carbonic anhydrase II was modulated by the active form of Vitamin D, 1,25(OH)2D3, in osteoclasts and was a potential marker for osteoclast differentiation (Billecocq et al., 1990; Lomri and Baron, 1992; Quélo et al., 1994). From an egg production standpoint, Gill and Gangwar (1990) conducted experiments with hens under heat stress conditions and characterized uterine carbonic anhydrase isoform patterns. The staining intensity within each of 4 carbonic anhydrase isoform electrophoretic bands was reduced as was carbonic anhydrase activity. The authors hypothesized that this was a potential underlying factor contributing to the thin egg shells often associated with excess heat exposure.

Alkaline phosphatase, along with carbonic anhydrase also plays a key role in egg shell quality. Common (1934;1938) reported that serum phosphatase was increased in older laying hens when compared with adult males or sexually immature pullets. In this report, it was also noted that serum phosphatase was increased in hens fed a calcium deficient diet (0.37% CaO) and in chicks with rickets. With regard to chicks, serum phosphatase was observed to increase for the first 10 to 12 d post-hatch before reaching a plateau at approximately 3 wk. Applegate and Lilburn (2002) would later report that this was also the approximate age at which tibia and femur ash would plateau in broiler chicks. Egg production was considered to be a major contributing factor to higher alkaline phosphatase levels in hens and enzyme activity in different regions of the oviduct, albeit in varying concentrations (Auchinachie and Emslie, 1934; Sanz and Lorenzo, 1950). In pullets at the onset of lay versus older hens, Peterson and Parrish (1939a,b) observed that average plasma phosphatase and inorganic phosphorus concentrations were considerably higher at the start of a 26-h egg cycle. Gutowska et al. (1943) supported the concept that phosphatase was a key component in shell mineralization, though they hypothesized that actual calcification was not dependent on a source of localized phosphatase. These authors speculated that the process of bone demineralization and subsequent calcium delivery to the shell gland was largely a function of phosphatase activity in the blood.

As alluded to above, plasma calcium levels and phosphatase activity are variable within a shell formation cycle, between individual hens and across genotypes and this was subsequently confirmed by Taylor et al. (1965). This variability was used as the basis for genetic selection, the selection trait being high blood phosphatase within a randombred population of hens (Wilcox et al., 1962; Wilcox, 1963). During the first years of selection, Tanabe and Wilcox (1960) confirmed the age and sex effects previously mentioned while also showing significant increases in serum alkaline phosphatase in response to selection. Using the genetic lines as a model, Wilson and Wilcox (1963) used inhibitors of serum phosphatase and sampled different tissues for phosphatase activity. Phosphatase levels in the kidney, liver and bone were decreased to a lesser extent than was intestinal phosphatase. This led these authors to surmise that the genetic increase in serum phosphatase primarily involved the intestine. After 5 generations of selection, the high phosphatase line exhibited a corresponding increase in egg production which supported the earlier observation by Gutowska et al. (1943). Alkaline phosphatase activity was also found in the outer membranes of large ovarian follicles (Wilcox and Cloud, 1965) which supported the histochemical identification of phosphatase in the well vascularized theca interna by Abe et al. (1962). These authors also noted that the increase in alkaline phosphatase activity was in tandem with the incremental deposition of yolk materials in smaller developing follicles. The latter 2 reports confirm the earlier report by Moog (1944) that in the blastoderm of unincubated fertile eggs, compact yolk granules stain positively for alkaline phosphatase.

Snapir and Perek, 1970a, Snapir and Perek, 1970b reported that during an egg cycle, the egg was in the isthmus for approximately an hour during which time 2 shell membranes were formed. This was followed by approximately 20 to 21 h in the left oviduct or shell gland. They determined phosphatase levels within these regions of the oviduct in young versus old hens and leghorn versus broiler type hens. The latter 2 breeds have well recognized differences in egg production (increased in leghorns) and shell quality (improved in broiler type hens). In the young hens, egg production, shell mass and egg shell quality were consistently increased while there were no age effects on alkaline phosphatase or blood calcium nor differences in shell gland or isthmus phosphatase activity. Carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase were significantly higher in the isthmus versus the uterus in both age groups with increased carbonic anhydrase observed in the young hens in each segment of the oviduct. The authors also observed that there were no differences in either calcium concentration or alkaline phosphatase in the shell gland of leghorn and broiler type hens. They concluded that there was no relationship between calcium levels in the uterus and plasma and as such, alkaline phosphatase activity was not affected by rate of lay or shell quality. These results supported those of Hertelendy and Taylor (1961) who suggested that the higher calcium level in the isthmus was a function of the initial mineralization of the mammillary layer. These studies also supported the observation by that the uterus contained little available calcium and was largely a secretory tissue.

Medullary Bone

From an historical research perspective, Foote (1911) published an extensive treatise on the comparative cross-section histology of femurs sampled from multiple reptilian, avian, and mammalian species and from the data concluded “Bone exhibits a very ready structural response to functional demands.” This is an appropriate description for the observation 20 yr later by Kyes and Potter (1934) who described the ossification of the bone marrow cavity in tandem with ovarian follicular growth in pigeons. Richardson (1935) subsequently published an anatomical review of the different segments of the oviduct and concluded that “the fowl uterus was a vertebrate organ liberating a secretion which, in view of the chemical composition of the egg shell, must contain calcium salts in high concentration with a trace of magnesium carbonate…”. Also working with pigeons Bloom et al. (1941) used the term “medullary bone” to describe the cellular and mineralization sequences that occurred during a 2-egg clutch sequence. One of the critical observations made in this study was that maximal osteoclast activity was associated with shell formation and when no shell was present in the shell gland, osteoblasts were predominant. In studies utilizing Ca45, Driggers and Comar (1949) estimated that 60-75% of dietary calcium could be accounted for in the shell with the remainder coming from body stores, a sizable increase over the 10% body calcium suggested by Deobald et al. (1936). Tyler (1954) also incorporated labelled Ca in a daily AM feed allocation. Autoradiographs of the shells produced on subsequent eggs showed a unique pattern of mineral layering which was related to the time of oviposition. This was an indication of how much of the shell calcium was from the feed, bone or a combination of both. Mueller et al. (1964) would subsequently show that the correlation between Ca45 specific activity in medullary bone and egg shell calcium was 0.845, confirming the importance of skeletal medullary bone in shell formation.

As mentioned previously, the egg spends approximately 20 h in the uterus or shell gland, with the largest proportion of shell deposition occurring over the last 15 h, prior to oviposition (Warren and Scott, 1935; Burmester, 1940; Hertelendy and Taylor, 1961). Brown and Badman (1962) dissected small samples from the magnum, isthmus and uterus for determination of tissue relative respiration rates in Barcroft differential respirometers as described by Dixon (1951).

They reported that both alkaline phosphatase level and the respiration rate QO2 (uL/mg dry wt/h) in the uterus was higher than in the isthmus or magnum. They suggested that transport of calcium across the uterine wall required considerable energy, hence the maximal rates of oxygen uptake shortly before the initiation of shell calcification. The speculation by Feinburg et al. (1937) and Common and Hale (1941) relative to a potential reservoir of available calcium destined for shell formation would become the focus of medullary bone research as influenced by endocrine, cellular, and enzymic factors, in particular the 2 zinc containing enzymes, carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase. At the time of sexual maturity, ovarian follicular growth is primarily under the influence of androgen and estrogen and there is a concomitant increase in uterine carbonic anhydrase (Pearson et al., 1977; Nys et al., 1986). Medullary bone develops as an outgrowth of endosteal bone, can fill the marrow cavity and is a non-structural bone (Simkiss, 1967; Miller, 1978; Dacke et al., 1993). There were several fundamental studies that were instrumental to the understanding of how estrogen and parathyroid hormone influenced medullary bone turnover during shell formation (Benoit et al., 1941; Riddle et al., 1945; Riddle and McDonald, 1945; Urist et al., 1960). Research on the cellular regulation of shell mineralization soon focused on alkaline and acid phosphatase activity (Taylor et al., 1965). The activity of both is low at the time of oviposition but subsequently each enzyme goes through distinct, sequential changes in activity as the egg passes through the different stages of the shell mineralization cycle. Medullary bone contains an active population of osteoclasts and when combined with a large surface area and extensive vascularization, the end result is a rapid increase in calcium availability when compared with cortical bone (Hurwitz, 1965; Talmadge, 1969). Under the influence of parathyroid hormone, osteoclasts develop a characteristic ruffled border which is the region where activation and release of acid phosphatase occurs (Miller, 1977). The high proportion of osteoclasts in medullary versus cortical bone also contributes to the potential for extensive remodeling (van de Velde et al., 1984). Acid phosphatase increases quickly during the first half of shell mineralization (∼ 10 h) before reaching a plateau which corresponds to maximal osteoclast activity. Osteoclast associated bone resorption is dependent on localized acidification facilitated by carbonic anhydrase (Gay et al., 1984; Baron et al., 1985). At approximately the time of oviposition and completed mineralization, there is a dramatic decline in acid phosphatase activity. This decline is followed by a gradual rise in osteoblast generated alkaline phosphatase activity which peaks at the time of initial shell deposition on the subsequent egg, approximately 8 to 9 h after the previous oviposition. There is little change in blood enzyme levels if shell mineralization is not occurring. Similar to what was observed for osteoclast related acid phosphatase secretion, alkaline phosphatase is a marker for the osteoblast mediated redeposition of calcium in medullary bone.

Embryonic Development – Skeletal Growth

The 1920′s represented a decade of considerable scientific interest in the cellular components of long bone growth, selected mechanisms underlying tissue-mineral interactions and the enzyme(s) involved therein, primarily phosphatase. Many of these studies incorporated young mammals and chick embryos as the tissues did not need decalcification prior to analysis.

In young rabbits, Robison (1923) reported that in the kidney and immediate area near ossifying cartilage/bones, there was an enzyme (later shown to be phosphatase) which “rapidly hydrolyzes hexosemonophosphoric ester and glycerophosphoric ester with liberation of phosphate ions”. There was reduced enzyme activity in the liver, pancreas, and spleen and no activity in nonossifying cartilage and muscle. Robison and colleagues subsequently showed that the enzyme was also present in the teeth of young animals, had an optimal pH range of 8.4 to 9.4 and that blood organic phosphorus occurred in 2 forms, only one of which was available for ossifying cartilage via enzymatic hydrolysis (Robison and Soames, 1924; Kay and Robison, 1924). The hydrolysis within ossifying cartilage only occurred in the vicinity of and after the establishment of an ossification center in the developing bone (Martland and Robison, 1927).

Murray and Huxley (1925 a,b) grafted embryonic limb buds from 4 d chick embryos onto the chorionic membrane in 7 d embryos and the explants were capable of self-differentiation into complete embryonic femurs. It was during this time that Fell (1925) published a detailed histological treatise on chick embryonic long bone development from approximately 2 d of incubation through hatch. Two critical observations from this extensive report were that initial ossification occurs at both the distal and proximal ends of the developing bone and the similarities in mammalian and avian long bone growth at the earliest stages of development. Kay (1926) would soon thereafter publish a relatively short but seminal paper in which it was observed that in chick embryos, bone phosphatase activity was approximately 3-fold greater at 12 d of incubation than in newly hatched chicks and enzyme activity was unevenly distributed throughout the embryonic bone. This paper confirmed the presence of 2 phosphoric esters in the blood of which the smallest fraction was the substrate for bone phosphatase and in young animals (rabbits), there were major differences relative to the ontogeny of phosphatase activity in the bone and kidney. Martland and Robison (1927) subsequently noted that purified phosphatase from bone had optimal activity at pH 8 to 9 and was significantly reduced at pH 6.0. Fell (1928) continued with explant research, in this case with embryonic limb buds from 8 to 9 d chick embryos, which subsequently differentiated into a diaphyseal type tissue with hypertrophic cells, an epiphyseal region with smaller chondrocytes and in some instances regions of bone formation. For an excellent review of the earliest research on embryonic metabolism, readers are directed to Needham (1925). Fell and Robison (1929) joined forces on studies with explanted femora from 5 to 6 d chick embryos. They observed no phosphatase activity at the time of explantation but the embryonic bones would eventually synthesize the enzyme and activity increased dramatically over the course of the experiment. During the time in culture, the femurs increased in length 3-fold with a normal cellular progression from epiphyseal to prehypertrophic and hypertrophic chondrocytes. Readers are directed to 2 excellent overviews from that period on the proposed role of phosphatase in bone development (Kay, 1926;1932). Gomori (1939) observed that during the course of histological processing for phosphatase determination, alcohol dehydration only reduced phosphatase activity by approximately 20%. The author noted that this approach was based on the report by Martland and Robison (1927) who were successful in using alcohol precipitation during the course of phosphatase purification. The procedure subsequently developed by Gomori (1939) allowed for visualization of free phosphate at the active enzyme site in fixed tissues incubated with phosphate substrate(s) in the presence of calcium ions. Using a newly validated histochemical method described by Gomori (1939), Kabat and Furth (1941) would subsequently show that phosphatase activity could be observed in embryonic tissues that were still functionally immature. Using the same histochemical technique, Moog (1944) reported that the unincubated blastoderm stained positive for alkaline phosphatase and nuclei were more intensely stained than the cytoplasm. They also observed that by 5 d of incubation, true hypertrophic cartilage cells could be discerned which stained strongly for phosphatase and by 6 d, phosphatase accumulation began along the diaphysis and moved toward the epiphysis where it ended at the junction with a layer of flattened cells.

Embryonic Development–Incubation

In commercial practice, millions of fertile eggs are either set each day or subjected to a “quiescent” biological storage period for incubation at a later point in time. Once eggs are set, the biological process of embryonic development has relatively few requirements, a consistent source of heat, some form of humidity control and the ability to turn eggs with some degree of regularity. One of the key physiological processes during incubation is keeping a balance between the moisture produced by embryonic metabolism and moisture lost via egg shell permeability. During the course of commercial incubation, factors such as the length of preincubation storage, egg/embryo size as determined by hen age, the balance of embryonic heat production with external temperature, and the type of incubation practiced (i.e., single stage vs. multi-stage) all contribute to a rather complicated endeavor whose success is often measured in small percentage differences (Reijrink, et al., 2008; Mortola and Al Awam, 2010; Noiva, et al., 2014). For an excellent and detailed review of the complexity of incubational physiology and metabolism, readers are directed to Everaert, et al. (2011).

Successful embryonic development requires a continual balance between evaporative moisture loss via the egg shell and maintaining adequate water for embryonic development (Ar and Rahn, 1980). When a fertile egg is produced, the albumen contains most of available water which is gradually transferred to the yolk sac via the process of “sub-embryonic fluid” (SEF) production by the blastoderm (Baggot, 2001; 2002).

In the earliest stages of incubation (48 h), the initiation of water transfer from the albumen to the yolk sac occurs and this results in fluid pools under the blastoderm. This fluid was alternately referred to as sub-blastodermic fluid (Murray, 1933), liquified yolk (Romanoff, 1943) and sub-embryonic fluid (SEF; Baggott et al., 2002). In both the domestic fowl and Japanese quail, the simple task of egg turning can influence the quantity of fluid produced during the period of its formation (Deeming, 1989a,b,c; Baggott et al., 2002). The production of SEF by the quail blastoderm is dependent on the availability of sodium, the movement of which from albumen to the SEF is dependent upon carbonic anhydrase (Babiker and Baggott, 1992; Latter and Baggott, 2002). These authors hypothesized that the location of the enzyme “on the yolk-facing, basolateral, plasma membrane of the endodermal cell catalyzes the hydration of carbon dioxide to form bicarbonate and hydrogen ions. The protons are exchanged passively for sodium ions at the surface of the cell facing the albumen.” Bakst and Holm (2003) subsequently confirmed that carbonic anhydrase was also located on the yolk sac membrane of turkey embryos and had a key role in the stability of fertile eggs subjected to long term pre-incubation storage. As mentioned previously, the periodic turning of eggs is a key component of incubation (Deeming, 1989a,b,c; Deeming and Deeming, 1991). Carbonic anhydrase exists as multiple isoforms and De Winter et al. (2008) observed that increased carbonic anhydrase IV expression in turned eggs was associated with increased SEF production from 84 to 96 h of incubation in Japanese quail embryos. This was in contrast with carbonic anhydrase IX expression which was highest in the blastoderm but was not affected by turning.

Plimmer and Lowndes (1924) were among the first to describe the egg shell as being important in the regulation of embryonic moisture loss. They linked an observed increase in the “lime” concentration in the shell membrane with a loss of calcium from the shell beginning at approximately 11 d of incubation. This was confirmed by Buckner and Martin, 1925a,b) who also speculated that from 0 to 9 d, a low level of embryonic CO2 diffused through the shell membrane and initiated a small but measurable release of shell Ca which diffused back through the membrane to the embryo. Van Goor (1940), as cited by Clark (1951), reported that by 3 d, carbonic anhydrase was present in embryonic optic vesicles but did not appear in the blood until 12 d. Clark (1951) subsequently showed that the enzyme was sequentially produced in the retina, stomach, kidney, brain and lens of the eye. Johnston and Comar (1955), using radiolabeled Ca45, confirmed that at approximately 11 to 12 d, Ca45 mobilized from the shell passed into the embryonic circulation, confirming the observations by Van Goor (1940). The labelled calcium became an increasing proportion of total embryonic ash and was correlated with morphological changes in embryonic development.

During the course of incubation, there are 2 phases of embryonic growth, the differentiation of tissues and associated morphological changes during the first half of incubation followed by continued tissue growth and maturation (Baggott et al., 2002). During the initial, differentiation phase of incubation, there are 4 distinct extra-embryonic membranes (amnion, chorion, yolk sac membrane and allantois) which modulate both the accumulation and loss of embryonic fluids during the course of allantoic and amniotic sac expansion (Baggott, 2001). These compartmental changes are influenced by incubation temperature, humidity and turning of the eggs within incubators (Deeming and Deeming, 1991).

The chorioallantois membrane represents the fusion of 2 membranes and is responsible for much of the respiratory exchange activity within developing avian embryos (Crooks and Simkiss, 1975). The membrane fully lines the inner shell by 11 d of incubation and its’ cells are fully differentiated by 14 d. The chorioallantois membrane has been shown to be an important component of active calcium transfer beginning at approximately 14 d and then continues through hatch. This process is not coupled with either sulfate or phosphate transport (Terepka et al., 1969). The process of calcium ion transport requires extensive oxidative phosphorylation (Garrison and Terepka, 1972). The liberation and transfer of calcium from the shell to the embryo is mediated by the chorioallantoic membrane as governed by embryonic age (Johnston and Comar, 1955; Terepka et al. (1969) and is temporally correlated with carbonic anhydrase activity within the membrane (Tuan and Zrike, 1978). There is also a chorioallantoic membrane specific calcium binding protein which is different than the Vitamin D induced calcium binding intestinal protein (Tuan and Scott, 1977; Tuan, 1984; Tuan and Knowles, 1984).

Yolk - Embryo Zinc Relationship

As discussed earlier in this paper, the development of updated zinc analytical methods allowed Birckner (1919) to confirm the observation by Weitzel (1914) on the presence of zinc in egg yolk using significantly less sample material. Romanoff and Romanoff (1949) reported that zinc is found almost entirely in the yolk with very little in the albumen and shell. Tupper at al. (1954), using radiolabeled 65Zn-glycine, would subsequently confirm the zinc-egg yolk observation and would go on to describe the tight binding affinity between zinc and lipovitellin. Phosvitin and lipovitellin along with calcium are packaged within a granule fraction in the yolk which becomes a major storage site of multiple trace minerals in addition to zinc (Bellairs et al., 1972; Grau et al.,1979; Richards, 1991). Kienholz et al. (1965) would subsequently confirm that the yolk accounted for all the labeled zinc in the egg following an intramuscular injection of radiolabeled 65Zn. Verheyen et al. (1990) reported that even with very high levels of the dietary zinc (10,000 ppm) used to induce a molt, zinc accumulated only in the yolk over the course of a 6 d molt period.

From the perspective of mineral transfer out of the yolk, Dewar et al. (1974) reported that the pattern of zinc, copper and manganese transfer from the yolk to the embryo existed in 2 phases, initial rapid uptake from ED 5 to 10 with more gradual uptake from ED 10 to 18 followed by a somewhat quiescent period from ED19 through hatch. These authors noted that the data for zinc did not suggest that the amount in the egg was correlated with the amount transferred to the embryo. Thirty-five years later, Yair and Uni (2011) reported a similar 2-phase pattern of zinc uptake between ED 0 to 11 and ED 11 to 17 albeit without citing Dewar et al. (1974). These authors (Yair and Uni, 2011) calculated approximately 94% total disappearance of zinc at the time of hatch and also reported that 37% of total egg zinc was associated with the albumen at the start of incubation, considerably higher than most other reports in the literature (Romanoff and Romanoff, 1949; Tupper et al., 1954; Naber, 1979; Richards, 1997). Yair and Uni (2011) reported both the uptake of zinc from the yolk sac and the effect of organic zinc administered in ovo at ED 17 which resulted in a small but significant increase in yolk zinc content. In a subsequent study, they confirmed the increase in yolk zinc via in ovo administration but did not show any differences between “enrichment” with organic versus inorganic zinc (Yair et al., 2015). It is interesting that the similar patterns of embryonic zinc uptake reported by Dewar et al. (1974) and Yair and Uni (2011) mirror the significant decline in zinc dependent alkaline phosphatase reported by Li et al. (2014) during the latter stages of incubation.

The composite of papers from the Uni research group (Yadgary et al., 2011; Uni et al., 2012; Yair et al., 2013) also reported intestinal and skeletal measurements during both the late embryonic and post-hatch growth phases through market ages. Yair and Uni (2011) hypothesized that “high-metabolism, fast-growing broiler embryos and chickens may reach levels of mineral deficiency that can lead to metabolic disorders”, citing Tona et al. (2004) and Angel (2007). It should be noted that using unselected and selected broiler lines differing greatly in growth and carcass traits, Fasenko (2004; 2009) found few differences in selected aspects of embryonic metabolism when egg size was equalized prior to incubation. To conclude this section, the literature on the biological relationships underlying embryonic skeletal development in commercial poultry is still not clear, partly due to a variety of experimental designs incorporating genetics, incubation, age of breeder hen and trace mineral manipulation of the external (maternal diet) and internal (in ovo) environment. Interested readers are encouraged to read the following papers (Favero et al., 2013; Oliveira et al., 2015; Hopcroft et al., 2019) and an extensive review by Torres and Korver (2018) who concluded that “embryonic bone formation has largely ceased at 18 d of incubation and limited nutrients are not the causal effect of the plateau in bone growth”.

Zinc and Practical Poultry Nutrition

During the 1920's, when critically important research on the ontogeny of bone development was occurring in embryonic chicks and young rats, it was a relatively quiet research period with respect to studying the importance of dietary zinc. Stirn et al. (1935) discussed the difficulties in creating an effective zinc deficient diet although Newell and McCollum (1933) had hinted at a possible relationship between dietary zinc and skeletal growth. In a subsequent report, Day and McCollum (1940) admitted they had new data which “invalidated the conclusion from our laboratory several years ago that the element probably is not a dietary essential”. Their updated approach to creating a zinc deficient diet resulted in a 4-fold improvement in body weight gain in control rats, no differences in bone or kidney alkaline phosphatase but significant increases in total bone ash and bone zinc content.

The challenges presented and discussed in these early attempts at creating a zinc deficiency were major factors associated with the dearth of dietary zinc research in poultry, particularly as it related to practical type diets. As alluded to previously, visible deficiency symptoms were critical to finding dietary solutions for rickets (multiple species) and perosis (poultry). In the mid to late 1950's, reports from multiple labs suggested that zinc deficiency symptoms and requirement research was affected by a number of dietary interactions including the source of protein (animal versus plant), the level of macrominerals (primarily calcium) and the experimental research microenvironment. As noted by O'Dell et al. (1958), the observation by Tucker and Salmon (1955) on the need for zinc to alleviate parakeratosis in swine and the interaction with dietary calcium was key to a renewed interest in diet induced zinc pathologies. This was followed a few short years later by Prasad et al. (1963) with the groundbreaking observations on the effects of zinc deficiency in humans associated with phytate and high fiber diets.

Practical poultry nutrition research with the byproduct rice bran (Titus and Ginn, 1931) was an important first step in the cascade of research efforts that ultimately identified manganese deficiency as being a primary causative factor in the onset of perosis. From a practical nutrition standpoint, this sparked considerable interest in the area of “unidentified growth factors” in other grain byproducts (Combs et al., 1954; Camp et al., 1955; Morrison et al. 1955). At this point in time, the research results with byproducts or byproduct blends with “unidentified growth factors” activity were often inconsistent. This was attributed to variability within the same source of a given by-product along with potential carryover effects in chicks if byproducts were included in the hen diet (Dannenburg et al., 1955). These authors incorporated corn dried distillers solubles (DDS) into practical type diets for chicks and reported positive growth responses from a diet containing the ash equivalent of 3% DDS. The growth data confirmed the results of Couch et al. (1955) and Morrison et al. (1955) who showed a similar response with the ash of a byproduct mix which contained DDS. In a subsequent study, Morrison et al. (1956) incorporated 6% DDS or the ash equivalent in diets fed to chicks from “depleted” hens fed diets without DDS and observed not only increased growth at 4 wk but more importantly, increased bone ash and a decreased incidence of leg abnormalities and enlarged hocks. O'Dell and Savage (1957) hypothesized that the diet used by Dannenburg et al. (1955) was deficient in potassium and zinc, critical trace nutrients in DDS. In subsequent experiments, O'Dell et al. (1958) fed a modified Dannenburg basal diet (7 ppm zinc) with supplemented potassium to which zinc or the ash of 5% DDS was added (total 63 ppm zinc). A distinct set of zinc deficiency symptoms were observed including reduced growth, shortened and thickened long bones, hock enlargement, frizzled feathers but only a small decrease in bone ash. Zinc supplementation and/or the addition of DDS ash alleviated the deficiency symptoms to some extent. In a subsequent experiment with 3 levels of calcium (1.1%, 1.6%, 2.1%) and zinc (0, 63 ppm), supplemental zinc eliminated a calcium induced exacerbation of the zinc deficiency symptoms. The observation by O'Dell on “shortened and thickened” bones and “hock enlargement” resulted in these measurements becoming popular metrics in subsequent zinc deficiency studies.

During this same 5-to-7-yr time period, numerous zinc requirement studies were conducted using isolated soy protein (O'Dell and Savage, 1957), acid extracted soybean protein (Roberson and Schaible, 1958a, Roberson and Schaible, 1958b), soy protein versus casein/gelatin (Morrison and Sarett, 1958), soy protein versus egg white protein (Moeller and Scott, 1958), and autoclaved soybean meal and EDTA (Kratzer et al.,1959). These studies confirmed the hypothesis that zinc utilization was impaired in plant vs. animal protein sources, often resulting in increased zinc requirement estimates. The plant protein effects were in fact shown to have a phytate component

(O'Dell et al., 1964; Savage et al., 1964) which partially explained some of the benefits associated with heat treatment of soybean meal (Supplee et al., 1958; Kratzer et al., 1959). Lease (1966; 1972;1975; Lease and Williams Jr, 1967) would subsequently report variable effects on zinc availability, both positive and negative, with autoclaved sesame meals from different sources and in comparisons of both in vitro and in vivo zinc availability with other oil seed meals.

The source of dietary protein and other environmental interactions were critical in conducting the first studies aimed at defining a dietary zinc requirement for poultry. Edwards et al. (1958) conducted numerous experiments with chicks and observed that distinct zinc deficiency symptoms could be observed when glass-distilled water and plastic (non-galvanized) coated battery cages were part of the immediate rearing environment. Young et al. (1958) reported that at 10 d post-hatch, chicks fed a basal diet (15 ppm zinc) required 40 ppm supplemental zinc (55 ppm total) for maximal growth and optimal tibia length:width ratios but only 10 ppm added zinc was needed for maximal tibial ash. At 21 d, the maximal growth response was observed with approximately 25 to 30 ppm zinc added to the basal diet (15 ppm zinc), slightly less than what was observed at 10 d. The same levels of added zinc needed to optimize the tibia length:width ratio (40 ppm) and bone ash (10 ppm) was also the case in older chicks. At 21 d, the elimination of enlarged hocks and optimal feather growth were also achieved with 40 ppm zinc supplementation. This was only slightly higher than the total zinc requirement range for maximal growth at 9 wk (42–45 ppm) observed by Mehring et al. (1956). Morrison and Sarett (1958) would subsequently observe that 25 ppm supplemental zinc added to a soy protein basal diet (30 ppm zinc) resulted in optimal growth and skeletal responses. Young et al. (1958) reported a detailed histological description of bones from zinc deficient chicks. The observed pathology symptoms were similar to those reported by Wolbach and Hegsted (1953) in choline and manganese deficient chicks without, however, the “slipped tendon” (condyle) characteristics reported with choline/manganese deficiency. Leach and colleagues would later describe the specificity of manganese relative to the biochemical mechanisms underlying the etiology of perosis (Leach and Muenster, 1962; Leach et al., 1969; Liu et al., 1994).

It is easy today to recognize the challenges with plant proteins at the time of O'Dell et al. (1964) as most likely being a function of phytate and its binding capacity for phosphorus and divalent minerals. What is not widely known is that one of the first phytase experiments incorporated an extract of a hydrolyzed Aspergillus ficcum phytase culture (International Mineral Corporation, Libertyville, IL) in diets with different cottonseed meals (Rojas and Scott, 1969). The phytase treatment increased the energy values of the processed cottonseed meals and phytin hydrolysis released some phytate bound proteins resulting in a marked reduction in the zinc requirement of the chicks. The same enzyme culture was subsequently used by Nelson et al., 1968 for his study with phytate phosphorus, soybean meal and plant protein digestibility. It should be mentioned that with the widespread use of fungal and microbial phytases in commercial poultry diets and zinc levels often well above published requirement levels, some authors have cautioned about the potential for a negative feedback effect of excess supplemented zinc on phytase activity. These studies have included both the type of phytase (fungal, microbial) and form of the supplemented zinc (inorganic, organic) in their studies (Maddiaiah et al., 1964; Santos et al., 2015; Akter et al., 2017; Philippi et al., 2023).

One of the consistent observations over the course of skeletal research related to zinc is the minimal effect of zinc deficiency or supplementation on bone ash in chicks and poults with noticeable differences in “shortened and thickened” bone morphometry (Young et al., 1958; Sullivan, 1961; Starcher and Kratzer, 1963; Davies and Motzok, 1971). There were consistent decreases in alkaline phosphatase activity associated with the minimal changes in bone ash and this was partially explained by differences in the distribution of the enzyme within different segments of the growing bone (Starcher and Kratzer, 1963; Westmoreland and Hoekstra, 1969). In zinc deficient poults, the highest enzyme activity was in the diaphyseal (mid-shaft) region of the bone whereas in zinc adequate treatments the opposite was observed, increased activity in the epiphyseal region where active chondrocyte maturation and calcification was occurring (Starcher and Kratzer, 1963). These authors also noted that zinc deficient poults had decreased enzyme activity compared with pair-fed control poults who exhibited no feathering or leg anomalies and concluded that phosphatase was not involved in bone mineral deposition. Westmoreland and Hoekstra (1969) later observed that in regions of non-calcifying bone, phosphatase activity is largely intercellular whereas in areas of intense calcification there is considerable enzyme staining of the matrix. These authors proposed 2 distinct roles for alkaline phosphatase related to the need for matrix synthesis/secretion in regions of active bone growth compared with the more mature, diaphyseal regions which already had a rich supply of secreted collagen/matrix mucopolysaccharides. The latter hypothesis would support the earlier observations by Starcher and Kratzer (1963) on the importance of regional differences in enzyme function (cellular differentiation versus mineralization). Davies and Motzok (1971) would subsequently report a 70% decline in tibia alkaline phosphatase activity associated with zinc deficiency, largely due to a decline in enzyme synthesis/concentration versus a simple decrease in activity.

Zinc in Breeder Diets

The research underlying the importance of zinc in commercial breeder hen diets had 2 distinct components. The first was identifying an overt zinc deficiency as defined by clinical and production metrics and the second component were the physiological responses/outcomes that were observed with dietary zinc levels above those needed for optimizing production traits.

The potential importance of dietary zinc in breeder diets was initially the result of experiments with “depleted” hen diets without the inclusion of byproducts purported to have “unidentified growth factors” which facilitated post-hatch chick growth (Morrison et al., 1955; Waibel et al., 1955). Morrison et al. (1956) subsequently suggested that the factor was a mineral cation associated with bone formation. Data from O'dell and Savage (1957) suggested that zinc could replace some of the unidentified factors in distillers dried solubles for chicks with similar observations in turkey poults reported by Supplee et al. (1958). Citing unpublished data from their lab, Edwards et al. (1959) reported that at 14 d post-hatch, chicks hatched from hens fed a zinc deficient diet (10 ppm zinc) had reduced body weight.

Blamberg et al. (1960; 6 ppm zinc) and Kienholz et al. (1961;10 ppm zinc) conducted zinc deficiency experiments with hens and in both of these reports, a deficiency decreased the yolk zinc content, reduced hatchability and increased skeletal anomalies in chicks that hatched. In the latter paper, it was noted that increasing calcium from 2.25 to 4.0% exacerbated the effects of zinc deficiency. Kienholz et al. (1961) also reported that in hens fed diets low in zinc, mortality was still elevated when hatched chicks were fed zinc adequate diets. Berg et al. (1962) observed that high levels of calcium fed to hens at the onset of production exacerbated marginal zinc deficiency resulting in abnormal feather development in chicks, similar to what was described by Young et al. (1958). In a second series of studies, Berg et al. (1963) fed hens fed graded levels of calcium (1.75 to 3.75%; 30 ppm zinc) beginning at 21 wk. All the eggs produced during the first 10 d of production and weekly thereafter through the first 4 or 6 wk were incubated. Abnormal feather scores associated with marginal zinc deficiency were evident in the chicks from the initial 10 d egg set and severity was increased with higher levels of dietary calcium. The condition improved with age in all treatments but was eliminated with 50 ppm supplemental dietary zinc.

The second part of the hen-dietary zinc story encompassed the benefits of zinc supplementation independent of conventional production traits and as previously mentioned, feather anomalies became a “new metric” by which zinc adequacy could be defined. Sunde (1972) reported that 10 to 20% of leghorn type chicks from commercial hatcheries would exhibit feather fraying with diets containing 38 to 44 ppm zinc with no other effects on early growth.

During the first week post-hatch, diets with 78 ppm would alleviate the problem. Stahl et al. (1986) fed a basal corn-soy diet (28 or 34 ppm zinc by analysis) with supplemental zinc levels of 10, 20, or 40 ppm. The basal control diet was adequate for egg production, fertility, hatchability and post-hatch chick growth in several long-term layer studies over 4 yr. Across all levels of dietary zinc, there were no differences in 3-wk body weights but there was some degree of zinc-related feather fraying in all treatments but unlike Berg et al. (1963), the anomaly did not disappear with age. It was unpublished results described in Stahl et al. (1986), however, that set the stage for considerable hen related zinc research over the ensuing 30 yr. These authors noted that zinc supplementation improved chick immune responses as determined by a hemagglutination antibody assay which suggested that supplemental zinc could enhance chick immunity. These observations were subsequently confirmed by Stahl et al. (1989).

Stahl et al. (1990) then conducted short- and long-term studies (12 wk; 24 wk) with 20, 200, and 2000 ppm supplemented dietary zinc and observed no adverse reproductive effects (fertility, hatchability, 3 wk chick weight) nor any differences in liver, pancreas, or tibia zinc across all levels of supplementation.

The above results started a paradigm change in turkey and broiler breeder hen diet considerations relative to the production benefits of zinc supplementation alone, the immunologic benefits of supplementation and their potential interactions with zinc form (i.e., inorganic, inorganic). Not all of these initial studies were similar in terms of the immune responses tested or the levels/forms of zinc supplements (Kidd et al., 1992a,b; Kidd et al., 2000). It is the goal of this report to make readers aware of this potentially important area relative to zinc nutrition. Readers are directed to the following reports in the poultry literature that address the breadth of research during this era (Kidd, 2003, 2004, 2007; Huang et al., 2019).

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Over the last century, there has been considerable data generated on the role of zinc in multiple biological systems. As mentioned near the outset, zinc is the second most abundant trace mineral in the body after Fe and is a co-factor in all 6 classes of enzymes recognized by the International Union of Biochemistry (McCance and Widdowson, 1942; Vallee and Falchuk, 1993). The primary goal of this review was to survey the foundational literature on zinc over the last century and integrate those reports into our awareness of those challenges facing the poultry industry. We have chosen to discuss in detail the 2 zinc dependent enzymes, carbonic anhydrase and alkaline phosphatase, because of their critical importance in optimizing egg shell quality, medullary bone turnover and pre- and post-hatch skeletal development which are as important today as they were 80 yr ago. There more recent zinc and trace mineral data in general has often focused on the pros and cons of organic vs. inorganic sources of trace minerals which the authors have purposely chosen not to review in detail. For an excellent discussion on the topic, readers are directed to Cao et al. (2000). In this realm, there is also considerable variability within traditional inorganic zinc sources (zinc sulfate, zinc oxide) in solubility and bioavailability estimates (Edwards and Baker, 1999) which can influence an organic/inorganic source comparison. The “potential” nutraceutical benefits of dietary zinc above recommended requirement levels were briefly mentioned but readers were left with appropriate citations and not an in-depth discussion.
