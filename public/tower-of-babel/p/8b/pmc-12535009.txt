The role of personal traits on the dignity of individuals living with schizophrenia: a qualitative study
Authors: Elham Amiri, Rahim Baghaei, Hossein Habibzadeh, Hossein Ebrahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchizophrenia significantly detrimentally affects the social functioning of individuals afflicted, particularly in the domains of interpersonal relationships, professional engagements, and personal self-care. These patients are viewed as a group facing discrimination across various aspects of social life, where their personal dignity is accorded lower respect, and they encounter challenges in accessing education, property rights, and the fair administration of justice, ultimately resulting in a breach of their dignity. The individual traits of patients play a vital part in safeguarding or compromising their dignity. This topic remains understudied within the sociocultural framework of Iran.MethodsThis qualitative study was carried out employing conventional content analysis. The collection of data was accomplished through the utilization of semi-structured and in-depth face-to-face interviews, conducted with 16 patients with schizophrenia (good or partial insight), 4 family caregivers, 2 nurses, 3 psychologists, and 1 service worker. The data were analyzed according to Graneheim and Lundman.ResultsThe main categories included “Strategies for Problem-Solving” (two subcategories: “Having a cognition-oriented approach”, and “Having a problem-oriented approach”), “Patient’s level independence and dependence” (two subcategories: “Patient’s dependence on others”, and “Independent”), “Patient’s behavioral dimensions and expectations” (two subcategories: “Respectful behavior”, and “Having too many expectations”), and “Patient’s clinical status” (two subcategories: “Disease status”, and “Level of insight”).ConclusionThis research found that the dignity of individuals with schizophrenia is linked to levels of autonomy and empowerment. Patients acknowledged that attempting to take control of their problems enabled them to preserve their dignity to the greatest extent feasible. Patients attempted to cope with the situation by acquiring new skills and enhancing self-empowerment. Therefore, nurses must be aware of how to provide support to patients with schizophrenia. Implementing counseling programs in medical facilities and community-based health clinics for families supporting individuals with schizophrenia is crucial to assist with patient empowerment and rehabilitation, thus enabling patients to reintegrate into society while preserving their dignity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07477-w.

Introduction

Mental disorders are considered one of the greatest health threats. Some studies have shown that the prevalence of mental disorders exceeds 29% [1]. One of the most devastating mental disorders is schizophrenia [2].

The burden of long-term disability associated with schizophrenia is far greater than that of any other mental disorder. Financial management is a challenge for many patients. Individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia frequently encounter limitations in executing routine tasks [3]. The violation of dignity can result from vulnerable conditions such as disabling diseases and can induce harmful consequences for patients, such as anxious preoccupation and hopelessness and lower for positive attitude [4]. On the other hand, protecting a patient’s dignity is linked to higher patient satisfaction and self-worth, shorter hospital stays, enhanced ability to manage their illness, and a sense of their life’s value [5].

Dignity is a complex and difficult concept to define. Respect for dignity ultimately boils down to respecting the status, worth, and honor of others and treating them well [6]. The two main forms of dignity are: “human dignity”, which represents the universal value of humanity, and “social dignity”, which is considered a product of an individual’s interaction with society, its culture, and traditions [7]. The patients themselves explicitly identified aspects of dignity in a comprehensive review of 9 qualitative and 13 quantitative studies, viewing this dimension as influenced by several key components (such as autonomy, respect and acceptance), which also encompassed spiritual and faith-related concerns [8].

In psychiatry settings, patients’ dignity can be compromised in a multitude of ways. Healthcare professionals have been found to exhibit a range of negative behaviors, encompassing rudeness, indifference, condescension, dismissal, disregard, intrusion, objectification, restriction, labelling, contempt, discrimination, revulsion, and deprivation [9]. Patients’ dignity is reportedly lost when their participation is disregarded and they do not receive adequate support [10]. Tehranineshat et al.‘s study identified poor communication as a significant threat to dignity [11]. In healthcare contexts, an individual’s dignity is vulnerable in two primary respects. Firstly, due to illness, a person may experience reduced control over their bodily functions, emotional well-being or mental capabilities. Second, the presence of illness and the stipulations inherent in healthcare delivery impose a plethora of constraints on individuals’ autonomy. This phenomenon is particularly salient in the context of an involuntarily admitted psychiatric inpatient [6].

It is worth noting that patients with psychiatric disorders are more affected by the social repercussions of their condition than by the mental illness itself, because they not only have to cope with the symptoms of their illnesses but are also subjected to abuse and widespread disregard [12]. People with severe psychiatric disorders continue to face stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination in their daily lives, as well as within the healthcare system and among healthcare professionals, resulting in adverse effects on patients’ dignity [9].

Investigating the role of personal traits on the dignity of individuals living with schizophrenia through qualitative research is necessary due to the complexity and multidimensionality of human dignity [13]. Despite such ambiguities, questions arise such as what is the role of personal traits on the dignity of individuals living with schizophrenia? How can the performance of patients improve their dignity or hinder it?

In recent years, policy efforts around the world have focused on improving the delivery of mental health services. However, due to the information gap in Iran regarding the role of personal traits on the dignity of individuals living with schizophrenia, the present study aimed to explain the role of personal traits on the dignity of individuals living with schizophrenia using a content analysis approach.

Methods

Study design

Taking into account the research objectives, the data were collected and analyzed using conventional qualitative content analysis to facilitate the understanding of communicative meanings [14], the discovery of individual interpretations of life phenomena, and the subjective data content [15]. Therefore, some categories can be directly extracted from the data to establish a novel and comprehensive dataset [16].

Participants and study setting

Information was gathered from 16 individuals with schizophrenia, 4 family caregivers, 2 nurses, 3 psychologists, and 1 service worker at three psychiatric hospitals, a daycare center, and four round-the-clock mental health facilities in northwest Iran. These centers were governed by rules and regulations, such as the implementation of locked exit doors and the scheduling of specific times for food and medicine distribution. The sampling process encompassed not only the centers but also extended to the homes and workplaces of the eligible participants.

Sampling was conducted after obtaining ethics approval from the Urmia University of Medical Sciences’ ethics committee (IR.UMSU.REC.1401.099). Upon gaining access to the relevant institution and hospital, the interviewer proceeded to meet with the personnel, where he introduced himself, presented a certificate and code of ethics, and requested that they identify patients who fit the study’s entry criteria for an interview. Patients were formally identified with a psychiatric diagnosis by a qualified psychiatrist. The interviewer was a psychiatric nurse with expertise in identifying patient’s insight, and she had 5 years of working experience in psychiatric departments. The participants were recruited using a combination of purposive sampling and snowball sampling with a focus on maximizing variation. Prior to conducting each interview, the researcher initially telephoned the participant, identified himself, and provided an explanation of the research’s objective. Data saturation is achieved once the interview has been completed. Saturation of data was reached when further sampling yielded no new data but merely redundant information that duplicated previously collected data. Saturation was achieved in the 23rd interview, following which two additional interviews were conducted to confirm that saturation had been reached and that no new information was forthcoming.

The inclusion criteria for patients stipulated that (1) individuals with schizophrenia had to be evaluated by a physician according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5); (2) they must have partial or complete insight into their mental illness; (3) they had to have been suffering from the condition for more than two years; (4) they had to be at least 18 years of age; (5) Not having received electroconvulsive therapy in the past six months; and (6) they had to be able to communicate effectively.The inclusion criteria for family caregivers consisted of (1) having maintained consistent contact with the patients for more than two years and (2) expressing a willingness to participate in the study.The inclusion criteria for healthcare personnel specified that (1) they must have had at least one year of experience delivering psychiatric services and (2) they had to be interested in collaborating with the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 26 participants. A sample of the interview is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1). The interviews took place in an intimate atmosphere with the consent of the participants. The interviews began with a general question about the participants’ inpatient days and were followed by specific questions about the influence of personal traits on patients in preserving dignity. The interviewer went on to delve deeper into their experiences by asking a series of probing questions including: “Can you clarify that?” and “Please explain?” We continued to follow up on the original responses by posing further questions to patients, healthcare professionals, and family caregivers. Each interview lasted between 45 and 70 min. All interviews were recorded and transcribed. Researchers documented their comprehension. Participants reviewed interview transcripts for clarity.

Data analysis

The constant comparative approach developed by Graneheim and Lundman (2004) was used to analyze the data [15]. After each interview session, the audio files were carefully transcribed. The overt and covert messages in the transcribed file were subject to a conventional qualitative content analysis. The meaningful units of data were regarded as words, sentences, and paragraphs, based on this classification. The meaningful units of data were condensed, abstracted, and labelled with codes. The codes were grouped into subcategories according to their current similarities and distinctions. The data were ultimately summarized within the main subcategories, after which the primary category was abstracted.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity, defined as the systematic practice of critically examining one’s own biases, assumptions, and professional identity, constitutes an essential element of qualitative research [17]. In the field of psychiatry, where the dynamics between researchers and participants are profoundly affected by power disparities, the importance of reflexivity becomes particularly pronounced [18].

Researchers systematically evaluated the extent to which their professional backgrounds, personal experiences, and conceptual frameworks regarding mental health impact their interpretation of data and interactions with study participants.

Consequently, the researchers endeavored to set aside their own knowledge, beliefs, values, and experiences in order to accurately and objectively articulate the perspectives of participants concerning the notion of dignity for individuals with schizophrenia. The researchers made no assessment of the data and accepted them without amendment.

Furthermore, reflexivity extends to encompass the intricacies of the researcher-participant relationship [19]. In psychiatric settings, where participants may experience feelings of vulnerability or exhibit deference to the researcher’s authority, reflexivity fosters a heightened awareness of how these dynamics influence the ongoing dialogue. Through the implementation of reflective practices, researchers were able to cultivate a more open and genuine atmosphere for participants.

Rigor

The trustworthiness of the qualitative outcomes was assessed using the criteria of credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability [13].

In terms of credibility, the researcher’s continuous presence in the field afforded the researcher a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon. Adequate time was designated for the collection and examination of data, facilitating the authors’ acquisition of profound insights. The study’s credibility was established by the researchers’ consistent interaction with the participants and data, regular review of the interviews over a prolonged period, and expert feedback on the interview process. To guarantee the accuracy and reliability of the translations, two researchers proficient in both languages independently translated the Persian text into English. A native English translator, holding a PhD, was consulted to guarantee accurate translation of English terms into Persian. The collected data were subsequently reviewed with the participants, comprising nurses, psychologists, family caregivers, service worker, and patients, and then analyzed using a triangulation method that involved individuals from differing genders, marital statuses, educational backgrounds, occupations, and age groups, as well as maximum variation sampling based on conflicting data. Extensive documentation and detailed reporting of interviews, coding, and analysis were meticulously maintained at each stage of the research process to guarantee the transferability of the study.

The researcher sought to enhance transferability by providing a detailed account of the study’s context, including the participants, sampling methods, and timing and location of data collection, thereby allowing the reader to form a favorable impression of the data’s transferability.

Another external audit was conducted to evaluate the dependability of the research by comparing the external auditors’ interpretation of the issues with the researcher’s own interpretation. In terms of confirmability, numerous interviews, extracted codes, and categories underwent rigorous evaluation by two faculty members proficient in the analysis of qualitative research data.

Strategies for problem-solving

Patients with schizophrenia had unique capacities to preserve patient dignity, varying from one patient to another which is classified in the form of two subcategories: “Having a problem-oriented approach” and “Having a cognition-oriented approach”.

Having a problem-oriented approach

Because in chronic diseases such as schizophrenia, the emphasis is on controlling the symptoms of the disease and not the treatment, the patient has a very important role in the management of the disease. Some patients admitted that by trying to empower themselves to face issues, they were able to maintain their dignity as much as possible. Patient’s clinical status

This category was described by “Disease status” and “Level of insight”. The level of insight means the patient’s acceptance or non-acceptance of the disease. Acceptance and adaptation to new conditions are an important phase in the life of a person suffering from schizophrenia. Some characteristics of mental illness (e.g. lack of awareness of the illness and refusal to take medication) interfere with better ability to cope with life. The acute symptoms of disease, which are involuntary and disease-related, such as hallucinations, delusions, mood swings, and emotional lability, lead to aggression and protests from those around them and violate the patient’s dignity. Discussion

This study was to elucidate the role of personal traits on the dignity of individuals living with schizophrenia. The main categories included “Strategies for Problem-Solving” (two subcategories: “Having a cognition-oriented approach”, and “Having a problem-oriented approach”), “Patient’s level independence and dependence” (two subcategories: “Patient’s dependence on others”, and “Independent”), “Patient’s behavioral dimensions and expectations” (two subcategories: “Respectful behavior”, and “Having too many expectations”), and “Patient’s clinical status” (two subcategories: “Disease status”, and “Level of insight”).

In this study, the main strategy of patients exposed to environmental violations of their dignity was to have a cognitive-based approach, which is characterized by the characteristics of “Concealment”, “Self-isolation” and “Mutual disregard for those around them”. These strategies were consistent with Link et al.‘s (1989) modified labeling theory about the effects of stigma and the strategies people use to cope with it. They emphasized that people may use certain strategies, such as Concealment and Self-isolation to protect oneself from the negative attitudes of others. According to this theory, adopting these strategies could protect patients from some negative aspects of labeling, but it could also worsen their social isolation [20].

Another strategy used by participants in this study to maintain their dignity was to obtain information and learn the skills needed to cope with a new life situation. The entitlement to be informed regarding one’s medical condition is unquestionably one of the most self-evident rights patients [21]. Therefore, any information based on patients’ needs contributes to maintaining their autonomy and dignity [22]. The patient’s autonomy and participation in healthcare choices are closely linked to their agency, dignity, and entitlements. Patient empowerment is now widely regarded as a crucial aspect of the recovery process, and healthcare professionals are being viewed as having a responsibility to promote it [23]. However, in the Eastern world, including Iran, it is common to hide the truth from patients because this region is family-oriented and the principle of “not harm” takes precedence over individual preferences. Telling a patient the truth is a sign of respect for the individual’s autonomy. Therefore, hiding the truth is considered as an inhuman behavior. There is also a need to revise Iran’s code of ethics to include the concept of telling the truth in concrete rather than general terms [24]. If patients learn basic facts about their disease and its management, they will be able to make informed decisions about their care. Second, after patients have been instructed on coping mechanisms, they can utilize these strategies to address persistent symptoms, the effects of which should become evident in terms of relapse and re-hospitalization [25].

Patients’ resources are crucial to maintaining their personal identity, dignity, and autonomy autonomy [7]. Patients with schizophrenia are often unable to carry out everyday activities. Dependency can threaten the patient’s dignity, as maintaining autonomy is an important factor in improving patient dignity [24]. However, financial management is a challenge for many patients to live independently. Independent living and community integration are areas that require more attention and work. They must be trained in meal preparation, laundry, and medication administration [3]. Patients’ independence can be promoted through social support [26]. Receiving social support is a crucial treatment element in the recovery of individuals suffering from psychiatric disorders. People with greater social support are less likely to be hospitalized in a psychiatric facility, regardless of whether they are admitted voluntarily or involuntarily [27]. Nurses can assist patients and their relatives in understanding how to cope with medical conditions, the importance of taking prescribed medicine, follow-up care arrangements, and enhancing connections between patients and their loved ones [28].

Relationships are a problem for people with schizophrenia. Many patients find it difficult to build a strong relationship because they cannot participate fluently in a conversation. If their relationship is ever successful, it often weakens over time and patients gradually isolate themselves [3]. People with psychiatric disorders typically have fewer social connections, which are often perceived as being of lower quality compared to those without these conditions. Individuals with mental health conditions often experience negative social consequences due to their heightened susceptibility to social maladjustment, limited social connections, and interpersonal problems. A lack of social support typically results in a negative impact on symptom management, hospital stay duration, and mortality rates [29].

There is a significant connection between identity dignity, illness, and hospitalization, as illness can undermine a person’s independence and pose a threat to their identity, which in turn undermines their dignity. In hospitals, patients encounter strangers, have to give up their daily activities and their personal and social roles, and are asked to carry out tasks assigned to them by medical staff, causing patients to develop new needs and risk losing their dignity [30]. In our study, most complaints were reported by involuntary patients. Involuntary patients are admitted without their consent and usually require close isolated observation, and sometimes physical restraint. The loss of control may also undermine the core of their personality and confidence. Individuals subjected to coercive practices reported feeling trapped and stripped of their humanity, especially when experiencing restrictive measures such as seclusion or involuntary admission. Significant psychological distress or even traumatic experiences have been documented in as many as 70% of instances [31].

In healthcare, a person’s dignity is threatened in two main ways. First, due to the disease itself, a person may lose control of their body, emotional life, or mental abilities. Secondly, illnesses and the need for health care severely limit human freedom. This is particularly true when a patient is involuntarily admitted psychiatric inpatient [6]. In Iranian psychiatric centers, patients’ lives are dominated by hospital routine, which separates them from the outside world of the hospital keeps them behind closed doors, and often deprives them of the opportunity to continue their education, work, or receive services. They are deprived of public services and other facilities [32].

Another influential factor in respecting patients’ dignity depends on patients’ behavior and expectations. Each person has their own needs that must be taken into consideration. In the present study, there was a mutual relationship between the patients and the people around them. The amount of respect that patients receive depends on the amount of respect that patients have for those around them. Patients who cooperated in doing housework helped in family financial matters, participated in family activities, expressed emotions and feelings, and showed appreciation received more attention and respect. Patients stated that when they report their problems to family members or nurses and they don’t see their concern for their problems, their trust in them is lost. Ignoring the patient’s requests by the family caused the patients to raise their voices. Dignity is a mutual relationship. When a person treats others with dignity, others will reciprocate in the same manner. Hicks states that a person who sees his dignity in danger will be able to violate the dignity of others and in such a situation, to protect their dignity, they forcibly take a series of countermeasures that lead to the violation of mutual dignity [33]. Despite the patient being right in any case due to their condition, unfortunately, in some instances, conflicts between patients and their caregivers are mutual.

Conclusion

This study found that the dignity of individuals with schizophrenia is linked to their autonomy and sense of empowerment. Patients reported that attempting to take control of their lives and confront their issues enabled them to preserve their dignity as much as they were able. Patients attempted to navigate the situation by acquiring new skills and enhancing their self-sufficiency. Therefore, nurses must be aware of how to provide support to patients with schizophrenia. Offering counseling services at medical facilities and community-based health care centers to family members of patients with schizophrenia is crucial to equip them with patient empowerment strategies and rehabilitation techniques, ultimately facilitating a smoother transition back to life outside the hospital while preserving patients’ dignity.

Limitations

In qualitative research, as demonstrated in this study, concerns regarding the validity and reliability of findings differ significantly from those in quantitative research approaches. In qualitative research, the key inquiry revolves around the extent to which researchers demonstrate rigor and thoughtfulness throughout the entire research process. The interpretation of results from interviews and their subsequent discussion with participants may be impacted by the researchers’ pre-existing understanding, both during the interview process and while analyzing the narratives. The extent to which researchers can set aside their preconceptions, a process known as bracketing [34], is a topic of debate, but an awareness of these challenges was taken into account during interviews with participants and in the development of subcategories and categories.

Implications

The findings of this research indicate a connection between the dignity of individuals with schizophrenia and their levels of autonomy and empowerment. Patients reported that attempting to empower themselves to confront challenges allowed them to preserve their dignity to the greatest extent feasible. Patients attempted to cope with the situation by acquiring new skills and enhancing their self-empowerment. Consequently, nurses must be aware of the methods to assist individuals with schizophrenia. Implementing counseling programs within medical centers and community-based health facilities is crucial for families supporting individuals with schizophrenia, as it enables empowerment of the patients and prioritizes rehabilitation treatment, ultimately facilitating a smoother transition back to life outside the hospital while preserving patients’ dignity.
