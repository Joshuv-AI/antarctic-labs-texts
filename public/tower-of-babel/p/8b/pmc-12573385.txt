New patients with duplication of the pituitary gland–plus syndrome, including a PTCH2 variant and a literature review
Authors: Kochakorn Buasri, Pattima Pakhathirathien, Thiparom Sananmuang, Sarayuth Dumrongwongsiri, Anucha Thatrimontrichai, Gunlawadee Maneenil, Arthaporn Khongkraparn, Lukana Ngiwsara, Phannee Sawangareetrakul, Jisnuson Svasti, Anne Slavotinek, Duangrurdee Wattanasirichaigoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuplication of the pituitary gland (DPG)-plus syndrome is an extremely rare developmental malformation of unknown aetiology.MethodsTwo unreported patients of DPG-plus syndrome are described. Underlying genetic defects were explored, including chromosomal microarray (CMA), whole exome sequencing (WES) and mRNA analysis. A literature review was presented.ResultsPatient 1 had DPG, palatal cleft, bifid tongue, intraoral teratoma, lingual hamartoma and duplicated basilar artery and odontoid process. Patient 2 had DPG, epignathus teratoma, a nasal mass, choanal atresia, cleft palate, bifid tongue, abnormal basilar artery and fused upper cervical spine. CMA yielded normal results. WES of patient 1 disclosed a novel splice site PTCH2 variant, c.1590+1G>A, leading to exon 12 skipping and an in-frame deletion of 44 amino acids. WES of patient 2 revealed no candidate variants. A literature review of 51 cases showed mostly reported in childhood and female sex (80%). The leading anomalies identified included DPG (100%), cleft palate (68.6%), anomalous cervical spine (56.9%), hypothalamic mass/enlargement (58.8%), intraoral teratoma (58.8%), basilar arterial abnormalities (43.1%) and bifid/trifid tongue (23.5%). Non-craniofacial anomalies were found in <10% of cases. Late complications included precocious puberty, all in female patients, and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism in a few patients.ConclusionsTwo new cases of DPG-plus syndrome were reported, with rare findings of epignathus and choanal atresia. We propose that DPG-plus syndrome may result from a double hit in one of the genes involved in SHH signalling, arising from a germline pathogenic variant with mosaicism for a somatic pathogenic variant or digenic/oligogenic inheritance of the SHH signalling-related genes.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

Duplication of the pituitary gland (DPG)-plus syndrome is a rare complex, developmental malformation characterised by having a duplication of a pituitary gland with/without a duplication of its stalk. With less than 60 cases reported worldwide, the condition’s aetiology, clinical progression and prevalence remain extremely limited.1 The first case was reported by Ahlfeld in 1880.2 3 As more cases were documented over time, the spectrum of associated anomalies linked to DPG was categorised into five groups: central nervous system defects, craniofacial anomalies, nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal masses, vertebral malformations and other related conditions.4 5 This constellation of anomalies was later termed DPG-plus syndrome by Manjila et al in 2011.6 Even though numerous genetic studies have tried to explain the syndrome’s pathogenicity, a clear mechanism is still unknown. Due to the unique sets of presentations associated with DPG-plus syndrome, current literature hypothesised that this condition is not a sporadic isolated event, but rather a possible genetic condition related to the early developmental process.4 6 So far, none have been reported in Southeast Asia.

Here, we presented the first known two cases found in the Thai population, both born with a midline cleft palate, bifid tongue, intraoral masses and DPG. We performed an exploratory study using whole exome sequencing (WES). A comprehensive literature review was also presented.

Materials and methods

Clinical subjects

The study included two infants with DPG-plus syndrome, followed up during 2022–2023. The medical records were retrospectively reviewed, and further physical examinations were obtained as needed.

WES and mRNA analysis

DNA was extracted from peripheral blood following a standard protocol. WES of the patients and their parents was performed on Illumina Hiseq4000 sequencer by Macrogen (Seoul, Republic of Korea). The WES data were quality checked and analysed, following the previously established protocol,7 and using the human phenotype ontology term ‘hamartoma’ (HP0010566: 75 genes), ‘bifid tongue’ (HP0010297: 24 genes) and ‘precocious puberty in female patients’ (HP:0010465: 10 genes) to filter for the candidate variant(s). Databases used for determining possible pathogenicity and minor allele frequency of the allele identified were as follows: ClinVar, the Human Genome Mutation Database and population databases including gnomAD (V2.1.1; GRCh37; 141 456 samples), 1000Genome, the Exome Variant Server and the Thai genome database (2184 alleles, T-REx database, https://trex.nbt.or.th/).

A standard PCR-Sanger sequencing of genomic DNA was performed to validate the presence of candidate variant(s) detected in the patients and the family members. To evaluate candidate splicing variants, total mRNA was extracted from whole blood, using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by cDNA synthesis using iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix for RT-qPCR (Bio-Rad, USA), following the manufacturer’s instruction. PCR-Sanger sequencing of the cDNA was done, using PrimeSTAR GXL DNA Polymerase (R050A TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan). Primers were designed for the PTCH2 sequence (NM_003738.5) flanking exons 9 and 13 (forward: 5′-CCTATGCCTGTGTGACCATGCTGC-3′ and reverse: 5′-GCGAAATGGGCAAGATTCCAGC-3′). The PCR products were separated by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The cDNA bands representing normal and aberrant transcripts were cut out from the agarose gel and purified using NucleoSpin PCR Clean up and gel extraction Kit (Machey-Nagel) before being subjected to Sanger sequencing.

Literature review

Previous publications published in the English language during years 1880–2024 were searched using the terms ‘DPG-plus syndrome’, ‘duplicated pituitary’ and/or ‘double hypophysis’.

Results

Clinical description of two patients

Patient 1 was a 7-month-old girl who was born vaginally, after an uneventful pregnancy, to a 32-year-old mother, P2002, at gestational age (GA) of 38 weeks, with Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at 1 and 5 min, respectively (figure 1A). Birth weight was 3005 g (10th–50th centile), length was 54 cm (>97th centile) and head circumference was 33 cm (10th–50th centile). Her family history was unremarkable. At birth, she was found to have a cleft palate with palatal mass, bifid tongue, lingual mass and two tonsillar cysts. Excision of the tongue mass with partial cystectomy of peritonsillar cysts on the day of life (DOL) 3 revealed lingual hamartoma with acute suppurative inflammation of the cysts.

Patient 2: (C) At birth, a large pedunculated mass covered with skin, protruding out from the oral cavity and a smaller mass protruding through the left nasal cavity. (D) The facial profile at age 9 months. Prominent eyes, telecanthus and microretrognathia were noted. (E) The floor of the mouth, showing a bifid tongue (T) and abnormal mandibular ridges (M).

Physical examination at 1.8 years, at our centre, revealed a length of 84 cm (25th–50th centile) and a weight of 13.3 kg (>97th centile) (figure 1B). There was a midline cleft palate with pharyngeal masses, bifid tongue and hypertelorism, with an intercanthal distance of 3.4 cm (2 SD) and palpebral fissure length of 2.8 cm (>2 SD). Her developmental milestones were normal except for the delayed expressive language, in which she could speak a few one-syllable words. Other physical examination findings were unremarkable. Almost 90% of her nutrition had been obtained through an NG/OG tube since birth.

Her CT scan of the oropharynx and the neck demonstrated a significant palatal defect with a fatty mass with calcification, suggesting mature teratoma, and a duplication of the odontoid process (figure 2) was seen. MRI of the brain and pituitary gland at 6 months of age demonstrated duplication of the pituitary glands and pituitary stalks, thickening of the floor of the third ventricle with tuberomammillary fusion, absence of midline sella turcica and pituitary infundibulum and a duplicated basilar artery (figure 2).

The patient underwent surgical removal of a midline palatal mass and had bifid tongue repair (with a common tongue base) at 20 months. Preoperative investigation revealed normal levels of thyroid hormones and morning cortisol. The pathology confirmed a mature teratoma containing hair and tooth. At the time of this report, the patient is 4 years old, has normal growth parameters (75th centile for weight and height) and has achieved psychomotor development with mildly delayed language expression and mild attention deficit and hyperactivity noted.

Patient 2 was a female preterm infant who was born at GA of 35 weeks, to a 27-year-old healthy woman (figure 1C). The pregnancy was complicated by polyhydramnios. A foetal intraoral mass of 1.7×1.6 cm in size was first detected at GA of 25 weeks. A foetal MRI done at GA of 32 weeks revealed a large, well-defined, homogenously pedunculated mass of 4.6×2.5 cm in size, protruding from the oral cavity through the lips and suspected to originate from the hard palate. The delivery was via an elective caesarean section and ex utero intrapartum treatment, including an endotracheal tube inserted. Apgar scores were 6 and 8 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. Birth parameters were weight of 2235 g (10th–50th centile), length of 47 cm (50th–90th centile) and head circumference of 33 cm (50th–90th centile). Physical examination at birth showed a protruding mass with a soft consistency and covered by skin, with the size of 3×3×2 cm, originating from the hard palate. There was also a yellowish mass protruding from the left nasal cavity (figure 1C).

MRI of the brain and craniofacial part performed on DOL 7 confirmed the foetal MRI findings, near total occlusion of the oral cavity and obliterated oropharynx and the protruding mass containing fat, soft tissue and teeth, consistent with epignathus teratoma (figure 3).

The patient underwent surgical intraoral and nasal mass removal using a telescope. The intraoperative findings also visualised abnormal midline mandibular fusion causing a bilateral mandible ridge at the midline, bifid tongue (two distinct tongue bases) and bilateral choanal atresia (figure 1E). A pathological study confirmed a mature teratoma of the oral mass. Postoperative MRI, obtained on DOL 34, identified an intrahypothalamic hamartoma or tuberomammillary fusion associated with a duplicated pituitary gland and its stalk, low bifurcation of the basilar arteries and incomplete fusion of the upper cervical spine (C1–C2 vertebrae) (figure 3).

Re-examination after the surgery revealed hypertelorism and microretrognathia. Evaluation for pituitary hormones was done, which indicated normal thyroid functions and a low level of morning cortisol at 0.53 µg/dL (normal range 5–25 µg/dL) with ACTH 25.8 pg/mL (normal range 15–20 µg/dL), leading to a suspicion of central adrenal insufficiency. Treatment with a low dose of hydrocortisone was given. A tracheostomy was done to secure the airway due to choanal atresia. The patient was discharged on DOL 64.

Abdominal ultrasound showed mild hydronephrosis on the left side at age 2 months, which spontaneously resolved at 8 months. A follow-up MRI at 14 months and a craniofacial CT scan at 2 years showed an increase in the size of the residual tumour in the oral cavity and a new mass, likely a mature teratoma, at the floor of the mouth. The ACTH stimulation test was performed, which yielded normal results, leading to discontinuation of the hydrocortisone. The patient experienced multiple episodes of aspiration pneumonia during the age of 6–9 months due to oral motor dysfunction and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, necessitating the placement of a gastrostomy tube for feeding (figure 1D).

Developmentally, the patient was delayed, able to sit and stand with support at the age of 1.5 and 2 years, respectively. The patient is now 2.5 years old and can recognise her name when being called.

Genetic data

Both patients had normal karyotypes (46, XX) and showed no abnormality detected by chromosomal microarray (CMA, CytoSNP-850Kv1.1 BeadChip, Illumina).

WES analysis of patient 1 disclosed a heterozygous variant at the donor splice site of intron 12 of the PTCH2 gene (NM_003738.5), c.1590+1G>A. The mRNA (cDNA) study revealed both normal and aberrant transcripts (figure 4). Sanger sequencing of the mutant transcript indicated exon 12 skipping, resulting in an in-frame deletion of 44 amino acids (aa) with a predicted PTCH2 peptide of 1163 aa residues instead of the normal 1205 aa residues (figure 4). The variant was classified as a variant of unknown significance (VUS; PM2) according to the ACMG/AMP 2015 guideline for pathogenicity classification.8 The variant was not previously reported in the databases referred to in the methods; we deposited it in ClinVar and received an accession number SCV005401084.9 Additionally, variants in midline development and/or pituitary development (BMP4, CDON, GLI2, GLI3, HESX1, KIAA0556, LHX9, NKX2-1, PROP1, PTCH1, SHH, TBX19 and TGIF1), syndromic and non-syndromic forms of hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CCDC141, CHD7, FANCA, FANCC, FANCD2, FANCE, FANCG, IL17RD, KISS1R, NSMF, PMM2, SEMA3E and WDR11) and genes in the Wnt, Notch and Shh signalling pathways related to hypothalamic–pituitary development (PIT1, PROP1, LHX3/LHX4, PROKR2, OTX2, TGIF and HESX1) were not identified.

Data from the literature review

We found 42 articles, including 51 previously reported patients. Only 49 patients with available detailed information, together with the two present patients, 51 were included in the analysis.1610 The frequency of five associated findings is summarised and shown in table 1 and the Venn diagram (figure 5). 80% were female patients. The leading anomalies identified were double pituitary gland (100%), defects of midline sella turcica (68.6%), cleft palate (68.6%), hypothalamic mass/enlargement (58.8%), intraoral teratoma/dermoid (58.8%), cleft/fusion or duplication of the vertebrae (56.9%), arterial abnormalities (43.1%) and bifid/trifid tongue (23.5%). Of those with a bifid tongue, seven cases had a common tongue base, while three cases had two distinct tongue bases (or double tongue).6 36 Another two patients were reported as having a trifid tongue.13 15 Few cases had choanal atresia. Non-craniofacial anomalies were present in 5.9–9.8% of the patients, including urinary tract and cardiac anomalies and diaphragmatic hernia. Some cases were found to have mild to severe global developmental delays. Among 51 reported cases, only 17 patients were older than 8 years for female patients and 9 years for male patients. Discussion

We report two female patients with DPG-plus syndrome, representing the first description from Southeast Asia. Both patients shared characteristic malformations of the syndrome.

The syndrome impacts individuals across both the paediatric and adult populations, although among those diagnosed in adulthood, the symptoms tend to be milder and less prevalent,34 35 38 41 according to the review. Notably, DPG-plus syndrome affects female patients more than male patients, with a 4:1 ratio, but there is yet no explanation for this discrepancy. It has been observed that about 43–69% of the patients have a defect of midline sella turcica and hypothalamic mass/enlargement along with a DPG, cleft palate, intraoral teratoma, arterial abnormality, bony defects of C1–C2 vertebrae and lower frequency of bifid tongue.

For craniofacial anomalies as illustrated in the Venn diagram, 83% (29/35) of those with a cleft palate were also found to have a nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal teratoma. Additionally, 88% (29/33) of those having nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal masses had a cleft palate. Only 5.9% (3/51) had only an isolated cleft palate without any other malformations and 3.2% (2/51) had isolated intraoral mass without any other defects.1322 2931 On rare occasions, 7.8% (4/51) lingual hamartoma was found in addition to an intraoral teratoma.35 40 43

Sporadically, the patients develop endocrinological abnormalities later in life as a consequence of the disrupted hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. About 81.8% (9/11) of the female patients developed precocious puberty, whereas none of the male patients experienced such a problem; instead, these male patients (2/2) had hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (table 1).1 3 5 29 32 38 39 41 42 45 Interestingly, an impairment of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis was not evident, or it was only a transient finding. The extracraniofacial malformations, such as urinary tract/cardiac/gastrointestinal anomalies and spinal cord defects, are not common (table 1), but they deserve screening for the prevention of serious complications.

Due to the extreme rarity of the syndrome and the fact that CMA and WES have not been in use for a long time, most previously reported cases did not have similar testing. Only four cases had CMA, and none of them showed any significant results known to be related to the syndrome.6 42 43 45 None has WES done.

Based on the CMA analysis, we excluded genomic imbalance due to chromosomal microdeletion/microduplication as the cause underlying DPG-plus syndrome in the present patients. For the first time, we identified a variant of PTCH2 in a patient with DPG-plus syndrome (patient 1). Although the variant was found to disrupt splicing and was not present in the reference databases, it was considered a VUS and did not segregate with the condition. It is too premature to conclude that there is an association between PTCH2 and DPG-plus syndrome and that this variant predisposed the patient to the development of DPG-plus syndrome.

PTCH2, highly expressed in the brain, plays a role in cellular growth and may be a sonic hedgehog (SHH) receptor. PTCH2 is one of the top five proteins interacting with SHH (https://string-db.org/cgi/network), a secreted protein known to mediate the signalling activities of the notochord and floor plate. Recent data suggested that PTCH2 may play crucial roles in the facial development of mice and humans.46 47

We hypothesise that DPG-plus syndrome may be a condition that comes from a double hit in one of the genes in the SHH signalling pathway, with the second hit does not present in the germline but perhaps in some of the affected tissues in mosaic form, or it could be digenic and involve two genes in SHH signalling, etc, and alternatively, it could represent oligogenic inheritance. This would also be consistent with the non-penetrance of the PTCH2 variant in patient 1’s family. We know there is no proof and no increase in DPG-plus cases in those with known pathogenic SHH pathway variants. However, there is some support for this in the involvement of the SHH pathway, such as CDON variants (a SHH receptor) being identified as the cause of pituitary stalk interruption syndrome in a few patients,48 49 and oligogenic and complex genetic heterogeneity in the others.50

The intricate process involved in the formation of the pituitary gland results from two distinct parts: the anterior pituitary gland (adenohypophysis) derived mainly from Rathke’s pouch and the posterior pituitary gland (neurohypophysis), derived from the neural ectoderm.51 52 Bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) and the SHH pathway are involved in the process of pituitary development.51 52

Currently, there is limited understanding of the pathogenesis of DPG-plus syndrome. Many authors proposed that the incidence is due to multiple defects in pituitary organogenesis related to neurulation in early embryonic development. The syndrome is thought to be caused by either a disruption in a single specific insult or a secondary induction signal to create a duplication.4 6 Several proposed mechanisms include teratogens, failed twinning, median cleft syndrome, blastogenesis defects—splitting notochord during early embryonal development and genetic abnormalities; however, none have been confirmed.2 6 10 Specific teratogens have not been identified; twinning should involve the whole face; median cleft syndrome should be limited to the facial part and not extend to the brain structures; and relevant genetic abnormalities have not been evident.

Blastogenesis defects refer to embryonic maldevelopment from the time of fertilisation until the end of gastrulation or during the first 4 weeks; examples of blastogenesis defects include neural tube defects, oesophageal atresia, abdominal wall defects and anal atresia. These defects can involve the midline and mesoderm development, affecting two or more developmental fields. Blastogenesis defects are sporadic and have a low empiric recurrence risk.53 It was proposed that the splitting of the rostral part of the notochord and/or precaudal plate could result in duplication of the hypophysis, double pituitary gland, palatal cleft and double tongue; however, this has not been proven by animal models.6 10 Although an increased risk for blastogenesis defects has been observed (with an OR of 2.8) among those conceived by assisted reproductive therapies, DPG-plus syndrome has not been noted.54 Blastogenesis defects of the caudal part of the notochord were also described, such as duplicated spinal column and epidural sacs and split spinal cord (spinal dysraphism) at the lumbar level along with imperforate anus and a posterior urethral valve.55

Genetic control of notochord development is still poorly understood; several transcription factors (ie, HOX, SOX, TBX), growth factors (FGF, BMPs) and secreted morphogens (ie, SHH) could potentially interplay in the development of DPG-plus syndrome. There is also possible genetic heterogeneity in this condition. Alternatively, epigenetic abnormalities, ubiquitous (generalised) or tissue-specific and time-specific during embryonic development, are also possible underlying mechanisms. Studying the effects of genetic variants identified, including animal models, should be done in the future.

The incidence of DPG-plus syndrome is exceedingly rare, with only 51 patients documented over the past 144 years. However, it is likely underestimated, as some patients may be asymptomatic and only discovered incidentally during brain imaging, especially in male patients. Conversely, there are cases with extremely severe manifestations that are incompatible with life, so some cases may not have been reported. Although a syndrome characterised by multiple midline defects and a strong female predominance has been identified, the genetic basis remains unclear. Endocrinological monitoring may benefit individuals with early diagnoses, and further research is needed to provide a deeper understanding of the syndrome.
