The Usefulness of Vascularized Flap Craniotomy for Open-Wound Depressed Skull Fractures
Authors: Yoichiro Nakahara, Jin Kikuchi, Yoshikuni Kotaki, Yusuke Otsu, Keisuke Sugi, Hidenobu Yoshitake, Tetsuya Negoto, Motohiro Morioka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND IMPORTANCE:Free bone flap craniotomy for open-wound depressed skull fractures poses a high risk of infection due to free bone fragments. This risk escalates in the presence of compound fractures with accompanying dural injuries. The primary objectives in managing depressed skull fractures include infection prevention and the correction of cosmetic deformities. This report describes the efficacy of vascularized flap craniotomy, which preserves blood flow to bone fragments, in reducing infection risks.CLINICAL PRESENTATION:In this study, we present a 54-year-old man with a head injury and a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8. He had an open wound in the left parietal temporal area and depressed skull fractures with compound bone fragments. The patient underwent vascularized flap craniotomy, focusing on maintaining blood supply to the bone fragments, resulting in successful wound healing without acute infections.CONCLUSION:Vascularized flap craniotomy is an effective surgical option for treating open-wound depressed skull fractures, potentially reducing the risk of infection by maintaining blood flow to bone fragments. Further studies are required to validate its efficacy in larger patient populations.

Depressed skull fractures, especially when open, often require surgical intervention. These procedures must be performed with caution owing to the high risk of infection, particularly in complex scenarios involving free bone fragments or free bone flap craniotomies. The presence or absence of open wounds can substantially influence this risk. Dural injuries are present in over half of compound fractures involving open wounds, with infection rates ranging from 2.5% to 10.6%.1,2 This report describes a case of a compound fracture, wherein successful wound healing was achieved through vascularized flap craniotomy while preserving blood flow from the temporal muscle fascia to the bone fragments. In this study, we describe the usefulness of this technique.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 54-year-old man sustained a head injury in a motorcycle accident without helmet use. His consciousness level was 8 on the Glasgow Coma Scale (E1V2M5), with no sign of paresis. He had an open wound in the left parietal temporal area (Figure 1A). Head computed tomography revealed depressed skull fractures with an 18-mm inward displacement (Figure 1B and 1C). Owing to the considerable deformity of the depression and the high risk of infection posed by the open wounds, emergency surgery was performed.

An open Y-shaped split wound at the left parietal temporal area, measuring approximately 5 cm × 7 cm A. Head computed tomography (axial image) shows depressed skull fractures with internal displacement and hemorrhagic contusion to the left frontoparietal lobe B. 3-dimensional reconstruction images show multiple fracture segments C.

To avoid infection, vascularized flap craniotomy was performed, preserving the temporal muscle attachment to the bone flap instead of a free-flap craniotomy. Given the Y-shaped split of the wound, a skin incision line was designed to extend the injury point (Figure 2A). Burr holes were created, and the bone surrounding the depressed skull fractures was cut using a side cutter, excluding the frontobasal side (Figure 2B). At the base of the bone flap (frontobasal side), the bone was flipped while preserving blood flow from the musculoperiosteum (Figure 2C and 2D).

Preoperative schema. A vascularized flap craniotomy was planned because of the high risk of infection in an open wound. The skin incision line is designed to extend the injury point A, and the bone around the depressed fracture is cut into an inverted U shape using a side cutter. By contrast, the base of the bone flap (frontobasal side) is cut using a wire saw only at the inner plate B to flip the bone flap, preserving blood flow from the musculoperiosteum C and D. A wire saw is also called a Gigli saw or “senkyo” in Japanese.

We designed the craniotomy area where a side cutter would be used in an inverted U-shape and incised the attached membrane to a depth sufficient for creating a burr hole (Video and Figure 3A). To facilitate the passage of the spatula-leading wire saw, an additional burr hole was created (Figure 3B). After cutting for the craniotomy using a side cutter, except for the frontobasal side, the bone on the frontobasal side was cut from the inside out using a wire saw (Figure 3C). The bone was then flipped like a hinge using the principle of leverage (Figure 3D). All internal free bone fragments were removed while preserving the external bone fragment connected to the galea aponeurotica. The depressed and deformed bone was repositioned (Figure 3E). There was no damage to the dura mater or a subdural hematoma. The bone flap was sutured and fixed to the skull using nylon (Figure 3F), and cranial closure was performed.

After passing the spatula through the base, a wire is guided and inserted into the base B and C. To create the bone flap, the craniotomy area, designed in an inverted U shape, is initially incised with an electric drill. The base of the bone flap (frontobasal side) is cut only at the inner plate using a wire saw, and the bone flap is flipped like a hinge D. The depressed, deformed bone is repositioned manually with no damage to the dura mater E. The bone flap is sutured and fixed to the skull with nylon, and cranial closure is performed F.

Postoperatively, the wounds healed well with no acute-phase infectious complications. Although motor aphasia and clumsiness of the right hand persisted, the patient's general condition improved. The patient was discharged 19 days after surgery, and no complications were observed 1-year postsurgery.

The patient consented to the procedure and the publication of his image. The case report was approved by the appropriate ethics review board.

DISCUSSION

The incidence of compound fractures with open wounds in depressed skull fractures is markedly high, ranging from 75% to 90%,2,3 while infection rates for compound depressed skull fractures reportedly vary between 2.5% and 10.6%.1,2 One-stage surgery has been associated with an infection rate of 3.6%, and this rate was increased to 17.5% in the two-stage surgical approach incorporating cranioplasty.4 Regarding the timing of surgery, the rate of infection increases to 36.5% when surgery on a contaminated wound is delayed beyond 48 hours.4 Nonetheless, it has been suggested that, in the absence of overt infection, bone fragment removal may not be necessary even after 48 hours.4,5 Accordingly, one-stage and early (within 48 hours) surgery is recommended for open-wound depressed fractures with compound fractured fragments. Furthermore, increased ingenuity is necessary to prevent infections.

Surgery for depressed skull fractures is generally advised when there is an inward displacement of ∼10 mm or more from the skull surface, often associated with destructive cranial fractures.6 Historically, surgical treatment of depressed skull fractures has been conducted in 2 stages: initial debridement and craniectomy, followed by delayed cranioplasty.3,4,7 Recent advances have demonstrated that single-stage autologous cranioplasty is a safe alternative. In cases in which autologous repair is not feasible, single-stage titanium mesh cranioplasty has been deemed safe.5,8,9 However, this approach is unsuitable for open wounds owing to the risk of infection. Our vascularized flap craniotomy technique maintains contiguous blood flow from the musculoperiosteum and may reduce the risk of infection by facilitating immune responses.

Vascularized flap craniotomy was initially developed in 1889, with a bone flap attached to the surrounding temporal muscles.10,11 In 1912, free bone flap craniotomy emerged as a faster and simpler surgical option.11,12 Despite its complexity and prolonged duration, the vascularized flap approach is associated with a notably lower infection rate than the free flap approach.11,13-15 Shelef et al11 reported that a vascularized flap maintains blood flow to the bone, thus preserving its metabolic and immunological functions. This was evidenced by the higher radioactive uptake of 99m-Tc-methyl diphosphonate in the vascularized flap than in the free flap, indicating enhanced vascular supply and osteoblastic activity. Thus, vascularized flap craniotomy can be considered an effective surgical approach for infection prevention, especially in the presence of open wounds. Therefore, we selected this approach to minimize the infection risk.

The Gigli saw, developed by Leonardo Gigli in 1893, was originally designed for obstetrical use to address obstructed labor and substantially advanced surgical practices by enabling more efficient lateral pubiotomies.16-18 Despite the common use of electric power drills in contemporary neurosurgery, the Gigli saw remains relevant owing to its specialized benefits.18,19 It allows for precise cutting at designated angles while protecting the dura mater,18,19 creating a vascularized bone flap connected by the surrounding tissue.

The injury circumstances and anatomical location should be carefully evaluated. In the case of open-wound head trauma, a vascularized craniotomy can offer the best surgical repair if possible. The best source of blood supply is the temporal muscle, and the galea is considered the second-best source. Even in the case of a depressed fracture localized to the frontal or parietal lesion, craniotomy can be expanded to a certain extent with temporal muscle attached. For the occipital lesion, where the thick galea of occipital muscles extends, vascularized craniotomy can be performed. However, in the case of a large craniotomy with minimal muscle attachment, accompanied by insufficient blood supply to the whole craniotomy, this surgical method is inappropriate.

Importantly, in cases of compound fractures with open wounds and exposed cranial bones, adequate removal of the contaminated bone is necessary, thereby rendering this craniotomy technique unsuitable. Although preserving as much blood flow as possible to reduce the risk of infection is desirable, this technique may not always be feasible, depending on the fracture severity and location. The procedure for vascularized flap craniotomy can be slightly challenging and may result in longer operative times. However, these difficulties can be overcome by proficient use of the Gigli saw.

This report has limitations. The patient sample was a single case. Despite achieving a successful outcome, additional cases are essential to confirm the technique's effectiveness. The feasibility of performing vascularized flap craniotomy may depend on the fracture location.

CONCLUSION

Single-stage vascularized flap craniotomy can be valuable in treating depressed skull fractures with open wounds because it maintains blood flow to the bone fragments, potentially reducing the risk of infection.
