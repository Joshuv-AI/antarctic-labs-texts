HeyJay! A corpus of atypical speech for spoken language understanding and automatic speech recognition
Authors: Laureano Moro-Velazquez, Helin Wang, Alison Gunzler, Milind Rao, Ankur A. Butala, Lora Clawson, Venkatesh Ravichandran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Speech technologies, such as automatic speech recognition or spoken language understanding, are not usually adapted to atypical speech, i.e., the speech of people with dysarthria, dysphonia, or another type of speech impairment. That prevents atypical speakers from leveraging speech assistants or other human-machine-interaction-powered platforms, which could make their lives easier or increase their independence. In this article, we present HeyJay!, a new corpus of atypical speech in English language from participants with neurodegenerative disorders, including Parkinson’s Disease, or Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis. The current corpus version comprises 8,669 utterance recordings, including supervised transcriptions and intent annotations. In this study, we demonstrate the validity of the corpus by applying it to automatic speech recognition, spoken language understanding, and data augmentation tasks. Additionally, the dataset includes speech quality ratings for each participant, performed by expert speech and language pathologists. This corpus, the first one with intent annotation of atypical speech that is publicly available, is intended to create more fair speech technologies for atypical speakers by adapting and improving the state of the art, and to facilitate further research in the field.

Background & Summary

The ability to speak to communicate effectively is essential to safety, independence, social engagement, and, therefore, quality of life1–6. Unfortunately, this communication method is often lost to persons with atypical speech (PwAS) when family, caregivers, peers and assistive devices often fail to comprehend. Atypical speech is usually characterized by atypical articulation, prosody, phonation, respiration, or a combination of several of these aspects, and it is common in people with movement disorders such as Parkinson’s Disease (PD), Multiple Sclerosis (MS), Cerebellar and Spinocerebellar Ataxias, or Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS). This particular population has its independence compromised by movement limitations and would benefit from technological advancements to enhance or complement their motor capabilities, thereby reducing their reliance on caregivers. Speech assistants on dedicated devices or smartphones and speech-controlled robots could help this population recover part of their independence and reduce the impact of the movement disorder in their lives by carrying out specific tasks, such as turning on and off appliances or unlocking a door. However, these types of smart assistants usually struggle to understand them.

Spoken language understanding (SLU) and automatic speech recognition (ASR) are two of the base technologies for speech assistants. SLU models prioritize semantic representations by learning to map the spoken utterance directly to an intent label, bypassing the step of producing a textual representation7, in contrast to ASR models which prioritize text accuracy. SLU and ASR technologies are not always trained to understand atypical speech because training commonly requires hours of exposure to PwAS before learning their speech patterns, which is not commonly available8–10. New atypical speech corpora, together with their associated transcription or intent annotations would enable the training and adaptation of new ASR or SLU systems for PwAS. Similarly, this speech can be used to create numerous hours of synthetic atypical speech to enhance training outcomes11–14.

On the other hand, future real-time voice conversion and augmentative speech technologies for PwAS will allow them to communicate more fluently with others face-to-face, on video calls, phone calls, and consequently, increase their participation in educational, social, and professional scenarios10.

One of the primary reasons for the limited research on atypical speech technologies is the scarcity of publicly available datasets, which can be attributed to several factors. To name a few, this population does not usually record their speech or appear in podcasts or public resources involving speech, making it difficult to gather new datasets; to record long sessions is exhausting for some PwAS due to their motor impairments; transcribing or annotating the recordings might require the collaboration of the participant or specialists on atypical speech, which can be expensive and time-consuming; although 10% of the population in the US reports some kind of speech disability15 and 5% consider they have “a very big problem” with their voice16, there has historically been little industry investment in technologies for atypical speech, with a few exceptions17,18.

Some corpora were created to develop or improve speech technologies for PwAS and are available for research upon request. The first example, UA-Speech19, contains recordings from 19 speakers with cerebral palsy (CP). Each speaker recorded 765 isolated words, including 300 distinct, uncommon words, computer commands, radio alphabet, digits, and other common words. The dataset includes video recordings of the speaker’s mouth and aims to study misarticulation in speakers with CP. On the other hand, the Torgo20 corpus features 8 dysarthric speakers with CP or ALS and gender- and age-matched control speakers. The speakers read texts from different sources, including texts from the TIMIT21 dataset. The corpus also includes electromagnetic articulography, which allows for the detailed analysis of the speakers’ articulatory movements. A more recent dataset is the Speech Accessibility Project (SAP) corpus, which includes 211 individuals with PD (self-reported) and transcriptions in its first release22. Each SAP participant was required to read between 350 and 400 scripted phrases and to answer prompts designed to elicit spontaneous speech. None of these datasets is suitable for modern SLU systems as they only contain simple commands, isolated words, or fragments of text that do not have annotated intent and, therefore, can be used to train or adapt ASRs but not SLU directly, which would be more beneficial for this type of population with a high prevalence of motor problems and increased dependence. An exception is the Domotica corpus23, which contains Dutch dysarthric speech commands. However, the recordings contain utterances limited to 27 automation tasks with intention annotation, and the dataset contains only 17 speakers. Consequently, and excluding SAP, none of these datasets contains speech from more than 19 PwAS.

Other datasets contain much larger amounts of participants and data, but these are not commonly available. Mulfari et al.24, collected speech recordings from 208 PwAS in Italian which they used to adapt ASRs in that language. A paramount example of proprietary datasets of atypical speech is the Euphonia corpus25. The Euphonia project is a Google initiative aimed at creating a large dataset conceived to train new speech technologies for PwAS. It contains more than 1 million utterances of read English speech from more than 1000 PwAS. However, the corpus is not publicly available.

To address these gaps, in this study we present HeyJay!, a new corpus of atypical speech recordings in English to enable research and development of machine conversation partners and speech technologies and methods for PwAS, meant to improve their lives. HeyJay! is an atypical speech corpus with speech materials optimized for the training of SLU and ASR. The current version of the corpus includes speech samples from 36 speakers with speech impairment caused by Parkinsonian and related movement disorders, ALS and different types of Ataxia, among others, under a consent form that permits distribution. Participants recorded fluent speech commands used to control a speech assistant, and the resulting recordings have associated transcriptions, intent annotations, and other metadata about the speaker, such as age, sex, or main diagnosis. The metadata also contains perceived voice quality, annotated by three expert speech and language pathologists (SLPs). The distribution of this corpus will enable researchers worldwide to advance augmentative and alternative communication for individuals with atypical speech and further research on the automatic evaluation of the speech characteristics of PwAS. The primary goals of this article are (1) to describe the dataset; (2) to detail the data collection and curation process; (3) to provide some validation experiments demonstrating that general speech technologies struggle with atypical speech contained in HeyJay!; (4) to show that models can be effectively adapted using HeyJay! to improve performance on atypical speech; (5) to show that HeyJay! can be successfully used on new data augmentation techniques.

The Johns Hopkins Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the data collection (IRB00350349) and data sharing (IRB00434789). All participants signed an informed consent that explained the project in detail and disclosed that the data would be shared. We informed the participants of the potential risks incurred by participating in this study, including breach of confidentiality and usage of the data for future unknown purposes not considered in the project description. The consent process was conducted by a research coordinator and took between 10 and 20 minutes per participant to ensure that all participants understood their involvement in the project. All participants were compensated.

Speech recording using HermeSpeech Recorder

We developed an open-source web platform, HermeSpeech Recorder26 (https://github.com/Neuro-Logical/HermeSpeechRecorder), to record participants in person and remotely. HermeSpeech Recorder is an intuitive tool created to capture speech data from large groups of participants and transfer it to a safe remote storage. It offers simple controls for participants to start, pause, stop, and repeat recordings of designated scripts or speech tasks. Recordings were encrypted and securely stored in the cloud using an Azure server, in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) standards for data privacy and protection. The platform supports asynchronous uploads, allowing for reliable use even in areas with limited internet access. We created one profile per participant and uploaded and assigned them multiple scripts to be read.

Participants were asked to record a minimum of 320 complex commands, although not all participants finished the assigned tasks. Utterances were recorded separately and sequentially and, therefore, no segmentation was required. The participants repeated the utterance recording if they made any reading mistake (word repetition, substitutions, etc) not related to their speech impairment, following the instructions we provided. As HermeSpeech Recorder allowed participants to repeat the recording if they made mistakes, the percentage of correctly recorded sentences was maximized, and the subsequent human supervision workload and transcription correction, minimized. Silence at the beginning and ending of each segment was removed using the SILERO (https://github.com/snakers4/silero-vad, package accessed on October 5 2024) voice activity detector.

The recordings took place in two different scenarios:in the clinic, supervised by a research coordinator and using the same computer equipped with a Focusrite Scarlett external soundcard and an AKG C520 headset condenser microphone.at home using the participant’s own computer and microphone. In these cases, the participants received a video with clear recording instructions followed by a short training over video-conference with a research assistant who ensured that they understood the recording requirements. All participants first recorded three test utterances that were reviewed by the researchers of this project to monitor if the recording conditions were optimal and no significant background noise or channel distortions were present. In the cases in which researchers observed recording issues, participants were guided to resolve them before starting the actual recordings.

Recorded materials

Participants were presented with various sentences in English to be read as if they were talking to a speech assistant named Jay. For that reason, all sentences started with the wake word Hey, Jay that gives the name to the corpus. The sentences overlapped in intent with those from the Fluent Speech Commands dataset (FSC)27 (https://fluent.ai/fluent-speech-commands-a-dataset-for-spoken-language-understanding-research/) for 17 recording sessions (one session per participant). The rest of the recording sessions included sentences overlapped in transcription and intent with the SLURP (short for Spoken Language Understanding Resource Package) Dataset28 (https://github.com/pswietojanski/slurp). These two benchmarks were selected as they represent basic (FSC) and challenging (SLURP) benchmarks of SLU systems. This will also allow researchers to combine HeyJay! with these two benchmarks to train larger models in future experiments.

In the portion of HeyJay! compatible with the FSC dataset (HeyJay-FSC), the intent of an utterance was labeled using three slots: action (there are 6 different actions), object (14 different objects), and location (4 possible locations). This yielded 31 different combinations of actions/objects/locations and 248 unique phrases, given that for the same intent, we can have different sentences, as in the original FSC dataset. For example, “Hey Jay, turn off the living room’s lights”, and “Hey Jay, turn off the lights in the living room” will have the same intent. On the other hand, the sentence annotation from SLURP (HeyJay-SLURP) follows a more complex hierarchical structure: (1) scenario: represents the general domain or context of the utterance (e.g., “calendar”, “weather”); (2) intent: captures the user’s specific objective within that domain (e.g., “calendar/set_event”, “weather/temperature”); (3) slots and actions: semantic slot annotations that identify key elements in the utterance such as times, places, names, or items that are essential to fulfilling the task. For instance, in the utterance “Set a reminder for 3 P M tomorrow”, the system would tag it under the “reminder” scenario, assign it the intent “reminder/set”, and recognize “3 pm October 12 2025” as a time-related slot. The SLURP dataset offers a collection of intent annotations tailored for goal-directed spoken interactions across common real-world domains, including topics like weather, music, travel, and cooking. This comprehensive annotation approach makes SLURP particularly valuable for developing and benchmarking SLU models that need to interpret both user intent and detailed semantic information.

The corpus also contains metadata, including age, sex, disease etiology, dysarthria subtype, severity of the speech impairment, and annotation of the perceived speech characteristics of each participant. Three SLPs with more than 10 years of experience blindly evaluated each participant: one of them evaluated all the participants and the other two, 23 out of the 36 participants. Each pathologist received a set of eight audio samples per speaker and evaluated the overall severity of atypical speech characteristics. Ratings ranged from 0 to 4, with 0 representing typical speech and 4 reflecting a highly impaired condition, in accordance with the Rating Scale for Deviant Speech Characteristics outlined in Chapter 3 of Duffy 201929. Such characteristics included overall dysarthria severity, overall articulatory severity, imprecise consonant articulation, prolonged phonemes, repeated phonemes, irregular articulatory breakdowns, presence of distorted vowels, overall voice quality, harsh voice, hoarse/wet voice, breathy voice, strained/strangled voice, voice stoppages, and voice flutter. Data Records

Dataset sharing

The dataset30 is shared in a restricted-access repository (DOI: 10.3886/ICPSR39448) at ICPSR to ensure compliance with the Johns Hopkins Medicine IRB and HIPAA regulations. To have access to the dataset, researchers must fill out and sign a data usage agreement which explicitly restricts the use of the data and forbids any attempt to identify individuals, repurpose, or redistribute the data. Researchers requesting access must also describe their planned research in their application with the goal of assessing an ethical use of the data. After the agreement is approved (typically, after three weeks), researchers receive access to the dataset. This type of sharing mechanism protects participants’ rights and personal information, prevents institutions from misusing the dataset, and still enables research, safeguarding the interests of both participants and researchers. Researchers can request access to the dataset at: https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/39448.

The audio is organized into one folder per recording session (i.e., per participant), each containing all corresponding audio recordings in .wav format. Folder names match the randomly assigned participantID, which consists of a six-digit numeric code. Within each folder, audio files follow the naming convention participantID_uttnumber.wav, where uttnumber is a five-digit consecutive index starting at 00000, representing the utterance number for that participant.

The dataset also includes two tabular annotation files containing the speech transcriptions and intent labels associated with each audio recording: one for the HeyJay-FSC subset and one for the HeyJay-SLURP subset. Because the intent annotations for HeyJay-SLURP follow a more complex hierarchical structure, the corresponding semantic annotations are additionally provided in a separate .json file distributed with the dataset.

Finally, a tabular metadata file is provided at the participant level. This file includes demographic and clinical information, such as age, sex, diagnosis, and dysarthria subtype, as well as all perceptual annotations assigned by the SLPs. Because three SLPs annotated the data independently, individual ratings are reported in separate columns, identified by an SLP_ID suffix at the end of the variable name. For example, the column voice:flutter_3 corresponds to the perceived severity of voice flutter assigned by SLP 3.

Potential usage

Due to the extensive annotations included in HeyJay!, the corpus has the potential to be used in new research experiments and multiple applications to improve automatic atypical speech recognition and SLU, diagnosis support tools of neurological disorders through speech, or automatic measurement of speech impairment severity. It can also be used to research traditional speech technologies and downstream tasks, which are underexplored in atypical speech scenarios, such as wake word detection, forced alignment, voice activity detection, speaker identification, sex identification, and age classification. In all these scenarios, HeyJay! can be used to:benchmark performance of SLU and ASR systems on atypical speech.fine-tune models already pre-trained with much larger datasets of typical speech.be combined with already existing atypical speech datasets such as UA-Speech, Torgo or SAP.

Technical Validation

Data curation and supervision

The transcription of each recording was supervised at least once by one of our two human supervisors, who manually corrected any of them when necessary. The supervisors were instructed to ensure the transcription reflected the recorded speech verbatim, capturing every word, repetition (e.g., Hey hey Jay), stutter (e.g., He hey Jay), and filler words (e.g., uh, um). No extra text or metadata was added to the transcriptions to indicate the presence of events. Any abbreviations (e.g., am) were transcribed as individual capital letters separated with a space (e.g., A M). In the same manner, numbers were transcribed as words (e.g., two thousand). In the rare cases in which, after correcting the transcription, the intent of the sentence changed, the recording was excluded from the corpus. We did not perform phonetic transcription of the speakers’ pronunciation. For instance, if a participant replaced one phoneme of a word due to their speech impairment, the transcription reflected the original word the speaker was trying to pronounce, which was included in the reference sentence we asked the participant to read.

After all recordings were supervised at least once, 360 utterances were randomly selected and supervised again as a way to gauge the final quality of the annotations. Less than 4% of these needed some type of re-adjustment. From those, 75% of the times this adjustment involved small transcription corrections. Without correcting such transcription errors, we estimate that the inherent word error rate (WER) of the dataset is estimated at 0.51%. This WER is attributable to the speakers’ reading errors and the human supervision errors, and is comparable to other speech datasets such as Librispeech.

Although all recordings were based on predefined scripts that did not include any personal or identifying information, we conducted a manual sanitization of each recording to ensure there was no private personal information such as names, addresses, etc. on the recordings (none were found). We also removed any spontaneous utterances not included in the original scripts that might pose a re-identification risk.

Spoken Language Understanding experiments

To validate the usefulness of HeyJay! to develop atypical SLU, we used the data to train end-to-end atypical SLU models with and without data augmentation, following a preliminary work34. The goal of the data augmentation techniques was to increase the number of hours per speaker to train SLU models. The data augmentation techniques employed included SpecAugment35, WaveAugment36, and text-to-speech Atyp-TTS34. Overall, Atyp-TTS employs the same encoder and duration prediction concept as Grad-TTS37 to encode an atypical mel-spectrogram and atypical phoneme duration for a certain atypical speaker, respectively. However, in contrast to Grad-TTS, the diffusion model used as decoder is trained using two specially formulated speaker and atypical losses. The goal of these losses is to transfer the learning from an atypical voice conversion model, DuTA-VC38 trained in a previous work and successfully used for data augmentation. All the Atyp-TTS details are included in Wang et al.34.

To validate HeyJay!’s utility on atypical SLU applications, recordings from the original FSC and SLURP datasets were employed in this study to train the SLU models, along with HeyJay! and synthesized atypical speech. Specifically, we employed 17 atypical speakers from HeyJay! who read sentences with the same intent as the FSC27 (HeyJay-FSC) and 19 atypical speakers reading sentences extracted from the SLURP dataset28 (HeyJay-SLURP).

Limitations

This dataset is the first one to contain atypical speech from a variety of conditions, with intent annotations, and be publicly available. While meaningful, 8,669 utterances may be relatively small for training certain deep learning models, and the dataset will have to be used to adapt pre-trained models, or in combination with other datasets22, or synthetic data34. Moreover, this current version does not include some forms of atypical speech, such as developmental or stuttering, which may restrict applicability to the broader atypical speech population. In the same sense, although the dataset includes several neurological disorders, in some cases, the number of participants for a given disorder may not capture the full range of atypical speech manifestations associated with the disease. Our goal was to provide a variety of articulatory and atypical speech forms and HeyJay! fulfills that requirement. However, HeyJay! is an ongoing project, and new versions of the dataset will continue to increase the number of utterances, participants, etiologies, and atypical speech types.

A healthy cohort was not recorded to focus all our efforts and budget on atypical speech, which is scarcer, and because datasets such as FSC and SLURP can serve as control datasets with similar intent annotations in SLU scenarios, whereas other general-use datasets can serve as controls in ASR-related scenarios.

Whereas reading complex commands including a wake word can be very useful to develop and adapt modern SLU systems, spontaneous speech and dialogues in future iterations would enhance the value of HeyJay!, as it would better capture emotion changes and how PwAS actually communicate in daily life. Incorporating spontaneous speech would further enhance the dataset, however, this presents substantial challenges. Accurately transcribing spontaneous atypical speech typically requires involvement from the speakers themselves or a familiar caregiver, as automatic or external transcription often leads to significant errors due to intelligibility issues. Moreover, given that many of our participants have neurodegenerative conditions such as ALS or PD, the demands of both recording and annotating spontaneous speech can be physically exhausting, potentially limiting the duration and quantity of data that can be reasonably collected. Thus, while we are actively working toward expanding the dataset with spontaneous content, the scripted nature of the current version is a practical and ethically informed compromise, enabling us to maximize both data quality and participant comfort. In the same manner, future iterations should include phonetic and prosodic annotations in addition to transcription and intent, to facilitate research on speech production and also speech technologies, such as forced alignment.

In spite of these limitations, HeyJay! fills a crucial gap in the research landscape and supports the development of fairer speech technologies.
