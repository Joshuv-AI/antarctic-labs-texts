Understanding gestational diabetes in Hong Kong: women’s needs, self-management challenges, and the potential of digital health solutions in culturally sensitive care
Authors: Bronya Hi Kwan Luk, Polly Haixia Ma, William Wing Kuen Lam, Agnes Yuen Kwan Lai, Rosenna Wai Ming Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance first recognised during pregnancy, poses serious health risks to mothers and newborns. The alarmingly high GDM prevalence in Hong Kong (25.9%), which is double the global average, necessitates urgent examination of context-specific management challenges. This qualitative study explored the experiences of Hong Kong Chinese women with GDM, investigating their perceived needs, self-management barriers, and recommendations for culturally appropriate healthcare interventions.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 16 women diagnosed with GDM across Hong Kong. Participants included both pregnant women and those within 12 months postpartum, all of whom were managing their condition in a community setting. The interview guide was developed using the Health Belief Model (HBM) as a framework. Thematic analysis was then used inductively to identify key patterns in participants’ self-management experiences and healthcare needs related to GDM.ResultsAnalysis confirmed the relevance of all core HBM constructs (perceived susceptibility/severity, barriers, benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy), revealing their unique manifestations in this context. Key barriers included cultural conflicts in diet, time and fatigue limiting exercise, and insufficient healthcare support. Motivators included protection of the baby’s health and cues from healthcare providers and peers. Women expressed a strong preference for culturally tailored digital health tools to bridge these gaps.ConclusionThere is an urgent need for integrated, family-centered digital health solutions that offer culturally adapted dietary advice, trimester-specific guidance, and embedded psychological support. Hong Kong’s sociocultural context provides an ideal setting for developing such interventions, with lessons applicable to other populations navigating tensions between tradition and modern healthcare.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Women’s experiences, Self-management, Health belief model, Digital health, Qualitative research, Culturally adapted interventions.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance recognised during pregnancy [1, 2], poses considerable health risks to both mothers and their newborns. For mothers, GDM increases the likelihood of complications such as hypoglycaemia, pregnancy-induced hypertension, preeclampsia, and caesarean delivery [3–5]. For newborns, it increases the risks of macrosomia, birth injuries, respiratory issues, and long-term metabolic disorders such as childhood obesity and early-onset type 2 diabetes [2, 6]. The prevalence of GDM varies globally, affecting 5.2%–13.7% of pregnant women in the United States and Canada [7], 7.2%–8.4% in Europe [8]. In contrast, Asian populations experience a disproportionately higher burden, with prevalence rates consistently ranking among the highest globally, driven by genetic susceptibility and rapid dietary transitions [9, 10]. Reported rates in Asia are notably high, for example, reaching 14.8% in China [11], and as high as 25.9% in Hong Kong [12]. This statistic highlights that women in Hong Kong are at a particularly high risk of GDM, underscoring the need for targeted interventions.

To mitigate the high risk of GDM, self-management through lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes, physical activity, and blood glucose monitoring, is widely recommended [1, 13]. Self-management, defined as an individual’s ability to manage symptoms, treatments, and lifestyle changes associated with a chronic condition [14, 15], is crucial for effective GDM control. However, adopting and maintaining these lifestyles changes can be challenging for women, particularly in the context of pregnancy.

GDM is a serious health challenge during pregnancy that requires rigorous self-management to mitigate the risks to both the mother and child. While structured interventions and clinical guidelines for self-management exist, a significant body of evidence indicates that pregnant women often struggle to adhere to them [16, 17]. Current research has predominantly focused on the efficacy of these interventions in achieving clinical outcomes, overlooking the reasons for this gap in adherence—namely, the profound psychosocial and practical burdens that hinder successful uptake. Studies have shown that women diagnosed with GDM frequently experience profound emotional distress, including feelings of frustration, helplessness, and even abandonment, both during pregnancy and following delivery [18–21]. The sudden imposition of stringent self-management demands compounds these psychological struggles. For instance, a GDM diagnosis dramatically increases women’s caregiving responsibilities as they must adhere to strict dietary modifications, monitor blood glucose levels, and adjust lifestyle habits, all while navigating the physical and emotional challenges of pregnancy. A previous study also highlighted that these dietary changes often create financial strain, as healthier food options may be cost-prohibitive, and cultural conflicts, as traditional eating practices may no longer be feasible [21].

Women also reported lacking support from their family and loved ones in self-managing their GDM [22]. Many women feel isolated when family members or partners fail to understand the condition’s demands, leading to frustration and disengagement from care [23, 24]. Compounding this issue, healthcare systems often provide inconsistent or insufficient guidance [25–27]. Lawrence et al. [28] noted that women frequently feel confused when seeking clarification from medical professionals, as they receive vague or contradictory advice. Additionally, they encounter substantial barriers to effective self-management, including insufficient personalised care, limited autonomy in decision-making, inadequate follow-up support, and a lack of tailored dietary guidance [18–20, 28]. The cumulative effect of these challenges underscores a critical gap in contemporary GDM care: while clinical guidelines emphasise the importance of glycaemic control, they often overlook women’s holistic needs, including their emotional well-being, socioeconomic constraints, and cultural contexts.

The strikingly high prevalence of GDM in Hong Kong (25.9%) [10], which is approximately double the global average, underscores the urgency to address the identified research gap. Hong Kong’s unique sociocultural milieu, where deeply rooted traditions interact with globalised modern influences, generates distinct lifestyle patterns that considerably affect health behaviours. As the population navigates this complex interplay between conventional practices and contemporary demands, these sociocultural dynamics present challenges and opportunities for GDM management. Therefore, this qualitative study sought to understand the experiences of women with GDM within this unique sociocultural context. It was guided by the following research questions:

What are the perceived needs and self-management barriers experienced by women with GDM in Hong Kong?

Methods

Study design

This study used a qualitative descriptive design to explore the experiences of women diagnosed with GDM. The methodology was selected to elicit rich, detailed account about self-management behaviours, barriers, and motivators, capturing nuanced real-world challenges often overlooked in clinical assessments. Data collection persisted until thematic saturation was achieved, which was defined as two consecutive interviews without the emergence of new substantive themes.

Participants

Participants were recruited using convenience and snowball sampling methods from multiple maternal healthcare clinics in Hong Kong. A research assistant (RA) identified and invited potential participants. Inclusion criteria were: (a) women aged 18–45 years; (b) either currently pregnant with GDM or had a history of GDM in a recent pregnancy (within 12 months postpartum); (c) diagnosis of GDM confirmed via a standard 75 g oral glucose tolerance test; and (d) Chinese and fluent in either Cantonese or Mandarin to ensure linguistic and cultural homogeneity within the sample, which is essential for capturing nuanced experiences and ensuring data accuracy in a qualitative study. Exclusion criteria were: (a) inability to provide informed consent and (b) having a pre-existing diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (Type 1 or Type 2) prior to pregnancy. Postpartum women (up to 12 months) were included to capture a complete narrative of the self-management journey, including the transition to postpartum care. Their perspective also allowed for richer reflection on challenges and motivators, free from the immediate stress of active pregnancy.

Interviews were conducted in a setting chosen by the participant (e.g., a private clinic room or their home) in their preferred language, either Cantonese or Mandarin, by a native-speaking researcher. All settings were ensured to be private and quiet to facilitate a confidential and in-depth conversation.

Theoretical framework and interview guide development

This study adopted the health belief model (HBM) [29] as its theoretical framework due to its proven utility for examining the psychological and behavioural determinants of health-related decision-making. The HBM informed the development of the semi-structured interview guide (Table 1) to ensure comprehensive coverage of key constructs (e.g., perceived susceptibility, barriers, benefits, and self-efficacy). However, to prioritize the emergence of participants’ own perspectives and unexpected themes, we employed an inductive reflexive thematic analysis approach, as outlined by Braun and Clarke [30, 31], rather than a deductive framework analysis. This approach allowed us to explore the data openly without being constrained by the pre-existing HBM categories, while acknowledging that the HBM provided a valuable sensitizing framework for data collection.

The interview guide was developed with input from a multidisciplinary team of researchers in nursing, midwifery, and public health. The questions focused on participants’ perinatal experiences, perceptions, and challenges in GDM self-management.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee (Reference: HE-RD/2023/2.13). Before obtaining informed consent, all the participants were provided with written study information detailing the research objectives, procedures, and their rights. The consent process explicitly guaranteed the participants’ rights to voluntary participation, confidentiality protection, and unrestricted withdrawal without affecting their clinical care. Specific permission was obtained for audio recording, and the participants were assured of secure storage and anonymisation during transcription. The participants retained the right to modify or withdraw their data until formal analysis began.

Data analyses

Thematic analysis was conducted following the framework outlined by Braun and Clarke [30]. The process began with data familiarization. Two researchers then independently coded an initial batch of transcripts, meeting to compare codes and develop a consensus-based codebook. This codebook was applied iteratively to subsequent data batches. Through discussion and constant comparison, codes were collated into potential themes, which were refined and defined to ensure they accurately represented the dataset. Thematic saturation was determined as no new themes arising from two consecutive interviews, which was achieved after the 16th interview.

Thematic saturation was defined as the point where two consecutive interviews yielded no new themes or subthemes. This was achieved through an iterative process of collecting and analyzing data in batches. After an initial batch (n = 5) was coded, subsequent batches (n = 5–6) were collected and analyzed until the final interview (n = 16) confirmed no new themes emerged.

Trustworthiness of the study

The trustworthiness of this study was ensured through concrete strategies aligned with established qualitative criteria [32]. Credibility was strengthened via peer debriefing, where the research team regularly challenged coding decisions and interpretations, and member checking, where a subset of participants (n = 5) confirmed the accuracy of preliminary findings. Dependability was achieved by maintaining a detailed audit trail documenting all analytical decisions and changes to the codebook. Confirmability was established through reflexivity, wherein researchers documented their preconceptions in a journal to mitigate bias. Finally, transferability was supported by providing thick, descriptive data—including detailed participant demographics and verbatim quotes—to allow readers to assess the relevance of findings to other contexts.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 16 participants were recruited from April to May 2024. Their characteristics are summarised in Table 2. The participants’ ages ranged from 29 to 41 years. One quarter of the participants were currently pregnant, whereas the remaining 75% were in the postnatal period. Most of the participants (75%) had attained tertiary education, and approximately two thirds (62.5%) were employed. However, only one third of the participants (37.5%) engaged in regular exercise. The participants’ body mass indexes ranged from 18.0 to 29.4 kg/m2.

Theme 1: perceived susceptibility & severity

The diagnosis of GDM fundamentally shifted participants’ perception of their pregnancy, triggering an immediate assessment of personal risk and the potential severity of outcomes for themselves and their infants. This theme captures the potent emotional and cognitive response to the diagnosis, which served as a critical initial motivator for engaging in self-management.

Shock & fear at diagnosis

The initial reaction to the GDM diagnosis was, for most, one of profound shock and fear. The diagnosis was a significant psychological event that triggered distress, particularly among participants who perceived themselves as having a low risk due to a healthy lifestyle. This unexpected diagnosis sharply amplified existing pregnancy anxieties. For many, this initial fear was not paralyzing but instead became a catalyst for seeking information and, ultimately, for behavioural change.

I was shocked and deeply distressed… So afraid. I never imagined I’d develop GDM as I’d always maintained such careful eating habits. (P3)

I was horrified and worried about my health and the baby. That fear is why I started follow the diet earlier. (P4)

Understanding tangible risks

Beyond the initial emotional shock, participants engaged in a process of rationalizing specific, tangible risks associated with GDM. Their awareness of these potential complications was a crucial motivator for rigorous self-management, reinforcing adherence to dietary modifications and glucose monitoring protocols. Most of the participants understood the primary consequences for both the mother and baby. They recognised that GDM could lead to excessive glucose transfer to the foetus, resulting in macrosomia (increased birth weight), which increases the risk of obstructed labour and related complications. Additionally, they acknowledged risks like neonatal hypoglycaemia. For themselves, they understood that GDM significantly elevates the risk of developing diabetes later in life.

It will cause babies to become bigger, making delivery more difficult. (P14)

For adults… maybe when you get older, the chance of developing diabetes is higher. (P8)

The participants expressed mixed feelings as they acknowledged the potential complications of GDM for their babies’ health and the possibility of developing permanent diabetes themselves.

I was horrified and worried about my health and the baby. Honestly, I was half-prepared – being of advanced maternal age… Plus, both of my parents have diabetes. (P4)

Theme 2: perceived barriers

The theme of Perceived Barriers encompassed the significant practical, social, and systemic challenges that complicated daily self-management.

Dietary adherence challenges

Dietary modification presented some of the most pervasive barriers, which were often deeply rooted in cultural and emotional contexts.

Sub-theme: cultural & familial conflicts

Participants faced significant conflicts between traditional Chinese dietary practices and clinical GDM management guidelines. A primary example was the consumption of tonic soups and broths (e.g., chicken, fish maw, bone broth), which are culturally believed to strengthen the mother and baby. However, these are often high in fat and calories, directly contradicting dietary advice for managing gestational weight gain and blood glucose [33, 34]. This tension extended to staple foods, where participants met family opposition when trying to replace white rice with healthier alternatives like mixed-grain rice.

At first, my family became an obstacle – they insisted I shouldn’t drink sweet soups but must consume tonics, fish maw, fruits, and such. I used to eat casually, just having whatever the elders gave me… They constantly brought me these soups. (P10)

I switched to mixed-grain rice later on – it completely changed my eating habits. My family was opposed at first, of course. They were worried it would affect the baby…. (P10)

These conflicts were a source of considerable stress, especially for women living with older relatives who persisted in preparing traditional foods deemed unsuitable for GDM management.

[The elders I live with] never remembered the dietitian’s warning about bone broth. They kept cooking it… If I refused to drink it, they’d get upset. (P4)

Sub-theme: emotional & psychological toll

Beyond social conflict, the dietary restrictions themselves exacted a heavy emotional toll. Participants reported considerable difficulties, citing persistent hunger and the burden of managing restricted diets. The rigidity of the requirements made meal planning feel joyless and demoralising.

I’m always hungry but with limited ingredients… You just eat whatever’s at home. Buying and cooking proper meals takes time and energy, so I often give up. (P3)

The rigidity of GDM dietary requirements also exacted an emotional toll. The participants described meal planning as joyless and demoralising, particularly when glucose levels remained unstable despite strict adherence. This frustration led some to disregard nutritional guidelines entirely.

Sometimes I lose my appetite completely. Eating bland food constantly makes life feel joyless… I’ve stopped following the nutritionist’s advice because the diet is too restrictive. (P8)

Exercise adherence challenges

Beyond diet, participants encountered significant barriers to maintaining physical activity.

Sub-theme: social obligations

Nearly all participants emphasised time poverty due to childcare responsibilities, which severely constrained their ability to exercise.

Having to care for my older daughter left me no time for exercise. (P1)

Sub-theme: physical fatigue

The participants described considerable difficulties in maintaining physical activity regimens due to pregnancy-related exhaustion, particularly during the third trimester. Common barriers included profound fatigue, musculoskeletal discomfort, and mobility challenges associated with increased gestational weight.

Not many [exercises] – too tired, [my] belly [is] too big, and foot pain. So, I did less. (P3)

Systemic & informational barriers

In addition to personal and social challenges, systemic gaps in healthcare support constituted a significant barrier.

Sub-theme: inadequate guidance from HCPs

Many participants reported receiving health education materials, such as informational brochures, distributed by government hospitals through health care professionals (HCPs). However, many raised concerns regarding the lack of practical details in these resources. Importantly, the participants highlighted that the brochures often lacked comprehensive guidance, particularly concerning standardised portion measurements and adaptable dietary recommendations. This absence of precise, actionable information rendered the materials impractical for daily implementation in real-world contexts.

[The government booklet] explained how many apple slices or orange segments count as one portion but never specified quantities for foods like 10 blueberries… I stopped opening that book completely. The food options were limited, and those measurement units made no sense. (P6)

Sub-theme: psychological support gaps

The participants overwhelmingly called for government healthcare professionals to integrate structured psychological and emotional support into GDM care protocols. Specifically, they highlighted the need for evidence-based therapeutic counselling, sustained motivational engagement, and empathetic emotional reassurance to address the psychosocial challenges associated with GDM self-management. Empirical evidence suggests that such professionally administered support systems could substantially alleviate the anxiety and psychological distress commonly experienced by individuals trying to adhere to stringent GDM self-care regimens.

What I needed most was psychological support… I felt completely abandoned emotionally. I was terrified – constantly hungry, yet no one told me my baby was too small. I had no idea what to do, with no one to talk to. (P2)

Theme 3: perceived benefits

While navigating significant barriers, participants also clearly identified the positive outcomes of GDM self-management. The theme of Perceived Benefits captures these motivating factors, which reinforced their commitment to dietary and lifestyle changes.

Benefits for the infant

The primary motivation for many women was the profound desire to protect their baby’s health. Participants recognized that strict self-management directly reduced the risk of serious neonatal complications, such as macrosomia and hypoglycaemia, thereby ensuring a safer delivery and healthier start for their infant.

Following the diet and exercise regime reduces risks, resulting in fewer neonatal complications such as jaundice or hypoglycaemia post-delivery. (P3)

Benefits for the mother

In addition to infant-focused benefits, participants experienced significant personal health gains. For mothers, some of the participants highlighted that GDM self-management offers several benefits, including a healthier diet, helping the mother to achieve better weight control, and adopting a healthier lifestyle.

All my meals now have much less salt and sugar. Surprisingly, my weight didn’t increase – it dropped. I genuinely feel healthier. (P7)

Collectively, these perceived benefits for both the infant and the mother served as powerful positive reinforcements, aligning with the HBM’s proposition that the perceived value of an action is a key driver of health behaviour.

Theme 4: cues to action

This theme encompasses the critical triggers, both external and internal, that prompted participants to initiate or intensify their self-management behaviours. These cues moved them from understanding the risks to taking concrete action.

Vicarious learning

Witnessing the real-life consequences of GDM in their social circles served as a powerful, emotional trigger for action. Participants who knew someone who experienced complications, such as a friend whose baby was hospitalized, reported a heightened sense of urgency and a more vigilant approach to their own management.

After my friend’s baby was hospitalised due to her gestational diabetes and preeclampsia, I was terrified – so I strictly controlled my blood sugar. (P11)

HCP influence

Health care professionals acted as the most direct and authoritative source of cues to action. Their formal diagnosis, explanations of risks, and even punitive feedback were consistently cited as moments that immediately changed behaviour. Participants viewed HCPs as legitimate authorities whose directives demanded compliance.

Usually, when my glucose levels ran high, and the doctor scolded me, I tightened my control for a while. (P6)

The doctor told me, “You have gestational diabetes,” so I did… When I heard the consequences were serious, I followed the guidelines accordingly. (P13)

The role of digital tools

Beyond interpersonal interactions, digital resources and desired technological solutions emerged as a distinct category of modern cues. Participants actively used or expressed a need for digital tools to facilitate their self-management, highlighting a gap in current care that technology could fill.

Sub-theme: online knowledge acquisition

Faced with information gaps, participants proactively turned to online platforms like YouTube and Xiaohongshu to independently seek out dietary advice and practical management tips.

I searched online for GDM diets and watched health programmes where doctors explained what to eat and how to control GDM. (P9)

Sub-theme: desire for timely professional support

The desire for on-demand, asynchronous communication with professionals was a key finding. Participants envisioned a system where they could receive immediate answers to practical questions, reducing anxiety and improving adherence.

If I could message a professional anytime – like ‘How much of this can I eat?’ – it would make diet control so much easier. (P6)

Sub-theme: activity monitoring as a motivator

For exercise adherence, participants identified self-monitoring technology as a potential motivator. The idea of tracking progress and receiving feedback through an app was seen as a way to combat inertia and lack of motivation.

An app that records daily walks – like how long or far you go after meals –would motivate me. (P16)

Theme 5: self-efficacy

Self-efficacy emerged not as a fixed trait, but as a dynamic and fluid state that women continuously negotiated throughout their GDM journey. This theme captures the constant tension between feeling in control and feeling helpless, a tension influenced by daily experiences.

Fluctuating confidence in GDM management

Participants’ confidence in managing GDM was characterized by significant fluctuation, best described as a ‘half-half’ experience. This psychological state was constantly reshaped by daily events: confidence was bolstered by mastery experiences (e.g., achieving stable glucose levels) and positive feedback, yet it was readily eroded by unpredictable physiological responses (e.g., a high reading despite adherence) and the psychological fatigue of constant self-regulation. This finding underscores that self-efficacy is a dynamic process, not a binary state.

I’m half confident, half not. I know what to do, and I can do it most of the time. But one bad reading can make me question everything. (P4)

This understanding suggests that effective support interventions should aim not to instill permanent confidence, but to provide tools that help women navigate these inevitable fluctuations and build resilience during periods of doubt.

Discussion

This study applied the Health Belief Model (HBM) to explore the self-management experiences of Hong Kong Chinese women with GDM. A central finding was that the unique sociocultural context of Hong Kong fundamentally shapes the manifestation of core HBM constructs. While our analysis confirmed the established relevance of perceived susceptibility, severity, barriers, benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy, it uniquely revealed how local factors like cultural dietary conflicts and filial piety pressures specifically moulded these perceptions.

Participants faced multifaceted barriers that were deeply embedded in their environment. These included conflicts between dietary restrictions and cultural food practices, physical activity limitations exacerbated by time constraints and third-trimester fatigue, insufficient guidance from healthcare providers, and a notable lack of psychological support. Conversely, their engagement in self-management was driven by perceived susceptibility to complications, an understanding of GDM severity, appreciation of the benefits, and cues to action from healthcare providers and peer experiences. Notably, digital tools emerged as a modern and crucial enabler, providing real-time information and support that is often absent in traditional care settings.

Barriers to GDM self-management

A key finding from our participants was the experience of multifaceted barriers deeply embedded in their local environment. They encountered challenges and cultural conflicts in modifying their diet and exercise. These findings align with previous studies highlighting similar barriers, such as difficulties with diet control, persistent hunger, and frustration in following food advice guidelines [35–37]. In one study, women reported that pregnancy became a physical hindrance to performing exercise [23]. During the third trimester, they reported that fatigue, dizziness, swollen legs, and leg cramps made regular exercise challenging [38]. Additionally, their expanding abdominal size further hindered their ability to stay active.

Our findings highlight that a significant barrier arose from the conflict between clinical guidelines and established local cultural norms. Hong Kong Chinese family dietary practices are deeply rooted in southern Chinese food therapy traditions, which place a high value on long-boiled soups (e.g., chicken, fish maw, and herbal broths) as sources of vital essence (精髓) believed to strengthen maternal–foetal health (補身) and replenish qi and blood (補氣血), alongside cultural preferences for carbohydrate-rich staple foods [39]. These traditions and cultural preferences for carbohydrate-rich staple foods often conflict with evidence-based GDM management recommendations [23]. From a clinical perspective, these soups can be high in hidden fats and calories, while the emphasis on refined carbohydrates like white rice contributes to high glycaemic loads, both of which directly challenge the core goals of GDM management: glycaemic control and appropriate gestational weight gain [40]. This cultural conflict creates considerable challenges for daily self-care, as Chinese women with GDM struggle to reconcile medical advice with strong intergenerational expectations and filial obligations. These women often face a dilemma between prioritising their health needs and maintaining family harmony, which compounds the psychological burden of GDM management [41].

These findings highlight an urgent need for culturally sensitive dietary guidelines that incorporate a broader spectrum of Chinese foods [42] and educate both women with GDM and their families, particularly older members who often influence household eating patterns. Evidence from a review and meta-analysis of 23 randomised controlled trials demonstrates that family-based interventions can improve family support, diabetes control, and psychological well-being in adults with diabetes, particularly in Asian regions [43]. Family-based interventions for women with GDM have also been implemented in Western countries [44], although further refinement is needed to optimise outcomes [45]. These interventions could serve as a reference for local healthcare providers to involve families in designing effective strategies to support women with GDM.

Our results identified a critical gap in structured psychological support, which participants reported as a major barrier. Women expressed a strong need for real-time, personalised feedback to manage the emotional toll of GDM. This finding underscores the importance of integrating psychosocial care, which is supported by existing evidence. Pregnancy is a short but important period requiring women to adopt new health behaviours rapidly [46]. Adapting to dietary and exercise changes during this time is inherently stressful as the outcomes directly affect both the mother and unborn child, even with diligent adherence. The absence of structured psychological support in clinical settings was particularly notable.

These findings reveal the importance of integrating comprehensive psychological care into standard GDM management protocols. Psychosocial interventions have been shown to positively influence self-management behaviours, lifestyle changes, depression, anxiety, self-efficacy, stress, and glucose levels in women with GDM [47, 48]. Informational and motivational support interventions provide tailored advice and encouragement and can significantly enhance self-management [47, 48]. Additionally, educational interventions that improve women’s knowledge of and motivation in GDM self-management are particularly effective, empowering them to make informed decisions and adopt healthier behaviours [49]. Thus, implementing the type of support requested by our participants is an evidence-based strategy.

Health beliefs and cues to action as motivators

Women in this study reported profound shock upon receiving a GDM diagnosis, particularly those who perceived themselves as having a low risk due to healthy lifestyles. Consistent with prior research [46, 50], the GDM diagnosis prompted them to reassess their health status and recognise their new high-risk condition. This urgent threat appraisal triggered immediate concerns about their health and pregnancy outcomes. The diagnostic experience [51] served dual roles: first, as a susceptibility marker, it helped women realise that metabolic complications could develop despite healthy behaviours, and second, as a behavioural catalyst, it motivated them to engage in intensive self-education about GDM management. These initial emotional responses were often followed by heightened concerns about pregnancy outcomes, which further encouraged the participants to seek GDM-related knowledge and adopt self-management strategies actively.

Notably, women’s perceived benefits of GDM self-management (reducing the risk of medical complications for both the mother and baby) further sustained their efforts. External triggers such as healthcare providers emphasising outcome-linked benefits or peers sharing success stories, a nuance that extends traditional HBM frameworks by highlighting sociocultural influences, often reinforced these beliefs. Notably, although the diagnostic experience initially heightened susceptibility perceptions [51], ongoing adherence relied on women’s ability to reframe GDM as a controllable condition with tangible rewards, aligning with the findings of Karavasileiadou et al. [21] regarding benefit-focused motivation. The perceived severity of GDM, such as the risks of macrosomia, emergency caesarean session, and long-term complications of developing type 2 diabetes in later life, intensified behavioural change urgency, consistent with the findings of previous studies [25, 52].

This study identified two key behavioural cues for women with GDM: professional medical guidance and peer support through digital platforms. The participants valued healthcare professionals’ evidence-based recommendations while relying on peer networks for culturally adapted implementation strategies, consistent with findings from systematic reviews of online support communities for GDM management [53]. These digital platforms proved particularly effective when delivering culturally tailored solutions, such as peer-shared recipe modifications or provider-customised advice that translated clinical guidelines into practical actions. The synergy between professional authority and peer-experiential knowledge in digital environments promoted sustainable self-management practices. However, maintaining motivation requires continuous support, which current healthcare systems often struggle to provide due to resource constraints. Women strongly desire the increased involvement of healthcare professionals in digital health tools to improve information quality and patient care [54]. Increased engagement in digital health platforms and strategic use of social media for health education could significantly enhance knowledge-sharing and support for women managing GDM.

Digital tools: bridging gaps in GDM care

Digital health technologies—from glucose-tracking mobile applications to AI-driven coaching platforms—are redefining gestational diabetes self-management [55]. Designed for the transient yet intensive demands of GDM, these tools provide real-time, personalized support that complements traditional care [56]. Beyond traditional HBM constructs, digital tools have emerged as novel enablers, addressing real-time needs unmet by routine care. The findings of this study underscore the need for digital health solutions for women with GDM. Evidence suggests that digital platforms can deliver self-management training as effectively as conventional face-to-face interventions [57]. These tools offer several advantages, including real-time support and feedback, overcoming geographic barriers, and reducing healthcare costs through resource pooling [56]. Mobile applications are well-suited for women with GDM as they address clinicians’ limited time to manage and educate patients requiring short-term therapy adjustments [54, 58, 59]. Educational resources can be accessed anytime and anywhere, facilitating efficient utilisation of healthcare resources [54, 58, 59]. Women reportedly prefer mHealth apps (mobile applications) featuring clinician-approved recipes, comprehensive food databases, and glycaemic index data to simplify dietary choices [60].

Digital platforms also enhance healthcare professionals’ ability to deliver personalised care. An integrative systematic review of GDM self-management found that digital tools facilitate self-monitoring through data tracking and feedback, provide tailored information, support behavioural changes through customised coaching features, and expand access to information and peer support [61]. Moderate-to-high-certainty evidence indicates that digital health interventions improve glycaemic control and reduce caesarean delivery rates among pregnant women with GDM, thus positively affecting maternal and foetal outcomes [55]. These findings underscore the need for contextually appropriate digital tools tailored to local GDM management. Such solutions could simultaneously support women and educate family members, mitigating cultural conflicts surrounding dietary practices.

Digital tools are not merely adjuncts but also essential enablers of sustainable GDM care [55, 62]. By integrating these technologies, healthcare providers can transform barriers into opportunities, delivering personalised, culturally sensitive, and continuous support that aligns with women’s experiences. Adopting digital health solutions represents a feasible and impactful approach to improving care delivery and clinical outcomes [54, 58]. This transformation is particularly relevant given the growing demand for healthcare services and the widespread availability of mobile technology and data coverage globally.

Contextualising the findings: why Hong Kong?

This study’s findings offer potential explanations for the notably high prevalence of GDM in Hong Kong, moving beyond purely physiological factors to consider socio-cultural and structural determinants. The intense cultural conflict surrounding traditional Cantonese “food therapy” (食療) [41] and carbohydrate-rich diets creates a unique and significant barrier to dietary adherence that may be more pronounced than in Western contexts [39]. Furthermore, the familial pressure and obligation to conform to elder expectations, rooted in filial piety (孝道), adds a layer of psychological distress that can undermine self-management efforts. The reported gaps in tailored, culturally competent guidance from healthcare providers mean women are often left to navigate these complex conflicts alone. When combined with the city’s fast-paced lifestyle, which exacerbates time constraints and physical fatigue, these factors converge to create a socio-ecological risk environment that exacerbates disease prevalence and complicates effective management. Therefore, the high rates of GDM in Hong Kong may be attributable not only to genetic predisposition but also to this specific socio-cultural environment that simultaneously heightens risk and complicates management. Consequently, effective interventions must be as much about cultural translation and family engagement as they are about clinical science.

Implications for practice

This study calls for the development of integrated, family-centred digital health solutions to support GDM self-management. Digital platforms should be designed to deliver culturally adapted dietary guidance, trimester-specific physical activity support, and embedded psychological care, actively involving family members as partners in the process. These platforms would function best within a dual-support framework that combines automated tracking with personalised communication from healthcare providers.

To implement this effectively, healthcare providers require training in remote monitoring, motivational messaging, and cultural competency for virtual consultations. Concurrently, healthcare institutions must invest in robust IT infrastructure that leverages AI-assisted data analysis while ensuring strict data privacy [63]. This dual focus on workforce capacity and technological infrastructure is essential for scaling these interventions successfully.

Recommendations for future research

To translate these findings into practice, future research should prioritize implementation science approaches to develop and pilot the family-centered digital solutions proposed in this study. Key foci should include evaluating their impact on clinical outcomes (e.g., glycaemic control), user experience, and family engagement metrics. Furthermore, research is needed to explore the longitudinal impact of GDM on maternal mental health beyond the postpartum period. The evidence generated from such studies is crucial for informing sustainable, evidence-based policy adjustments to maternal care programs.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The sample included 16 Chinese women, achieving saturation but excluding ethnic minorities in Hong Kong and potentially introducing selection bias, limiting generalizability and perspective diversity. Future studies should use purposive sampling for greater diversity in ethnicity, age, socioeconomic status, and GDM severity. Furthermore, the analysis did not distinguish between perspectives of women currently managing GDM and those reflecting postpartum; potential recall bias from this hindsight is a consideration. Future longitudinal research is needed to track evolving health beliefs and self-efficacy from diagnosis through postpartum.

Additionally, reliance on self-reported data may not fully capture GDM management efficacy; reviewing clinical record books could offer more objective insights. Finally, the exclusive focus on Hong Kong Chinese women may limit geographic applicability. Comparative studies of Chinese women in other regions could elucidate sociocultural influences on self-management practices.

Conclusion

This study illuminated the complex interplay of factors shaping GDM self-management among Hong Kong Chinese women by applying the HBM. The findings confirm the relevance of the HBM’s core constructs—perceived susceptibility, severity, barriers, benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy—while crucially revealing how they are uniquely manifested within Hong Kong’s sociocultural context. Participants navigated significant challenges, including conflicts between medical advice and traditional food practices, physical constraints, and systemic gaps in support. Yet, they demonstrated resilience and a strong receptivity to culturally tailored digital health solutions.

Hong Kong’s distinct position as a global Asian city with deep Chinese cultural roots creates challenges and opportunities for GDM management. It serves as an ideal crucible for developing next-generation, family-centered GDM interventions that successfully bridge evidence-based medicine with cultural sensitivity. The insights gained here have the potential to inform care models for diverse urban populations globally who are navigating similar tensions between tradition and modernity in health.
