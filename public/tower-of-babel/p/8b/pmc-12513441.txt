Investigating obstacles to the routine use of videolaryngoscopy
Authors: Andrew Baigey, Alistair F McNarry, Patrick A Ward
Published: 2025

Despite the well documented benefits of videolaryngoscopy (VL) over direct laryngoscopy (DL) and the international upsurge in VL availability during the COVID-19 pandemic,1 current use does not reflect the default recommendation in recent guidelines.2 The Project for Universal Management of Airways (PUMA) guidelines for preventing unrecognised oesophageal intubation are among those published since 2020, recommending “routine use of a videolaryngoscope […] whenever feasible”.3 There is a paucity of studies exploring barriers to VL. We sought to investigate airway managers’ current VL use, attitudes towards routine VL and perceived obstacles to VL, in Scotland.

We devised an online survey (https://app.onlinesurveys.jisc.ac.uk) examining four areas: respondent demographics; current VL use; attitudes towards routine VL; and, perceived obstacles to widespread VL adoption (Table 1, Supplement Digital Content). The Checklist for Reporting Of Survey Studies (CROSS) checklist was followed for survey design and reporting; the analysis considered nonresponses as ‘missing completely at random’.4 Questions were revised following a pilot among ten selected airway management experts. The survey was circulated electronically to anaesthetists (all grades), anaesthetic associates (AAs) and operating department practitioners (ODPs)/anaesthetic nurses working at National Health Service (NHS) public hospitals across Scotland via anaesthetic department administrators. The survey was open for responses from the 7th to 28th August 2024.

We received 217 responses from the four NHS Scotland regions (Table 2, Supplement Digital Content). Some questionnaires were incomplete; therefore, data were analysed with variable denominators. Most respondents were consultants (138/217, 64%), followed by resident doctors (54/217, 25%), ODPs/anaesthetic nurses (13/217, 6%), Specialty and Specialist doctors (6/217, 3%) and AAs (6/217, 3%). The three most frequently cited barriers to VL use were concerns about deskilling in DL (137/197, 70%), retaining DL skills for soiled/bleeding airways (107/198, 54%) and lack of perceived benefit during routine airway management (90/192, 47%). Consultants were less likely to cite deskilling as a barrier (77/123, 63%) than residents (45/52, 87%) (P = 0.002, Fisher's exact test). Regarding current VL use, 7/212 (3%) of respondents reported never using VL, 41/212 (19%) reported infrequent use, 93/212 (44%) reported regular use, 42/212 (20%) reported use for most intubations and 29/212 (14%) reported use for every intubation (unless another advanced technique was deemed more appropriate).

Respondents were asked to rate their agreement with the statement, “Recent evidence creates a strong argument for universal use of videolaryngoscopy in anaesthesia to improve efficiency and safety of tracheal intubation”5 using a 10-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 10 = strongly agree); the modal response was 10 and the median response 7 (n = 217) (Fig. 1).

We believe our survey is the first to specifically investigate potential obstacles to VL. The most frequently cited barrier to VL across all groups was concern over deskilling at DL. There is no existing study that proves skill fade in DL with increasing VL use, especially since DL can continue to be taught and practiced with Macintosh-style VL. The second most frequently cited barrier was the need to retain DL skills for soiled/bleeding airways; again, this does not appear to be supported by current literature. VL has been demonstrated to outperform DL in the presence of severely soiled airways, albeit in specific situations outside the operating theatre environment.6,7 Decontamination processes were cited as the third most important barrier; in Scotland, some health boards continue to employ a multistage cleaning process for reusable videolaryngoscopes (despite manufacturers recommending simpler alternatives) which may explain this. Anaesthetic nurses/ODPs were more likely to cite this barrier (8/12, 67%) compared with resident doctors (19/48, 40%) and consultants (39/124, 31%) (P = 0.0431, Fisher's exact test) – possibly because they shoulder the burden of cleaning. The third most frequently cited barrier to VL use was perceived lack of benefit in routine airway management. This likely reflects that failed intubation is a rare event; however, we assert that for the individual patient, such an event may be catastrophic if adequate oxygenation cannot be maintained. A shift in the mindset of airway managers is required, moving from needing VL to achieve successful tracheal intubation (because DL failed) to wanting to use VL for the myriad benefits (better first pass success, fewer hypoxaemic events, reduced trauma, better recognition of oesophageal intubation)8 – particularly since the majority of intubation difficulties are unanticipated. During the COVID-19 pandemic, VL use increased9 because there was clear benefit to both the patient and the airway manager (for example, increased first-pass success during a time-critical procedure, shared view at laryngoscopy and increased patient–operator distance); however, this change was not sustained.2 Demonstrating such obvious advantages during routine airway management is more challenging, since patients with difficult airways occur less frequently during an individual airway manager's clinical practice. However, when considered at a national level, widespread adoption of VL has been proven to reduce the incidence of complications related to tracheal intubation.10

Responses were obtained from all grades of airway managers/assistants from all NHS Scotland regions; however, our findings may not be representative or generalisable. Survey completion was voluntary with distribution reliant upon department administrators making it impossible to calculate a population denominator or survey response rate. No data were gathered on local VL provision (e.g. device type, blade, number of devices per operating theatre), however the number of respondents indicating limited access/availability or competition for devices as a barrier to personal use in theatres was only 33/217 (15%). Our survey did not include airway managers from outside Scotland or those practicing in Emergency Medicine or Critical Care. It is likely that similar barriers to VL exist in other countries; therefore, we plan to distribute our survey (with minor alterations and language translation) to airway managers across Europe.

Default VL use (where possible) is likely to feature in increasing numbers of airway management guidelines. While the majority of respondents reported agreement with the argument for universal use of VL in anaesthesia, only 29/212 (14%) reported universal use of VL in their current clinical practice.

Our survey reports a national snapshot of perceived barriers to VL use; however, to address these barriers and deliver meaningful change in clinical practice, local airway leads must explore the existence of these barriers at their own individual hospitals. This will allow them to tailor structured education and training on VL to meet their specific needs. Such programmes must include effective communication regarding the absence of evidence suggesting that DL skill fade is associated with increased VL use.

We intend to further investigate the reasons why so many respondents expressed support for universal VL, but were currently not achieving this in their own clinical practice. Similarly, we wish to understand the issues surrounding environmental sustainability as our results seem contrary to the ESAIC consensus document.11 Streamlining decontamination processes appears readily achievable in the short-term, whilst modifying attitudes towards VL efficacy may represent a longer-term goal.
