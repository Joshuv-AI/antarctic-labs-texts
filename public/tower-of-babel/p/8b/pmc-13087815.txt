Match Rates for a Surgical Mentorship Program for Underrepresented Learners
Authors: Ben Li, David-Dan Nguyen, Sheharzad Mahmood, Armaghan Alam, Armaan K. Malhotra, Betel Yibrehu, Kennedy Ayoo, Najma Ahmed, Andrea Covelli, Savtaj Brar, Adom Bondzi-Simpson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This quality improvement study reports on outcomes of a surgical mentorship program for underrepresented learners in terms of surgical residency match rates.

Introduction

Students from underrepresented groups (ie, underrepresented in the medical profession relative to their numbers in the general population1) face substantial barriers in pursuing surgical careers, including a lack of mentorship, financial constraints, and bias or discrimination.2 As a result, they are underrepresented in surgical departments across North America.3,4 Equitable access to inclusive mentorship may help alleviate these barriers.5 The UpSurge program was created by the University of Toronto Department of Surgery in 2022 with a focus on providing mentorship to underrepresented learners.6 UpSurge pairs medical students with surgical faculty, fellows, and residents; hosts educational workshops; and provides merit-based scholarships for underrepresented groups.6 UpSurge is now the largest surgical mentorship program in Canada, with more than 200 mentor-mentee pairings and more than CAD$140 000 in funding (to convert to US dollars, multiply by 0.73).6 The UpSurge program costs approximately CAD$32 000 to run annually, primarily related to the online mentorship platform subscription fee, materials/facilities for surgical skills workshops, scholarships, and room bookings and refreshments for career exploration and networking events. UpSurge continues to expand its programming and outreach, with local chapters being established at academic institutions across Canada.6

While the UpSurge framework has been previously described,6 its impact measured by surgical residency match rates has not to date been reported. In this study, we report the 3-year outcomes of the UpSurge mentorship program and outline future directions for programming and scholarship. Our hypothesis was that UpSurge participants have good match rates to surgical residency programs given dedicated mentorship from surgical faculty and trainees.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

We conducted a retrospective review of all available data from the University of Toronto UpSurge mentorship program between 2022 and 2025. This report follows the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) 2.0 reporting guideline for quality improvement studies. This quality improvement project reporting routinely collected and anonymized outcomes of the UpSurge mentorship program was exempt from institutional research ethics board review and the need for informed consent from study participants based on guidance from the Canadian Tri-Council Policy Statement on Research Ethics.7

Overview of the UpSurge Mentorship Program

The UpSurge mentorship program runs annually, recruiting medical students in Canada and around the world, including international medical graduates (IMGs). The program is advertised through student advocacy groups, surgical interest groups, and social media. All learners who self-identify as underrepresented individuals are eligible. The Association of American Medical Colleges definition of underrepresented in medicine was adapted for the program and includes those “underrepresented in the medical profession relative to their numbers in the general population.”1 This encompasses, but is not limited to, individuals who self-identify as belonging to a racial and ethnic marginalized or minoritized group; Indigenous; economically disadvantaged; immigrants; refugees; students with disabilities; LGBTQIA2S+ (lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer/questioning, intersex, asexual/agender, 2-spirit, and other sexual and gender minority groups); and students who have lived in rural communities.8 Mentors (surgical faculty, fellows, or residents) are recruited through local surgical department mailing lists, division chairs, and surgeons-in-chief.

On registration, mentees and mentors complete intake forms that collect demographic information, training backgrounds, and interests. Mentor-mentee matches are facilitated through an online platform where mentees are matched to mentors based on their interests and expertise.9 This platform supports registration, video meetings, group chats, and quarterly surveys, facilitating streamlined data collection and real-time program improvements.9

While UpSurge provides participants with recommendations on key aspects of successful mentorship, mentors and mentees individually determine the nature of their mentorship relationship, including the frequency of meetings, areas of support, and goals. This flexible format enables the mentorship relationship to evolve naturally and allows mentees to be supported in a way that best suits their needs. In addition to mentorship, UpSurge organizes educational events to provide information about surgical careers, gives guidance on residency applications, and supports community building and networking.

Real-time program improvements included the introduction of an identity-congruent matching stream in which mentors and mentees are matched based on self-reported identity characteristics in addition to specialty interests. This change was based on feedback from participants regarding the value of capturing additional dimensions of belonging that may be particularly important for underrepresented learners. Mentorship recommendations include a comprehensive document distributed to all mentors and mentees at the beginning of the program with evidence-based recommendations on successful mentorship relationships.10,11 For mentees, the document outlines strategies for building a strong foundation with their mentor, keeping communication open, and taking a lead in their mentorship journey. For mentors, the guide describes strategies for respecting their mentee’s goals, values, and decisions, helping to increase their mentee’s awareness of and belief in their own potential, and maintaining regular contact with their mentees. Educational events include surgical skills workshops, career exploration and networking events facilitated by surgical faculty and trainees, and mock residency interviews, which are conducted multiple times throughout the year.

The UpSurge Excellence in Surgery Scholarship

The UpSurge Excellence in Surgery Scholarship was established in 2023 to support underrepresented final-year Canadian medical students in pursuing surgical electives outside of their home institutions. Each year, 8 students are awarded CAD$1000 to CAD$1500 to cover travel, housing, and other costs associated with pursuing surgical electives at outside institutions. Applicants are required to complete a detailed application with information about their electives at outside institutions, curriculum vitae, and personal letter regarding their experiences in advocacy and leadership related to equity in surgery. Each year, more than 25 applications are submitted for 8 awards.

Residency Match Data

UpSurge holds quarterly check-ins with participants through surveys and group meetings to track progress, assess needs, and invite feedback. Residency match information, including specialty and location, was obtained from mentees through self-report as well as through publicly available residency program websites and social media platforms, including online profiles of residency programs and mentees. These data were ascertained and confirmed by multiple members of the UpSurge research team composed of surgical residents and faculty involved in residency selection committees. There were no missing data on match results using this method.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed to report the characteristics of Canadian medical students and international medical students and IMGs who participated in UpSurge. This included the institution or region of their medical school, their medical school year (or graduation status for IMGs) at the time of UpSurge participation, the number of years they participated in UpSurge, and their self-reported identity. International medical school regions were classified based on the World Bank regional classifications.12 These characteristics were reported as the number and proportion of participants. Similarly, mentor characteristics, including their specialty and role (faculty, fellow, or resident), were reported as numbers and proportions.

Residency match outcomes of Canadian medical graduates (CMGs), including their specialty and location, were graphically represented. Given that not all medical students in UpSurge completed medical school at the time of this analysis (November 2025), only those who graduated were counted in the denominator for match rates. A subgroup analysis of residency match outcomes for IMGs was conducted given the smaller sample size of this cohort. Additionally, the characteristics and outcomes of the UpSurge Excellence in Surgery Scholarship recipients, including their medical school, match specialty, and match location, were reported.

An exploratory multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the independent associations between the collected covariates (medical school, medical school year at the time of UpSurge participation, IMG vs CMG status, self-reported identity, and mentor characteristics) and the primary outcome of matching to a surgical discipline. The hypothesis was that IMG status would be independently and negatively associated with matching to a surgical discipline given that international students may face additional barriers to matching to Canadian residency programs.13 There were no missing data for the variables and outcomes of interest. Statistical significance was set at a 2-tailed P < .05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Mentee and Mentor Characteristics

Overall, 235 mentees (175 [74%] Canadian medical students and 60 [26%] international medical students or IMGs) participated in the University of Toronto UpSurge mentorship program between 2022 and 2025. All applicants who self-reported as underrepresented in medicine were accepted into the program. Mentees were not asked to provide information about the specific underrepresented group with which they self-identified, and therefore, this information was not reported in this study. Canadian medical students came from 14 of the 18 medical schools across the country. Most students were in their first year (29%) or second year (42%) of medical school. Most Canadian medical students identified as a member of a racial and ethnic marginalized or minoritized group (56%) or of low socioeconomic status (23%). International medical students and IMGs attended medical schools in 30 different countries across 11 geographic regions, including Western Europe (25%), the Caribbean (18%), the Middle East (10%), South Asia (10%), and North Africa (10%). Most international participants had already graduated from medical school when they participated in UpSurge (50%) and identified as a member of a racial and ethnic marginalized or minoritized group (60%) (Table 1). Overall, there were 225 mentors from 11 surgical disciplines, covering all the Canadian Resident Matching Service (CaRMS) direct-entry surgical specialties.14 Of the mentors, 56% were faculty (at all career stages, including early-career, midcareer, and late-career faculty), 42% were residents (ranging from years 1 to ≥5), and 2% were fellows (eTable in Supplement 1).

Residency Match Outcomes for CMGs

Of the 175 Canadian medical students who participated in UpSurge, 52 (30%) were eligible to be residents at the time of this analysis. Overall, 30 of 52 students (57.7%) matched to surgical disciplines, with the top specialties being general surgery (19 of 30 [63%]), plastic surgery (4 of 30 [13%]), and obstetrics and gynecology (2 of 30 [7%]). Of the students who matched to nonsurgical specialties, most matched to internal medicine (7 of 22 [32%]), family medicine (3 of 22 [14%]), and radiology (3 of 22 [14%]) (Figure 1).

Subgroup Analysis of Residency Match Outcomes for IMGs

Of the 60 international medical students and IMGs who participated in UpSurge, 43 (72%) were eligible to be residents at the time of this analysis. Seventeen (40%) matched to a residency program, with 8 (19%) matching to a surgical discipline and 9 (21%) matching to a nonsurgical specialty. The remaining participants pursued non-MD clinical work in Canada, clinical work outside Canada, research, or were not reported (Figure 2). Among the 8 participants who matched to a surgical discipline, 3 (37.5%) became surgical residents in Canada and 5 (62.5%) became surgical residents outside of Canada. IMGs matched to various surgical subspecialties including neurosurgery, general surgery, and ophthalmology. Figure 3 outlines the specialties and locations of IMGs who matched to residency.

Exploratory Analysis of Factors Associated With Matching to Surgical Residency

Exploratory multivariable analysis showed an independent negative association between IMG (vs CMG) status and matching to a surgical discipline (adjusted odds ratio, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.03-0.96; P = .04). There were no independent associations between the number of years in UpSurge, medical school, medical school year at the time of UpSurge participation, self-reported identity, or mentor characteristics on matching to a surgical discipline.

Characteristics and Outcomes of UpSurge Excellence in Surgery Scholarship Recipients

There were 16 scholarship recipients between 2023 and 2025 from 5 medical schools across Canada. Fourteen (88%) matched to surgical disciplines, with the top specialties being general surgery (4 [25%]), plastic surgery (4 [25%]), and urology (3 [19%]). Two scholarship recipients (12%) did not enter surgical residency programs; 1 recipient (6%) matched to internal medicine and 1 recipient (6%) was unmatched (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we reported several key findings. First, the UpSurge mentorship program is large and diverse, with 235 medical students and graduates participating over 3 years from 14 medical schools across Canada and 30 countries internationally. More than one-quarter of mentees came from medical schools outside of Canada. Mentors were similarly diverse, representing all CaRMS surgical specialties.14 Second, surgical residency match rates were high for CMGs, with 58% of eligible participants matching to surgical specialties. This is higher than the 13.3% of CMGs who match to surgical disciplines in Canada but also represents a group of students self-selecting for surgical mentorship.15 Similarly, in the UpSurge Excellence in Surgery Scholarship recipients there was a high match rate of 88% to surgical specialties. Third, our subgroup analysis found that the IMG match rate to surgical residency programs in Canada was 7% of eligible participants. This can be compared with the 5.2% of IMGs who match to surgical specialties in Canada based on CaRMS data.15 However, this comparison should be cautiously interpreted as the absolute difference is small and this analysis was not designed to make direct statistical comparisons with historical CaRMS data.

Of the IMGs who participated in UpSurge and matched to residency, approximately one-half matched to surgical disciplines. This is notable given that IMG match rates to Canadian residency programs, particularly surgical specialties, has traditionally been low.13 It is also important to note that the immigration status (eg, permanent residency or citizenship) of IMGs in UpSurge was not collected, so some IMGs may not have been eligible to apply for Canadian residency positions, thus artificially lowering the overall match rate observed in this study.14 Future work is needed to better assess the match outcomes of eligible IMGs who participate in UpSurge.

Furthermore, the national match rate is not specific to applicants who are underrepresented in medicine.15 CaRMS is currently collecting data on equity, diversity, and inclusion through a self-identification questionnaire including self-reported Indigenous, racialized, disability, socioeconomic, and LGBTQIA2S+ status.15 Future CaRMS reports should consider including match data on applicants underrepresented in medicine to provide a more appropriate contextual benchmark for mentorship programs designed for underrepresented learners.15 Overall, participants in UpSurge have surgical residency match rates in Canada that are above the national average.

While many mentorship programs for medical students exist, a limited number are directly targeted to underrepresented students interested in surgery and few have reported their outcomes in terms of residency match rates.16,17,18,19 To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the objective outcomes of a surgical mentorship program for underrepresented learners. Surgical residency match rates are critical for assessing program impact and justifying the importance of equity-based mentorship for underrepresented learners. Rigorous evaluation is essential to support the legitimacy and sustainability of advocacy-based initiatives in academic surgery. Both CMG and IMG match rates to surgical disciplines in Canada for UpSurge participants are above the national average.15 However, IMG match rates to surgical specialties remain low and IMG status was negatively associated with matching to a surgical discipline. Thus, increased focus on surgical mentorship for IMGs is critical.

This work also highlights the broader implications of developing and implementing advocacy-based initiatives in academic surgery. During uncertain times in the context of equity and inclusiveness, many health professionals are unsure about how they can promote change through advocacy-based work.20,21 As we look toward building models that integrate equity-based scholarship into surgical education, this work highlights strategies for applying rigorous program evaluation and quality improvement methods to examine the outcomes of advocacy-based initiatives and report their impact.22,23

By prospectively collecting program metrics, linking them to objective indicators of learner trajectories (ie, residency match outcomes), and using exploratory analytic methods to inform future research, this work suggests a model for embedding rigorous scholarship within grassroots advocacy. Importantly, these methods do not claim causality but instead establish accountability, generate hypotheses, and legitimize equity-based scholarship within traditional academic structures that value measurable outcomes. In doing so, UpSurge moves mentorship beyond goodwill, passion, and volunteering to a reproducible, educational, and policy-based approach that may be helpful to health care leadership and policymakers.

Future Directions of UpSurge

UpSurge is now entering a phase of strategic growth and evaluation. National expansion to other institutions through the development of local chapters will broaden access for underrepresented learners. To guide this expansion, we are analyzing mentee and mentor feedback surveys and conducting interview-based qualitative evaluations. This will help us further characterize the experiences of mentees and mentors and better understand the factors contributing to successful mentorship.

In the 2025-2026 cycle, we are piloting an identity-congruent stream in which a subset of mentors and mentees will be matched based on self-reported identity characteristics instead of specialty interests alone. This initiative is intended to capture additional dimensions of representation and belonging that may be especially relevant for students from groups historically excluded from surgical training. Through these efforts, UpSurge seeks to strengthen its capacity to provide equitable and accessible mentorship opportunities. Future research will report long-term outcomes of the program and sustained impact on learner progression, career decisions, and contributions to diversifying the surgical workforce.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It provides a multiyear evaluation of surgical residency match outcomes for a national mentorship program designed for underrepresented learners. The study included a large and geographically diverse cohort, complete ascertainment of match outcomes, and transparent benchmarking against national reference data. Together, these features support the validity of the findings and provide a foundation for ongoing evaluation and future research.

This study also has limitations. There is likely an element of selection bias in UpSurge participant match rates, given that learners who register for this program likely have a preexisting interest in surgery. The attribution of match outcomes to the mentorship program vs inherent participant characteristics and motivations requires further study.

There is potential for reporting bias given the use of self-reported and publicly sourced match data. Further validation of match results is needed to confirm our findings.

Given that the UpSurge mentorship program deliberately follows a flexible format with no minimum expectations for meeting frequency, the quality and intensity of mentorship relationships were not assessed in this study. Qualitative work is underway by the UpSurge Research Group to further characterize mentor-mentee relationships in UpSurge and to assess their impact on participant satisfaction and outcomes.

We did not capture applicants’ intentions at the time of residency application. Among participants who matched to nonsurgical specialties, we were unable to ascertain if this reflected a change in desire or an unfavorable outcome. Similarly, among participants who matched to surgical disciplines, we could not determine whether this was a primary or secondary specialty of interest. Qualitative research is underway by the UpSurge Research Group to answer these questions.

UpSurge was founded in 2022, and the duration of available follow-up is insufficient to comprehensively assess attrition from surgical residency among UpSurge participants who matched to residency programs. Ongoing longitudinal follow-up is underway to evaluate training completion and to further characterize persistent systemic barriers faced by underrepresented learners in surgery.

The multivariable analysis was meant to be hypothesis-generating and exploratory. Larger sample sizes are needed to add robustness to our findings.

Conclusions

In this 3-year quality improvement study using retrospective data of participants in a dedicated surgical mentorship program for underrepresented learners, we found that UpSurge is a large and diverse mentorship program with high surgical residency match rates. Continued investment into programs such as UpSurge may help improve surgical workforce diversity.
