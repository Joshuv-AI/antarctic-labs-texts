Molecular weight fractionated extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) impart different aggregation characteristics on polystyrene nanoplastics
Authors: Feng-Yi Li, Gui-Yun Song, Qing-Xin Zhang, Mi-Hong Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores how EPS with varying molecular weights (MW) adsorb onto polystyrene nanoplastics coated with bare (PS-Bare), carboxylic (PS-COOH), or amine (PS-NH₂) functional groups. Experiments were performed in NaCl and CaCl2 to characterize the formed eco-corona and their impacts on nanoplastics aggregation kinetics. The results show that while eco-corona shares similar chemical compositions, their thickness (ECT) correlates positively with both adsorbed EPS mass and MW. In NaCl, PS-Bare exhibited the narrowest ECT range (0.88–7.17 nm), followed by PS-COOH (1.78–9.43 nm), whereas PS-NH₂ displayed the broadest span (2.33–13.95 nm). The ECT further increased in CaCl2. Enhanced ECT caused higher critical coagulation concentration (CCC). In NaCl, PS-Bare’s CCC rose from 343 mM to 466–1291 mM, and PS-COOH’s from 339 mM to 577–1373 mM. Under 5 mg C L⁻¹ EPS, PS-NH₂ maintained a stable hydrodynamic diameter (Dh ≈ 150 nm) in both NaCl and CaCl2, indicating strengthened steric stabilization. In CaCl2 with 5 mg C L⁻¹ EPS, similar stabilization trends were observed. However, at 10 mg C L⁻¹, high-MW EPS reduced CCC via bridging effects. For PS-NH2, low-MW EPS (5 mg C L⁻¹) increased Dh to 900–1200 nm through charge neutralization and weak steric hindrance. The EPS <3kDa further enlarged Dh to 1400 nm by patch-charge attraction. This study reveals that EPS molecular weight, by regulating eco-corona thickness and surface charge distribution, is a key factor influencing the colloidal stability of nanoplastics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42401-6.Keywords: Nanoplastics, Extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), Molecular weight, Eco-corona, Aggregation kinetics, Colloidal stability, Surface functionalizationSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nanoplastics (NPs) have received considerable attention as emerging contaminants1. The potential risks posed by nanoplastics to natural environments and ecosystems are closely linked to their aggregation behavior2–5. Extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) are heterogeneous, high-molecular-weight biopolymers secreted by microorganisms during growth and metabolism. They primarily consist of polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and humic-like substances, constituting a major fraction of natural organic matter (NOM) in aquatic and soil systems1,6,7. Upon nanoplastics environmental entry, they inevitably undergo interactions with EPS. Co-existing EPS can form an adsorbed layer, referred to as the “eco-corona,” on the surface of nanoplastics8,9. This layer confers unique surface properties that modulate nanoplastics aggregation via mechanisms including electrostatic interactions, steric hindrance, cation bridging, and patch-charge attractions, thereby affecting the environmental mobility and biotoxicity of nanoplastics10–12. In general, the eco-corona can alter nanoplastics surface charge, regulating the DLVO force between nanoplastics particles13. However, thicker eco-corona induces stronger steric repulsion (non-DLVO force), thereby enhancing the dispersion of nanoplastics14,15. Conversely, aromatic groups from the eco-corona can enhance aggregation through hydrophobic interactions16,17. Furthermore, divalent cations (e.g., Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺) may bridge carboxyl or aromatic groups in the eco-corona, facilitating the formation of hetero aggregates15,18.

The physical and chemical properties of eco-corona, including charge, thickness, and functional group composition, have been shown to depend on EPS concentration as well as its physicochemical properties10,11,14,19. The molecular weights (MW) and components of EPS determine the properties of eco-corona20,21. EPS exhibits a broad MW range, from < 1 kDa to over 2000 kDa. High-MW fractions predominantly comprise polysaccharides and proteins, whereas humic substances are more concentrated in low-MW fractions, as comprehensively reviewed by Flemming and Wingender7. This compositional heterogeneity has been consistently observed across diverse EPS sources22,23. Adsorption behaviors of EPS on mineral surfaces vary significantly with molecular weights. For instance, low-MW EPS fractions exhibit preferential adsorption onto bentonite and montmorillonite24, whereas high-MW fractions display greater affinity for ferrihydrite and goethite25. Preferential adsorption of EPS can impart distinct surface properties to nanoplastics, altering their ζ-potential or conferring eco-corona with variable thicknesses and specific functional group compositions. These multifaceted effects have been demonstrated in studies on the interactions between EPS and various nanoparticles, including nanoplastics19 and TiO₂26.

Nanoplastics are frequently surface-coated or functionalized, which can modify their surface charge and surface energy, thereby altering the interaction between nanoplastics and proteins. For instance, Yu et al.18 first demonstrated this by comparing the aggregation kinetics of nanoparticles with different surface modifications. Subsequent studies extended these findings to more complex systems: Tallec et al.27 examined nanoplastic fate in model aquatic environments; Wang et al.28 confirmed similar trends in natural waters with sulfate-modified polystyrene, and Wu et al.29 further revealed the modulating effects of salinity and humic acid on differently charged nanoplastics. Collectively, these investigations underscore the pivotal role of surface chemistry in mediating interactions between nanoplastics and environmental constituents. The molecular mechanisms by which different functional groups govern EPS adsorption have also been explored. Building on this foundation, positively charged amine-modified polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NH2) exhibit strong electrostatic attraction, which dominates the adsorption of negatively charged NOM components (e.g., carboxyl groups of proteins, humic substances, and polysaccharides), typically resulting in the highest adsorption capacity and the thickest eco-corona. In contrast, negatively charged carboxyl-modified polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-COOH) generate electrostatic repulsion with most negatively charged NOM components, thereby inhibiting initial adsorption. Unmodified polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-Bare) display a weakly negative to neutral surface, leading to relatively weak electrostatic interactions with NOM11,28,30. Moreover, the strongly hydrophobic surface of PS-Bare could also serve as its primary adsorption driving force. It can interact with hydrophobic aromatic domains in EPS (e.g., aromatic rings of humic substances and hydrophobic cores of proteins) through hydrophobic interactions and π-π stacking, leading to a moderate adsorption capacity31–33. For PS-COOH and PS-NH₂, the contribution of hydrophobic interactions is relatively minor due to their hydrophilic functional groups.

Solution chemistry including pH, electrolyte type, and concentration, profoundly impacts eco-corona properties through charge regulation and conformational changes. Early studies established the fundamental roles of inorganic ions and pH in nanoplastic aggregation34,35. Subsequent investigations refined this understanding by quantifying specific interfacial parameters: Tiemeyer et al.36 determined adsorbed layer thickness as a function of ionic strength, while Stecher and Plank37 further elucidated the influence of polymer concentration on layer thickness. Building on this foundation, the specific effects of pH and cation type on EPS-nanoplastic interactions can be examined in detail. Under acidic conditions, protonation of NOM carboxyl groups weakens electrostatic attraction with PS-NH₂ but may enhance hydrophobic and π-π interactions with PS-Bare31. Regarding electrolyte composition, Ca²⁺ exhibits a distinct behavior compared to Na⁺: it can coordinate simultaneously with carboxyl groups of NOM and negatively charged regions on nanoplastic surfaces, forming a “bridge” between particles15,16,26. This bridging mechanism substantially enhances NOM adsorption capacity on PS-COOH and PS-Bare, emerging as a key force alongside electrostatic attraction.

Substantial progress has been made in elucidating the interactions between micro/nanoplastics and bulk NOM38. However, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the role of EPS in modulating the colloidal behavior of nanoplastics, particularly across three key dimensions. First, while the stabilizing effect of NOM is well-documented, the molecular-weight-dependent behavior of heterogeneous EPS fractions under varying electrolyte conditions remains largely unexplored. Given that EPS composition varies drastically in aquatic systems and is further modified by processes like mineral adsorption and photodegradation39, clarifying how distinct MW fractions regulate nanoplastics stability is scientifically imperative. Second, although kinetic parameters such as the critical coagulation concentration (CCC) are widely reported29,34,35,40, quantitative relationships between eco-corona properties (e.g., layer thickness, surface charge) and interparticle forces remain unestablished, limiting the mechanistic prediction of aggregation behavior. Third, current insights rely heavily on simplified model systems: most eco-corona studies focus on model proteins10,11,41, neglecting the key roles of abundant non-protein biomolecules (e.g., polysaccharides, humic-like substances) that dominate native EPS. This exclusion introduces potential inconsistencies between laboratory observations and real environmental scenarios.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study systematically investigates the adsorption behavior of EPS with varying molecular weights on surface-functionalized nanoplastics in NaCl and CaCl2. Furthermore, the evolution of eco-corona characteristics was evaluated, and the underlying mechanisms by which the eco-corona influences the colloidal stability of nanoplastics were elucidated. Bare polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-Bare), along with those coated with carboxylic (PS-COOH) and amine (PS-NH2) groups, were selected as model nanoplastics in this study, representing common types of plastic particles. The EPS with varying molecular weights were obtained through ultrafiltration. Through the comprehensive use of batch adsorption experiments, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and time-resolved dynamic light scattering techniques (DLS), a multidimensional evaluation was executed encompassing adsorbed mass of EPS, thickness, functional group, and surface charge of eco-corona, as well as aggregation kinetics of nanoplastics. Compared to previous studies that primarily focused on bulk NOM or single proteins, this work represents the first systematic effort to reveal how EPS molecular weight fractions differentially regulate the aggregation behavior of nanoplastics through distinct mechanisms (steric repulsion, cation bridging, and patch-charge attraction) in mono- and divalent electrolytes. This work deepens the mechanistic understanding of EPS–nanoplastic interactions and provides a predictive framework for evaluating the fate of nanoplastics in natural aquatic environments.

Materials and methods

Polystyrene nanoparticles

Bare polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-Bare), along with those coated with carboxylic (PS-COOH) and amine (PS-NH2) groups were used in the present investigation (Macklin Biochemical Technology, Shanghai, China). Their morphology, particle size, and ζ-potential have been reported in the previous work30. In brief, the three polystyrene nanoparticles displayed well-defined spherical morphology. Based on the analysis of 100 particles, the SEM-derived diameters of PS-Bare, PS-COOH, and PS-NH₂ are 101.4 ± 5.7 nm, 98.3 ± 6.9 nm, and 118.5 ± 7.4 nm, respectively. As documented in prior studies30, the hydrodynamic diameters (Dh) of PS‑Bare and PS‑COOH NPs were consistent with the core sizes determined by SEM, whereas PS‑NH₂ displayed a larger Dh. This discrepancy arises because SEM solely characterizes the nanoparticle core, while DLS incorporates contributions from the hydrated layer and surface functional coatings. The corresponding ζ-potentials in ultrapure water were measured as 36.9 ± 1.73 mV, -38.6 ± 1.56 mV, and 35.1 ± 1.68 mV. Over the pH range of 4–9, PS-Bare and PS-COOH demonstrated a trend toward elevated negative zeta potentials, while PS-NH2 showed a corresponding reduction in its positive charge (Fig. S1). Electrolytes can compress the electric double layer, resulting in diminishing ζ-potential (Fig. S2). Under neutral pH conditions, the ζ-potential of PS-Bare in 500 mmol L⁻¹ NaCl and 50 mmol L⁻¹ CaCl2 solutions was measured as -11.1 ± 1.92 mV and − 11.2 ± 1.47 mV, respectively, indicating minimal influence from electrolyte type. In contrast, PS-COOH exhibited stronger negative ζ-potentials, with values of -14.5 ± 1.52 mV (NaCl) and − 13.9 ± 1.24 mV (CaCl2). Conversely, PS-NH2 displayed positive ζ-potentials, measuring 11.9 ± 1.97 mV and 16.5 ± 1.22 mV in the respective solutions.

EPS extraction and purification

The Bacillus subtilis strains were pre-cultured in 30 mL of tryptone soy broth (TSB) medium for 12 h, then transferred to 100 mL of fresh TSB medium and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 24 h to obtain highly active cells. After centrifugation (5000 × g, 15 min), the cells were washed three times with 0.9% NaCl solution and resuspended to a wet cell weight of approximately 5 mg·mL⁻¹. An equal volume of physiological saline containing 2% Na2EDTA was added to the bacterial suspension to chelate divalent metal ions, weakening the interaction between cells and EPS. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 3 h, followed by low-temperature centrifugation (5000 × g, 4 °C, 20 min) to separate EPS-stripped cells. The supernatant was collected, and the cells were washed three times with physiological saline to ensure complete EPS recovery. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane and dialyzed using a 1 kDa molecular weight cut-off Viskase dialysis bag. Initial dialysis in ultrapure water for 48 h (with water changes every 12 h) was followed by an additional 72 h in Milli-Q water (with three daily water changes) to remove residual impurities. The purified EPS solution was vacuum freeze-dried for subsequent analysis and storage.

A 15 mL ultrafiltration centrifuge unit (Millipore, Billerica, MA) was used to separate the samples into five molecular weight fractions: > 100 kDa, 30–100 kDa, 10–30 kDa, 3–10 kDa, and < 3 kDa. The process involved stepwise filtration, where the retentate from each stage was transferred to a filter membrane with a lower molecular weight cutoff, enabling progressive fractionation from larger to smaller molecules. To minimize residual low molecular weight EPS in the retained fractions, the trapped solution was rinsed multiple times with Milli-Q water until the filtrate became nearly colorless, indicating effective removal of impurities. The concentrated fractions were then collected and diluted with Milli-Q water for subsequent experiments. The carbon content of each sample was quantified using a TOC Analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan), and mass recovery evaluation demonstrated an overall recovery rate of approximately 85%. All pristine EPS and fractionated samples were stored at 4 °C in darkness prior to further analysis.

The concentration of all EPS samples was uniformly expressed as dissolved organic carbon (DOC) in mg C L⁻¹, measured using a TOC analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan). This normalization ensured that each EPS fraction was compared on an equal carbon mass basis, regardless of differences in its non-carbon constituents or molecular weight. For adsorption and aggregation experiments, stock solutions of each EPS fraction were diluted with the corresponding electrolyte (NaCl or CaCl2) to achieve target TOC concentrations (0.5–20 mg C L⁻¹). This approach allowed consistent evaluation of adsorption capacity and colloidal stability across EPS fractions with varying MW and composition.

Characterization of EPS and PS NPs

The polysaccharide and protein contents were measured through the phenol-sulfuric acid method and Coomassie brilliant blue staining (Bradford assay), employing glucose and bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the respective quantification standards. The total organic carbon (TOC) and total nitrogen (TN) contents were quantified by TOC/TN Analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan). Functional groups of EPS and eco-corona were determined to use a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The hydrodynamic diameter (Dh) and zeta potential of polystyrene nanoplastics were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK) equipped with a 633 nm He-Ne laser, with measurements conducted at a scattering angle of 173°.

Adsorption experiments

The concentrations of nanoplastics in natural aquatic environments range from approximately 1.5–32 µg L⁻¹42. Although DLS can theoretically detect particles as low as ∼0.001 mg L⁻¹43, acquiring reproducible aggregation kinetics data requires a sufficiently high concentration to ensure signal stability and measurement consistency. Reliable DLS analysis was not achievable at 1 mg L⁻¹ due to the low scattering intensity and poor signal-to-noise ratio of dilute nanoparticle suspensions. Thus, 20 mg L⁻¹ was selected in this study, which is consistent with the levels (10 or 20 mg L⁻¹) commonly used in laboratory simulations reported in previous literature10,11,44.

Batch adsorption experiments were performed to investigate EPS adsorption onto PS NPs under different electrolyte and pH conditions. The solutions contained either 350 mmol L⁻¹ NaCl or 30 mmol L⁻¹ CaCl2, with the pH adjusted from 4.0 to 9.0. These electrolyte concentrations were selected because polystyrene nanoparticles in the absence of EPS displayed maximum aggregation rates under these conditions, thereby enabling independent assessment of the effects of EPS adsorption on nanoparticle kinetic behavior.

Prior to the adsorption experiments, the polystyrene nanoparticles stock solution and EPS solution were adjusted to the target pH using 0.01 mol L⁻¹ HCl or NaOH and equilibrated for 24 h. The polystyrene nanoparticles suspension was then subjected to water bath sonication for 30 min before introducing the EPS-containing electrolyte solution. Each sample had a final volume of 12 mL, with the polystyrene nanoparticles’ concentration maintained at 20 mg L⁻¹, while the EPS concentration ranged from 0.50 to 20.0 mg C L⁻¹.To ensure consistency with the exposure times of the aggregation kinetics experiments, all samples were placed in 15 mL centrifuge tubes and mixed via end-to-end rotation for 60 min, with triplicate experiments performed. Following mixing, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min to isolate unabsorbed EPS in the supernatant. The residual EPS concentration in the supernatant was quantified using a TOC/TN Analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan), and the adsorbed amount was determined by subtracting the unabsorbed concentration from the initial concentration.

The ζ-potentials of various eco-corona were determined following the adsorption experiments. The surface functional groups of the eco-corona were characterized using FTIR spectroscopy of the centrifuged precipitates.

Determination of eco-corona thickness

To quantify the thickness of the eco-corona on the surface of polystyrene nanoparticles, this study monitored changes in Dh before and after particle interaction with EPS. The experiment maintained consistent parameters, including pH, EPS concentration, and reaction time, to align with the adsorption experiment settings and ensure data comparability. To minimize particle aggregation interference during size measurement, measurements were conducted at a lower electrolyte concentration (200 mmol L⁻¹ for NaCl and 10 mmol L⁻¹ for CaCl2). Dh values for each condition were determined by averaging 100 repeated measurements to ensure data reliability. Aggregation kinetics experiments

The time-resolved dynamic light scattering technique (TR-DLS) was employed to systematically investigate the aggregation kinetics of polystyrene nanoparticles in a controlled solution environment. Initially, the ultrasonically dispersed polystyrene nanoparticle suspension was mixed with an electrolyte solution containing a defined concentration of pristine or molecular weight-fractionated EPS. The 1 mL reaction system consisted of 20 mg L⁻¹ polystyrene nanoparticles and 5–10 mg C L⁻¹ EPS at pH 7.0, with varying concentrations of NaCl (1–1500 mmol L⁻¹) or CaCl2 (0.1–70 mmol L⁻¹). After a brief vortex to initiate the reaction, the instrument automatically monitored the hydrodynamic diameter Dₕ(t) at 1 min intervals for a total of 60 min.

According to Chen and Elimelech45,46, the initial aggregation rate constant (k) is proportional to the initial rate of change in the hydrodynamic diameter (dDₕ/dt) and inversely proportional to the initial number concentration of polystyrene nanoparticles (N₀). To determine dDₕ/dt, experiments were performed following the method of Huangfu et al.47. The slope of the hydrodynamic diameter Dₕ(t) evolution over time was obtained by linear least-squares regression from the onset of aggregation (t₀) until Dₕ exceeded 1.5 times its initial value (Dₕ₀). This data processing approach has been widely adopted in nanoparticle aggregation kinetic studies15,26,40.

To quantify the influence of solution conditions on nanoparticle aggregation, the attachment efficiency α (0 ≤ α ≤ 1) was introduced. As defined by Chen and Elimelech46 and Huangfu et al.47, α is the ratio of the rate constant k under a given condition to the rate constant under favorable (fast) aggregation conditions (kfast), which normalizes the rate constants obtained under different conditions.

Since the initial particle number concentration N₀ was kept constant across all experiments with varying ionic strength, the calculation of α can be simplified using the initial slopes of the aggregation profiles: α is determined as the ratio of the initial slope at a given electrolyte concentration to the initial slope under favorable aggregation conditions. Here, (dDₕ/dt)fast was obtained as the average of multiple measurements in the diffusion-limited regime (i.e., above the critical coagulation concentration). To ensure consistent comparison among EPS with different molecular weights, the diffusion-limited aggregation rate constant measured for polystyrene nanoparticles in the absence of EPS was uniformly used as kfast for all experimental series. This baseline value was determined separately for the NaCl and CaCl2 systems.

Results and discussion

The constituents in MW-fractionated EPS exist heterogeneities

The molecular weight of EPS was fractionated using ultrafiltration coupled with high-performance size exclusion chromatography (HPSEC), yielding components with molecular weights spanning a broad range from 2.42 to 21.2 kDa (Tables 1 and Fig. S3). The molecular weight distribution was determined through quantitative analysis of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) concentrations within each fraction. The carbon weight percentages (wt%) were 36.05, 7.69, 3.96, 4.71, and 33.21 for EPS<3kDa, EPS3−10kDa, EPS10−30kDa, EPS30−100kDa, and EPS>100kDa, respectively, exhibiting a recovery efficiency of 85.61% relative to the pristine EPS sample.

Table 1 compared the compositional characteristics of pristine and MW-fractionated EPS. The results demonstrated that proteins were predominantly concentrated in the EPS>100kDa fraction, while polysaccharides exhibited primary distribution across medium and low molecular weights ranges (EPS10−30kDa, EPS3−10kDa, and EPS<3kDa fractions). Notably, the EPS<3kDa fraction contributed 36.0% of TOC and 43.9% of TN despite minimal proteins and polysaccharides in this fraction. This carbon-nitrogen enrichment pattern in low-MW fractions corroborates existing literature21,48. The phenomenon likely stems from substantial non-polysaccharide/non-protein components (e.g., humic acids, nucleic acids) within the EPS<3kDa fraction.

FTIR spectra further validated these biochemical constituents (Fig. S4a). The absorption bands at 1654 cm⁻¹, 1559 cm⁻¹, and 1454 cm⁻¹ correspond to the C = O stretching of Amide I, N-H/C-N bending of Amide II, and -CH3/-CH2 deformations of Amide III in proteins, respectively. The band at 1410 cm⁻¹ is ascribed to symmetric C = O stretching of carboxylate groups, while the band at 1220 cm⁻¹ arises from P = O stretching in the phosphodiester backbone of nucleic acids or phosphorylated proteins. Bands at 1132 cm⁻¹, 1072 cm⁻¹, and 1036 cm⁻¹ are attributed to C-O/C-C stretching and C-O-C/C-O deformation vibrations in polysaccharide structures21. FTIR spectral analysis revealed a progressive increase in the peak area ratio of Amide I (1610–1712 cm⁻¹) to polysaccharide-associated functional groups (930–1180 cm⁻¹) with rising molecular weights, corroborating the compositional findings (Fig. S4b).

EPS with diverse molecular weights forms eco-corona with comparable chemical properties but differing thicknesses

The adsorbed mass of EPS on PS NPs at neutral pH 7.0 was presented in Fig. 1a and b. Three types of polystyrene nanoparticles displayed similar adsorption trends, with the adsorbed mass increasing as the initial concentration and molecular weight of EPS increased. At the same concentration, the adsorbed mass of each fraction depended on the electrolyte and the coating type of polystyrene nanoparticles. Both negatively charged PS-Bare and PS-COOH demonstrated higher adsorption capacity in CaCl2 than in NaCl, attributable to the Ca²⁺ bridging effect18,49. Under identical electrolyte conditions, EPS adsorption on PS-COOH was more pronounced than on PS-Bare, likely owing to enhanced bridging effect26. Positively charged PS-NH₂ showed strong EPS adsorption through electrostatic attraction18,50. Specifically, lower-MW fractions (EPS <3kDa, EPS 3−10kDa, and EPS 10−30kDa) adsorbed 1.4–1.9 times more on PS-NH2 than on PS-Bare and PS-COOH, while higher fractions (EPS30−100kDa and EPS>100kDa) reached 2.3–2.6 times greater adsorption. For pristine or specific EPS components, the adsorbed mass on PS-NH₂ were not influenced by the two electrolyte solutions. The findings were confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy and zeta potential analyses (Fig. S4, Fig. S7 and Fig. S8).

Adsorption isotherms of MW-fractionated EPS on surface-functionalized polystyrene nanoparticles.

Given that pH influences surface charge distribution of PS NPs and species of EPS in aqueous solution, the adsorption of 10 mg C L⁻¹ EPS on the three types of PS NPs was further investigated at pH 4.0 and 9.0. At pH 4.0, all three polystyrene nanoparticles exhibited strong EPS adsorption (Fig. 1b–d). As the pH increased from 4.0 to 7.0, the adsorption capacity of EPS gradually declined; however, no significant change was observed between pH 7.0 and 9.0. This trend aligns with the zeta potential variations across the pH range of 4–9 (Fig. S1). The rise in pH enhances the negative surface charge of PS-Bare and PS-COOH while reducing the positive charge of PS-NH2. Additionally, the rapid deprotonation of EPS molecules51, reducing electrostatic attraction and hindering EPS adsorption on polystyrene nanoparticles. It is also noteworthy that, at pH 4.0 and 9.0, the effects of molecular weight and electrolytes on adsorption behavior closely follow the trends observed at pH 7.0.

Figure 2a–c showed the eco-corona thickness (ECT) at pH 7.0. The ECT was obviously controlled by the mass of adsorbed EPS (Fig. S5). In NaCl solutions, the maximum ECT (ECT max) for PS-Bare exhibited the following order: EPS > 100kDa (7.17 ± 0.36 nm) > pristine EPS (6.86 ± 0.29 nm) ≈ EPS 30−100kDa (6.81 ± 0.40 nm) > EPS 10−30kDa (2.51 ± 0.31 nm) > EPS 3−10kDa (1.54 ± 0.21 nm) > EPS <3kDa (0.88 ± 0.27 nm) (Fig. 2a). A similar trend was found in CaCl2 solutions, albeit with elevated ECT max values (Fig. 2b). This was consistent with the results on the mass of adsorbed EPS (Fig. 1a and b). Similarly, the ECTmax for PS-COOH in NaCl solutions was 1.2-2.0 times that of PS-Bare, with slightly higher values in CaCl2 solutions. The adsorbed mass of EPS30−100kDa and EPS>100kDa on PS-NH2 was about 2.1 times that of PS-Bare, yielding ECTmax spanning from 11.57 ± 0.59 nm to 14.55 ± 0.48 nm (Fig. 2c). The electrolyte has no obvious impact on the ECTmax of PS-NH2, due to its consistent adsorbed mass across different electrolytes.

Maximum eco-corona thickness (ECTmax) on PS-Bare, PS-COOH, and PS-NH₂ as a function of EPS molecular weight. Detailed error ranges for ECTmax values are provided in Table S1.

Because the adsorption of EPS onto PS NPs was enhanced under the acidic condition, Dh was measured before and after interaction with the EPS at pH 4.0 to investigate the effect of pH on the eco-corona thickness. The concentration-dependent ECT profiles at pH 4.0 were illustrated in Fig. S6. A comparable trend was noticed in the concentration-dependent ECT profiles at pH 4.0 and 7.0. ECTmax was slightly greater at pH 4.0 than 7.0 under identical experimental conditions (Fig. 2d–f). In detail, the ECTmax of lower-MW fractionated EPS is more significantly affected by pH, whereas the higher-MW counterparts exhibit a weaker response. Taking PS-Bare as an example, in NaCl solution, when the pH decreased from 7.0 to 4.0, the ECTmax of EPS<3kDa increased from 0.88 to 1.98 nm, representing a 125% increase. In contrast, the EPS>100kDa only increased from 7.17 to 8.11 nm, a 13% increase, demonstrating a more limited change.

The influence of electrolyte concentration on the ECT was investigated to assess whether elevated ion concentrations enhance ECT (Table S2). The results indicate that increasing the Na+ concentration from 200 to 300 mmol L⁻¹ did not lead to an increase in the ECTmax for PS-Bare and PS-COOH. This clearly demonstrates that excess Na⁺ has no significant influence on the ECT. At lower EPS concentrations (< 5 mg C L− 1), ECT values measured under 20 mmol L⁻¹ and 10 mmol L⁻¹ Ca²⁺ conditions were essentially consistent. However, at higher EPS concentrations (> 10 mg C L− 1), reliable ECT measurements could not be obtained because significant heteroaggregation occurred between the PS NPs and EPS. Similar results have been reported for various nanoparticles, including PVP-coated silver, gold, and fullerene45,52,53. In contrast, for PS-NH₂, the ECT max remains unchanged regardless of variations in either Na⁺ or Ca²⁺ concentrations.

In comparison to bulk EPS, a noticeable difference in the FTIR spectra of the eco-corona was the diminished intensity of the band at 1400 cm⁻¹. These observations are consistent with prior research, suggesting that aromatic components dominate interactions with polystyrene microparticles and the relatively low affinity of carboxyl groups for PS-Bare44. Although there are differences in the functional groups of EPS with different molecular weight fractions, the eco-corona of PS-Bare and PS-NH2 are chemically similar, primarily consisting of aromatic structures. For PS-COOH, the FTIR spectral changes of the eco-corona formed in NaCl solution resembled those of PS-Bare and PS-NH₂, while an inverse spectral pattern emerged in CaCl2 solution. Notably, the prominent peak at 1400 cm⁻¹ in CaCl2 solution confirms that the carboxyl group plays a critical role in the formation of the PS-COOH eco-corona.

In NaCl solutions, the ζ-potentials of PS-Bare and PS-COOH remained negative after interaction with EPS, exhibiting nearly identical absolute values regardless of the adsorbed mass and molecular weight of EPS (Fig. S8a and b). However, the ζ-potential slightly decreased in CaCl2 solutions with high concentrations of EPS. The eco-corona significantly reduced the ζ-potential of PS-NH2, with positive values decreasing as EPS concentration and molecular weight increase. The charge reversal of PS-NH2 occurred in both NaCl and CaCl2 solutions containing high-MW EPS (EPS30−100kDa and EPS>100kDa). Under identical testing conditions, no significant difference in ζ-potentials was observed within the pH range of 4.0–7.0, indicating that pH has a negligible effect on the surface charge of the eco-corona (Fig. S8c and d). A thorough characterization of eco-corona was carried out in this section. The eco-corona formed by different MW fractions on the polystyrene nanoparticles differ primarily in terms of their physical properties, such as adsorbed mass and thickness, rather than their chemical properties.

Thicker eco-corona improves dispersion of polystyrene nanoparticles via steric repulsion

In this study, pH had a minor influence on eco-corona thickness and no significant effect on ζ-potential. The Dh of polystyrene nanoparticles remained statistically consistent over the pH range from 4.0 to 7.0, indicating that increased adsorption mass at pH 4.0 was insufficient to induce any significant changes in eco-corona properties or polystyrene nanoparticles aggregation behavior (Table S3–5). Consequently, the aggregation kinetics were measured at neutral pH.

In the EPS-free system, the variation in attachment efficiency (α) between PS-Bare and PS-COOH adheres to the classical DLVO theory, exhibiting distinct reaction-limited and diffusion-limited regimes (Fig. S9). As electrolyte concentration rises, electrostatic repulsion between particles diminishes due to electric double-layer compression, leading to a substantial increase in α (Fig. S9). Within the reaction-limited regime, α escalated from approximately 2% to 83% (NaCl, 10–200 mM) and from ~ 2.5% to 93% (CaCl2, 1–20 mM). Upon transitioning to the diffusion-limited regime, however, aggregation kinetics attained its maximum (α = 1) and became independent of further electrolyte concentration changes. The critical coagulation concentration (CCC) is operationally defined as the electrolyte concentration at which the α curve shifts from the reaction-limited to the diffusion-limited plateau. Accordingly, the measured CCC values for PS-Bare were 343 mM (NaCl) and 28.8 mM (CaCl2), while those for PS-COOH were 339 mM (NaCl) and 27.2 mM (CaCl2). The markedly lower CCC for Ca²⁺ compared to Na⁺ is attributed to the enhanced charge neutralization capacity of the divalent electrolyte. The ratio of CCCs for CaCl₂ and NaCl follows a proportionality of z− 3.75 for PS-Bare and z− 3.64 for PS-COOH, aligning with the Schultz-Hardy rule, which predicts a proportional fraction of nanomaterials ranging from z⁻⁶ to z⁻², where z denotes the valence of Ca²⁺ counterions (z = 2)40.

In contrast, PS-NH₂ displayed no typical DLVO behavior, maintaining stability even at 1000 mM NaCl and 50 mM CaCl2. Since all polystyrene nanoparticles were tested under identical conditions, the pronounced divergence in their aggregation behavior must arise from the distinct chemical properties conferred by their surface functional groups.

In elevated NaCl concentrations, EPS introduction elevated the CCC values while reducing α values of PS-Bare (Fig. 3a and b), demonstrating EPS-mediated inhibition of particle aggregation. This inhibitory mechanism correlates directly with eco-corona thickness development. At 1, 2, and 5 mg C L⁻¹ EPS concentrations, PS-Bare achieved 49.1%, 59.2%, and 59.4% of its ECTₘₐₓ (6.86 ± 0.29 nm), whereas PS-COOH attained 43.2%, 58.3%, and 79.9% of its ECTₘₐₓ (8.67 ± 0.54 nm) (Fig. S5). Progressive eco-corona thickening increased PS-Bare’s CCC from 961 to 1032 mM and PS-COOH’s from 982 to 1043 mM (Fig. 3a–d), confirming EPS concentration-dependent enhancement of colloidal stability. PS-COOH developed a thicker eco-corona than PS-Bare at equivalent EPS levels, yielding superior stabilization. ζ-potential analysis revealed minimal variations post-EPS introduction (Fig. S8), excluding surface charge as the dominant regulatory factor. These findings establish eco-corona thickness as the principal determinant of aggregation behavior in NaCl solutions, with carboxyl groups amplifying stability through enhanced corona formation. In addtion, PS-COOH displayed analogous response patterns in replicate experiments (Fig. 4b, e).

EPS with specific molecular weights has differential impacts on the stability of PS-Bare and PS-COOH. Compared to pristine and lower-MW EPS (EPS10−30kDa, EPS3−10kDa and EPS<3kDa), higher-MW EPS (EPS>100kDa and EPS30−100kDa) can effectively prevent the agglomeration of PS-Bare in NaCl solutions. The CCC values are positively associated with the molecular weights of EPS (Fig. 3). As the molecular weight increases, the thickness of the eco-corona on PS-Bare and PS-COOH rose from 0.88 ± 0.27 nm to 7.17 ± 0.36 nm and from 1.78 ± 0.62 nm to 9.43 ± 0.41 nm, respectively (Fig. 2a and b). Concurrently, the ζ-potentials of both remain stable within ranges of approximately − 34 mV to − 30 mV and − 35 mV to − 31 mV, respectively (Fig. S8a and b). The findings revealed that the steric hindrance repulsion induced by high-MW EPS in NaCl solution was significantly stronger than that of low-MW fractions. Compared to PS-Bare, PS-COOH formed a thicker eco-corona while maintaining a similar ζ-potential, resulting in superior dispersibility and colloidal stability. To further validate the hypothesis that high-MW EPS enhanced spatial repulsion, this study applied extended DLVO theory to calculate interaction energy between EPS-coated PS-Bare particles (initial concentration of 10 mg C L⁻¹) at neutral pH and 300 mM NaCl (Fig. S10). The results confirmed a substantially enhanced repulsive force between particles, particularly at shorter distances, where high-MW EPS induced a more pronounced spatial repulsion effect. These findings demonstrated that, in addition to electrostatic repulsion, EPS introduces short-range steric hindrance effects, with high-MW EPS contributing far more significantly than low-MW fractions.

In the CaCl2 solution, consistent with observations under NaCl conditions, the pristine and different molecular weight fractions of EPS exhibited inhibitory effects on PS-Bare and PS-COOH aggregation at lower concentrations (Fig. 3e and f). At a pristine EPS concentration of 5 mg C L⁻¹, the eco-corona thickness formed on PS-Bare and PS-COOH surfaces were 5.78 ± 0.31 nm and 7.84 ± 0.19 nm, respectively. The increase in the CCC values (PS-Bare: 38.8 mM; PS-COOH: 56.1 mM) can be attributed primarily to the steric repulsion effect induced by the eco-corona. However, the presence of Ca²⁺ may partially destabilize the system. As Ca²⁺ concentration increased, the absolute zeta potential gradually decreased (Fig. S11), leading to an increase in the Dh of PS-Bare and PS-COOH from approximately 100 nm to 1000 nm, indicating mild aggregation. Nevertheless, in the CaCl2 environment, the influence of the molecular weight on eco-corona thickness and zeta potential remained similar to that in the NaCl system (Fig. 2a and b, Fig. S8a). Compared to the control group without EPS, all EPS molecular weight fractions at 5 mg C L⁻¹ induced the formation of thicker eco-corona, thereby increasing the CCC value and ultimately enhancing the dispersion stability of PS-Bare and PS-COOH (Fig. 5a, b, d and e).

Aggregation profiles of PS-NH₂ in 350 mmol L⁻¹ NaCl or 30 mmol L⁻¹ CaCl2containing pristine and molecular weight-fractionated EPS. a, b NaCl with 5 and 10 mg C L⁻¹ EPS; c, d CaCl2 with 5 and 10 mg C L⁻¹ EPS. All measurements were performed at pH 7.0 ± 0.2.

Ca2+ mitigates steric repulsion in the presence of high-MW EPS with high-concentration via the bridging mechanism

Ca²⁺ can weaken steric repulsion through bridging effects and electric double layer compression. Notably, Ca²⁺ bridging not only promotes the adsorption of EPS on the surface of PS NPs but also induces heterogeneous aggregation. A schematic illustration of this bridging mechanism is provided in Fig. 6. The influence of this effect on the aggregation behavior of polystyrene nanoparticles depends on the concentration of EPS, the molecular weight distribution, and the properties of functional groups on the particle surface. When the EPS concentration was 10 mg C L⁻¹, the CCC and α exhibited a nonlinear relationship with molecular weight, differing significantly from the trend observed at 5 mg C L⁻¹ (Figs. 3e and f and 4). In the presence of low-MW EPS (EPS<3kDa, EPS3−10kDa, and EPS10−30kDa), the CCC of PS-Bare increased while α decreased, indicating inhibited aggregation. Conversely, when EPS30−100kDa, EPS>100kDa, and pristine EPS were present, the CCC was lower than that of the EPS-free control, and α increased, demonstrating enhanced aggregation (Fig. 4a). Among all EPS fractions, EPS>100kDa induced the most significant aggregation, suggesting that the enhanced aggregation by pristine EPS was primarily attributed to this component. The PS-COOH system exhibited a similar response pattern (Fig. 4b). Compared to PS-Bare, carboxylation significantly strengthened the Ca²⁺ bridging effect26, leading to faster aggregation of PS-COOH.

Schematic illustration of EPS adsorption and subsequent aggregation mechanisms of polystyrene nanoparticles (PS NPs). Purple and orange spheres represent negatively and positively charged PS NPs, respectively; green halos denote the adsorbed negatively charged EPS eco-corona. In NaCl, steric repulsion dominates; in CaCl2, Ca²⁺ bridging promotes aggregation of negatively charged PS NPs, while patch-charge attraction governs aggregation of positively charged PS NPs.

Low-MW EPS with low-concentration promotes PS-NH2 aggregation via changing the surface charges

The stability behavior of PS-NH2 is significantly correlated with the molecular weights and concentration of EPS (Fig. 5). At both tested concentrations, EPS>100kDa and EPS30−100kDa effectively stabilized PS-NH₂ dispersion systems. At an EPS concentration of 5 mg C L⁻¹, the pristine and the higher-MW EPS (EPS30−100kDa and EPS>100kDa) induced slight aggregation of PS-NH₂. However, when the concentration was increased to 10 mg C L⁻¹, the Dh remained stable at approximately 200 nm. In contrast, the EPS<3kD promoted rapid particle size growth. No significant differences in hydrodynamic diameters were observed between the two electrolyte systems.

Under varying concentration conditions, EPS < 3 kDa significantly enhanced the aggregation of PS-NH₂. This phenomenon cannot be fully explained by charge neutralization alone. Although adsorption of EPS < 3 kDa rendered the PS-NH₂ surface more electropositive compared to other EPS fractions, the resulting particle size growth rate exceeded that of the pristine system (Fig. 5). This enhanced aggregation of positively charged PS-NH₂ is attributed to patch-charge attraction. Despite the overall surface potential remaining positive (as confirmed by zeta potential measurements), the adsorption of negatively charged EPS moieties creates localized charge heterogeneities on the particle surface. These heterogeneous patches enable electrostatic attraction between adjacent particles, even in the absence of net charge reversal. This mechanism is well-documented in colloidal systems involving oppositely charged polymers or natural organic matter, where patch-charge interactions govern aggregation at non-neutral surface potentials54,55. Collectively, the zeta potential and aggregation kinetic results provide direct experimental evidence for the operation of patch-charge attraction in this system. These findings align with previous reports indicating that the adsorption of negatively charged natural organic matter onto positively charged nanoparticles can generate a non-uniform “patchy” charge distribution14,56. Such surface charge heterogeneity promotes particle aggregation via electrostatic attraction, confirming that the accelerated aggregation of PS-NH₂ induced by EPS < 3 kDa is driven primarily by patch-charge attraction rather than simple charge neutralization.

These results collectively demonstrate that the colloidal stability of EPS-coated nanoplastics is highly sensitive to both electrolyte type and particle surface charge. Divergent interaction pathways are observed in mono- and divalent electrolytes, leading to distinct stabilization or aggregation outcomes. Conclusions

This study demonstrates that EPS molecular weight, through its control over eco-corona thickness and surface charge heterogeneity, is a key determinant of nanoplastics colloidal stability in complex electrolyte matrices. The stabilizing effects of eco-corona on PS-Bare and PS-COOH undergo fundamental transformations depending on the electrolyte environment. In NaCl, stabilization relies on steric repulsion, with high-MW EPS providing stronger short-range repulsion than low-MW fractions. In CaCl2, the role of EPS is concentration-dependent: at low concentrations (≤ 5 mg C L⁻¹), steric stabilization prevails; at high concentrations (≥ 10 mg C L⁻¹), high-MW EPS triggers cation bridging, promoting aggregation. For positively charged PS-NH₂, colloidal stability can be precisely modulated by selecting EPS of specific molecular weight. High-MW EPS fractions (EPS > 100 kDa and EPS 30–100 kDa) effectively stabilize the suspension via combined electrostatic and steric forces, whereas low-MW EPS (EPS < 3 kDa) promotes aggregation through patch-charge attraction. Medium-MW EPS and pristine EPS act as “molecular switches,” where their concentration governs aggregation behavior by regulating the extent of charge neutralization on the nanoplastics surface.

This work establishes that the interplay between nanoplastics surface functionalization and EPS molecular weight distribution is a dual determinant of aggregation behavior. By systematically linking eco-corona properties (thickness, charge, bridging potential) to specific aggregation outcomes, our findings provide a mechanistic basis for predicting the fate of nanoplastics in natural waters where EPS composition varies spatiotemporally. From an environmental risk assessment perspective, these results imply that the same nanoplastics type may exhibit vastly different transport potentials depending on the MW profile of ambient EPS; therefore, incorporating EPS MW distribution into fate models is essential for accurate exposure assessment.

Future work should extend this mechanistic framework to EPS from diverse microbial sources (e.g., fungi, algae) and to other polymer types (e.g., polyethylene, polypropylene) to develop a more generalized interaction model. Moreover, investigating the long-term evolution of eco-corona structure and its reversibility under fluctuating solution chemistry will further improve our understanding of nanoplastics behavior in dynamic aquatic environments.
