Trends and disparities in the research and development of infectious disease vaccines from 2015 to 2025
Authors: Min Du, Weiyang Chen, Guanqiao Li, Wannian Liang, Jue Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResearch assessing the dynamic landscape of infectious disease vaccine candidates has been limited. We extracted comprehensive data on vaccine candidates through the Pharmcube system as of March 21, 2025, to analyze the latest trends and disparities in the research and development (R&D) landscape for infectious disease vaccines since 2015. The global vaccine R&D landscape comprises 919 candidates. The top three diseases targeted for vaccine R&D are COVID-19 (245 candidates), influenza (118 candidates), and HIV (68 candidates). Among these, 25% (231 candidates) are nucleic acid vaccines, followed by recombinant protein vaccines (125 candidates) and viral vector vaccines (73 candidates). More than 50% of the candidates are in the pre-phase II stage, while nearly 15% are in phase II (144 candidates) or phase III (137 candidates). Of the 36 countries or territories reporting vaccine candidates, China leads with 313 candidates, followed by the USA with 276 candidates and the UK with 63 candidates. The majority of the candidates are being developed independently or collaboratively by private companies/industry in China (289 candidates), France (30 candidates), the Republic of Korea (27 candidates), Germany (26 candidates), the USA (217 candidates), and the UK (43 candidates). The USA, France, and the Republic of Korea mainly predominantly develop mRNA vaccines, while China primarily develops recombinant protein vaccines, and the UK focuses on viral vector vaccines. Strengthening interagency and international cooperation, along with implementing supportive policies, may be effective in accelerating R&D for infectious diseases in the future.KEYWORDS: Infectious diseases, vaccines, trends, differences, research and development

Introduction

In the 21st century, a series of severe infectious disease outbreaks has emerged, including the 2003 severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS) outbreak, the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic, the 2012 Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus outbreak, the 2013–2016 Ebola epidemic in West Africa, the 2015 Zika epidemic, the Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, and the 2022 multi-country mpox outbreak.1 Although the World Health Organization (WHO) has declared that the COVID-19 pandemic is subsiding, the delayed resurgence of other respiratory diseases – such as influenza,2 Mycoplasma pneumoniae,3 and pertussis4 – has become a critical issue due to additional strain on healthcare systems and potential risk for vulnerable populations, including children. Furthermore, the burden of other infectious diseases, including dengue, Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), tuberculosis, and malaria, continues to challenge global public health, particularly in low- and lower-middle-income countries.1 For example, since the beginning of 2024, dengue has surpassed COVID-19 in the global number of deaths.5 Therefore, based on the lessons learned during the COVID-19 pandemic, sustained and collaborative efforts are essential worldwide to address the threats posed by infectious diseases.

Vaccination is an effective and efficient method for preventing infectious diseases. In May 1974, the World Health Assembly established the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) to extend the protective benefits of vaccination.6 It is estimated that since 1974, over 150 million lives have been saved, and 40% of the reduction in global infant deaths can be attributed to vaccination.6 While immunization is one of the most successful public health interventions, coverage plateaued in the decade leading up to the COVID-19 pandemic, with significant setbacks occurring in 2020 and 2021.7 According to WHO immunization coverage data from 2023, coverage has not yet returned to 2019 levels.7 Therefore, vaccination remains one of the most critical public health priorities, both during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

To address this issue, the WHO released the Global Vaccine Market Report 2024 (2024 GVMR) to enhance vaccine access and improve immunization coverage by analyzing vaccine markets.8 This report covers six key components related to vaccines: volume and financial value, manufacturing and supply, national stock-outs, vaccine-specific supply dynamics and supply security, procurement and pricing, and vaccine regulation.8 The report highlights that, as of 2023, no additional national regulatory authority has achieved a maturity level sufficient to regulate vaccine production effectively.2

Notably, advanced research and development (R&D) of vaccines has been crucial in achieving immunization coverage, particularly for emerging infectious diseases such as mpox, as well as for infectious diseases with high mortality rates for which no vaccines currently exist, such as HIV. However, there are limited studies that specifically address the landscape of R&D for infectious disease vaccines. The 2024 GVMR primarily analyzes the market status of vaccines but does not include R&D efforts related to these vaccines.8 Our previous study provided an overview of the global state of R&D on prophylactic vaccine candidates for infectious diseases globally before 2023; however, it did not encompass the latest developments following the COVID-19 pandemic in 2023 and 2024.9 The COVID-19 pandemic has likely influenced vaccine R&D for other infectious diseases. On one hand, the centralized R&D efforts for COVID-19 vaccines may have delayed the development of other vaccines. Additionally, the diversion of resources could pose significant challenges to resuming prior vaccine R&D after the pandemic. On the other hand, the R&D of COVID-19 vaccines has advanced the maturity of technologies and platforms for vaccine development, which may benefit the R&D of existing vaccines and facilitate the creation of new ones. Therefore, understanding the current state of vaccine R&D is essential for comprehending changes in vaccine development before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as for identifying the shortcomings and future directions.

With the objective of providing evidence to the governments and international organizations for vaccine R&D, rather than focusing solely on the existing vaccine market, we comprehensively analyzed the latest trends and differences in the R&D landscape for infectious disease vaccines from 2015 to 2025. Additionally, we examined differences in the number of infectious disease vaccine candidates during different periods of the COVID-19 pandemic, utilizing the most recent data from the Pharmcube system in this updated study, compared to our previous study conducted in 2023.9 Based on the hypothesis that the vaccine R&D has evolved over the past decade, particularly in 2023 and 2024 following the COVID-19 pandemic, our study aims to provide more detailed insights into the vaccine R&D landscape. This includes understanding critical aspects such as specific infectious diseases, geographical locations, and technological advancements. Ultimately, our findings may assist in formulating international and national policies and contribute to addressing key research gaps in the future.

Methods

Data source

Data on vaccine candidate pipeline for infectious diseases worldwide were obtained from the Pharmcube database. This database is curated from authoritative platforms that provide information on drugs and clinical trials, including the US National Institutes of Health-funded Clinical Trial Registry, the Chinese NMPA’s Registration and Information Disclosure Platform for Drug Clinical Studies, the Chinese Clinical Trial Register, the European Clinical Trials Registry, the Japanese Clinical Trials Registry, the World Health Organization’s International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, PubMed, Web of Science, and other sources (scientific conferences, company press releases, published reports, and investor presentations).9 As of March 21, 2025, the Pharmcube system offered comprehensive information on global vaccine candidates, including their technology platforms, geographic locations, types of developers, R&D phases, and the updated dates.10

We included vaccines that met all of the following criteria: 1) vaccines that had not yet received marketing authorization as of the cutoff date of March 21, 2025; 2) vaccines with the potential to prevent infectious diseases; and 3) vaccines that had entered into clinical development in any country in or after 2015. We excluded vaccines that had been explicitly discontinued or are in an unknown status. A total of 919 candidates were included in this analysis. The data administrator of the Pharmcube system, along with authors including Min Du and Jue Liu, verified the data quality. The flow diagram of inclusion and exclusion is shown in Figure S1.

Study design

In our cross-sectional study, we manually verified the data and subsequently categorized vaccines based on various parameters, including technology platforms, geographic locations, types of developers, R&D phases, and updated dates. Based on their mechanisms and technology platforms, all investigational vaccine candidates were classified into the following categories: recombinant protein, mRNA, RNA, DNA, inactivated, viral vector, conjugate, attenuated, virus-like particle, toxoid vaccines, and others (for agents that could not be classified into the aforementioned categories, such as live vaccines, dendritic cell vaccines, bacterial vector vaccines, etc.) or placed in the unknown group (for agents that were unclassifiable due to insufficient information).9 The geographic location was determined by the location of the developer, as provided in the Pharmcube database. For projects with partnerships, the countries of the leading developer were identified. When a developer has multiple locations or headquarters, the geographic location was designated as the primary headquarters publicly declared for vaccine R&D. For the classification of the developers, candidates were divided into three categories: “Private/industry” (for projects involving only listed or non-listed pharmaceutical companies), “Academic/non-profit” (for projects involving only universities, institutions, public welfare organizations, or government agencies), and “Cooperation” (for projects involving both types of entities).

Statistical analysis

The number of vaccines, categorized by disease, technology platform, and development phase, was illustrated using stacked column graphs. We depicted the spatial distribution of vaccine development locations on a map and highlighted geographical differences in developer types and diseases using bicyclic graphs. Trends in the number of vaccines by disease and technology platform or phase from 2015 to 2025 were represented through line graphs. Furthermore, we calculated the mean number of countries involved in partnerships for each country to assess the degree of international cooperation on vaccines. An interrupted time-series analysis (ITS) model was employed to evaluate differences in the number of vaccine candidates during various COVID-19 pandemic periods from 2015 to 2024, adjusting for year and autocorrelation (its.analysis package).11 We divided the 10-year span into three segments: the pre-period from 2015 to 2019, the pandemic period from 2020 to 2022, and the post-period from 2023 to 2024. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1).

Results

As of March 21, 2025, the global landscape of vaccine R&D included 919 candidates identified since 2015. Among these, the top three diseases targeted for vaccine development were all viral in origin: COVID-19 (245 candidates; 27%), influenza (118 candidates; 13%), and HIV (68 candidates; 7%) (Figure 1A). Additionally, 25% (231 candidates) of these were nucleic acid vaccines, followed by recombinant protein vaccines (125 candidates, 14%) and viral vector vaccines (73 candidates, 8%) (Figure 1B). Furthermore, more than 50% of the candidates are in phases prior to phase II (investigational new drug application [IND]: 8%; phase I: 38%; phase I/II: 15%), while nearly 15% were in phase II (144 candidates; 16%) or phase III (137 candidates; 15%) (Figure 1C). Among the top six diseases with the most candidates, except pneumococcal infection (conjugate: 33 candidates), nucleic acid vaccines predominated in other five diseases (Figure 2). For respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccines, no candidates across different technology platforms had entered the new drug application/biologic license application (NDA/BLA) phase. The highest number of candidates entering the phase NDA/BLA was observed in COVID-19 vaccines (18 candidates), followed by influenza vaccines (11 candidates) and pneumococcal infection (3 candidates).

A total of 36 countries or territories reported vaccine candidates. China (313 candidates) reported the highest number of candidates, followed by the United States (US, 276 candidates) and the United Kingdom (UK, 63 candidates) (Figure 3). France, Germany, and the Republic of Korea each reported between from 32 and 35 candidates. Among the countries or territories reporting more than 10 candidates, Germany had the highest average number of partnerships, with a mean of 2.43. The majority of the candidates were being developed independently or collaboratively by private companies or industry in China (289 candidates; 92%), France (30 candidates; 86%), the Republic of Korea (27 candidates; 84%), Germany (26 candidates; 81%), the US (217 candidates; 79%), and the UK (43 candidates; 68%) (Figure 4). China (9 candidates; 3%) had the lowest proportion of the candidates developed independently or collaboratively by academic or other nonprofit organizations. Except US (32 candidates; 12%), the other five countries or territories reported less than 10% of their candidates developed through cooperation, particularly China (15 candidates; 5%) and the UK (3 candidates; 5%). The geographic distribution of candidates by disease, technology platforms, and R&D phases is illustrated in Figures S2–S4. Except France (15 candidates for influenza; 56%), the other five countries or territories primarily focused on COVID-19. The US, France, and the Republic of Korea mainly developed mRNA vaccines, while China primarily focused on recombinant protein vaccines, and the UK concentrated on viral vector vaccines.

The segmented interrupted time-series analyses indicated no significant differences in the number of vaccine candidates during the pre-period (2015 to 2019), the pandemic period (2020 to 2022), and the post-period (2023 to 2024) (P = .535, Figure S5 and Figure 5), except human papilloma virus (HPV) vaccines (P = .041). The trends in the number of vaccine candidates by disease and R&D phase from 2015 to 2025 are shown in Figure S6. In phase I, the R&D for the COVID-19 vaccine began in 2020, peaked in 2022, and then started to decline. In contrast, influenza, HPV, and RSV vaccines all peaked in 2024. Figure S7 illustrates the trends in the number of vaccine candidates by disease and technology platform from 2015 to 2025. HPV and RSV showed an increased number of nucleic acid candidates in 2024 compared to previous years. Additionally, from 2022 to 2024, the nucleic acid candidates represented the predominant technology platform for influenza vaccines.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the trends and differences in the R&D landscape for infectious disease vaccines from 2015 to 2025, as well as to explore the difference in vaccine R&D during different periods of the COVID-19 pandemic. As of March 21, 2025, the global vaccine R&D pipeline included 919 candidates, reflecting both significant expansion and ongoing challenges in advancing candidates to late- stage development. The predominance of COVID-19, influenza, and HIV vaccine candidates underscores alignment with urgent health priorities, while the recent increase in influenza, HIV, HPV, pneumococcal, and RSV vaccine after 2022 suggests a gradual diversification of focus beyond COVID-19. National differences also carry important implications. China contributed the largest number of candidates, primarily through private-sector initiatives, and achieved the highest proportion of candidates in phase III or NDA/BLA. In contrast, the US, France, and the Republic of Korea focused on mRNA platforms; China emphasized recombinant protein vaccines; and the UK relied more on viral vector approaches. These patterns highlight how national innovation systems shape vaccine development pathways. Overall, the findings indicate that while global vaccine R&D capacity has grown, greater academic involvement, broader platform strategies, and sustained efforts beyond pandemic-driven surges will be essential for building long-term preparedness.

This updated study on the top three targeted diseases from 2015 to 2025 aligns with our previous overview of R&D in 2023.9 Although safe and effective COVID-19 vaccines were available before 2023, the emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) variants and the waning immune protection continue to necessitate the development of new COVID-19 vaccines.12–14 Consequently, R&D for COVID-19 vaccines remained the largest segment of vaccine research in 2023.

However, in 2024, the influenza vaccine is expected to become the primary focus of vaccine R&D. As a highly prevalent respiratory illness that consistently affects populations worldwide, the demand for influenza vaccines is significantly influenced by their variable effectiveness each year.15 From 2021 to 2023, greater attention was directed toward COVID-19 vaccine candidates rather than influenza vaccines due to the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the conclusion of the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent cessation of nonpharmaceutical interventions and behavioral changes (such as wearing masks) may lead to outbreaks of seasonal influenza.16,17 Therefore, promoting the development of new influenza vaccines in 2024 is warranted. Additionally, advancements in new platforms, particularly mRNA vaccines, suggest that next-generation influenza vaccines including mRNA and combination vaccine candidates, are anticipated in the future.15 As shown in our results, from 2022 to 2024, nucleic acid candidates were the most prominent technology platform for influenza vaccines.

Our study also demonstrated that mRNA vaccines serve as a primary technological platform for addressing other diseases, including HIV and HPV. However, most vaccine advancements have been concentrated before phase II, particularly for RSV vaccines, with no vaccine yet entering the NDA/BLA phase. RSV vaccines consistently encounter to development challenges, including the early age of infection, the ability of RSV to evade innate immunity, the history of RSV vaccine-enhanced disease, and the lack of an animal model fully permissive to human RSV infection.18 Therefore, developing a safe and effective RSV vaccine is a global health priority and a critical research objective. Our study observed an increased number of nucleic acid RSV vaccine candidates in 2024 compared to previous years. An increasing number of RSV vaccine candidates in various formats, including mRNA, are currently under development for the future.19 Overall, enhanced policy support is essential to accelerate vaccine development.

Of the 36 countries or territories, China reported the highest number of vaccine candidates, followed by the US. Our previous overview indicated that, prior to 2023, the US had reported more candidates than China.9 This study further found that HPV vaccine candidates, as well as candidates for respiratory diseases including influenza, pneumococcal infection, and RSV, were more prevalent in China than in the US. Two speculative reasons for this trend are as follows: existing 9-valent HPV vaccines are expensive and limited in availability, and developers located in China are actively accelerating the R&D of local HPV vaccines to provide sufficient and more affordable options.20 We observed that the HPV vaccine R&D from 2015 to 2024 has been continuously increasing. Additionally, developers in China may be placing greater emphasis on vaccine development for other respiratory diseases during 2023–2024 to address the potential threat posed by multiple respiratory pathogens following the lifting of COVID-19 restrictions in China in 2023. Although the differences were not statistically significant, the number of vaccine candidates targeting other respiratory pathogens increased during this period. The distinction may be attributed to the fact that HPV vaccine development is a long-term, ongoing objective, whereas respiratory diseases have received heighted attention due to the COVID-19 pandemic.20 Meanwhile, we found that the R&D efforts in China are progressing, but are infrequently conducted by academic or other nonprofit organizations. Strengthening cross-institutional cooperation in the future may facilitate information exchange, reduce costs, and expedite the translation of research findings into practical applications.

Additionally, the US, France, and the Republic of Korea primarily developed mRNA vaccines, while China focused on recombinant protein vaccines, and the UK concentrated on viral vector vaccines. Each platform offers distinct advantages. For example, nucleic acid vaccine platforms provide high flexibility for addressing pathogens with significant variability; viral vector vaccines have the potential to induce robust and long-lasting immune responses; and recombinant protein vaccines are well characterized by a well-understood safety profile, stability, and ease of manufacturing.9 Although different countries have their predominant technology platforms, the diversity of pathogens and the varying characteristics of infectious diseases at the national geographical levels underscore the importance of strengthening international cooperation, including technical exchanges and information sharing, to expedite the vaccine development process. In addition, policy environments strongly influence R&D priorities. For instance, government funding schemes, regulatory pathways, and national immunization strategies can incentivize the development of specific vaccines. Likewise, policy emphasis on diseases such as HPV in high-income countries or tuberculosis in low- and middle-income countries has helped shape research directions. Thus, both epidemiological and policy differences jointly drive vaccine development, highlighting the need for coordinated global strategies to avoid duplication and ensure the efficient allocation of resources.

Our study had several limitations. First, although we utilized the systematic, long-term, and comprehensive data from the Pharmcube database, information bias may have occurred due to delays in data updates, which could prevent the data from fully reflecting the current situation, particularly regarding the development phases. Nevertheless, the results still provide a valuable reference for the global vaccine development, and we plan to conduct more in-depth research in the future. Second, factors that may have influenced global vaccine R&D from 2015 to 2024, such as GDP, were incompletely collected. Consequently, we did not adjust for these potential factors, which may have affected our assessment of the differences in the number of vaccine candidates during various COVID-19 pandemic periods. Additionally, it is important to note that the ITS analysis only indicated a statistical difference in the number of HPV vaccine candidates across different periods, rather than a causal association or the effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on vaccine R&D.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the development of vaccine candidates has been relatively concentrated on specific diseases and countries, with distinct technological platforms varying by country. There were no significant differences in the number of vaccine candidates for most diseases across the different periods of the COVID-19 pandemic. However, vaccine development has been primarily driven by the private sector or industry, with insufficient collaboration from academic and nonprofit sectors, particularly in China. Therefore, strengthening interagency and international cooperation, along with implementing supportive policies, may be effective in accelerating R&D for infectious diseases vaccines in the future.
