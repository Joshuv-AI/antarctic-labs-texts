From comics to care: exploring the education potential of graphic novel in organ transplantation journey
Authors: Veronica Moretti, Enrica Baraldi, Patrizia Burra, Stefano Ratti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrgan transplantation profoundly enhances survival and quality of life for patients with end-stage organ failure but presents challenges such as lifelong immunosuppression, complications and psychosocial stress. The graphic novel I nuovi noi (‘Our new selves’) leverages Graphic Medicine to address these challenges, combining visual storytelling with healthcare insights. Developed collaboratively with patients, healthcare professionals and artists, the graphic novel offers an empathetic narrative that fosters understanding, improves treatment adherence and enhances communication. Based on structured interviews with 36 different healthcare professionals, this study evaluates the novel’s impact on post-transplant care, underscoring the potential of Graphic Medicine to enrich medical education and patient support frameworks. Our analyses of healthcare professionals’ opinions reveal the graphic novel’s educational potential on three levels: health professionals, patients who had transplants and the public. The findings underscore the importance of tailoring communication tools to specific populations to maximise their impact in medical education and patient care.Keywords: Graphic Medicine, medical education, health care education

Introduction

Organ transplantation represents one of the most transformative advancements in modern medicine, offering patients with end-stage organ failure a second chance at life. The procedure has evolved significantly over the decades, with advancements in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive therapies and postoperative care dramatically improving survival rates (Watson and Dark 2012; Lechler et al. 2005). Today, transplantation is not only a life-saving intervention but also a field that continually pushes the boundaries of science and medicine. The success of organ transplantation has led to its expansion across multiple organ systems, with kidney and liver transplants being the most common and extensively studied.

Particularly, kidney transplantation (KT), the most frequently performed solid organ transplant worldwide, is considered the optimal treatment for end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). ESKD is often the consequence of chronic kidney disease (CKD), a condition affecting over 850 million people globally associated with significant morbidity and mortality (Webster et al. 2017; Gupta et al. 2021). CKD progression to ESKD imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide (Lv and Zhang 2019). Moreover, early-stage CKD is often asymptomatic, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment (Wouters et al. 2015). By the time patients progress to ESKD, renal replacement therapies become essential for survival, with KT regarded as the most effective treatment option (Robinson et al. 2016). KT has witnessed significant growth in recent decades, driven by improved surgical techniques, advanced immunosuppressive therapies and enhanced postoperative care (Dominy et al. 2023; Abuazzam et al. 2023; Carcella et al. 2023). While transplantation offers improved quality of life and survival rates compared with dialysis, it presents challenges, including the necessity for lifelong adherence to immunosuppressive regimens and the adoption of specific lifestyle modifications to ensure graft survival (Hucker et al. 2017; Baker et al. 2017; Stoler et al. 2023). Post-transplant complications, such as infections, cardiovascular disease and graft rejection, further highlight the complexity of managing these patients (Stoumpos et al. 2015; Codina et al. 2021).

On the other hand, liver transplantation (LT) is a life-saving procedure for patients with end-stage liver disease (ESLD) and acute liver failure. ESLD often results from conditions such as cirrhosis, chronic hepatitis B or C infection, alcoholic liver disease and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (Lim and Kim 2008), while acute liver failure is commonly caused by drug-induced liver injury or viral hepatitis (Stravitz and Lee 2019). Hepatocellular carcinoma is another significant indication for LT, particularly in patients whose tumours meet the Milan or University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Criteria, which predict favourable outcomes post-transplant (Duffy et al. 2007; Muhammad et al. 2021; Toniutto et al. 2021). Globally, the incidence of liver disease has been rising, with liver cancer as one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths (Singal et al. 2023), emphasising the growing demand for transplantation. LT is unique in that, unlike KT, there is no equivalent to dialysis as bridging therapy, making timely intervention critical for patient survival. The procedure has seen considerable advancements in surgical techniques, organ preservation methods and post-transplant care, significantly improving survival rates (Terrault et al. 2023). Indeed, 1-year survival after LT is approximately 85–90%, while 5-year survival rates reach 70–75% (Terrault et al. 2023). However, the field faces substantial challenges, including a persistent shortage of donor organs, necessitating innovative strategies such as living-donor LT and expanded criteria donor utilisation to meet the growing demand (Khalil et al. 2023). Post-transplant management of liver recipients involves navigating complex medical challenges, including the risk of graft rejection, recurrence of the original disease and complications like infections and metabolic disorders (Agostini et al. 2023).

In light of the complexities associated with KT and LT, the fields are committed to improving graft and patient survival, expanding access to transplantation and addressing the persistent challenges of organ shortages and post-transplant care. Achieving these objectives necessitates addressing clinical uncertainties through rigorous research, equipping healthcare professionals with advanced training and systematically collecting and analysing data from transplant recipients. These efforts require not only the expertise and commitment of medical professionals but also robust collaboration with health institutions, scientific societies, research organisations and patient advocacy networks. Given the multifaceted challenges of transplantation, integrating the patient experience into care strategies emerges as a crucial approach. Understanding patients’ lived experiences, including their perceptions, challenges and emotional responses, provides valuable insights that can inform personalised care, enhance adherence and ultimately improve outcomes. By prioritising the patient’s perspective, healthcare systems can develop more holistic interventions that address not only medical needs but also the psychological and social dimensions of life after transplantation.

Patient experience—spanning from diagnosis to treatment—is indeed a vital aspect of the healthcare journey. Patient experience encompasses all interactions shaping patients’ perceptions and is heavily influenced by the organisational culture of healthcare providers (Wolf et al. 2014). In this line, narrative medicine has emerged as an approach that focuses on understanding the patient’s experience of illness by considering its psychological, social and existential dimensions alongside overall well-being (Marini 2015). Over recent years, this method has become pivotal in exploring the clinical history and daily lives of patients, offering deeper insights into their experiences and challenges (Charon 2001). Unlike traditional medical research, which prioritises rigorous scientific protocols and evidence, narrative medicine emphasises improving the patient’s ‘conditions’—a term encompassing everything patients perceive, describe and report (Charon 2022; Robertson et al. 2023).

This perspective is particularly relevant for KT and LT recipients, as they navigate complex medical, psychological and social dimensions of post-transplant life. For KT recipients, studies indicate that patients often hold a more optimistic outlook than their healthcare providers regarding various aspects of their post-transplant health, including their expected lifespan, graft longevity and their ability to manage complications and coexisting conditions (Boaz and Morgan 2014; Matas et al. 2015; Maass et al. 2019). Many KT recipients identify graft survival as the most critical outcome, often prioritising it over concerns such as weight gain, cardiovascular risks or even mortality with a functioning graft. Additionally, patients frequently express a willingness to endure significant adverse effects if it means avoiding graft loss (Mahajan et al. 2024). However, non-adherence to treatment regimens—a major issue among KT recipients—underscores the importance of understanding patient perspectives. Research suggests that around 22–30% of patients who had KT struggle with medication adherence (Patzer et al. 2016; Denhaerynck et al. 2005), a challenge that requires a collaborative approach and holistic consideration of factors hindering compliance (Vrijens et al. 2012; De Pasquale et al. 2016). Discrepancies between patients’ and healthcare providers’ perceptions of adherence further highlight the need to prioritise the patient perspective in addressing these barriers to effective treatment. LT recipients face similar challenges, with additional nuances tied to the unique aspects of liver disease. Patients often report significant anxiety and depression stemming from the chronic nature of their condition and the uncertainty surrounding their prognosis (Kaplan et al. 2023). Furthermore, LT recipients frequently express concerns about the recurrence of their underlying liver disease, including conditions like hepatitis or hepatocellular carcinoma, adding an additional layer of psychological stress (Kimbell and Murray 2015). These complexities underscore the importance of understanding LT recipients’ perspectives to address their medical and psychosocial needs comprehensively. In this context, narrative medicine plays a crucial role in identifying and addressing the factors influencing treatment adherence and overall well-being for both KT and LT recipients.

Despite the effectiveness of narrative medicine in placing patients’ stories at the centre of care, certain dimensions of illness—particularly those related to visual, emotional and sensory experiences—risk being overlooked when relying solely on verbal narratives. While powerful, verbal language does not always fully capture the complexity of an experience that profoundly and intricately involves the body, emotions and interpersonal relationships. In this context, we propose aligning narrative medicine with an approach that, in recent years, has provided a novel channel for narrating and visualising illness stories: Graphic Medicine (GM). GM—the intersection of comics and healthcare, a term coined in 2012 by British physician and illustrator Ian Williams—emerges as a promising tool of narrative medicine. Originating as a niche field, GM leverages the power of visual storytelling to address complex medical topics in an engaging and accessible manner (Maatman et al. 2022; Rakower and Hallyburton 2022; Green 2015). GM operates at three critical levels: educating the general population about medical and healthcare topics, enhancing awareness and communication skills among healthcare professionals and empowering patients to better understand and manage their conditions (Maatman et al. 2022). This approach fosters a deeper understanding of medical concepts, bridges communication gaps and enhances adherence to therapeutic regimens (Green 2015; de Rothewelle 2019; Myers et al. 2019; Tsao and Yu 2016). Previous research has demonstrated the effectiveness of graphic novels in medical education and patient communication, as highlighted by their application in fields such as anatomy education and body donation awareness (Moretti et al. 2023; De Stefano et al. 2023). The comics medium also offers distinctive narrative strategies particularly suited to healthcare communication, such as the ability to break down complex information into sequential visual units, the interplay of words and images to convey layered meanings and the use of dialogue to give patients a voice. These features are effectively employed in works like Con-Ciencia Médica by Monica Lalanda, which translates clinical dilemmas into accessible narratives, or in MK Czerwiec’s Taking Turns, where patient and caregiver perspectives emerge through dialogic exchanges that highlight the relational dimensions of care.

Additionally, building on the potential of GM to enhance awareness about body donation (De Stefano et al. 2023), the University of Bologna’s Department of Biomedical and Neuromotor Sciences and Department of Sociology and Business Law collaborated with the Academy of Fine Arts of Bologna to create the graphic novel Il primo paziente (‘The First Patient’) (Ratti et al. 2024). This innovative work combined medical science with artistic storytelling to demystify the process and significance of postmortem body donation. Another example of the use of GM for addressing patient care is the graphic novel Pancreas: Biografia a fumetti di un organo (‘Pancreas: comics biography of an organ’) of the series Graphic Anatomy, which focuses on the experiences of individuals living with pancreatic disorders (Ratti and Moretti 2024; Moretti et al. 2025). These endeavours underscore the efficacy of GM as a tool for medical education and public engagement, highlighting its role in addressing complex and sensitive topics within healthcare.

As mentioned above, the journey of patients who had KT and LT extends beyond the operating room, encompassing medical, psychological and social dimensions. Indeed, recipients often navigate profound shifts in identity and might also face societal stigma related to their condition. Under this light, the GM novel I nuovi noi1 (‘Our new selves’) was developed to address kidney and liver transplant recipients’ emotional, psychological and educational needs in the post-transplant phase (Voglino et al. 2020) (figure 1, available in supplementary material 1). I nuovi noi integrates visual learning theories and empathetic storytelling to create an innovative educational resource tailored to patients who had transplants and healthcare providers.

The project I nuovi noi was conceived to leverage the unique strengths of the comics medium—immediacy, brevity and accessibility—qualities often absent in traditional institutional communication. By using the ‘hyperbole’ typical of graphic storytelling, the creators visually encapsulated the concept of ‘constant dialogue with one’s body’, employing amplified bodily features or anthropomorphised organs as visual metaphors to render internal experiences visible and emotionally resonant. The visual metaphor of a patient ‘talking to their new kidney’ was met with enthusiastic approval during early reviews of the script and artwork. Author Voglino also highlighted the creative challenges involved in presenting critical healthcare concepts with a blend of precision and subtle humour, catering not just to readers but also to communication professionals, clinicians and experienced patients. He drew from his own experience as a transplant recipient and collaborated closely with illustrator Vittori to ensure fidelity to real-life scenarios and themes. Each detail—from text to illustrations—underwent meticulous scrutiny by all stakeholders, with the final product earning widespread appreciation for its effectiveness in healthcare communication (figure 2, available in supplementary material 1)2.

The production process of I nuovi noi unfolded in three key phases. The first involved presenting the concept, including the storyline, character designs and sample pages. Next came the completion and thorough review of the script and storyboard, ensuring precise alignment of text and imagery. Finally, a ‘quality control’ phase polished the finished story with colour, lettering and additional content. This step-by-step methodology, adapted from standard editorial practices, proved ideal for creating a graphic novel in the complex healthcare domain. Its characters, Luca and Gabriella, embody the different experiences that organ recipients can face, providing readers with relatable scenarios that resonate on both emotional and practical levels. Preliminary feedback from transplant specialists and patients highlights the graphic novel’s efficacy in enhancing understanding and promoting adherence. The narrative’s accessible language, coupled with its ability to illustrate complex medical concepts, sets it apart from traditional educational materials. Furthermore, its potential to foster a stronger doctor–patient relationship underscores the value of integrating GM into transplant care pathways.

The present work aims to explore the perspectives of healthcare professionals who have engaged with I nuovi noi to assess their opinions on the utility of such a tool for supporting patients during kidney and liver post-transplant pathways. Qualitative research has proven instrumental in understanding the lived experiences of transplant recipients. This approach focuses on exploring the psychological, social and practical dimensions of post-transplant life through methods such as interviews and participant observation. Qualitative research provides valuable insights into adherence barriers, the impact of transplantation on quality of life and the dynamics of patient-healthcare provider relationships. It is not a new concept in the context of KT and LT. In fact, qualitative methods have been previously used to evaluate patients who had KT and LT both before and after interventions (Tshimologo et al. 2022; Jayanti et al. 2013; Strauss et al. 2022; Rehsmann 2022). Incorporating qualitative findings into patient care strategies has the potential to enhance adherence and improve outcomes by addressing patients’ needs. By examining the development, application and reception of I nuovi noi, this article aims to contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of graphic novels in medical education and patient care. As the field of GM continues to evolve, its integration into healthcare practice offers a unique opportunity to improve outcomes and enrich patient experience.

Material and methods

The aim of this study was to gather detailed and in-depth feedback from healthcare professionals involved in the distribution and use of the graphic novel I nuovi noi, with a specific focus on their practical experience and the perception of the messages conveyed.

The study addressed three primary research questions:

What are the healthcare professionals’ opinions regarding the graphic novel in terms of its validity, accuracy and overall effectiveness?

To address the research objectives, a qualitative study was conducted. Structured interviews were carried out between October and December 2021 online. The interview scheme and the declaration of informed consent are reported in supplementary material 2. The participants for the qualitative interviews were purposefully identified by the research team, with the selection based on their prior engagement in using the graphic novel with patients. These individuals were chosen due to their direct experience with the material, which positioned them as key informants capable of providing rich, detailed insights into its implementation and perceived impact. This targeted approach ensured that the study captured perspectives that were both relevant and grounded in practical application.

The sample consisted of 36 healthcare professionals from diverse regions of Italy, encompassing the North, Centre and South. The participants included 24 transplant specialists and 12 nurses (see details in supplementary material 2). The inclusion of nurses in the sample is particularly relevant given their pivotal role in the transplant care pathway. From the moment patients are placed on the waiting list to the post-transplant phase, specialised transplant nurses act as key intermediaries, providing medical guidance, emotional support and continuity of care. Their involvement is essential not only for the coordination of clinical procedures but also for fostering patient adherence to treatment and facilitating communication between different healthcare professionals. Given this centrality in patient care, their perspectives offer invaluable insights into the emotional and organisational dimensions of transplant management. Their areas of expertise primarily focused on organ transplants, with a majority involved in kidney transplants and a smaller proportion specialising in liver transplants. This distribution reflects the multidisciplinary nature of transplant teams and their geographic variability within the Italian healthcare system.

The interview guide was specifically developed for this study, with questions designed to explore the participants’ experiences and perspectives in alignment with the research objectives.

Questions focused on the following specific topics (see box 1):

Interview topics

General impressions: opinions on the project and the graphic novel; the role of the graphic novel in the context of transplant centres’ efforts to educate and inform patients about compliance and lifestyle.

The analysis was conducted following the principles of Grounded Theory, an inductive approach particularly suited for exploring under-researched areas and generating theory from data (Glaser et al. 1968; Charmaz 2003). Grounded Theory was deemed especially appropriate given the scarcity of prior research investigating the use of graphic novels in healthcare education and practice. This methodological choice allowed for the emergence of themes directly from the data, ensuring a nuanced and contextually sensitive interpretation (see table 1).

Process of analysis

This process also included the development of a central sensitising concept: the graphic novel as a mediating tool for patient socialisation and education (Glaser 1978).

Throughout the analysis, constant comparison was employed to iteratively compare data, codes and categories, ensuring internal consistency and theoretical saturation (Glaser and Strauss 1967). The use of memo-writing facilitated the documentation of analytical decisions, reflections and emerging theoretical insights, enhancing the rigour of the analysis.

To enhance the validity of the study findings and achieve a comprehensive understanding of the phenomena (Patton 1999), the researchers employed ‘investigator triangulation’. This method, as described by Denzin (2007), involves the engagement of multiple researchers in the same study to provide diverse observations and interpretations. The involvement of three researchers ensured both the corroboration of findings and the incorporation of varied perspectives, thereby broadening the scope of understanding of the phenomenon (Denzin 2016). To ensure a systematic, communicable and transparent coding process (Bauer et al. 2000; O’Brien et al. 2014), intercoder reliability was tested by having researchers independently code the same section of a transcript and subsequently compare their results.

Facilitating therapeutic engagement

The graphic novel emerges as a powerful tool for promoting compliance and adherence among patients who had transplants, addressing a fundamental challenge in postoperative care. Compliance encompasses not only the regular intake of prescribed medications but also adherence to follow-up visits, lifestyle modifications and the broader educational framework required for managing chronic conditions. This tool effectively bridges the gap between initial patient–provider discussions and long-term self-management, ensuring critical messages are reinforced in a format that is both engaging and accessible. Participants highlighted the role of the graphic novel in reiterating key points discussed during consultations, making it an integral component of their communication strategy. As some respondents noted,

we reiterate key concepts from the very first meetings. (interviewed 06)

I distribute it during consultations, and it helps reinforce the concepts already explained. (interviewed 12)

By embedding these concepts into an engaging narrative, the graphic novel enables patients to revisit crucial information at their own pace, reducing the cognitive overload often experienced during clinical interactions. Some interviewees underscored its impact by stating,

therapeutic compliance is crucial, and this graphic novel helps reinforce these aspects even after discussions. (interviewed 32)

it helps patients internalize important notions like regular medication intake. (interviewed 04)

Furthermore, the narrative structure and visual appeal of the graphic novel cater to diverse learning preferences, making it a versatile educational tool. Unlike traditional brochures or verbal instructions, the medium facilitates a deeper engagement with the material, fostering a sense of responsibility and self-awareness in patients. This dual role of reinforcing therapeutic adherence while supporting patient education aligns with contemporary approaches to patient-centred care, which prioritise accessible and meaningful communication. Beyond its value as an educational and engagement tool for patients, the graphic novel also serves as a reflective instrument for healthcare professionals. By portraying the lived experiences of patients, the graphic novel offers a unique lens through which clinicians and caregivers can examine their own practices, attitudes and communication strategies. This reflective dimension underscores the dual function of the medium—not only as a tool for patient education but also as a catalyst for professional introspection and growth. Several participants acknowledged the impact of the graphic novel on their own perspectives. Some remarked,

even we as professionals have reflected on reading the graphic novel. (interviewed 16)

it made me think about parts of the patient experience I might not always focus on. (interviewed 28)

highlighting how the medium prompts a reconsideration of their approaches to care. The narrative and visual elements of the graphic novel provide an immersive experience, encouraging healthcare providers to empathise more deeply with the emotional and practical challenges faced by their patients.

A particularly compelling insight from the interviews pointed to how the graphic novel has influenced communication strategies within clinical settings. As one respondent noted,

it made us reconsider how we communicate with patients. (interviewed 09)

it made me think we often treat patients in the same way, but we should better distinguish between those who come to transplantation acutely and those who have a chronic disease. (interviewed 33)

This self-reflection is critical in fostering patient-centred care, as it allows professionals to identify potential gaps in their approach to delivering information and emotional support. By engaging with the medium, clinicians are prompted to rethink how they can convey complex medical concepts in a way that is not only comprehensible but also sensitive to the patient’s emotional and psychological state. In essence, the graphic novel acts as a bridge between the technical and humanistic aspects of healthcare. It provides professionals with an opportunity to step back and reevaluate their role, not just as medical experts but as partners in the patient journey.

Adapting to post-transplant experience

The graphic novel plays a pivotal role in supporting patients’ psychological and social adjustment after a transplant, emphasising both the physical and emotional transformations they face. This ‘socialization to therapy and routines’ includes integrating new habits and accepting changes to their corporeality. As one professional noted,

the scene of the scar was particularly appreciated by patients. (interviewed 05)

highlighting how visual narratives help patients process the physical realities of their post-transplant bodies. Similarly, another stated that the graphic novel,

helps patients better understand post-transplant body changes. (interviewed 21)

facilitated acceptance and reduced emotional distress. Beyond physical transformation, the novel normalises lifestyle adjustments, addressing common patient concerns about activities such as travelling, keeping pets or engaging in social habits like alcohol consumption. The restriction on alcohol, in particular, can be difficult for some patients to accept, leading them to repeatedly question whether they can resume their previous habits. For instance, a healthcare professional reflected on a patient who had a liver transplant who, through the narrative, asked whether they could drink champagne again. This example illustrates how the novel helps patients navigate these changes, contextualising lifestyle modifications within their new health realities and fostering a better understanding of the long-term implications of their medical condition. Another practitioner noted the relevance of discussing protective measures like mask-wearing, linking this to broader themes of adjusting domestic routines. As one participant suggested,

patients would benefit from a different, more intensive approach, with education tailored not only to medication but also to lifestyle and the acceptance of their new reality. (interviewed 01)

By making these abstract challenges tangible and relatable, the graphic novel serves as a tool for guiding patients toward adapting to life after transplantation with greater confidence and clarity.

The graphic novel effectively addresses the complexities of adapting to life after a transplant, emphasising the psychological and physical challenges patients face. One notable aspect is the socialisation to a ‘new biography’, which includes reshaping one’s identity and routine in light of significant physical and lifestyle changes. For example, while the novel touches on body image through references to scars, participants suggested a deeper exploration of this theme, encompassing fears related to surgery, the duration of hospitalisation and the technical aspects of the transplant process. One interviewee noted,

There are a couple of references to the scar, but we could delve further into this aspect, including details about the surgical moment and hospitalization. (interviewed 36)

The graphic novel’s ability to resonate with the diverse experiences of patients who had transplants was frequently highlighted:

Reading it, I realized how different patients’ experiences are. For some, the transplant completely changes their life; for others, it doesn’t. (interviewed 17)

One day, she’s scaling mountains and doing athletics; the next, she wakes up with a new liver. It’s a sudden, total transformation. (interviewed 10)

This narrative helps to normalise the varied journeys of post-transplant adaptation, allowing patients to feel represented. The relatability of the characters also plays a pivotal role in fostering connection and engagement. The male protagonist, Luca, is portrayed as an everyday person:

He’s not the typical athletic guy you see on TV or in movies. He has a goatee, he’s not stylishly dressed… he seems like one of us. (interviewed 22)

This grounded portrayal helps patients feel closer to the story and less alienated by unattainable ideals. As some participants summarised,

The fact that he’s not perfect or ‘beautiful’ makes him more credible. This helps patients identify with him and feel less distant from the narrative. (interviewed 04)

The character of Gabriella, the transplant recipient, is well-crafted, and patients can easily relate to her. She’s not like movie characters, all tall, blonde, and blue-eyed. (interviewed 28)

Beyond addressing lifestyle and therapy, the graphic novel underscores the importance of immunosuppressive therapy and adherence to routines. One impactful scene involves a nurse commending a patient for his punctuality with medication and medical check-ups:

You’re always on time; well done. (interviewed 29)

This message subtly reinforces the importance of routine without overwhelming patients with technical details, such as specific medication regimens. By combining relatable characters, insightful narratives and practical advice, the graphic novel offers a holistic approach to supporting patients as they navigate their post-transplant lives.

Challenges and targeted impact

The graphic novel has been highlighted as a particularly effective and inclusive tool, offering unique advantages in patient education and support. Its accessibility has proven valuable in scenarios where traditional reading materials are impractical. As one healthcare professional noted,

It’s particularly useful for patients who can’t hold a booklet, for example, or for those who simply don’t have their glasses with them in the hospital. (interviewed 02)

such representation helps patients feel seen and understood, contributing to a stronger sense of inclusion and care. Young patients, in particular, respond positively to the graphic novel, often engaging with it enthusiastically. One professional shared,

When we give out these booklets, patients feel considered. Especially younger ones, who read it with interest and often give positive feedback. They even make light jokes, like saying, ‘Someone else cares about us’. (interviewed 06)

This sense of being attended to is reinforced by the format itself, which combines entertainment with education in a way that is both dynamic and approachable. Unlike more rigid and schematic educational materials, such as dietary plans or clinical pathways, the graphic novel’s vibrant and immediate storytelling captures attention and integrates seamlessly into patient education programmes:

I nuovi noi is an additional tool, but it’s different. It’s more lively, immediate, and engaging because of its comic format. It’s not as rigid and structured as the other materials we provide. (interviewed 18)

By presenting information in a more human and accessible manner, the graphic novel serves as an effective supplement to traditional educational methods, adding depth and relatability to the patient’s experience. The graphic novel approach has garnered praise from healthcare professionals for its clarity, empathetic tone and effectiveness in capturing attention and aiding the retention of essential messages. The relatable portrayal of characters has been identified as a crucial strength, fostering patient identification with the protagonists and thereby enhancing the credibility of the conveyed messages. However, its utility is not without limitations, particularly regarding generational differences among patients. While younger patients appear to resonate more strongly with the medium, finding it both relatable and engaging, healthcare professionals acknowledge that older adults often struggle to connect with the visual and narrative style of the comic. As some specialists noted,

for older adults, the comic is less effective: something different, perhaps a visual format, is needed. (interviewed 25)

it’s more familiar to those accustomed to a visual style but less suited to older adults. (interviewed 27)

Additionally, professionals recognise that the graphic novel could further expand its scope to address themes often overlooked in healthcare communication. For instance, the desire for parenthood, pregnancy and the broader topic of sexuality remains underexplored, particularly among younger patients. One practitioner emphasised,

For younger people, topics like parenthood or pregnancy are highly relevant. Sexuality, which is part of everyday life, could also be addressed in relation to practical issues like infection risks. Reading about these could encourage patients to ask questions they might not feel comfortable raising directly. (interviewed 30)

These insights highlight the dual challenge and opportunity of refining the graphic novel to meet the diverse needs of its audience. By addressing such gaps, the medium could further solidify its role as an innovative tool for patient education and support, offering targeted impact across varying demographic and psychosocial dimensions.

Discussion

The comic book I nuovi noi represents the first Italian experience of an interdisciplinary graphic novel created with the aim of producing awareness in all the actors involved in organ transplantation journey. Indeed, this graphic novel has been developed merging together the experience of professionals and patients with the result of an informative narrative fiction which represents the more characteristic challenges that patients who had transplants have to face in their adaptation to their new selves.

The qualitative analyses of the health professionals’ opinions exposed to this comic reveal the educational potential of graphic novels in organ transplantation journeys on three specific levels. The overarching concepts that emerged underline the role of this communicative tool in facilitating the therapeutic engagement, in adapting to post-transplant experience and in challenging the communication regarding this field among different demographic groups. These three general concepts can be aligned respectively to the education potential of health professionals, patients who had transplants and general population categories. The interviews show that this graphic novel could have an impact directly on health professional category facilitating their work with the patients at both communicative and practical levels. Therapeutic compliance is indeed one of the main challenges in all chronic medical conditions, including organ transplantation (Kisielska et al. 2023; Martin et al. 2005), and the role of health professionals is pivotal in helping patients to understand and accept their new condition (Jawoniyi et al. 2018). For this reason, the development and continuous update of communicative skills is essential for a right and effective doctor–patient communication (Berman and Chutka 2016). Nevertheless, health professionals are exposing themselves to an increasing level of responsibilities and technical and non-technical knowledge requests that could evolve in distress and burnout (Batanda 2024; Northwood et al. 2021; Rubin et al. 2021). New accessible educational tools, such as comics, could be an additional and loose way to support medical education, especially for soft skills (Myers et al. 2019; Tsao and Yu 2016) without overloading their work. The change of perspective induced by reading a comic book with patients’ angle is also a powerful and useful tool able to access trivial, but relevant aspects of patients’ lives who had transplants, that during everyday work could be forgotten or taken for granted (Bachini and Ghobrial 2025). Therefore, even if the main target of this graphic novel is patients, it might have an educational outcome also for medical professionals creating a virtuous cycle and an alliance between these two categories. An alliance among patients and healthcare professionals is considered to play a key role in therapeutic and lifestyle compliance (Velasco-Durántez et al. 2023; Stubbe 2018).

Surely the main goal of this graphic novel is to address patients who had transplant education. As a matter of fact, the interviews underline its potential in the socialisation and support of patients in the adaptation to a new biography, corporeity and routine through an immediate and educational narrative format. Patients’ awareness-raising through comics has been proposed in different healthcare realities (Alemany-Pagès et al. 2022; McNicol 2017; De Stefano et al. 2023) and approaches (Ratti et al. 2024; Michielutte et al. 1992) always considering the limitations related to different clinical and social subcategories in relation to the communicative efficacy of the tool. The encouragement in the change of life induced by organ transplantation could be very challenging, especially in specific patients’ categories who have to face a lifelong adaptation, clashing both with the feelings of guilt, sometimes present, and with the possible drastic change in their routine and bodies (Carter et al. 2024). The qualitative analyses make stand out the point of view of health professionals who have specific experience with patients who had transplants, in order to highlight the potentialities and limitations of the future impact on patient education. More in detail, the representation of characters as everyday people, who embed the principal aspects of psychological and physical post-transplantation life changes, could be a way to help patients feel represented, therefore active protagonists of their new lives, promoting a positive attitude towards their new condition. At the same time, basic and useful medical routine information is shown to give practical tips and help to patients. Indeed, many patients who had transplants reported that their main issues were usually related to basic adaptation to new life routine and rules (Nielsen et al. 2019; Sheikhalipour et al. 2018). Finally, the education potential of patients is also based on the creation of an informal trust agreement with healthcare professionals in order to explain to the patients that post-transplant life is a teamwork of all the community involved in it. Only a holistic and collaborative medical approach has been shown to be effective in the care and management of patients who had transplants (Kalariya et al. 2023).

The third level of education is the broader one, therefore the general population. GM has been used in different contexts and it has been shown to be able to reach many and different groups of populations due to its immediate and simple accessibility (De Stefano et al. 2023). It appears that awareness medical comics could be strategical for the categories of young people, who could sympathise with this medium, allowing the access of specific medical themes that normally could be considered as taboo. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that comics are an effective medium across various populations (Rakower and Hallyburton 2022); however, young people and young adults, being the primary audience, might experience a greater impact (Spiegel et al. 2013). At the same time, people with specific physical limitations could also benefit from a light and accessible education tool. Therefore, both the physical and conceptual characteristics of this tool of communication have an impact on its potentialities and effectives. Nevertheless, it seems that comics could not be considered as the best tool for all the categories, especially for elderly patients who could have limitations in approaching graphic novels. These insights testify to the strength and limitations of scientifically validated medical graphic novels in the public, underlining the need for their test and validation in different specific subcategories.

Overall, this work underlines the potential of collaborative, interdisciplinary and scientifically validated projects aimed at raising awareness in specific healthcare contexts. Specifically, the use of graphic novel could be effective in the education of specific demographic categories and populations, creating a positive reinforcement between different groups. The understanding of the potentialities and the limitations of a specific education and communication tool is therefore essential to reach the right targets and to evaluate its impact especially in the medical field.

Limitation of the study

This study acknowledges several limitations that require caution in interpreting the findings and suggest future research directions. First, as in qualitative research, the sample is not representative of the broader population. Participants were selected based on their involvement with the graphic novel, providing insights into their perceptions rather than generalisable conclusions. This limitation is inherent to qualitative methodologies, which prioritise depth over statistical representativeness. Second, focusing on healthcare professionals’ perceptions prevents measuring the graphic novel’s actual impact on patient compliance or communication. While participants noted its benefits, longitudinal studies are needed to assess its true effectiveness. Future research could evaluate whether exposure improves medication adherence, doctor–patient interactions or lifestyle adjustments. Third, the study excludes patient perspectives. Understanding their engagement, reflections and behavioural impact would enrich the findings. A planned study will explore this aspect to enhance understanding of the tool’s effectiveness. Finally, this research is a localised case study. While contributing to knowledge on graphic novels in healthcare, findings are limited by participant characteristics, healthcare systems and cultural context. Broader studies in diverse settings are needed for validation.
