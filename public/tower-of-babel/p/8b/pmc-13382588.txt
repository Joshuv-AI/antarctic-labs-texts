Effects of gelatin/tea tree oil composite coating on postharvest quality and volatile flavor regulation in strawberry fruits
Authors: Xu Liu, Yanyan Zheng, Mengqing Lu, Yiting Ren, Hua Chen, Tianyu Li, Kunyu Sun, Jing Zong, Jiejie Tao, Yuelan Liu, Baogang Wang, Jinhua Zuo, Yunxiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrawberries are highly susceptible to postharvest microbial infection, leading to rapid quality loss. This study developed a gelatin-based film containing tea tree essential oil. The film showed increased opacity, improved mechanical strength, and enhanced barrier properties. Strawberries were stored at 20 ± 1 °C and 85–95% RH for 8 days. The coating reduced decay, maintained firmness, and limited weight loss. It suppressed the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, including superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide, as well as malondialdehyde, delayed the decline of ascorbic acid and total phenolics, and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, and peroxidase. In addition, the coating slowed the reduction of soluble solids and titratable acidity. Volatile metabolomics analysis indicated increased levels of sweet- and fruity-associated aroma compounds and reduced accumulation of fatty- and vegetable-like odor compounds. Overall, the coating delayed decay and preserved flavor quality, showing strong potential for strawberry preservation.Keywords: Gelatin, Tea tree essential oil, Strawberry, Flavor quality, Volatile metabolomics

Introduction

Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa Duch.) is one of the most economically important fruits of the Rosaceae family and is highly valued by consumers for its attractive appearance, characteristic aroma, and pleasant taste (Benashvili et al., 2025). The flavor quality of strawberry is primarily determined by the combined effects of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and sugar-acid balance, both of which strongly influence consumer preference (Fan et al., 2021). However, strawberries are highly perishable due to their soft texture and high moisture content, making them extremely susceptible to mechanical damage and microbial infection during postharvest storage and transportation. These factors accelerate decay and flavor deterioration, often leading to substantial losses in marketability and economic value (Xing et al., 2025). Therefore, developing effective strategies to extend shelf life while maintaining flavor quality and food safety remains a major objective in postharvest preservation research.

In recent years, functional preservation films and coatings based on natural biopolymers, including proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids, have received increasing attention due to their environmental compatibility, safety, and sustainability. Current research in green food packaging mainly focuses on incorporating natural antimicrobial agents into polymer matrices to develop composite films or coatings with both antimicrobial and preservation functions. Gelatin, a water-soluble protein derived from animal skin and bone, exhibits excellent film-forming ability, transparency, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, making it a widely used base material in eco-friendly food packaging (Ma et al., 2026). However, gelatin films exhibit relatively limited antimicrobial and antioxidant activities, which restrict their application in fruit preservation. Therefore, incorporating plant-derived bioactive compounds with natural antimicrobial and antioxidant properties to enhance their functional performance has become an important research direction for gelatin-based preservation materials (Guo et al., 2024). For instance, carvacrol nanoemulsion incorporated into gelatin matrices significantly inhibited fungal infection in fresh-cut navel oranges while preserving VOC content and reducing flavor deterioration (Xie et al., 2025). Similarly, gelatin films containing chitosan-ferulic acid-TiO₂ nano-hybrid particles effectively suppressed mold infection in strawberries and partially preserved desirable fruity aroma characteristics (Xu et al., 2024).

Plant essential oils, natural volatile compounds extracted from plant tissues such as flowers, leaves, and fruits, possess strong antimicrobial and antioxidant properties and have attracted considerable interest in food preservation (Valarezo, Ledesma-Monteros, Jaramillo-Fierro, Radice, & Meneses, 2025). Previous studies have demonstrated that plant essential oils, such as lemon essential oil, clove essential oil, and cinnamon essential oil, exhibit excellent antimicrobial activity and effectively extend the shelf life of strawberries (Hassan et al., 2025; Piechowiak & Skóra, 2023; Yang, Zhang, Xiao, & Dong, 2025). Among various essential oils, tea tree essential oil (TTO), derived from Melaleuca alternifolia, is rich in terpene derivatives and exhibits strong broad-spectrum antimicrobial and antioxidant activities, making it a promising natural alternative to synthetic preservatives (Yue et al., 2020). Importantly, TTO has been classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, supporting its safe use in food preservation (Hammer, Carson, Riley, & Nielsen, 2006). Previous studies have shown that TTO fumigation significantly inhibited the growth of Botrytis cinerea in strawberries and reduced the loss of soluble solids, thereby helping to maintain flavor quality (Shao, Wang, Xu, & Cheng, 2013). Moreover, antimicrobial coatings based on quaternary ammonium chitosan combined with TTO showed strong preservation effects by reducing fruit decay and maintaining ascorbic acid levels (Liu et al., 2025). Collectively, these studies suggest that TTO has significant potential for preserving fruit flavor and nutritional quality.

Although gelatin and tea tree essential oil have been studied in the context of film-based preservation, systematic investigations on the effects of gelatin/tea tree essential oil films on the maintenance of strawberry flavor quality and the regulation of volatile metabolites remain scarce. In particular, whether the volatile components of tea tree essential oil alter the intrinsic aroma profile of strawberries has not yet been thoroughly elucidated. Therefore, this study aims to develop and characterize gelatin/TTO composite films prepared by incorporating TTO into a gelatin matrix following ultrasonic dispersion, and to investigate their potential for maintaining strawberry quality during storage. The physicochemical properties of the films were characterized, and the effects of coating treatment on strawberry decay, texture, weight loss, reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism, antioxidant capacity, and sugar-acid-related flavor quality were systematically evaluated. In addition, volatile metabolomics was performed to elucidate the regulatory effects of gelatin/TTO coating on key aroma compounds, providing theoretical support for the application of gelatin/TTO films in delaying strawberry decay and maintaining or improving fruit flavor quality.

Materials and methods

Materials

Gelatin (Bloom value 225) was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Tea tree essential oil (TTO, 100%) and glycerol (>99%) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (China). Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa cv. ‘Hongyan’) fruits were harvested from a commercial plantation in Changping District, Beijing, China. Fruits free from mechanical damage, uniform in size and color, and at approximately 70% maturity were selected and transported to the laboratory on the same day.

Preparation of gelatin film and gelatin/TTO composite film

Gelatin powder (1.0 g) was dissolved in 100 mL distilled water with stirring at 50 °C until fully solubilized. Glycerol (30%, w/w of gelatin) and Tween-80 (3%) were then added and mixed thoroughly, followed by ultrasonic treatment for 1 h to obtain the gelatin solution. Tea tree essential oil (0.5%, v/v) was incorporated into the gelatin solution and homogenized using a high-speed shear disperser at 15,000 rpm for 3 min to form a TTO-gelatin emulsion, followed by ultrasonic degassing for 1 h.

The gelatin solution and TTO-gelatin emulsion were separately cast onto circular plastic dishes (15 cm in diameter) and dried at room temperature for 2 days, followed by equilibration in a sealed desiccator (25 ± 1 °C, 50% RH) for 2 days before peeling. The resulting films were designated as M (gelatin film) and ME (gelatin-TTO composite film).

Effects of film coating on strawberry physicochemical properties

Fruit treatment

Strawberries were randomly assigned to three groups: CK (no coating), M (gelatin film), and ME (gelatin/tea tree essential oil film). The CK group received no coating treatment, whereas the M and ME groups were evenly coated with gelatin film and gelatin/tea tree essential oil film on the fruit surface, respectively. Strawberries were placed in commercial plastic clamshell boxes with dimensions of 30 × 20 cm. A custom-made acrylic sheet (35 × 25 cm) was used to fabricate film sheets, which were then applied to fully cover the strawberry surface inside the boxes. Samples were collected on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8. On day 0, only the CK group was sampled, with three replicates of 15 strawberries each (45 fruits in total). From day 2 onward, all three groups were sampled, with three replicates per group per time point and 15 strawberries per replicate. A total of 585 strawberries were used in the experiment. All samples were stored at 20 ± 1 °C and 85–95% relative humidity. Physicochemical parameters were measured every 2 days, with all measurements performed in triplicate and results expressed as mean values.

Firmness

Firmness was measured using a texture analyzer equipped with a P/2 probe (2 mm diameter). Three puncture points per fruit were tested (nine measurements total). Measurement parameters were: speed 2 mm·s−1, penetration depth 10 mm, and trigger force 10 N.

ROS-related compounds

The contents of H₂O₂, O₂−•, and MDA were determined using commercial assay kits (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Total phenolics

Total phenolic content (TPC) was measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Garimella, Jaddu, & Pradhan, 2025). Two grams of freeze-dried strawberry tissue powder were extracted with 20 mL of 70% ethanol by grinding and ultrasonic treatment for 60 min. The extract was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. A 0.5 mL aliquot of the supernatant was diluted with 4.5 mL distilled water, mixed with 0.5 mL Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 1.5 mL 20% sodium carbonate, and incubated at 75 °C for 10 min. After cooling, absorbance was measured at 760 nm, and results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per 100 g dry weight (DW).

Ascorbic acid

Ascorbic acid content was determined using a modified 2,6-dichlorophenol indophenol (DCIP) titration method (AOAC International, 2023). Freeze-dried strawberry tissue powder equivalent to 10 g fresh weight was used and homogenized with 20 g·L−1 oxalic acid solution and transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask. The extract was diluted to volume with the same oxalic acid solution and filtered through filter paper. An aliquot (10 mL) of the filtrate was titrated with standardized DCIP solution until a light pink color persisted for 15 s. A standard ascorbic acid solution was used to calibrate the DCIP solution prior to analysis. The ascorbic acid content was calculated according to the volume of DCIP consumed and expressed as mg ascorbic acid per 100 g dry weight (mg·100 g−1). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as the mean value.

Antioxidant enzyme activities

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using commercial assay kits (Solarbio). Catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities were measured spectrophotometrically. Catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activities were determined using the crude enzyme extract obtained from freeze-dried strawberry samples. For CAT activity, 0.1 mL enzyme extract was added to 2.9 mL of 20 mM H₂O₂ prepared in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The decomposition of H₂O₂ was monitored by recording the decrease in absorbance at 240 nm for 1 min. CAT activity was expressed as ΔOD240·min−1·g−1. For POD activity, the reaction mixture consisted of 2.8 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), 0.1 mL of 2% guaiacol, 0.1 mL of 1% H₂O₂, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm resulting from guaiacol oxidation was recorded for 1 min, and POD activity was expressed as ΔOD470·min−1·g−1. For APX activity, the reaction mixture contained 2.7 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) supplemented with 0.5 mM ascorbic acid and 0.1 mM H₂O₂, along with 0.3 mL enzyme extract. The oxidation of ascorbic acid was monitored by measuring the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm for 1 min. APX activity was expressed as ΔOD290·min−1·g−1 (Zhang et al., 2022).

Soluble solids content and titratable acidity

The soluble solids content (SSC) of strawberries was measured using a portable refractometer (PAL-1, ATAGO, Japan). Prior to measurement, the refractometer was calibrated with distilled water according to the manufacturer's instructions, and the reading was adjusted to 0°Brix. Three fruits were randomly selected per treatment, and each was measured three times (nine readings in total). The mean value was used as the SSC result.

The titratable acidity (TA) was determined using an automatic potentiometric titrator. Three independent 5 mL juice samples were each diluted to 50 mL with deionized water, thoroughly mixed, and titrated with 0.1 mol·L−1 NaOH. Results were expressed as percentage of citric acid.

Electronic nose analysis

A portable electronic nose (PEN3 E-nose, Airsense Analytics GmbH, Germany) was employed to analyze volatile compounds in strawberry fruits. Approximately 3 g of frozen strawberry tissue powder was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial and equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min prior to detection. The operating conditions were as follows: sampling interval of 1 s, flushing time of 120 s, zeroing time of 5 s, pre-sampling duration of 5 s, and an injection flow rate of 300 mL·min−1.

Volatile organic compounds (VOC) analysis and metabolomics

Samples were harvested, weighed, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. For VOC analysis, 500 mg of powdered tissue was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial containing NaCl-saturated solution and equilibrated at 60 °C for 5 min before exposure to a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS fibre for 15 min. VOCs were analyzed using GC–MS (Agilent 8890 GC coupled with 7000D MS) equipped with a DB-5MS column. The injector was set at 250 °C, helium was used as the carrier gas (1.2 mL·min−1), and the oven program ranged from 40 °C to 280 °C. MS detection was conducted in EI mode (70 eV) under SIM mode (Tao et al., 2025).

Data analysis was performed in R. PCA was conducted with the prcomp function after unit variance scaling. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) were calculated and visualized using the ComplexHeatmap package. Differential metabolites were identified based on VIP > 1 combined with |Log₂FC| ≥ 1 (two groups) or P < 0.05 (ANOVA, multi-group), with VIP scores derived from OPLS-DA (MetaboAnalystR).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three biological replicates, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 27.0.1. Differences among treatments were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Graphs were generated using Origin 2021 and Adobe Illustrator 2020.

Results and discussion

Characterization of gelatin/tea tree essential oil composite film

Appearance, opacity, and color

Exposure to ultraviolet radiation can accelerate nutrient degradation and lipid oxidation in fruits, leading to deterioration of flavor and nutritional quality (Liu et al., 2019). Packaging films with light-shielding properties can therefore help extend shelf life and maintain flavor quality (Zhang & Rhim, 2022). In the present study, both M and ME films exhibited good film-forming properties (Fig. 1A). The incorporation of TTO increased the opacity of the M film from 1.06 mm−1 to 1.28 mm−1 (Fig. 1B), which may be attributed to the light-scattering effect of the essential oil dispersed within the film matrix. Previous studies have shown that the addition of essential oils to chitosan-based films similarly increases film opacity (Liu et al., 2024), which is consistent with the findings of this study. In addition, the color characteristics of packaging materials can influence consumer acceptance of food products (Li, Zhao, Li, Zhuang, & Wang, 2025). Colorimetric analysis indicated that the incorporation of TTO affected the color parameters of the M film. Compared with the M film, the L* value of the ME film was significantly increased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C), indicating enhanced brightness, while the a* value was significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D), suggesting a slight shift of the film color toward green along the red-green axis. The b* value showed an increasing trend but did not differ significantly (P > 0.05) (Fig. 1E), indicating minimal change in the yellow hue of the film. Although L* and a* values exhibited statistically significant differences, their absolute changes were small and did not substantially affect the overall visual appearance of the films, as confirmed by the film photographs (Fig. 1A). Therefore, the incorporation of TTO improves the light-blocking performance of the films without significantly altering their appearance, which is favorable for their practical application in fruit and vegetable packaging.

SEM, FTIR, and mechanical properties

SEM analysis showed that the M film exhibited a dense and uniform surface structure. After the incorporation of TTO, a few localized depressions appeared on the film surface (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1 in Supplementary Material 1), which may be attributed to the uneven evaporation of the essential oil during film formation and drying. Similar microstructural changes were previously observed when oregano essential oil was incorporated into quince seed mucilage films (Jouki, Yazdi, Mortazavi, & Koocheki, 2014). FTIR analysis revealed that the M film exhibited a pronounced absorption peak at 3307.3 cm−1 (Fig. 2B), corresponding to the stretching vibrations of -OH and -NH groups (amide A band). The peak at 2939.2 cm−1 was assigned to the asymmetric C—H stretching vibrations of aliphatic -CH₂/-CH₃ groups in the gelatin molecular chain, which is a characteristic absorption of the M film (Méndez, Cervera, & Reyes, 2025). After TTO incorporation, no new characteristic peaks appeared in the FTIR spectra, indicating that no new covalent bonds were formed in the system. The amide A band shifted from 3307 cm−1 to 3300 cm−1 (Fig. 2B), suggesting that TTO altered the vibrational environment of -NH/-OH groups in the gelatin matrix, possibly due to enhanced hydrogen-bonding interactions. Additionally, the amide I and amide II bands also exhibited red shifts, which is similarly attributed to strengthened hydrogen bonding (Jackson & Mantsch, 1995; Méndez et al., 2025). Comparable spectral changes have been reported in studies incorporating fennel essential oil into gelatin-based films (Ameur et al., 2025).

Microstructural, intermolecular interactions, and mechanical properties of M and ME films. (A) SEM images of M and ME films. (B) FTIR spectra of M and ME films. (C) Tensile strength of M and ME films. (D) Elongation at break of M and ME films.

Excellent mechanical properties are essential for postharvest fruit and vegetable packaging materials (Geng et al., 2023). TS and EB are key indicators for evaluating film mechanical performance. The TS and EB of the M film were 67.5 N and 19.8%, respectively (Fig. 2C, D). After TTO incorporation, TS and EB increased to 72.1 N and 28.0%, respectively (Fig. 2C, D), which may be attributed to strengthened intermolecular interactions between TTO and the gelatin matrix, consistent with the red shifts observed in the amide A, I, and II bands in the FTIR results. Previous studies have also shown that incorporating Sichuan pepper essential oil into poly (vinyl alcohol)/β-cyclodextrin nanofiber films significantly enhanced TS and EB (Zhang et al., 2022), aligning with the findings of this study. The enhancement in TS and EB indicates that incorporation of TTO induced only minor surface morphological changes without compromising mechanical stability. Overall, the ME film exhibited improved structural integrity and mechanical performance, supporting its potential application in fruit preservation packaging.

Effects of ME treatment on fruit storage quality

Strawberries are highly perishable during storage. The ME treatment effectively inhibited fungal growth (Fig. 3A) and delayed the onset of decay by 2 days (Fig. 3B). As storage progressed, the decay rates of strawberries in the CK, M, and ME groups gradually increased. On day 8 of storage, the decay rates reached 57.8%, 40.6%, and 31.1% for the CK, M, and ME groups, respectively. Throughout the storage period, the ME group consistently exhibited the lowest decay rate, which was significantly lower than those of the CK and M groups (P < 0.05), likely due to the antimicrobial properties of TTO. Previous studies have demonstrated that terpinen-4-ol, a major component of TTO, disrupts fungal cell membrane permeability, thereby exerting strong antifungal activity and significantly reducing decay in strawberries treated with TTO fumigation (Alves et al., 2022; Yadav, Kumar, Mahant, Khatkar, & Rao, 2017), which is consistent with the results of this study and further supports our conclusions. Moreover, the gelatin-based film may have provided an additional protective barrier on the fruit surface, limiting external microbial contamination and delaying pathogen invasion. The combined effects of the film matrix and TTO likely contributed to the lower decay rate observed in the ME-treated strawberries (Nadim, Ahmadi, Sarikhani, & Amiri Chayjan, 2015).

Effects of CK, M, and ME treatments on quality attributes and oxidative status of strawberry fruit during storage. (A) Changes in appearance, (B) decay rate, (C) firmness, (D) weight loss, (E) O₂−• content, (F) H₂O₂ content, and (G) MDA content of strawberry fruit during storage.

Fruit firmness and weight loss are key indicators of postharvest quality. During storage, firmness declined progressively in all groups. The firmness of the M group remained consistently higher than that of the CK group, although the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, the ME group maintained significantly higher firmness than the CK group (P < 0.05). By day 8, firmness in the M and ME groups was 8.3% and 30.7% higher than that in the CK group, respectively (Fig. 3C), indicating that ME treatment effectively delayed fruit softening.

Fruit weight loss continuously increased throughout storage, likely due to fruit respiration and nutrient consumption (Ruan, Kang, & Zeng, 2022). The CK group exhibited the highest weight loss, while the ME group showed the lowest. On day 8, the weight loss in the M and ME groups was reduced by 19.2% and 20.6% compared to the CK group, respectively (Fig. 3D), demonstrating that ME treatment effectively mitigates fruit weight loss. Previous studies have also reported that chitosan-based coatings incorporated with TTO significantly delay the decrease in firmness and increase in weight loss of strawberries, which is in agreement with the present results (Liu et al., 2025). The improved firmness retention and reduced weight loss observed in the ME group may be attributed to the barrier properties of the gelatin-based film, which can limit moisture transfer and gas exchange between the fruit and the surrounding environment. In addition, the antimicrobial activity of TTO may reduce microbial infection and associated tissue degradation, thereby contributing to the maintenance of fruit texture during storage.

Effects of ME treatment on ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation

Fungal infection in fruit is frequently accompanied by a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Tian, Qin, & Li, 2013). Excessive ROS induces membrane lipid peroxidation, disrupts membrane integrity, and accelerates cellular damage (Morgan, Kim, & Liu, 2007). To evaluate oxidative stress under different treatments, the contents of O₂−• H₂O₂, and malondialdehyde (MDA) were determined. The results showed that O₂−• and H₂O₂ levels in all groups (CK, M, and ME) initially increased and then decreased during storage (Fig. 3E, F). Throughout the storage period, the ME group consistently maintained significantly lower O₂−• and H₂O₂ contents than the CK and M groups (P < 0.05). On day 8 of storage, the O₂−• and H₂O₂ contents in the ME group were 11.2% and 6.6% lower, respectively, than those in the CK group. This effect was likely attributable to the antioxidant activity of TTO. In addition, the reduced ROS accumulation in the ME group may be associated with the antimicrobial activity of TTO and the barrier properties of the gelatin film, which could alleviate biotic stress and reduce respiration-related ROS generation during storage. These findings indicate that ME treatment effectively suppressed ROS accumulation during storage. Similar ROS-inhibitory effects have been reported in blueberries treated with gelatin coatings containing cinnamon essential oil (Piechowiak, Grzelak-Błaszczyk, Sójka, Skóra, & Balawejder, 2022).

MDA, the final product of lipid peroxidation, reflects the extent of membrane damage (Murshed, Lopez-Lauri, & Sallanon, 2013). MDA content in all groups followed a similar pattern, peaking on day 4 (Fig. 3G), consistent with the ROS trend. Throughout storage, the ME group exhibited significantly lower MDA content than the CK and M groups (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between CK and M. By day 8, MDA contents in CK, M, and ME were 39.59, 40.31, and 36.11 nmol·g−1, respectively, representing an 8.8% reduction in ME compared with CK. The lower MDA content further suggests that ME treatment helped maintain membrane integrity by mitigating ROS-induced lipid peroxidation damage. Collectively, ME treatment suppressed ROS accumulation and reduced MDA formation, thereby alleviating membrane lipid peroxidation damage.

Effects of ME treatment on non-enzymatic antioxidants

Non-enzymatic antioxidants in fruit play an important role in scavenging excess ROS and mitigating lipid peroxidation damage. Ascorbic acid and total phenolics are key antioxidant components closely associated with oxidative resistance. Ascorbic acid directly scavenges ROS, whereas phenolic compounds inhibit radical formation through hydrogen donation and metal ion chelation (Gęgotek & Skrzydlewska, 2022). During storage, ascorbic acid content in all groups showed an overall declining trend, with a slight increase observed on day 2 (Fig. 4A). The ME group consistently maintained higher ascorbic acid content than the CK and M groups, indicating that ME treatment delayed the degradation of ascorbic acid. On day 8 of storage, the ascorbic acid content in the ME group was 5.6% higher than that in the CK group.

Effects of CK, M, and ME treatments on antioxidant systems of strawberry fruit during storage. (A) Ascorbic acid content, (B) total phenolic content, (C) SOD activity, (D) CAT activity, (E) APX activity, and (F) POD activity of strawberry fruit during storage.

Total phenolic content initially increased and then declined, peaking on day 4 in all groups (Fig. 4B). At this time, total phenolics in the ME group were 10.05% and 7.42% higher than those in CK and M, respectively. The increase in total phenolic content during the early storage period may be attributed to pathogen-induced stress, which activates the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway in fruit tissues (Yousuf, Wu, & Siddiqui, 2021). Throughout storage, total phenolic content in the ME group remained significantly higher than in CK and M (P < 0.05). By day 8, the total phenolic content in the ME and M groups was 13.9% and 2.1% higher, respectively, than that in the CK group. Previous studies reported that TTO combined with aloe gel reduced losses of ascorbic acid and phenolics in lime fruit (Abou-Zaid et al., 2024), consistent with the present findings. These results demonstrate that ME treatment reduced antioxidant depletion and enhanced the overall antioxidant capacity of the fruit.

Effects of ME treatment on antioxidant enzyme activities

Antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and peroxidase (POD), constitute the primary defense system against oxidative stress by eliminating excess ROS and maintaining redox balance (He et al., 2025; Yang, Cao, Su, & Jiang, 2014). SOD activity in all groups initially increased and then declined during storage (Fig. 4C), peaking on day 4. At this time, SOD activities in CK, M, and ME were 92.00, 92.34, and 102.10 U·g−1, respectively, with ME showing an 11.0% increase compared with CK. Throughout storage, SOD activity in ME remained significantly higher than in CK and M (P < 0.05). By day 8, SOD activity in the ME group was 11.4% higher than that in the CK group. Maintenance of SOD activity is closely associated with reduced oxidative damage (Xu et al., 2021).

CAT catalyzes the decomposition of H₂O₂, thereby reducing intracellular ROS accumulation (Yao, Xu, Farooq, Jin, & Zheng, 2018). CAT activity in all groups also showed a rise-and-fall pattern (Fig. 4D). While CK and M peaked on day 4, ME reached peak CAT activity on day 6 and maintained higher CAT activity than CK and M throughout storage. By day 8, CAT activity in the ME and M groups was 38.5% and 4.1% higher, respectively, than that in the CK group, indicating delayed CAT decline. Similar trends have been reported in button mushrooms treated with TTO-containing composite films (Wang et al., 2024).

APX decomposes H₂O₂ using ascorbic acid as an electron donor, thereby reducing oxidative stress (Gao, Chai, Cheng, & Cao, 2017). APX activity peaked on day 2 in all groups (Fig. 4E). At this stage, APX activity in M and ME was 0.99 and 1.25 times that of CK, respectively. Throughout storage, ME maintained significantly higher APX activity than CK and M (P < 0.05), indicating delayed enzyme decline.

POD, another key antioxidant enzyme, eliminates H₂O₂ using various electron donors (Feng et al., 2022). Its activity generally increased during storage (Fig. 4F). ME consistently exhibited higher POD activity than CK and M, and by day 8, POD activity in M and ME increased by 5.6% and 50.6%, respectively, compared with CK. The enhanced activities of SOD, CAT, APX, and POD in the ME group suggest that the antioxidant defense system was better maintained during storage. This enhanced enzymatic antioxidant capacity likely contributed to the lower ROS and MDA contents observed in ME-treated fruit, thereby helping preserve cellular integrity and delay senescence. Similar responses have been reported in fruits treated with essential oil-based coatings, where improved antioxidant enzyme activities were associated with reduced oxidative stress (Taheri, Behnamian, Dezhsetan, & Karimirad, 2020).

Overall, ME treatment regulated ROS metabolism, suppressed ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation, maintained non-enzymatic antioxidant levels, and stabilized the antioxidant enzyme system. These coordinated effects alleviated oxidative stress-induced cellular damage, delayed postharvest senescence, and improved physiological stability and storage quality of strawberry fruit.

Effects of ME treatment on fruit flavor quality

Effects of ME treatment on sugar-acid balance

Soluble solids content (SSC) is an important indicator of strawberry flavor and overall quality. During storage, SSC in the CK, M, and ME groups gradually declined (Fig. 5A). The reduction in SSC was less pronounced in the M and ME groups than in CK, and SSC in the ME group remained significantly higher than in CK throughout storage (P < 0.05), indicating that ME treatment effectively delayed SSC depletion. Similar findings were reported in lime fruit treated with TTO-aloe composite coatings, where SSC loss was significantly slowed (Abou-Zaid et al., 2024).

Effects of CK, M, and ME treatments on flavor quality of strawberry fruit during storage. (A) Changes in soluble solids content and (B) titratable acidity of strawberry fruit during storage.

Titratable acidity (TA) reflects the organic acid level and is closely associated with fruit metabolic activity. TA gradually decreased during storage (Fig. 5B). Throughout the storage period, TA in the ME group remained higher than in CK, whereas the M group showed higher TA only on day 2. The decline in TA is likely associated with increased respiration and metabolic consumption during storage (Theunuo, Sema, & Sarkar, 2023). ME treatment may limit oxygen diffusion into the fruit by forming a semi-permeable physical barrier, thereby suppressing respiration and delaying TA depletion (Thapa, Sapkota, & Adhikari, 2020). Collectively, ME treatment not only maintained firmness and reduced weight loss but also slowed SSC and TA decline, demonstrating better preservation of fruit flavor quality during storage.

Electronic nose analysis of aroma characteristics

The electronic nose is an advanced gas-sensing system equipped with ten sensors responsive to different volatile organic compounds (VOCs), where signal intensity correlates with VOC concentration. Compared with traditional sensory evaluation, it provides greater objectivity, efficiency, and reproducibility (Yu et al., 2022).

Radar plot analysis (Fig. 6A-D) showed that the aroma profile of CK fruit progressively diverged from that of fresh fruit (0 d) during storage, with the greatest difference observed on day 8. The variation was mainly associated with sensors W1S and W1W, which are sensitive to short-chain hydrocarbons and sulfur-containing compounds, respectively (Yan et al., 2025). Sensor responses increased with storage time. Previous studies have suggested that sulfur-containing volatiles are related to enhanced enzymatic activity following membrane damage (Xiao et al., 2025), indicating progressive membrane deterioration in CK fruit, likely associated with increased ROS accumulation.

Electronic nose response patterns of strawberry fruit under CK, M, and ME treatments during storage. (A) 2 d, (B) 4 d, (C) 6 d, and (D) 8 d of storage.

In contrast, ME-treated fruit exhibited minimal variation in W1S and W1W responses compared with fresh samples, indicating greater aroma stability. These results suggest that ME treatment did not adversely affect fruit flavor and was more effective in maintaining the original aroma profile and overall flavor stability of strawberries.

Volatile metabolomic analysis of strawberry fruit

To further clarify the effects of ME treatment on strawberry VOCs, GC–MS-based volatile metabolomics was conducted on CK-0d, CK-8d, and ME-8d samples. Quality control (QC) samples showed highly overlapping total ion chromatograms, confirming good signal stability and data reliability (Fig. S2 in Supplementary Material 1). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed tight clustering within groups and clear separation among groups, indicating that both storage and ME treatment significantly influenced volatile metabolite composition (Fig. 7A).

Volatile metabolomic analysis of strawberry fruit. (A) Principal component analysis (PCA) score plot of volatile metabolites in strawberry fruit. (B) Composition and abundance of volatile metabolites in strawberry fruit. (C) Number of common differential metabolites among CK-0d vs. CK-8d, CK-0d vs. ME-8d, and CK-8d vs. ME-8d. (D) Classification of common metabolites among CK-0d vs. CK-8d, CK-0d vs. ME-8d, and CK-8d vs. ME-8d. Relative contents of longifolene (E), Cyclohexane, 1-ethenyl-1-methyl-2,4-bis(1-methylethenyl)-, (1.alpha.,2.beta.,4.beta.)- (F), and butyl caprylate (G) in strawberry fruit under ME treatment.

A total of 1738 volatile metabolites were identified and classified into 15 categories. Terpenes (19.4%) and esters (19.2%) were the dominant groups, followed by ketones (10.4%) and heterocyclic compounds (10.2%). Other detected classes included alcohols (8.6%), aldehydes (5.8%), acids (5.4%), hydrocarbons (5.2%), phenols (4.0%), aromatics (3.2%), amines (3.1%), ethers (2.3%), nitrogen-containing compounds (1.5%), halogenated hydrocarbons (0.8%), and sulfur-containing compounds (0.2%) (Table S1 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. 7B).

Differential metabolite analysis revealed significant variation among comparison groups. In CK-0d vs. CK-8d, 606 metabolites were upregulated and 100 downregulated (Table S2 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S3). In CK-0d vs. ME-8d, 877 metabolites were upregulated and 58 downregulated (Table S3 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S3 in Supplementary Material 1), indicating a reduction in volatile loss under ME treatment. In CK-8d vs. ME-8d, 504 metabolites were upregulated and 70 downregulated (Table S4 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. S3 in Supplementary Material 1). Across comparisons, 204 common differential metabolites were identified (Fig. 7C, Table S5 in Supplementary Material 2), primarily terpenes, esters, and ketones (Fig. 7D). Terpenes and esters are recognized as the major volatile organic compounds contributing to strawberry aroma formation and overall flavor quality (Forney, 2001). ME treatment increased compounds associated with citrus, fruity, and sweet-woody aromas (e.g., Longifolene) (Fig. 7E), while reducing undesirable herbal or waxy volatiles such as cyclohexane derivatives and butyl caprylate (Fig. 7F, G). These results indicate that ME treatment enhanced desirable aroma characteristics while suppressing off-flavor formation, thereby improving overall flavor quality.

Relative odor activity value (rOAV) is an approach that integrates the sensory threshold of individual compounds to identify key aroma-active components in foods, and is widely used to elucidate the relative contributions of different volatile compounds to the overall aroma profile of a sample. An rOAV ≥1 indicates that a compound contributes directly to the perceived flavor of the sample (Huang et al., 2026). Compounds with rOAV >1 were analyzed in the CK-0d, CK-8d, and ME-8d groups. In CK-0d, 159 key aroma-active compounds with rOAV >1 were identified, among which esters and terpenes accounted for 30.1%, while aldehydes and alcohols together represented 28.8% (Table S6 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. 8A). In CK-8d, a total of 219 compounds with rOAV >1 were detected, with esters and terpenes accounting for 36.8% and aldehydes and alcohols for 25.5% (Table S6 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. 8B). The ME-8d group exhibited the highest number of aroma-active compounds (262 in total), of which esters and terpenes accounted for 39.2%, whereas aldehydes and alcohols comprised 22.0% (Table S6 in Supplementary Material 2, Fig. 8C). These results indicate that ME treatment enhanced the relative contribution of ester- and terpene-derived aroma compounds in strawberry fruit. Esters and terpenes are generally considered the most important classes of volatile compounds contributing to fruity and floral aromas in strawberries (Forney, 2001). Several typical fruity aroma compounds, such as α-ionone, methyl cinnamate, and methyl anthranilate, maintained high rOAV values after ME treatment (Fig. 8D-F).

rOAV-based analysis of key volatile compounds contributing to strawberry flavor and the impact of ME treatment. (A) Composition of key aroma compounds (rOAV >1) in CK-0d, (B) CK-8d, and (C) ME-8d strawberry fruit. rOAV values of α-ionone (D), methyl cinnamate (E), methyl anthranilate (F), (E)-2-octen-1-ol (G), 2-octen-1-ol (H), (E, E)-2,4-heptadienal (I), terpinen-4-ol (J), γ-terpinene (K), and α-pinene (L).

During fruit senescence, membrane lipid peroxidation generates aldehydes through lipoxygenase-mediated oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, which are subsequently reduced to alcohols by alcohol dehydrogenase (Cho, Ryu, Hamayun, Park, & Kim, 2023). Many alcohols exhibit fatty or green odors associated with flavor deterioration (Zhao et al., 2024). Compounds such as (E)-2-octen-1-ol, 2-octen-1-ol, and aldehydes including (E, E)-2,4-heptadienal increased during storage and negatively affected aroma quality. ME treatment significantly reduced their relative contribution (Fig. 8G-I), thereby favoring the development of desirable aroma characteristics.

Tea tree oil contains characteristic volatiles such as terpinen-4-ol, γ-terpinene, and α-pinene (de Groot & Schmidt, 2016). ME treatment slightly increased the relative abundance of terpinen-4-ol and γ-terpinene in fruit (Fig. S4A, B in Supplementary Material 1), whereas no significant change was observed for α-pinene (Fig. S4C in Supplementary Material 1); their rOAV values remained below 1 (Fig. 8J-L), indicating that TTO-derived volatiles did not significantly influence the overall strawberry flavor profile under the present conditions.

To date, limited studies have investigated whether the aromatic constituents of tea tree essential oil exert adverse effects on fruit flavor. In this study, based on volatile metabolite profiling and rOAV analysis, we demonstrate that tea tree essential oil does not exert adverse effects on the overall flavor profile of strawberries under the present conditions, thereby providing new evidence supporting its applicability in fruit and vegetable preservation for maintaining quality and flavor attributes.

Conclusion

A functional gelatin/tea tree essential oil (TTO) composite film was successfully developed. ME treatment markedly reduced strawberry decay, maintained firmness, limited weight loss, enhanced antioxidant defenses, and slowed the decline of SSC and TA. Volatile metabolite analysis revealed increased sweet and fruity aroma compounds and reduced fatty and vegetable odors, with no adverse effect on fruit flavor (Fig. 9). Overall, the gelatin/TTO composite film effectively delays postharvest deterioration and preserves flavor quality, providing both practical guidance and theoretical support for green preservation and sustainable food packaging.
