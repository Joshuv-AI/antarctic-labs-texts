Associations between adverse childhood experiences and breastfeeding initiation and duration: a longitudinal birth cohort study in Pakistan
Authors: Emilie E. Egger, Sarah C. Haight, Paola Bojorquez-Ramirez, John A. Gallis, Allison Frost, Siham Sikander, Joanna Maselko, Ashley K. Hagaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RationalePrior studies have found that a history of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) is associated with breastfeeding initiation and duration, but the evidence is inconsistent. ACEs are associated with poor maternal and infant health outcomes. Despite low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) carrying a disproportionate amount of poor maternal and infant health outcomes and despite evidence that breastfeeding could alleviate some of these outcomes, little research has assessed the relative impact of ACEs on breastfeeding initiation and duration of exclusive breastfeeding in LMICs.ObjectiveThe study investigates whether maternal ACEs are associated with breastfeeding initiation and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months postpartum among a cohort of women from rural Pakistan.MethodsThe prospective cohort analysis included 869 women 18–45 years of age from the Bachpan Cohort Study. Multivariable modified log-Poisson regression models were used to assess the relationship between any ACE exposure with breastfeeding initiation (n = 755) and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 (n = 758) and 6 months (n = 809). Models were adjusted for known confounders.Results58% of participants reported experiencing at least 1 ACE. 17.5% of women with a history of at least one ACE reported initiating breastfeeding within an hour of childbirth. 43.8% of women with at least one ACE reported exclusively breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum and 7.6% of women with at least one ACE reported exclusively breastfeeding within the last 24 h at 6 months postpartum. There was no significant difference between groups for initiating breastfeeding. However, women who had experienced ACEs were less likely to recall exclusively breastfeeding at 3 (RR = 0.8; 95 CI = 0.7, 1.0) and 6 months (RR = 0.7; 95% CI = 0.5, 1.2) postpartum compared to women with no self-reported history of ACEs.ConclusionOur findings indicate that ACEs may contribute to differences in exclusive breastfeeding. Understanding the long-term effects of ACEs on breastfeeding is important to help inform the design of intervention programs to prevent or mediate the negative repercussions of ACEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28648-8.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends breastfeeding as the ideal form of nutrition for infants [1, 2]. WHO recommends initiating breastfeeding within one hour of birth [3]. Initiating breastfeeding within one hour of childbirth is pivotal because it guarantees that infants receive colostrum— the first breast milk produced for 2–4 days after childbirth [4]. Compared to other stages of breast milk, colostrum is concentrated with antibodies that provide passive immunity for babies [4, 5]. Initiating breastfeeding within one hour of childbirth has also been shown to be associated with the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding practices [6].

Breastfeeding has been shown to enhance infant health in many ways. In low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) such as Pakistan, exclusive breastfeeding until six months can be particularly protective against malnutrition [7–10]. More broadly, breast milk has been associated with lower rates of a variety of childhood illnesses, such asthma and wheezing [11, 12]. Breastfeeding is also associated with mental and emotional health benefits, such as maternal-child attachment and reduced physiological stress, anxiety, and postpartum depression for breastfeeding mothers [9].

Breastfeeding initiation and duration of exclusive breastfeeding vary widely across LMIC [13]. The World Health Organization defines exclusive breastfeeding as an infant receiving no liquids besides breastmilk, including water “with the exception of oral rehydration solution, or drops or syrups of vitamins, minerals or medicines” [14]. Between 2010 and 2018 in LMIC, only 45.1% of infants were breastfed at 6 months. One in five babies born in Pakistan is breastfed within one hour of birth and 48% are exclusively breastfed at six months old (based on 24-hour recall) [15]. Infant mortality has been declining in Pakistan over the past decade, reaching 62 deaths per 1000 live births in 2018 [16]. Child malnutrition is the cause of nearly 50% of these deaths [17]. Exclusive breastfeeding can reduce infant deaths up to 10 percent [18].

While many birthing people are aware that breastfeeding provides optimal nutrition benefits for infants, qualitative studies have consistently demonstrated that most are unable to identify the specific benefits associated with breastfeeding [19]. In Pakistan, 52% and 54% of women received antenatal counseling about the initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding, respectively [16]. Counseling rates were highest in urban areas and among women who had relatively high education status [16]. Many therefore use cultural practices known to reduce hunger and respiratory illnesses in their infants [17]. For instance, mothers sometimes discard colostrum in Pakistani villages because they perceive it as not having value for a child’s wellbeing [20, 21]. In one study, 14% of mothers in urban and semi-urban Pakistan discarded colostrum [22]. Many Pakistani mothers complementary feed their infants during the first three days of life with water, ghee, animal milk, and herbal tea, which many Pakistani mothers believe optimizes newborn nourishment and health outcomes [20, 21, 23]. This is referred to as prelacteal feeding [24]. Among children who were breastfed, 76% received pre-lacteal feeds within the first three hours of life [16]. Pakistani mothers also face the wide variety of common barriers to breastfeeding such as: time management, physiological changes after delivery, perception of insufficient breast milk, high maternal work load, additional maternal stress from initiating breastfeeding early, cesarean section deliveries, employment, and limited workplace support [20, 25].

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), categorized as abuse, neglect, and household challenges, are important determinants of maternal and infant well-being [26, 27]. Several studies suggest that the prevalence of ACEs is consistently highest among LMIC countries [28–31]. ACEs have been found to have an enduring effect on maternal health prior to and during pregnancy [28]. Evidence suggests that ACEs induce physiological changes that lead to increased risk of poor health and behavioral outcomes [32, 33]. An increase in the number and severity of ACEs also appears to be linked to worse health outcomes throughout the life course, indicating the existence of a dose-response relationship [34–36]. Evidence suggests that conditions associated with ACEs, such as substance use and intimate partner violence, can reduce the initiation and continuation of breastfeeding, due to higher rates of chronic health conditions and psychosocial difficulties with pregnancy [25, 37, 38].

Several studies have examined the association between ACEs and breastfeeding outcomes; however, results of these studies vary and few have been conducted in LMIC [39, 40]. One study reported that ACEs are associated with an increased likelihood of exclusive breastfeeding, but not breastfeeding initiation, while another found an association between experiencing child sexual abuse and a higher breastfeeding initiation rate [26, 41]. Another reported a significant association between ACE count and an increased likelihood of initiating breastfeeding, but not continued breastfeeding at two months postpartum [42]. To our knowledge, no research has assessed the relationship between ACEs and breastfeeding outcomes in Pakistan. This study investigates whether experiencing ACEs is associated with breastfeeding initiation and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months among a cohort of women from a rural subdistrict of Pakistan. Our study tests the associations between ACEs and breastfeeding outcomes in LMIC. Given evidence that breastfeeding can contribute to maternal and infant health, particularly in resource-strained contexts, investigating early-life exposures that may hinder breastfeeding uptake may have significant public health impact.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data come from the Bachpan Cohort Study, which enrolled women from rural communities in Kallar Syedan, a subdistrict of Rawalpindi, Pakistan [43]. The Bachpan Cohort Study is an ongoing prospective pregnancy-birth cohort study designed to investigate the effects of depression on child health and development [43]. Eligibility criteria for the Bachpan Cohort Study included being married, in the third trimester of pregnancy (≥ 28 weeks’ gestation), at least 18 years of age, intending to remain in the study area, not requiring immediate inpatient care for medical or psychiatric reasons, and being able to speak Urdu, Punjabi, or Potohari [43]. Within the Bachpan birth cohort is the Thinking Health PLUS Program (THPP), a cluster-randomized trial evaluating a peer-delivered psychosocial treatment for depression [43]. Researchers screened all eligible pregnant women residing in the study area at the time of recruitment for depression using the Urdu validated Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [44]. The study defined women from each of the 40 village clusters who scored a PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 as screening positive for depression. These women were invited to join the Bachpan Cohort, while one of every three women who did not meet the threshold was invited to participate as the non-depressed reference group [43]. Researchers used the ≥ 10 threshold to enroll pregnant women into the trial portion of the cohort. A total of 1154 pregnant women enrolled into the cohort and completed baseline screening between October 2014 and February 2016 [43].

For the present study, the study team analyzed data from 869 participants related to ACEs, breastfeeding initiation, and breastfeeding continuation. Data from the following timepoints were utilized in this analysis: third trimester during pregnancy (baseline), 3 months, 6 months, and 36 months postpartum. Fewer women participated in the 3-month study point than the 6-point. This can likely be explained by the Pakistani custom of chilla, in which postpartum women return to their maternal home for the first 40 days following birth [45]. This could have made them unavailable to participate in the study due to a temporary change in physical location.

Measures

Outcomes

The three primary outcomes of interest originate from the World Health Organization breastfeeding recommendations to initiate breastfeeding within the first hour of birth and exclusively breastfeed for the first 6 months of life. These include: (1) time of breastfeeding initiation (“When was the baby’s first breast milk feed?”) and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months (“What has the child had in the last 24 hours? )” [3]. 24-hour recall is a WHO-accepted indicator for retrospective questions about breastfeeding exclusivity at 3 and 6 months [46]. In addition, we incorporate data from 3 months postpartum to help us better understand breastfeeding practices between birth and 6 months. Children who were only fed breast milk in the last 24 hours (no reported feeding of ghutti, herbal water, water, or tea) were categorized as having been exclusively breastfed. We inquired about these three liquids, because they are the non-breastmilk liquids most commonly given to infants in Pakistan. The study team treated each of the three outcome variables as binary variables (yes/no). The breastfeeding questionnaire is included as an appendix file.

Exposure

We used the ACE questionnaire to measure experiences of abuse, neglect, household dysfunction, and community dysfunction during childhood [27]. We measured ACEs using the 12-item ACE-IQ at 36-months postpartum, which retrospectively asks about an individual’s exposure to adverse experiences prior to the age of 18. This questionnaire has been validated for use in international settings and was additionally adapted for use in the Bachpan Cohort Study [27, 47]. Our team removed sexual abuse questions because of the high probability of underreporting due to stigma and fear in the community [27].

We operationally defined ACEs in three ways [27]. ACEs variables included (1) a binary yes/no variable for any ACE reported, (2) a continuous variable for number of experiences, and (3) a binary indicator for each of the four ACE domains (neglect [emotional neglect; physical neglect], family psychological distress [alcohol and/or drug abuser in the household; incarcerated household member; someone depressed, mentally ill, institutionalized or suicidal], home violence [physical abuse; emotional abuse; household member treated violently], and community violence [bullying; community violence; collective violence]. We coded binary variables for each ACE domain ‘yes’ if a woman experienced any of the ACEs within the domain. Because there is no agreed-upon standard about how to present ACEs, presenting them from several angles allows for the data to reveal nuances and patterns that might be missed if choosing only one method.

Control variables

We adjusted all statistical models described below for mother’s age at baseline, education level (> 5th grade) as a proxy for childhood socioeconomic status [48], trial arm (control, intervention, non-depressed), and assessor. We considered age, education, and trial arm to confound the relationship between ACEs and breastfeeding given their associations with both exposure and outcomes. Assessor was not a theoretical confounder but was included to control for potential variability in data collection across interviewers.

Statistical analyses

We calculated descriptive statistics for sociodemographic variables by whether an individual experienced any ACE. Descriptive statistics for each ACE operationalization were calculated by breastfeeding outcomes.

To examine whether missingness differed by key demographic factors, we compared baseline characteristics across three groups: participants present at 36 months with complete ACE data (n = 869), those lost to follow-up (n = 265), and those present at follow-up, but missing ACE data (n = 20; Supplementary Table 1). To account for potential bias from missingness, we used inverse probability weighting (IPW) in all models. Weights were the inverse probability that an observation was not missing at the 36-month wave or not missing ACE data, based on observed characteristics. We included the marginal probability of being observed in the numerator of the IPW to stabilize weights. We included baseline characteristics associated with missingness at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months and those theoretically likely to be associated with missingness in the IPW model: socioeconomic status based on assets, parity, depressive symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire-9), maternal disability (WHO-DAS), perceived stress (Cohen Perceived Stress Scale), household size (children and adults), whether the grandmother lived in the home, and whether the husband worked [48]. The confounding factors described above (maternal age, education, trial arm, and assessor) were also included in the IPW model. We weighted all models by IPW.

We used multivariable modified log-Poisson regression models with robust standard errors accounting for clustering by village to assess the relationship between each type of ACE variable and the three respective outcomes— breastfeeding initiation within the first 24 h of childbirth (n = 755; measured at 3 months), 24 h recall of exclusive breastfeeding at 3 months (n = 758), and 24 h recall of exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months ( n = 809). Modified log-Poisson models were chosen over logistic regression models because odds ratios are commonly misinterpreted as risk ratios in global health research with common outcomes such as ours; overstating associations of risk can be especially costly and dangerous in this context [49]. Moreover, the use of robust standard errors accounts for the misspecification of the outcome distribution (i.e. Poisson instead of binomial distribution) and allows researchers to report valid risk ratios for binary outcomes [49]. Confidence intervals exclusive of 1.0 were considered statistically significant and are denoted in the Tables. We used Stata 15.1. to perform statistical analyses.

Ethics

The institutional review boards at the Human Development Research Foundation (Pakistan), the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (United States), and Duke University (United States) approved the study. Written informed consent was required for study participation and was acknowledged with the participant’s signature or by a witness’s signature in the case of the participant being illiterate. We conducted our research in accordance with the “Common Rule” set out in the Belmont Report and the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical Trial number: not applicable.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Of the total 1154 women enrolled at baseline, 869 answered questions about ACE data at 36 months, resulting in 755, 758, and 809 women for each of our outcomes—breastfeeding initiation, and exclusive breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months, respectively. Baseline demographic characteristics and key outcomes did not differ significantly by missingness (Supplementary Table 1).

Table 1 displays demographic and breastfeeding characteristics of the sample stratified by history of ACE exposure (yes/no). Nearly 60% of women in the cohort reported experiencing at least one ACE domain [27]. Women with an ACE history on average had a greater number of living children (1.53 vs. 1.35 children), and greater household size (8.61 vs. 8.30 children and adults) than women with no ACE history. Most women had a primary education greater than 5th grade (66.7%). Overall,15.63% of women in the study initiated breastfeeding within one hour of birth, 47.89% reported exclusively breastfeeding at 3 months, and 8.65% reported exclusively breastfeeding at 6 months.

ACEs and 24-hour recall of exclusive breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum

Less than half of women with an ACE history (43.78%) reported exclusively breastfeeding in the previous 24 h at the 3-month postpartum visit, while over half of women with no ACE history (52.94%) reported exclusively breastfeeding in the previous 24 h at the 3-month postpartum visit.

Table 3 presents descriptive statistics for each operationalization of ACE stratified by exclusive breastfeeding during the 3-month postpartum visit. More than half of women who exclusively breastfed and did not exclusively breastfeed their child for the last 24 h had a history of ACEs (50.41% vs. 59.49%, respectively). The average number of ACEs among women who exclusively breastfed is 0.98, while the average number of ACEs among women who did not exclusively breastfeed their child is 1.18.

Panel 2 in Table 5 shows the ARR between each ACE domain exposure and 24-hour recall of exclusive breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum. Women exposed to ACEs were less likely to have exclusively breastfed compared to women with no ACE exposure (ARR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.70, 0.95). Total ACE Score and the community violence domain were also associated with 24-hour recall of exclusive breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum (Model 5 and 9, respectively). With every additional exposure to an ACE, women were 10% less likely to have reported exclusively breastfeeding (ARR = 0.93; 95% CI: 0.88, 1.00). Women exposed to community violence ACEs were nearly 40% less likely to have exclusively breastfed for the last 24 h (ARR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.42, 0.93) than women not exposed to ACEs. We found no significant association between any of the other ACE type variables and exclusively breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum (Models 3–5).

ACEs and 24-hour recall of exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months postpartum

Approximately 7.64% of women with an ACE history reported exclusively breastfeeding in the previous 24 h at the 6-month postpartum visit, while 9.97% of women with no ACE history reported exclusively breastfeeding in the previous 24 h at the 6-month postpartum visit.

Table 4 presents descriptive statistics for each type of ACE variable stratified by exclusive breastfeeding during the 6-month postpartum visit. Most women who exclusively breastfed and did not exclusively breastfeed their child for the last 24 h had a history of ACEs (50.00% vs. 57.24%, respectively). Discussion

Our study provides novel evidence suggesting that the experiences of ACEs in Pakistan could be associated with lower breastfeeding rates at three and six months postpartum. The findings indicate that mothers with a history of ACEs, increased exposure to ACEs, and exposure to community violence are significantly less likely to report exclusively breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum. We did not find an association between the experience of ACEs and breastfeeding initiation. To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the relationship between ACEs operationalized as different variables with breastfeeding outcomes in rural Pakistan. The most common ACEs experienced among women in this study fall within the home violence domain (specifically, physical and emotional abuse).

Our study found a significant impact of ACEs on exclusively breastfeeding at 3 and 6 months postpartum. Notably, at 3 months postpartum, each additional ACE was associated with a 10% lower likelihood of exclusively breastfeeding in the past 24 h (ARR = 0.9; 95% CI: 0.9, 1.0). The study also found that the percentage of women reporting exclusive breastfeeding in the past 24 h at 3 months postpartum—regardless of ACE history—was more than five times higher than at 6 months postpartum which is the WHO’s recommended duration for exclusive breastfeeding [2]. Our study builds on prior research linking ACEs to shorter breastfeeding duration by suggesting a cumulative effect of ACEs on breastfeeding outcomes. One study in Bangladesh also showed a dose-response relationship between childhood maltreatment and rates of exclusive breastfeeding. Unlike the previous study, our study used “point in time” data collection at two data collection points [50]. It also used ACE-IQ an adapted standardized, multi-item measure to assess maltreatment. Our findings differ from a previous study that found a higher association between ACE count and breastfeeding initiation, but none in breastfeeding exclusivity at two months postpartum [42]. However, we conducted our study in an LMIC, which may suggest that the relationship between ACE count and breastfeeding outcomes varies by context [42]. This finding warrants research on the relationship between ACE count and breastfeeding outcomes in other South Asian countries and LMIC to understand this relationship.

Our study also contributes to the burgeoning scholarship on ACEs in LMIC by identifying which types of ACEs may be more prevalent in these settings and highlighting the usefulness of the ACE framework across diverse contexts [29]. The ACE-IQ may not capture child adversity in its entirety [51–54]. There is currently no way for the measure to account for protective factors against childhood adversity, such as stable emotional relationships with family and community members or how children build resilience [51, 55]. Furthermore, various populations may conceptualize adversity and hardship differently [56].

Our study found that community violence was associated with a lower rate of exclusive breastfeeding at three months postpartum. Our study adds to research that suggests that experiencing forms of violence can decrease breastfeeding initiation, exclusivity, and duration [38, 57]. Interventions to support women experiencing interpersonal violence during the postpartum period have been successful in reducing violence and depression [58, 59]. Some of this research has been conducted in LMIC [60]. However, the relationship between experiencing violence and breastfeeding can differ depending on racial identity and type of violence experienced [61]. Future research could investigate the mechanisms that lead to decreased breastfeeding initiation, exclusivity, and duration among women who have experienced community violence as children and could address whether providing therapeutic and breastfeeding support could increase these metrics. For example, breastfeeding promotion interventions could include peer support groups for women who have experienced neglect, sexual assault, or who are currently experiencing domestic violence [62, 63]. Breastfeeding interventions can also serve as a bridge to social services [64]. Additionally, women in Pakistan who have experienced ACEs often experience higher rates of depression and anxiety [27].

The high prevalence of exposure to physical and emotional abuse at home as children among this cohort may explain why exposure to ACEs is associated with lower likelihoods of exclusive breastfeeding recall at 3 months postpartum. Physical and emotional abuse experienced during adolescence has been found to be associated with risk of adult intimate domestic violence [65–68]. Continued violence and an unsupportive environment at home may mitigate any of the potential mental health benefits that breastfeeding may have; a 2024 study in Pakistan noted that women who experienced intimate partner violence were less likely to initiate breastfeeding and exclusively breastfeed [69]. The integration of social services to address the high prevalence of abuse that many children may be experiencing needs to be prioritized to prevent home abuse from occurring [70]. The compilation and distribution of resources for children and families experiencing abuse are also needed to strengthen communities and mitigate the negative effects of ACEs later in life. Additionally, future research exploring how experiencing childhood sexual abuse may influence breastfeeding outcomes in rural Pakistan is warranted, especially given the high prevalence and underreporting of such abuse [50, 71–73].

Our study suggests that more research should investigate cultural facilitators to breastfeeding, such as antenatal counseling on the importance of breastfeeding or living in an area where initiating breastfeeding is a cultural norm. Culturally safe breastfeeding promotion programs should be developed to address the pervasive misunderstandings and myths that exist about breastfeeding in Pakistan so that women can make informed decisions about their breastfeeding practices and receive desired support [17, 20, 74]. When birthing people are cared for in a culturally safe way, they tend to experience better perinatal outcomes and to also continue with recommended care [75–78]. Breastfeeding promotion support groups could be a way to integrate health services that address domestic violence, food insecurity, and generally build positive community support, especially in areas in which prenatal breastfeeding counseling is prominent, such as Pakistan [79].

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, our study is the first to assess the relationship between ACEs and breastfeeding outcomes in Pakistan. The study also used an ACE questionnaire that has been validated for LMIC settings. This study also provides descriptive statistics about the prevalence of breastfeeding initiation and exclusive breastfeeding recall among women in rural Pakistan which to our knowledge is not available elsewhere. Additionally, the study collected breastfeeding data at multiple time points and employed multiple operationalizations of ACEs.

Our study has limitations that warrant discussion. First, we assessed the exposures (ACEs) after the outcomes. This is typical of ACE reporting because the ACE questionnaire is a retrospective assessment of childhood experiences that irrespective of which time point they had been assessed for in the Bachpan Cohort could not be captured in real time. However, ACEs is the only validated questionnaire that attempts to capture adverse childhood events among adults. Breastfeeding outcomes are self-reported and there is the potential to overreport breastfeeding recall due to social desirability bias. Another limitation is that we only exclusive breastfeeding in the last 24 h and we did not ask about exclusive breastfeeding duration. The WHO-approved 24-hour recall measure was most feasible due to our field work restraints. Although education level was adjusted for as a proxy for childhood SES, there is a possibility of residual confounding by childhood SES. Moreover, findings within ACE domains (particularly, the community violence domain) should be interpreted with caution due to small sample sizes. Because so few participants reported breastfeeding at six months (n = 70), these estimates could be unstable. Finally, while we use women’s education level as a proxy for childhood socioeconomic status, we cannot fully disregard the possibility of residual confounding by childhood socioeconomic status.

The Bachpan Cohort had a higher reported prevalence of ACEs compared to most high-income countries but lower prevalence of ACEs compared to other LMIC [27] It is important to consider that the prevalence of ACEs in this cohort may be underestimated. The potential underestimated prevalence of ACEs in this study may explain the wide confidence intervals between most ACE type variables with each breastfeeding outcome and no clear dose-response relationship. This information is crucial because a study of women of Bangladesh found that the type of maltreatment experienced affected exclusive breastfeeding differently: women who experienced childhood sexual abuse, specifically, had significantly lower likelihoods of breastfeeding their children compared to women who were not sexually abused as children [40]. According to this information, an underestimation of ACE exposure in this study would result in lower estimated associations toward the null.

Conclusion

ACEs are a serious public health concern that may have long term repercussions on overall well-being across the life course. We found that for each additional ACE exposure, women were less likely to have reported exclusively breastfeeding for the last 24 h at 3 months postpartum and that experiencing community violence as a child was also associated with lower rates of exclusive breastfeeding at 3 months postpartum. It is important to identify interventions that can prevent the occurrence of ACEs in rural Pakistan that can negatively impact a mother’s well-being and child’s development. Integration of a life course perspective in medical settings can ensure women are connected to and have access to resources they need to be well and provide good care to their children. It is worth considering looking into medical screenings of ACEs to determine whether this is a viable recommendation. Women can be screened, counseled and linked to support. The findings of this study also help understand the prevalence of breastfeeding outcomes in rural Pakistan. Understanding the long-term consequences of ACEs for women among a population that is already overburdened with other challenges is important for the development of tailored interventions that support the health of women and their children.
