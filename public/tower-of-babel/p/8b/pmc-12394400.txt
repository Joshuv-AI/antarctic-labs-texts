Dual-model approach for accurate chest disease detection using GViT and swin transformer V2
Authors: Kamal Ahmad, Hafeez Ur Rehman, Babar Shah, Farman Ali, Irfan Hussain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe precise detection and localization of abnormalities in radiological images are very crucial for clinical diagnosis and treatment planning. To build reliable models, large and annotated datasets are required that contain disease labels and abnormality locations. Most of the time, radiologists face challenges in identifying and segmenting thoracic diseases such as COVID-19, Pneumonia, Tuberculosis, and lung cancer due to overlapping visual patterns in X-ray images. This study proposes a dual-model approach: Gated Vision Transformers (GViT) for classification and Swin Transformer V2 for segmentation and localization. GViT successfully identifies thoracic diseases that exhibit similar radiographic features, while Swin Transformer V2 maps lung areas and pinpoints affected regions. Classification metrics, including precision, recall, and F1-scores, surpassed 0.95 while the Intersection over Union (IoU) score reached 90.98%. Performance assessment via Dice Coefficient, Boundary F1-Score, and Hausdorff Distance demonstrated the system’s excellent effectiveness. This artificial intelligence solution will help radiologists in decreasing their mental workload while improving diagnostic precision in healthcare systems that face resource constraints. Transformer-based architectures show strong promise for enhancing medical imaging procedures, according to the study results. Future AI tools should build on this foundation, focusing on comprehensive and precise detection of chest diseases to support effective clinical decision-making.Keywords: Chest disease detection, Gated vision transformer, Swin transformer V2, Artificial intelligence in radiology, And lung cancer detectionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The most severe global health challenges include thoracic diseases such as pneumonia, tuberculosis, lung cancer, and the recently emerged COVID-19. The World Health Organization reports that lower respiratory tract infections, along with other lung diseases, rank as major causes of death worldwide, resulting in approximately 4 million deaths each year1. Timely and precise identification of these diseases enables healthcare providers to start effective treatments that lead to better patient outcomes. Chest X-ray (CXR) imaging stands out as the primary choice for initial imaging because it combines accessibility with cost-effectiveness and strong capabilities to detect lung abnormalities2. Even experienced radiologists face difficulties interpreting CXRs despite their proven clinical usefulness. The challenge of differentiating diseases with overlapping visual patterns arises from subtle variations in texture, shape, and density3,4. Diagnostic consistency is significantly affected by variations in interpretation between different observers as well as by the same observer at different times5. The scarcity of trained radiologists, alongside rising imaging workloads, demands strong automated computer-aided diagnostic systems to support clinicians and enhance diagnostic precision and efficiency6.

Deep learning breakthroughs through convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have completely transformed medical image analysis. Models such as CheXNet7, DenseNet-1218, and ResNet-509 achieved promising results in thoracic disease detection by utilizing large annotated datasets such as ChestX-ray810 and CheXpert11. CNN-based models demonstrate superior performance compared to traditional machine learning methods and achieve radiologist-level results in certain scenarios12. However, CNN architectures present built-in limitations that hinder their ability to process complex medical imaging tasks effectively. These models depend on local receptive fields, making them less effective at recognizing extended spatial relationships necessary for analyzing large anatomical structures in CXRs13. Extensive annotated datasets are required for CNN training to avoid overfitting, although obtaining such datasets remains challenging because medical image annotation requires substantial manual effort14. The performance of CNNs in practical clinical environments deteriorates due to class imbalance issues, subtle disease markers, and dataset biases15.

These challenges have led to the development of transformer-based models as a strong alternative. Transformers were first created for natural language processing tasks16 but have now achieved considerable success in vision applications. Vision Transformers (ViT) introduced a new approach for image classification using self-attention mechanisms to analyze global contextual relationships between image patches treated as input tokens17. Transformers naturally learn both local and global dependencies without requiring convolutional kernel design, making them well-suited for medical imaging applications18. The successful implementation of ViT inspired the creation of several transformer-based models that target its computational drawbacks and enhance feature representation capabilities. The Swin Transformer utilizes a hierarchical architecture with shifted windows to enable scalable modeling without compromising computational performance19. Through its design, the Swin Transformer excels in dense prediction applications, including object detection and semantic segmentation, making it highly suitable for medical image segmentation tasks20. The latest advancement in this architecture, Swin Transformer V2, enhances the base model with better training stability, improved handling of different input resolutions, and expanded scalability for extensive vision tasks21. These enhancements are particularly valuable for medical imaging applications that require precise boundary identification across varying image resolutions.

Gated Vision Transformers (GViT) have introduced gating mechanisms into attention models, allowing dynamic control of information flow based on task relevance22. Gating techniques in medical imaging help models improve region discrimination, enhancing classification accuracy by minimizing interference from irrelevant background noise. Recent developments have established transformer-based architectures as a promising research direction for creating CAD systems designed to detect chest diseases. However, many current studies lack clinical usefulness because they focus exclusively on either classification or segmentation tasks. Medical treatment planning requires accurate diagnoses through both comprehensive disease classification and precise localization of affected areas.

Literature review

Medical imaging and artificial intelligence researchers have investigated automated thoracic disease detection methods using chest radiography for more than twenty years. Traditional machine learning approaches dominated early detection methods, utilizing artificially designed features like texture descriptors and histogram patterns together with classifiers such as support vector machines or decision trees23. The variability and complexity of chest X-ray (CXR) images constrained the generalizability of these methods24. The field of medical image analysis experienced a breakthrough with the development of deep learning techniques, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs). CNN-based models outperformed traditional handcrafted feature approaches by learning hierarchical features automatically from raw image data25. For instance, Lakhani et al. used deep CNNs to identify tuberculosis from CXRs and achieved a 96% accuracy rate26. Similarly, Yao et al. created frameworks that detect multiple thoracic diseases at the same time through multi-label classification techniques27. VGGNet28, Inception29, and ResNet30 CNN models have been adapted for chest disease detection, demonstrating deep architectures’ ability to extract both local and global diagnostic features.

Even though CNNs experienced significant advancements, their fundamental drawbacks remained unchanged. The localized nature of convolution operations limits CNNs’ ability to model long-range spatial dependencies31. The difficulty of analyzing CXR images arises from abnormalities that can cover extensive lung areas or where regional subtle cues link with distant structures that CNNs struggle to model. CNN models experience interpretability problems that prevent easy clinical usage unless explainability tools like Grad-CAM or occlusion sensitivity maps are implemented32. Researchers progressively incorporated attention mechanisms into CNN structures to address these challenges. Attention-guided CNNs target diagnostically crucial areas in CXR images to enhance both model focus and interpretability capabilities33. Guan et al. introduced an attention-based multiple instance learning approach for disease detection. Similarly, Ahn et al. built weakly labeled chest X-ray examinations for thoracic disease classification34.

Before the development of transformers, researchers went through different phases. I. Sutskever et al. introduced a sequence-to-sequence architecture that uses stacked LSTMs to perform machine translation tasks35. The model reached BLEU scores of 34.8 and 36.5 for English-to-French translation through reranking SMT outputs. The model demonstrated effective processing of extended sentences because reversing the word order in source language texts improved learning outcomes by making dependencies easier to manage.

The encoder–decoder structure used in neural machine translation was refined through the introduction of a soft attention mechanism36. The model generates target words by dynamically attending to relevant parts of the input instead of encoding the whole source sentence into a fixed-length vector. This method boosted translation quality and matched the performance of leading phrase-based systems while producing attention alignments that matched human intuition.

The Transformer model represents a new approach to sequence transduction, using attention mechanisms exclusively to remove the need for both recurrence and convolutional methods. The model attained better translation results through enhanced parallelization capabilities and quicker training times37. It set new standards by reaching BLEU scores of 28.4 (English-German) and 41.0 (English-French) on the WMT 2014 dataset, exceeding previous state-of-the-art achievements.

This paper demonstrates the Vision Transformer (ViT), which processes image patch sequences exclusively without depending on convolutional layers38. The imaging capabilities of Vision Transformers reach high benchmarks when training on extensive datasets, then fine-tuning benchmark standards, including ImageNet and CIFAR-100, at lower operational expenses than traditional CNNs. The Swin Transformer implements shifted window methods to construct its hierarchical vision transformer architecture that achieves efficient local and global image pattern recognition19. The method addresses the limitations of transformer implementation for visual applications with adjustable self-attention procedures. The Swin Transformer stands out as an adaptable vision backbone because it showcases leading performance in frameworks that perform image classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation assessments.

This research presents Swin Transformer V2 as a large-scale vision model that enhances training stability, resolution transfer, and data efficiency21. The model implements three novel components using residual post-norm coupled with cosine attention, log-spaced position bias, and SimMIM self-supervised pretraining. The three-billion-parameter model achieves record-breaking results in diverse vision tasks, demonstrating substantial efficiency gains over earlier billion-scale models in both data usage and training time. Attention-enhanced CNNs succeeded in bettering feature focus, yet retained the traditional convolution-based operations’ disadvantages in modeling global contexts. As a result of these limitations, researchers started investigating transformer-based architectures originally developed for natural language processing tasks. Carion et al. presented DETR (Detection Transformer), which showed that transformers can model spatial relationships effectively without requiring handcrafted anchors for object detection tasks39. Vision-specific modifications like DeIT (Data-efficient Image Transformer) decreased dependency on large datasets by improving training efficiency40. Chen et al. developed MedT as one of the first transformer-based medical image segmentation models, which demonstrated better performance than CNN baselines across numerous biomedical imaging datasets41. UNet-like architectures saw advanced segmentation capabilities through the integration of transformer encoders, as shown by TransUNet on both 2D and 3D medical image modalities42.

The creation of hybrid models that integrate both CNN and transformer components marked an essential advancement for computational efficiency. The CoTr (CNN-Transformer Hybrid) model extracted initial features using convolutional layers and then processed global context information through transformers43. The PVT (Pyramid Vision Transformer) architecture introduced hierarchical feature extraction similar to CNN feature pyramids to improve performance with dense prediction tasks44. The computational cost of applying vanilla Vision Transformers to high-resolution CXR images remains high because the self-attention mechanism exhibits quadratic growth with increased image size45. The Swin Transformer resolved the computational bottleneck by implementing window-based attention mechanisms combined with shifting techniques that lowered computational costs while sustaining performance levels46. The hierarchical structure of Swin enabled effective segmentation because it captured detailed and broad contextual information at multiple scales47.

The advancement of Swin Transformer V2 included post-normalization techniques along with cosine attention mechanisms and dynamic learning rate scaling, helping maintain stable training performance across various input resolutions21. Medical imaging applications gain a significant advantage from robustness because imaging protocols and equipment lead to large variations in dataset resolutions. Research into gating mechanisms has occurred alongside architectural changes to enable dynamic control of information flow within models. Feature map selection in Gated-Attention Networks occurs based on task relevance, and this principle has been enhanced through Gated Vision Transformers (GViT)48. The GViT framework incorporated explicit gates into its attention mechanism, enhancing the model’s focus on essential areas while minimizing distractions from irrelevant background noise49. GViT’s ability to target essential features gives it a distinct advantage in analyzing CXR images, where subtle abnormalities often become indistinguishable from anatomical variability and artifacts.

Multiple research studies have validated the effectiveness of GViT-like structures for medical uses. Park et al. implemented gated transformers for brain tumor classification, which led to better attention localization and higher classification accuracy than standard transformers50. Similarly, Xu et al. used gated attention mechanisms to identify diabetic retinopathy in retinal fundus images, resulting in state-of-the-art detection performance51. Research on thoracic disease detection has led to the application of transformer models for solving multi-label classification problems. Wang et al. developed the Multi-Label Transformer (MLT), which can detect multiple thoracic diseases with high sensitivity simultaneously52. Further, the Disease-Aware Transformer (DAT) was developed by Zhu et al., using disease-specific attention modules to better distinguish between pathologies that look visually similar53. Current research tends to address either classification or segmentation separately, without integrating both tasks into a single framework. Classification-only models lack spatial localization capabilities essential for diagnosis, while segmentation-only systems rely on pre-existing disease knowledge. Merging classification and localization functionalities into one system represents a vital research gap that needs to be addressed. K. Chen et al., represent the latest among dual-task models that use shared transformer backbones to perform classification and segmentation simultaneously54. The performance of these models shows degradation through task interference since optimizing one task negatively affects the other55. The development of transformer modules that can achieve task-specific optimization while preserving mutual task compatibility continues to be an unsolved problem.

Despite the impressive achievements of current strategies, there are still limits. CNN-based models have local receptive fields and, hence, have trouble with long-range dependencies. Transformer models tend to be computationally expensive and require large data sets for training. The hybrid architectures that integrate classification and segmentation tasks often suffer from performance issues. In addition to that, numerous known techniques do not have clinical significance, as they poorly cover the aspects of localization and classification. Finally, they lack sufficient validation in actual clinical databases; this constrains their injurious application to health settings.

This study presents a dual-model system that merges GViT capabilities for disease classification with Swin Transformer V2 capabilities for lung segmentation and disease localization. The system maintains distinct models for classification and segmentation tasks to eliminate interference and fully utilize the unique advantages of each transformer architecture. Multiple dataset evaluations show that the proposed approach works well and achieves better performance metrics than the current best single-model and hybrid systems. Transformer-based models have transformed medical image analysis by solving CNNs’ inherent limitations, and combining specialized transformer architectures for disease categorization and abnormality localization offers an innovative route to enhance CAD systems for thoracic disease detection.

Methodology

In this study, we implemented a two-stage deep learning-based framework for the classification and segmentation of chest diseases using Chest X-ray (CXR) images, focusing specifically on five categories: The study examines five distinct categories for chest disease analysis, which include COVID-19 cases along with Pneumonia, Tuberculosis, Lung Cancer, and Normal cases. For the classification task, we utilized the Gated Vision Transformer (GVIT), trained on three publicly available datasets that are COVID-19 Radiography Database includes 3,616 images of COVID-19 cases and 10,192 Normal images along with 1,345 Viral Pneumonia chest X-rays56, Tuberculosis Chest X-ray Database contains 2,800 images of TB and 3,500 Normal images57, while the NIH Chest X-rays dataset features 14 thoracic diseases including masses and nodules related to lung cancer58. After images are classified, a lung segmentation process isolates lung regions while reducing background noise and improving the extraction of disease-specific features. For the disease segmentation stage, we used the Swin Transformer V2 model, trained with class-specific masked datasets: Our disease segmentation stage utilized the Swin Transformer V2 model, which was trained using datasets with segmentation masks including COVID-19 Segmentation and Severity Scoring Dataset59, RSNA Pneumonia Detection Challenge Dataset60, Chest X-ray Masks and Labels for Tuberculosis61, JSRT Dataset with Segmentation Masks for Lung Cancer and Lung cancer segmentation dataset with Lung-RADS class62 and Chest X-ray Lung Segmentation Dataset for lungs segmentation63. The structured methodology of initial disease classification with GVIT and subsequent lung and disease segmentation using Swin Transformer V2 allowed for accurate identification and localization of these diseases. Model integration

Unified diagnostic pipeline

A combined diagnostic method that uses Gated Vision Transformer (GViT) for classification and Swin Transformer V2 for object detection has been developed to improve chest disease detection accuracy and robustness from radiograph images. Each diagnostic step benefits from model optimizations specific to classification and localization tasks by utilizing the combined strengths of both models. The diagnostic process starts by using GViT to analyze chest radiographs to determine their fitness within COVID-19, Pneumonia, Tuberculosis (TB), and Cancer disease classifications. Once the classification ends, the Swin Transformer V2 starts detecting areas that correspond to the identified disease category.

Input Preprocessing Radiograph standardization during preprocessing requires adjustments to their size, resolution, and intensity levels. This preprocessing stage assists models in preserving uniform input formats, thereby reducing input variation that affects their performance.

Classification with GViT The GViT model ingests preprocessed radiographs to identify the disease category—the classification output functions as a crucial decision point that activates subsequent processing steps.

ROI Detection with Swin Transformer V2 After disease classification, the Swin Transformer V2 model works to detect and localize affected regions in radiographs. The model uses bounding boxes and segmentation masks to detect relevant areas.

Post-Processing and Visualization Non-maximum suppression (NMS) and thresholding as post-processing methods enhance the detection of regions. The final output consists of the classification result combined with a segmented image that distinctly marks affected disease areas. The output created provides clinicians with essential information for their diagnostic processes.

Model synchronization and workflow

The modular pipeline design of the workflow enables both the GViT model and the Swin Transformer V2 model to function cooperatively while maintaining synchronization and seamless integration. The GViT model initiates the workflow by generating results for thoracic disease classification, which in turn directs the Swin Transformer V2 to perform joint object detection and segmentation tasks. Optimized data pipelines and shared memory structures facilitate efficient intermediate data exchange between models for feature maps and classification scores while reducing latency for concurrent operations. The diagnostic process becomes faster when the Swin Transformer V2 and GViT model work asynchronously because Swin Transformer V2 parameters are loaded while GViT processes the image input. The system contains an error-handling feature that identifies and resolves classification-detection stage inconsistencies while a feedback mechanism re-evaluates classification to guarantee accurate final results when discrepancies occur. The pipeline’s scalable and adaptable architecture allows the addition of new models and diagnostic tools so future challenges can be met without requiring major system re-engineering.

Conclusion

The study presents a new dual-model framework that utilizes the Gated Vision Transformer (GViT) for chest disease classification and the Swin Transformer V2 for object detection and localization. Our approach resolves the main difficulties that radiologists encounter when trying to differentiate between various chest diseases, which show similar features in radiographic images, such as COVID-19, Pneumonia, Tuberculosis, and lung cancer. The proposed system achieves enhanced diagnostic accuracy by combining model strengths while delivering precise localization of affected regions. The experimental results show that our dual-model approach produces high classification accuracy along with strong performance in precision, recall, and F1-score metrics. The application of Swin Transformer V2 for precise segmentation greatly improves result interpretation by enabling detailed localization of abnormalities. Reducing diagnostic uncertainty through this system proves especially beneficial for healthcare settings with limited resources because radiologists there face high volumes of work and urgent cases. Through its findings, this research expands medical imaging AI research by demonstrating how transformer-based models improve diagnostic precision. Advanced models help to lessen radiologists’ cognitive burden while decreasing misclassification rates, which leads to improved patient care. Subsequent research endeavors will likely concentrate on dataset expansion and the inclusion of additional disease categories while refining existing models for better real-time clinical performance.
