Pharmaceutical Metabolite Data Base, PharmMet DB: Reference data base for drug metabolites generated by human liver S9 fraction
Authors: Jennifer Jeon, Zachery R. Jarrell, Choon-Myung Lee, Grant Singer, Jaclyn Weinberg, Ken H. Liu, Edward T. Morgan, Young-Mi Go, Dean P. Jones
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drug monitoring is an essential component of precision therapeutics, yet existing data bases to support therapeutic monitoring are limited to data curated from the scientific literature or predicted in silico. We used human liver S9 fraction to generate metabolites from 1114 therapeutic drugs spanning diverse drug classes. Metabolites were analyzed by liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry, annotated through differential analysis of preincubation and postincubation samples, curated by comparison to predicted metabolites from BioTransformer 3.0, and compiled into a human liver pharmaceutical metabolite resource, named “Pharmaceutical Metabolite Data Base (PharmMet DB).” Liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry showed heterogeneity in product generation, with some drugs mostly being converted to predicted metabolites, while others were converted to hundreds of unpredicted products characterized by mass-to-charge ratio and chromatographic retention time. Phase I metabolism was dominant, with 30,752 oxidized drug metabolites. Glucuronidation was dominant for phase II metabolism, with 6311 drug metabolites. Notably, 89% of tested drugs produced at least 1 metabolite that was not predicted on BioTransformer 3.0, and these novel metabolites were most frequently detected for anti-inflammatory, central nervous system and antimicrobial drug classes. PharmMet DB provides experimental metabolite profiles to detect therapeutic drug exposures in human biospecimens without a requirement for prescription history. PharmMet DB usage with human epidemiology will advance pharmacometabolomics to improve understanding of drug efficacy, adverse reactions, and interactions in precision medicine.Significance StatementPharmaceutical Metabolite Data Base is a new data base of therapeutic drug metabolites suitable for use with liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry to monitor patient adherence, detect unreported drug use, for example, in clinical trials, and enhance pharmacoexposomics and pharmacogenomics research. The data base was generated by incubation of therapeutic agents with human liver S9 fraction and curated relative to in silico predicted metabolites. Associated metadata for metabolic processes and drug classes enhance utility for clinical use, especially with untargeted metabolomics analyses of human samples.

Introduction

Therapeutic drug monitoring is important to advance pharmacological research and ultimately provide health care with capabilities for precision therapeutics. Currently, available untargeted high-resolution metabolomics methods are well suited to enhance therapeutic drug monitoring because combinations of standardized liquid chromatography (LC), electrospray ionization (ESI), and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) provide powerful capabilities to detect and measure hundreds of therapeutic drugs and drug metabolites.1 In principle, standardized LC-HRMS approaches could provide an alternative to labor-intensive and costly targeted analyses and improve the detection of low-abundance therapeutic drug metabolites in large-scale studies.1,2

Data bases and computational tools such as DrugBank3 and BioTransformer4 are available to support the use of HRMS for therapeutic drug monitoring. DrugBank is an extensive assembly of documented drug and drug-metabolite relationships curated from the scientific literature.3 BioTransformer uses advanced machine learning and rule-based algorithms for in silico prediction of drug metabolites. Limitations exist for the detection of metabolites not previously reported in the literature and for predicted metabolites without experimental validation. To address these limitations, we developed the Pharmaceutical Metabolite Data Base (PharmMet DB), which provides experimentally validated metabolite data for 1114 therapeutic drugs. Integrated use of these data with LC-HRMS workflows can enhance therapeutic drug monitoring, especially for clinically undocumented therapeutic drug use and detection of low-abundance drug metabolites.

The use of PharmMet DB for therapeutic drug monitoring leverages concepts of enzyme-based chemical identification using human liver S9 fractions.5 Multiple metabolites derived from a single parent drug create a composite “metabolite signature” which can be leveraged to enhance detection and identification, overcoming a key limitation of targeted drug monitoring when signals are too low for ion dissociation (tandem mass spectrometry) analysis.5 This strategy improves sensitivity and expands the scope of analyses for efficacy, safety, and pharmacokinetics of therapeutic agents. The human liver S9 system introduces phase I reactions, such as oxidation-reduction reactions catalyzed by the cytochrome P450 family enzymes,6 and phase II conjugation reactions to enhance water solubility for excretion.7 The liver S9 fraction contains microsomes and cytosol and is a cost-effective and reproducible in vitro system to demonstrate human metabolic processes.8 With the addition of appropriate cofactors, the system generates phase I and II metabolites9 to facilitate drug identification in untargeted metabolomics workflows through accurate mass-to-charge (m/z) matching.

The present study describes the development, structure, and characterization of PharmMet DB. By employing standardized LC-HRMS analyses, the data base addresses critical challenges in consistency and reproducibility for the detection of human liver metabolites from diverse drugs. By requiring detection of at least 2 drug metabolites for identification, one can minimize false positives to obtain a scalable and cost-effective design for clinical and epidemiologic investigations. In principle, PharmMet DB can support therapeutic drug monitoring in human samples without reliance upon prescription history or patient compliance.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials

Unless otherwise stated, materials were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Human liver S9 fractions (H0640.S9) and NADPH-regenerating system (K5000-10) were obtained from XenoTech. UDP-glucuronic acid, glutathione, 3′-phosphoadenosine-5′phosphosulfate, acetyl-CoA, and ammonium acetate were purchased from Sigma Aldrich at ≥98% purity. Formic acid (Optima Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry [LC-MS] grade), acetonitrile (Optima LC-MS grade), and water (Optima LC-MS grade) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Drug selection and prioritization

To enhance capabilities for high-throughput drug biomonitoring, we selected a diverse range of therapeutic drugs commercially available in U.S. and international drug collections. These include Food and Drug Administration–approved and currently available therapeutics as well as some that have been withdrawn but are still relevant to archival samples available in human sample repositories. All selected compounds were from Microsource Spectrum Collection (Discovery Systems, Inc), supplied either as 10 mM in DMSO in 96-well microplates or as 5 mg solid samples in glass screw-top vials; each compound was accompanied by chemical and biological annotations (structure, CAS Registry number, molecular weight, formula, and relevant literature). Prioritization of drugs was informed by review of the literature and data from DrugBank 5.0,3 public health statistics from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention,10,11 and literature available on PubMed.12 This approach included clinically significant drugs spanning common therapeutic classes, with a focus on highly prescribed drugs and those with known metabolic side effects.

Drug incubation and metabolic activation

Drug metabolism experiments were conducted using human liver S9 fractions (H0640.S9, XenoTech) as described by Liu et al.5 In brief, pooled human liver S9 fractions (20 mg/mL protein) were aliquoted into 0.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes, stored at −80 °C, and thawed at room temperature before use. Stock solutions of selected compounds were diluted to 40 μM with water before being mixed with the S9 fraction (final protein concentration 0.5 mg/mL in the reaction). A cofactor mixture was prepared containing 10 mM UDP-glucuronic acid, 2 mM glutathione, 2 mg/mL 3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate, 0.1 mM acetyl-CoA, and an NADPH-regenerating system (K5000-10, XenoTech) comprised 1 mM NADP+, 5 mM glucose-6-phosphate, and 1 unit of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase. On a 96-well plate, 15 μL of S9 fraction (diluted 10-fold in 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) was mixed in a 1:1 ratio (v:v) with 0.2 M Tris-Cl (pH 7.5 containing 2 mM MgCl2) to make a total of 30 μL. Then, the drug stock solution (15 μL each) was added and preincubated at 30 °C for 5 minutes before the reaction. The reaction was initiated by adding 15 μL of the cofactor mixture (final reaction volume: 60 μL), and the incubation continued at 30 °C for 24 hours. At the end of the incubation period, 3 volumes of ice-cold acetonitrile were added to stop the enzymatic activity, and samples were frozen at −20 °C prior to mass spectral analysis. For mass spectral analysis, samples were thawed, centrifuged to remove precipitated protein, and the supernatants were transferred to 96-well sample plates for analysis.

LC-HRMS analysis

A dual-column, dual ESI strategy was employed with hydrophilic interaction LC (HILIC) coupled to positive ESI and C18 chromatography coupled to negative ESI. The run time was 5 minutes for each LC-HRMS platform. HILIC was used to capture relatively polar metabolites, and reverse-phase C18 chromatography was used to capture relatively nonpolar metabolites. For each analysis, 5 μL of the sample was injected onto a Thermo Scientific Vanquish UHPLC coupled to a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap ID-X Tribrid mass spectrometer. MS1 spectra were acquired at 60,000 resolution with an m/z scan range of 100 to 1000, and MS2 spectra were acquired at 15,000 resolution with HCD 35%. For the HILIC method, a Waters Aquity UPLC BEH Amide HILIC column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm particle size) was used with mobile phases consisting of water (solvent A) and 95% acetonitrile (solvent B), both modified with 1 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid. The gradient began at 90% B for 0.6 minute, ramped linearly to 20% across 1.95 minutes, and held at 20% for 2.45 minutes. For the C18 method, a Waters UPLC Hypersil Gold C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.9 μm particle size) was used with mobile phases consisting of water (solvent A) and 99% acetonitrile (solvent B), both modified with 1 mM ammonium acetate. The gradient began at 1% B for 0.5 minute, ramped to 99% B across 0.75 minute, and held for 3.75 minutes. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min for both methods. S9 biotransformation samples were prepared in experimental duplicates, and each was analyzed by LC-HRMS with a single technical replicate. This study is available at the NIH Common Fund’s National Metabolomics Data Repository website, the Metabolomics Workbench, https://www.metabolomicsworkbench.org, where it has been assigned Study ID ST003991. The data can be accessed directly via its Project; DOI: https://doi.org/10.21228/M8W55K.13

Metabolite detection and data analysis

Raw files were processed using MZmine 2.53 with ADAP chromatogram builder. Candidate drug metabolite feature detection was carried out by comparing mass spectral data (MS1) and retention times (RT) between preincubation (0 hour) and postincubation (24 hours) samples. Curation of observed mass signals was performed by cross-referencing the detected signals (±5 ppm) with predictions from the BioTransformer data base. For the potential metabolites from S9 incubations, the identification criteria included: (1) detection of the metabolite exclusively in samples containing both the precursor and cosubstrates, (2) mass accuracy for predicted signal within ±5 ppm and coelution of co-occurring adducts within 3 seconds, (3) a time-dependent increase in signal intensity from 0 to 24 hours with a minimum of 10-fold higher peak area compared to the maximum observed in all other samples. It should be noted that this MS detection approach does not provide information about positional isomers. Additionally, some metabolic products detected at the 24-hour time point that were not predicted by BioTransformer 3.0 were categorized as novel drug metabolites, which require further structural identification. Unpredicted metabolites are reported by paired m/z and RT. Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed with R version 4.0.3 and GraphPad Prism 6.0.

Results

PharmMet DB structure

PharmMet DB is accessible through a user-friendly interface at (https://clinicalbiomarkerslaboratory.shinyapps.io/PharmMet/). As described later, the data base was generated from the input list of 1114 drugs, with fields containing predicted metabolites and reaction type from BioTransformer 3.0, detected drug metabolites from S9 incubations curated against the predicted metabolites, associated LC-HRMS data for accurate mass m/z for detected drug metabolites, and m/z and RT for novel metabolites not predicted by BioTransformer 3.0. Two types of search methods are available in the PharmMet DB: (1) drug-based and (2) LC-MS–based search. The drug-based search allows users to filter results by drug name, drug group, drug type, reaction phase, and/or reaction type (Fig. 1). The LC-MS–based search allows users to search by m/z, and RT, with an adjustable m/z tolerance. Additionally, an expanded panel supports access to additional information such as the molecular formula, reaction details, associated biosystems, enzymes, InChI, InChIKey, and SMILES predicted from BioTransformer (Fig. 1). The complete data base is also available for download at (https://github.com/ClinicalBiomarkersLaborabory/PharmMet), allowing the integration with other annotation platforms, such as xMSannotator,14 for users interested in applying additional annotation criteria.

Therapeutic drug composition of PharmMet DB

The complete breakdown of drug classes of 1114 therapeutic drugs in PharmMet DB (Supplemental Table 1) includes drugs distributed across 10 therapeutic categories (antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory, cardiovascular, central nervous system (CNS), endocrine and metabolic, respiratory, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, hematological, and others) (Fig. 2). The largest group includes antimicrobial agents (n = 256; 23%), followed by anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory drugs (n = 219; 20%), CNS drugs (n = 163; 15%), and cardiovascular agents (n = 216; 19%) (Fig. 2). Smaller but pharmacologically significant groups include endocrine and metabolic drugs (n = 91; 8%), respiratory (n = 31; 3%), gastrointestinal (n = 25; 2%), hematological (n = 11; 1%), and other groups (n = 76; 7%) (Fig. 2). Detection rates by biotransformation reaction types

Drug metabolites generated by the human liver S9 fraction were curated by accurate mass m/z matching relative to the in silico predictions from BioTransformer 3.0. A total of 130,464 metabolites were predicted across all drugs using BioTransformer 3.0, representing both phase I and II reactions (Fig. 3). Among them, 59,869 (45.89%) were detected experimentally by LC-HRMS following human liver S9 fraction incubation. Phase I oxidation reactions generated the most predicted (n = 59,296) and detected metabolites (n = 30,752) with detection rate of 52% (Fig. 3). Reduction (n = 7114 detected of 10,899 predicted) and hydrolysis (n = 3184 detected of 5744 predicted) reactions showed higher detection rates compared to oxidation at 65% and 55%, respectively (Fig. 3). Among the phase II reactions, methylation (50%) and acetylation (45%) showed high detection rates, while glutathione-conjugation reaction was detected at lowest rate (21%) (Fig. 3). Although S-adenosylmethionine was not supplemented in the incubation mixture, detection of methylated metabolites (3136 detected of 6235 predicted) is consistent with low-level methylation activity supported by endogenous S-adenosylmethionine in the liver S9 fraction. While the proportion of methylated metabolites detected was relatively high, the total number of features was substantially lower than that observed for major phase I reactions. These results highlight the variability in detection efficiency across various biotransformation reaction types, which may be influenced by prediction accuracy, enzymatic activity, and analytical detectability under in vitro conditions.Fig. 3Detection coverage of predicted metabolites by biotransformation reactions. Detection rates by therapeutic drug groups

Metabolite detection coverage varied across therapeutic drug groups (Fig. 4). CNS drugs had the highest number of detected metabolites (n = 17,967), corresponding to 49% of 36,406 predicted metabolites in this group (Fig. 4). Cardiovascular (n = 13,089 detected of 28,302 predicted), endocrine and metabolic (n = 4685 of 10,064), and anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory (n = 11,578 of 24,929) also had higher numbers of detected metabolites and comparable detection rates with 46%, 47%, and 46%, respectively (Fig. 4). Antimicrobial drugs had the second highest number of predicted metabolites (n = 33,203) and a slightly lower detection rate of 43%. In contrast, musculoskeletal drugs had the lowest detection rate at 33% (n = 1108 detected of 3391 predicted) (Fig. 4). Detected metabolites from CNS drugs were primarily from oxidation (n = 15,238), glucuronidation (n = 4923), and sulfation (n = 2323). By contrast, cardiovascular, endocrine, and anti-inflammatory drugs mainly underwent reduction (11%–13% vs 7% in CNS). Sulfation was more prominent in the CNS compared to most groups, whereas musculoskeletal drugs, although lower in total number, were dominated by oxidation reactions (52%). These findings highlight that drug class-specific features, such as structural complexity or metabolic accessibility, may influence metabolite detection. Observed differences may also be explained by differences in matrix effects or differences in ionization efficiency in LC-HRMS analysis across different therapeutic drug groups.Fig. 4Metabolite detection rates by therapeutic drug group. Detection of novel metabolites

In addition to metabolites matching in silico predictions, we observed many mass spectral signals representing metabolites that were not predicted by BioTransformer 3.0. A representative LC-HRMS example is shown for carvedilol (Fig. 5), comparing 0- and 24-hour incubations and illustrating precursor loss (m/z 407.1950), formation of predicted hydroxy- and glucuronide metabolites (m/z 455.1801, 583.2273), and detection of unpredicted features. Out of 1114 drugs analyzed, 997 (89%) of drugs produced at least 1 nonpredicted metabolite, which were the LC-HRMS features detected only in postincubation samples and not matched to any predicted m/z within ±5 ppm tolerance (Fig. 6). Across all drug groups, a total of 486,215 unpredicted metabolite features were detected. Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory drugs contributed the highest number of unpredicted features (n = 126,210), followed by antimicrobial drugs (n = 100,617), cardiovascular drugs (n = 88,653), and CNS drugs (n = 62,693) (Fig. 6). While the gastrointestinal group included only 25 drugs total, all drugs showed evidence of unpredicted metabolism, with most at very high proportions. The number of drugs with at least 1 unpredicted metabolic feature within each group also varied, with antimicrobial (n = 235), CNS (n = 198), and anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory (n = 192) groups showing the highest drug counts (Fig. 6). These findings underscore the need for additional refinement of current biotransformation models to capture the enzymatic complexity observed under experimental conditions. Inclusion of these unpredicted, empirically detected metabolites in PharmMet DB provides an expanded experimental reference to support untargeted therapeutic drug detection in clinical metabolomics studies.

Discussion

PharmMet DB provides a resource with experimentally validated drug metabolite data to support high-throughput therapeutic drug monitoring. While not completely comprehensive, the 1114 active therapeutic agents included in PharmMet DB include a majority of commonly used drugs in the United States. The associated LC-HRMS characteristics of drug metabolites address critical gaps in pharmacological and toxicologic research, where the inability to identify low-abundance and undocumented metabolites has been a critical challenge in cross-platform comparison of drug monitoring for nondocumented drug use.

PharmMet DB provides an organized assembly of a vast number of drug metabolites, which can be used to systematically examine untargeted metabolomics data for therapeutic drug usage. Notably, hundreds of metabolites were generated from some drugs, consistent with prior research highlighting the diversity of metabolites among therapeutic drugs, particularly those with complex chemical structures.15 In addition to predicted metabolites, metabolites from reactive intermediates, secondary metabolic reactions, or novel metabolites involving cytochrome P450 enzymes16,17 can potentially be useful for new pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and drug toxicity studies. The high prevalence of such metabolites across all drug classes highlights the complexity of drug metabolism and suggests the need for further mechanistic studies.

Limitations and future directions

A limitation of the design was reliance upon the liver S9 fraction for biotransformation. While providing a robust in vitro system for hepatic drug metabolism, not all drug-metabolizing activities are present in this fraction. Thus, PharmMet DB is expected to lack metabolites formed by enzymes that are not abundant or active in the liver S9 fraction or that are generated in extrahepatic compartments (eg, intestine, kidney, liver, plasma). Expanding the data base to include metabolic profiles from extrahepatic tissues and the intestinal microbiome will further enhance utility for comprehensive pharmacoexposomics studies. Comparison of in vitro S9-derived metabolite profiles to animal and clinical samples will be essential for determining the physiological importance of these metabolites. We demonstrated examples of this translation in our previous work using orthogonal criteria for identification.5 MS2 data were collected during data acquisition and are available on Metabolomics Workbench (ST003991). Second, while HRMS offers high sensitivity and specificity, the identification of novel metabolites from nearly 1000 drugs will require considerable research for isolation and structural elucidation. Because of the high-throughput design to study 1000 drugs, limited detection of some metabolites may have occurred due to limited cofactor availability or ionization suppression.

Future therapeutic monitoring studies will need to focus on the detection and biological activity of the novel metabolites to understand their potential role in pharmacodynamics and toxicity. These characterizations will need structural verification and definition of relevant pathways and enzymes. Extended chromatographic methods and detailed analysis of ion dissociation (MS2) data will be essential to verify chemical structures and resolve isomers. Ultimately, verification with authentic standards and measurement in human matrices will be required to establish physiological relevance. Our prior research shows that liver S9-generated metabolites, as found in PharmMet DB, are useful in clinical contexts3; future extension to therapeutic drug monitoring, exposomics studies, and pharmacovigilance can be expected to enhance the detection of drug exposures and improve the interpretation of adverse drug reactions.

Conclusion

The PharmMet DB represents a new resource for drug metabolism research, enabling high-throughput detection of human liver–derived therapeutic drug metabolites, including signals from previously undocumented metabolites. By integrating enzymatic metabolite generation with LC-HRMS workflows, PharmMet DB addresses critical limitations of existing tools and facilitates more accurate biomonitoring. This can enhance the development of precision therapeutics to provide reliable, scalable platforms for global pharmacometabolomics.
