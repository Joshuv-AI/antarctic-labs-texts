Sex Differences and Psychological Assessments in Chronic Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Harvey Sudharta, Marson Putra, Ika Suswanti, Kevin Kristian, Isadora Gracia Sahusilawane, Jimmy Fransisco Abadinta Barus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMental illnesses often coexist with pain, forming a vicious cycle that exacerbate disabilities. Existing sex biases and conflicting study results remain contentious. The primary outcome of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to compare different psychological assessment scores across sexes in chronic pain populations.MethodWe performed a systematic review of the association of mental health outcomes among different sexes in the chronic pain population based on a comprehensive search of PubMed, ScienceDirect, ProQuest, and EBSCOhost. A meta-analysis was conducted based on available data to clarify the association between psychological test results and sex differences. Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI tool) was used to verify the quality of the evidence. The Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) with a confidence interval (CI) of 95% were used to determine the association.ResultsNine cross-sectional studies were included in the meta-analysis (3,003 participants), comprising four anxiety and eight depression assessments. The JBI tool rated all studies as high quality. The analysis showed that females had significantly lower anxiety scores (SMD - 0.10; p = 0.03) with chronic pain. The depression scores did not differ between sexes (SMD 0.13; p = 0.20).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that females had significantly lower anxiety scores than males in chronic pain populations, with no differences in depression. Therefore, care should unbiasedly address the pain experience and mental health in males, which is often underrecognized among chronic pain sufferers.

Introduction

Pain is one of the most common symptoms for patients seeking medical care (Cohen et al., 2021). When pain persists beyond normal tissue healing time or recurs for more than three months, it is classified as chronic pain—a leading cause of disability worldwide (Treede et al., 2019). Globally, chronic pain affects approximately 20–30% of adults, representing a major public health challenge (Cohen et al., 2021). According to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), chronic pain is categorized into two types; chronic primary pain as a distinct clinical disorder, and chronic secondary pain as a symptom of other diseases (Treede et al., 2019). In the latest International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), chronic pain diagnoses can be further specified based on pain severity, temporal characteristics, and psychosocial factors. Psychological determinants are increasingly recognised as major contributors to chronic pain onset, persistence, and exacerbation (Landmark et al., 2024). Therefore, assessing psychological factors is essential for understanding the biopsychosocial mechanisms underlying chronic pain (Barke et al., 2022).

Chronic pain often leads to maladaptive cognitions and behaviours, reduced daily functioning, and heightened stress, which may worsen the pain experience. Conversely, psychological distress can perpetuate chronic pain and contribute to long‑term disability (Landmark et al., 2024). Individuals with better psychological well‑being tend to report lower pain severity and improved physical functioning (Williamson et al., 2021). Mental health disorders and chronic pain frequently co‑occur, potentially due to shared biological and psychosocial mechanisms (Yao et al., 2023). Thus, chronic pain can only be best understood through the biopsychosocial framework (Kovačević et al., 2024). Individuals with chronic pain are three times more likely to have depressive symptoms as compared to those without chronic pain (Meda et al., 2022), with a strong correlation reported between depression and chronic pain, such as back pain (Yang et al., 2023). Furthermore, patients with severe pain also experience higher levels of mental distress compared to those with mild to moderate pain (Hnatešen et al., 2022). Despite the strong evidence, many psychological symptoms associated with chronic pain have gone unnoticed due to individual variability in responses as well as the lack of adequate assessment tools in clinical practice. Nevertheless, appropriate psychological instruments are needed to objectively evaluate psychological states and behavioral characteristics in individuals with chronic pain (de Graaff et al., 2021).

Pain experiences and mental health-related outcomes appear to vary between sexes (Morgan et al., 2024). Although extensive literature has examined sex differences in psychological factors, the extent to which these differences manifest in chronic pain populations remains unclear. Some studies report a higher prevalence of mental health disorders among females (Idowu et al., 2022; Maestre-Miquel et al., 2021) while the other suggest higher mental distress among males (Chatmon, 2020). Females also tend to report greater pain sensitivity, a wider range of pain syndromes, and stronger affective responses to pain (Templeton, 2020). Various factors may contribute to these sex differences, including sex-hormonal milieu, social gender inequality, gender-based violence, and gender discrimination (Idowu et al., 2022; Templeton, 2020).

Despite these observations, sex is often overlooked in clinical and rehabilitation approaches to chronic pain (eClinicalMedicine, 2024). Moreover, the relationship between sex, psychological symptoms, and chronic pain remains inconclusive, and no previous systematic review or meta‑analysis has comprehensively synthesized this evidence. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta‑analysis of cross‑sectional studies to evaluate sex differences in anxiety, depression, and related psychological symptoms among individuals with chronic pain.

Method

2.1 Study Registration and Methodology

This study followed the latest Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement guideline with a predetermined search strategy to identify, select, appraise, and synthesize studies (Page et al., 2021). The protocol used in this study has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024554916).

2.2 Literature Search

For the systematic review and meta-analysis, we conducted a structured literature search from PubMed, ScienceDirect, EBSCOhost, and ProQuest using the following MeSH term “Sex”, “Emotions”, “Anxiety”, “Depression”, “Sleep disorders”, “Psychological tests”, and “Chronic pain”. table 1 provides our complete search. All studies from inception until December 2024 were included. We focused on depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders, as these are the most common psychological disorders found in patients with chronic pain. The search terms for psychological tests were broad enough to encompass every study assessing psychological states, as there are various validated scales or inventories to assess psychological disorders. We also reviewed each article’s references to identify additional studies not determined by the initial search.

2.3 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for the present study are: (1) Human cross-sectional studies, (2) studies in chronic pain population, (3) studies assessing psychological state using any validated assessment tools, and (4) studies analyzing the difference between sex (male vs female) in terms of assessment tools score. The following studies were excluded: (1) all types of review studies, case reports, case series, unpublished articles, conference papers, preliminary reports, and correspondences, (2) studies on acute/subacute pain population, (3) studies with non-validated psychological assessment tools or non-English assessment tools, (4) studies lacking sex-stratified quantitative data, and (5) interventional studies reporting only pre-/post-intervention comparisons.

2.4 Data Extraction

Two researchers independently screened and evaluated all retrieved articles based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, which were then cross-checked following data extraction using the same table. If the two researchers are unable to reach a unified agreement, the issue will be discussed among all researchers. We extracted the following data from every individual study: (1) first author; (2) publication year; (3) region; (4) study design; (5) study population (sample size); (6) sex (women to men ratio); (7) type of chronic pain, (8) assessment, (9) assessment tools/instrument; (10) study outcomes. The types of chronic pain were further categorized into malignancy chronic pain and non-malignancy chronic pain for subgroup analysis purposes. The specific outcome measures recorded for the systematic review were the difference in mean scores of psychological assessment tools for depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders among different sexes. We resolved all conflicting data with consensus among all the authors to ensure the data’s accuracy.

As part of data extraction, we recorded whether each study explicitly considered causal directionality between chronic pain and psychiatric symptoms (anxiety and/or depression). Specifically, we noted whether studies: (1) treated pain as an exposure and anxiety/ depression as outcomes only; (2) tested bidirectional associations or reverse causality; (3) used longitudinal designs with temporal ordering sufficient to infer direction; or (4) applied causal inference methods (e.g., cross-lagged panel models, instrumental variables, mediation analysis, or other approaches designed to address reverse causation). We report the number of studies in each category and use this information to qualify interpretation of pooled findings.

2.5 Risk of Bias Assessment

In the present study, two independent authors use the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI tool) to assess the risk of bias of the included studies. The JBI tool is a 10-item critical appraisal tool for the cross-sectional study and is the preferred tool, especially in analytical cross-sectional studies. The JBI tool is also suitable to be used for assessing descriptive cross-sectional studies (Ma et al., 2020). We present the total score and inter-rater reliability test (Cohen’s κ value) for this risk of bias assessment. Any disagreement between the two authors will be resolved by third parties.

2.6 Meta-Analysis

The standardized mean differences (SMD) or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI) in mental health outcomes across sexes was the effect-size measure used in the present study. For this study, the effect size was calculated as the mean score for females minus that for males divided by the pooled standard deviation. A positive effect size reflects a higher mean score for females.

Chi-square based Q statistical test was used to examine the interstudy heterogeneity. The statistics I2, calculated as the percentage of the total variation of all included studies in the analysis, was used to measure the heterogeneity of effect sizes. We used the Higgins and Green’s criteria (unimportant if 0% < I2 < 40%; moderate if 30% < I2 < 60%; substantial if 50% < I2 < 90%; and considerable if 75% < I2 < 100%) (Higgins et al., 2023). The results would prove significantly heterogeneous if I2 was larger than 50%. The fixed-effect model was used when there was no significant inter-study heterogeneity; otherwise, the random-effect model was used. If the data is sufficient, subgroup analysis will be conducted to find the possible cause of heterogeneity

We assessed publication bias by visually inspecting funnel plots and using the Egger’s bias test, considering a p-value < 0.05 as indicative of publication bias. If there were at least 10 studies, a funnel plot was used to assess publication bias; otherwise, Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation methods were used for continuous and dichotomous outcomes. The symmetric distribution of dots along the no-effect line in the funnel plot indicated the absence of the publication bias while the asymmetric distribution indicated the presence of the publication bias.

We conducted sensitivity and meta-regression analyses to ensure the meta-analysis's robustness. In this meta-analysis, sensitivity analyses were performed using the leave-one-out approach. This analysis was carried out by eliminating one study at a time and looking for changes in the meta-analysis's overall effect size. This analysis shows how each study influences the overall estimate of the other studies. All statistical tests were done using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.3 (Review Manager (RevMan)., 2014) and Comprehensive Meta Analysis (CMA) (Borenstein, 2022).

2.7 Level of Evidence of the Meta-Analyses

We rate the strength of our meta-analysis by applying the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) method as described in the GRADE handbook. GRADE is a structured framework for evaluating and rating the quality of evidence, taking into factors like study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Overall certainty of the evidence was reported as high, moderate, low, or very low quality (Prasad, 2024).

Results

3.1 Search Result

The initial search from the electronic database yielded 915 studies, of which 78 were duplicates and therefore, excluded. After screening the remaining 837 studies by title and abstract, we acquired 24 studies with a full-text eligibility assessment. These articles were reviewed based on the following criteria: human cross-sectional studies, chronic pain population, studies with psychological state measurement using any validated instruments, and available data for both male and female groups. Out of twenty-four full texts reviewed, fifteen articles were excluded: twelve lacked complete data for both groups, two only compared interventions in pre-/post-intervention studies, and one focused on a subacute pain population. Eventually, nine studies were deemed eligible and included in our systematic review, and out of those, eight studies were included and processed in the final meta-analysis. One study was excluded from the final meta-analysis because it used different assessment tools than the others. 3.2 Study Characteristics

All nine studies included are cross-sectional. The study populations in most studies are adults, with mean age ranging from 37.8 years to 51 years. The types of chronic pain in the ten study populations across nine included studies are non-specific pain (3/10), musculoskeletal pain (2/10), spinal pain (2/10), low-back pain (1/10), temporomandibular disorder (TMD) pain (1/10), and urogenital pain (1/10). Pain duration ranged from 2.2 to 16.3 years.

3.3 Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias assessment using JBI critical appraisal checklist for cross-sectional studies resulted in all studies rated as high quality (>50%) by two independent reviewers. Score ranged from 75% to 100%. Inter-rater reliability testing resulted in Cohen’s κ value of 0.88 (very good/almost perfect agreement) before further discussion with the third authors. Agreed end-results were presented in table 3. Most studies failed to identify several important confounding factors, such as a history of previous mental problems, current drug use, or any other confounding factors.

3.4 Mental Health Outcomes and Sex Differences

The results on mental health outcomes of each individual study are summarized in table 4. The study groups were divided into women and men for analysis. Of the nine studies, five (Adams et al., 2008; Canales et al., 2019; Edwards et al., 2003; Fillingim et al., 2003; Harris & D’Eon, 2008) reported that women had greater anxiety/depression scores than men. However, there was only one study (Adams et al., 2008) showing statistically significant results. Furthermore, the study by Heinberg and colleagues (Heinberg et al., 2004) found conflicting results, with higher anxiety/depression scores in women with urogenital chronic pain but not with chronic lower back pain. In contrast, the remaining three studies (Keogh et al., 2006; Ramírez-Maestre & Esteve, 2014; Rovner et al., 2017) revealed that women had lower anxiety/depression levels than men. On the other hand, we found no studies comparing sleep disturbances between sexes in chronic pain populations.

The most used validated assessment tools in the present study were Pain Anxiety Symptom Scale (PASS), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). The original PASS is a 40-item self-report inventory used to assess several dimensions of pain-related anxiety responses. It examines four subscales including cognitive anxiety, escape/avoidance, fearful thoughts, and psychological symptoms of anxiety. The shortened version has 20 items from the main 40-item scale. The subjects rated all items using the 6-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 5 (always). The total score ranged from 0 (no pain anxiety) to 100 (severe pain anxiety), with ≤34 indicating mild, 35 to 67 indicating moderate, and ≥68 indicating severe pain anxiety. The PASS is well validated in adults and younger populations and therefore suitable for the population in the present study (Alonso-Fernández et al., 2022). The BDI is a widely used, 21-item, self-report measure assessing symptoms of depression according to diagnostic criteria listed in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual for Mental Disorders (DSM). The subjects rated all items using the 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. Total scores ranged from 0 to 63, with ≤13 interpreted as no depression, 14 to 19 as mild depression, 20 to 28 as moderate depression, and ≥29 as severe depression. This tool has been extensively applied for research purposes and clinical practice use. Many have studied its validity and reliability across different populations and countries with results showing good internal consistency and test-retest reliability (García-Batista et al., 2018). The HADS is a 14-item self-rating patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) for both hospital and community settings. The 14 items are equally divided for anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D) measurements. The subjects rated all items using the 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. Total score ranged from 0 to 42, or 0 to 21 if both anxiety and depression are separated individually. A score of ≤7 indicates no anxiety or depression, 8 to 10 indicates mild, 11 to 14 indicates moderate, and ≥12 indicates severe anxiety or depression (Beekman & Verhagen, 2018). It is commonly used in epidemiological research to estimate the prevalence of significant anxiety and depression symptoms among adults, including those without any physical symptoms. It is widely used and tested with satisfactory psychometric properties (Sivertsen et al., 2023).

We examined whether the included studies explicitly addressed bidirectional associations or reverse causality between chronic pain and anxiety or depression. One study by Ramirez (Ramírez-Maestre & Esteve, 2014) partially treated pain as an exposure and anxiety/depression as outcomes: although anxiety and depression were modeled as outcomes, pain was also modeled as an outcome of other psychosocial variables, so the study did not implement a simple unidirectional “pain → mood” exposure–outcome framework. The remaining studies were cross‑sectional or descriptive and therefore provide no temporal evidence to distinguish cause from effect. None tested bidirectional associations or reverse causality, used longitudinal designs with temporal ordering sufficient to infer direction, or applied causal‑inference methods to formally address reverse causation.

3.5 Quantitative Data Synthesis on Mental State Outcomes and Sex in Chronic Pain

A meta-analysis of the association between mental state outcomes with sex in chronic pain sufferers was evaluated by calculating the SMD of all eight studies. Four studies (Edwards et al., 2003; Keogh et al., 2006; Ramírez-Maestre & Esteve, 2014; Rovner et al., 2017) were analyzed for anxiety (figure 2), whereas eight studies (Adams et al., 2008; Edwards et al., 2003; Fillingim et al., 2003; Harris & D’Eon, 2008; Heinberg et al., 2004; Keogh et al., 2006; Ramírez-Maestre & Esteve, 2014; Rovner et al., 2017) were analyzed for depression (figure 3). The SMDs were chosen due to the difference in assessment tools used in the measurement of anxiety and depression. To identify variations in the results, we ran a subgroup analysis on every assessment tool individually.

Meta-analyses of anxiety and depression in females vs males in chronic pain sufferers

Subgroup analyses of depression in female vs male in chronic pain sufferers measure by HADS and BDI/BDI-II

Meta-analysis showed that women reported a lower anxiety score (SMD - 0.10; 95% CI: - 0.19 – - 0.01; p = 0.03) than men with chronic pain, but the depression scores (SMD 0.13; 95% CI: - 0.07 – 0.02; p = 0.20) were not different across sexes in chronic pain populations. The interstudy heterogeneity in the anxiety group was 8% and considered low. However, in the depression group, the heterogeneity was 79% and considered high. We used the random effect model for the depression group. We performed a subgroup analysis for the depression group to find the possible cause of heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis included only the HADS assessment tools for depression showed that women had a significantly lower HADS-D score compared to men (SMD - 0.12; 95% CI: - 0.22 – - 0.2; p = 0.02). The current finding was not altered by subgroup analysis that included only BDI/BDI-II assessment tools for depression in the random-effect model analysis (SMD 0.24; 95% CI: - 0.02 – 0.51; p = 0.07). The random-effect meta-analysis weights the studies relatively more equally than the fixed-effect meta-analysis (Higgins et al., 2023). This is more suitable for high heterogeneity studies as in the BDI/BDI-II analysis.

Analysis on BDI/BDI-II assessment tools for depression in the random-effect model analysis (SMD 0.24; 95% CI: - 0.02 – 0.51; p = 0.07) was not statistically significant. The random-effect meta-analysis weights the studies relatively more equally than the fixed-effect meta-analysis, therefore it is more suitable for the high heterogeneity studies in this group (Higgins et al., 2023). Other subgroup analyses on all pain categories, including non-specific pain, musculoskeletal pain, and spinal/low-back pain, yielded no significant findings.

3.6 Publication Bias

Funnel plot could not be generated and was not presented in the present study due to a lack of studies, which was less than the recommended number of ten. For anxiety studies, Kendall’s Tau was 0,25 (p = 0.17) for the Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test. For depression studies, Kendall’s Tau was 0,00001 (p = 0.5) for the Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test. A strong correlation indicates publication bias in funnel plot asymmetry, but this study found no such correlation, suggesting low publication bias.

3.7 Level of Evidence of the Meta-Analyses

Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) assessment results were presented in table 5. The study design affects the starting evidence level, and cohort studies, as an observational study, begin with low confidence but can be upgraded due to characteristics such as big effect studies, the direction of plausible effect, and so on (Prasad, 2024). We used the Meader checklist to fill in the GRADE assessment (Meader et al., 2014). We graded the current evidence level as low since it began with low confidence but was not further degraded due to its low risk of bias, inconsistencies, and indirectness. All authors agreed that the substantial concerns with imprecision did not justify further downgrading the current evidence.

3.8 Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis on both anxiety and depression (table 6) groups showed the robustness of the results. The effect size did not vary considerably as observed in the leave-one-out method. In the anxiety group, the SMDs value varied between -0.07 to -0.13 with a mean SMD of -0.10, similar to our meta-analysis (SMD - 0.10; 95% CI: -0.19 – - 0.01; p = 0.03). In the depression group, the SMDs value varied between 0.05 to 0.18 with a mean SMD of 0.13, similar to our meta-analysis (SMD 0.13; 95% CI: - 0.07 – 0.32; p = 0.20). Thus, the effect size for both anxiety and depression groups remained consistent between medium values. The observed interstudy heterogeneity was 24% (low) and 79% (high) for anxiety and depression groups respectively.

3.9 Meta-regression Analysis

In the current analytic model, no study covariates (mean/median age and women-to-men ratio) were shown to be substantially associated with greater anxiety/depression scores across a range of study-level variables. We hypothesized that the duration of pain, pain scale, and types of chronic pain would be strongly associated, however this information was lacking across studies and needed to be investigated further.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we investigated the relationship between several mental health outcomes (anxiety and depression) and sex differences in chronic pain populations. We compared the scores of numerous validated assessment tools across sexes. We were unable to find studies that compared sleep disturbance between sexes in chronic pain groups. Anxiety and depression were the most studied, thus they were the focus of the meta-analysis.

This meta-analysis showed that anxiety scores were lower in females with chronic pain, while depression was not significantly different between sexes in the overall chronic pain populations. Although the subgroup analysis using the HADS-D assessment tools for depression showed that women had a statistically significant lower HADS-D score compared to men (SMD - 0.12; 95% CI: - 0.22 – - 0.2; p = 0.02), we encourage all readers to interpret this with caution as HADS-D is less reliable for depression assessment compared to BDI (Sivertsen et al., 2023). Subgroup analysis with only BDI/BDI-II showed no significant differences (SMD 0.24; 95% CI: - 0.02 – 0.51; p = 0.07) across sexes. Although the BDI‑II has demonstrated strong internal consistency with Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient of 0.89 (Lee et al., 2017; Sivertsen et al., 2023), different depression instruments weight cognitive and somatic symptoms differently, which can influence scores in chronic pain populations. The BDI‑II has been validated in people with chronic pain and shows good internal consistency in that context, but several authors note potential overlap between somatic depressive items and pain‑related symptoms, which may inflate scores in some patients (Harris & D’Eon, 2008). The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was developed for use in hospital and community settings and minimizes somatic items to reduce confounding with physical illness; nevertheless, its lower internal consistency for depression in some samples suggests caution when comparing HADS‑D results with other instruments. Sensitivity analyses showed that the effect size for both anxiety and depression groups remained consistent between medium values.

In addition, the effect size in the anxiety analysis is relatively small (< 0.2), despite the statistically significant finding. As such, this result may be considered clinically marginal by some, suggesting that the overall findings should be interpreted with caution. However, we believe these results represent an important initial step toward addressing the social stigma that portrays females as more susceptible to mental health issues than males. Therefore, our findings highlight that the psychological aspects of males are equally important and should not be overlooked in the management of chronic pain.

There were several hypotheses that may explain these conflicting results. Although depression and anxiety reportedly occur more than twice as prevalent in women than men, most studies were conducted in a non-chronic pain setting. Furthermore, they mainly studied young/reproductive-age women (approximately ages 14 to 25 years), and the ratio decreases with age (Kalin, 2020). Many factors may influence pain perception in chronic pain settings. Those factors not only include underlying diseases, disease severity, and pain duration, but also other factors such as hormonal factors and social stigma could also have an impact (Perugino et al., 2022). Some sex hormones, such as estrogen, which is greater in females, have been shown to reduce nociception in peripheral and central neuropathic pain due to their neuroprotective properties (Chen et al., 2021). Differences in pain threshold or severity across sexes may have a direct impact on mental health. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported a moderate positive correlation between pain severity and both anxiety and depression (Fonseca-Rodrigues et al., 2021). Social stigma across sexes may affect pain characteristics and mental health outcomes. Men are assumed to be stoic, more tolerant of pain, and less likely to seek medical care, whereas women are portrayed as more vulnerable, sensitive to pain, and more eager to seek medical attention (Perugino et al., 2022). Men may experience greater intensity of pain as a result of delayed therapy. Furthermore, males and their role as financial providers may suffer substantially from mental health problems when their chronic pain is severe enough to cause work impairment (Mckenzie et al., 2022). On the other side, some research found that women were underestimated and undertreated since they complain more than men and are seen as inaccurate reporters of pain (Zhang et al., 2021).

There are several limitations in the present study. First, this study examined scores between sexes that measure the severity of anxiety/depression, regardless of whether they satisfy a clinical threshold for either. Thus, the findings reflect differences in symptom severity between sexes rather than differences in prevalence. Due to inadequate data on chronic pain populations, no analysis of prevalence across sexes was conducted. Second, most of the studies included in the present study did not specify the use of medications in their study population. Only two studies reported the type of medications used in managing chronic pain, and one study reported the frequency of drugs used. None mention the most recent time the medicine was used prior to assessment. This information was crucial as pain directly affected patients’ mental state. Therefore, stratified analysis using naive or treated subjects was not performed due to a lack of the corresponding information. Third, other relevant risk factors, such as previous instances of mental illness or occupation, were not balanced between groups, which may have influenced the current study's results. Fourth, most studies were unicenter and had a modest sample size, with only one major population-based research including over a thousand participants. Lastly, the absence of studies that formally address bidirectionality or reverse causation limits causal interpretation of our pooled findings. Because nearly all included studies were cross‑sectional or treated depression/anxiety as simple outcomes, it remains unclear whether psychological symptoms drive pain, pain precipitates mood disturbance, or both processes operate simultaneously. This uncertainty has practical consequences: interventions that target only one domain may miss upstream drivers or sustaining mechanisms in the other. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs and explicit causal‑inference approaches (for example, cross‑lagged panel models, repeated‑measures cohorts, instrumental‑variable analyses, or Mendelian randomization) to disentangle temporal ordering and mechanisms. In the meantime, clinicians should routinely assess both pain and mental health and consider integrated treatment strategies, since the likely bidirectional relationship implies that addressing one domain may benefit the other.

In this study, females were found to have lower anxiety scores in the chronic pain populations, but there was no difference in depression across sexes. Although women traditionally have more tendency to mental health illnesses, men with chronic pain should also receive equal care. Overall, this study highlights the importance of properly addressing sex disparities when managing mental health issues especially in chronic pain populations.
