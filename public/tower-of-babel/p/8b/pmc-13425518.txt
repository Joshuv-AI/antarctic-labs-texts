Mechanical Stimulation on Cardiac Cells with Feedback Control System
Authors: Kyotaro Kanazashi, Ayu Sasaki, Arisa Mizutani, Ayaka Namiki, Yuuta Moriyama, Toshiyuki Mitsui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mechanical cues encoded in the extracellular microenvironment play essential roles in regulating cardiac rhythm and tissue-level coordination; however, systematic interrogation of when mechanical inputs are most effective remains technically challenging. While substrate stiffness and cyclic stretch are widely used to condition cardiomyocytes in vitro, most platforms lack the temporal precision required to probe phase-dependent mechanosensitivity during spontaneous beating. Here, we report a biomaterials-based mechanical stimulation platform that enables real-time, phase-specific delivery of localized stretch to autonomously beating cardiomyocyte aggregates cultured on soft polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrates. By integrating live imaging-based beat detection with piezo-driven microscale actuation, mechanical stimuli were triggered at defined fractions of the intrinsic interbeat interval (IBI), allowing direct comparison of in-phase and out-of-phase mechanical perturbations. Using one-dimensional cardiomyocyte aggregates as a model system, we show that randomly timed mechanical stimulation rarely altered intrinsic rhythm (∼0.3% of beats). In contrast, phase-locked stimulation applied near the end of the contraction–relaxation cycle (∼90% of the IBI) reproducibly induced transient IBI shortening, reversed contraction-wave propagation, and shifted pacemaker activity toward the site of stimulation. Mechanical stimulation delivered at midcycle (∼50% of the IBI) produced minimal effects. Displacement-resolved analysis revealed that phase-specific stimulation redistributed mechanical output within the aggregate: contraction amplitude was maintained or enhanced near the stimulation site while being attenuated in distal regions. These results demonstrate that developing cardiac tissues exhibit narrow temporal windows of mechanical susceptibility and robust resistance to perturbation outside these phases. This platform provides a versatile biomaterials-enabled framework for dissecting dynamic mechano-electrical feedback, optimizing mechanical conditioning strategies, and engineering cardiac tissues with controllable rhythmic properties.

Introduction

The heart is a mechanically active pump in which contraction–relaxation cycles are synchronized across large populations of cardiomyocytes. − Sustaining this rhythm requires continuous adaptation to load, mediated by mechano-electrical feedback in which mechanical stress and strain are transduced into biochemical signaling and changes in electrophysiological state. Perturbation of this feedbacksuch as during hypertrophy or heart failuredrives myocardial remodeling that includes altered tissue stiffness, extracellular-matrix composition, and excitation–contraction coupling. During embryogenesis, analogous mechanosensitive pathways couple contractile forces to morphogenetic programs, linking tissue-level mechanics to proliferation, differentiation, and maturation of cardiac lineages. , Thus, both functional stability and developmental plasticity depend on the capacity of cardiac cells to interpret and respond to a dynamically changing mechanical environment.

Early primary studies established that mechanical state can feed back onto cardiac excitation. Lab et al. showed that mechanically dependent changes in intact frog ventricle alter action-potential morphology, repolarization, and afterdepolarization formation, and later work in mammalian ventricular muscle linked shortening-dependent action-potential changes to altered myoplasmic Ca2+ handling. , Kohl and colleagues further extended this framework by demonstrating potential electrical interactions between mechanosensitive fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes in the sinoatrial-node region, and by showing that axial stretch increases spontaneous pacemaker activity in isolated sinoatrial-node cells. ,

Because controlled mechanical loading of the intact heart is experimentally constrained, in vitro systems have become essential for isolating specific mechanical variables and quantifying downstream responses at the cellular and tissue scales. In this context, mechanical cues are often separated into static components set by substrate stiffness or topography, and dynamic components that recapitulate cyclic deformation during each beat. At the single-cell level, acute stretch can modulate intracellular Ca2+ dynamics and membrane excitability, consistent with mechanically gated pathways that couple deformation to electrophysiological responses. , These observations provide a mechanistic basis for considering mechanical inputs not only as maturation cues, but also as potential timing-dependent perturbations to rhythm.

Building on these foundations, soft-lithography and PDMS-based microfabrication have enabled controlled application of anisotropic strain with defined magnitude and direction in organized cardiomyocyte preparations. , Such platforms reveal that the impact of stretch is governed not only by amplitude, but also by its spatiotemporal structure, which can influence maturation and the emergence of synchronized activity. Notably, mechanical interactions transmitted through substrate deformation and cell–cell junctional coupling can support intercellular coordination, indicating that rhythmic entrainment can arise through mechanical communication in addition to classical electrophysiological coupling.

Recent reviews have synthesized how progressively refined stretch platforms have advanced cardiac mechanobiology. ,, Engineered-tissue systems that couple culture substrates to programmable actuation have expanded the parameter space of mechanical conditioning, enabling systematic control of strain geometry and timing. ,− In-plane stretch devices applying biaxial or uniaxial deformation to cardiomyocyte monolayers have supported quantitative, subcellular analyses of mechanosignaling and highlighted the importance of cyclic loading for physiological fidelity. More recent approachesincluding soft robotic actuators, phase-tunable stretch systems, and stimuli-responsive scaffolds that dynamically modulate stiffness or loading profiles,further extend control over the temporal structure of mechanical cues. Consistent with these technical advances, experiments in which stretch is applied at defined points in the beat cycle demonstrate phase-dependent responses, including systematic shifts in contraction timing under prolonged cyclic loading. Mechanical stretch is therefore a well-recognized modulator of cardiac function, contributing to mechano-electric and mechano-mechanical feedback across multiple biological scales. ,

In the intact heart, these effects contribute to coordinated regulation of rhythm and contractile output, including the Bainbridge and Frank–Starling responses. , However, in isolated ventricular cardiomyocytes, mechanically induced excitation remains rare, and the determinants of successful triggering are incompletely characterized.

Single-cell studies indicate that acute stretch more commonly produces subthreshold electrical responses or localized Ca2+ signaling than whole-cell depolarization or contraction. , Quantitatively, stretch-induced action potentials have been reported in only ∼1% of trials in isolated cardiomyocytes, and these rare events are strongly phase dependent: identical perturbations can be ineffective across most of the action-potential cycle yet become effective within narrowly defined excitable phases (e.g., late repolarization). Complementary theoretical and computational work supports this interpretation by showing that depolarization threshold and afterdepolarization susceptibility depend critically on stretch timing and rate.

Phase-dependent mechanosensitivity also emerges at the multicellular level. Nitsan et al. demonstrated that localized deformation delivered by a piezo-driven probe can entrain cardiomyocytes and initiate propagating contractions across engineered cardiac sheets, with successful triggering confined to a narrow phase window of the intrinsic beat cycle. These results provide direct evidence that cardiomyocytes can communicate mechanically through substrate deformation and junctional coupling, and that mechanical entrainment obeys stringent phase constraints analogous to electrical pacing.

Together, these studies motivate a central working principle: the physiological effect of a mechanical perturbation depends on its phase relative to the intrinsic contraction cycle. , However, most existing stretch systems do not synchronize perturbations to spontaneous beating with sufficient temporal precision to systematically interrogate phase dependence at the microscale.

To address this limitation, we developed a real-time feedback system that detects spontaneous contractions and triggers localized stretch at predefined fractions of the intrinsic interbeat interval (IBI). The platform integrates live imaging-based beat detection with piezo-driven actuation of a tungsten probe to deliver brief, spatially confined displacements to cardiomyocyte aggregates cultured on soft PDMS substrates, enabling direct comparison of in-phase (late-cycle) and out-of-phase (midcycle) perturbations.

Using one-dimensional cardiomyocyte aggregates with propagating contraction waves, we found that mechanically induced rhythm modulation is strongly timing dependent. Randomly timed stimulation rarely altered intrinsic rhythm, whereas feedback-locked stimulation delivered late in the cycle reliably induced transient shortening of the IBI and frequently reversed propagation direction, consistent with a transient shift of the dominant initiation site toward the stimulated region. In contrast, stimulation delivered near midcycle produced minimal effects.

Materials and Methods

Isolation of Primary Cardiac Cells

Primary cardiac cells, comprising predominantly cardiomyocytes (CMs) and cardiac fibroblasts (CFs), were isolated from ventricles of 5 day-old Japanese Momiji chick embryos (Yamagishi Jikkenchi, Mie, Japan) following established protocols. , Ventricular tissue was excised under sterile conditions and minced into ∼1 mm fragments using fine scissors. The tissue was enzymatically digested in 0.25% trypsin (Gibco) prepared in Ca2+/Mg2+-free PBS at 37 °C with gentle agitation. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F-12 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U mL–1 penicillin, 100 μg mL–1 streptomycin, and 1% l-glutamine. To enrich for CMs, cells were preplated for 150 min at 37 °C, allowing fibroblasts to preferentially adhere. The nonadherent, CM-enriched supernatant was collected and used for patterned seeding.

Fabrication of Collagen-Patterned Polydimethylsiloxane Substrates

Linear patterns for cellular alignment were generated on PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning) using soft lithography. , A 10 μm-thick SU-8 (MicroChem) layer was spin-coated onto a silicon wafer, exposed through transparency photomasks containing 100 μm-wide parallel lines, and developed to form the master mold.

PDMS (10:1 base/curing-agent ratio) was poured onto the mold, degassed, and cured at 70 °C for 2 h. Patterned PDMS sheets (10 × 10 mm, 0.5 mm thick) were peeled off and aligned against a flat PDMS sheet of identical size, forming temporary microchannels whose ends remained open.

Collagen type I–P (10 mg mL–1 in 1 M acetic acid; Nitta Gelatin, Japan) was introduced into the channels and vacuumed for 1 h to ensure complete filling. Upon vacuum release, collagen selectively adhered to the flat PDMS surface beneath the channels. After separation of the patterned PDMS, excess collagen was rinsed away with sterile PBS, leaving well-defined linear collagen tracks.

Formation of 1D Cardiac Cell Aggregates

CM-enriched suspensions were seeded at ∼5 × 104 cells cm–2 onto collagen-patterned PDMS substrates. To enhance capture efficiency by collagen tracks, substrates were gently rocked in the incubator. After 18 h, unattached cells were removed by gentle washing. Linear aggregates (1 mm long, 80–120 μm wide) were consistently formed. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 with routine medium exchange.

All imaging and mechanical stimulation experiments were performed at 37 °C using an incubator-integrated imaging system. Before recording, the samples were transferred to the imaging incubator and allowed to stabilize for at least 2 h. Aggregates exhibited autonomous beating with typical IBIs of 0.5–1.5 s.

Mechanical Probe for Localized Stretching

A custom microscale stretching probe was fabricated from a 0.3 mm tungsten wire. The wire tip was electrochemically etched in 1 M KOH using a DC voltage, following established STM-tip fabrication procedures. The apex was coated with agarose, increasing the radius of curvature to ∼25 μm to prevent membrane damage. A 30 mm wire segment was inserted into a hypodermic needle to form a rigid holder. This assembly was attached to a bimorph piezoceramic actuator (LPD3713X, Nihon Ceratec), with the opposing actuator end mounted on a 3-axis micromanipulator. The wire was bent ∼90° near its apex such that actuator-driven motion produced near-horizontal displacement of the tip. During positioning, the probe was advanced toward the substrate at ∼30° relative to horizontal, enabling visual confirmation of contact without compressing the aggregate (Figure b). For stretching, the tip was aligned parallel to the aggregate’s long axis and perpendicular to the PDMS surface. Actuator drive amplitudes produced 40–50 μm tip displacement at ∼800 μm s–1.

Live Imaging and Mechanical Stimulation Platform

Experiments were conducted using a custom incubator-integrated imaging system. USB microscopes (Dino-Lite Premier AM4313; 30 fps, ×500 magnification, AnMo Electronics Corp.) provided inverted-configuration images of cardiac aggregates. Imaging and actuator control were implemented in LabVIEW (National Instruments), with mechanical stimuli delivered by voltage waveforms generated through an analog output of NI myDAQ ADDA module (National Instruments, model 781325-01). Real-time image acquisition and processing were used to detect beat timing and regulate stimulus delivery, as illustrated in Figure .

Beat Detection and Propagation Analysis

Sequential phase-contrast image frames were acquired as 8-bit grayscale images at 640 × 480 pixels. Beat timing was extracted from a frame-difference motion signal computed from consecutive frames. ΔIn(x,y)=|In(x,y)−In−1(x,y)|

For each region of interest, the motion signal was reduced to a scalar time series by summing ΔI n over a 50 × 50 pixel ROI at each frame. For elongated aggregates, two or more ROIs were positioned along the strand to infer propagation direction from the temporal order of detected contraction onsets. Propagation direction was assigned only when the onset delay between ROIs exceeded one frame interval of 0.033 s.

The ROI motion signal typically exhibited a double-peak waveform corresponding to contraction and relaxation separated by a brief pause. The interbeat interval, IBI, was defined as the time between successive contraction peaks. Peak detection used a dynamic threshold to accommodate amplitude drift, set to 80% of the preceding contraction peak amplitude. To reduce probe-induced motion artifacts in stimulated recordings, ROIs used for beat detection were placed approximately 50 μm away from the probe apex whenever signal amplitude permitted. Slight global background movement was occasionally visible in some recordings. To minimize its influence, beat-detection ROIs were placed on dense contracting cardiomyocyte regions, and PIV analysis was restricted to manually defined myocardial masks, excluding background regions and floating debris.

Phase-Specific Mechanical Stimulation Protocol

Stimuli were timed relative to the intrinsic contraction phase using a three-beat averaging scheme. The IBIs from the three preceding beats were averaged, and the next stimulus was delivered under one of two phase conditions: late-cycle stretch at 90% of the averaged IBI, approximately before the expected next beat, or midcycle stretch at 50% of the averaged IBI. Pulse-like voltage waveforms delivered through the ADDA module triggered the actuator to generate single stretch events. The actuator speed and displacement profile were set to approximate the native contraction motion measured by image processing.

To prevent unstable feedback operation caused by erroneous beat detection or excessive IBI shortening, probe actuation was automatically halted when the detected IBI fell below 0.3 s. This threshold was introduced because, below this interval, the controller could no longer reliably reference the intrinsic beat cycle. Beat-detection ROIs were also positioned away from the probe apex whenever possible to minimize probe-induced motion artifacts.

Propagation direction was monitored across two selected ROIs. Under baseline conditions, one region consistently initiated contraction later than the other. Reversal of this sequence following stimulation indicated pacemaker shifting.

PIV-based displacement analysis for contraction amplitude and phase-resolved comparison

Contraction and relaxation dynamics were quantified from Dino-Lite video recordings (640 × 480 px, 1.25 μm/px) using a block-matching particle-image-velocimetry (PIV) approach based on correlation analysis with subpixel refinement, as originally established in experimental mechanics. A binary mask was manually drawn to isolate the myocardial tissue, and each frame was divided into overlapping square windows (32 × 32 px, 50% overlap). For each window, the displacement between a relaxed reference frame and a target frame was estimated by searching for the best-matching region in the subsequent image using a sum-of-squared-differences criterion. Subpixel refinement was applied to improve the precision of the displacement vectors, and only vectors within the myocardial mask were retained. Displacement magnitudes were converted to micrometers using the pixel calibration, yielding a sparse displacement field that represented the contractile deformation of the tissue.

To analyze mechanical propagation across the full beat cycle, we performed phase-resolved displacement estimation using the same method. For each beat, a relaxed frame immediately prior to contraction onset was chosen as the reference, and displacement fields (Δx) were computed for four characteristic phases: the onset of shortening, peak contraction, the onset of relaxation, and late relaxation. Vector maps were overlaid on the corresponding raw images, with hue encoding displacement angle and arrow length reflecting |Δx|, while |Δx| magnitude maps highlighted spatial variations in mechanical activity. Directional distributions were quantified from the same displacement vectors using normalized polar histograms, allowing phase-dependent changes in dominant orientation to be compared between beats. The maximum displacement amplitude of each beat was defined as the spatial mean of |Δx| at the phase exhibiting the largest magnitude. These measurements were applied to both baseline beats and beats occurring during IBI shortening, defined as beats for which the IBI decreased to ≤70% of the preceding baseline value.

Contraction and relaxation velocities were calculated from PIV-derived displacement fields as the mean frame-to-frame rate of change of the ROI-averaged displacement magnitude, evaluated over the approximately linear portion of the trace (from onset to 80% of peak displacement for contraction, and from 80% back to baseline for relaxation).

Results

We next asked whether shortening depended on the timing of stretch within the intrinsic cycle. Autonomous beating reflects a time-dependent recovery of excitability over IBI. Mechanical perturbations are therefore expected to depend on phase within the cycle. − , In these periodic-stretch recordings, shortening events were infrequent. A total of approximately 32 shortening events were observed out of 6110 stimuli, corresponding to about 0.52% of all stimuli.

Figure f summarizes shortening events as a function of stretch phase within the contraction–relaxation cycle. Data were collected from 5 samples; raw counts and per-sample values are reported in Table S1. The beat cycle was divided into ten equal phase bins of 36° each. Shortening events clustered in the final two bins, indicating that stretches delivered immediately before the next spontaneous beat were most likely to precipitate shortening. In these events, activation initiated near the probe and propagated distally, consistent with stretch biasing the effective initiation site.

Shortening events persisted for several seconds. Across 20 events, event durations ranged from 4 to 10 s. Following the onset of recovery, the IBI generally returned to baseline within approximately 3 s. In three cases, the IBI transiently exceeded baseline before stabilizing. Even with continued periodic stretching, the IBI returned to the prestretch value. Because periodic stimulation continued throughout shortening, the stretch phase relative to the evolving beat cycle drifted during each episode. This drift prevented a phase-resolved analysis of recovery dynamics under continuous stimulation.

In summary, probe-driven periodic stretch can transiently shorten the IBI and reverse propagation direction in one-dimensional cardiomyocyte aggregates. Although infrequent, the effect was strongly phase dependent and occurred preferentially when stretch was delivered late in the beat cycle, immediately preceding the next spontaneous contraction. Resolving how shortening is initiated and how recovery proceeds under continued stretch requires stimulation that is precisely synchronized to the intrinsic beat cycle.

Phase-Specific Stimulation with Real-Time Feedback Control

Periodic stimulation that was not synchronized to the intrinsic cycle produced IBI shortening only rarely, as quantified in Figure . We therefore implemented real-time feedback control to deliver stretch at defined phases of the autonomous contraction cycle. The 90% IBI phase was selected based on physiological reasoning and prior evidence for phase-dependent mechano-electrical coupling. This timing corresponds to the late beat-cycle interval, immediately before the expected next spontaneous beat, when the tissue is likely approaching the threshold for the next excitation and may therefore be more susceptible to mechanical perturbation. The 50% IBI phase was selected as a contrasting midcycle condition, when the tissue is expected to be relatively less susceptible to mechanically induced triggering. This choice was also supported by the periodic-stimulation experiments, in which IBI-shortening events clustered in the final two-phase bins of the beat cycle.

The system estimated the IBI from three consecutive contractions detected by motion analysis in a dense cardiomyocyte cluster selected for stimulation along a one-dimensional aggregate. Stretch was triggered either at 90% of the averaged IBI, corresponding to approximately 0.1 s before the next expected beat, or at 50% of the cycle, corresponding to the midpoint between beats. The precision of phase targeting is quantified in Supporting Information Figure S1.

Phase-locked stimulation induced rapid IBI shortening, reaching up to ∼30% in this example, and the shortened rhythm persisted for more than 10 s before returning toward baseline.

Despite differences in the order of acquisition, 90% phase stimulation consistently produced IBI shortening, visible as denser rasters, and frequently reversed activation order, indicated by magenta overlays. By contrast, 50% phase stimulation produced minimal changes in IBI and propagation in most samples. Across the intrinsic IBI range of 0.6–1.8 s, shorter baseline IBIs exhibited smaller absolute shortening, whereas longer baseline IBIs showed more pronounced shortening. In the sample shown in Figure j, the IBI transiently decreased below 0.3 s, at which point probe actuation was automatically halted to avoid an unstable feedback regime in which the controller no longer referenced the true IBI. During recovery from shortening, samples with baseline IBIs below ∼1 s often returned to their prestimulus period more abruptly, whereas samples with baseline IBIs above ∼1 s tended to recover more gradually. In some episodes, including those in Figure k,l, the IBI transiently exceeded its steady-state value before returning to baseline, and the IBI became temporarily more variable during this overshoot. In addition, Figure l,m show occasional reversals of propagation direction that occurred outside the immediate stimulus-locked timing, indicating that once induced, altered activation order can persist beyond the stimulus phase that initiated it.

Under 50% phase stimulation, shortening events were less frequent and were not prominent in most samples. Instead, several recordings showed intermittent lengthening of the IBI, and in some cases, 50% stimulation was followed by episodes of markedly prolonged intervals, as seen in Figure n,o.

To quantify timing dependence, we computed the probability of an IBI-shortening event per applied stretch stimulus (Figure s). The pooled event probability was highest under 90% phase stimulation and lower under either 50% phase or periodic stimulation; raw counts and per-sample values are reported in Table S1.

A notable exception was observed in the sample corresponding to Figure g,l and q. In this case, the 50% phase recording in Figure q shows propagation from the magenta ROI toward the green ROI even though stimulation was delivered at midcycle. This pattern was not initi– ated by the 50% phase stimulus; instead, it reflects a persistent change in activation sequence induced earlier during 90% phase stimulation. Once established, the beat order did not return to the original green-to-magenta sequence after the stimulation phase was changed. Together, these results indicate that real-time phase feedback converts an infrequent mechanically induced response into a reproducible, phase-dependent modulation of beat timing and propagation.

Contraction speeds were ∼10–12 μm s–1, and relaxation speeds were ∼9–10 μm s–1.

To test whether these ROI-specific changes were consistent across preparations, we quantified peak displacement amplitudes for beats immediately before and after stimulation that induced IBI shortening in six individuals. Peak displacement at ROI1 was maintained or slightly increased during shortening, whereas peak displacement at ROI2 decreased in all samples shown in Figure f,g. Normalizing each IBI-shortened beat by its corresponding regular beat emphasized this contrast, with ratios above unity at ROI1 and predominantly below unity at ROI2 as shown in Figure h. Together, these results show that IBI shortening is accompanied by a reversal in mechanical activation sequence and a redistribution of displacement amplitude, with maintained motion near the stimulation site and attenuated motion at a distant cluster.

Discussion

Mechanical stimuli interact with the cardiac rhythm through mechano-electric feedback pathways that couple deformation to excitability and contraction. In one-dimensional cardiomyocyte aggregates, spontaneous activity propagated with a stable activation order, consistent with dominance of an intrinsic initiation site. Periodic probe stretch applied without beat-to-beat phase targeting perturbed this rhythm only intermittently, yielding rare and short-lived IBI shortening. This low inducibility indicates that most of the intrinsic cycle is mechanically resistant, and that effective triggering requires delivery within a restricted temporal window.

Phase-locked stimulation exposed a marked asymmetry in mechanical susceptibility. Stretch delivered late in the cycle, near the next expected beat, reproducibly increased the probability of IBI shortening and frequently reversed propagation direction, whereas midcycle stimulation produced little change. This dependence on stimulus timing is consistent with established work showing that mechanically evoked excitation and ectopy depend on the interaction between mechanical input and the evolving state of repolarization and refractoriness. ,− ,, In intact tissue, the same timing constraint underlies a narrow vulnerable window for mechanically triggered premature excitation. , The clustering of shortening events near the end of the cycle supports the view that late-cycle stretch accesses a high-gain regime of mechano-electric coupling, while earlier phases remain comparatively insensitive. Although the present study focused on two representative phases, 50% and 90% of the IBI, a fully resolved phase–response curve would require systematic stimulation across additional phase conditions and a larger data set. Future studies using higher frame-rate imaging and phase sweeping across the full beat cycle will be needed to define the precise width and shape of the mechanically sensitive temporal window.

Propagation reversal emerged as a defining signature of mechanically induced shortening. Once shortening occurred, the stimulated region advanced in the activation sequence and transiently dominated initiation, consistent with the ability of localized deformation to bias excitation and entrain multicellular preparations. ,, This behavior parallels oscillator-based descriptions of phase resetting and transient entrainment in coupled excitable systems. , Notably, several preparations retained the reversed activation order beyond the phase that initially induced it, indicating hysteresis-like dynamics in which competing initiation sites can persist until the native hierarchy reasserts control.

Several mechanisms may contribute to this phase-specific propagation reversal. One possibility is local conduction heterogeneity within the one-dimensional aggregate. Because the preparations beat spontaneously, activation likely reflects competition among regions with different intrinsic automaticity, excitability, and coupling to neighboring cells. Late-cycle stretch may transiently increase excitability near the stimulation site or lower the effective activation threshold, allowing this region to initiate activation before the original dominant site. ,− Whether such mechanically biased activation dominates would depend on local conduction velocity, the distance between the stimulation site and the intrinsic initiation site, intercellular coupling, and the phase of stimulation. This interpretation is consistent with the observation that IBI shortening and propagation reversal were most common when stretch was delivered late in the cycle, when the timing margin between mechanically advanced activation and the next intrinsic beat is narrow. The persistence of reversed activation order in some preparations further suggests mechanically induced pacemaker competition, in which the stimulated region transiently overrides the original dominant initiation site. Direct identification of the responsible mechanisms, including stretch-activated membrane currents, calcium-mediated depolarization, local conduction changes, and altered cell–cell coupling, will require future experiments combining phase-locked mechanical stimulation with voltage imaging, calcium imaging, or intracellular electrophysiology.

We note that the two-ROI, 30 fps imaging approach used here limits temporal resolution to approximately 33 ms per frame, which precludes construction of spatially continuous isochronal activation maps of the contraction rising phase as typically obtained in high-speed voltage or calcium imaging. At this frame rate, such maps would yield only coarse, discretized timing differences rather than the smoothly varying activation fronts that make isochronal analysis most informative. Future studies employing higher frame-rate imaging and multipoint spatial sampling along the aggregate will enable fully resolved isochronal maps, analogous to standard conduction velocity analyses, and provide a more direct visual representation of propagation reversal.

PIV-based displacement analysis revealed that shortening altered not only timing but also the spatial distribution of mechanical output. During shortening, activation shifted earlier near the probe and later at a distant cluster, while peak displacement was maintained or increased at the stimulation site and reduced distally. This spatial asymmetry suggests that premature activation redistributes deformation rather than scaling contraction uniformly. A reduction in distal displacement is consistent with incomplete mechanical recovery during accelerated cycles, which can limit tension development away from the mechanically biased region. These observations highlight that phase-specific perturbations can reweight where mechanical work is expressed within a connected preparation, even when the overall displacement-field orientation is preserved.

Stretch delivered late in the beat cycle may also be interpreted as a localized preload-like perturbation during the diastolic interval. In the Frank–Starling framework, increased diastolic stretch can enhance subsequent contraction through length-dependent activation of cardiac sarcomeres. The maintained or slightly increased displacement amplitude near the stimulation site during IBI shortening is consistent with this interpretation. However, the present perturbation was spatially localized and transient, rather than a uniform preload applied to the entire aggregate. This distinction may explain why displacement amplitude was maintained or enhanced near the probe while being reduced at a distant region. In the present study, actuator drive amplitude was held constant, producing approximately 40–50 μm probe-tip displacement, but stretch magnitude was not systematically varied and local tissue strain or force was not directly quantified. Future work using calibrated strain or force measurements, including bead tracking, substrate deformation analysis, or traction force microscopy, will be required to determine how applied stretch magnitude quantitatively relates to contractile output.

A limitation of the present study is that experiments were performed with spontaneously beating aggregates rather than electrically paced preparations; future integration of pacing with phase-locked stretch will provide a more controlled framework for assessing dynamic mechano-electric effects. In addition, embedding fluorescent fiducial markers, such as microbeads, within the PDMS substrate would enable direct traction force microscopy, allowing quantitative analysis of local strain and the relationship between applied stretch magnitude and contractile force output.

Conclusions

Together, the data define a beat-resolved mechanical “gain function” in developing cardiac tissue: localized deformation delivered late in the intrinsic cycle is most effective at resetting timing and reassigning the initiation sequence, whereas stimulation applied earlier is largely filtered out. Most stretch-conditioning platforms impose preprogrammed waveforms without synchronizing to spontaneous beating. ,− By combining live beat detection with phase-locked microscale actuation, the present approach enables systematic mapping of phase dependence in spontaneously beating aggregates and provides a route to compare mechanistic models of mechano-electrical coupling against cycle-resolved experimental perturbations. ,,
