Use and reporting of intervention mapping for adaptation: a systematic review protocol
Authors: Eunyoung Kang, Hugo R Sánchez-Blas, Elizabeth O Obekpa, Maria E Fernandez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntervention adaptation is likely to occur to some extent when implementing interventions in new implementation contexts. Using systematic frameworks can guide intentional and effective adaptation processes. Intervention Mapping for Adaptation (IM-ADAPT) is a framework that offers step-by-step guidance for systematic, theory-based intervention adaptation. Despite the increasing use of IM-ADAPT, there is limited understanding of the contexts in which it has been applied and how effectively it is used and reported. Addressing this knowledge gap can improve current adaptation practices and inform future enhancements of the IM-ADAPT framework and the broader science of intervention adaptation. This review aims to (1) determine the context in which IM-ADAPT is used, (2) assess how studies apply IM-ADAPT tasks and (3) evaluate how these studies report their IM-ADAPT findings.Methods and analysisThis protocol followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines. The eligibility criteria include original peer-reviewed English articles that used Intervention Mapping or IM-ADAPT to adapt interventions. We searched PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, Embase and the Intervention Mapping literature library, and conducted forward citation searches using two landmark articles from inception to 19 July 2024. At least two independent coders will screen the records to determine eligibility and extract data from the included articles. Any discrepancies will be resolved through regular co-author meetings or in consultation with a senior author. The study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/D5TCP).Ethics and disseminationAs this systematic review only used published data, no ethics approval was required. We will disseminate the findings of this review through open-access channels and journals.

Introduction

The adaptation of evidence-based programmes (EBPs) is crucial for both their initial implementation and sustainment in new contexts.13 Adaptation is defined as the modification of EBPs to meet the preferences and needs of target populations and contexts.4 During the early adoption or implementation phase, efforts typically begin by investigating how the EBPs fit into a new priority population and implementation context.1 5 If the EBPs are deemed a good fit for the new population and context, implementation planners may determine that it is more beneficial to adapt the existing programme rather than develop a brand-new one, which often requires significant time and resources. Adapting EBPs in such cases can improve their fit with the priority population and context, ultimately enhancing their adoption and implementation.1 5 Even after EBPs are successfully adopted and implemented, adaptations may still occur or become necessary during the sustainment phase.1 2 The sustainment of EBPs is influenced by the interplay among dynamic outer and inner context structures, processes and supports, all of which can continue to evolve and change.1 2 As a result, to be tailored to this dynamic sustainment context, EBPs may inevitably require or benefit from adaptation to be effectively sustained in dynamic implementation contexts.1 2

Adaptation can occur in various aspects of EBPs, including service settings (eg, who delivers the intervention, financing sources), priority populations (eg, health literacy, age-appropriateness, response to the needs of new populations), mode of delivery (eg, number of intervention sessions, session length, intervention dose), cultural adaptations (eg, cultural sensitivity, adapted imagery tailored to cultural norms) and core intervention components (eg, mechanisms of action).6 For instance, cultural adaptations may involve incorporating culturally relevant examples and language to ensure that the intervention resonates with the new priority population, which has been shown to improve engagement and outcomes. Adaptations may also include adjustments in the mode of delivery, such as using mobile platforms for interventions originally designed for in-person delivery, which can broaden access and improve scalability and health equity, especially in resource-limited settings. Given the breadth and complexity involved, navigating these adaptations presents several challenges.

One of the most salient challenges is to systematically identify the core elements (core functions or mechanisms of action) of EBPs that are essential to achieve the desired outcomes and maintain them through systematic adaptation.79 Often, the core elements of the EBPs may be preserved, while peripheral components such as delivery modes, intervention materials and languages may be adapted.10 For example, group education may be adapted and delivered in an individual format to better align with cultural norms in a new context, thereby enhancing its fit. Sometimes, new core elements may be added during adaptation, especially when the original EBPs did not address a crucial determinant in achieving the intended outcomes. For instance, in healthy eating interventions, knowledge about healthy alternative recipes might not have been part of the original intervention but could be included if the new population is less familiar with such diets, and this lack of knowledge poses a significant barrier to healthy eating.

Rigorous adaptation of EBPs needs to consider complex factors ranging from the fidelity of the intervention, core functions (mechanisms of action) and forms, its delivery mode and sociocultural contexts. To navigate this complex process, implementation science researchers and practitioners emphasise the importance of using systematic frameworks and approaches for rigorous adaptation of EBPs.11 While several frameworks exist, they generally serve two main purposes: (1) providing conceptual guidance and (2) offering step-by-step instructions for completing the adaptation.4 11

Intervention Mapping for Adaptation (IM-ADAPT) is an innovative and practical framework that serves both purposes: conceptual and practical approaches.12 13 Using IM-ADAPT provides both a conceptual framework for guiding the process as well as concrete step-by-step tasks for evidence-based, theory-informed adaptation, ensuring rigorous consideration of the functions and forms of EBPs.12 13 IM-ADAPT was developed based on Intervention Mapping (IM), a systematic and iterative approach that guides the development of complex interventions using theories, models or frameworks.12 13 IM-ADAPT includes the following six adaptation tasks. First, it involves assessing the needs and assets of the new population and/or setting and developing a logic model of change. Second, it requires discovering evidence-based interventions (EBIs) that potentially match the targets for change and assessing their level of evidence and fit. If an EBI has already been selected, this step involves determining the level of evidence and fit for the selected EBI. The third task is to assess the fit of the selected EBI and plan adaptations. The fourth task is to adapt intervention components. The fifth task involves planning for the implementation of the adapted EBI. Finally, the sixth task is to test the adapted intervention. We have provided an in-depth description of IM-ADAPT in the Methods and analysis section.

A participatory process is another key aspect of IM-ADAPT to promote effective, dynamic and equitable adaptation.12 13 The adaptation process involves assessing how the EBP aligns with the target population and context. Active participation of community members who will use or deliver the EBP in these assessments and activities can ensure ecologically valid assessment and adaptation.12 In turn, adapters can better identify ecologically valid EBPs, adapt EBPs tailored to the needs of the target context and population, and evaluate the adapted EBP.12

Recommendations by experts to use systematic frameworks for rigorous adaptation have led to a growing number of adaptation studies using IM-ADAPT in recent years. However, there is a considerable knowledge gap in how commonly and rigorously current adaptation studies use IM-ADAPT and report their findings. Poor application of IM-ADAPT can compromise its potential benefits and hinder the effective adaptation of EBPs. A recent survey study on the use of IM-ADAPT for adaptation highlighted that adapters reported low self-efficacy in completing IM-ADAPT tasks.14 While it may not be feasible or necessary for all studies to adhere to every IM-ADAPT task due to various reasons such as logistical limitations (eg, limited funding or research time) or others, the rigorous use of IM-ADAPT tasks can maximise the adaptation process’s rigour. To improve adaptation practices, it is necessary to advance IM-ADAPT guides and education. The initial step in this advancement is the use of IM-ADAPT to synthesise the current empirical evidence regarding the use and reporting of IM-ADAPT.

The purpose of this systematic review is to synthesise existing evidence on the use and reporting of IM-ADAPT. The review has three aims: (1) to determine the context in which IM-ADAPT is applied, (2) to assess how adaptation studies use IM-ADAPT tasks and (3) to evaluate how these studies report their IM-ADAPT findings. The insights gained from this review will enhance our understanding of current adaptation practices using IM-ADAPT, inform rigorous EBP adaptation and suggest future directions for improving the IM-ADAPT framework and advancing the science of EBP adaptation.

Methods and analysis

This systematic review protocol was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines (online supplemental material 1).15 The study protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/D5TCP). The planned start date for this systematic review was March 2022, and the anticipated end date is October 2025.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination of this study.

Eligibility criteria

We included articles that: (1) explicitly used IM-ADAPT or IM to guide intervention adaptation, (2) employed any study design, including protocols and (3) were peer-reviewed English publications. We excluded review or meta-analysis papers, conference papers, dissertations, guidelines, commentaries, letters, editorials, proceedings/abstracts/posters/papers or book chapters.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed/Medline, Web of Science and Embase using a search strategy developed with the terms “Intervention Mapping” and “Adapt*”. Table 2 includes the search strategies for each database and other search methods described below. We also searched the Intervention Mapping database library (https://www.interventionmapping.com/). In the Intervention Mapping library, we reviewed titles, programme names and related paper names to identify records for full-text review. Lastly, we conducted a citation search using two initial articles16 17 on IM-ADAPT in Web of Science, searching relevant records with the terms “intervention mapping” (All Fields) OR “Adapt*” (All Fields), and extracted all records for full-text review. The search strategy for all sources was conducted from inception to April 2023 and updated on 19 July 2024.

Data management and selection process

Two co-authors (EK and HRS-B) extracted search records from the databases and uploaded them to the Covidence online review screening platform. Duplicates were removed using the automatic process built into Covidence. Three independent screeners (EK, EOO, HRS-B) will independently screen titles/abstracts and full-text records using the eligibility criteria described above. At least two screeners will complete the screening, with records randomly assigned. Any discrepancies will be resolved through regular weekly co-author meetings or by consulting a senior author (MEF).

Data collection processes, data items, prioritisation and data synthesis

Three authors (EK, HRS-B and EOO) will independently extract data using Microsoft Excel. At least two authors will extract data from each article, ensuring that all records are double-coded. The same process described above will be used to resolve any discrepancies.

Table 3 describes the data items and their prioritisation for each aim. For data extraction related to the second and third aims, we will employ deductive qualitative content analysis.18 This analysis includes preparation, organising and resulting phases. In the preparation phase, we will select portions of sentences as the unit of analysis and familiarise ourselves with the data in the included studies. In the organising phase, we will develop structured analysis matrices based on IM-ADAPT subtasks, code the data according to the codebook and synthesise them (online supplemental material 2). The extracted data were synthesised using a narrative approach. In the final reporting phase, we will present the analysis findings. Discrepancies will be resolved during regular co-author meetings or through consultation with a senior author (MEF). As one of the co-authors (MEF) of this review is also a co-developer of the IM-ADAPT framework, we implemented a multicoder approach to minimise potential bias. Independent coding was conducted by primary coders who were not involved in the development of IM-ADAPT. While these steps helped reduce bias, we also highlight the benefit of having an expert in the IM-ADAPT framework involved in the review process to enhance the rigour of the work. MEF was not engaged in primary coding.

Risk of bias in individual studies

We will assess the risk of bias in individual studies using a tool developed by Hawker et al.19 This measure includes nine question items that examine various aspects of the manuscript, ranging from the abstract and title, introduction and aims, method and data, sampling, data analysis, ethics and bias, results, transferability or generalisability, and implications and usefulness. Each item is rated on a 4-point scale: good, fair, poor and very poor. Unlike other tools that focus on specific sections, such as the rigour of outcome evaluation designs or reporting, this measure encompasses broader aspects of the manuscript. We believe it is particularly well-suited for the types of methods-focused articles likely to be included in this review.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this systematic review synthesises data from previously published studies, it does not require ethics approval. The review will adhere to ethical guidelines by using only publicly available data, with no direct involvement of human subjects or collection of new primary data. We will disseminate the findings widely through open-access peer-reviewed journals and presentations at academic conferences. To promote transparency and facilitate further research, the dataset generated from this review will be made openly accessible via the Open Science Framework. A digital object identifier (DOI) will be assigned to the dataset for easy citation and retrieval, enabling further exploration and validation by relevant individuals.
