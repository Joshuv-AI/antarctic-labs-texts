Identification of clinically meaningful, overlapping obstructive respiratory disease subtypes via data-driven approaches in a primary care population
Authors: Maria Pikoula, Jennifer K. Quint, Constantinos Kallis, Albert Henry, Spiros Denaxas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive respiratory conditions, including asthma, bronchiectasis, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), are increasingly recognised as heterogeneous syndromes with significant overlap. Multiple disease pathways contribute to phenotypes that do not always align with textbook definitions, limiting the effectiveness of a one-size-fits-all approach. This study aims to identify, validate, and characterise clinically meaningful airway disease subtypes using electronic healthcare records (EHR) and unsupervised machine learning clustering techniques.MethodsWe applied k-means clustering to 626,651 patients with a diagnosis of asthma, bronchiectasis, or COPD, using linked national structured EHRs in England. Twenty-one clinical features, including risk factors and comorbidities, were analysed, with dimensionality reduction via principal component and multiple correspondence analyses. Associations between cluster membership and exacerbations, as well as respiratory and cardiovascular mortality, were assessed. Over 3,696,962 person-years of follow-up, 102,522 deaths were recorded. Cluster stability was evaluated after five years, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) were conducted to explore genetic associations with cluster membership.ResultsSeven clusters were identified, each encompassing patients across traditional diagnostic labels. Distinct clinical patterns emerged as follows: (1) High BMI female predominant, (2) Older male-predominant with diabetes and cardiovascular disease, (3) Eosinophilic atopic, (4) Older non-comorbid, (5) Non-comorbid low BMI, (6) Neutrophilic smoker, (7) Anxious/depressed female-predominant.The cluster with cardiovascular comorbidities showed the highest rates of hospital admissions for exacerbations. Neutrophilic cluster 6 is a potential novel subtype marked by persistent neutrophilia and poor outcomes. Cluster stability over five years ranged from 38% to 78%. GWAS revealed significant genetic loci in a cluster enriched for allergic disease and eosinophilia, suggesting shared genetic mechanisms.ConclusionsThis study provides a data-driven dissection of the heterogeneity underlying obstructive airway diseases in a large, real-world population. Unsupervised machine learning applied to national-scale EHR data revealed distinct and partially stable subtypes that transcend conventional diagnostic boundaries. These findings highlight the complexity and overlap of airway disease phenotypes and demonstrate the value of clustering approaches for uncovering clinically and biologically meaningful subgroups. This work lays the foundation for further exploration into mechanisms and prognosis within and across airway disease phenotypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03953-x.

Introduction

A growing body of research indicates that obstructive respiratory conditions such as asthma [1], bronchiectasis, and COPD [2], are highly heterogeneous syndromes. These conditions consist of distinct underlying subtypes with markedly different clinical manifestations, pathophysiological mechanisms and risk profiles. Furthermore, more than one condition can coexist leading to an increase in clinical severity and mortality [3].

Asthma, bronchiectasis, and COPD patients are at risk of acute exacerbations. This sudden worsening in symptoms includes breathlessness, increased sputum volume, sputum purulence, cough, wheeze, and coryzal symptoms. Exacerbations usually last several days and are triggered by infection (bacteria or viruses) or by environmental triggers. Exacerbations are an important feature of all three diseases and contribute to disease progression and mortality. They have important consequences for patients and health care providers including a negative impact on health-related quality of life, decline in pulmonary function and increased utilisation of health care resources.

Inevitably, owing to the heterogeneous nature of these conditions, not all patients have similar exacerbation rates or response-to-treatment profiles. Patients should thus be managed individually (according to their patient profile) and not in a one-size-fits-all manner [4, 5]. Currently, little is known about the relationships among pathological features, clinical patterns of disease and response to treatment regimens in order to enable precision medicine approaches for diagnosing, managing and treating these conditions [6]. The heterogeneity of COPD for example has been widely recognised and several studies have sought to identify the underlying mechanistic differences between patients via unsupervised machine learning (ML) methods such as clustering [7–9]. Previous studies, however, have several limitations: (a) source data are based on study designs such as clinical trials or investigator-led cohort studies and are thus limited in sample size, (b) cross-sectional data that include risk factors, biomarkers and related comorbidities thus limiting the ability to examine longitudinal subtypes associated with disease progression and capture the dynamic nature of respiratory diseases, and (c) tend to be underrepresented e.g. mild and moderate disease or women.

To address these limitations, we leveraged longitudinal, linked EHRs from a large, nationally representative population in England. This real-world dataset enabled the capture of disease trajectories, comorbidities, and outcomes over time, providing a unique opportunity to explore the dynamic and overlapping nature of airway diseases at scale. Using unsupervised machine learning techniques, we aimed to discover, validate, and characterise clinically meaningful subtypes across asthma, bronchiectasis, COPD, and their overlap, offering new insights into the heterogeneity of these conditions beyond traditional diagnostic boundaries.

Methods

Data sources

We used two EHR data resources. The cohort derived from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) [10] was used to define clusters whereas the cohort derived from the UKBiobank [11] was used to externally validate clusters and to perform genome-wide association studies (GWAS).

CPRD

CPRD is a real-world research service supporting retrospective and prospective public health and clinical studies [10]. We obtained anonymised, EHR from CPRD Aurum [12], a primary care database broadly representative by age, sex, deprivation, and regional distribution, and covering approximately 20% of GP-registered patients in England. CPRD Aurum provides anthropometric measurements, laboratory tests, clinical diagnoses, symptoms, prescriptions, and medical procedures, coded with the SNOMED CT medical ontology.

Primary care data from the February 2022 build [12] were linked to the Office for National Statistics (ONS) for mortality, and hospitalisation data from NHS England’s Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) Admitted Patient Care (APC) database. Data is linked by NHS Digital, the statutory trusted third party for linking data, using identifiable data held only by NHS Digital. Selected general practices consent to this process at a practice level, with individual patients having the right to opt-out.

Codelists for primary care factors were generated using SNOMED-CT and British National Formulary ontologies; we used our standardisable, reproducible methodology, available on GitHub: for drug [13] and medical/phenotype codelists [14], respectively. Codelists for hospitalisations used ICD-10 codes.

UK biobank

The UKBiobank recruited 502,641 men and women aged 40 and 69 years between 2006 and 2010 from primary care practices, spanning England, Scotland, and Wales [11]. Linked data from primary care practices are available for roughly 45% of the UKBiobank cohort (228,909 participants).

We derived a validation cohort from the participants for whom linkage was available with primary care data, and defined all variables in an identical fashion to the CPRD cohort. Longitudinal follow-up for health‐related outcomes and mortality was available for all participants via linked secondary care records and death registrations via the office for national statistics. Validated genotyping data were available for the majority of participants.

We used an open cohort design, so patients entered the study when they met the inclusion criteria. We did not impose an upper age limit and we allowed for diagnoses of more than one respiratory disease. We set the index date for each participant to the date of the first asthma/bronchiectasis/COPD diagnosis recorded in primary care while the participant was eligible. Patients were censored on the earliest date among the following: (a) death from any cause (as defined in ONS or CPRD using the deathdate variable), (b) transferring out of the primary care practice, (c) the last practice data collection, (d) the end of the study period.

Covariates

The following baseline covariates used in generating the clusters were selected by a Respiratory Consultant and derived from primary care consultation recordings: Age at index, Body mass index (BMI), smoking status (never-smoker, current or ex), atopy (any of hay fever/allergic rhinitis, eczema, allergic reactions), median serum eosinophil and neutrophil counts, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), diabetes, anxiety, depression, ischemic heart disease (IHD), hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and heart failure. For the sex variable, the value recorded in the patient demographic table by the general practitioner (GP) was used. Covariate and comorbidity phenotypes have been defined using previously published phenotyping algorithms from the CALIBER resource which have been used in over 100 publications using robust methodologies [18–22]. In order to explore subtypes that span across diagnostic labels, we purposefully did not include the specific airway disease(s) patients were diagnosed with as cluster-defining features. Spirometry measurements were not included for two reasons: (1) missing from more than 50% of the cohort and (2) not missing at random, e.g. in patients with bronchiectasis, spirometry values were missing for over 75% of patients. Such high missingness as well as the pattern of it was not amenable to multiple imputation techniques and it would lead to exclusion of high numbers of bronchiectasis patients. We did not use medications (such as inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators) as they can be often linked to the disease the patients have been diagnosed with and it would add a bias in the cluster analysis. The codelists used for the definition of variables in both the CPRD and UKBiobank datasets have been provided in the Supplementary Material (Tables S1 and S2) and associated files.

Outcomes

We used an a priori-specified set of clinically relevant features to evaluate and interpret (but not to generate) clusters: (a) rate (events over length of time in the study) of severe (resulting in a hospital admission) or moderate (resulting in a primary care consultation) acute respiratory exacerbation, (b) respiratory and cardiovascular-related mortality and underlying cause of death and (c) Emergency Department attendance. Acute respiratory exacerbations recorded in primary care and in hospital were identified using previously validated phenotyping algorithms [23, 24].

Statistical methods

Data preprocessing

The data preprocessing pipeline, as well as the rationale for selecting it, has been previously described and evaluated [25]. We randomly split the data into a training set (80%) and test set (20%). Statistical analyses were performed on the training data only unless otherwise specified. We performed multiple correspondence analysis (MCA [26]) on categorical covariates and PCA [27] on all numerical covariates (age, BMI, neutrophil count, and eosinophil count). The resulting components of each method were combined and a second round of PCA was performed, resulting in 6 final components.

Cluster analysis

Clustering methods are computational techniques that identify subsets in high-dimensional variable spaces by grouping them according to their similarity. These methods have been previously successfully applied to subtyping studies in airway disease [9, 28, 29]. We applied k-means and hierarchical clustering (HC) algorithms [30] using the distance between points in Euclidean space as the distance metric for both methods. We iteratively applied k-means and hierarchical clustering to examine different values of k between two and eight and based the final choice of clusters on the average best silhouette coefficient [31], and supplemented this with additional calculations of mean within-cluster distances and their standard deviations, as well as metrics such as the Calinsky-Harabasz [32] and Davies-Bouldin [33] scores. To test the stability of the clustering solutions, we performed bootstrap analyses by re-sampling the training dataset with replacement and performing k-means clustering, comparing the original solution with each bootstrapped cluster assignment using the adjusted rand index (ARI) [34]. The ARI was also used to compare clustering solutions with k values adjacent to the one selected. We used descriptive statistics to summarise and compare demographic characteristics, risk factors and clinical covariates within and between clusters and assigned cluster labels manually based on clinical input. We used the t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) [35] high-dimensional data visualisation method to project the dimensionally reduced components used for cluster formation onto a 2-dimensional space.

Clusters were evaluated on the basis of results obtained from the training dataset. We validated the results in unseen data: a test set derived from the original CPRD cohort and kept separate from the training set, and an external validation set from the UK Biobank. Both the test and validation data underwent identical preprocessing steps with parameters derived from the training data. The allocation of clusters on the unseen data was based on the clustering algorithm trained on the training dataset.

Cluster associations with mortality and exacerbation events

Hazard ratios were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models with time to event as the timescale. For each patient, follow-up began at the date of their earliest recorded diagnosis of asthma, bronchiectasis, or COPD within the study period. Patients were followed until the first occurrence of the outcome of interest, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period. Models were adjusted for baseline age and cluster label. Mortality outcomes were limited to deaths with cardiovascular or respiratory causes.

We compared cumulative acute exacerbations and ED attendance between clusters over five years. Cluster stability over time was assessed on patients remaining in the cohort at five years post-index date.

Genome-wide association studies

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) aim to identify associations between genetic variants (genotypes) and observable traits (phenotypes) by comparing the distribution of genetic variants across different phenotypic groups. In this study, we conducted a case-control GWAS using data from the UK Biobank, testing the additive effect of allelic dosage on each identified phenotype cluster using logistic regression models adjusted for sex. For each phenotype cluster, individuals assigned to other clusters were excluded from the control group to maintain cluster specificity.

We included imputed single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) that met the following quality criteria: minor allele frequency (MAF) > 1%, imputation INFO score > 0.3, and missing call rate < 5%. A total of 170,936 unrelated individuals (unrelated up to the third degree) of European ancestry, with both genotyping data and phenotypic data (including Hospital Episode Statistics [HES] and primary care linkage), who passed quality control and consented to UK Biobank research use, were eligible for analysis. Details on genotyping and imputation procedures can be found in the UK Biobank documentation (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/scientists-3/genetic-data/).

Genome-wide significance was defined using a threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁸. To identify independent genetic signals and define genomic risk loci, we performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping. This step involves calculating LD structure to group correlated SNPs. We used PLINK 2.0 [36], a widely adopted software tool for GWAS analysis, to perform the initial association testing and LD calculations. Clumping was based on summary statistics and a reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3.

Further annotation and identification of independent significant SNPs, lead SNPs, and genomic risk loci were carried out using the FUMA (Functional Mapping and Annotation of GWAS) software [37] FUMA automates the clumping process using pre-computed LD information and facilitates biological interpretation of GWAS results.

For gene-level analyses, we used MAGMA (Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation) [37] which performs gene-based and gene-set analyses via multiple linear regression. SNPs were mapped to 18,926 protein-coding genes, with genome-wide significance defined using a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of P < 2.642 × 10⁻⁶.

All analyses were conducted using Python 3.7 and associated open-source scientific libraries, including scikit-learn, scipy, pandas, and numpy. All code used to generate the analysis has been uploaded onto the following online resource: https://github.com/spiros/bmc-pulm-med-data-driven-respiratory. Summary statistics from our GWAS were cross-referenced with the GWAS Catalog [38] to identify previously reported associations.

Cluster analysis

MCA resulted in three numerical factors, explaining 99% of the variance. The PCA resulted in four factors. We performed PCA on the combination of these 7 factors and used the resulting vectors as input to the clustering algorithms. K-means identified 7 clusters as the optimal number based on the silhouette criterion (Supplementary Material Figure S1a), whereas Hierarchical Clustering (agglomerative) identified two (Figure S1b). The cluster solution obtained using the k-means algorithm had a consistently higher silhouette coefficient, therefore k-means with 7 clusters was chosen as the optimal solution. The rationale for choosing k = 7 is detailed in full in the Supplementary Material document where additional metrics are also presented (Tables S3, S4).

To provide an alternative quantification metric of cluster overlap and help the reader gain insight into how each cluster relates to one another we created a plot of t-SNE embeddings (Figure S2, Supplementary Material).

Cluster 1: high BMI female-predominant

The mean age was 49.3 years, which was younger than the cohort average. The mean BMI of 40.1, and many of the patients were morbidly obese. Compared with the 60% cohort average, 72.1% of the participants were female. With respect to comorbidities, there was a higher prevalence of diabetes, relatively low rates of anxiety, and higher levels of depression than the cohort average. Compared with the overall cohort, there was a high prevalence of asthma and the lowest rates of COPD.

Cluster 2: older male-predominant with diabetes and CVD comorbidities

With a mean age of 75.1 years, this cluster is composed of the oldest patients, who are also more likely to be male (60.3%). It is the only predominantly male cluster with the lowest prevalence of never-smokers - just at only 6.9% and the highest prevalence of ex-smokers at 58.3%. Various health conditions, including hypertension, diabetes, IHD, heart failure and atrial fibrillation, were highly prevalent. Patients in this cluster had the highest rates of COPD and bronchiectasis and the lowest rates of asthma compared with the cohort average. One in five patients has more than one obstructive airways disease diagnosis.

Cluster 3: eosinophilic atopic

The mean age of 50.1 years, balanced between males and females. BMI was lower than the cohort average at 26.8. The main characteristic of this cluster is their high eosinophil count with a mean of 0.6 10^9. Low rates of most comorbidities except atopy, which includes diagnoses of hay fever, and eczema were reported. Compared with the cohort average, there were high rates of asthma and low rates of COPD and bronchiectasis.

Cluster 4: older non-comorbid

With a mean age of 68.1, this is the second oldest cluster. Balanced between males and females, with a high uptake of vaccination both for flu and pneumococcus. Despite being older, patients in this cluster tend to have fewer comorbidities, comparable with the cohort average. Lower prevalence of asthma compared with the cohort average, however, this cluster has the highest prevalence of airway disease overlap (2 or 3 diagnoses) and is the second highest in bronchiectasis and COPD prevalence. 1 in 5 patients has more than one obstructive airways disease diagnosis.

Cluster 5: Non-comorbid low BMI

This is the largest and youngest cluster, with a mean age of 45.2. Patients in this cluster are more likely to be female, with the lowest average BMI, more likely than average to be never-smokers, and have lower rates of all comorbidities. Nearly 80% have an asthma diagnosis and a lower prevalence of COPD and bronchiectasis.

Cluster 6: neutrophilic smoker

Patients in this cluster are young, with a mean age of 48.1 years. They are more likely to be female and current smokers (highest prevalence out of all clusters at 57.6%), although a significant proportion are never-smokers (13.5%). They have a higher average neutrophil count than all other clusters and generally have low levels of other comorbidities. The prevalence of each airways disease is comparable to the cohort average.

Cluster 7: Anxious/depressed female-predominant

This cluster has the highest proportion of females, with an average age of 54.9 years. This cluster has the second-highest prevalence of current smokers and is characterised by high levels of depression and anxiety. The prevalence of each airways disease is comparable to the cohort average.

Cluster 1: high BMI female-predominant

This cluster had lower rates of primary care and hospital exacerbation. ED attendance for respiratory-related symptoms was greater than the cohort average. They were otherwise as likely as the cohort average to visit the ED. The death rate by the end date of the study was 7.8%. The leading cause (29.8%) was cancer, with lung cancer being the most common type, followed by breast cancer.

Cluster 2: older male-predominant with diabetes and CVD comorbidities

This cluster had the highest rates of hospital exacerbation, as well as ED attendance for any medical complaint. These patients also had higher than average rates of primary care exacerbations, which cumulatively over 5 years were second only to those of cluster 6 (Neutrophilic smoker). The death rate by the end of the study was 43.8%. Cardiovascular disease was the leading cause of death (28.9%), followed by respiratory causes (26.8%).

Cluster 3: eosinophilic atopic

This cluster had exacerbation and ED attendance rates similar to the cohort average. The death rate by the end of the study was 10.6%. The leading cause of death was respiratory disease (27.6%), closely followed by cancer (26.9%).

Cluster 4: older non-comorbid

This cluster had higher rates of primary care exacerbation than the cohort average and similar levels of hospital exacerbation. The rate of ED attendance was comparable to the cohort average and the cumulative rate of ED attendance for respiratory presentations increased over time. The death rate by the end of the study was 26.6%. The leading cause of death was cancer (28.1%), followed by respiratory causes (27.6%).

Cluster 5: Non-comorbid low BMI

This cluster had the lowest rates of exacerbation in both primary and secondary care. They had average rates of ED attendance for any complaint and lower rates for respiratory complaints. The death rate by the end of the study was 5.2%. The leading cause of death was cancer (35.8%).

Cluster 6: neutrophilic smoker

This cluster had higher rates of primary care exacerbation than the cohort average, with the highest cumulative rate of primary care exacerbation compared with all other clusters. Cumulative rates of hospital exacerbation were second only to cluster 2 (Older males with Diabetes and CVD comorbidities). They were more likely to present to ED with a respiratory complaint. The death rate by the end of the study was 17.3%. The leading cause of death was respiratory disease (37.1%), followed by cancer (27.5%).

Cluster 7: Anxious/depressed female-predominant

This cluster had exacerbation and ED attendance rates similar to the cohort average. Cumulatively, they were the 3rd most likely cluster to present to ED with respiratory complaints and overtook cluster 4 in terms of ED attendance for any medical condition. The death rate by the end of the study was 12.1%. The leading cause of death was cancer (29.3%), with lung cancer being the most common, followed by breast cancer. Death from respiratory causes was second most common, accounting for 27.2% of deaths.

Results on test set and validation cohort (UK Biobank)

Test set results are included in Supplementary Material Tables S5. Cluster characteristics as well as outcome variables were comparable to those of the training cohort. The distribution of cluster characteristics on the Biobank (validation) cohort can be seen in Table 5. Outcomes are presented in Table 6. Overall, the UK Biobank participants were on average 3 years older than those in the CPRD cohort (58.5 vs. 55.9 years). They had similar average BMI and eosinophil counts, but lower on average neutrophil counts (3.9 × 10^9 compared with 4.5 × 10^9 in the CPRD cohort). The sex distribution was more balanced with females making up 58.5% vs. 60% of the overall cohort. Biobank participants were much more likely to be non-smokers (28.9% compared with 15.9% in the CPRD cohort) and less likely to be current smokers (36.2% vs. 44.6% in the CPRD cohort). Patients in the UK Biobank cohort had lower rates on all comorbidities bar hypertension. Due to this finding, a sensitivity analysis was run which excluded hypertension from the list of features. This did not lead to significant differences in the final optimal solution and the cluster composition.

Cluster stability

The robustness of the clustering solution was assessed using a bootstrap sampling method. We constructed 100 bootstrapped samples of the training set and performed k-means clustering with k = 7 clusters, comparing the adjusted rand index (ARI) between each bootstrap and the original k = 7 solution. The average rand index was 0.793 with a standard deviation of 0.17, indicating that the majority of patient pairs are co-assigned to a cluster in each independent bootstrap clustering solution relative to the original clusters.

The ARI was also used to compare clustering solutions adjacent to the one chosen. We calculated the ARI between the k = 6 and k = 7, as well as the k = 7 and k = 8 solutions. ARI67 was 0.79, whereas ARI78 was 0.68. This indicates that the k = 7 clustering solution is close to k = 6, with the majority of patients co-assigned in both solutions. Conversely, the k = 8 solution appears to diverge from k = 7, as shown by the lower ARI78.

Temporal Cluster stability at five years was assessed by redefining the CPRD cohort and relevant variables with all patients remaining at five years past their index date. The cluster membership was assessed by performing identical data preprocessing and cluster fitting steps to the new cohort. The stability of each cluster is visualised in Fig. 7. Overall, cluster stability ranged between 78% (cluster 5: Non-comorbid low BMI) and 38% (cluster 6:neutrophilic, smoker).

GWAS

The total number of cases and controls used in GWAS as well as a summary of all significant results are shown in Table 7. The entire cohort and two out of the seven clusters resulted in at least one genetic variant associated at a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8(Figures S3-S5 in Supplementary material).

The eosinophilic-atopic cluster was enriched for variants in chromosomes 6,9, and 13 and the relevant gene-based test was significant for the genes in the HLA region in chromosome 6 among others (Figure S6 Supplementary material). According to the GWAS Catalogue, the identified SNPs in this cluster have previously been associated with allergic disease and asthma (for details see supplementary file “GWAS_catalogue_k3.xlsx”).

The older non-comorbid cluster was enriched for variants in an intergenic region of chromosome 12. No significant genes were identified in the gene-based test. Finally, the entire cohort was enriched for variants in Chromosomes 6, 9, 15, and 20. The gene-based test identified multiple significant genes predominantly in the chromosome 6 HLA region, as well as the IL13 gene in chromosome 5.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify airway disease phenotypes that transcend the established diagnoses of asthma, bronchiectasis, and COPD. The majority (60%) of the cohort participants were female, a reflection of asthma - the most frequent diagnosis - being more prevalent in females [39]. Additionally, bronchiectasis is more common in females than in males [17], whereas COPD incidence is increasing in females [40]. Consequently, the cohort male: female ratio reflects the current trends, and all bar one clusters were majority female.

Cluster 5 (non-comorbid, low BMI) was the largest, with 27% of patients allocated to it. This cluster contained the highest percentage of never-smokers. Patients in this cluster were less likely to exacerbate and present to A&E, and had the overall lowest age-adjusted mortality risk. This likely represents a group of mostly healthy patients with mild asthma, although some patients with COPD and/or bronchiectasis were also included. This was also the most stable cluster, with 78% of patients re-assigned at five years.

Cluster 7 (anxious/depressed female-predominant) had the highest proportion of females (79%). Patients in this cluster were likely to be smokers and they had the highest prevalence of depression and anxiety, as well as GORD and atopy. Patients in this cluster were more likely than average to attend ED for both respiratory and any medical complaint. Early interventions to target mental health and lifestyle interventions such as social prescribing, and smoking cessation, could be of benefit to this cluster.

Cluster 1 (High BMI female-predominant) patients were relatively young and free of most comorbidities. They were less likely to exacerbate in the terms defined in this study but more likely to attend ED with respiratory concerns than the cohort average. This phenotype and associated outcomes has been previously reported in the literature in both asthma [41] and COPD [9]. They were also, in age-adjusted terms, at increased risk of CVD and respiratory-related death. Patients in this cluster are likely prone to infections and CVD due to diabetes, and could be suited to early interventions targeting metabolic health.

Another female-predominant cluster was Cluster 6 (neutrophilic smoker). The patients in this cluster were young, and non-comorbid, however, they had poor outcomes regarding exacerbations and their mortality risk. The higher neutrophil count appears to be consistent for these patients, as all white cell count values were taken as the median value over a 2-year span, and therefore are not likely to reflect spurious results. This is a group of patients that has not been focused on before, as opposed to patients with high eosinophil counts. This was the least stable cluster, with only 38% of the patients remaining in it after 5 years. The majority (25%) of patients leaving the cluster were re-categorised at 5 years as low BMI - non-comorbid. This result indicates that patients can move from poor outcomes clusters to good outcomes clusters. Patients in this group could therefore be appropriate for early interventions such as smoking cessation.

Cluster 2 (Older male-predominant with Diabetes and CVD comorbidities) was the only predominantly male cluster, with a high prevalence of comorbidities. They had the highest rates of hospital exacerbation, as well as ED attendance for any medical complaint. This cluster has been reported in several other phenotyping studies in COPD [42] and it is possible that certain patients with asthma or bronchiectasis that are likely to behave similarly may be overlooked. To improve outcomes, patients in this cluster could benefit from having their CVD risk factors addressed, for example, screening early for and optimising diabetes control.

Despite being older, patients in cluster 4 (Older, non-comorbid) tend to have fewer comorbidities compared to the cohort average, except for dyslipidemia and diabetes. This cluster has the highest prevalence of airway disease overlap and is the second highest in bronchiectasis and COPD prevalence. The low rates of comorbidities and high vaccination uptake, coupled with lower rates of hospitalisations for acute exacerbations despite high rates of primary care exacerbations, likely point to a group of patients that tend to be more successful at managing their chronic disease.

Cluster 3 (eosinophilic atopic) was balanced between males and females and had low rates of most comorbidities except atopy, which includes diagnoses of hay fever and eczema. Given that many of the features used to generate clusters involved comorbidities rather than airway disease-specific characteristics, it was unsurprising that this was the only cluster with significant GWAS findings. The significant SNPs found in this cluster were primarily related to genes involved in allergies/asthma and immune function. As previously identified in other studies [43, 44] asthma and allergic disease have a shared genetic architecture, including genes in the HLA region.

Finally, possibly due to the predominance of asthma, and high prevalence of atopic conditions, the cohort as a whole had significant GWAS results that were shared with the eosinophilic cluster as well as multiple hits in the HLA region.

Limitations

Silhouette score

We acknowledge that the resultant silhouette coefficients were lower than typical thresholds (e.g., 0.5) often cited for well-separated clusters. This observation is not uncommon in real-world clinical datasets, particularly within heterogeneous conditions such as obstructive respiratory diseases. The inherent complexity and overlapping nature of clinical phenotypes often preclude the formation of perfectly distinct clusters, suggesting a higher degree of continuum rather than discrete categories. Despite a lower silhouette score, the clinical interpretability and validation of our identified clusters, through their association with meaningful clinical outcomes and their alignment with established clinical presentations underscore their clinical significance.

Robustness

To assess the temporal stability and reliability of our identified clusters, a formal robustness analysis was conducted by assessing cluster stability over a 5-year period. Cluster stability at five years ranged from 38% to 78% (average 64%), which is stable compared with other reported cluster stability studies in asthma and COPD [45, 46]. The observed stability demonstrates a considerable degree of temporal consistency for the identified patient subtypes, lending further support to their clinical relevance and persistence over time.

Representativeness of the external validation cohort (UK Biobank)

The lower comorbidity rates in the external validation cohort reflect the overall better health of Biobank participants who are volunteers and, unlike the CPRD cohort, are not representative of the UK population [47]. In addition to this, only European ancestry individuals were included, further reducing the sample, and making this analysis only relevant to this population subset.

Sample size

Only half of the UKBiobank patients had linked primary care records, effectively reducing the possible sample size to half, resulting in less statistical power to observe enrichment for specific clusters. Given the typically small effect sizes common genetic variants have on complex disease, thus requiring large numbers to detect a statistically meaningful association, it is not surprising that the GWAS did not identify variants in most clusters.

Definitions of disease

The definitions of obstructive airways diseases can also influence the results of GWAS analyses. In our study, the definition was based solely on primary care recordings to reflect the definition in the CPRD cohort. Other UKBiobank studies on COPD have used spirometry values and self-reported smoking [48] or self-reported diagnosis, GOLD-criteria based, and ICD-coded [49]. Despite all studies being run on the UKBiobank they have resulted in different variants.

Given that many of the cluster-defining features (comorbidities, vaccination status, etc.) are environmental exposures that are not heritable in the population, it is not surprising that GWAS did not identify statistically significant variants in most of the clusters. Finally, disease heterogeneity and the likely involvement of multiple pathways is likely to affect the strength of the genetic association signals.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complexity and clinical diversity of obstructive airway diseases by uncovering seven phenotypic clusters that extend beyond traditional diagnostic categories. These data-driven subtypes revealed nuanced patterns of comorbidity, exacerbation risk, healthcare utilisation, and mortality - offering a more granular view of disease burden in routine care settings. Importantly, certain clusters, such as those characterised by persistent neutrophilia or mental health comorbidities, point to under-recognised patient groups with poor outcomes who may benefit from more targeted interventions. The partial longitudinal stability of clusters further supports their clinical relevance, though also underscores the dynamic nature of airway disease phenotypes. While genetic enrichment was limited to the eosinophilic-atopic subtype, likely due to the influence of non-heritable factors and sample constraints, these results still provide a foundation for more personalised and biologically-informed approaches to disease management.
