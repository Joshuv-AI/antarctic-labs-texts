Machine learning and SHAP interpretation for predicting coronary heart disease-diabetes comorbidity with dietary antioxidants
Authors: Kangrong Li, Gaoming Zeng, Zixi Zhang, Jiayi Zhu, Siyuan Tan, Zhongjun Ma, Qiuzhen Lin, Zhenjiang Liu, Na Liu, Qiming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coronary heart disease (CHD) and diabetes mellitus frequently co-occur through shared mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammation. Whether specific dietary antioxidants mitigate CHD-diabetes comorbidity remains unclear. Using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2005–2018 data (n = 9,279), we developed an interpretable machine-learning pipeline in which standardisation and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) were embedded inside each fold of tenfold cross-validation to prevent data leakage. Six algorithms (Random Forest, Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), K-nearest neighbours, Naive Bayes, support vector machine, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)) were compared on discrimination, calibration and decision-curve net benefit. XGBoost achieved the highest AUC-ROC (0.774, 95% CI 0.759–0.788); Random Forest showed the lowest Brier score (0.111), the calibration slope closest to unity (0.939) and the highest net benefit, and was retained for interpretation. Weighted-quantile-sum regression showed an inverse association between the antioxidant composite and comorbidity risk (OR per quantile 0.87, 95% CI 0.80–0.95; P = 0.001). In mutually adjusted logistic regression, only magnesium retained an independent protective association (per 1 SD: OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66–0.96; P = 0.016). SHAP identified theobromine (0.020) and lycopene (0.016) as leading protective contributors. Findings support targeted dietary-antioxidant strategies as candidate modifiable factors for cardiometabolic comorbidity prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51080-2.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD) encompasses disorders affecting the heart and blood vessels, including coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease, and deep vein thrombosis. CHD frequently coexists with diabetes mellitus, which constitutes a major comorbid condition in CHD patients. Notably, CHD represents the leading cause of mortality and disability among individuals with diabetes1–3. The concurrent occurrence of CHD and diabetes mellitus has received increasing scientific attention, given that diabetes and CHD share overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms and common etiological factors. These primarily include insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, hypercoagulability, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, and ferritin deposition. Particularly, insulin resistance and chronic hyperglycemia may potentiate inflammatory responses, exacerbate oxidative stress, induce endothelial dysfunction, and accelerate atherosclerotic progression4–7.

Dietary antioxidants (e.g., flavonoids, vitamins, polyphenolic compounds) are central in mitigating oxidative stress-mediated cellular damage through free radical scavenging, inhibition of lipid peroxidation, and modulation of redox signaling pathways8–10. Within the pathological context of cardiometabolic disease-diabetes comorbidity, the preventive potential of dietary antioxidants has attracted considerable scientific attention in recent years. Although epidemiological evidence indicates an inverse association between antioxidant intake and the risk of individual metabolic disorders (CHD or diabetes), their specific protective effects in the complex pathophysiology of cardiometabolic-diabetes comorbidity remain incompletely elucidated11–14. Therefore, further investigation into the protective effects of dietary antioxidants against CHD-diabetes comorbidity is of significant importance.

This observational study leveraged data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the association between dietary antioxidant intake and CHD-diabetes comorbidity using machine learning (ML) approaches. Compared with conventional statistical methods, ML algorithms demonstrate enhanced capability in processing multidimensional nutritional datasets and detecting nonlinear relationships among metabolic risk factors, thereby improving risk stratification accuracy. Our analytical framework incorporated model benchmarking, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for feature interpretation, complemented by weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression to assess both cumulative and component-specific effects of antioxidant mixtures.

Participants and methods

Participants

NHANES (conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics) collects demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related data for health assessment. Participants from NHANES 2005–2018 were considered candidate subjects for this study. The study included individuals with complete data on dietary antioxidant intake, CHD diagnosis, and diabetes diagnosis. Participants with missing baseline characteristic data or those without diabetes diagnosis were excluded. Detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria are presented in Fig. 1. Specifically, of the 70,190 participants sampled by NHANES 2005–2018, 9,549 were excluded for missing 24-h dietary-recall data, 20,384 for the absence of a physician diagnosis of diabetes (the analytic cohort was intentionally restricted to adults with diabetes, such that the comparison is between diabetes with comorbid CHD and diabetes only), and 30,978 for missing key covariates (education, poverty-income ratio, body mass index, hypertension, smoking, or alcohol use), yielding a final analytic sample of 9,279 participants (comorbid CHD-diabetes, n = 1,152; diabetes only, n = 8,127).Fig. 1Participant selection flow for the NHANES 2005–2018 analytic cohort. Dietary antioxidant intake

Dietary intake data for 45 antioxidants (including flavonoids, vitamins, polyphenolic compounds) were obtained from NHANES. Participants completed two 24-h dietary recall interviews at the Mobile Examination Center, separated by 3–10 days. The daily average intake of dietary antioxidants was calculated.

Diagnosis of CHD and diabetes

CHD was identified in participants’ self-reported questionnaire data. Diabetes mellitus was ascertained through affirmative responses to the question “Has a doctor ever told you that you have diabetes?” or by meeting at least one of the following criteria: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L, 2-h oral glucose tolerance test plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, random serum glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or current use of anti-diabetic medications. Participants with both self-reported CHD and laboratory- or self-report-confirmed diabetes formed the comorbidity group (cases); all other participants (those with neither condition, CHD only, or diabetes only) were pooled into a single non-comorbidity control group for the primary analysis.

Collection of baseline features

Baseline characteristics were collected across three domains: demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health status. These variables included age, sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races), educational attainment (< 9th grade, 9–11th grade, high school graduate, some college, college graduate), family poverty-to-income ratio, body mass index (BMI), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), and hypertension status. Data on age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, and family income ratio were obtained from the Demographic Data module in NHANES, BMI measurements from the Examination Data module, while smoking and alcohol use information derived from the Questionnaire Data module. Hypertension was defined as having ≥ 3 recorded systolic/diastolic blood pressure readings ≥ 130/80 mmHg and/or self-reported use of antihypertensive medications or physician-diagnosed hypertension.

Pre-processing of ML features

The dataset comprised 54 initial variables (48 continuous and 6 categorical). To address multicollinearity among dietary antioxidant-related variables, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF), with degree-of-freedom-adjusted VIF values greater than 3 indicating redundancy. The Boruta algorithm — a Random-Forest-based wrapper method — was then employed for feature selection, systematically evaluating variable importance through iterative comparisons between original features and their permuted shadow counterparts, with up to 100 Boruta iterations (maxRuns = 100) using a Random Forest comparator of 500 trees (ntree = 500). Feature significance was determined upon convergence stability, with importance metrics extracted via the attStats function and normalised using a customised adjustdata function15,16.To prevent information leakage, z-score standardisation and the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE; dup_size = 3), which generates synthetic minority samples through k-nearest-neighbour interpolation in feature space, were embedded inside each cross-validation (CV) fold using the mlr3pipelines package, so that synthetic minority samples and feature scaling were derived only from training folds and test folds contained only original observations 17,18.

SHAP interpretability analysis

We implemented SHAP on the Random Forest model (selected as the interpretation vehicle based on its calibration performance and decision-curve net benefit rather than on discrimination alone), quantifying feature importance via mean absolute Shapley values and producing case-level decision paths, in line with recent applications of SHAP to machine-learning models of cardiovascular disease risk in NHANES19. The reference output E[f(x)] was defined as the expected model output over the training distribution, and feature contributions are reported on the log-odds scale (converted to probability for presentation); we acknowledge that SHAP-ranked importance can be sensitive to feature correlation and does not imply causality, so downstream statements are framed as associations rather than causal effects20.

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified by comorbidity status and compared using survey-weighted analyses. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± SE, with categorical variables summarized as weighted proportions (%). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard error, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. To avoid the observation-independence assumption required by Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) (which is violated when all six models share the same 10 cross-validation folds), pairwise model comparisons of the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) were performed using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiplicity. Within the mlr3 machine learning framework, we implemented six discriminative models: Random Forest (RF), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), k-Nearest Neighbours (k-NN), Naive Bayes (NB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost).

The benchmarking framework systematically evaluates ML model performance through standardized assessment protocols. Following clinical prediction model guidelines, we implemented uniform evaluation metrics comprising accuracy, F-Beta, AUC-ROC, sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR), accounting for class imbalance in clinical datasets. AUC-ROC served as the primary model selection criterion, with other metrics providing complementary performance validation. To mitigate evaluation bias, we employed tenfold cross-validation with stratified sampling. We further assessed calibration by the Brier score, calibration slope and intercept from logistic recalibration of predicted log-odds, and loess-smoothed calibration curves, and examined clinical utility by decision-curve analysis (DCA)21,22.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v4.4.1) with specific methodological implementations: WQS regression employed the “gWQS” package and the Boruta algorithm was executed through the “Boruta” package (v8.0.0). Analytical workflows incorporated the survey, mlr3, mlr3pipelines, kernelshap, and shapviz packages for data weighting, class balancing, machine learning pipeline construction, and model interpretation. Statistical tests were two-sided and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To comply with APA bias-free language guidelines, race/ethnicity categories throughout this paper are described as participants’ self-identified race/ethnicity as recorded in NHANES.

Survey weights

Descriptive comparisons between groups incorporated the NHANES complex survey design — using the dietary-day-one sample weights, primary sampling units and strata provided by NCHS — through Taylor-series linearisation in the survey and srvyr R packages. Machine-learning models were fitted without survey weights 23; weighting-related demographics (age, sex, self-identified race/ethnicity and the poverty-income ratio) were retained as features so that the algorithms could account for the complex sampling design implicitly.

Discussion

This study employed explainable machine learning to analyse associations between dietary antioxidant intake and CHD-diabetes comorbidity using NHANES 2005–2018 (n = 9,279). Among the six evaluated learners, XGBoost attained the highest AUC-ROC (0.774, 95% CI 0.759–0.788) under a leakage-free cross-validation pipeline, closely followed by LightGBM (0.770), SVM (0.769) and Random Forest (0.766); Random Forest achieved the lowest Brier score (0.111) and the greatest decision-curve net benefit across the clinically relevant threshold range, and was therefore retained as the vehicle for SHAP-based interpretation. SHAP analysis identified theobromine (0.020) and lycopene (0.016) as the leading dietary protective contributors, with magnesium additionally independently protective in the mutually adjusted logistic sensitivity analysis (Table S2). To our knowledge, this is the first NHANES-based prediction model for CHD-diabetes comorbidity to integrate dietary antioxidants with demographic, lifestyle and clinical covariates under a leakage-free cross-validation pipeline. No single learner dominated across all pre-specified evaluation metrics: XGBoost attained the highest AUC-ROC (0.774; 95% CI 0.759–0.788), while LightGBM (0.770), SVM (0.769) and Random Forest (0.766) were within one standard error; Random Forest attained the lowest Brier score (0.111), calibration slope closest to unity (0.939) and the greatest net benefit across the clinically relevant threshold-probability range of 0.10–0.50 (Figs. 13 and 14; Table S4). Because current methodological consensus emphasises that probability calibration21 and decision-curve net benefit22 are more consequential than marginal AUC differences for clinical deployment, we retained Random Forest as the single vehicle for SHAP-based interpretation. We view this as a choice reflecting clinical translatability rather than a claim of overall model superiority, and we did not perform formal model averaging because downstream interpretation is better served by a single, tractable classifier.Theobromine, a methylxanthine alkaloid primarily found in cocoa products, has demonstrated multiple cardiometabolic benefits. As reported by Elham et al. in a systematic literature review, theobromine improves lipid profiles—including reductions in triglycerides and LDL-C—and enhances vascular function24. These effects are particularly relevant for CHD prevention given dyslipidemia and vascular dysfunction are established risk factors. However, theobromine’s impact on glycemic status remains inconclusive, with some studies indicating adverse effects on postprandial glucose levels25. While theobromine may support cardiovascular health, its role in diabetes management requires further investigation. Our study identified theobromine as a negative predictor for CHD-diabetes comorbidity risk, aligning with its cardiovascular benefits while highlighting the need for elucidating its metabolic effects.

Lycopene, a carotenoid pigment abundant in tomatoes and other red fruits, is recognised for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Shafe et al. reviewed lycopene’s multiple health benefits, including reductions in fasting glucose and improvements in endothelial function in type 2 diabetes26. Ma et al. further reported that an XGBoost-based model using lycopene intake and related covariates predicted CHD in Chinese individuals with diabetes27. These findings align with lycopene’s proposed mechanisms—reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, and modulating lipid and glucose metabolism26,27. In our model, lycopene consistently emerged as a protective predictor, reinforcing its potential role in dietary strategies for CHD-diabetes comorbidity prevention, even though causal inference from SHAP importance alone is not warranted. Our individual-antioxidant logistic sensitivity analysis (Table S2) showed that only magnesium retained a statistically significant protective association with CHD-diabetes comorbidity after mutual adjustment (per 1 SD: OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.66–0.96, P = 0.016). The triangulation of the WQS composite (Table 2), the mutually adjusted individual logistic regression (Table S2) and the SHAP importance ranking (Fig. 9) on magnesium makes it unlikely that this protective signal is an artefact of a single modelling approach. Despite encouraging biological plausibility—magnesium’s roles in insulin-signalling and vascular tone are well characterised—residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., whole-food dietary patterns rich in magnesium) cannot be excluded, and our findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. Machine learning is increasingly applied to CHD-diabetes comorbidity risk prediction, and recent work has demonstrated value across clinical, imaging and omics datasets15,19,27–29. Our contribution is to add a dietary-antioxidant dimension under a rigorous leakage-free pipeline, pairing interpretable mixture modelling (WQS) with patient-level SHAP explanations and formal calibration/net-benefit assessment. This combination addresses a gap in the nutritional-epidemiology literature where mixture effects of antioxidants on comorbid cardiometabolic disease have rarely been examined with concurrent individual-level interpretability and clinical-utility evaluation.Under the MLR3 framework, six discriminative algorithms—RF, LightGBM, K-KNN, NB, SVM, and XGBoost—were evaluated. The RF algorithm employs bootstrap aggregating with multiple decision trees to enhance predictive accuracy while mitigating overfitting, particularly effective in high-dimensional medical data analysis through embedded feature importance evaluation30,31. LightGBM optimizes gradient boosting through gradient-based one-side sampling and exclusive feature bundling, achieving superior computational efficiency for large-scale datasets32. K-KNN operates via instance-based similarity measures in kernel-transformed space, demonstrating robustness in imbalanced data scenarios without requiring explicit model training33. NB leverages conditional probability estimation under feature independence assumptions, maintaining stable performance in high-dimensional sparse data contexts34. SVM constructs maximum-margin hyperplanes through kernel tricks to address nonlinear classification boundaries35,36, while XGBoost enhances gradient-boosted decision trees via parallel tree construction and regularization techniques20,37,38.

Our findings demonstrate that RF achieved the highest performance among evaluated learners in constructing CHD-diabetes comorbidity diagnostic models. The ensemble learning framework effectively mitigates overfitting through collaborative predictions from multiple decision trees, in line with the stable cross-validated discrimination, calibration and net-benefit behaviour observed in this cohort. RF not only deciphers nonlinear interactions between metabolic parameters and cardiovascular events, but also robustly processes high-dimensional heterogeneous data from electronic health records. Its bootstrap aggregation strategy ensures clinical prediction stability by reducing base learner variance. Compared to opaque deep learning architectures, RF’s feature importance quantification and decision path visualization mechanisms provide interpretable evidence for biomarker screening and clinical decision-making while maintaining diagnostic accuracy—a critical advantage in personalized management of high-risk comorbid patients39.These interpretability properties are further reinforced by recent methodological advances on tree-ensemble interpretability: post-hoc tree smoothing has been shown to stabilise variable-importance rankings in small-to-moderate samples, providing a principled complement to the Boruta-based selection and SHAP-based attribution used here40.

We acknowledge several important limitations. First, coronary heart disease was ascertained from participants’ self-reported questionnaires, which is subject to misclassification bias and may inflate discrimination compared with objective angiographic assessment; we discuss this explicitly. Second, our model was evaluated only by internal nested cross-validation within NHANES and has not been externally validated; external validation in an independent cohort is required before clinical adoption. Third, although we adjusted for principal demographic, socio-economic and clinical covariates, residual confounding from glucose- or lipid-lowering medications, anti-hypertensives and unmeasured disease severity may persist. Fourth, SHAP values are association-level importance measures and do not represent causal effects; accordingly, we frame our findings as modifiable candidates rather than causal mediators. Fifth, although our SHAP-based analysis provides both global and case-level attributions, complementary explainability paradigms — such as counterfactual-path reasoning — could supply non-additive, instance-level narratives of how specific feature changes would modify an individual patient’s predicted risk, and their integration with SHAP represents a natural next step towards clinically deployable decision support41.

Conclusion

In summary, this study deepens understanding of the role of dietary nutrients in CHD-diabetes comorbidity, highlighting the potential protective effects of antioxidants—particularly theobromine, lycopene and magnesium. By integrating WQS regression, mutually adjusted logistic regression and SHAP-based interpretable machine learning, we provide triangulated evidence for potentially modifiable dietary targets. The Random Forest model demonstrated favourable calibration and decision-curve net benefit in tenfold cross-validation and may serve as a candidate tool for risk stratification in this population; independent external validation remains a prerequisite before clinical translation.
