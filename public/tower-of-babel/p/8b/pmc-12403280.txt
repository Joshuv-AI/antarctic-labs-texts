Predictive value of the STOP-Bang Questionnaire for respiratory depression risk in patients undergoing painless gastrointestinal endoscopy
Authors: Xunbin Qiu, Mengting Huang, Qun Wang, Ping Guo, Xiaoli Yang, Yue Liu, Yujian Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is an independent risk factor for perioperative respiratory complications. The STOP-Bang Questionnaire (SBQ) is a widely used screening tool; however, its utility in predicting respiratory depression during deep sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy warrants further exploration. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive performance of the SBQ for respiratory depression in this patient population.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study enrolled patients who underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy under deep sedation at Dongguan Binhaiwan Central Hospital from November 2024 to February of the following year. The data collected included demographics, medical history, vital signs, and SBQ scores. Standard sedation monitoring was complemented by portable sleep monitoring systems to record the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI). Patients were grouped based on their lowest intraoperative pulse oximetry (SpO₂) level. Spearman correlation, Kappa test, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and binary logistic regression were used to analyze the relationships between SBQ scores, AHI, and respiratory depression events.ResultsData of 349 patients were included in the final analysis. Transient hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) occurred in 65 (18.6%) patients, with 12 (3.4%) patients experiencing severe hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 80%). Both SBQ scores and AHI demonstrated significant negative correlations with SpO₂ levels (r = -0.520 and r = -0.737, respectively; both P < 0.001). Using SpO₂ < 85% as the threshold, the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) for the SBQ score was 0.942. The optimal SBQ cutoff value was 2.5, yielding a sensitivity of 0.871 and a specificity of 0.912. Logistic regression identified snoring (OR = 14.240), observed apnea (OR = 7.092), and elevated BMI (OR = 10.904) as independent predictors of severe hypoxemia.ConclusionRespiratory depression events are relatively common during deeply sedated gastroenteroscopies. The SBQ score effectively predicts this risk, particularly in identifying patients susceptible to severe hypoxemia. An SBQ score ≥ 3 serves as a practical threshold for recognizing high-risk individuals, with snoring, observed apnea, and high BMI as key warning indicators. SBQ is a valid and concise preoperative screening tool for this patient group.Clinical trial registrationRegistry: ClinicalTrials.gov; Name: Preoperative Assessment of Hypoxia Risk in Painless GI Endoscopy; URL: https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=272808.

Brief summary

Current knowledge/study rationale

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is associated with an increased risk of respiratory depression during deep sedation for procedures such as painless gastrointestinal endoscopy. SBQ is a widely used screening tool for OSA in perioperative settings; however, its specific predictive value for respiratory depression in this context remains under-explored.

Study impact

This prospective observational cohort study of 349 patients demonstrates that a SBQ score of 3 or higher is a reliable predictor of respiratory depression during deep sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy. ROC curve analysis revealed an area under AUC of 0.942, indicating high sensitivity and specificity. These findings provide anesthesiologists with a straightforward risk stratification tool to optimize perioperative management and support the development of safer sedation strategies, ultimately reducing the incidence of hypoxemia and enhancing patient safety.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)is characterized by recurrent partial or complete upper airway collapse during sleep, resulting in apnea and hypopnea. Globally, the estimated prevalence of OSA ranges from 9 to 38% (Benjafield et al. 2019). In China, recent estimates suggest an OSA prevalence of approximately 12% in adults aged 30–69 years, with moderate-to-severe OSA affecting 37.5% of this population (Huang et al. 2022).

Patients with OSA face elevated respiratory risks during sedation or anesthesia owing to multiple mechanisms, including sedative-induced suppression of genioglossus muscle tone, opioid-related depression of the respiratory center, and airway narrowing exacerbated by the supine position, all of which significantly increase the incidence of hypoxemia (Kim et al. 2016).

Although the factors associated with intraoperative hypoxemia have been studied, large-scale preoperative screening tools for high-risk patients with OSA remain limited. Polysomnography (PSG), the gold diagnostic standard, is costly and time-consuming. Traditional tools such as the Berlin Questionnaire suffer from low completion rates and limited practicality. In contrast, the STOP-Bang Questionnaire (SBQ) is a concise and effective OSA screening tool known for its high sensitivity, ease of use, rapid completion, suitability for clinical assessment, and correlation with the severity of OSA (Chung et al. 2008; Luo et al. 2019). Studies have shown that an SBQ score ≥ 3 achieves a sensitivity of 91.4% for moderate-to-severe OSA and 95.4% for severe OSA in sleep clinic populations (Wang et al. 2014).

However, epidemiological data on OSA in sedated endoscopy patients in China are scarce, with existing evidence primarily from perioperative settings involving general anesthesia and intubation. This study aimed to conduct a prospective, cross-sectional investigation to assess the prevalence of high OSA risk in patients undergoing deep sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy in our region. Additionally, we sought to examine the association between SBQ scores and respiratory depression events during the peri-procedural period to provide evidence for risk-stratified sedation management strategies.

Methods

Study population

Following approval from the Dongguan Science and Technology Bureau (Project No.: 20231800937022 and 20,231,800,935,512), this prospective study was conducted at Dongguan Binhaiwan Central Hospital from November 2024 to February of the subsequent year. We enrolled individuals who voluntarily underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and colonoscopy under deep sedation with monitored anesthesia. All participants provided written informed consent after a discussion with the research staff on the day preceding the procedure. The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, scheduled EGD and colonoscopy, and no prior OSA diagnosis. Exclusion criteria were: (1) allergy to propofol, eggs, or soy; (2) pregnancy; (3) severe cardiopulmonary comorbidities (e.g., congestive heart failure, arrhythmias, active asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) or baseline SpO₂ < 98%; (4) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status III–V; and (5) refusal to participate or inability to complete the questionnaire.

Preoperative assessment

Certified registered nurse anesthetists collected demographic and baseline clinical data, including personal information, initial vital signs (e.g., heart rate), and medical history, and performed a focused physical examination emphasizing the oropharyngeal structures. The SBQ was completed jointly by patients and their families, covering eight items based on criteria adapted for Asian populations: snoring (S), tiredness/fatigue during the daytime (T), observed apnea during sleep (O), high blood pressure history (P), body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2 (B), age > 50 years (A), neck circumference > 40 cm (N), and male gender (G). Each affirmative response was scored 1 point, and a negative response was scored 0 points, with the total score assessing OSA risk (Zhang and Xiao 2015).

Procedure

Monitoring protocols

A standardized sedation monitoring protocol was implemented: preoperative oxygen at 3 L/min via a nasal cannula, continuous monitoring of pulse oximetry (SpO₂), electrocardiogram, and non-invasive blood pressure using conventional systems. Respiratory data, including the Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI), were acquired in real-time during the procedure using the ALICE-PDX portable polysomnography system, which was calibrated preoperatively to ensure accuracy. The system integrated a nasal pressure cannula, thoracic and abdominal movement belts, and a six-axis body position sensor to record respiratory events.

Event definitions

Respiratory events were defined according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines: apnea was defined as cessation of nasal pressure signal for ≥ 10; and hypopnea as a ≥ 30% reduction in nasal pressure signal amplitude lasting ≥ 10 (Berry et al. 2012).The lowest intraoperative SpO₂ value was recorded as an indicator of the oxygenation status. A stepwise airway management algorithm was employed during deep sedation: if SpO₂ decreased to 80–90% for 60, oxygen flow was increased to 6 L/min and a jaw-thrust maneuver was performed. If SpO₂ fell below 80%, mask ventilation with positive pressure was initiated. Failure to improve oxygenation (SpO₂ < 80% for 60 s) prompted immediate advanced airway establishment.

Drug administration protocol

A dose-titration approach was followed: an initial loading dose of butorphanol 0.5 mg combined with ciprofol 0.4 mg/kg was administered, followed by supplemental ciprofol boluses of 0.15 mg/kg/dose at intervals ≥ 2 min to maintain sedation depth. The sedation level was assessed using the Ramsay Sedation Scale, targeting a score of 5–6 (drowsiness with sluggish response to glabellar tap or loud auditory stimulus). Discharge criteria required: ① Ramsay score recovery to 2 (patient awake and oriented)and② accompaniment by a responsible adult caregiver.

Data analysis and statistics

Sample size estimation

The sample size calculation adhered to diagnostic test guidelines. Using Buder's formula, based on a reported SBQ sensitivity for OSA diagnosis of 0.90, an acceptable margin of error of 0.09, an anticipated prevalence of 5%, with α = 0.05 and power (1-β) = 0.9, the minimum required sample size was 173 participants. To account for potential data loss (e.g., incomplete procedures or missing AHI data) and to enhance statistical power for secondary analyses, we enrolled 360 eligible subjects, of which 349 were included in the final analysis.

Data processing

Stratified analysis was employed: ① continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD); ② categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages); ③ SpO₂ levels were categorized into four levels: normal (SpO₂ ≥ 95%), mild decrease (95% > SpO₂ ≥ 90%), moderate decrease (90% > SpO₂ ≥ 85%), and severe decrease (SpO₂ < 85%); and④ AHI was categorized into four groups: normal (AHI < 5), mild (5 ≤ AHI < 15), moderate (15 ≤ AHI < 30), and severe (AHI ≥ 30). Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test (for expected cell counts < 5) were used to compare categorical variables across groups.

Statistical inference

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to explore the association between SBQ scores, AHI categories, and SpO₂ levels. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using SpO₂ as the covariate to determine the optimal diagnostic cutoff values for SBQ and AHI scores. The DeLong test was used to compare the area under the ROC curves (AUCs). Principal component analysis was used to identify key predictive factors within the SBQ, and multivariate logistic regression was performed to calculate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 360 participants meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were enrolled, all completing the SBQ assessment. Patients underwent SBQ completion and physical examination the day before the procedure and fasted on the day of surgery. In 3 patients, the colonoscope failed to reach the cecum, rendering the examination incomplete. An additional 8 patients were excluded because missing intraoperative data or deviations in the AHI data. Thus, the data from 349 patients were included in the final analysis. Transient hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) occurred in 65 (18.6%) patients, with hypoxemia lasting over 60 s in 46 patient (13.2%). A tiered intervention approach was used: for SpO₂ between 80–90%, the oxygen flow was increased from 3 L/min to 6 L/min with a jaw-thrust maneuver, effectively correcting hypoxemia in all cases. Severe hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 80%) occurred in 12 (3.4%) patients, with oxygenation improving rapidly after bag-mask ventilation, and none of the cases required advanced airway insertion.

Quantitative data

The mean age of participants was 45.89 ± 12.29 years (range: 20–78 years). The time to recover from deep sedation to a Ramsay score of 3 was 16.54 ± 5.28 min. The average ciprofol dose was 41.36 ± 9.56 mg. No significant differences were observed in these metrics between SpO₂ groups (Table 1). SBQ item positivity rates and group comparisons

Positivity rates for SBQ items were as follows: snoring (S) 19.48%, tiredness (T) 3.15%, observed apnea (O) 8.60%, high blood pressure (P) 16.62%, BMI > 30 kg/m2 (B) 6.3%, age > 50 years (A) 32.66%, neck circumference > 40 cm (N) 6.88%, and male gender(G) 43.84%. Patients were divided into four groups based on intraoperative hypoxemia severity, with proportions of 57.31%, 21.20%, 9.74%, and 8.88%, respectively (Table 1). Statistical analysis revealed significant differences across SpO₂ groups for all SBQ items except age (p < 0.05). Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction confirmed that the SpO₂ < 85% group had significantly higher positivity rates for snoring (p < 0.001), observed apnea (p < 0.001), and BMI > 30 kg/m2 (p < 0.001) compared to the SpO₂ ≥ 95% group. For continuous variables, Tukey's post and Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test indicated significant differences in BMI and AHI between the SpO₂ < 85% group and all other groups (p < 0.001).

Correlation analysis

Spearman’s correlation analysis indicated a significant negative relationship between SBQ scores and SpO₂ levels (r = −0.520, P < 0.001) and between AHI and SpO₂ levels (r = −0.737, P < 0.001). A kappa test assessing agreement between AHI classification and SpO₂ monitoring yielded a Kappa value of 0.112 (P < 0.001), suggesting a statistically significant but weak agreement (Table 2). Discussion

This prospective observational cohort study of 349 patients undergoing deep sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy validates the STOP-Bang Questionnaire (SBQ) as a robust tool for predicting intraoperative hypoxemia risk. Transient hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%) occurred in 18.6% of patients, with 8.9% experiencing severe hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 85%). SBQ scores ≥ 3 demonstrated excellent diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.942, 95% CI: 0.896–0.987), with snoring (OR = 14.240), observed apnea (OR = 7.092), and BMI > 30 kg/m2 (OR = 10.904) identified as significant predictors (p < 0.05). The wide confidence intervals reflect the low incidence of severe hypoxemia (8.9%, n = 31) and sparse positive responses for certain SBQ items (e.g., observed apnea, 8.6%; BMI > 30 kg/m2, 6.3%), which increased estimation variability, potentially exacerbated by collinearity among related variables like snoring and observed apnea. Nevertheless, the significant p-values and lower confidence interval bounds affirm the predictive utility of these factors.

OSA is a well-established independent risk factor for perioperative respiratory complications. Benjafield et al. reported a global prevalence of mild to severe OSA (AHI ≥ 5) in adults ranging from 9 to 38%, with rates of 13%–33% in men and 6%–19% in women (Benjafield et al. 2019). despite lower average BMI in Asian populations, OSA prevalence in Chinese adults is comparable to Western populations, with moderate-to-severe OSA affecting 3.7%–4.1% (Tan et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2019). Although PSG remains the diagnostic gold standard, its high cost and complexity limit widespread use, prompting the development of screening tools like the Berlin Questionnaire, STOP Questionnaire, and SBQ. The SBQ is preferred for large-scale screening because of its simplicity, clarity, rapid completion, and high sensitivity and specificity (Luo et al. 2019). However, its predictive performance in patients undergoing deep sedation requires further validation.

Frequency analysis of the eight SBQ items in this study revealed significant differences (P < 0.05) for all items except age. This lack of significance for age may be attributed to younger patients requiring higher sedative doses, with dose-dependent respiratory depression potentially offsetting the expected positive correlation with age. This finding aligns with a study from the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University, where age differences between the OSA and non-OSA groups were not statistically significant among 218 patients with suspected OSA (He and Wang 2017).Previous studies recommend an SBQ score ≥ 3 as the high-risk threshold for OSA; in our cohort, 15.54% met this criterion,which is lower than the 36.8% reported in a Singaporean population survey (Chung et al. 2013).Our study confirmed that patients and their families, under healthcare personnel guidance, could efficiently complete the questionnaire, supporting its feasibility for large-scale clinical screening.

Among the 349 patients analyzed, 12 experienced severe intraoperative respiratory depression (lowest SpO₂ < 80%). Bag-mask ventilation assistance restored SpO₂ above 80% in these cases, and subsequent management with increased oxygen flow maintained saturation within safe limits, eliminating the need for advanced airway intervention.

Initial correlation analyses revealed significant inverse relationships between SBQ scores and hypoxemia severity (Spearman’s r = −0.520, P < 0.001) and between AHI values and hypoxemia severity (r = −0.737, P < 0.001), indicating that higher SBQ and AHI scores corresponded to lower oxygen saturation. Although the Kappa test showed statistically a significant agreement between AHI classification and hypoxemia occurrence, the agreement was weak. This suggests that AHI monitoring is not fully equivalent to SpO₂ monitoring, although some studies have indicated that AHI may provide earlier warnings before arterial oxygen desaturation becomes apparent (Lin et al. 2018).

The mean AHI in our sample (16.4 ± 10.8) was notably higher than expected during natural sleep. Previous research indicates that AHI values increase significantly under sedation in patients without OSA or with mild OSA, whereas changes are less pronounced in those with moderate-to-severe OSA (Park et al. 2020).This may be due to the stronger inhibitory effect of deep sedation on airway muscle tone than that of natural sleep, compounded by the central respiratory depressant effects of anesthetics, leading to increased AHI severity during sedation. Notably, all patients in this study were positioned laterally and received continuous oxygen at 3 L/min via a nasal cannula, which likely mitigated the extent of hypoxemia (Kim et al. 2016).

ROC analysis using SpO₂ thresholds of 95%, 90%, and 85% showed that AHI outperformed SBQ in identifying mild-to-moderate hypoxemia. Evidence suggests that AHI monitoring provides a more precise and comprehensive assessment of respiratory events during deep sedation than pulse oximetry alone, potentially offering earlier warnings (Lin et al. 2018). However, for predicting severe hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 85%), the diagnostic performance of the SBQ (cutoff ≥ 3) was comparable to that of AHI (AUCs of 0.942 and 0.970,respectively), consistent with findings from another Chinese study (Wang et al. 2014). Beyond deep sedation, SBQ may also be valuable for respiratory monitoring during the recovery phase after airway device removal.

Although the SBQ demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy at the SpO₂ < 85% threshold (significant AUC), clinical practice often requires a lower tolerance for hypoxemia risk, making SpO₂ < 90% a more appropriate warning threshold. At this level, the optimal SBQ cutoff remained 2.5 (clinically applied as ≥ 3), with a sensitivity of 0.62 and specificity of 0.96, supporting its clinical utility. Logistic regression analysis incorporating all eight SBQ factors identified snoring, observed apnea, and elevated BMI as significant independent risk factors for intraoperative hypoxemia, with ORs of 14.240, 7.092, and 10.904, respectively. Thus, preoperative risk screening should prioritize these indicators to optimize respiratory safety during sedation.

All patients with an SBQ score ≥ 5 experienced moderate or severe hypoxemia during the procedure. For such patients undergoing complex therapeutic endoscopic procedures (e.g., esophageal stenting, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy [PEG], endoscopic sphincterotomy [EST]), securing the airway (e.g., via endotracheal intubation) should be strongly considered. However, the SBQ has limitations; while a score of < 3 makes severe hypoxemia unlikely, it does not guarantee respiratory stability during sedation.

This study had several limitations. First, while OSA is a major independent risk factor for perioperative respiratory events, other factors related to the patient and procedure (e.g., anesthesia duration, drug choice, and procedure type) also contribute (Zhang et al. 2022). Although our sample size was adequate, this was a single-center study, potentially limiting generalizability. We also considered the low incidence of severe hypoxemia (8.9%), the limited proportion of positive responses to SBQ items (e.g., tiredness: 3.15%; neck circumference > 40 cm: 6.87%; BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2: 6.30%), as well as potential collinearity among predictors. These factors may compromise the precision of current estimates, underscoring the need for larger-scale studies to enhance the robustness and accuracy of future evaluations. Second, conducting the study on an outpatient basis might have excluded high-risk patients. Finally, the enrolled patients underwent screening or simple diagnostic endoscopic procedures, and more invasive and lengthy therapeutic procedures were excluded.

Conclusion

In conclusion, respiratory depression, often linked to underlying OSA, is prevalent in patients undergoing esophagogastroduodenoscopy and colonoscopy under deep sedation. The SBQ score accurately predicts the risk of abnormal respiratory events in this context. From a cost-effectiveness perspective, SBQ is a valuable, efficient, and straightforward preoperative screening tool for patients scheduled for gastroenterological procedures.
