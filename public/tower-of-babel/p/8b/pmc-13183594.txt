White matter hyperintensities in Alzheimer's disease in the era of anti‐amyloid therapies
Authors: Antoine Garnier‐Crussard, Mahsa Dadar, Nicolas Villain, Andreas Charidimou, Grégoire Boulouis, François Cotton, Adam M Brickman, Gaël Chételat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhite matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are highly prevalent in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and arise from interacting vascular pathologies (including hypertensive small vessel disease and cerebral amyloid angiopathy) alongside inflammatory and neurodegenerative processes. In the era of anti‐amyloid monoclonal antibodies, this heterogeneity is increasingly relevant for both treatment efficacy and safety. WMHs may signal mixed AD–vascular pathology that dilutes the cognitive benefit of amyloid‐targeting therapies and may also index vulnerability of the neurovascular unit that predisposes to amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs), although direct evidence remains limited. In this perspective, we synthesize current knowledge on the origins of WMHs in AD, review advanced magnetic resonance imaging and biomarker approaches that aim to refine lesion characterization in vivo, and discuss how WMHs should be interpreted in memory clinic practice when considering anti‐amyloid therapies. We conclude with a research roadmap to integrate WMH phenotyping into precision risk–benefit assessment and ARIA prediction .Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, anti‐amyloid therapy, amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities, ARIA, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, precision medicine, quantitative magnetic resonance imaging, MRI, white matter hyperintensities, WMHs

INTRODUCTION

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) have been studied extensively in the context of aging and neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD). WMHs are defined as hyperintense on T2­ or fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences and typically symmetrical between hemispheres (Figure 1). 1 Their definition thus relies solely on their imaging characteristics, but not on their putative pathophysiology and mechanistic origin. Yet, while common in many neurological conditions, such as inflammatory or infectious diseases of the central nervous system, they might reflect distinct pathophysiological processes. Beyond these neurological conditions, WMHs are also frequently seen in so‐called normal aging, that is, in older adults without dementia. In this context, WMH volumes should be interpreted in the context of age, and age‐associated WMH normative curves stratified by sex are now available in very large datasets. 2 WMH origin is usually considered to be “vascular,” as a feature of cerebral small vessel disease. However, their precise pathophysiology and relevance to AD have been debated since WMHs were first described in 1986 as “leukoaraiosis.” 3 , 4 , 5 Clarifying the pathophysiology and clinical consequences of WMHs in AD is becoming increasingly critical for clinical decision‐making and patient care, particularly with respect to the safety of emerging treatments for AD and increasing recognition that most cases of dementia involve multiple etiologies. 6 Indeed, with the availability of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies, the nature and extent of WMHs in AD may provide information to guide therapeutic decision‐making. The presence and extent of WMHs in AD should raise questions about (1) the origin of cognitive symptoms and therefore the therapeutic target, namely, whether the clinical phenotype is driven primarily by AD pathology, vascular damage, or a combination, and (2) the potentially increased risk of adverse events, particularly the development of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs). This perspective, developed by an international group of neuroimaging researchers, neurologists, geriatricians, neuropsychologists, and neuroradiologists, provides a brief overview of the pathophysiology of WMHs in AD. We will describe neuroimaging markers that may help elucidate the mechanisms underlying WMHs in AD and discuss the clinical interpretations of WMHs for AD diagnosis and the potential impact on the use of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies. Finally, we will propose a roadmap for future studies aimed at disentangling the etiologies of WMHs in AD, with the goal of advancing precision medicine.

HETEROGENEITY OF WMHs IN AD

The biological basis of WMHs in AD is heterogeneous and not completely understood. 3 , 4 Several reviews previously summarized the possible origins of WMHs in aging and AD in detail. 4 , 5 , 7 , 8 Here, we briefly highlight the main mechanisms relevant to AD, as this heterogeneity is important for interpreting imaging findings and for understanding the possible clinical implications of WMHs in the context of anti‐amyloid therapies.

Pathological correlates of WMHs

Pathological studies indicate that WMHs are associated with a range of tissue abnormalities, including demyelination, axonal loss, vacuolation, reduced glial density, endothelial alterations, and inflammatory or microglial activation. 5 , 9 However, these histopathological features are not specific to a single mechanism. Rather than reflecting one unique process, WMHs likely represent a final common appearance in MRI scans of tissue injury caused by different and partly overlapping pathophysiological pathways. 8

Vascular contributions and vascular risk factors

A substantial body of evidence supports a vascular contribution to WMHs. Modifiable vascular risk factors, especially hypertension, are consistently associated with greater WMH burden. Neuropathological studies also frequently report ischemic rarefaction of white matter, reduced small vessel density, endothelial dysfunction, gliosis, and inflammatory changes. 5 In addition, the stereotypical distribution of WMHs in aging and dementia—typically beginning in periventricular regions and extending into deep white matter—is often interpreted in relation to chronic hypoperfusion and the vulnerability of watershed territories. 3

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and AD‐related vascular pathology

In AD, WMHs may also reflect amyloid‐related vascular injury, particularly CAA. 10 This possibility is supported by the association between WMHs and other vascular amyloid‐related markers, including cerebral microbleeds. 11 Moreover, increased WMH burden is observed genetically determined forms of AD and in Down syndrome, including in individuals with relatively limited exposure to traditional vascular risk factors. 12 , 13 These observations suggest that WMHs in AD cannot be explained solely by hypertensive small vessel disease and that AD‐related vascular pathology or other non‐ischemic processes may play an important role.

Neurodegenerative and mixed mechanisms

Not all WMHs in AD are easily attributable to vascular mechanisms alone. Some studies reported spatial relationships between WMHs and AD‐related neurodegenerative changes, including regional atrophy and tau burden, raising the possibility that part of the WMH signal may reflect secondary degeneration of white matter tracts. 4 , 14 , 15 , 16 At the same time, vascular dysfunction may itself promote neurodegeneration, and the relationship is likely bidirectional. 17 More broadly, other AD‐related pathophysiological processes such as blood–brain barrier dysfunction, inflammation, glial dysregulation, and myelin dysfunction probably interact in complex ways. WMHs in AD should therefore not be viewed as arising from mutually exclusive vascular or neurodegenerative mechanisms, but rather as composite imaging markers of heterogeneous and interacting pathological processes.

Overall, this heterogeneity is a major challenge for interpretation because conventional MRI signal abnormalities do not provide direct biological specificity. 8 This limitation motivates the use of more advanced imaging approaches to better characterize WMH subtypes and their clinical significance in AD, as discussed in the following sections.

NEUROIMAGING TECHNIQUES TO DISTINGUISH WMHs ACCORDING TO THEIR ORIGIN

In the previous section, we acknowledged the heterogeneity of the biological pathways leading to WMHs in AD and the controversies surrounding this still unsolved question. The next logical question is whether we can distinguish these different pathways in vivo. In this section, we will address that question by describing three complementary neuroimaging approaches that may help disentangle this heterogeneity (Figure 2). First, the feature analysis of each lesion may provide useful clues. Second, advanced quantitative imaging methods can further characterize the underlying tissue properties. Third, examining WMHs in the context of other imaging and fluid biomarkers may offer a more comprehensive understanding of their origin—and thereby improve diagnosis.

Neuroimaging techniques and fluid biomarkers to distinguish the WMHs according to their origin. This personalized, precision‐based approach aims to disentangle the heterogeneity of WMHs in vivo and move away from an approach in which each increased white matter signal in FLAIR‐MRI is simply labelled “WMH". Instead, a more integrated, personalized approach is adopted, in which the pathophysiology of each WMH lesion can be detected using multimodal biomarkers and notably qualitative and quantitative analysis of WMH signals. Qualitative features: topography and appearance of lesions

Studies on WMHs have typically examined their topography using voxel‐based and region of interest‐based approaches. The most frequent anatomical classification of WMHs is based on their distance from the ventricles, that is, periventricular versus deep WMHs, but several studies also investigated their lobar distribution or their distribution across white matter tracts. As WMH spatial distribution and signal intensity are associated with cognitive performance in community‐dwelling older adults, we assume that this information may help inform AD risk. The topography of WMHs differs between healthy individuals and those with AD, notably with a more posterior distribution in the latter 14 , 18 (Figure 1B). This consistent observation suggests that WMHs located in posterior regions might reflect and/or promote AD‐related pathological processes. Among these processes, CAA, a condition frequently associated with AD and also showing a predominantly posterior distribution of biomarkers, could contribute to this anatomical distribution when WMHs conform to a characteristic topography. Specific WMH features are related to CAA, such as the juxtacortical 19 and multiple subcortical spot (“multispot”) patterns 20 (Figures 1C and 3). This multispot pattern, defined as more than 10 small circular or ovoid hyperintense lesions in the subcortical white matter of both hemispheres on FLAIR MRI, 10 , 20 was included in version 2.0 of the Boston criteria as a feature of CAA to increase the sensitivity of these criteria. Thus, this pattern enables the diagnosis of “possible” CAA, even in the absence of hemorrhagic features of CAA, or “probable” CAA when associated with classic hemorrhagic features, such as microbleeds and siderosis. Note that the specificity of the multispot pattern is contextual and the diagnostic utility of this pattern outside of hemorrhagic CAA populations is less specific and might also reflect other mechanisms.

In this context, to date, the “possible CAA” diagnostic category should not be used in clinical practice in memory clinics and particularly not to make decisions in AD patients. Beyond the multispot pattern of WMHs, large posterior “patches” of WMHs (Figure 1C) were not included in the Boston criteria, as one study in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage showed that this pattern was not more frequent in CAA than in hypertensive angiopathy, unlike the multispot pattern. 20 However, exploratory neuropathological data in patients with definite CAA revealed that posterior patches of WMHs share similar histopathological substrates with multispot WMHs in CAA and are also characterized by markers of microglia activation and astrocytosis. 21 Moreover, a recent study demonstrated that severe posterior periventricular (i.e., posterior patches of WMHs) and a high posterior/anterior periventricular WMH ratio were associated with probable CAA rather than hypertensive angiopathy. 22 Therefore, the extent to which these large posterior patches of WMHs in part reflect CAA, inflammatory processes, and/or coexistent hypertensive angiopathy remains unresolved, and further studies are needed to clarify the association with CAA and their shared pathophysiology.

Another pattern of WMHs frequently observed in older adults is the linear peri‐basal ganglia pattern (Figure 1D), as well as the brainstem pattern of WMHs, classically associated with hypertensive angiopathy rather than CAA or AD. 20 Both the localization (e.g., posterior lesions) and the patterns (e.g., multispot) of WMHs may provide valuable clues about their potential origin, but further studies are needed to better characterize the textural features of WMHs in AD. Mixed pathologies are common (hypertensive arteriolosclerosis and CAA often coexist in AD), and WMH pattern classifiers have limited specificity outside stroke populations: These qualitative indices are probabilistic cues, not categorical determinants. Characterizing WMHs by their shape, proximity to other structures, sphericity, and proximity to other lesions may also provide insight into their underlying pathophysiology. Finally, experience from multiple sclerosis research could be useful in the field of AD: In multiple sclerosis, lesion characterization based on features such as the paramagnetic rim on phase image or central vein sign has been integrated into diagnostic criteria, as these lesions inform on pathophysiology, disease activity, and prognosis. The field of AD could draw inspiration from this approach by applying or adapting such lesion‐based analyses to the study of WMHs.

Quantitative features: Advanced MRI metrics

Beyond the use of MRI to identify the extent and location of WMHs, WMH structural MRI signals on T1w, T2w, and FLAIR contrasts (or their normalized ratios) are related to underlying pathology and risk for clinical AD. 23 , 24 , 25 Other neuroimaging techniques such as diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) and quantitative MRI (qMRI) can also provide further insight into the specific pathophysiological properties of the WMHs. While MRI measures physical properties of the tissue, biological properties are intrinsically reflected in the physical properties and can be indirectly probed. Microstructural MRI metrics allow for the characterization and quantification of iron and myelin density, axonal integrity, and water content, paving the way for deep phenotyping of the pathophysiology of WMHs in vivo. 26 , 27

Recently, a DWI study with fixel metrics within main white matter tracts showed different effects of cerebral small vessel disease and AD on white matter integrity. 28 DWI measures were previously associated with markers of Aβ and tau aggregation, as well as astrocytic and glial activity. 29 , 30 Beyond diffusion MRI, there is an increased interest in qMRI methods. qMRI signals are influenced by the interactions of water with tissue components and can provide information about the local microstructural environment, 31 including myelination, iron, fiber integrity, and water content, and are correlated with post mortem histology characteristics. 32 T1 values are positively correlated with water content and gliosis and negatively associated with iron and myelin content. 33 T2 is positively correlated with the free water fraction and decreases with myelin‐bound water and iron deposition. 33 One study showed that individuals with greater amyloid load had WMHs characterized by lower T2 and higher T1 signals, 24 suggesting that quantitative MRI features could enable a more refined in vivo characterization of WMHs. T2* is also a marker for iron content and is increased with demyelination and sensitive to the presence of Aβ plaques. 34 To date, qMRI has been shown to be a promising but far from clinical deployment. While there is no one‐to‐one correspondence between these MRI features and the neuropathological changes associated with WMHs, they can be used in combination as aggregate biomarkers to derive neuroimaging‐based signature profiles that can determine the underlying pathophysiology of WMHs with greater sensitivity and specificity. 27 The relevance and feasibility of these metrics for clinical use have not yet been demonstrated, and future studies are needed.

Interpreting WMHs in the context of other biomarkers

WMH interpretation needs to consider other potential lesions. Cerebral small vessel disease is characterized by visible signs of microvascular damage on MRI scans. Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging (STRIVE‐2) have standardized the various features of small vessel disease, including recent small subcortical infarcts, lacunes, enlarged perivascular spaces, cerebral microbleeds, cortical superficial siderosis, and cortical cerebral microinfarcts. 1 The presence and topography of these non‐WMH lesions may provide information about the WMHs themselves and the vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia. For example, the presence of several lacunes and deep microbleeds increases the probability that hypertensive angiopathy drives the WMHs, while the presence of lobar microbleeds and enlarged perivascular spaces in the centrum semiovale may suggest that CAA may contribute to WMHs. Considerable overlap exists between small vessel disease etiologies and their MRI manifestations, making no single biomarker, including cerebral microbleed distribution, unequivocal for establishing the underlying pathology. The interpretive context further depends on the study population, whether community‐based older adults, memory clinic patients, or individuals in stroke units. Beyond small vessel disease MRI biomarkers, other pathophysiological biomarkers, such as biomarkers of AD pathology (fluidic analysis of amyloid beta [Aβ] 42, Aβ40, total tau, phosphorylated tau, and/or tau or amyloid positron emission tomography [PET]), as well as markers of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration, may inform on the pathophysiological pathways involved for each patient. Fluid‐based biomarkers of cerebral small vessel disease and neuroinflammation may eventually complement this panel of pathophysiological biomarkers, supporting precision medicine approaches to determine the vascular contribution to WMHs, 35 even though these biomarkers are still far from clinical implementation. Recent results from studies that incorporate these biomarkers 35 , 36 implicate blood–brain barrier and inflammatory processes in the pathways that link WMHs to AD risk and progression, suggesting “intrinsic” or “endogenous” cerebrovascular factors in the AD pathophysiological cascade.

IMPACT OF WMHs ON CURRENT RECOMMENDATIONS FOR ANTI‐AMYLOID THERAPIES

WMHs may influence the use of anti‐amyloid therapies through at least two distinct but related mechanisms. First, extensive WMHs – particularly when reflecting substantial cerebrovascular disease – may indicate that cognitive impairment is not driven predominantly by AD pathology, thereby potentially reducing the expected clinical benefit of amyloid‐lowering therapies. Second, WMHs may reflect underlying neurovascular unit vulnerability and/or CAA‐related vascular fragility, which could increase the risk of ARIAs. These two issues are considered separately below.

WMHs “of vascular origin” in AD: Risk of dilution of the cognitive benefit of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies?

In a memory clinic setting, WMHs of vascular origin could coexist with other brain pathologies. While the presence of cerebral copathologies is common, the specific impact of these pathologies on clinical phenotypes remains unclear. In other words, the key question at the individual patient level is this: Which pathology is responsible for which symptoms? In the era of disease‐modifying therapies, this question is essential to ensure that patients receive treatment targeting the relevant underlying pathology. The International Working Group (IWG) and the Alzheimer's Association workgroup recommended that these copathologies be considered in diagnostic formulation. 37 , 38 To assist clinicians in their diagnosis, in the case of a discrepancy between the clinical phenotype (e.g., presence of semantic or non­fluent variants of primary progressive aphasia) and biomarkers of AD pathology, the IWG recommended that the clinicians rely on the phenotype and follow‐up to determine the final diagnosis. 37 For WMHs, there is no validated and agreed‐upon cut‐off to determine whether the volume and location of WMHs are sufficient to explain cognitive impairment. The International Society for Vascular Behavioral and Cognitive Disorders (VasCog) proposed in 2014 that the predominantly vascular origin of a cognitive disorder depends on the nature, volume, number, and location of vascular lesions and is influenced by several factors such as brain and cognitive reserve. 39 It is generally accepted that focal WMHs visible only on T2‐weighted MRI could eventually lead to dysexecutive mild cognitive impairment but are unlikely to be responsible for the development of dementia without the presence of other brain lesions. For WMHs to contribute to dementia, they likely need to be confluent and extensive or focal but accompanied by other features of small vessel disease, such as lacunes. 39 , 40 Recently, the VasCog‐2‐WSO (World Stroke Organization) criteria specified that WMHs are considered significant contributors to cognitive impairment when they are extensive and confluent, or when periventricular Fazekas = 3 and/or deep Fazekas = 2 or 3. 40 It is important to recognize that although AD pathology (tau and amyloid) remains a major determinant of dementia in older adults, brain copathologies – comprising combinations of neurodegenerative and vascular lesions – also substantially contribute to the clinical phenotypes observed. 6 In summary, mild to moderate WMHs may contribute to mild cognitive impairment (corresponding to a minor neurocognitive disorder in Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition [DSM‐5] terminology), most often presenting with dysexecutive features – such as reduced mental flexibility and memory difficulties driven predominantly by retrieval rather than storage deficits, but they typically do not lead to dementia in the absence of other contributing pathologies. The presence of moderate to severe WMHs (i.e., confluent and extensive lesions) can be a driver of dementia (major neurocognitive disorder according to DSM‐5 criteria), either independently or—more frequently—in conjunction with AD or other neurodegenerative pathologies. In AD, WMHs can also influence the clinical phenotype, in particular by exacerbating slowed information processing or executive dysfunction.

The recent phase 3 studies on anti‐amyloid immunotherapies systematically excluded patients with severe (i.e., extensive and confluent) WMHs at baseline (Table 1). One of the arguments was to exclude non‐AD contributors to clinical phenotype (i.e., vascular contribution to cognitive impairment), notably to exclude the possibility that many patients diagnosed with AD have predominant vascular cognitive impairment, diluting treatment effects. Thus, the potential benefits of anti‐amyloid therapies in individuals with AD and comorbid extensive WMHs are unknown. Moreover, in the context of WMHs reflecting cerebrovascular dysfunction, we may also hypothesize that the presence of WMHs could be associated with limiting antibody delivery to brain parenchyma.

WMHs as a potential indicator of neurovascular unit vulnerability or CAA: Higher risk of ARIAs?

As patients with severe (i.e., extensive and confluent) WMHs at baseline were excluded from phase 3 trials, we lack data on the safety and efficacy of these drugs in patients with severe WMHs. A frequent and sometimes serious side effect with these anti‐amyloid immunotherapies is ARIAs, which manifest as ARIA‐edema/effusion (ARIA‐E) and/or ARIA‐hemosiderosis/cerebral microbleeds (ARIA‐H). Beyond the exposure to anti‐amyloid immunotherapies and the apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, one of the main risk factors of ARIAs is the presence of pretreatment cerebral microbleeds, emphasizing the relevance of careful screening for cerebral microbleeds or cortical superficial siderosis prior to treatment to assess the risk of ARIAs and guide therapeutic decisions. The association between pre‐existing lobar cerebral microbleeds or cortical superficial siderosis and ARIAs indicates that CAA and/or the endothelial dysfunction may play a significant role in ARIA pathophysiology. We do not discuss evidence regarding the association between cerebral microbleeds, cortical superficial siderosis, and the risk of ARIA in this perspective. Beyond features of hemorrhagic CAA, a high mean arterial pressure (≥107 mmHg) and the use of antihypertensive treatments were associated with greater and lower risks, respectively, of ARIA‐E in donanemab's phase 3 trial. 41 These findings suggest that cerebrovascular dysfunction, extending beyond CAA‐related features, may also contribute to ARIA risk.

Regardless of the use of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies, several studies showed an association between WMHs and microbleeds, 11 , 20 , 42 , 43 including an association between baseline WMHs and the risk of new incident microbleeds (both lobar and non‐lobar microbleeds). 11 With regard to anti‐amyloid immunotherapies, safety data on the association between mild to moderate WMHs and risk of ARIAs, are very scant to date. In 2016, a study of 262 participants from bapineuzumab clinical trials showed no association between baseline WMHs (none or focal WMHs in 264/301 participants versus beginning of confluent or diffuse WMHs in 37/301 participants) and the risk of ARIA‐H. 44 In a secondary analysis of the GRADUATE trials of gantenerumab, Salloway and collaborators examined 1939 participants (placebo, n = 946; gantenerumab, n = 993; ARIA‐E in 247 of 993 [24.9%]). 45 They showed that WMH severity, measured with the Fazekas visual scale, was associated with the risk of ARIA‐E, as well as with concurrent new ARIA‐H, with the odds ratio ranging from 1.62 to 2.20 depending on the model. For lecanemab, very few analyses are yet available. In the updated safety results from CLARITY‐AD, WMHs were associated with ARIA‐E in logistic regression analyses evaluating the impact of various risk factors. 46 Very recently, Rohatgi and collaborators studied 27 cases of ARIAs (six with ARIA‐E, 13 with ARIA‐H, and eight concurrent ARIA‐E/H) in patients treated with lecanemab, compared to 27 age‐ and sex‐matched ARIA‐negative controls. 47 They showed that a higher burden of deep WMHs at baseline (measured with the Fazekas scale) was associated with ARIAs. However, this association was not confirmed when the model was adjusted for baseline cognitive performance, enlarged perivascular spaces, and APOE ε4 status. Evidence linking WMHs alone to ARIA risk remains extremely limited, and several confounders should be taken into account (e.g., APOE ε4 status, pretreatment lobar microbleeds, siderosis, vessel fragility, Aβ clearance dynamics). Given the evidence to date, caution is warranted when interpreting any potential relationship between ARIA risk and WMH burden or pattern.

The American Heart Association recently noted that “there are no data on whether more recently identified non‐hemorrhagic MRI markers of CAA, such as severe perivascular spaces in the centrum semiovale or WMH in a multispot pattern, are also associated with increased ARIA risk, which is an important question for future registry data collection.” 48 Indeed, future secondary analyses of randomized controlled trial and real‐world registries will be required to address this gap (see Section 5, “Roadmap for future studies”). More broadly, the extent to which clinically relevant complications are associated with non‐hemorrhagic features of CAA also remains insufficiently established. In current practice, when anti‐amyloid immunotherapies are being considered in individuals with imaging suggestive of “possible CAA” based primarily on non‐hemorrhagic markers, a prudent approach is to factor this uncertainty into the risk–benefit discussion explicitly. Careful assessment for hemorrhagic features on GRE‐T2* remains important, and when there is residual doubt, sequences with greater sensitivity for hemosiderin deposits can be considered. In particular, two‐ or three‐dimensional echo‐planar imaging (EPI) susceptibility‐weighted techniques such as susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI, Philips, Siemens) or T2 star‐weighted angiography (SWAN, General Electric) may improve the detection of subtle hemorrhagic lesions in some cases. However, the incremental prognostic value of hemorrhagic features detected only on susceptibility‐weighted sequences (but not on GRE‐T2*) for incident ARIAs is currently unknown. This uncertainty is relevant because the Boston criteria were established using GRE‐T2* radiological findings, and GRE‐T2* sequences were also used in phase 3 anti‐amyloid trials (baseline and follow‐up) to define safety cut‐offs for ARIA risk at inclusion and to grade ARIA severity. Prospective studies that harmonize susceptibility‐weighted sequences and anchor their findings to ARIA outcomes are therefore needed before sequence choice can be used to meaningfully up‐ or down‐classify treatment risk. In patients with WMH pattern is suggestive of CAA but without hemorrhagic features (i.e., in “possible CAA”), closer MRI surveillance could be considered on an individualized basis—analogous to current practice in APOE ε4 carriers 49 —but the optimal relevance, nature, and frequency of follow‐up cannot yet be specified from existing data.

In conclusion, rather than supporting prescriptive thresholds, the current evidence suggests several factors clinicians may want to weigh before initiating anti‐amyloid immunotherapies in the presence of WMHs. These include (1) the overall burden and severity of WMHs, recognizing that extensive WMHs (e.g., with periventricular Fazekas = 3 and/or deep Fazekas = 2 or 3) often signal substantial non‐AD contributions to cognitive impairment and may dilute the relative clinical weight of parenchymal amyloid as a driver of symptoms; in such situations, any expected benefit from amyloid removal is intrinsically harder to anticipate, and these patients were also under‐represented or excluded in pivotal trials, limiting generalizability; (2) the topography and pattern of WMHs, particularly when suggestive of CAA, while acknowledging the current absence of validated links to ARIA risk; (3) the presence, number, and distribution of hemorrhagic markers on standard GRE‐T2* imaging, with selective use of susceptibility‐weighted sequences when the clinical question hinges on detecting subtle lesions; and (4) patient‐level modifiers of ARIA risk (e.g., APOE genotype, age, vascular comorbidities) and the individual's values regarding potential benefits versus risks. Pending more definitive trial and registry evidence, WMHs should therefore be viewed less as a stand‐alone contraindication or trigger for fixed management rules and more as one component of a structured, transparent, and patient‐centered risk–benefit appraisal. This complexity underscores the need for such decisions to be made within a multidisciplinary framework, ideally involving expertise in both cognitive and vascular neurology.

ROADMAP FOR FUTURE STUDIES

This perspective highlights WMHs as a promising yet underexplored window into the complex interplay between neurodegenerative and vascular processes in AD, with important implications for both research and clinical practice. It also opens several avenues for future research. In what follows, we outline some potential study directions in a non‐exhaustive manner (Figure 4).

Roadmap for future studies. Moving from global burden metrics to lesion‐centered phenotyping, longitudinal network‐level mechanistic testing, and multimodal biomarker integration with causal modeling may transform WMHs into biologically meaningful markers. A critical next step will be to move beyond global subject‐level summaries (i.e., the description of “WMH volumes” in an individual) and toward a lesion‐centered framework that acknowledges the substantial heterogeneity of WMHs within individuals (i.e., the description of the nature of each individual lesion based on an analysis of its features and texture). Leveraging advanced imaging techniques to extract intra‐lesional signals may allow individual WMHs to be characterized, 24 , 27 clustered, and mapped according to distinct mechanistic signatures, thereby revealing subtypes defined by their imaging features or spatial distribution. For example, analyzing the signal from DWI, qMRI or PET imaging within WMH lesions could provide radiomic metrics for these lesions. These metrics could then be used to cluster the lesions and disentangle their potential underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Such an approach would open the door to more precise associations with clinical outcomes. In parallel, longitudinal studies are needed to test the hypothesis that a subset of WMHs reflects secondary processes such as Wallerian degeneration by linking regional neurodegeneration, metabolic dysfunction, and tau pathology at baseline to the subsequent emergence of WMHs in structurally connected white matter tracts, for example. Combining tractography and disconnectome analyses with longitudinal imaging could help elucidate the network‐level propagation of white matter injury, particularly within circuits known to be vulnerable in AD, including the medial temporal lobe, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, and the splenium of the corpus callosum. 14 Recent advances in fluid (e.g., blood‐based biomarkers of AD pathology, microvascular damage, or endothelial dysfunction 50 ) and PET (e.g., with neuroinflammation tracers as TSPO) biomarkers provide unprecedented opportunities to dissect the pathophysiological pathways underlying WMHs and their interactions. Integrating markers of amyloid and tau pathology with measures of microvascular injury, endothelial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation – within longitudinal and multivariate frameworks such as mediation or structural equation modeling – may clarify causal relationships and reveal converging or diverging biological cascades. 35 , 50

Together, these approaches have the potential to transform WMHs from non‐specific radiological findings into biologically meaningful markers, refine our understanding of mixed pathologies in AD, and ultimately guide more targeted diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies. This better characterization of WMHs may help to study the associations between WMHs and potential risk of ARIAs. Collaborations with the sponsors of phase 3 trials of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies with high amyloid clearance (EMERGE/ENGAGE for aducanumab, CLARITY‐AD for lecanemab, GRADUATE for gantenerumab, and TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ for donanemab) could allow access to baseline MRI scans (including FLAIR‐weighted sequences) for centralized analysis. This approach would enable WMH segmentation, evaluation of patterns or quantitative analysis of lesions, and estimation of whether baseline WMHs are associated with subsequent ARIA events, after adjustment for confounders (e.g., APOE ε4 status, pretreatment lobar microbleeds). Such analyses could improve our ability to estimate ARIA risk, thereby supporting patient information and shared decision‐making.

GENERAL CONCLUSIONS

WMHs correspond to non‐specific radiological abnormalities due to fluid accumulation in the tissue reflecting white matter damage. These common lesions could be treated as accessible biomarkers of the diverse pathways involved in the neurobiology of AD, ranging from endothelial dysfunction to damage to large white matter tracts. From this perspective, we propose that, while still preliminary, valuable insights may be derived from imaging, both qualitative and quantitative, in an effort to determine the origin of WMH in vivo. This approach carries important implications for the accurate diagnosis of neurocognitive disorders and may also improve the assessment of both the efficacy and safety of anti‐amyloid immunotherapies. Finally, we outline a roadmap for future studies to catalyze progress in this field. More than 35 years after WMHs were first described and the initial questions emerged about their relationship with AD pathology, we now need concrete answers to enable precision medicine, from diagnosis to treatment, in patients with AD.
