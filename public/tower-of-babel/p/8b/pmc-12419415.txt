Efficacy of Fu’s subcutaneous needling in the treatment of neck-type cervical spondylosis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hui Ren, Xiaole Wang, Ting Fang, Lang Liu, Xiaolan Zhao, Jingxian Lv, Fushui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:As a minimally invasive technique, Fu’s subcutaneous needling (FSN) has been widely adopted in Chinese clinical practice for managing neck-type cervical spondylosis (CS). However, current evidence regarding its therapeutic efficacy remains inconclusive due to methodological limitations in existing studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore sought to quantitatively synthesize available randomized controlled trials to evaluate the clinical effectiveness and safety of FSN for this prevalent musculoskeletal disorder.Methods:This study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251036627). The databases searched include PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform (Wanfang), VIP Database (VIP), and China Biology Medicine disc (CBM). A comprehensive search was conducted across the 8 electronic databases from their inception through April 18, 2025. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials comparing FSN with non-FSN therapies for neck-type CS.Results:This study included 10 clinical studies with a total of 696 patients suffering from neck-type CS. The results of the meta - analysis revealed that the experimental group had significant advantages over the control group in terms of clinical efficacy. Specifically, for the total effective rate (odds ratio = 5.45, 95% confidence intervals [CI] = [2.75, 10.81], Z = 4.85, P < .00001), cure rate (odds ratio = 2.25, 95% CI = [1.51, 3.34], Z = 4.01, P < .00001), visual analogue scale score (VAS, mean difference = −1.21, 95% CI = [−1.30, −1.12], Z = 25.10, P < .00001), and Neck Disability Index score (NDI, mean difference = −1.33, 95% CI = [−1.92, −0.75], Z = 4.48, P < .00001), the experimental group outperformed the control group.Conclusion:This study indicates that FSN is a safe and effective strategy for managing neck-type CS. Therefore, it can be considered as a common clinical treatment modality for neck-type CS.

1. Introduction

Cervical spondylosis (CS) is a common musculoskeletal disorder worldwide, and its incidence has been rising significantly due to the aging population and changes in modern lifestyles.[1,2] According to clinical features, CS is categorized into neck type, radiculopathy type, myelopathy type, and other types.[3] Neck-type CS, as the most prevalent subtype of CS, is characterized by neck pain, stiffness, and limited range of motion, primarily involving the soft tissues around the cervical spine such as muscles, ligaments, and joint capsules. It may be accompanied by changes in the physiological curvature of the cervical spine, but there are no symptoms of nerve compression yet.[3,4] It not only severely impairs patients’ quality of life but also imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden on healthcare systems. Although various interventions such as drug therapy, physical therapy, and massage are available in the treatment of neck-type CS, their efficacy varies widely, and some therapies are associated with side effects or long-term dependence.[5–7] Therefore, exploring safe and efficient non-pharmacological therapies is of significant clinical importance for the prevention and treatment of neck-type CS.

Fu’s subcutaneous needling (FSN), invented by Professor Zhonghua Fu,[8,9] is a minimally invasive interventional technique developed based on traditional acupuncture theory. It involves sweeping movements in the loose connective tissue layer beneath the skin to stimulate local microcirculation, relieve muscle tension, and regulate neural function. In recent years, multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that Fu’s subcutaneous needling has obvious advantages in pain relief and functional improvement for neck-type CS.[10–12] In addition, FSN has also shown efficacy in other musculoskeletal disorders, such as scapulohumeral periarthritis and lumbar disc herniation.[13,14] Its mechanism of improving local blood circulation by releasing subcutaneous connective tissue has been preliminarily verified. However, issues such as small sample sizes, high heterogeneity in intervention protocols, and inconsistency in outcome measures have led to insufficient reliability and generalizability of the existing evidence. Therefore, this study conducts systematic review and meta-analysis methods to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of FSN for neck-type CS, with the aim of providing more reliable evidence-based medical evidence for clinical practice and laying a foundation for the standardized application and promotion of FSN therapy.

2. Materials and Methods

Since this study did not involve direct patient recruitment or collection of personally identifiable information, ethical approval was deemed unnecessary. The study protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251036627) prior to commencement, ensuring transparency and methodological rigor.

2.1. Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across 8 major biomedical databases from their inception dates through April 18, 2025, including 4 English-language databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Embase. As well as 4 Chinese databases: China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform (Wanfang), China Biology Medicine disc (CBM), and VIP Database (VIP). The search methodology was a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. The search terms encompassed floating needle, Fu’s subcutaneous needling, superficial needling, subcutaneous needling, FSN, neck-type CS, CS of neck type, and neck pain. Taking PubMed as an example, the search strategy is systematically presented in Table 1.

2.4. Data extraction

Two researchers independently carried out literature screening and data extraction. In the event of any discrepancies, Professor Liu Fushui was consulted to review the results. The extracted data consisted of the following aspects: the first author, the publication year, the sample size, the ages of patients, the duration of illness, the intervention methods, the treatment period, and the outcome indicators.

2.5. Basic characteristics of included literature

This meta-analysis included 10 RCTs involving a total of 696 patients with CS (348 in intervention group and 348 in control group). All intervention groups received either FSN or FSN combined with other therapies. For control interventions: 3 studies used acupuncture alone, 3 studies employed electroacupuncture or electroacupuncture combined with other therapies, 1 study utilized transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, 1 study applied Long’s spinal manipulation technique, 1 study adopted western medicine combined with application therapy, 1 study implemented traction therapy. Regarding outcome measures: 6 studies established clinical efficacy criteria based on the “Guiding Principles for Clinical Research of New Chinese Medicines (Trial)” using total effective rate and cure rate as evaluation index, All 10 studies adopted VAS score as an outcome measure, 4 studies employed the NDI score as the efficacy evaluation index. 2.6. Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers utilized the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool to evaluate the potential risk of bias in all the included RCTs. The assessment encompassed the following aspects: Random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and research personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and the identification of other potential sources of bias. In the event of any discrepancies or disagreements between the 2 reviewers, a third independent researcher was consulted for arbitration.

2.7. Statistical analysis

The data extracted by the 2 researchers were analyzed using Review Manager 5.4 software. Efficacy indicators were transformed into dichotomous outcome data. The statistical indices included the odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence interval (95% confidence intervals [CI]). For continuous data, such as VAS score and NDI scores, the mean difference (MD) and the 95% CI were employed as statistical measures. Heterogeneity was deemed significant when P < .05 or I² > 50%, and in such cases, a random-effects model was adopted. On the contrary, if P ≥ .05 and I² ≤ 50%, the heterogeneity was considered nonsignificant, and a fixed-effects model was utilized. Publication bias was evaluated by generating a funnel plot using RevMan 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) statistical software.

3. Results

3.1. Literature search results

A total of 232 literature were obtained through the preliminary literature search. After removing duplicate literatures using EndNote 21 software, 166 literatures remained. Following full-text reading and re-screening, 10 RCTs[15–22,24,25] were ultimately included in this study, involving a total of 696 patients (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the study.

3.2. Quality assessment of included literature

Two independent researchers evaluated the methodological quality of the included RCTs by utilizing the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool. All RCTs[15–22,24,25] described the methods of random sequence generation and were judged to be at low risk of bias. Regarding allocation concealment, only 1 RCT[21] was judged to be at low risk of bias because it employed appropriate opaque envelopes to achieve allocation concealment. As for the blinding of participants and personnel, given that it was not practicable to implement in the studies we included, all RCTs[15–22,24,25] were judged to be at high risk of bias. In terms of blinding of outcome assessment, none of the studies mentioned it, so all RCTs[15–22,24,25] were judged to be at unclear risk of bias. All RCTs[15–22,24,25] reported complete data and were judged to be at low risk of bias. Concerning selective reporting, the protocols of these trials conducted in China were not published, making it difficult to evaluate the selective reporting bias of these trials. Therefore, all RCTs[15–22,24,25] were judged to be at unclear risk of bias (Figs. 2 and 3).

3.3. Total effective rate

Six studies reported the total effective rate of FSN therapy, involving 456 participants (228 in the experimental group and 228 in the control group). The result showed that FSN therapy for neck-type CS has more advantages than control group (OR = 5.45, 95% CI = [2.75, 10.81], Z = 4.85, P < .00001) in improving total effective rate (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of the total effective rate of FSN in the treatment of neck-type cervical spondylosis. FSN = Fu’s subcutaneous needling.

3.4. Cure rate

Six studies reported the cure rate of FSN therapy, involving 456 participants (228 in the experimental group and 228 in the control group). The result showed that FSN therapy for neck-type CS has more advantages than control group (OR = 2.25, 95% CI = [1.51, 3.34], Z = 4.01, P < .00001) in improving total effective rate (Fig. 5).

Forest plot of the cure rate of FSN in the treatment of neck-type cervical spondylosis. FSN = Fu’s subcutaneous needling.

3.5. VAS score

Ten studies reported the VAS score of FSN therapy, involving 696 participants (348 in the experimental group and 348 in the control group). The result showed that FSN therapy for neck-type CS has more advantages than control group (MD = −1.21, 95% CI = [−1.30, −1.12], Z = 25.10, P < .00001) in decreasing VAS score (Fig. 6).

Forest plot of the VAS score of FSN in the treatment of neck-type cervical spondylosis. FSN = Fu’s subcutaneous needling, VAS = visual analogue scale.

3.6. NDI score

Four studies reported the NDI score of FSN therapy, involving 260 participants (130 in the experimental group and 130 in the control group). The result showed that FSN therapy for neck-type CS has more advantages than control group (MD = −1.33, 95% CI = [−1.92, −0.75], Z = 4.48, P < .00001) in decreasing NDI score (Fig. 7).

Forest plot of the NDI score of FSN in the treatment of neck-type cervical spondylosis. FSN = Fu’s subcutaneous needling, NDI = Neck Disability Index.

3.7. Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a sensitivity analysis using Stata17 software. By adopting the method of item-by-item exclusion, we analyzed the data of the included literatures. The analysis results showed that there were no significant changes in each Meta-analysis. This result indicates that the results of this Meta-analysis have relatively high reliability (Fig. 8).

Sensitivity analysis of the study.

3.8. Publication bias

Since most studies typically conduct publication bias analysis when including at least 10 studies, and considering the number of studies in our analysis met this criterion, the VAS score was selected as an example of the outcome for publication bias assessment. We used a funnel plot, a common tool for evaluating publication bias, to visualize the results. The generated funnel plot demonstrated no obvious asymmetry in this meta-analysis. Both the Begg’s test (P = .210) and Egger’s test (P = .500) results indicated that there was no statistically significant publication bias (Figs. 9–11).

4. Discussion

Neck-type CS, the earliest stage of CS, is a common clinical subtype characterized by neck-shoulder pain, stiffness, and limited mobility.[26] In recent years, with the growing percentage of sedentary office workers and manual laborers, neck-type CS has become a prominent musculoskeletal disorder worldwide, displaying a remarkable trend of juvenilization.[27] The development of neck-type CS results from multifactorial interactions.[6] Prolonged poor posture and chronic cervical strain lead to persistent muscle tension, which compresses cervical blood vessels and impairs microcirculation, ultimately causing ischemic hypoxia and metabolic waste accumulation in peri-cervical tissues.[28] Meanwhile, muscle hypertonicity and cervical facet joint dysfunction may mechanically irritate or compress neural structures, eliciting aberrant nerve signaling.[29] These pathological processes trigger sterile inflammatory responses, wherein released cytokines further sensitize nociceptors, exacerbating pain and swelling while disrupting the normal architecture and function of cervical tissues.[30] Currently, emerging evidence suggests that FSN demonstrates significant efficacy in treating neck-type CS.[14,31,32] There are multiple modern scientific bases for the treatment of neck-type CS with FSN. In terms of nerve regulation, the sweeping movement stimulates nerve endings, regulating nerve conduction and relieving pain.[33] Regarding the improvement of blood circulation, the stimulation of FSN can dilate blood vessels, increase blood perfusion in the neck, and simultaneously improve microcirculation, facilitating the supply of nutrients and the excretion of metabolic waste.[34] In reducing the inflammatory response, FSN can inhibit the release of inflammatory factors, regulate the immune - inflammatory response, and also promote the absorption of inflammation by means of improving blood circulation.[35] As a non-pharmacological therapy, FSN directly targets the affected muscles through sweeping movements combined with reperfusion activities, rapidly relieving muscle spasm and enhancing regional blood flow.[9] Unlike traditional acupuncture, which stimulates acupoints, FSN emphasizes precise intervention on “myofascial trigger points,” aligning with the theoretical framework of “myogenous pain” in modern pain medicine. Additionally, the “noninvasive” feature of subcutaneous needle insertion in FSN may reduce the risk of tissue injury caused by deep puncture in traditional acupuncture, improving patient tolerance. With advantages of simple operation, minimal safety hazards, and high cost-effectiveness, FSN has been clinically applied for over 20 years, benefiting numerous patients with neck-type CS and other chronic pain conditions, thus holding significant clinical implications.

This meta-analysis included 10 eligible studies. Current evidence indicates that FSN improves clinical efficacy for neck-type CS by increasing the total effective rate and cure rate, while reducing VAS and NDI scores. However, several limitations exist in this study. First, due to the invasive nature of acupuncture therapies, implementing single-blind, double-blind, or multi-blind trial designs remains challenging. Consequently, none of the included studies described blinding methods, directly affecting the quality assessment of the research. Second, only a few RCTs reported details of allocation concealment, potentially limiting the credibility of conclusions regarding FSN’s clinical efficacy and safety. Although most studies evaluated “cure,” “effectiveness,” and “ineffectiveness” based on participants’ subjective experiences, the lack of quantitative criteria for these outcomes remains controversial.

In conclusion, FSN appears more effective than conventional acupuncture or other therapies for neck-type CS. However, methodological flaws in the included studies may overestimate its therapeutic benefits. Therefore, further high-quality, standardized studies are urgently needed to provide robust evidence, thereby establishing more scientific treatment protocols and standardized guidelines for clinical practice.
