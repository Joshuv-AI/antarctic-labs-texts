Clear cell meningioma: Experience at a medical center
Authors: Jorge Luis Olivares Peña, Hector Ricardo Lara Torres, Jorge Luis Olivares Camacho, Jorge Arturo Santos Franco, María del Pilar Cruz Dominguez, Fiacro Jimenez-Ponce, Gabriela Martínez Díaz, Jesús Ricardo Cazadero Márquez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Clear cell meningiomas (CCMs) are a rare variant, represent approximately 1% of all craniospinal meningiomas reported worldwide, and typically appear in young and adult individuals, more frequently in women. Although they are classified by the World Health Organization as grade 2, they are meningiomas that often behave aggressively and have a high recurrence rate.Case Description: 337 records of patients with a histopathological diagnosis of intracranial meningiomas were retrospectively analyzed between January 2017 and December 2022. Three CCM cases are presented as examples. From the 337 records analyzed, 10 were CCM-related (2.96%). This paper describes three cases of meningiomas operated on at La Raza National Medical Center Specialty Hospital (HECMNR) (HECMNR, in Spanish), which belongs to the Mexican Institute of Social Security. The first case was operated with a Simpson grade I resection of the lesion and then developed other two recurrence events, which required another two interventions with Simpson II and III resections, respectively, despite oncological therapy. The second instance utilized a Simpson III resection and was promptly subjected to radiotherapy and currently developed tumoral recurrence that will soon require resection. The third case used a Simpson V resection, since the severe cerebral edema prevented a higher degree of resection.Conclusion: CCMs have a high risk of recurrence, making reintervention necessary, and therefore, cranial deformity and severe functional and neurological complications may follow, which explains the difficulty in managing this pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Clear cell meningiomas (CCMs) are infrequent neoplasms in comparison to other types of meningiomas, accounting for about 1%.[3] They are primarily published as case reports or small series of cases. Nevertheless, these instances provide ample insight into their aggressive behavior. Most of these tumors usually present intracranial location, although there are also cases reported in the spinal canal.[17]

Due to their low prevalence, there are no specific epidemiological and histological data on this type of meningioma in Mexico; therefore, this paper analyzes our experience with this type of tumor.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical and image records were retrospectively analyzed for all patients undergoing surgery for intracranial meningiomas at La Raza National Medical Center Specialty Hospital (HECMNR) from January 2017 to December 2022.

Inclusion criteria: records of patients over 18 years of age, with diagnosis by imaging and histopathology of CCM, operated at HECMNR.

Exclusion criteria: records of patients under 18 years of age, who did not have a complete dossier, records without a definitive diagnosis by histopathology of CCM, and records of patients not operated at HECMNR.

RESULTS

Complete clinical and imaging records were obtained for 337 patients who underwent surgery for some type of intracranial meningioma. From this universe of patients, 10 patients with a histopathological diagnosis of CCM were reported, which represents 2.96%.

Representative cases

Case 1

A 64-year-old female began with sleep-wake inversion and hyperactive delirium, followed by left brachio-crural hemiparesis and increased volume in the frontal region toward the right side. Imaging studies showed an intracranial neoplastic-like lesion compatible with meningioma [Figures 1: Panel 1: a-c]. Surgical resection was scheduled. Using a bifrontal craniotomy, Simpson I resection was performed on a very vascularized yellowish-white eraser-like lesion implanted in the anterior third of the sickle with occlusion of the superior sagittal sinus; in addition, it had considerable infiltration into the frontal bone [Figures 1: Panel 1: d-f]. Histopathology revealed CCM.

She was discharged uneventfully. She was not sent for an oncological assessment under the argument that a Simpson grade I resection had been performed. Tumor recurrence was evidenced around the 3rd postoperative year. A new surgical treatment was proposed, in which a grade II resection on Simpson’s scale was performed [Figures 1: Panel 2: a-c]. The histopathological report confirmed the diagnosis of grade 2 meningioma, with a histological subtype of clear cells [Figures 1: Panel 1-4]. In the immediate postoperative period, she was treated by radiosurgery with a dose of 17 Gy.

Follow-up was interrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic, so 6 years after her first surgery, she came back with general health deterioration, malnutrition, transcortical motor aphasia, mental function disorders, bilateral papilledema, and quadriparesis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed tumor recurrence with cystic images and invasion into the lateral wall of the orbit, in addition to postsurgical changes.

Surgical reintervention was performed, this time accomplishing a Simpson grade III excision. The postoperative course was favorable. At discharge, an assessment by oncology and radiotherapy was ordered; however, on this occasion, no new management was recommended. At present, the magnetic resonance (MR) shows small seeding in the middle third of the sickle and left pterional region.

Case 2

A 74-year-old male whose illness started with generalized tonic-clonic seizures, nominal aphasia, and right pyramidal syndrome. MR showed an extrinsic neoplastic lesion, located in the left frontoparietal convexity. Surgical treatment was performed with a Simpson grade II resection on a highly vascularized lesion of yellow appearance, having an eraser-like consistency, and firmly adhered to the convexity and the middle third of the falx cerebri [Figure 2]. The patient was discharged without complications. Histopathology diagnosed CCM. Due to the findings, he was sent for an assessment and complementary treatment by radiation oncology, receiving radiotherapy with fractionated doses of 54 Gy. He currently has a small tumor recurrence in the anterior third of the falx, which we will follow conservatively with MRI.

Case 3

A 65-year-old female with holocranial headache that initially was mild in intensity and occasional, but gradually evolved to be of greater intensity (Visual Analog Scale 9/10), became a daily occurrence, and was eventually accompanied by nausea and vomiting. She progressively presented decreased visual acuity of the left eye, bradypsychia, bradylalia, and delirium. She underwent specialized care until approximately 6 months after the onset of symptoms. Examination revealed papilledema, decreased visual acuity, left third cranial nerve palsy, and right pyramidal syndrome. MRI showed an extrinsic, neoplastic-like intracranial lesion located in the left sphenoid wing, with edema and displacement of contiguous encephalic structures.

The neurosurgical procedure was performed for neoplastic resection, which was accomplished only with Simpson grade V, finding a lesion with a mixed consistency, with friable and aspirable areas and others with a highly vascularized eraser-like appearance, infiltrating the cerebral cortex with a poor dissection plane. She left the operating room orally intubated and sedated due to severe cerebral edema and hemorrhage, which limited resection. In the intensive care unit, she developed severe gastrointestinal hemorrhage that could not be stabilized, ultimately leading to her death. Histopathology revealed a clear cell World Health Organization (WHO) grade 2 meningioma [Figure 3].

DISCUSSION

CCMs are a very rare variant. They were first reported in 1990 by Manivel and Sung[3,6,17] and are noted for having sheets of meningothelial cells, with round or polygonal nucleoli with clear, glycogen-rich cytoplasm and prominent interstitial and perivascular collagen.[7,14] They account for about 1% of all meningiomas, with ranges varying from 0.2% to 2%.[5] Most reports indicate that they tend to occur more frequently in young individuals and women.[2,4,10,11] They are usually located in the cerebellopontine angle and the lumbosacral spinal canal.[15] They tend to spread to other organs in a short-term period, usually within 6 months. At present, the WHO classifies it as a grade 2 meningioma,[1,5,6] due to its propensity to a high frequency of recurrence and poor prognosis.[10] Treatment of choice is surgical excision; however, its recurrence ranges between 37% and 80%, even after apparently complete resections.[10] Radiosurgery and radiotherapy are reserved for residual or recurrent cases.[15]

Histopathological diagnosis can be difficult, especially because of its resemblance to clear cell renal carcinoma, and can be confused with metastasis;[7] hence, several antibodies have been investigated to differentiate them, such as anti-CD10, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and CA9.[7] The RCC antibody is found exclusively in the immunohistochemical analysis of RCC and thus helps to make the differential diagnosis, while the other two support the diagnosis of CCM.[7]

CCMs are associated with the mutation of the SMARCE1 gene, which stands for switch/sucrose nonfermentable-related, matrix-associated, actin-dependent regulator of chromatin, subfamily E, member 1, a tumor suppressor known to play a substantial role in this type of tumor, which, when mutated, does not produce chromatin, favoring its growth.[8,12,13,16]

Out of a universe of 337 meningiomas surgically treated in our hospital in a period of 6 years, 10 cases (2.96%) were CCMs. The main author of this article operated 190 meningiomas, which represents 56.3% of all meningiomas in that period of time, including three CCMs, that is, a third of the total cases with this specific type of meningioma at this hospital [Table 1].

In the literature, the series with more case reports has a prevalence of 0.2–0.8% of all meningiomas. Although it has been asserted that the mean age of presentation is very variable, between 26 and 59 years old, cases have been described in individuals between 17 and 18 years of age and even younger.[2,13]

The three cases presented had in common intracranial hypertension secondary to the volume effect, associated with severe edema, causing herniations, displacements, and neurological deficit. Once operated, they were managed jointly with oncology using radiotherapy. All of them recurred and required reintervention by the author.

Out of the total CCMs examined at the hospital, there was a tumor recurrence of 50% in 5 years. In the three cases presented, we observed that recurrence does not only depend on Simpson’s grade resection,[9] but there may be other factors involved such as hypervascularity of the tumor, infiltration to the cerebral parenchyma, or bone invasion. The treatment of this type of meningioma certainly is very complex given the recurrence despite surgical interventions, whereas oncological management deteriorates quality of life, causes complications, and elevates the likelihood of progression to a higher histological grade with a more aggressive behavior.

At present, genetic and molecular studies in this type of meningioma show a promising outlook for the prognostic management of these tumors. Antigen-specific immunotherapy for these tumor cells remains under investigation but seems to be a viable and complementary option to surgery and oncological management.[14]

Furthermore, CCMs often present radiological characteristics such as hypervascularity, indistinct tumor margins, infiltrating tumor interdigitates with brain, marked peritumoral edema, observed in contrast-enhanced preoperative MRI, making resection difficult due to the risk of hemorrhage. In addition, we were confronted with a complex arachnoid environment that necessitated the application of advanced microsurgical techniques, such as microneurosurgery, precise tissue dissection, and meticulous hemostasis, with the utmost precision to mitigate the risk of injuring the cerebral parenchyma and/or vascular structures, including both arterial and venous systems. Furthermore, during the intraoperative phase, moderate cerebral edema was encountered, which significantly compounded the difficulty of the microsurgical dissection, demanding meticulous attention to preserve adjacent neural tissue and avoid compromising critical structures, while minimizing the risk of neurological sequelae in the patient.

As for surgical transoperatory findings, diffuse cerebral invasion and the lack of a clear dissection plane were found in all cases.

CONCLUSION

CCMs are rare and usually have a poor functional prognosis, being frustrating pathologies for neurosurgeons, since, despite accomplishing Simpson I resections, there is a propensity to recurrence. Oncological support based on radiosurgery or conventional radiotherapy does not prove to be sufficient for growth control, making several reinterventions necessary to control them. Esthetically, they tend to be deformed despite the reconstructions that can be made in the affected bone areas, and even when reconstructed, recurrence deforms the affected area again. Joint management with oncology and psychiatry services becomes necessary to provide the best possible prognosis to these patients.
