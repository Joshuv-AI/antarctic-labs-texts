Age-specific clinical profiles and economic burden of pediatric tuberculosis: a 5-year retrospective study in central China
Authors: Xiaojun Wang, Cuili Yang, Meilan Zhou, Cuiping Xiao, Xiaolu Yu, Qian Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch on the economic burden of pediatric tuberculosis (TB) remains scarce. This study aims to investigate the hospitalization characteristics, direct medical expenses, and key determinants of hospitalization costs in pediatric TB patients.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of the hospitalization information system of a tertiary specialized tuberculosis hospital in Hubei Province, China. We extracted hospitalization records of pediatric TB patients aged 0–14 years who were admitted between January 2018 and December 2022. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to analyze the determinants of hospitalization expenses, using a binary outcome variable defined by whether the total hospitalization cost exceeded the median value.ResultsA total of 401 hospitalized pediatric TB cases were enrolled, with patients aged 0–4 years, 5–9 years, and 10–14 years accounting for 19.9%, 15.5%, and 64.6%, respectively. The median total hospitalization cost was 10,608 CNY (1485 USD), and the median out-of-pocket expense was 9248 CNY (1295 USD). Notably, the 10–14 years group had significantly higher median total hospitalization cost (12963 CNY, 1815 USD) and median out-of-pocket expense (10431 CNY, 1460 USD) than the 0–4 years group (8539 CNY, 1195 USD; 8124 CNY, 1137 USD, respectively) and 5–9 years group (8192 CNY, 1147 USD; 7180 CNY, 1005 USD, respectively, all P < 0.0001). Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that older age (10-14years: OR = 3.18, 95%CI: 1.02–9.90), surgical treatment (OR = 10.66, 95%CI: 4.91–23.13), multiple hospitalizations (2 hospitalizations: OR = 6.13, 95%CI: 2.43–15.51; ≥3 hospitalizations: OR = 17.81, 95%CI: 3.96–80.03), and longer hospital stays (OR = 11.04, 95%CI: 5.55–21.96) significantly increased hospitalization expenses.ConclusionsPediatric TB patients experience significant economic burden due to high overall hospitalization costs and substantial out-of-pocket expenses. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) poses a formidable challenge to global public health, affecting individuals of all ages. Its implications for children are particularly alarming due to the unique challenges it presents and the long-term consequences it can have [1]. Annually, a substantial number of children worldwide are infected with TB. The Global TB Report 2024 indicates that nearly 12% of new TB patients are children [2]. In pediatric populations, TB not only manifests as a high incidence and mortality rate, but also imposes long-term health impacts, such as impeded growth and development, impairment of immune function, and psychosocial dysfunction, causing long-term economic pressure on their families [3–5]. Compared to adults, pediatric TB has a higher risk of progressing to active disease. Primary infection often leads to systemic dissemination, and the atypical clinical manifestations make diagnosis difficult, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis, and the complexity and delay of treatment [6–9].

In terms of disease-caused economic burden, pediatric TB tends to incur substantial direct medical costs due to cumbersome diagnostic procedures and complex treatment regimens. Despite the availability of national subsidy policies and medical insurance coverage for some TB patients, the limited scope of such support still leaves these patients facing considerable financial strain [10]. According to data from a national survey in China, direct medical costs of tuberculosis account for nearly 70% of the total expenditure [11]. In addition, indirect costs, such as parental labor loss due to caregiving, subsequent rehabilitation, and nutritional support also exacerbate the overall burden [12–14]. Particularly in economically disadvantaged regions, high medical expenditures often lead to deterioration in family economic conditions, exacerbating the social issues of poverty caused by illness and relapse into poverty due to illness, posing significant obstacles to achieving sustainable development goals [15].

Given this, a comprehensive analysis of the characteristics of childhood TB, including age distribution, etiological characteristics, medical insurance coverage, and economic burden during treatment, is crucial for developing targeted prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies. However, existing research predominantly focuses on the epidemiological characteristics of pediatric TB, with scant attention given to the clinical features and healthcare burden associated with the disease among children. To address this gap, we have embarked on a study involving the collection of data from designated TB healthcare facilities on pediatric TB cases. We aim to provide a scientific basis for optimizing resource allocation in healthcare, enhancing service efficiency, and providing new insights and strategic directions for reducing the family and social burden of childhood TB.

Methods

Data sources and collection

Hubei Province, located in central China, covers a total area of 185,900 square kilometers and administers 13 prefecture-level administrative divisions. In 2022, the permanent population was 58.44 million, with per capita healthcare expenditures of 2538 Chinese Yuan (CNY) for urban households and 907 CNY for rural residents [16]. Urban and rural areas are categorized by the household registration address of the patients (urban areas refer to municipal districts and county-level cities; rural areas refer to counties and rural towns).

The data were sourced from the Hubei Provincial Tuberculosis Clinical Technical Guidance Center, which serves as the primary institution for pediatric tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment in Hubei Province, and were specifically extracted from the center’s Hospital Information System—a system that records real-time and accurate data at the time of expense incurrence. The study encompassed a 5-year observation period (2018-01-01 to 2022-12-31) and children aged < 15 years diagnosed with TB disease were included. Comprehensive clinical documentation encompassed:

Demographic parameters (sex, age (age stratification: <5, 5–9, 10–14 years), household registration status).

Disease history (tuberculosis exposure, prior treatment).

Disease history (tuberculosis exposure, prior treatment).

Healthcare utilization metrics (surgical procedures, hospitalization frequency/dates, insurance coverage status).

Healthcare utilization metrics (surgical procedures, hospitalization frequency/dates, insurance coverage status).

Diagnostic classifications (primary/secondary tuberculosis diagnosis).

Economic burden indicators (total hospitalization expenditures, diagnostic test expenses, non-drug treatment expenses, drug treatment expenses, inpatient care expenses, other expenses).

Definitions

TB history refers to the patients’ previous clinical or etiological diagnosis of tuberculosis, or a history of anti-tuberculosis treatment. Household identity was divided into local and non-local. In China Non-local patients are migrants of a mobile population who live and work outside the place of household registration, and do not acquire local household identity through the Chinese Hukou system [17]. According to the Technical Guidelines for Tuberculosis Prevention and Control in China [18], TB is classified into pulmonary TB (PTB), extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), and PTB combined with EPTB (referred to as “Combined TB”), and etiologically positive TB refers to tuberculosis confirmed positive via any of the following methods: sputum smear, Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture, or molecular testing.

The hospitalization costs refer to the cumulative sum of direct inpatient medical costs incurred by patients over a five-year period at designated TB hospitals, including diagnostic expenses, non-drug treatment expenses, drug treatment expenses, inpatient care expenses and other expenses, as detailed below:

Diagnostic expenses: Including fees for laboratory tests and imaging examinations.

Diagnostic expenses: Including fees for laboratory tests and imaging examinations.

Non-drug treatment expenses: Including surgical fees, physical therapy fees, and interventional procedure fees.

Non-drug treatment expenses: Including surgical fees, physical therapy fees, and interventional procedure fees.

Drug treatment expenses: Covering costs of all types of drugs, including oral, injectable, and topical preparations.

Drug treatment expenses: Covering costs of all types of drugs, including oral, injectable, and topical preparations.

Inpatient care expenses: Including nursing fees at all levels.

Inpatient care expenses: Including nursing fees at all levels.

Other expenses: Including bed fees, medical material fees, and hygiene supplies fees.

Other expenses: Including bed fees, medical material fees, and hygiene supplies fees.

Out-of-pocket expenses refer to the portion of total hospitalization costs remaining after deducting medical insurance reimbursement, including two parts: personal co-payment for in-catalog items and personal self-payment for out-of-catalog items. The costs are expressed in CNY. 1CNY ≈ 0.14 United States Dollar (USD).

The number of hospitalizations represents the total number of hospital stay during the five-year period. The total length of stay is the aggregate of the number of days between admission and discharge dates for each hospitalization during the five-year period. The daily hospitalization expense is calculated as the total expense divided by the total length of hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables were presented as number (percentages, %). Wilcoxon rank-sum test or chi-square (χ2) test were used to examine differences among different characteristics of patients. Multivariable logistic regression model was employed to analyze the determinants of hospitalization expenses, using a binary outcome variable defined by whether the total hospitalization cost exceeded the median value, and multiple independent variables including gender, age, household identity, TB history, surgical treatment, medical insurance status, type of TB, number of hospital admissions, and length of hospital stay. To address potential information loss from dichotomization, a supplementary generalized linear regression model was also conducted on the continuous hospitalization cost variable to confirm the robustness of the findings. Statistical analyses were performed by using SAS 9.4 for Windows (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). In two-sided test, a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

General characteristics of pediatric TB patients

A total of 401 hospitalized pediatric TB cases were included in our study from 2018 to 2022, 52.6% were male, and 58.4% were non-local, and none died during hospitalization. Among these cases, 70.6% had a history of TB, 17.2% had undergone surgical treatment, and 75.1% possessed medical insurance, 18.4% had been hospitalized ≥ 3 times, and 49.6% had longer lengths of stay (≥ 10 days) (Table 1).

Characteristics of pediatric TB patients in different age groups

As shown in Table 1, the 10–14 age group accounted for the highest proportion, reaching 64.6%, while the 0–4 and 5–9 age groups accounted for 19.9% and 15.5%, respectively. There were statistically significant differences in gender, TB history, surgical treatment status, medical insurance status, classification of TB, number of hospitalizations, and length of hospital stay among pediatric TB patients of different age groups (all P < 0.01). The proportion of patients without TB history, undergoing surgical treatment, and suffering from EPTB was significantly higher in the 0–4 years age group than that of older children (all P < 0.0001). Conversely, children aged 10–14 years had significantly higher proportion of hospitalizations (≥ 3 times) and longer lengths of hospital stay (≥ 10 days) compared to children in other age groups (all P < 0.0001).

Expenses of hospitalized pediatric TB patients

The median and mean of the total hospitalization expenses of pediatric TB patients were 10,608 CNY (1485 USD) and 16,631 CNY (2328 USD), respectively. The total expenses were significantly higher among those who were female, had a prior history of TB, had underwent surgical interventions, held medical insurance coverage, exhibited concurrent combined TB, and had a higher frequency of hospital admissions coupled with prolonged hospital stays (Fig. 2). The median of out-of-pocket expenses was 9248 CNY (1295 USD), and the median proportion of out-of-pocket expenses was 91.1% (IQR: 57.8%–100%).

We further analyzed the proportion of different types of expenses in hospitalization expenses, and the diagnostic test expenses, non-drug treatment expenses, drug treatment expenses, inpatient care expenses and other expenses were statistically significant differences in three age groups (all P < 0.01). Among children aged 0–4, non-drug treatment expenses accounted for the highest proportion, whereas for those aged 5–9 and 10–14, diagnostic costs were the highest (Table 2; Fig. 1D).

Factors associated with high hospitalization expenses in pediatric tuberculosis patients

Multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that the risk of high costs for children aged 10–14 years was 3.18 times higher than for those aged 0–4 years (95%CI: 1.02–9.90). The risk for surgically treated patients was 10.66 times higher than for non-surgically treated ones (95% CI: 4.91–23.13). In terms of hospitalization frequency, the risk for patients with 2 and ≥ 3 admissions were 6.13 times (95% CI: 2.43–15.51) and 17.81 times (95% CI: 3.96–80.03) higher than for those with only 1 admission, respectively. The risk for patients with a total length of stay ≥ 10 days was 11.04 times higher than for those with < 10 days (95% CI: 5.55–21.96, all P < 0.05, Fig. 3). Discussion

Individuals undergoing treatment for TB may incur substantial economic costs, and often face heavy financial burdens, despite the availability of free treatment services [10, 11]. The findings of our study contribute significantly to the understanding of pediatric TB in terms of its demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, and economic burden.

The World Health Organization (WHO), in its “End TB Strategy,” explicitly states that no TB-affected household should face catastrophic costs [19]. Additionally, research has indicated that “TB” is one of the diseases with the highest out-of-pocket expenditures in outpatient and inpatient treatment for pediatric infectious diseases [20]. In our study, the median of the total hospitalization expenses of pediatric TB patients was 10,608 CNY (nearly1485 USD), which not only exceeded the total hospitalization expenses of children under 15 years old in Kashgar, Xinjiang, China (10194 CNY) [21], but also surpassed the sum of direct medical expenses (outpatient and inpatient) and indirect medical expenses of TB patients reported in Sichuan Province, China (9223 CNY) [22]. Notably, the median of out-of-pocket expenses reached 9248 CNY (1295 USD), which was 3.6 times (2538 CNY) the per capita healthcare consumption expenditure for urban resident households in Hubei in 2022 [16]. Although this study did not investigate outpatient expenses or indirect expenses, the aforementioned high hospitalization costs and out-of-pocket expenses are sufficient to indicate that pediatric patients and their families are under heavy financial pressure, and may even incur catastrophic expenditures. The primary reason may be that children, as a susceptible and vulnerable group, often face complex treatments accompanied by substantial diagnostic and therapeutic costs. Additionally, most pediatric TB patients in this study were of non-local household registration, not covered by local medical insurance or unable to claim direct reimbursement within the hospital system, which further increased the out-of-pocket proportion. Moreover, the care of pediatric TB patients entirely depends on their parents, further increasing the indirect medical expenses for their families.

This study identifies both consistent characteristics and specific differences in hospitalization features and costs among children of different age groups. In terms of commonalities, the proportion of non-local household registration was relatively high across all age groups—approximately 60% of the patients were non-local migrants who left their place of household registration to seek medical treatment, yet the treatment outcomes for this group were often unsatisfactory [23]. This phenomenon could stem from the unequal distribution of medical resources, compelling children with TB to seek treatment in distant locations. Although this study did not explore the indirect medical burdens of hospitalized children, the associated costs, including transportation, accommodation, companionship, and parental work disruptions, are substantial, as evidenced by other studies [24–26]. For instance, a study conducted in Uganda revealed that 80% of households reported suffering financial losses and missed workdays due to their child’s illness during medical care seeking [12]. This underscores the urgency to reinforce the equitable distribution of TB diagnosis and treatment services across regions and enhance diagnostic and treatment capabilities specifically for pediatric TB. Regarding age-specific differences, two key distinctions were identified: First, the proportion of patients suffering from EPTB and undergoing surgical treatment were significantly higher in the 0–4 years age group, particularly lymph node TB, which was consistent with previous studies [27, 28]. Second, Children aged 10–14 years had the highest hospitalization costs and out-of-pocket expenses. This is closely linked to their clinical features: their conditions may be more complex, requiring longer treatment durations, thereby exacerbating the economic burden [29–31]. Specifically, in our study, this age group accounted for a relatively high proportion of Combined TB cases, and also had the highest proportion of patients with ≥ 3 hospitalizations and total hospital stays ≥ 10 days. Furthermore, we found that this age group had the highest diagnostic costs, which may be attributed to the need for differential diagnosis to avoid misdiagnosis, thereby increasing total costs and out-of-pocket expenses.

This study found that surgical treatment, number of hospitalizations, and length of hospital stay are significant factors influencing the hospitalization costs for pediatric TB patients. Notably, children exhibit a high incidence of EPTB, which leads to an increased demand for surgical treatment. Surgical treatment itself require a prolonged recovery and observation period, during which patients necessitate meticulous nursing, ongoing treatment, and frequent check-ups, all of which contribute to a prolonged hospital stay [20]. Furthermore, diagnosing pediatric TB is challenging, and frequently needs multiple medical consultations to confirm the diagnosis, thereby extending the overall treatment duration. Additionally, we observed that pediatric TB patients with medical insurance had higher hospitalization costs in the univariate analysis. Although this finding was not statistically significant in the multivariable analysis, it still merits our attention—as it contradicts the goal of medical insurance to reduce patients’ financial burden. The main reasons for this may include the following aspects: firstly, the medications, tests, or treatment items used for pediatric patients may not be covered by medical insurance, or the reimbursement rate is relatively low, resulting in higher out-of-pocket expenses for patients; secondly, patients with medical insurance may be more inclined to choose high-quality medical services and treatment plans; thirdly, the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric TB are usually more complex than those for adults, requiring more tests and treatments, which consequently extends the duration of hospitalization [32, 33].

Our study acknowledges several significant limitations that merit careful consideration. Primarily, the data were sourced exclusively from Hubei Province. This geographical confinement, coupled with variations in economic income levels and medical reimbursement policies across diverse regions, fundamentally limits the generalizability of our findings to other geographical areas or healthcare environments. Secondly, the relatively low microbiological confirmation rate of pediatric tuberculosis in this study was another notable limitation. This is mainly attributable to the high difficulty of specimen collection in young children (who struggle to cooperate in providing qualified sputum samples), the localized lesions and low Mycobacterium tuberculosis load in children with primary tuberculosis, and the interference of prior anti-tuberculosis treatment in 70% of the enrolled children with a tuberculosis history, all of which easily led to false-negative results and may affect the accuracy of subsequent data analysis. Thirdly, there are also deficiencies in inpatient cost data collection: we cannot rule out the possibility that patients purchase non-insurance-covered medications outside the hospital, and off-site reimbursement is highly likely given the high proportion of non-local patients. These factors may underestimate total expenses and overestimate out-of-pocket proportions. Lastly, the analysis period of this study spans from 2018 to 2022, and we did not adjust for inflation in the cost data. Due to the lack of official province-level medical price inflation adjustment coefficients for Hubei Province during this period, the comparability of medical costs across different years may be affected by factors such as inflation and changes in medical service prices. As a subsequent step in our research endeavor, we will strive to conduct a more comprehensive and in-depth assessment and investigation to address these limitations.

Conclusion

Our study provides valuable insights into the characteristics and economic burden of pediatric TB. Children are not only susceptible to PTB alone, but also suffer from EPTB concurrently or even both conditions simultaneously. Notably, the out-of-pocket expense proportion for these patients is unusually high (exceeding 90%), and children aged 10–14 years incur significantly higher hospitalization costs. In addition, surgical treatment, multiple hospitalizations, and prolonged hospital stays are also key factors increasing the medical burden. Given this, pediatric TB is an important public health issue, and involves complex economic and social factors. Future efforts should focus on targeted measures: rationalizing medical resource allocation, improving early diagnostic capabilities for pediatric TB, especially strengthening the diagnosis and treatment capacity for extrapulmonary TB, and enhancing the fairness and efficiency of the medical insurance system, thereby reducing the socio-economic burden of pediatric TB.
