Disparities in Prenatal Diagnosis of Congenital Heart Defects: The Interplay of Insurance and Urbanicity
Authors: Joyce L Woo, Rupali Gandhi, Tara Iyengar, Lynn M Yee, Matthew M Davis, William A Grobman, Joyce Johnson, Angira Patel, Christina Laternser
Published: 2024

Congenital heart defects (CHDs) are the most common birth defects in the United States and a leading cause of infant mortality. 1 Prenatal diagnosis of CHD is associated with improved postnatal outcomes, 2 yet public insurance is associated with lower prenatal diagnosis rates. 3 Existing analyses have not fully characterized the intersecting effects of socioeconomic factors on prenatal diagnosis, which is necessary to guide policies and practices that address insurance‐related disparities in prenatal diagnosis. Given the demographic and economic differences between urban and nonurban (including suburban and rural) populations, 4 we hypothesized that the relationship between public insurance and prenatal diagnosis varies by residential urbanicity, or residence within versus outside city limits.

We performed a retrospective cohort analysis of linked medical record data from pregnant individuals and their infants. We identified infants who received their first CHD surgery requiring cardiopulmonary bypass in their first year of life at Advocate Christ Children's Hospital or Ann & Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago from 2019 to 2020. These were the only 2 centers in Chicagoland, the third‐largest metropolitan statistical area in the United States, performing CHD surgery during the study period.

The primary outcome was whether prenatal diagnosis of CHD occurred. The primary exposure was the pregnant individual's insurance status. We included the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children as a covariate to control for individual‐level socioeconomic status beyond insurance. Missing Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children values (n=146 [29.4%]) were imputed using a linear prediction model, including single parent status, race, ethnicity, educational level, age, and parity, yielding high correlation between imputed and raw values (ρ=0.75). Insurance and Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children status were not colinear based on variance inflation factors. To adjust for neighborhood‐level socioeconomic status, we included ZIP‐code level median home value quartiles. After testing multiple neighborhood deprivation variables, median home value achieved highest parsimony. We adjusted for comorbidities that influence prenatal diagnosis, including presence of critical CHD (age at surgery ≤60 days) 2 or extracardiac anomalies (eg, trisomy 21, Noonan syndrome, or gastroschisis).

Linear probability models were fit with robust SEs and hospital fixed effects. After testing separate variables measuring population density and urbanicity, we identified a moderating effect of urbanicity, and thus ran separate models for those living within (urban) and outside (suburban or rural) Chicago. Five sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate robustness. Both Institutional Review Boards approved this study (1714144‐5 and 2021‐4303) with waiver of informed consent. Because of the sensitive nature of the research, supporting data will not be made publicly available, nor available by request.

In total, 496 pregnant individuals and their infants with CHD met inclusion criteria; 47.8% (n=237) were publicly insured, 25.4% (n=126) resided within Chicago city limits, 11.1% (n=55) resided in rural areas, and 61.5% (n=305) had prenatal diagnosis of CHD. For those living in Chicago, association between public insurance and prenatal diagnosis was not significant (−10.7%; P=0.47). For those living outside Chicago, public insurance was associated with 15.4% lower probability of prenatal diagnosis (P=0.02). Large epidemiologic studies have demonstrated that those with public insurance are less likely to have prenatal CHD diagnosis. 3 We demonstrate that following more rigorous control for individual‐level socioeconomic status and stratification by urbanicity, the negative association between public insurance and prenatal diagnosis is statistically significant in suburban and rural settings, while insignificant in urban settings. This finding highlights the importance of considering intersectional factors in disparities research, specifically, the use of local, granular data and analytic consideration of the variety of effects that socioeconomic factors may have on prenatal diagnosis. Such analyses may better inform the design of policies and interventions to overcome insurance‐based disparities.

Our findings may be explained because insurance‐related barriers to obtaining prenatal diagnosis may be more readily overcome within a city because of factors such as a higher density of prenatal care providers or greater availability of public transportation. 5 Future research could investigate these hypotheses by estimating quasi‐causal impacts of these factors on prenatal CHD diagnosis. Concurrently, health care teams may ameliorate disparities by focusing on the unique barriers that may be faced by individuals who are publicly insured and living outside city limits.

Study limitations include possible unmeasured confounding that could impact the relationship between insurance and prenatal diagnosis, such as patient preferences in obtaining prenatal care. Our analysis does not include individuals who terminated their pregnancy, and may not extend to other metropolitan areas.

Policy aimed at ameliorating insurance‐related disparities in prenatal CHD diagnosis should be informed by granular data with consideration of intersectional socioeconomic effects. In this study of a large metropolitan area, the relationship between public insurance and prenatal diagnosis of CHD varies based on urbanicity. Specifically, individuals with public insurance residing in suburban or rural settings represent a key population for intervention to mitigate insurance‐based disparities in prenatal CHD diagnosis.
